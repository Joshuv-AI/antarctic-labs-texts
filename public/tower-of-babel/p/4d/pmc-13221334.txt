Exl1 from Pectobacterium activates a jasmonate-dependent immune network in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Everardo Jair Flores-Cuevas, Martha Torres, Damien Formey, Claudia Martinez-Anaya, Mario Serrano, Delia A. Narváez-Barragán
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial expansins contribute to the virulence of diverse plant pathogens, yet their biological activity and molecular roles during host interactions remain to be elucidated. Expansin-like protein, Exl1, from phytopathogens from the genus Pectobacterium, activates defense responses in Arabidopsis thaliana involving production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the jasmonic acid (JA), ethylene (ET), and salicylic acid (SA) pathways, ultimately reducing disease symptoms upon subsequent infections with P. brasiliense and the fungus Botrytis cinerea. However, the target of Exl1 within the plant cell wall and the downstream pathways associated with these responses are yet to be identified. Here, we analyzed the transcriptomic profile of A. thaliana leaves treated with Exl1 protein to provide a genome-wide transcriptional framework associated with Exl1-triggered immunity. Comparative transcriptomic analysis of Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 and JA-impaired mutant jar1-1 revealed distinct transcriptional patterns associated with JA-dependent responses at early time points. Abscisic acid (ABA)-related signaling was associated with later stages of the response, supported by the increased expression of the ABA-responsive gene CRK45 six hours after Exl1 treatment, and increased susceptibility of an ABA-impaired mutant. These findings suggest the involvement of multiple phytohormone-related pathways following Exl1 treatment. In parallel, extensive transcriptional reprogramming was observed, across diverse functional categories, reflecting a broad host response associated to the presence of Exl1. Together, these findings suggest that Exl1 induces an integrated signaling network that coordinates immune responses to strengthen plant defense, providing new insight for future functional studies on how bacterial expansins modulate plant-microbe interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11103-026-01723-9.

Introduction

Expansins are non-enzymatic proteins that mediate cell wall loosening in plants, facilitating developmental processes such as cell elongation, fruit ripening, and abscission while maintaining cell wall integrity (Cosgrove 2015; Zhang et al. 2025). Homologous expansin-like proteins have also been identified in bacteria, where their biological activity and molecular functions remain unclear (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020a). Genetic and functional studies indicate that bacterial expansins contribute to host colonization and virulence, as shown for Ralstonia solanacearum, Pectobacterium, Erwinia tracheiphila and Clavibacter michiganensis (Tancos et al. 2018; Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b; Rocha et al. 2020), as well as to colonization of the plant growth promoter Bacillus subtilis (Kerff et al. 2008). In the anaerobe Clostridium clariflavum, expansins are part of cellulosome complexes that contribute to plant biomass degradation (Artzi et al. 2016). Despite their implication in processes such as virulence, host colonization, and biomass degradation, the manner in which bacterial expansin activity at the plant cell wall is reflected in host transcriptional and defense-associated responses is not yet fully characterized.

Interestingly, pathogenic bacteria harboring expansin genes share a preference for colonizing the vascular system of their host, suggesting a selective advantage or evolutionary adaptation to this niche (Tancos et al. 2018; Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b). Bacterial entry into the host can occur through various routes, via roots, open wounds, natural openings such as stomata, or through inoculation by feeding insects (Pérombelon 2002; Toth et al. 2003). Regardless of the entry route, successful colonization requires bacterial movement through plant tissues to reach and invade the xylem by crossing the thick, reticulate secondary wall of the xylem vessels. This requirement highlights the importance of bacterial virulence factors, such as expansins, that interact with the plant cell wall during host colonization.

The Pectobacterium brasiliense expansin, Exl1, is an acidic protein that binds cellulose, although spectroscopic analyses indicate binding to other substrates as well (Tovar-Herrera et al. 2018). Fluorescently labelled Exl1 localizes at cell corners formed by xylem vessels and the surrounding cells, corresponding to the middle lamella, of celery petioles, a region particularly rich in pectin (Tovar-Herrera et al. 2018). Despite this clear association with plant cell wall structures, conventional biochemical analyses have not revealed significant modification of the cell wall following microbial expansins treatment. Moreover, the expected increase in cellulase activity, which would indicate greater substrate accessibility, is not observed under experimental conditions with low protein concentrations, although some studies report enhanced enzymatic activity at very high expansin loads (Kim et al. 2009; Artzi et al. 2016; Kumar et al. 2016). We previously reported that both celery and (A) thaliana leaves infiltrated with purified Exl1, trigger immune responses that confer partial protection against a subsequent infection by P. brasiliense or (B) cinerea. (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b). These defense responses include the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the activation of jasmonate (JA), salicylic acid (SA) and ethylene (ET) signaling pathways. Importantly, this response is specific to the wild type Exl1 protein and is not triggered by inactive Exl1 mutants or by the homologous expansin-like protein EXLX1 from B. subtilis (Kerff et al. 2008). This specificity suggests that the stimulus priming the plant is not the protein itself, but rather the consequence of Exl1 activity on the cell wall, possibly through the displacement or structural alteration of polysaccharides, or through changes in the mechanical properties of the cell wall (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b). To further understand how Exl1 activity influences plant responses associated with its activity at the plant cell wall, we analyzed the global gene expression changes in A. thaliana leaves treated with Exl1 and compared them with those of the jasmonate-deficient mutant jar1-1 (Chassot et al. 2007; Hael-Conrad et al. 2015), which fails to activate Exl1-induced priming (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b).

To characterize the genome-wide transcriptional responses associated with Exl1 treatment, we analyzed the transcriptomic profiles of Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 wild type and the JA-impaired mutant jar1-1, which was selected based on our previous observations that JA signaling is induced within minutes following Exl1 treatment (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b). Because JAR1 is required for active JA signaling, comparing Col-0 and jar1-1 allows us to determine to what extent the transcriptional response to Exl1 depends on a functional JA pathway. Within this framework, our study aimed to identify downstream pathways and signaling networks associated with Exl1 activity on the plant cell wall. This analysis revealed distinct transcriptional signatures linked to JA-associated responses at early time points, while ABA-related signaling was associated with later stages of the response. Additional transcriptional changes across diverse biological pathways were observed, providing a framework for further research.

Materials and methods

Exl1 infiltration, sample collection and pathogen challenge

Four-week-old leaves from A. thaliana ecotype Columbia-0 (Col-0), jar1-1 mutant (Chassot et al. 2007; Hael-Conrad et al. 2015), mpk3-1 (Wang et al. 2007), cyp79B2/cyp79B3 double mutant (Zhao et al. 2002), and aba2 (Schwartz et al. 1997), were vacuum infiltrated with 3.7 µM purified recombinant Exl1 (prepared as described in (Olarte-Lozano et al. 2014); the nucleotide sequence is provided in Additional file 1) diluted in 0.002% glucose solution, or with the (0.002%) glucose solution only (Mock). Leaves were subsequently inoculated with 6 µl of a LB (Luria-Bertani) medium suspension containing 108 P. brasiliense BF45 cells grown without antibiotics. LB medium consisted of 10 g tryptone, 5 g yeast extract, and 10 g NaCl per liter, and pH 7.0 (Bertani 1951). The same inoculation conditions were used for all treatments and genotypes. Plants were grown at 22 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod with approximately 60% relative humidity. Inoculations were performed 3 h after the onset of the light period.

RNA extraction, RNA sequencing, and differential expression analysis

Exl1-treated and Mock control leaves (as described above) were incubated in a humidity chamber at room temperature for one or six hours. Leaves from three independent plants were then ground in liquid nitrogen, and RNA was extracted using the Spectrum™ Plant Total RNA kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sample integrity and concentration were determined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Genomics, Santa Clara, CA, USA), at the Unidad de Secuenciación Masiva, Instituto de Biotecnología, UNAM.

RNA-seq libraries were prepared from total RNA using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform (75 paired-end cycles). Sequences are publicly available through the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under the accession number PRJNA993244 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA993244/). Contamination and adapter removal were performed using in-house Perl scripts. Fastq sequences were filtered based on quality (FASTQ Quality Filter v0.0.6, Q 33, http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/index.html) and mapped to the A. thaliana transcriptome (TAIR10) using Bowtie2 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012). Gene expression was determined using RSEM v1.3 (Li and Dewey 2011) and compared between the RNA-seq libraries using DEGseq v3.6 (Wang et al. 2010) and the FPKM data from RSEM, thanks to the IDEAmex platform (Jiménez-Jacinto et al. 2019). Only transcripts with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered (S1 data). Differential gene expression analyses were performed by comparing Exl1-treated samples to their corresponding genotype-matched mock controls (e.g., Col-0 Exl1 vs. Col-0 Mock and jar-1 Exl1 vs. jar-1 Mock). This approach allowed us to identify transcriptional changes specifically associated with Exl1 treatment within each genetic background. DEGs identified by genome-wide transcriptomic analysis were analyzed and classified into gene ontology classes (GO) using the PANTHER Classification System (PANTHER Overrepresentation Test, (Mi et al. 2013). Overrepresentation was evaluated against the A. thaliana reference gene list using the GO biological process complete annotation dataset. Statistical significance was assessed using Fisher’s exact test followed by Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05). Venn diagrams were generated in R to visualize overlaps among differentially expressed genes using the package ggvenn (version 0.1.19). For graphical representation and downstream interpretation, GO terms showing significant enrichment and broad gene representation were selected. Categories presented in the results section, were prioritized based on statistical enrichment and their biological relevance to plant stress, defense and cell wall-associated processes, as these are biologically relevant to plant-pathogen interactions and the suggested activity of expansin-like proteins (Tovar-Herrera et al. 2018; Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b). Genes highlighted within each category are representative examples from significantly enriched GO groups. Heatmaps were constructed using Heatmapper web interface (Babicki et al. 2016). Functional annotation and gene descriptions were curated using Uniprot and the TAIR databases (Swarbreck et al. 2007, 2021).

RT-qPCR validation of RNA-seq data

To validate our transcriptomic analyses, we assessed the expression of JAR1 and CRK45 genes by RT-qPCR in Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 and jar1-1 plants at one and six hours after Exl1 treatments (C1, C6, J1 and J6). These genes were selected based on their differential expression profiles observed in the RNA-seq analysis. For each treatment and time point, total RNA was extracted from 14 leaves collected from 10 independent plants were pooled to generate one biological replicate. Two independent biological replicates were obtained from separate experiments using independently grown plant populations. cDNA was synthesize using the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific). Each RT-qPCR reaction was performed in three technical replicates. qPCR data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001) to calculate the relative expression of JAR1 and CRK45 in each treatment relative to its respective mock controls and using UBQ10 and ACT2 as housekeeping genes (Czechowski et al. 2005; Aragón et al. 2021; Rojas Moreno et al. 2023; Martínez-Terrazas et al. 2026). Two independent biological replicates were obtained from separate experiments using independently grown plant populations, and each RT-qPCR reaction was performed in three technical replicates. No significant differential regulation of these genes was detected upon Exl1 treatment. The primers used were as follows, for JAR1: Fwd 5’-CTTTTGCCTTCAGAAACAGAG-3’, Rev 5’-CCCAGCTTTGAAGTTAGGGT-3’, for CRK45: Fwd 5’-GTTTACAAGGGTAGGCTTCAA-3’, Rev 5’-GCAGAAACCAAGAAGATTGATC-3’, for UBQ10 F 5’-ACCCTAACGGGAAAGACGAT-3’, R 5’-AGCCTGAGAACAAGATGAAGG-3’, and for ACT2 F 5’-GCCATCCAAGCTGTTCTCTC − 3’, R 5’-GCTCGTAGTCAACAGCAACAA-3’. Two independent biological replicates were included for each condition.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was evaluated using two-tailed unpaired t-tests after assessing normality with the Shapiro-Wilk test. p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and graphs were generated in R (version 4.1.1 (R Core Team 2022).

Results

Exl1 triggers transcriptional reprogramming in A. thaliana

To characterize genome-wide transcriptional responses associated with Exl1 treatment, we performed a transcriptomic analysis of A. thaliana Col-0 and the jasmonate-deficient mutant jar1-1 following Exl1 infiltration. As previously reported, Col-0 leaves displayed a primed defense, showing a 56% reduction in maceration incidence after subsequent infection with P. brasiliense (Fig. 1a), whereas jar1-1 mutants failed to develop protection and were 18% more susceptible (Fig. 1b). Transcriptomic profiles were generated for Col-0 and jar1-1 leaves at one and six hours post treatment (hpt). The first timepoint was aimed to explore early transcriptional events, while the six-hour timepoint corresponds to later responses. In Col-0 plants treated with Exl1, 817 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected at 1 hpt (385 were upregulated and 432 were downregulated, Fig. 1c). At 6 hpt, a similar number of genes were upregulated (441) and downregulated (459, Fig. 1d). Interestingly, jar1-1 mutant exhibited a stronger transcriptional response at 1 hpt, with 652 upregulated and 859 downregulated genes (Fig. 1e); whereas at 6 hpt, 538 DEGs were detected (190 upregulated and 348 downregulated, Fig. 1f). These findings indicate that the increased number of DEGs in jar1-1 mutant does not correlate with enhanced resistance to P. brasiliense infection, suggesting that functional JA signaling is associated with the transcriptional patterns observed in Exl1-treated plants.

Exl1 induces distinct transcriptional responses in Col-0 and jar1-1

To further explore transcriptional responses associated with Exl1 treatment in resistant (Col-0) and susceptible (jar1-1) backgrounds, we compared the global expression profiles across genotypes and time points. We identified specific and common DEGs between wild type and jar1-1 mutant using Venn diagrams that include all experimental conditions (Fig. 2). Within each genotype, only a small subset of DEGs was common between the two time points. In Col-0 and jar1-1 plants, respectively, 10 and 9 genes were co-induced, while 6 and 11 were co-repressed (Fig. 2A, B). These values represent roughly 1% of all DEGs detected in each genotype, indicating that most transcriptional changes were time-specific and that distinct sets of genes were differentially expressed at different stages following Exl1 treatment. When comparing both genotypes at each time point, 73 genes were co-induced and 159 were co-repressed at 1 hpt, whereas only 5 genes were co-induced, and 27 were co-repressed at 6 hpt. Notably, no genes were commonly upregulated across all genotypes and timepoints, and only four were found repressed (Fig. 2). To determine whether these transcriptional shifts patterns were associated with distinct physiological processes, we analyzed the biological functions overrepresented among the DEGs. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that, despite the low number of common genes, DEGs from both genotypes and timepoints clustered into similar functional categories. Col-0 DEGs showed enrichment in abiotic stress and JA-related responses, whereas jar1-1 displayed a broader enrichment in categories: response to water deprivation, response to biotic stimulus, and oxidative response (Fig. S1).

Given that DEGs from both genotypes converged on similar functional categories, we next examined how these transcriptional patterns were distributed across functional and signaling-related categories in both resistant (Col-0) and susceptible (jar1-1) plants (Fig. S2). Overall, Col-0 DEGs were predominantly upregulated in categories related to transport, the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), defense responses, transcriptional regulation, translation, phytohormone signaling, and general signaling processes. In contrast, jar1-1 plants showed broader downregulation (≤ 2-fold change; log2FC) across most of these categories, except for methyltransferases, which were upregulated (Fig. S2). These transcriptional differences are consistent with the contrasting phenotypes observed between genotypes (Fig. 1a, b), indicating an association between JA signaling and the overall transcriptional response to Exl1 treatment.

JA influences cell wall and signaling-related transcriptional responses to Exl1

To further characterize transcriptional differences between Col-0 and jar1-1 in response to Exl1 treatment, we examined DEGs from enriched functional categories to identify shared and genotype-specific responses (Fig. 3). Because of Exl1’s proposed activity in the plant cell wall, we first examined genes linked to cell wall associated processes. Although no receptor genes were differentially expressed at 1 hpt in Col-0 plants (Fig. 3a), analysis of genes related to cell wall biosynthesis and remodeling revealed early upregulation (1 hpt) of CSLA15 (Fig. S2), which participates in hemicellulose mannan synthesis (Del Carmen Rodríguez-Gacio et al. 2012). In addition, At5G45050, encoding a TIR-NBS-LRR-type intracellular immune receptor, was upregulated at this timepoint (Fig. S2). These transcriptional changes suggest the modulation of cell wall reinforcement or remodeling processes, and intracellular defense-related gene expression during early stages of the response. Notably, both genes were upregulated in Col-0 but not in the jar1-1 mutant, indicating that this early responses may depend on functional JA signaling.

Regarding plant signaling (Fig. 3b), genes associated with ROS production were upregulated in Col-0 at 1 hpt (CLH1) and 6 hpt (MDAR6, At5g21105, XDH2). In contrast, in the JA-deficient mutant, several genes within this pathway were mostly downregulated at 1 hpt (At4g29670, COR47, ERD10, PER4, At1g80130, At5g59080). Similarly, multiple protein kinases genes (At1g01540, PTI12, IRE3, KIPK1, MGH6.16, At3g51270) were upregulated in Col-0 plants, with significant changes observed at 6 hpt, whereas PK1B and RAF10 were downregulated in jar1-1. Mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), which are associated with stress and immune signaling (Bi et al. 2018), also showed genotype-specific transcriptional patterns: MAP3KA was upregulated at 1 hpt in Col-0, while MAPKKK19 and MPK17 were upregulated in jar1-1. These results suggest genotype-dependent differences in the transcriptional regulation of ROS and kinase-associated genes, consistent with JA signaling capacity between backgrounds. ROS involvement in Exl1-triggered responses was previously reported (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b), and the transcriptional patterns observed here provides a broader view of associated gene expression changes. To test the contribution of MAPKs to Exl1-associated responses, A. thaliana mpk3 mutant leaves were treated with purified Exl1 prior to pathogen challenge. Exl1 treatment reduced disease incidence in the mpk3 mutant compared to mock-treated controls (Fig. S3a), suggesting that MPK3 is not essential for Exl1-associated protection. Given that multiple kinase- and MAPK-related genes were transcriptionally regulated following Exl1 treatment, these findings are consistent with potential redundancy within kinase-associated signaling components, although further validation is needed.

JA and ABA mediated responses contribute to Exl1-induced protection

Given the central role of phytohormones in coordinating defense signaling, we examined transcriptional changes in genes involved in hormone synthesis and signaling. The predominant transcriptional signatures observed following Exl1 treatment were related to JA and SA pathways (Fig. 3c), consistent with our previous observations (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b). In Col-0 plants, genes associated with JA synthesis (ABCD1, JAR1) and JA-responsive genes (JAL34, CLH1, VSP2) were upregulated (Kariola et al. 2005; Theodoulou et al. 2005; Liu et al. 2005; Chassot et al. 2007; Xiang et al. 2011). Similarly, SA-induced genes, including JAL34 and CRK45, were upregulated at 1 hpt in Col-0 plants, as was BSMT1, which is linked to activation of the SA pathway (Chen et al. 2003), and the tryptophan synthase gene (At5g28237) whose metabolic intermediates have been proposed to promote SA synthesis (Miao et al. 2019). Interestingly, we also observed upregulation of abscisic acid (ABA) responsive genes (GRDP1 (Rodríguez-Hernández et al. 2014), ABCG40 (Kuromori and Shinozaki 2010), and CRK45 (Zhang et al. 2013) in Col-0 plants, suggesting participation of ABA-associated signaling in the Exl1-induced response.

To validate the phytohormone-associated expression patterns identified in the genome-wide transcriptomics analysis, we selected JAR1, a JA-related gene, and CRK45, whose expression is induced by SA and ABA, as candidates for RT-qPCR analysis based on their differential expression in our dataset (Fig. 3 and S3). Both JAR1 and CRK45 showed transcriptional patterns consistent with the RNA-seq data (Figs. 3 and 4), supporting the robustness of our genome-wide transcriptomic analysis and its value as a framework for subsequent functional studies. JAR1 expression significantly increased in Col-0 plants at 1 and remained elevated at 6 hpt (Fig. 4a), suggesting that JA-associated transcriptional responses occur during the early establishment and persist during the Exl1-induced response. JAR1 transcripts were also detected at low levels in the jar1-1 mutant, likely because this line is impaired, but not completely deficient in JA signaling (Chassot et al. 2007). In contrast, CRK45 was strongly upregulated at 6 hpt in Col-0 plants, consistent with a later-phase hormone-associated transcriptional response, whereas its induction was markedly reduced in jar1-1, indicating differential regulation in a JA-impaired background (Fig. 4b). To further assess the role of ABA in the Exl1-induced response, we utilized the A. thaliana ABA-impaired mutant aba2. Exl1-treated aba2 mutants exhibited significantly higher disease incidence compared to mock-treated controls upon pathogen challenge (Fig. 4c). These results indicate that ABA-dependent signaling is required for Exl1-mediated protection.

Auxin-associated genes (ABCB4 and CAMTA1 (Cho et al. 2008; Galon et al. 2010)) were also upregulated in Col-0 plants at 6 hpt in our transcriptomics dataset. Exl1-treated cyp79B2/cyp79B3 double mutant, impaired in auxin biosynthesis, exhibited a trend toward increased disease incidence compared to mock-treated controls (Fig. S3b), suggesting a possible contribution of auxin-associated signaling that warrants further investigation.

Interestingly, in jar1-1 plants, genes related to ABA (BFRUCT4, GBF3, STY46 (Yamada et al. 2010; Ramegowda et al. 2017; Dong et al. 2020), auxin (ABCB21(Kamimoto et al. 2012), and gibberellin (SCL13 (Yoshida and Ueguchi-Tanaka 2014), were upregulated as early as 1 hpt (Fig. 3), indicating altered hormone-associated transcriptional responses in the JA-impaired background.

Together, these results support the involvement of multiple phytohormone-associated responses following Exl1 treatment. JA-related gene expression was evident at early time points and remained elevated over time, whereas SA- and ABA-associated transcriptional responses were more evident at later stages. In the JA-impaired jar1-1 mutant, altered hormone-associated gene expression patterns were observed, underscoring the central role of JA signaling in shaping the Exl1-induced response.

Protein turnover and defense-related genes are upregulated in Col-0 plants

Beyond hormone-associated responses, Exl1 treatment was also linked to transcriptional changes in genes involved in protein turnover pathways (Fig. 3d). Several elements of the ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS), including E3 (RHF2A, PUB4) and HECT (KAK) ubiquitin ligases, E3 ubiquitin transferases (At2g47090), ubiquitin thioesterases (AMSH2), and F-box proteins (At1g80960), were upregulated in Col-0 (Fig. 3). In contrast, UPS components (BAH1, PUB45 and At1g06630 (Mudgil et al. 2004; Nawy et al. 2005; Samuel et al. 2008; Yaeno and Iba 2008)) were downregulated in the jar1-1 mutant. These patterns suggest differential transcriptional regulation of UPS-associated components between Col-0 and the JA-impaired background following Exl1 treatment.

Alongside these changes, genes associated with transcriptional regulation were upregulated at both 1 and 6 hpt in Col-0 plants but were mainly downregulated at 1 hpt in jar1-1 (Fig. 3e). Similarly, genes linked to protein biosynthesis showed both up- and downregulation at later stages (6 hpt) in Col-0, whereas no significant changes were observed in jar1-1 (Fig. 3f). Together, these patterns suggest broader transcriptional changes affecting protein turnover, gene regulation, and biosynthetic capacity in Col-0 following Exl1 treatment, which are markedly altered in the absence of functional JA signaling.

Finally, Exl1 treatment was also associated with transcriptional changes in genes linked to defense responses (Fig. 3g). In Col-0 plants, defense-related genes (KTI3, VSP2, SINE2 (Liu et al. 2005; Zhou et al. 2014; Arnaiz et al. 2018) were upregulated at 1 hpt, consistent with early transcriptional responses following Exl1 exposure. At 6 hpt, genes associated with stress tolerance (BSMT1 (Chen et al. 2003) and genes linked to pathogen resistance (CLH1 and ABCG34 (Kariola et al. 2005; Khare et al. 2017) were also upregulated, suggesting that immune response transcriptional changes persisted over time. Interestingly, jar1-1 plants genes linked to regulation of defense (BFRUCT4 and RPS5 (Yamada et al. 2010; Pottinger and Innes 2020)) were upregulated; however, genes associated with stress tolerance, such as those observed in Col-0, were not induced in this mutant. Together, these observations highlight differences in defense-associated gene expression between Col-0 and the JA-impaired background following Exl1 treatment.

Together, these results indicate that Exl1 induces extensive transcriptional reprogramming in A. thaliana, involving multiple regulatory pathways and phytohormone-associated processes, while highlighting a central role for JA signaling and revealing ABA as an additional component of the response. This genome-wide transcriptomic analysis also provides a framework for further investigation.

Discussion

Insights into perception and activity of Exl1

One intriguing aspect to the Exl1-induced immunity is how this bacterial protein activity is perceived and how it triggers defense responses in the plant. Previous studies have proposed that Exl1 activity on the cell wall may generate Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs), potentially through the release or displacement of polysaccharides (Tovar-Herrera et al. 2018; Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020a, b). In earlier work we suggested that Exl1 might target pectin components of the cell wall (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020a, b), however, in the present transcriptomic analysis we did not detect differential expression of genes directly related to pectin metabolism. Another possibility is that Exl1 alters the physical properties of the cell wall, thereby activating cell wall integrity (CWI) signaling (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2022). Impairment of CWI has been shown to induce the synthesis of structural polysaccharides (Pogorelko et al. 2013) and lignin deposition (Caño-Delgado et al. 2003), hallmarks of cell wall stress responses, yet we did not observe differential expression of genes associated with canonical CWI repair pathways. Nevertheless, in Col-0 plants we observed early (1 hpt) upregulation of CSLA15, a gene involved in hemicellulose mannan synthesis, which contributes to cell wall rigidity (Del Carmen Rodríguez-Gacio et al. 2012) and interacts closely with cellulose microfibrils (Obel et al. 2007; Yu et al. 2018). This observation suggests that Exl1 may promote remodeling of the cell wall matrix, potentially triggering compensatory reinforcement mechanisms. We also detected upregulation of AT5G45050, which encodes a TIR-NBS-LRR-type intracellular immune receptor (McHale et al., 2006), pointing to the activation of defense-related signaling following Exl-associated wall perturbation. Together, these observations suggest an early perception and response cascade linking cell wall modification to immune signaling, although the molecular components responsible for sensing Exl1 activity at the cell surface remain to be identified.

Previous studies have shown that the receptor-like kinase ERK1 regulates the perception of the Phytophthora capsici expansin (PcEXLX1), suggesting that PcEXLX1 might trigger immune responses via an LRR-RLP receptor (Pi et al. 2022). However, the mechanism underlying expansin perception remains unclear. In our dataset, we did not detect transcriptional induction of membrane receptor genes in Col-0 plants at 1 hpt. Instead, two receptor genes were downregulated (Fig. 3S). One of these was Receptor-like protein 32 (RLP32), which was also downregulated in jar1-1 at 1 hpt. RLP32 recognizes proteobacterial translation initiation Factor 1 (IF1) and activate PAMP-triggered immunity (PTI) in A. thaliana, conferring resistance to Pseudomonas syringae (Fan et al. 2022). The second gene, Stress Induced Factor 4 (SIF4, AT1G51820), which participates in the perception of cellooligosaccharides in A. thaliana and tomato (Zarattini et al. 2021), was also downregulated in jar1-1 at 6 hpt. Downregulation of receptors following stimulus perception has been previously reported (Tör et al. 2009). Therefore, considering the timing of our sampling, it is plausible that receptors involved in Exl1 activity perception had already undergone transcriptional repression by the time points analyzed here. In addition, key co-receptors involved in RLP32 y SIF4, such as BRI1-associated kinase 1 (BAK1) and suppressor of BIR1-1 (SOBIR1) (Zarattini et al. 2021; Fan et al. 2022), were not differentially expressed in our dataset. Further experiments will be required to determine whether these receptors and associated signaling components participate in Exl1 perception.

ROS and downstream signaling cascades triggered by Exl1

Following perception of Exl1 activity at the cell surface, the downstream transcriptional patterns observed here suggest rapid activation of defense-related signaling cascades. In our previous work, we experimentally demonstrated that Exl1 treatment induces accumulation of ROS in A. thaliana (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020a). Consistent with this observation, our transcriptomic analysis revealed upregulation of genes associated with ROS production and signaling, including CLH1, which contributes to ROS generation in response to tissue damage caused by Erwinia carotovora (Kariola et al. 2005), PTI12, which is activated by H2O2 during oxidative burst-mediated signaling in A. thaliana (Anthony et al. 2006), and XDH2, which modulates ROS metabolism during defense responses against powdery mildew in A. thaliana (Ma et al. 2016). In contrast, the jar1-1 mutant showed predominant downregulation of ROS-related genes (Fig. 3b). These observations suggest that ROS-associated signaling contributes to early defense responses triggered by Exl1 and is influenced by JA signaling.

In addition to ROS signaling, our transcriptomic analysis revealed transcriptional changes in genes associated with phosphorylation cascades linked to early defense signaling (Fig. 3b). In Col-0 plants, we observed upregulation of genes encoding the Ca2+-dependent kinase 9 (CIPK9) and the mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 3 (MAP3KA), an upstream component of MAPK signaling that activates MPK3/6 and contributes to resistance against bacterial and fungal pathogens (Bi et al. 2018). We also observed induction of Protein tyrosine phosphatase 1 (PTPI), which regulates MAPK activity through dephosphorylation of MPK4/6 and participates in redox regulation (Gupta and Luan 2003). These transcriptional changes suggest that kinase-mediated signaling may be engaged following Exl1 perception. However, experiments with the mpk3 mutant (Fig. S3a) showed that Exl1 treatment still reduced disease incidence, indicating that MPK3 itself is not essential for Exl1-mediated protection. These results suggests that kinase-associated signaling may contribute to the response triggered by Exl1, potentially through redundant MAPK components or parallel kinase pathways.

Interestingly, some of the genes identified here, such as CIPK9 and CAMTA1, are linked to Ca²⁺ signaling pathways (Galon et al. 2010). Nevertheless, our data provide only limited support for the involvement of calcium signaling in the Exl1 response.

JA, SA and ABA drive the Exl1-induced immune response

JA signaling contributes to the Exl1-induced defense response, as previously reported (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b). In the present transcriptomic analysis genes associated with JA biosynthesis and signaling, including ABCD1 and JAL34, were upregulated following Exl1 treatment. JAL34 belongs to the jacalin-like lectin domain family, and its wheat homolog TaJRLL1 has been shown to activate both JA and SA signaling pathways during fungal infection (Xiang et al. 2011), suggesting potential integration between these pathways. Consistent with this idea, SA-related genes were also upregulated at early timepoints (1 hpt, Fig. 3c), suggesting that SA-associated responses are engaged alongside JA during Exl1-triggered immunity. Although genes directly associated with ET pathway were not represented in our dataset, the upregulation of the ET-responsive transcription factor RAP2-1, supports a potential contribution of ET signaling, as previously reported (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b).

In addition to JA and SA associated responses, our data also indicate a contribution of ABA signaling to the Exl1-induced defense response. ABA-responsive genes, including GRDP1 (Rodríguez-Hernández et al. 2014), ABCG40 (Kuromori and Shinozaki 2010), and CRK45, which positively regulates resistance to Pseudomonas syringae in A. thaliana (Zhang et al. 2013), were all upregulated in Col-0 plants (Fig. 3c). Consistent with this pattern, CRK45 expression increased at 6 hpt but not at 1 hpt (Fig. 4). Given that CRK45 is responsive to ABA, this delayed induction suggests that ABA-associated responses may contribute to later stages of the Exl1-triggered response. Supporting this idea, Exl1-treated aba2 mutants lost the protective effect observed in Col-0 plants following pathogen challenge, indicating that ABA signaling is required for the full establishment of Exl1-mediated protection.

In addition, we observed upregulation at later timepoints (6 hpt) of genes associated with auxin signaling, including the auxin efflux transporter ABCB4/PGP4 (Cho et al. 2008), and CAMTA1, which participates in auxin signaling under abiotic stresses conditions (Galon et al. 2010). Together with the tendency toward reduced protection observed in the auxin-impaired mutant following Exl1 treatment (Fig. S3b), these observations suggest that auxin signaling may also be modulated during the Exl1-induced response.

Interestingly, in the jar1-1 mutant we observed induction of genes associated with ABA and gibberellin signaling, particularly at 1 hpt (Fig. 3c), consistent with the altered pattern CRK45 expression pattern detected in this background (Fig. 4b). Together, these observations highlight the importance of JA signaling in orchestrating the Exl1-induced defense response.

Furthermore, the upregulation of defense-related genes associated with biotic and abiotic stress responses (Fig. 3g), including KTI3, VSP2, SINE2, JAL34, ABCG40, CRK45 and CAMTA1 (Liu et al. 2005; Galon et al. 2010; Kuromori and Shinozaki 2010; Xiang et al. 2011; Zhang et al. 2013; Zhou et al. 2014; Arnaiz et al. 2018), together with the downregulation of the stress defense co-repressor LUH (Shrestha et al. 2014), further supports the activation of defense responses in Exl1-treated Col-0 plants.

Protein turnover as part of the Exl1-induced response

Finally, protein turnover is an important component of plant immunity (Sharma et al. 2016). In this context, it is notable that Exl1 treatment was associated with the upregulation of genes involved in protein ubiquitination (Fig. 3d), including ubiquitin-protein ligases previously reported to increase their expression in response to fungal pathogens (Pratelli et al. 2012), along with other components of the UPS. These transcriptional changes suggest that protein turnover may form part of the broader immune response triggered by Exl1 and point to the UPS as a potential regulatory layer that warrants further experimental validation. Interestingly, protein ubiquitination has been shown to regulate components of ABA signaling (Kou et al. 2024; Chung et al. 2025), raising the possibility that the UPS-related transcriptional changes observed here may intersect with ABA-associated responses induced by Exl1.

Figure 5 summarizes the major transcriptomic changes observed between Col-0 and jar1-1 plants in response to Exl1 treatment. In Col-0, Exl1 was associated with increased expression of genes involved in signaling and phytohormone pathways, with JA signaling and ROS responses strongly represented, consistent with previously reported responses (Narváez-Barragán et al. 2020b). Additional components, including ABA and, to a lesser extent, auxin-related pathways, may also contribute to the signaling triggered by Exl1. The induction of multiple genes related to the UPS further suggests a possible role for protein turnover in the regulation of these responses. Moreover, the increased expression of genes associated with transcriptional regulation, protein biosynthesis and biotic defense responses in Exl1-treated Col-0 plants reflects the broad transcriptional reprogramming induced by Exl1. In contrast, jar1-1 plants displayed altered signaling patterns, with several pathways upregulated in Col-0 showing reduced expression in the mutant background. Together, these observations emphasize the importance of JA signaling in shaping the Exl1-induced immunity.

Conclusion

In this study, genome-wide transcriptomic analysis of A. thaliana Col-0 and jar1-1 plants treated with the bacterial expansin Exl1 uncovered the involvement of ABA signaling in the plant response to this protein. Additional pathways potentially associated with this response include the ubiquitin proteosome system (UPS) and transcriptional regulators linked to plant defense. Together, these findings indicate that Exl1 induces a complex and multilayered immune response. The contrasting transcriptomic profiles observed in the jar1-1 mutant further highlight the importance of JA signaling in shaping the response to Exl1. Altogether, this work offers new insights into how expansin activity reprograms plant defenses through coordinated signaling networks and provides a framework for future studies aimed at understanding how bacterial expansins modulate plant immune signaling.
