Enolase 1 suppresses influenza A virus replication by blocking the nuclear import of the viral ribonucleoprotein complex
Authors: Xingtian Kong, Junwen Liu, Qiyun Zhu, Shuai Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfluenza A viruses (IAVs) pose a severe threat to humans and animals, and rely on numerous proviral host proteins to support their replication. Nevertheless, multiple host factors exert restrictive effects on the IAV life cycle through different mechanisms. Enolase 1 (ENO1), a glycolytic enzyme, has been identified to affect viral replication of limited viruses; and its role in the IAV life cycle remains poorly understood. In this study, we found that ENO1 is a negative regulator of IAV replication. Knockdown of ENO1 significantly enhanced IAV propagation, whereas its overexpression dramatically reduced viral growth. Further, ENO1 reduced IAV replication independent of the glycolysis pathway. ENO1 interacted with viral nucleoprotein (NP) and attenuated viral polymerase activity through blocking the nuclear import of the viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) complex. Mechanistically, ENO1 impaired the binding of NP to importin α3, but not to importin α1, α5, or α7, thereby preventing the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and suppressing IAV replication. Taken together, our study uncovered a novel function of ENO1 in IAV replication, and shed new light on understanding virus-host interactions. Our findings indicated that ENO1 may be a promising target for the development of antiviral drugs.Keywords: Influenza A virus, ENO1, NP, Importin α3, vRNP complex

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV) is an ongoing global threat to human and animal health, resulting in substantial economic and social impacts [1]. Four influenza pandemics in 1918 (H1N1), 1957 (H2N2), 1968 (H3N2), and 2009 (H1N1) led to tremendous damage [2]. In addition, highly pathogenic H5 and H7 influenza viruses can infect and kill humans with mortality rates of 49% and 39%, respectively [3–5]. IAVs of several other subtypes, such as H3N8, H9N2, and H10N3 [6–8], continually evolve to cross the species barrier and infect humans, raising concerns about new influenza strains with pandemic potential.

The IAV genome has eight segments of single-stranded, negative-sense RNA and encodes ten essential and at least eight accessory proteins [9]. Each viral RNA (vRNA) segment is packed into viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) complexes composed of multiple nucleoproteins (NPs) and a trimeric RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), which consists of polymerase basic protein 2 (PB2), polymerase basic protein 1 (PB1), and polymerase acidic protein (PA) [10]. The vRNP complex is the minimal structural unit in viral genome replication and transcription, which occurs in the nucleus of infected cells [11]. Upon transcription, the RdRp transcribes vRNA segments into capped and polyadenylated messenger RNAs (mRNAs). During replication, each vRNA segment is copied into positive-sense complementary RNA (cRNA) by viral RdRp, which then serves as a template for the generation of progeny vRNAs. The newly synthesized viral RdRp proteins and NP are imported into the nucleus, where they bind to vRNAs and cRNAs to form vRNPs and complementary ribonucleoproteins (cRNPs), respectively [12].

The nuclear localization signals (NLSs) of the NP are critical for the nuclear import of the vRNP complex. As an adaptor, importin-α can recognize the NLS of NP to mediate the nuclear import of NP and vRNP complex [13]. Additionally, host proteins such as MOV10, PLSCR1, EEF1D, and BinCARD1 play important roles in regulating the nuclear import of the vRNP complex [14–17], thereby affecting the viral life cycle. However, further host factors participating in the nuclear import of the vRNP complex remain to be characterized.

ENO1 is a glycolytic enzyme that catalyses the conversion of 2-phosphoglyceric acid (2-PG) to phosphoenolpyruvic acid (PEP) [18]. Beyond its enzymatic function, ENO1 exhibits diverse roles depending on its subcellular and extracellular localization and is involved in the various diseases, including various cancers, type 2 diabetes, and Alzheimer’s disease [19]. ENO1 has also been reported to be involved in the replication cycle of viruses, such as human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) and enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) [20, 21]. However, it is unknown whether ENO1 participates in the life cycle of IAV.

In the present study, we found that ENO1, a glycolytic enzyme, negatively regulates the propagation of IAV in a glycolysis-independent manner. ENO1 interacts with NP and inhibits the nuclear import of the vRNP complex. Further investigation revealed that ENO1 impairs the binding of NP to importin α3, thereby blocking the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and suppressing IAV replication.

Materials and methods

Facility

All experiments with live viruses were conducted within a biosafety level 2 laboratory in the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute (LVRI) of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS), which is approved for such use by the Chinese Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs. Details regarding the facility as well as the biosafety and biosecurity measures used have been reported previously [22].

Cells and viruses

Human lung carcinoma cells (A549) were cultured in F-12 K medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco). Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T) and Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) containing 10% and 3% FBS, respectively. All media were supplemented with 100 units/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco). All cells were cultured in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator at 37°C.

A/WSN/33 (WSN, H1N1), A/duck/Hunan/4/2018 (HN4, H6N6), and A/chicken/Hunan/38/2018 (HN38, H9N2) viruses were grown in 10-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chicken embryos.

Plasmids

The human ENO1 gene from total cellular mRNAs of A549 cells was amplified by using RT-PCR and cloned into the mammalian expression vector pRK containing a Flag at the N-terminus or pCAGGS bearing a V5 tag at the C-terminus. The open reading frames (ORFs) of PB2, PB1, PA, and NP of PR8 (H1N1) virus were cloned into pCAGGS. The three ENO1 truncation constructs expressing the N-terminal amino acids 1–97, the DNA-binding region spanning residues 98–236, and the C-terminal amino acids 237–435 were generated with a Flag tag at the N-terminus by means of PCR. The generation of four truncated NP plasmids expressing amino acids 1-180, 1-370, 181–370, and 181–498 was described previously [23]. Mutations were introduced into the ENO1 gene by using site-directed mutagenesis (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All plasmids were confirmed by sequencing.

Antibodies and reagents

The following primary antibodies were obtained from commercial sources: mouse anti-Flag mAb (A00187 -100, GenScript), rabbit anti-Flag pAb (20543-1-AP, Proteintech) mouse anti-HA mAb (A01244-100, GenScript), rabbit anti-HA pAb (51064-2-AP, Proteintech), mouse anti-V5 mAb (A01724-100, GenScript), rabbit anti-V5 pAb (14440-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-GAPDH pAb (10494-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-ENO1 pAb (11204-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-LaminA/C pAb (10298-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-KPNA4 pAb (12463-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-PB2 pAb (GTX125926, GeneTex), rabbit anti-PB1 pAb (GTX125923, GeneTex), rabbit anti-PA pAb (GTX118991, GeneTex), rabbit anti-NP pAb (GTX125989, GeneTex). Mouse anti-NP mAb was produced and stored in our laboratory. The secondary antibodies used for western blotting were goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (M21001S) and goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (M21002S), purchased from Abmart. The secondary antibodies used for confocal microscopy were Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) and Alexa Fluor Cy3 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), purchased from Invitrogen (USA). Lipofectamine LTX and Plus, Lipofectamine RNAiMAX, and NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents were obtained from Invitrogen (USA).

Mass spectrometry

Mass spectrometry was performed as previously described with little modifications [24]. The gene encoding NP was cloned into pDONOR201 vector and then recombined into lentiviral vector for the construction of SFB-tagged NP. For affinity purification, HEK293T cells stabling expressing SFB-NP were lysed with NETN lysis buffer (Saint-bio) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 20 min on ice. After centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4℃, the supernatants were mixed with streptavidin-conjugated beads for 1 h at 4℃. The beads were then washed three times with NETN buffer, and bounded proteins were eluted with NETN buffer containing 2 mg/ml biotin for 90 min at 4℃. S-protein beads were then added, and the mixture were incubated on ice for 1 h. After being washed three times with NETN buffer, the beads were then subjected to SDS-PAGE. Protein bands were excised and digested, and the peptides were detected by mass spectrometry.

SiRNA transfection and virus infection

A549 cells were transfected with siRNA targeting ENO1 (si-ENO1) or scrambled siRNA (NC) (Genepharma, Shanghai, China) at a concentration of 30 nmol by using the Lipofectamine RNAi MAX. At 36 h post-transfection, the knockdown efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR or Western blotting. Subsequently, A549 cells treated with siRNA were infected with H1N1 (MOI = 0.1), HN4 (H6N6) (MOI = 0.1), or HN38 (H9N2) (MOI = 0.1) virus. At 24 and 48 h post-infection, the supernatants were collected and virus titers were determined by plaque assays or TCID50 assays on MDCK cells.

Plaque assay

Plaque assays were performed with MDCK cells as described previously [25]. Briefly, MDCK cells grown in 12-well plates were infected with 10-fold serial dilutions of virus samples at 37°C for 1 h. The cells were then washed with 1 × MEM and overlaid with 1% Sea Plaque agarose (Lonza) in 1 × MEM including 0.3% BSA and 0.5 µg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin. After a 48-h incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA, and the plaques were stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The number of plaques was counted.

TCID50 assay

Briefly, MDCK cells grown in 96-well plates were infected with 10-fold serial dilutions of virus samples at 37°C for 1 h. The cells were subsequently washed with PBS three times and incubated for 48 h at 37°C. The 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) was calculated by using the method of Reed & Muench.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

The total RNA in cells was extracted by using TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the cDNA was synthesized by using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega). RT-qPCR assays were performed by using ChamQ Universal SYBR RT-qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) and Roche LightCycler 480 real-time PCR system. Relative RNA quantities were determined by using the 2‒∆∆CT method as described previously [26], with GAPDH serving as the internal control.

Western blotting

Proteins separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (BioRad). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS at room temperature for 1.5 h, and subsequently incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4°C. After a 1-h incubation with secondary antibody at room temperature, the blots were visualized with an ECL system (GE Healthcare). The intensities of the target bands were quantified by using the ImageJ (NIH, USA).

Co-immunoprecipitation

HEK293T cells transfected with the indicated plasmids were harvested and lysed with NP-40 Lysis Buffer (Beyotime) containing complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime) and PMSF (Beyotime) on ice at 24 h post-transfection. Cell lysates were centrifuged (12,000 rpm) for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were incubated with the respective antibodies, and gently rocked at 4°C for 6 h. Protein A/G-Agarose (Abmart) was then added, and the mixture was rocked at 4°C for a further 6 h. Subsequently, beads were washed with PBS containing 1% PMSF, and bound proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting.

Confocal microscopy assay

Confocal microscopy assay was performed as described previously [27]. A549 cells grown in glass-bottom dishes were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min at room temperature. The fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h. Then, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, and washed three times with PBS. The cells were subsequently incubated with the secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After three washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min to stain the nuclei. Images were observed using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

GST pull-down

GST pull-down assay was performed as described previously [27]. HEK293T cells grown in 6-well plates were transfected with Flag-NP plasmid using Lipofectamine LTX and Plus reagents (Invitrogen). At 24 h post-transfection, the cells were lysed with NP-40 Lysis Buffer containing a complete protease inhibitor cocktail and PMSF on ice. The lysates were mixed with glutathione magnetic agarose (Invitrogen) for 6 h at 4℃. Then, the purified GST or GST-tagged ENO1 were added, and the mixture was rocked for 6 h at 4°C. The bound proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting.

Separation of nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions

A549 cells treated with si-ENO1 or Flag-ENO1 were infected with H1N1 virus (MOI = 5). At 3 h post-infection, the cells were separated into the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions by using NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents. The amount of NP, LaminA/C, and GAPDH (nuclear and cytoplasmic fraction markers) in each fraction were assessed by western blotting with rabbit anti-NP pAb, rabbit anti-LaminA/C pAb, and rabbit anti GAPDH pAb, respectively.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed as previously described [28]. HEK293T cells treated with si-ENO1 or overexpressing ENO1 were transfected with plasmids expressing the vRNP complex proteins (PB2, PB1, PA, and NP) of PR8 (H1N1) virus, pPol I-Luc, and an internal control pRL-TK by using Lipofectamine LTX and Plus reagents. At 24 h post-transfection, cell lysates were prepared with a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega), and the luciferase activities were measured with a GloMax 96 microplate luminometer (Promega).

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was performed by Student’s two tailed unpaired t-test or ANOVA (analysis of variance) with GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Prism 8). P values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

ENO1 negatively regulates IAV replication

NP is an important component of the vRNP complex and plays an essential role in the RNA transcription and replication of IAV. To identify host factors that participate in the life cycle of IAV by interacting with NP, we performed affinity-purified mass spectrometry and identified a list of host factors that interacted with NP (Data not shown). Using a reporter virus (PR8-Nluc) and an RNA interference library, we identified ENO1 (Number 36) as a potential host factor associated with NP for the replication of IAV (Fig. 1A). Using small interfering RNA (siRNA) to knockdown the expression of ENO1, we found that the viral titers of all three IAV strains in si-ENO1-treated cells were significantly increased compared with those of NC-treated cells at 24 and 48 h post-infection (p.i.) (Fig. 1B-E). We also explored whether ENO1 upregulation has an effect on viral growth. As shown in Fig. 1F, Flag-tagged ENO1 was robustly expressed in transfected A549 cells, and the virus titers in A549 cells overexpressing ENO1 were remarkably lower than those in the control cells at 24 and 48 h p.i. (Fig. 1G-I). These results indicated that ENO1 negatively regulates influenza virus replication.

ENO1 is a glycolytic enzyme and its catalytic activity is mediated by K343 and K394, respectively [29]. To determine whether ENO1 relies on glycolysis in the regulation of IAV replication, we generated two ENO1 mutants, Flag-ENO1-K343E and Flag-ENO1-K394E, and evaluated their effects on IAV growth. The growth titers in A549 cells overexpressing each mutant were similar to those of cells overexpressing wild-type ENO1 at 24 and 48 h p.i. (Fig. 1J), demonstrating that ENO1 attenuates IAV replication in a glycolysis-independent manner.

Taken together, these results suggested that ENO1 is a negative modulator of IAV propagation.

ENO1 interacts with the NP of IAV

ENO1 was identified as a potential binding protein of IAV NP, we next explored whether ENO1 associates with NP. HA-tagged NP co-immunoprecipitated with Flag-tagged ENO1 when the two were co-expressed (Fig. 2A). A reverse co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay further verified this result (Fig. 2B). An additional co-IP assay revealed that ENO1 binds to NP in infected cells (Fig. 2C). A glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay indicated that ENO1 directly interacts with NP in vitro (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, confocal microscopy confirmed that viral NP and ENO1 clearly colocalizes in the nucleus and cytoplasm of A549 cells (Fig. 2E). These results demonstrate that ENO1 directly interacts with NP.

Next, we determined the region in ENO1 that is responsible for the interaction with NP. We performed a co-IP assay after generating three ENO1 truncation constructs expressing the N-terminal amino acids 1–97, the DNA-binding region spanning residues 98–236, and the C-terminal amino acids 237–435, based on the structure of ENO1. The co-IP assay revealed that the C-terminal 237–435 amino acid region of ENO1 is essential for the binding of ENO1 to viral NP (Fig. 2F). To define the specific region in NP that binds to ENO1, we generated four truncated NP plasmids expressing amino acids 1-180, 1-370, 181–370, and 181–498, as previously described [23]. The co-IP experiment confirmed that each domain of NP interacts with ENO1 (Fig. 2G).

ENO1 blocks viral polymerase activity

NP is the most abundant viral protein in IAV infected cells and is essential for the replication of IAV genome [30, 31]. Given that ENO1 interacts with NP and has an inhibitory effect on IAV propagation, we hypothesized that it might influence viral polymerase activity. A minigenome assay indicated that viral polymerase activity was significantly higher in si-ENO1 treated cells than that of the control cells (Fig. 3A). Western blotting analysis showed that the expression levels of the vRNP complex proteins were unchanged in siRNA-treated cells, indicating that knockdown of ENO1 enhanced the polymerase activity without changing the expression of the vRNP proteins of IAV (Fig. 3A). To confirm this result, we also examined the effect of overexpression of ENO1 on viral polymerase activity. As shown in Fig. 3B, ENO1 overexpression resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in viral polymerase activity, but no difference was observed in the expression of vRNP complex proteins. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the levels of vRNA, cRNA, and mRNA were remarkably higher in A549 cells treated with si-ENO1 than those in NC cells (Fig. 3C), indicating that viral replication and transcription were promoted by knockdown of ENO1 expression. In contrast, the levels of all three kinds of viral RNA were significantly reduced in A549 cells overexpressing ENO1 compared with those in the control cells (Fig. 3D), demonstrating that overexpression of ENO1 inhibited viral RNA replication and transcription. Taken together, these results suggested that ENO1 indeed affects the replication and transcription of the IAV genome.

ENO1 prevents the nuclear import of the vRNP complex

NP plays an essential role in the viral transcription and replication of IAV, and the self-oligomerization is the necessary step for the binding of NP to viral RNA [32, 33]. Since ENO1 was found to interact with NP and inhibit viral polymerase activity, we next investigated whether ENO1 plays a role in NP oligomerization. A co-IP assay showed that the amount of Flag-NP bound to HA-NP was equal regardless of ENO1 overexpression or not (Fig. 4A), implying that ENO1 has no effect on NP oligomerization. The vRNP complex is the functional unit for the transcription and replication of IAV, and the vRNP assembly in which NP participates plays an essential role in the life cycle of IAV [34, 35]. The results indicated that HA-NP coprecipitated PB2, PB1, and PA equally with or without ENO1 (Fig. 4B), demonstrating that ENO1 does not affect the vRNP assembly.

Following host cell entry and endosomal escape, the IAV vRNPs were released into the cytoplasm and transported into the nucleus by the interaction between the NP and IMPα family proteins [36]. We speculated that ENO1 might participate in this process. As shown in Fig. 4C, the amount of viral NP in the nucleus of A549 cells treated with si-ENO1 was much higher than that of the control cells treated with NC, suggesting that the nuclear import of the vRNP complex was enhanced when ENO1 expression was silenced. In contrast, the amount of NP in the nucleus of A549 cells overexpressing ENO1 was significantly decreased compared with that of the control cells, demonstrating that the nuclear import of the vRNP complex was inhibited when ENO1 was overexpressed (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, confocal microscopy revealed that ENO1 overexpression markedly suppressed the nuclear import of NP (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these results indicated that ENO1 inhibits the nuclear import of the vRNP complex during IAV infection.

ENO1 impairs NP binding to importin α3

Previous studies have shown that importin α family members, including importin α1, α3, α5, and α7, are essential for the nuclear import of vRNP complex [14, 37]. Given that ENO1 binds to NP and inhibits the nuclear import of the vRNP complex, we investigated whether it affects the interaction between NP and different isoforms of importin α. Co-IP assays revealed that ENO1 overexpression markedly reduced the amount of NP immunoprecipitated with importin α3 (Fig. 5C, D). However, ENO1 had no effect on the binding of importin α1, α5, or α7 to NP (Fig. 5A, B, E, F, G, H), suggesting that ENO1 impaires the interaction between NP and importin α3. These results demonstrated that ENO1 inhibits the nuclear import of NP by interfering with the complex formation between NP and importin α3.

To ascertain the role of importin α3 in the effect of ENO1 on IAV growth, we evaluated the impact of ENO1 overexpression on viral replication in A549 cells when the expression of importin α3 was downregulated. Western blotting analysis showed that importin α3 expression is significantly reduced in A549 cells treated with siRNA targeting importin α3 (si-Importin α3) compared with those treated with NC (Fig. 5I). As shown in Fig. 5J, knockdown of importin α3 reversed the inhibitory effect of ENO1 overexpression on the replication of the H1N1 virus at 24 and 48 h p.i., indicating that importin α3 is critical for the inhibitory role of ENO1 in the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and IAV replication. Taken together, these findings demonstrated that ENO1 disrupts the interaction between NP and importin α3, thereby impairing the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and IAV infection.

Discussion

In this study, we found that the glycolytic enzyme ENO1 negatively regulates the replication of IAV independent on the glycolysis pathway. Our results lead us to propose a model in which ENO1 blocks the binding of NP to importin α3, thereby preventing the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and suppressing IAV replication (Fig. 6).

Schematic model showing the role of ENO1 in the IAV life cycle. ENO1 interacts with NP and impairs the binding of NP to importin α3, thereby preventing the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and inhibiting IAV replication

As a primary pathway of metabolism, glycolysis is essential for energy metabolism and biosynthesis. Miyake et al. demonstrated that glycolytic enzyme pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) plays a central role in regulating the replication of IAV through interacting with PA and promoting IAV replication [38]. Mammals possess three enolase isoforms: α-enolase (ENO1), β-enolase (ENO3), and γ-enolase (ENO2). ENO1 is widely expressed across various tissues, whereas ENO2 is predominantly found in neuronal cells and ENO3 is mainly localized in muscle tissue. ENO1 exerts essential roles in diverse biological processes. In the cytoplasm, ENO1 participates in glycolysis, supports mitochondrial membrane stability, modulates signaling pathways, and contributes to cytoskeletal reorganization. On the cell surface, ENO1 acts as a plasminogen receptor and facilitates its conversion to plasmin. In addition, overexpression of ENO1 suppresses HIV-1 replication [20], whereas ENO1 knockdown reduces EV-A71 replication [21]. ENO1 is a glycolytic enzyme that catalyses the conversion of 2-phosphoglyceric acid (2-PG) to phosphoenolpyruvic acid (PEP) [18]. Beyond its enzymatic function, ENO1 exhibits diverse roles depending on its subcellular and extracellular localization and is involved in the various diseases. ENO1 has also been reported to be involved in the replication cycle of viruses, such as human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) and enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) [20, 21]. However, it is unknown whether ENO1 participates in the life cycle of IAV. Our findings revealed that ENO1 interacts with NP and impairs the binding of NP to importin α3, thereby inhibiting the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and hindering the replication of IAV. One limitation of the present study is that ENO1 is known to be involved in the regulation of cell growth through ferroptosis [39], and we did not explore whether ENO1 regulates IAV replication through ferroptosis and the regulatory mechanisms of ferroptosis in IAV life cycle.

Because the number of currently available anti-influenza drugs is limited and viral drug resistance frequently occurs, novel antiviral drugs targeting host factors remain to be explored. IAV relies on the host cell machinery to fulfill its life cycle. Therefore, host factors are ideal targets for the development of antiviral drugs [40, 41]. Previous studies have identified small-molecule inhibitors via host-virus interactions. Serine and arginine-rich splicing factor 5 (SRSF5) is an activator of M mRNA splicing and its inhibitor anidulafungin efficiently blocks IAV replication [42]. Protein disulfide isomerase A3 (PDIA3) interacts with hemagglutinin (HA) protein and LOC14 as a PDI inhibitor significantly suppresses the replication of IAV [43]. We found that ENO1 has an inhibitory effect on IAV replication, the drug analogs or agonists of ENO1 have the potential to be developed as an anti-influenza drug.

NP is a multifunctional protein that is crucial for the replication and transcription of the influenza virus genome [44, 45]. Polo-like kinase 3 (PLK3) enhances NP phosphorylation and oligomerization, thereby facilitating viral replication [46]. Tripartite motif-containing 41 (TRIM41) targets NP for ubiquitination and promotes NP proteasomal degradation, thus suppressing the replication of IAV [47]. Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein AB (hnRNPAB) interacts with NP and limits the nuclear export of viral mRNA, thus inhibiting IAV replication [48]. Our results demonstrate that ENO1 impairs viral replication by blocking the binding of NP to importin α3. These findings indicate that host factors interacting with NP affect IAV replication via different mechanisms.

The vRNP complex plays a critical role in the IAV life cycle and numerous studies have identified host cellular factors that affect its function [49]. Ras-related nuclear protein (RAN) attenuates IAV replication by constraining the nuclear export of the vRNP complex [50]. The cell growth-regulating nucleolar protein LYAR positively regulates the replication of IAV by facilitating the vRNP assembly [51]. BCL2-associated athanogene 6 (BAG6) restricts IAV replication via inhibiting the activity of vRNP [52]. Similarly, we here found that ENO1 negatively regulates the replication of IAV by decreasing viral polymerase activity and impairing the nuclear import of the vRNP complex without affecting its assembly.

In summary, we found that ENO1 negatively regulates the replication of IAV by interacting with NP and inhibiting the nuclear import of the vRNP complex. Furthermore, ENO1 blocks the binding of NP to importin α3, thereby preventing the nuclear import of the vRNP complex. Our findings reveal a novel function of ENO1 in regulating the replication of IAV and emphasize the importance of ENO1 as a potential target for antiviral drug development.
