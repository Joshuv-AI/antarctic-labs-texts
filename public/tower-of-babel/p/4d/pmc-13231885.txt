Plasma Melanin-Concentrating Hormone in Childhood Obesity and Its Association with Metabolic Parameters: A Clinical Observational Study
Authors: Imre Leiter, Beáta Lelesz, Csaba Hegedűs, József Németh, Beáta Pelles-Taskó, Gyula Ujlaki, Harjit Pal Bhattoa, Enikő Noémi Felszeghy, Szabolcs Lengyel, Dénes Páll, György Balla, Zoltán Szilvássy, Béla Juhász, Rita Kiss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionObesity is a global public health problem associated with complex neuroendocrine dysregulation. Melanin-concentrating hormone (MCH), a hypothalamic regulator of energy balance, promotes appetite and positive energy balance and has been linked to metabolic disturbances in adults. However, its clinical relevance in childhood obesity, where neuroendocrine alterations may precede overt metabolic complications, remains insufficiently characterized.AimThis study aimed to determine whether plasma MCH levels are associated with metabolic alterations in children and adolescents with obesity.MethodsIn this clinical observational study, 283 participants were classified according to World Health Organization BMI-for-age criteria into obese and normal-weight groups. Plasma MCH concentrations were measured using a validated high-sensitivity radioimmunoassay (analytical detection limit: 0.2 fmol/mL). Anthropometric parameters, glucose–insulin homeostasis markers (glucose, insulin, C-peptide, HOMA-IR), lipid profile, thyroid hormones, and liver enzymes were assessed. Associations were analyzed using Spearman correlation after non-parametric distribution testing.ResultsPlasma MCH exhibited limited and selective associations with metabolic parameters in obese children and adolescents. Modest correlations were observed within the glucose–insulin and thyroid-related domains, whereas associations with lipid profile measures were weak or inverse. These patterns suggest age-dependent neuroendocrine involvement in pediatric obesity.ConclusionElevated plasma MCH levels in childhood obesity are associated with selective neuroendocrine and metabolic alterations rather than overt dyslipidemia. In view of the cross-sectional design and modest effect sizes, these findings should be considered exploratory and require confirmation in longitudinal pediatric studies.

Introduction

Obesity

Obesity, defined as excessive accumulation of body fat, is a chronic condition associated with increased cardiometabolic risk and reduced life expectancy. The severity of obesity is often determined using the Body Mass Index (BMI), calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by height squared (m2).1 According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification for adults, BMI categories are as follows: underweight <18.5; normal weight 18.5–24.9; overweight 25–29.9; class I obesity 30–34.9; class II obesity 35–39.9; and class III (severe) obesity ≥40.

Both low BMI (reflecting undernutrition) and high BMI (reflecting overweight or obesity) are associated with increased morbidity and mortality, while normal or slightly overweight ranges are linked to lower risk.2–5 Excess adiposity is strongly associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, certain malignancies, and endocrine dysfunction. Large epidemiological analyses demonstrate a graded association between BMI and all-cause mortality, primarily driven by cardiovascular complications.6 According to estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Obesity Collaborators, obesity affects more than 2.11 billion adults worldwide and elevated BMI is responsible for approximately 4 million deaths annually.

While adult obesity represents a major global burden, increasing attention has focused on the rising prevalence of childhood obesity, and its long-term metabolic implications.7 The prevalence of obesity has risen dramatically worldwide. In Hungary, 23.9% of adults are classified as obese, ranking the country among the most affected within the European Union. Pediatric overweight and obesity are also highly prevalent: among children aged 7–9 years, approximately 25% are overweight or obese, consistent with the European regional estimates.8–10 Childhood obesity alone affects about 4% of Hungarian children, compared with a European average of approximately 10%.8 Although Hungary’s obesity prevalence in children remains slightly below the EU average, the high overall burden of pediatric adiposity represents a substantial public health concern.

In children and adolescents, overweight and obesity are defined using BMI percentiles adjusted for age and sex. Obesity is typically defined as a BMI at or above the 95th percentile, while overweight is defined between the 85th and 95th percentiles.11 It reflects excessive adipose tissue accumulation resulting from sustained positive energy balance and is characterized by increases in both adipocyte size and number. Although BMI remains a practical screening tool, it does not directly quantify fat distribution. Central adiposity, that is commonly assessed by waist circumference or waist-to-hip ratio has correlates more strongly with cardiometabolic risk than BMI alone.12–16 Increased abdominal fat accumulation is closely associated with early metabolic complications, including insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and hypertension.17–19 Therefore, complementary anthropometric and metabolic assessments are essential when evaluating obesity-related health risks in pediatric populations.

The clinical significance of childhood obesity extends beyond immediate metabolic risk, as early adiposity strongly predicts persistence of obesity into adulthood and is associated with intergenerational transmission of obesity-related conditions, including diabetes and cardiovascular disease.20 Notably, children who are overweight at age five are approximately four times more likely to remain overweight later in life compared with their normal-weight peers.21 This transmission may occur through multiple mechanisms, including genetic predisposition, shared environmental and behavioral patterns, maternal metabolic influences during pregnancy, and socioeconomic determinants that shape long-term lifestyle habits.22,23

These considerations emphasize the importance of investigating central regulators of energy balance, such as melanin-concentrating hormone, in the context of pediatric obesity.

Melanin-Concentrating Hormone (MCH)

MCH was first described in the pituitary gland of fish in 1983 and later identified in the hypothalamus of mammals in 1989.24,25 MCH is primarily expressed in the lateral hypothalamus and zona incerta, key regions involved in the regulation of appetite and energy balance.26 Beyond its central localization, MCH and its receptors have also been detected in peripheral tissues, including pancreatic islets, adipose tissue, and the gastrointestinal tract, suggesting potential systemic metabolic interactions.27 MCH participates in the regulation of sleep, mood, and reproduction,28,29 but its most prominent role is in energy homeostasis: it is an orexigenic, anabolic neuropeptide that promotes a positive energy balance.30 Central administration of MCH increases food intake and promotes lipid accumulation and fat storage in both white adipose tissue and the liver.31 Overexpression of MCH induces obesity by reducing energy expenditure and locomotor activity.32 Through its interactions with the endocrine system, MCH also influences glucose homeostasis by stimulating appetite, reducing thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue, and increasing fat storage.33 Recent studies show that prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) enhances MCH neuronal activity, which in turn increases food intake and contributes to obesity, especially under high-fat diet conditions.34 In contrast, ablation of MCH neurons increases energy expenditure and reduces fat depots through activation of brown adipose tissue.35 Pharmacological inhibition of the MCH receptor (MCHR1) has been shown to reduce body weight and food intake, highlighting the therapeutic relevance of this pathway.

Although MCH is primarily synthesized in hypothalamic neurons, several studies have demonstrated that it is detectable in peripheral circulation.36 MCH-expressing neurons project widely within central regulatory networks and interact with neuroendocrine axes regulating appetite, energy expenditure, and glucose homeostasis. In addition, MCH receptors are expressed in peripheral tissues, including pancreatic islets and adipose tissue, supporting a functional link between central MCH signaling and systemic metabolic regulation. While plasma MCH does not directly quantify synaptic hypothalamic signaling, it likely reflects integrated neuroendocrine output linked to metabolic state and may therefore serve as a clinically accessible biomarker of central metabolic regulation.

In adult populations, fasting plasma MCH concentrations have generally been reported within the low picogram/mL range, although reported values vary depending on assay methodology and physiological conditions. However, well-established normative reference ranges for circulating MCH in pediatric populations are currently lacking, and direct comparisons between adult and childhood levels remain limited. The limited availability of age-specific reference data highlights the need for further investigation of circulating MCH in childhood obesity and supports the relevance of the current analysis.

Elevated MCH activity has been implicated in adult obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, and endocrine disturbances. In contrast, clinical data in pediatric populations are scarce, and the relationship between circulating MCH concentrations and metabolic parameters in childhood obesity remains insufficiently characterized. In particular, it is unclear whether MCH-related alterations in children are preferentially associated with insulin resistance, lipid abnormalities, or broader endocrine adaptations.

Accordingly, the present study was designed to evaluate plasma MCH concentrations in a well-characterized pediatric cohort and to examine their associations with anthropometric and metabolic parameters. We hypothesized that childhood obesity would be characterized by elevated plasma MCH levels and that increasing MCH concentrations would show positive associations with markers of insulin resistance (insulin, C-peptide, HOMA-IR), while exhibiting weaker or absent associations with lipid profile parameters. Liver enzyme measurements were included to explore potential links between MCH and early hepatic metabolic involvement, and thyroid function parameters were assessed to evaluate possible interactions with endocrine regulation of energy expenditure. Through this approach, we aimed to clarify whether circulating MCH in pediatric obesity aligns primarily with glucose-insulin dysregulation or extends to broader metabolic and endocrine domains.

Materials and Methods

Study Population and Group Classification

Participants enrolled in the study were initially classified according to World Health Organization (WHO) BMI-for-age criteria. The control group consisted of normal-weight children, while the patient group included children classified as obese. The term “main study cohort” refers to the combined dataset used for overall correlation analyses.

Participants were consecutively recruited from the Department of Pediatric Endocrinology and Diabetology, Outpatient Unit of University of Debrecen Clinical Centre. Eligible patients were children and adolescents aged 5–18 years who presented either for primary diagnostic evaluation or scheduled follow-up examination during the study period. Enrollment was based on voluntary participation.

Prior to inclusion, detailed written information about the study was provided to the parents or legal guardians, and written informed consent was obtained. Age-appropriate assent was obtained from participating children whenever applicable. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Medical Research Council – Scientific and Research Ethics Committee (ETT TUKEB; approval number: 24858–5/2018/EKU) and by the Ministry of Human Capacities, Office of the Deputy State Secretary for the Chief Medical Officer, Department of Health Administration (approval number: 13180–10/2018/EÜIG).

Inclusion criteria comprised availability of complete anthropometric and biochemical datasets and absence of acute illness at the time of sampling.

Exclusion criteria included incomplete laboratory data (eg, missing thyroid function parameters), known endocrine disorders unrelated to obesity, chronic systemic diseases, and use of medications influencing metabolic or hormonal status. Following data cleaning procedures, outliers were identified using Z-scored MCH values, and values exceeding 2 standard deviations were excluded from further analysis.

Sample Collection and Storage

Blood sampling was performed exclusively within the framework of routine clinical laboratory evaluation indicated for diagnostic or follow-up purposes. Blood samples were collected by venipuncture in accordance with the approved ethical protocol and predefined sampling volumes, consisting of one serum separator tube for routine biochemical analyses and one EDTA-anticoagulated hematologic tube per participant for RIA determinations.

Following the fasting blood draw, serum samples were transported to the Laboratory Medicine Department for routine biochemical measurements, while the EDTA samples were transported on ice to the isotope laboratory for plasma processing and subsequent MCH determination under frozen conditions.

Serum samples were allowed to clot and subsequently centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 15 minutes prior to biochemical analysis. EDTA tubes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm at 4°C for 10 minutes, after which plasma was separated, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until isotope-based measurements.

Radioimmunoassay (RIA)

The following RIA kits I-125 kits were used: T3 [I-125] RIA kit (REF: RK-609CT), Insulin [I-125] IRMA kit (REF: RK-400CT), C-peptide [I-125] IRMA kit (REF: RK-84CT), (Izotóp Intézet Kft., Budapest, Hungary).

Plasma concentrations of MCH and VIP were determined in our isotope laboratory using radioimmunoassay (RIA) methods that were specifically developed and validated within our laboratory, offering high sensitivity with an analytical detection limit of 0.2 fmol/mL for both parameters, and allowing for precise and reliable quantification of these peptides. Gamma radioactivity was measured using an NZ310 gamma counter (Gamma, Budapest, Hungary), and MCH and VIP concentrations were determined from the calibration curve.

Serum Biochemical Analysis

Serum biochemical analyses were performed to assess metabolic and renal function, including glucose (Roche Glucose HK Gen.3), urea (Roche Urea), and creatinine (Roche Creatinine plus ver.2). Hepatic function was evaluated using the following enzymatic assays: aspartate aminotransferase (AST; Roche Aspartate Aminotransferase acc. to IFCC without pyridoxal phosphate activation), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; Roche Lactate Dehydrogenase acc. to IFCC ver.2), alanine aminotransferase (ALT; Roche Alanine Aminotransferase acc. to IFCC without pyridoxal phosphate activation), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT; Roche γ-Glutamyltransferase ver.2), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP; Roche Alkaline Phosphatase acc. to IFCC Gen.2).

Lipid metabolism was assessed using triglycerides (Roche Triglycerides), total cholesterol (Roche Cholesterol Gen.2), HDL-cholesterol (Roche HDL-Cholesterol Gen.4), LDL-cholesterol (Roche LDL-Cholesterol Gen.3), apolipoprotein A-I (Roche Tina-quant Apolipoprotein A-I ver.2), apolipoprotein B (Roche Apolipoprotein B ver.2), and lipoprotein(a) (Roche Tina-quant Lipoprotein(a) Gen.2).

Thyroid function was evaluated by measuring serum TSH (Roche Elecsys TSH) and free thyroxine (fT4; Roche Elecsys FT4 IV).

All measurements were carried out on a Roche Cobas analyzer platform.

Assessment of Insulin-Sensitivity

Insulin resistance was estimated using the widely accepted Homeostatic Model Assessment (HOMA-IR), widely applied in pediatric metabolic research. HOMA-IR is calculated from fasting glucose and insulin concentrations. Fasting glucose was measured as part of the routine biochemical assessment, whereas insulin concentrations were determined separately using a commercial IRMA kit.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of patients started with excluding patients who had incomplete datasets (eg.: missing fT4 value). After the initial filtering, the complete control and patient dataset was further refined by removing outliers with Z-scoring MCH values; MCH values over 2 SD were discarded. The statistical evaluation of the filtered dataset commenced with an assessment of the underlying distributional characteristics of the variables.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by repeating correlation matrices with and without the predefined outlier values in the patient, control, and pooled cohorts. The overall association patterns and interpretative conclusions remained unchanged, confirming the robustness of the findings.

Normality was evaluated using both the Shapiro–Wilk normality test and the D’Agostino & Pearson omnibus normality test within GraphPad Prism 9.1.2. As the majority of variables deviated from Gaussian distribution, non-parametric methods were applied. Subsequent association analyses were performed using a Spearman correlation matrix in GraphPad Prism 9.1.2 to evaluate associations between plasma MCH concentrations and clinical and metabolic parameters, including anthropometric variables (age, sex, BMI) and biochemical markers.

Of the initial 305 participants, 283 remained after data-cleaning procedures. Based on World Health Organization (WHO) BMI-for-age criteria, 46 individuals were classified as normal-weight and assigned to the Control group, while 237 were classified as obese and assigned to the Patient group. The term Main study cohort refers to the combined dataset of all included participants used for overall correlation analyses. Spearman correlation matrix analyses were performed separately for the Control group, the Patient group, and the Main study cohort.

To evaluate statistical sensitivity, a post hoc achieved power analysis was performed using G*Power (exact test: correlation, two-tailed; α = 0.005; assumed effect size r = 0.30). The achieved power was 0.991 for the main cohort (n = 283) and 0.973 for the patient group (n = 237), indicating adequate sensitivity to detect moderate associations. The smaller control group (n = 46) yielded lower statistical power under identical assumptions; therefore, subgroup-specific findings in this cohort are interpreted cautiously.

Mean age values reflected appropriate developmental stratification across groups. In the main study cohort, prepubertal participants had a mean age of 10.60 ± 0.15 years, while pubertal participants had a mean age of 15.49 ± 0.11 years (overall: 13.13 ± 0.17 years). Comparable distributions were observed in the control group (prepubertal: 10.47 ± 0.34 years; pubertal: 15.14 ± 0.26 years; overall: 12.80 ± 0.41 years) and in the patient group (prepubertal: 10.63 ± 0.17 years; pubertal: 15.56 ± 0.12 years; overall: 13.19 ± 0.19 years).

No statistically significant age differences were detected between control and patient groups within corresponding developmental categories (p = 0.3077), nor between the patient group and the main study cohort (p = 0.7610), confirming appropriate age balance across the analyzed populations.

Body mass index (BMI) values demonstrated clear distinctions between groups. In the main study cohort, BMI was markedly elevated both in prepubertal (26.28 ± 0.68 kg/m2) and pubertal participants (30.86 ± 0.84 kg/m2), with an overall mean BMI of 28.64 ± 0.56 kg/m2. As expected, the control group exhibited substantially lower BMI values (prepubertal: 17.44 ± 0.92 kg/m2; pubertal: 22.11 ± 1.34 kg/m2; overall: 19.78 ± 0.87 kg/m2), whereas the patient group showed consistently higher BMI values (prepubertal: 28.06 ± 0.69 kg/m2; pubertal: 32.49 ± 0.89 kg/m2; overall: 30.36 ± 0.59 kg/m2).

BMI was significantly higher in the patient group compared with controls (p < 0.0001). Although BMI values in the patient group were numerically higher than in the pooled main study cohort, the difference was modest but statistically significant (p = 0.0303), reflecting the inclusion of normal-weight individuals in the combined dataset. In all patient subgroups, pubertal participants displayed higher BMI values than their prepubertal counterparts, with only minor sex-related differences.

The clear separation of BMI values between control and patient groups (p < 0.0001), together with the absence of significant age differences between cohorts, confirms appropriate anthropometric stratification of the study population according to WHO criteria. These anthropometric findings confirm that the study population represents a well-characterized obese pediatric cohort.

From a methodological perspective, it should be noted that the inclusion of children in clinical investigations represents an inherent methodological and ethical challenge, as venipuncture is performed in pediatric populations only when clinically justified; therefore, blood sample collection and study participation were restricted to cases in which blood sampling was already indicated as part of routine clinical care. Accordingly, the nearly 290-patient population enrolled represent an adequate sample size for the purposes of the present research work, and provides a solid basis for a comprehensive evaluation of the predefined study criteria.

To our knowledge, the integrated correlation structure linking circulating MCH with metabolic and endocrine parameters in a pediatric population has not been systematically characterized previously, and thus provides novel descriptive insight into the interaction network of juvenile obesity. Across all datasets, anthropometric and metabolic indices formed a coherent cluster. In the pooled cohort, BMI showed strong positive correlations with insulin (r = 0.617, p < 0.0001), C-peptide (r = 0.662, p < 0.0001), and HOMA-IR (r = 0.594, p < 0.0001). Similar correlations were observed in the patient group, confirming a robust insulin resistance phenotype closely linked to adiposity.

In contrast, correlations between MCH and insulin resistance markers were weak. In the patient cohort, MCH demonstrated a minimal positive correlation with BMI (r = 0.128, p = 0.049), while correlations with insulin (r = 0.029, p = 0.657), C-peptide (r = 0.026, p = 0.689), and HOMA-IR (r = 0.017, p = 0.797) were not statistically significant. In the pooled cohort, MCH did not significantly correlate with BMI (r = 0.063, p = 0.287), insulin (r = 0.007, p = 0.909), C-peptide (r = 0.005, p = 0.931), or HOMA-IR (r = −0.009, p = 0.878).

Lipid parameters demonstrated coherent intercorrelation patterns, with expected positive intercorrelations among total cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, triglycerides, and apolipoprotein B, and inverse relationships with HDL-cholesterol and apolipoprotein A1. However, MCH did not show positive correlations with lipid abnormalities. Instead, weak but statistically significant inverse correlations were observed between MCH and total cholesterol (patient group: r = −0.156, p = 0.016; pooled cohort: r = −0.152, p = 0.010) and LDL-cholesterol (patient group: r = −0.129, p = 0.048; pooled cohort: r = −0.118, p = 0.048). No significant correlations were detected between MCH and triglycerides or HDL-cholesterol.

Hepatic enzymes demonstrated significant intercorrelations; however, their correlations with MCH were largely absent. No significant correlations were observed between MCH and ALT, AST, or GGT in either cohort. A weak inverse correlation between MCH and ALP was detected in the pooled cohort (r = −0.135, p = 0.023), but this finding was not consistently observed across groups. ALT and GGT showed moderate positive correlations with BMI (pooled cohort: ALT r = 0.461, p < 0.0001; GGT r = 0.562, p < 0.0001), consistent with adiposity-related hepatic involvement, whereas AST did not demonstrate meaningful correlations with anthropometric or metabolic variables.

Renal function parameters (urea and creatinine) showed expected internal correlations but did not demonstrate consistent or statistically meaningful correlations with metabolic or hormonal variables in the analyzed cohorts.

Thyroid-related parameters (TSH, fT3, fT4) showed significant intercorrelations, consistent with their physiological regulatory relationships. Notably, MCH showed a consistent inverse correlation with free thyroxine (fT4) in both the patient group (r = −0.192, p = 0.0029) and the pooled cohort (r = −0.180, p = 0.0024). In the pooled cohort, a weak negative correlation was also observed between MCH and free triiodothyronine (fT3) (r = −0.126, p = 0.033), whereas this correlation did not reach statistical significance in the patient group (r = −0.095, p = 0.144). No significant relationship was detected between MCH and TSH.

Sensitivity analyses including previously excluded outlier values did not materially alter the overall correlation structure or principal correlation patterns across cohorts, confirming robustness of the findings.

As an integrated interpretation, the correlation structure identified a strong adiposity-insulin resistance axis characterized by robust correlations between BMI, insulin, C-peptide, and HOMA-IR. In contrast, circulating MCH demonstrated only weak correlations with metabolic parameters. The initial hypothesis that elevated MCH levels would be accompanied by deterioration of the lipid profile was not supported. Although lipid parameters exhibited coherent intercorrelation patterns and correlated with BMI, MCH showed only weak inverse correlations with total cholesterol and LDL-cholesterol and no consistent correlations with other lipid markers.

Discussion

The present study characterizes MCH-related metabolic alterations in a well-defined pediatric and adolescent obese cohort, including 283 participants stratified by sex and pubertal status with an appropriate normal-weight control group. Correlation analyses revealed a metabolic profile dominated by adiposity and insulin resistance, with BMI strongly associated with insulin, C-peptide, and HOMA-IR, indicating that insulin resistance is already established in childhood. In contrast to our initial hypothesis, circulating MCH levels did not demonstrate significant correlations with classical insulin resistance markers in the obese cohort, and only weak associations were observed with selected anthropometric and lipid parameters. These findings suggest that peripheral MCH concentrations may not directly reflect insulin resistance status in pediatric obesity.

Lipid profile parameters demonstrated strong positive intercorrelations between total cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, and triglycerides, as well as inverse correlations with HDL-cholesterol and apolipoprotein A1. Although lipid parameters showed modest relationships with BMI and insulin resistance indices, their correlations with MCH were weak or inverse. Accordingly, our analyses do not support a direct association between elevated circulating MCH levels and dyslipidemia in this pediatric obese population.

The observed inverse lipid associations may reflect developmental metabolic characteristics specific to childhood and adolescence. This period is characterized by rapid somatic growth, enhanced membrane synthesis, active steroid and thyroid hormone production, and increased anabolic turnover. Puberty is also associated with transient physiological insulin resistance and dynamic hormonal remodeling.37 These processes influence substrate utilization and lipid handling, potentially buffering circulating lipid concentrations despite increased adiposity. Pediatric obesity may therefore represent an earlier stage of metabolic dysregulation in which endocrine and insulin-related alterations precede overt dyslipidemia, as reflected in clinical guidelines describing age-dependent metabolic trajectories in childhood obesity.38,39 In this context, the weak inverse correlations observed are unlikely to indicate protective metabolic effects, but rather may reflect age-specific metabolic compensation mechanisms that attenuate clinically manifest lipid abnormalities during early obesity.

From a pathophysiological perspective, these findings should be interpreted in light of the established role of MCH as a hypothalamic regulator of appetite, energy balance, and glucose metabolism.24–30

Experimental and translational evidence has consistently implicated MCH in the regulation of energy balance and metabolic homeostasis. In animal models, chronic activation or overexpression of MCH promotes increased energy intake, reduced energy expenditure, obesity, and insulin resistance.31,32,40 Conversely, MCH-deficient or receptor-modulated models demonstrate reduced body weight, resistance to diet-induced obesity, and improved insulin sensitivity.41 These data establish a mechanistic role for central MCH signaling in obesogenic processes, particularly under sustained metabolic stress.

In human studies, circulating MCH has been detected in peripheral blood and associated with metabolic regulation, although findings remain heterogeneous.36 Adult clinical and epidemiological investigations suggest that MCH activity is more strongly linked to long-term weight gain, dyslipidemia, and impaired glucose–insulin homeostasis.30,33 Pharmacological targeting of MCH receptors in adults has further demonstrated metabolic effects, including weight reduction and improved glucose metabolism,33 reinforcing the relevance of the MCH system in adult metabolic pathology.

In contrast, data in pediatric populations are limited. The present findings indicate a more selective and attenuated peripheral correlation pattern in children and adolescents, with limited associations between circulating MCH and lipid parameters. This divergence from adult observations may reflect developmental metabolic dynamics, differences in adiposity distribution, and shorter duration of metabolic exposure. Pediatric obesity may therefore represent an earlier stage in the trajectory of metabolic dysregulation, in which neuroendocrine alterations are detectable before the full manifestation of chronic metabolic complications typically observed in adults.

A consistent inverse correlation was observed between circulating MCH and free thyroxine (fT4), indicating that higher MCH levels were associated with lower circulating fT4 concentrations. MCH-producing neurons in the lateral hypothalamus participate in neuroendocrine circuits integrating energy balance and endocrine regulation.24–30,33 Experimental and molecular data suggest that MCH signaling interacts with broader hypothalamic pathways involved in metabolic adaptation.33–35 In obesity, subtle alterations of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis have been described, often reflecting adaptive regulatory adjustments rather than primary thyroid dysfunction. The observed negative association between MCH and fT4 may therefore reflect coordinated neuroendocrine adaptation to sustained positive energy balance rather than direct thyroid suppression.

The weaker association observed with free triiodothyronine (fT3) compared with fT4 may be related to peripheral conversion of T4 to T3 by deiodinase enzymes. Circulating fT3 concentrations are influenced not only by HPT axis activity but also by tissue-specific deiodination processes, which can partially buffer fluctuations in fT4 levels.42,43 Consequently, correlations with fT3 may appear attenuated relative to those observed with fT4. However, as deiodinase activity and reverse T3 were not assessed in the present study, this interpretation remains speculative and should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Interpretation of these findings should also consider potential confounding by age, sex distribution, pubertal stage, adiposity pattern, and fasting status. Although the cohort was stratified by sex and pubertal status and all samples were obtained under standardized fasting conditions, developmental hormonal dynamics and differences in body fat distribution may influence both circulating MCH levels and metabolic parameters. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded when interpreting the observed correlation patterns.

The present findings indicate that circulating MCH in pediatric obesity demonstrates limited and selective associations with metabolic parameters. Rather than functioning as a general biomarker of insulin resistance or dyslipidemia, peripheral MCH may reflect specific aspects of neuroendocrine adaptation during early metabolic dysregulation. Within this framework, pediatric obesity may represent a developmental stage in which endocrine and energy-regulatory pathways are altered before the full clinical manifestation of metabolic syndrome.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of the present study include its relatively large, well-characterized pediatric cohort and the simultaneous evaluation of anthropometric, metabolic, and neuroendocrine parameters under standardized conditions. This integrative design enhances internal consistency and enables the detection of early pathophysiological alterations within a developmental context.

Participants were consecutively recruited from the Outpatient Unit of Endocrinology and Diabetology, Department of Pediatrics, Clinical Centre, University of Debrecen, reflecting a real-world tertiary-care pediatric population. All samples were collected under standardized fasting conditions and processed using uniform preanalytical laboratory workflows, minimizing technical variability and enhancing internal consistency.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted as a single-center observational investigation, which may limit external generalizability. The analysis was based on single time-point measurements within a cross-sectional design; therefore, temporal sequence and causality cannot be established. Furthermore, statistical evaluation relied on correlation analyses without multivariable adjustment, and residual confounding related to developmental stage, adiposity distribution, or hormonal dynamics cannot be entirely excluded.

Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as associative and hypothesis-generating. Larger, multicenter, longitudinal studies incorporating repeated endocrine measurements and multivariable modeling will be necessary to clarify independent and temporally defined relationships between circulating MCH and metabolic regulation in pediatric obesity.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that circulating MCH in pediatric obesity exhibits limited and selective associations with metabolic parameters, with modest correlations observed primarily within the neuroendocrine domain rather than with lipid profile measures. These findings suggest that peripheral MCH levels may reflect age-dependent adaptations in central energy-regulatory pathways during early metabolic dysregulation, rather than established peripheral metabolic complications.

In view of the cross-sectional design and modest effect sizes, the observed associations should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. MCH has not been validated as a clinical biomarker, and its potential role in risk stratification or clinical decision-making requires confirmation in larger, multicenter, longitudinal pediatric studies incorporating multivariable modeling.

As the cohort was derived from a tertiary-care pediatric population within a defined clinical and geographic context, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to broader community-based populations or to pediatric cohorts with differing ethnic, socioeconomic, or environmental backgrounds.

This study contributes to a more precise characterization of MCH within the developmental trajectory of pediatric obesity and supports further investigation of neuroendocrine mechanisms in the early stages of metabolic disease.
