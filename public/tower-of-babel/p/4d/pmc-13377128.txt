Fragility of evidence or fragility of interpretation? Concerns regarding the robustness assessment of β-blockers post-MI in preserved/mildly reduced LVEF
Authors: Çağrı Zorlu, Elif Alçık, Elif Miray Alpay
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the recent article by Zuin et al. titled “β-Blockers after myocardial infarction with preserved or mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction: Fragility of contemporary evidence,” published in American Heart Journal Plus: Cardiology Research and Practice [1].

While the authors' effort to evaluate the statistical robustness of contemporary RCTs using fragility metrics is appreciated, several methodological, interpretive, and contextual issues warrant clarification.

The pooled analysis of 19,245 patients demonstrated a 9% relative risk reduction (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.84–1.03) and 0.9% absolute risk reduction for the composite of death, recurrent MI, or HF hospitalization (NNT = 111; FI = 5). The authors describe this as “modest” benefit with “fragile” results, yet they simultaneously note a generally favorable direction of effect across endpoints and acknowledge potential benefit in higher-risk subgroups (e.g., mildly reduced LVEF, hypertension, or arrhythmic risk). This framing appears internally inconsistent. A meta-analysis involving thousands of patients showing a consistent directional benefit, even if statistically borderline and fragile by fragility index, should not be downplayed to the extent of questioning routine use. Low event rates are expected in contemporary low-risk populations with high reperfusion rates and do not inherently invalidate modest but potentially clinically relevant effects.

The reliance on fragility index (FI) and reverse fragility index (RFI) derived from dichotomous event counts, while ignoring time-to-event data and censoring, is a notable limitation. As the authors themselves acknowledge, original trials reported hazard ratios; converting these to risk ratios for FI calculation introduces imprecision [2]. FI values are highly sensitive to low event rates, which are inherent to modern post-MI care (>93% PCI and optimized GDMT). Labeling results as “fragile” (FI ≤5) may overstate uncertainty when the underlying directionality remains consistent. Moreover, the pooled FI of 5 for the primary composite is prominently highlighted, while higher FI values for individual endpoints such as all-cause mortality (FI = 16) and recurrent MI (FI = 27) receive less emphasis.

The conclusion that routine β-blocker use “may not provide consistent or clinically meaningful benefit” contrasts with other contemporary syntheses. For example, the individual patient data meta-analysis by Rossello et al. reported a more substantial 25% relative reduction in a similar composite outcome among patients with mildly reduced LVEF [3]. Current guidelines have nuanced rather than eliminated recommendations for long-term therapy in this population, reflecting ongoing uncertainty rather than definitive lack of benefit [4], [5].

We have the following questions for the authors: Given the favorable directional consistency and potential subgroup benefits acknowledged in the discussion, why advocate moving away from a default strategy toward purely individualized therapy without clearer risk-stratification tools? How do the authors reconcile the modest pooled benefit (NNT 111) with historical evidence and the higher relative benefits observed in mildly reduced LVEF subgroups? Would sensitivity analyses incorporating time-to-event data or patient-level meta-analytic approaches alter the fragility interpretation?

We agree that an individualized approach is ideal and that further research is needed, particularly in well-defined subgroups. However, the current evidence, while imperfect, does not yet justify broadly de-emphasizing β-blockers in patients with LVEF ≥40% post-MI. Over-interpretation of fragility metrics in low-event settings risks therapeutic nihilism in a population that may still derive net benefit.

We appreciate the authors' contribution to this important debate and look forward to their response.
