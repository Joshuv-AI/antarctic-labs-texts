Fatal Clinical Course of Hepatic Portal Venous Gas Associated with Thrombosed Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm

INTRODUCTION

Hepatic portal venous gas (HPVG) may suggest an ominous pathology that requires immediate attention because it is frequently associated with life-threatening conditions such as mesenteric ischemia and bowel necrosis.1-5 On the other hand, differentiating HPVG from pneumobilia could be challenging, particularly in patients with non-specific or altered clinical presentations. Unlike HPVG, pneumobilia―air within the biliary tree―is generally a benign finding resulting from previous procedures such as endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, sphincterotomy, or biliary-enteric anastomosis. Accordingly, early recognition and accurate differential diagnosis between these entities are crucial for timely intervention because they may lead to different therapeutic strategies. This paper presents the case of a 78-year-old female who developed extensive HPVG, ultimately resulting in death despite intensive medical management.

CASE

A 78-year-old female presented to the emergency department with altered mental status. Her family found her in a deep drowsy state. She had a history of a bilateral occipital lobe infarction, abdominal aortic aneurysm with thrombus, chronic renal failure, hyperlipidemia, and had been taking medications, including warfarin and HMG Co-A reductase inhibitors. Two months ago, she was admitted to an outside hospital because of community-acquired pneumonia accompanied by a left femur greater trochanter fracture and a T11 compression fracture. Useful information could not be obtained from the physical examinations because of her drowsy state and lack of continuous loose stools associated with abdominal distension. Her vital signs were blood pressure 110/73 mmHg, heart rate 85 beats/minute, respiratory rate 28/minute, and body temperature 36.2°C. The blood tests results in the emergency department were as follows: White blood cell counts 7,250/mm3; hemoglobin 10.2 g/dL, platelet count 62,000/mm3; BUN/Scr 35.4/1.26 mg/dL; total bilirubin 1.7 mg/dL; aspartate aminotransferase 17 U/L; alanine aminotransferase 12 U/L; total protein 5.51; albumin 3.02 g/dL (3.5–5.2 g/dL); PT 23.4 sec (11.8–14.3 s); INR 1.64 (<1.5); PTT 43.6 sec (29–44 s); alkaline phosphatase 91 U/L; triglyceride 60 mg/dL (<150 mg/dL); C-reactive protein 6.6 mg/dL (<0.1 mg/dL); CK-MB 3.2 ng/mL (<4.0 ng/mL); Troponin-T 95.9 ng/L (15.59 ng/L); NT-proBNP 2,288 pg/mL (<738 pg/mL). The chest X-ray did not show any pneumonic infiltration. The abdominal X-ray showed paralytic ileus. In addition, it was difficult to assess the severity of abdominal tenderness and rebound tenderness because of her drowsy state. Brain CT and diffusion MRI for the evaluation of drowsy mentality did not show a definite acute cerebral infarction. Therefore, she was treated with sufficient hydration and intravenous antibiotics to cover a hidden infectious condition. Nevertheless, her neurologic signs aggravated and continued with diarrhea. The abdominal CT taken at the hospital on day 2 revealed a diffuse gas density in the portal vein and splenic vein (Fig. 1A–1D), associated with a huge abdominal aortic aneurysm (Fig. 1E, 1F) and pneumatosis intestinalis (Fig. 1G). Despite the intensive medical care with inotropics, the patient succumbed to her medical condition 12 hours later on hospital day 3.

DISCUSSION

The presence of gas in the hepatic region should be differentiated between HPVG and pneumobilia because HPVG is more frequently associated with clinically significant lesions and carries a substantially higher risk of mortality.1,2 When HPVG is accompanied by gas in the mesenteric venous system, it is often related to a bowel infarction, intra-abdominal sepsis, severe necrotizing pancreatitis, or bowel perforation because of other critical conditions.2-5 In 1978, Liebman et al. in the United States reported that the mortality rate of HPVG was as high as 75%. With the advances in imaging studies, HPVG is no longer regarded as a decisive predictor of severe clinical outcomes to the same extent previously thought.3,4,6 In 2018, a study of 1590 HPVG patients from Japan reported a mortality rate of 27.3%; 842 (53%) patients had bowel ischemia as an underlying disease, of whom 271 (32%) underwent a surgical resection for the bowel necrosis.7 The mortality rate was approximately 16.5% when bowel ischemia causing HPVG was successfully managed, but it remained as high as 56.4% when accompanied by sepsis.7 A study in 2025 by Wei et al.1 from China reported that the overall mortality rate of patients with HPVG was 28.6%. In the present case, several risk factors, including advanced age, vascular comorbidities, chronic renal failure, warfarin use, and recent immobilization, may have contributed to the impaired intestinal perfusion and mucosal integrity, ultimately leading to the fatal clinical outcomes of HPVS and pneumatosis intestinalis. Furthermore, the absence of definitive abdominal signs―attributable to her altered mental status―likely delayed the recognition of ischemia. Given that the disease course can be fulminant when accompanied by sepsis, the sudden deterioration in her clinical condition underscores the aggressive nature of mesenteric ischemia in the elderly and highlights the importance of prompt imaging and intensive care.

Radiologically, HPVG tends to appear in the peripheral branches of the portal vein, extending toward the liver capsule.3,8 In contrast, pneumobilia typically presents with centrally located gas within the biliary tree, often confined to the liver hilum, and follows the direction of bile flow (toward the porta hepatis). In the present patient, abdominal CT revealed branching gas patterns in the peripheral hepatic regions, consistent with HPVG rather than pneumobilia.

Although HPVG and pneumatosis intestinalis can be observed in benign conditions such as post-endoscopy or following enteral nutrition, the combination of these findings with clinical deterioration, elevated inflammatory markers, and hemodynamic instability is highly suggestive of transmural bowel necrosis. The recent literature suggests that elevated lactate, hypotension, and CT findings, such as bowel wall thinning, lack of enhancement, or portomesenteric gas, are strong indicators of a poor prognosis.9,10

HPVG and pneumatosis intestinalis are serious radiologic findings that often indicate underlying mesenteric ischemia. These signs in elderly patients with significant comorbidities may reflect an advanced disease state with a poor prognosis. Therefore, early imaging with a high index of suspicion for concomitant mesenteric ischemia, vigilant monitoring, and multidisciplinary decision-making are essential for optimal management. This case highlights the fulminant potential of HPVG with pneumatosis intestinalis caused by mesenteric ischemia, particularly when hepatic air is detected in patients presenting with unexplained neurological decline and abnormal abdominal imaging findings.
