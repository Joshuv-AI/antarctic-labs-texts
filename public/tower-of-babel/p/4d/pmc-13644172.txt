Incidence of toxigenic Bacillus cereus in ready-to-eat fermented foods in Osogbo, Nigeria: a potential health risk
Authors: Oluwafemi Bamidele Daramola, Richard Kolade Omole, Tomiwa Olumide Adesoji, Alabi Peter Dare, Ifeoluwa Victoria Daramola, Nana Aishat Yusuf-Omoloye, Aduragbemi Chukwuwem Emmanuel, Oluwawemimo Precious Owoeye, Adedotun Emmanuel Adekola, Nkem Torimiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacillus cereus (B. cereus) is one of several foodborne pathogens that continue to threaten the safety of ready-to-eat (RTE) foods, including fermented foods. A few studies have identified the presence of enterotoxigenic gene-encoding B. cereus in several southwestern states of Nigeria. However, Osun State remains the only southwestern state without data on the potential presence of enterotoxigenic gene-encoding B. cereus. In this study, a preliminary investigation was carried out using a convenience sampling method at three highly patronized locations (Shasha, Orisumbare, and Oja-Oba) of RTE fermented foods in Osogbo, Nigeria. Thirty-two samples comprising six varieties of RTE fermented foods (pap, iru, gari, fufu, kunu, and soy wara) were collected and analyzed for Bacillus species. Recovered Bacillus species were subjected to hemolysis test. Hemolytic Bacillus species were screened for enterotoxin gene operons (nheABC and hblACD) and antibiotic susceptibility. A total of 33 Bacillus species were recovered from the RTE fermented foods, among which four showed hemolytic properties. Furthermore, PCR screening showed three Bacillus isolates encoding nhe genes, and two encoding both nhe and hbl genes. 16 S rRNA nucleotide sequencing identified the three isolates as B. cereus. The three B. cereus isolates were resistant to β-lactam antibiotics (penicillin, cefadroxil, cefoxitin, and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid), trimethoprim, and fusidic acid; however, they were susceptible to gentamicin, linezolid, and chloramphenicol, and had a multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index of 0.64. Introduction

Navigating and ensuring work-life balance in urban and semi-urban settings has led to an increased dependence on ready-to-eat (RTE) foods [26, 34, 37]. The poor living and working conditions, as well as the increasing demands of out-of-home care, have forced many people to rely on informal food vendors for their daily food needs. This is sometimes due to its low cost, availability, and convenience [2, 16]. Fermented foods, many of which are traditionally processed foods, remain one of the most abundant and readily available vended foods in sub-Saharan countries [15]. Unfortunately, these foods, on many occasions, are processed, packaged, and vended under unhygienic conditions [15, 51], acting as vehicles for foodborne disease outbreaks [16]. A few RTE fermented foods undergo brief, minor heating just before consumption (such as pap and locust beans). Most are not; rather, consumed in their final form (such as gari, fufu, wara, kunu, soy milk, and many more), thus increasing the chances of foodborne diseases.

Among key food poisoning pathogens, B. cereus is a major concern in vended foods due to its heat-resistant endospores and biofilm-forming capabilities, which enable it to survive and persist in harsh environments and food products [27, 39]. Bacillus cereus produces two main toxin types responsible for distinct clinical syndromes: chromosomally encoded enterotoxin genes (nhe, hbl, bceT, cytK, and entFM) causing diarrhea, and the plasmid-encoded emetic toxin gene cluster (ces) associated with emetic syndromes [1].

Sadly, B. cereus has been underreported and lacks sufficient epidemiological data and surveillance reports in many countries, despite its huge threat to food safety. The significantly low global surveillance and monitoring report on B. cereus in clinical, environmental, and food settings compared to common bacteria has given this group little/no medical intervention and attention. Additionally, the lack of reports on B. cereus outbreaks has been limited by its initial mild symptoms (which could become severe with time) and its misidentification with symptoms from Staphylococcus aureus and Clostridium perfringens cases [22].

The growing global attention to antimicrobial resistance (AMR), driven by improper antibiotic use, is becoming increasingly challenging [38]. The 2024 World Health Organization (WHO) Bacterial Priority Pathogens List (WBPPL) designates several resistant bacteria as priority pathogens (WHO BPPL [53]). B. cereus has been implicated in food intoxications, and its treatment relies on antibiotics; however, the emergence of antibiotic-resistant strains can pose an imminent danger if it is not identified promptly. It is therefore important to monitor the antibiotic resistance profile of B. cereus to understand its resistance patterns for effective treatment.

Actively monitoring food contamination by B. cereus and mapping its antibiotic resistance profile is essential to understand its role as a potential custodian of antibiotic resistance genes spreading through the food production chain. A few studies have molecularly identified the presence of B. cereus in Southwestern states (such as Lagos, Oyo, Ekiti, Ogun, and Ondo) of Nigeria [3, 23, 42, 43]. Unfortunately, no study has reported the potential presence of enterotoxigenic gene-encoding B. cereus in RTE fermented foods in Osun State, a southwestern part of Nigeria. This study was conducted to investigate the presence of enterotoxigenic gene-encoding B. cereus in vended RTE fermented foods and assess their antibiotic resistance patterns. This research aims to provide insights into the potential health risks associated with RTE fermented foods in Osogbo (Osun State), filling a critical gap as the only uninvestigated metropolis in Southwestern Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Study area and design

This study was designed as a preliminary investigation and carried out in Osogbo, the capital of Osun State, Nigeria. It is located between Latitude 7.7827° N and longitude 4.5418° E. The study was conducted between May and October 2025 and adopted a convenience sampling technique in three densely populated and widely patronized RTE fermented foods vending locations (Shasha, Orisumbare, and Oja-Oba) within the Osogbo metropolis. The map (Fig. 1) showing the sampling locations of the RTE fermented foods was created using Grid3 Nigeria software (https://data.grid3.org/).

Sample collection

Given the preliminary investigation approach of this study, a total of thirty-two (32) RTE fermented food samples were collected to evaluate initial trends and establish a baseline for future, larger-scale investigations (Table S1). The RTE fermented foods were collected from different vendors within the sampling locations. They include six African locust beans “iru”, six pap “ogi”, six “gari”, five local cheese “wara”, five “kunu”, and four “fufu”. The RTE fermented food samples were collected using sterile zip-lock bags, adequately labelled, kept in an ice box, and transferred to the laboratory for microbiological analysis. Samples were processed within 4 h of sample collection.

Microbiological analysis

Sample preparation and rapid microbiological enumeration

Solid RTE fermented food samples were aseptically introduced into nutrient broth, while liquid and semi-solid RTE fermented food samples were dislodged in broth. One (1) mL of the homogenate was introduced into nine (9) mL sterile distilled water and serially diluted across three other test tubes labeled 10− 2, 10− 3, and 10− 4. Aerobic plate counts (APCs) and coliform counts were carried out using the 3 M Petrifilm enumeration protocol by plating 1 mL of the diluent on aerobic count Petrifilm plates (3 M Microbiology), and E. coli/coliform count Petrifilm plates (3 M Microbiology), and thereafter, spread-plated using their respective 3 M Petrifilm spreaders. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h (E. coli/coliform) and 48 h (APC) [50]. After the incubation period, observable discrete colonies on the Petrifilm plates were counted and documented as colony-forming units per gram (CFU/g). The experiment was carried out in duplicate, and mean values were determined.

Isolation and presumptive identification of Bacillus species

The isolation of Bacillus species was conducted as described in a previous study [51]. An aliquot (1 mL) of the 10− 4 diluent from each RTE fermented sample was plated on nutrient agar (NA) (HiMedia, India) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Cultured plates with Bacillus-like growth, such as large, grey-white, dry, irregular, flat, circular/lobate, and translucent colonies, were sub-cultured and Gram-stained. Isolates were presumptively identified as Bacillus spp. by the Gram stain technique under a microscope to appear as large, Gram-positive rod-shaped cells in pairs or chains. The 48-hour-old cultures were tested for endospore production using malachite green and safranin reagents. The presumptively identified isolates were further characterized for probable identification using biochemical tests, which include sugar fermentation (glucose, sucrose, lactose), gas production, hydrogen sulphide production, indole production, motility, citrate utilization, oxidase, 6.5% NaCl, and 1% starch hydrolysis.

Culture-dependent hemolytic test for Bacillus species

Hemolytic activity in the isolated Bacillus strains was assessed using a modified method previously documented by Olajugbagbe et al., [44]. An 18-24-hour-old culture of the Bacillus spp. was streaked on blood agar and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Post-incubation, plates were checked for halozones indicating blood lysis. Clear lysis (complete clearing) around the streak was recorded as β-hemolysis (hemolytic Bacillus). Green-hued zones were identified as α-hemolytic Bacillus, and no zone of lysis indicated γ-hemolysis (non-hemolytic Bacillus).

16 S rRNA nucleotide sequencing of Bacillus species

The genomic DNA of hemolytic Bacillus isolates cultured for 24 h in nutrient broth medium was extracted using the QIAGEN QIAamp DNA kit (Qiagen Inc., USA) as described by the manufacturer. PCR amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene was done using previously reported oligonucleotides and PCR conditions shown in Table 1. The amplified PCR products were run in a gel electrophoresis tank (at 160 V for 45 min) using 1.5% agarose stained with ethidium bromide. The amplified PCR products were visualized using an ultraviolet transilluminator, compared with a 100 bp molecular weight DNA ladder, and the gel image was photographed. The amplified PCR products were purified using 3 M Na acetate and 95% ethanol, conditioned at -20 °C for 30 min. Afterward, it was cold-centrifuged at 13,000 g/4°C for 10 min and subsequently at 7500 g/4°C for 15 min upon the addition of 70% ethanol. The resuspended products after decantation were quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). For genotypic characterization, the amplified DNAs were further purified using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems) and sequenced on an Applied Biosystems 3130xl Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The 16 S rRNA raw read sequences of the presumptive Bacillus isolates were viewed using FinchTV Sequence Alignment Editor (version 1.4.0) and Artemis software. The resulting sequences were aligned with the NCBI core nucleotide database to identify the best hits and subsequently matched with the resulting genus. The MUSCLE alignment approach was adopted for sequence alignment. Phylogenetic analysis was conducted with Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA) version 12, using the Neighbor-Joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates to infer evolutionary history. Evolutionary distances were determined using the Maximum Composite Likelihood model [33].

Enterotoxigenic genes detection in Bacillus species using PCR methods

Enterotoxigenic genes present in the hemolytic Bacillus species were identified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) methods [51]. Genomic DNA from 18-24-hour-old cultures of hemolytic Bacillus isolates was extracted using the QIAGEN QIAamp DNA kit (Qiagen Inc., USA) as described by the manufacturer. Target genes, oligonucleotides, and PCR conditions used in this study are listed in Table 1 [15, 25, 51]. The amplified PCR products were run in a gel electrophoresis tank (at 160 V for 45 min) using 1.5% agarose stained with ethidium bromide. The amplified PCR products, which were compared with a 100 bp molecular weight DNA ladder (New England Biolabs, USA), were visualized using a gel documentation system (U: Genius, Syngene, India) and photographed.

Antibiotic sensitivity test of Bacillus species

The antibiotic susceptibility profile of the Bacillus cereus was determined using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method [10]. Standardized (0.5 McFarland) inoculum of the isolates were spread-plated on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) plates using sterile cotton swab sticks. The seeded plates were allowed to dry for approximately 10 min at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C), after which they were impregnated with antibiotics (Oxoid and Mast, UK) using sterile forceps. Impregnated antibiotics discs and their concentrations included: Tetracycline (TC) (30 µg), Linezolid (LZD) (10 µg), Erythromycin (ERY) (15 µg), Penicillin (PC) (1 unit), Amoxicillin-Clavulanic acid (AMC) (3 µg), Cefadroxil (CFR) (30 µg), Cefoxitin (FOX) (30 µg), Gentamicin (GM) (10 µg), Chloramphenicol (CHL) (30 µg), Trimethoprim (W) (5 µg), and Fusidic acid (FC) (10 µg). The incubated antibiotic-impregnated MHA plates were observed for zones of inhibition and afterward measured in millimeters (mm) using a calibrated meter rule. The measured zones were interpreted as sensitive (S) or resistant (R), as indicated in the standard interpretative chart of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST, version 15) for Bacillus spp (European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing [20]). Staphylococcus aureus interpretative breakpoints were used as a surrogate for interpreting the susceptibility pattern of other antibiotics (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [13]).

Data analysis

This preliminary study utilized convenience sampling across 32 ready-to-eat (RTE) fermented foods and analyzed the obtained data using descriptive statistics. The biological and technical duplicates of the APCs for each food type from the same location were presented in mean and standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Enumeration of aerobic and coliform bacteria

The APC of the RTE fermented food samples are shown in Fig. 2. Rapid aerobic enumeration revealed that the highest APC for pap, ALB, and “fufu” were all observed at Oja-Oba, yielding 3.94 ± 0.76, 4.85 ± 0.15, and 5.0 ± 0.0 log CFU/g, respectively. The highest counts for “gari” occurred at Orisumbare (3.59 ± 0.17 log CFU/g), while peak counts for “wara” were shared between Shasha and Orisumbare (5.0 ± 0.0 log CFU/g), and “kunu” peaked at both Shasha and Oja-Oba (4.7 ± 0.0 log CFU/g). Overall, ”wara” exhibited the highest APC across the study, whereas “gari” exhibited the lowest (Fig. 2). Additionally, all RTE fermented food samples tested negative for coliforms, with the sole exception of the “kunu” sample from Oja-Oba, which had a count of 3.28 log CFU/g.

Incidence of Bacillus species and Bacilluscereus in RTE fermented foods

A total of thirty-three (33) Bacillus species were recovered from the RTE fermented food samples. Of the 33 Bacillus species, 30.3%, 27.3%, 6.06%, 9.09%, 15.2%, and 12.1% were isolated from pap, ALB, wara, gari, kunu, and fufu, respectively. From the presumptively identified Bacillus species, four (4) showed hemolytic properties (3 β-hemolytic and 1 α-hemolytic Bacillus species).

Molecular identification of Bacilluscereus

The 16 S rRNA read sequences of 894 bp, 895 bp, and 1110 bp were obtained for OGOR 2102, GAOO 21021, and KUSA 2102, respectively. The nucleotide BLAST analysis returned a 99% similarity match of the nucleotide sequences with the B. cereus 16 S rRNA ribosomal subunit repository in GenBank, among which OP830493.1 and ON715736.1 were closely related. The evolutionary tree and distance matrices generated using MEGA 12 showed high sequence alignment between the isolates and closely related B. cereus reference strains. The pairwise deletion of ambiguous positions for each sequence pair generated a final dataset comprising 1,624 positions, yielding an optimal tree with a total branch length of 0.631 (Fig. 3). The nucleotide sequences were deposited in GenBank as B. cereus under accession numbers PX492354 (GAOO 21021), PX492355 (OGOR 2102), and PX944888 (KUSA 2102).

Enterotoxigenic genes profile of hemolytic Bacillus species

The PCR screening confirmed the presence of nhe genes in the three B. cereus (Fig. 4, S1, and Table 2). The nheABC gene clusters were identified in B. cereus KUSA 2102 and B. cereus GAOO 21021, with the nheAB gene cluster detected in B. cereus OGOR 2102. For the hbl gene, B. cereus GAOO 21021 had hblA, and B. cereus OGOR 2102 had hblD. None of the B. cereus strains had all the hblACD gene clusters. B. cereus GAOO 21021 and OGOR 2102 encoded at least one of nhe and hbl genes. The presumptive Bacillus sp (GAOO 21022) with β-hemolytic property was negative for all enterotoxins assayed in this study.

Antibiotic profile of Bacillus cereus

The three B. cereus isolates recovered in this study were susceptible to gentamicin, linezolid, and chloramphenicol (Table 3). None of the recorded zones of inhibition interpretation falls within the intermediate range. Among the three B. cereus isolates recovered in this study, B. cereus OGOR 2102 was resistant to erythromycin, and B. cereus GAOO 21021 and KUSA 2102 were resistant to tetracycline. All B. cereus isolates recovered in this study showed phenotypic resistance to β-lactam antibiotics (penicillin, cefadroxil, cefoxitin, and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid), trimethoprim, and fusidic acid. The three B. cereus isolates recovered from this study had a multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index of 0.64, each showing phenotypic resistance to 7 of the 11 screened antibiotics.

Discussion

Isolation and toxigenic gene profiling of B. cereus from RTE fermented foods

The presumptively high APC counts in RTE fermented foods, as raised in this study, align with concerns from previous research on RTE fermented foods derived from cassava, such as gari and fufu [9, 16, 41], dairy products such as wara [4, 6] and cereal such as kunu, and ogi [5, 52]. The presence of coliforms in the sampled kunu drinks indicates potential hygiene failures and post-processing contamination during production [18]. The addition of unheated ingredients (spices and sweeteners) post-cooling, the use of non-potable water during sieving and dilution, and inadequate personal hygiene during manual packaging and container storage are likely to be responsible for microbial reintroduction and the presence of coliforms in kunu [18].

The recovery of B. cereus isolates from kunu and ogi (fermented maize- and/or millet-based foods) was also confirmed by a previous study [19]. Another study highlighted the presence of B. cereus in 43 of 75 cassava starch samples from bakeries [49]. This research further alluded to the presence of B. cereus in fermented food and its suggestive implicating role in gastrointestinal disorders [36]. Given their ability to transition from vegetative cells to spores and also produce biofilms, they generally survive in these food products, as well as in food processing systems [19, 56]. A few factors contributing to B. cereus presence in RTE foods, and by extension, fermented foods, include lack/inadequate personal hygiene, contamination of primary package and secondary containers, open display of RTE food items, and the poor/inadequate attention to food safety by consumers (Fig. 5) [30, 39]. The isolation of B. cereus isolates from starchy foods suggests a favourable environment, as these food products are often kept at ambient temperature, a condition that could favourably support emetic toxin (cereulide) production [1, 19]. Although sequence alignment identified the three isolates as B. cereus, 16 S rRNA sequencing offers limited discriminatory power within the closely related B. cereus group and thus, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) may be considered for definitive confirmation of their species-level identity.

Conceptual illustration of the potential risk associated with the consumption of RTE fermented foods

Our findings confirmed the presence of nheABC gene clusters and hblAD genes (diarrheal toxin-producing genes) in the recovered B. cereus strains. Both nhe and hbl enterotoxigenic genes are responsible for the production of enterotoxins, which create pores in lipid bilayers, causing cellular damage, including necrosis and mucosal injury [19]. This preliminary evidence suggests that enterotoxigenic genes can also be present in B. cereus recovered from RTE fermented foods [1, 47]. The constant presence of nhe in this study aligns with Böhm et al., [11] who suggest that, while horizontal gene transfer often causes B. cereus to lose hbl, cytK, and plcR virulence genes, the nhe operon is rarely lost due to exclusive vertical inheritance and may be responsible for the common cytotoxicity mechanism, including osmotic lysis.

Antibiotic susceptibility profiling of B. cereus from RTE fermented foods

Bacillus cereus in this study showed varying phenotypic susceptibility and resistance to the screened antibiotics. The three B. cereus isolates were susceptible to gentamicin, linezolid, and chloramphenicol, which was similar to findings from other studies [1, 14, 24]. A few reports also highlighted that B. cereus isolates were often susceptible to erythromycin [19, 55] and occasionally susceptible to tetracycline [1, 55]. The susceptibility of B. cereus to gentamicin, erythromycin, linezolid, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol reflects that these antibiotics disrupt bacterial protein synthesis by binding to highly conserved ribosomal subunits [1, 14, 24]. Consequently, they remain relevant for treating severe infections associated with B. cereus.

However, B. cereus remains a critical environmental and foodborne pathogen characterized by high rates of antibiotic resistance. The disc diffusion experiment showed that the B. cereus isolates were resistant to β-lactam antibiotics inhibiting cell wall synthesis (penicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, and cefoxitin). A few studies have also confirmed the phenotypic resistance of B. cereus isolated from RTE foods to this class of antibiotic [24, 47, 56]. This has been attributed to the production of endogenous β-lactamase enzymes by B. cereus, which are responsible for the hydrolysis of the β-lactam ring of β-lactam antibiotics [1], and this resistance pattern to β-lactam antibiotics indicates their continuous threat to food safety [28].

Other phenotypic resistance reported includes trimethoprim and fusidic acid. The phenotypic resistance of B. cereus to trimethoprim has also been reported in different plant-based foods [12, 40]. B. cereus has been documented to use its dhfr enzyme variant to block drug binding and may also use metabolic bypass and high folate production to survive the standard dose of trimethoprim [12]. The resistance of B. cereus to fusidic acid, on the other hand, has also been previously reported [12, 55], but contrasts with a study reporting high susceptibility [21]. Possible indices are that B. cereus possesses chromosomal fusA genes encoding ribosomal protection proteins that block fusidic acid from binding to elongation factor G [48].

Consistent with recent reports on spices, raw milk, and RTE foods, the three B. cereus isolates exhibited a high MAR index greater than 0.2, aligning them with typical foodborne and environmental resistant strains [7, 14, 28]. This widespread resistance highlights significant selective pressures and contamination risks from farm environments into the food supply chain [7]. Notably, while phenotypic resistance was observed, underlying resistance genes were not molecularly confirmed. Additionally, their clinical relevance may be subjective, as they are isolated from food not clinical infections.

Significance, concerns, and exigent response to the preliminary investigation

Despite B. cereus’ implication as a severe foodborne pathogen, its outbreaks have been rarely investigated or given adequate attention [1], particularly in Nigeria. This study, like many other studies, has highlighted the presence of enterotoxigenic genes in B. cereus isolated from RTE food products in Osogbo, Nigeria. This preliminary evidence is adding to the global [31, 46, 54] and national knowledge [3, 23, 42, 43] on the potential risk of B. cereus.

Although often viewed as an environmental isolate, the presence of enterotoxigenic genes in B. cereus potentiates it as a foodborne pathogen and may result in severe clinical cases and ultimately cause death [29, 46]. Moreover, despite nearly three decades of awareness and caution regarding the public health risks of RTE foods [17], national policies and regulations designed to address these risks remain severely underutilized [35]. To improve the culture of food safety in Nigeria and across the globe, relevant authorities should introduce and enact a sustainable and healthy food vending system policy that would mitigate risks associated with the consumption of foodborne pathogens, most importantly, B. cereus in RTE fermented foods [8, 45].

Conclusion

This study provides suggestive findings, highlighting the presence of enterotoxigenic genes in B. cereus in RTE-fermented foods sold in Osogbo, Osun State, Nigeria. The identification of B. cereus strains carrying toxigenic nhe and hbl gene clusters underscores a potential health risk to the safety of RTE fermented foods in the studied environment. While these B. cereus isolates were susceptible to protein synthesis-inhibiting antibiotics such as gentamicin, linezolid, erythromycin, chloramphenicol, and tetracycline, their phenotypic resistance to cell wall and folic acid inhibitors signals a worrying trend in multidrug resistance. The presence of these enterotoxigenic genes among the recovered strains indicates inadequate hygienic practices, especially poor food handling. Without timely interventions, rigorous food safety policies, and implementation of proper hygiene standards among vendors, there could be an imminent threat of foodborne illnesses. Ultimately, this preliminary evidence emphasizes the urgent need for detailed investigation, strict regulatory monitoring, and vendor education to mitigate the potential food safety risk associated with the informal food systems.

Limitations and future directions of the study

This study offers preliminary insights, however, it is limited in scope due to the small, non-seasonal convenience sample size (32) across three locations, which restricted the robust environmental representativeness. Furthermore, while only three B. cereus isolates were confirmed, the 16 S rRNA sequencing identification lacks the resolution to differentiate within the B. cereus group. The consideration of hemolytic strains only for genetic screening may have missed non-hemolytic strains. The present study excluded specific enterotoxigenic (entFM, cytK), emetic (ces), and antibiotic-resistant genes.

Future investigations should consider larger, randomized, multi-season sampling and incorporate whole-genome sequencing or specific markers (such as gyrB, panC) for precise identification. Additionally, studies should include quantitative bacterial counts, antimicrobial MICs, and evaluate active toxin production using cytotoxicity or protein expression assays.
