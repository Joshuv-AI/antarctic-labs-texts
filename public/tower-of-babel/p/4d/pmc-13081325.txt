Physical, tactical and technical performance of the most demanding passages of kinematic activity in elite soccer
Authors: Alberto Cortez, Farzad Yousefian, Hugo Folgado, João Brito, Bruno Travassos, Eduardo Abade, Hongyou Liu, Zunqi Niu, Bruno Gonçalves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the changes in physical, tactical, and technical performance during the most demanding passage of kinematic activity (MDPk) and immediately after (post-MDPk) in elite soccer players, with respect to their playing positions. The MDPk (≥ 15 km/h), was defined using a 1-min rolling average (n = 1016 observations), and was quantified as the distance covered at moderate-speed running (MSR), high-speed running (HSR), and sprinting (SPR). In addition, total distance covered (TD) was also included. Generalized linear mixed models were employed to assess mean changes in five physical, ten tactical, and six technical performance-related variables during the effective playing time from the onset of the MDPk to 1-min post-MDPk, across different playing positions. Across all positions, there was a significant decrease in all external load variables during the 1-min post-MDPk period (TD=-48%; MDPk=-89%; MSR=-84%; HSR=-93%; SPR=-97%). All technical variables exhibited a substantial decline in the post-MDPk regardless of playing positions, particularly in passes for wide midfielders and central forwards, and shots by central forwards. Team tactical variables decreased in the post-MDPk, especially for ball-in-play percentage and number of possession changes. Central defenders, fullbacks and central midfielders spent more time in their own third in the post period and running more towards their own goal in the MDPk, whereas WM and CF spent more time in opponent’s third in the post period.Our findings carry practical implications for training design, tactical planning, and load management. The observed decline in external load and technical actions following the MDPk suggests that players may experience performance drops, underscoring the need for conditioning drills that prepare athletes not only for peak but also for maintaining performance. The reduction in team tactical variables points to a potential shift toward more static or fragmented phases of play in the post-MDPk, which coaches may consider when designing tactical scenarios that simulate these transitions. Moreover, the positional behaviors identified suggest the need for role-specific conditioning and tactical training. Coaches should leverage this information to create situational drills that reproduce these patterns, ensuring that players are better prepared to execute their responsibilities in and after the MDP.Keywords: Soccer, Analysis, Peak Periods, Post Periods

Introduction

In soccer, numerous studies have investigated the complexity of the game, highlighting the multifaceted demands placed on players during competition [1–3]. In this regard, researchers have expanded this understanding by examining how players’ physical workloads, movement coordination, and fluctuations in performance across matches interact to shape individual and collective behavior throughout the game [4, 5]. As a result, the integration of physical, technical, and tactical components are essential for effectively preparing players for all phases of match play [6]. Building upon analysis of the factors influencing player performance, a clear understanding of the physical demands imposed on soccer players remains crucial [7].The physical demands of soccer are typically quantified using tracking technologies, which allow practitioners and researchers to monitor both training and match loads [8]. Recently, studies have increasingly focused on quantifying and analyzing the most demanding passages (MDP) of match play, which represent relatively short duration periods when players experience peak physical exertion [9–11].

Normally, the MDP span from 1 to 5 min using a rolling average technique [12–14]. This approach has gained greater ecological validity compared to average-match demands, which tend to underestimate the true physiological demands encountered during elite competition [15]. Several authors have already explored distinct aspects of MDPs, such as comparing match demands according to player positions [14, 16, 17], evaluating differences between training and competition demands [18, 19], and exploring the relationship between players’ high-intensity activities and physical characteristics [20]. Recently, a novel multifactorial approach has been applied where the researchers applied a 5-min rolling average to analyze the MDP of both mechanical and kinematic activities during competition [21, 22]. The authors quantified the kinematic MDP (MDPk) through employing a 5-min rolling average to assess the distance covered at speeds > 15 km·h⁻¹, and subsequently analyzed the absolute distances and relative composition (% of MDPk) of the MDPk constituent variables representing moderate-speed running (MSR), high-speed running (HSR), and sprinting (SPR).

Although previous research reported a consistent decline in physical output in the periods immediately following the MDP across various time intervals (1, 3, 5 and 10 min) [23–26].To further understand players’ performance during the match there is a need to consider that players’ performance in soccer is characterized by the interaction between physical, tactical, technical variables [27, 28]. Notably, a previous study by Fang and colleagues [11] has reported some fluctuations in the technical performance in elite soccer players after the MDP. Therefore, it is important to understand the level of decline in the physical outputs after the MDPk and how these fluctuations impact technical and tactical performance variables.

As soccer performance is characterized by its intermittent nature, with immediately following periods of maximal exertion, players exhibit significantly reduced physical performance across multiple movement metrics and time intervals [25, 26, 29]. These temporary reductions in physical output are often attributed to pacing strategies that prevent overload of specific physiological systems [30, 31]. However, there remains a limited understanding of how players behave tactically and technically during and after MDPs. Gaining insight into these behaviors is crucial for enabling coaches to design training tasks that effectively replicate these demands during training, even in specific technical and tactical sessions, which may allow optimizing players’ performance and reduce their risk of injury [32]. Therefore, this study aims: (i) to quantify the 1-min MDPk using a rolling average approach, (ii) to examine the changes in physical, tactical, and technical match performance from the 1-min MDPk (MDP) to the end of 1-min post-MDPk (Post) in elite soccer players and (iii) according to playing positions. It is hypothesized that in the Post, there will be a clear decline of players’ running distances, but also in the components of the MDPk. Simultaneously, related technical performance is expected to decrease. Additionally, it is hypothesized that tactical variables will behave differently depending on the playing position.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data access was provided through the official FIFA Data Plaform. Positional data were collected for all players across 64 matches during the FIFA World Cup Qatar 2022 using a multicamera computerized tracking system (TRACAB, Chyron Hego), with high-definition cameras operating at 25 Hz. The validity and reliability of the TRACAB systems have been previously established [33]. Goalkeepers were excluded from the analysis due to the unique positional demands of their role. Only outfield players who started the match and played at least 70 min were included, resulting in a final sample of 1016 observations for 399 players [34]. The players were divided into the following five categories of positions: fullbacks (FB, n = 89 players), central defenders (CD, n = 80), wide midfielders (WM, n = 93), central midfielders (CM, n = 93) and central forwards (CF, n = 44) [35].

MDP identification

The kinematic MDP (MDPk) was calculated as the maximal distance covered at speeds ≥ 15 km·h− 1 using a 1-min rolling average analysis. Subsequently, the criterion variable was decomposed into moderate-speed running (MSR; 15–19.8 km·h− 1), high-speed running (HSR; 19.8–25.2 km·h− 1), and sprinting (SPR; >25.2 km·h− 1) distances. Finally, the total distance covered (TD) within the MDP period was also calculated [21, 22]. If the MDP ended at less than 1 min before the end of half-time or full-time of the match, the data point was removed from the analysis. The criteria for the selection of the 1-min MDP was based on recent research on the MDP, that has consistently demonstrated that the physical demands decline as the time window for analysis increases. Studies comparing different windows (e.g., 1-, 3-, 5-, and 10-min) have shown that the 1-min window captures the highest peaks of physical intensity. Consequently, longer windows tend to mask these critical peaks by averaging high-intensity efforts with subsequent recovery phases, thereby underestimating true match intensity [36]. Therefore, selecting the 1-min window is extremely relevant, as it captures the periods of greatest physical, technical and tactical demands that characterize elite performance, while the subsequent moments reflect the players’ recovery processes and adjustment following these MDPs.

Variables

Based on previous studies of kinematic MDP analysis, TD, MDPk, MSR, HSR, SPR performance variables were chosen to quantify the physical match performance of players in this study [21, 22]. Additionally, 16 variables were selected to represent the technical (2 individual variables; 4 team variables) and tactical (6 individual variables; 4 team variables) match performance of players (Table 1). The individual variables pertain exclusively to player behavior, whereas the team variables reflect collective behavioral patterns observed across the designated time periods. Regarding the selection of the tactical variables, the aim was to characterize both team and individual tactical behaviors. At the team level, the percentage of time in ball-in-play (BiPPerc) and ball-in-play in possession (AttPerc) was used to quantify the proportion of effective playing time overall and with the ball [37]. The percentage of team possessions (PsnTeam) reflected the team’s overall control [38], while the number of possession changes lasting more than five seconds (PsnChg) represented transition frequency and game volatility, which are associated with match intensity and tactical transitions. The possessions were considered only if they were above 5 s to make sure they were consistent for all the analysis. The possession during contesting balls could always changing, and to make sure the team really lost the ball, 5 s was the time defined using previous research. An early study excluded all possessions lasting less than 3 s, reasoning that such brief sequences contribute little to understanding structured team play [39] Subsequently, different authors analyzed goal-scoring possessions during open play and found that the shortest possessions in all groups were under five seconds [40] More recently, a systematic review reported that ball-possession duration is typically classified into three categories—0–5 s, 5–12 s, and more than 12 s—with nearly 80% of possessions exceeding 5 s [37].

Selected tactical and technical performance-related variables

At the individual level, the Spatial Exploration Index (SpExIndex) captured the extent of players’ spatial dispersion and exploratory behavior, providing insight into positional adaptability and space utilization [41]. The Defensive and Offensive Direction (DefDrct, OffDrct) quantified the proportion of movements along the axis toward the team’s own or opponent’s goal, respectively, as indicators of tactical intent and transition behavior. Finally, the percentage of time each player spent in the own, central, and opponent thirds of the pitch (PlrOwn3rd, PlrCntrl3rd, PlrOpp3rd) was calculated to represent spatial occupation patterns linked to location [40]. Together, these variables provide a comprehensive framework linking possession context, spatial behavior, and movement direction to the physical demands of the MDP.

Statistical analysis

The generalized linear mixed modeling (GLMM) was conducted using the academic version of the statistical analysis system (SAS® OnDemand for Academics). The GLMM models estimated the mean values for each physical, technical and tactical performance-related parameter in the MDP and Post periods. A non-clinical magnitude-based inference approach was used to determine changes in the mean of each parameter of the MDP and Post period. Estimated magnitudes and their confidence limits were expressed in standardized units, where standardization was achieved by calculating the difference between the mean values of Post and MDP, divided by the standard deviation of MDP. Effect sizes were evaluated qualitatively using the following scale: values < 0.2 were considered trivial, between 0.2 and 0.6 small, between 0.6 and 1.2 moderate, between 1.2 and 2.0 large, and > 2.0 very large [42]. Effects were considered clear if the 99% confidence interval did not include both positive (> 0.2) and negative (< -0.2) values simultaneously [43]. Clear effects were reported with a qualitative probability that the true effect was substantial or trivial, using the following scale: most unlikely (< 0.5%), very unlikely (0.5–5%), unlikely (5–25%), possibly (25–75%), likely (75–95%), very likely (95–99.5%), and most likely (> 99.5%) [42].

Discussion

This study aims to understand changes in physical, tactical, and technical performance during (MDP) and after (Post) the kinematic MDP. The main findings were: (1) in the Post, all players exhibited a marked decrease in external load across all physical variables (TD = -48%; MDPk = -89%; MSR = -84%; HSR = -93%; SPR = -97%), with similar patterns for each position on the field; (2) in the Post period, a substantial decline in all technical variables were observed across all positions, especially in passes for WM and CF and shots for CF; (3) in terms of all the team variables, regardless of positions, they decreased in the Post, especially for Ball in Play Percentage and number of Possession Changes; and (4) individual tactical behavior results showed that CD, FB and CM spent more time in their own third in the Post and running more towards their own goal in the MDP while WM and CF spent more time in opponent third in the Post.

In this study, MDP was identified using a 1-min rolling average analysis, quantifying the greatest distance performed above 15 km/h.1 As expected, HSR and SPR distances showed a very large decrease in the Post compared to the MDP. A previous study showed a similar significant decrease in HSR after the peak, of 64% to 89% for the different time windows (1-, 5- and 10-min) [24]. Regarding TD, the decrease was considerable smaller than the other variables, suggesting that, in line with previous studies, the greatest differences between peak and post periods are observed in high-intensity actions (i.e. HSR, SPR). Similar results were found in previous studies with higher windows (5 min) [11]. Suggesting that players reduce their running distances in higher intensities after the MDP period, that allows them to make positional adjustments and recover [44]. Indicating that these temporary decreases in running distances could be influenced by several factors, such as tactical changes, pacing strategy, fatigue, or stoppages of a match. A more accurate interpretation of these changes is only possible through a better contextual understanding of the specific situation [45, 46].

A recent study reported significantly greater HSR (15–20 km/h) and very high speed running (20–24 km/h) in the 1-min peak compared to the comparable peak ball in play (BIP) period of the respective variables [47]. The authors suggest that during the BiP passages, changes in tactical context (e.g. transitional phases, ball possession, etc.) may require players to reposition themselves to retain tactical stability, which may underpin such differences. Nevertheless, the analysis of physical performance between the periods while controlling for effective time, on the one hand, may provide evidence of less activity performed within a similar time-frame, which may be explained by the onset of transient fatigue development [29]. On the other hand, the sole analysis of BiP periods (i.e. effective time periods with periods of stoppages removed) may in effect underestimate physical demands between the periods.

In fact, while the decrement in high-intensity activity observed between MDP and Post period may be attributed to many factors, the results of this study clearly indicate a change in players’ tactical behaviors between these periods. In the Post, CD and FB spent more time in their own third, but CD move more in the direction of the opposite goal, while CF spent more time in the opponent third and move more in the direction of their own goal. Suggesting that after high-intensity periods, teams often adjust their collective positioning to conserve energy without compromising tactical effectiveness [48, 49]. This is likely to reflect a compensatory mechanism to conserve energy and reduce the frequency of high-intensity actions [50]. Such adaptations indicate a re-prioritization of strategic objectives, particularly emphasizing defensive stability and risk reduction [51]. These tactical modifications may involve reducing high pressing, increasing defensive compactness, or varying offensive transition speeds, all of which help maintain control of the game while reducing physical demands. In this sense, tactical adjustments act as load management strategies that help explain the drop in physical output without necessarily indicating a decline in performance.

Although such observations may be due to changes in the tactical context between periods, it has also been previously suggested that players may be experiencing transient fatigue in the period following the MDP [25, 29, 29, 52]. In this sense, our data supports the finding of a previous study that, under fatigue conditions, the influence of these interactions becomes more pronounced, players demonstrate a tendency to conserve energy through conservative tactical decisions [52, 53]. Simultaneously, effort self-regulation emerges as an adaptive strategy, especially among experienced players, who adjust their intensity based on subjective perceptions of fatigue, tactical demands, and match context [24]. Therefore, the interaction between transient fatigue and effort self-regulation should be considered when interpreting post-MDP decreases in physical output.

Practitioners usually use total match data to prepare players or competitions, and in this regard, the MDP should not be overlooked. While it has been argued that relying solely on MDP data for training may only prepare athletes for the average MDP demands, while optimal performance emerges not from the isolated functioning of physical, technical, or tactical domains, but from their continuous integration and adaptive modulation throughout the match. Having contextual data regarding technical and tactical actions may more effectively inform training design and prescription, allowing for a more individualized and adaptive approach to performance preparation. The integration of these findings enables coaching staff to develop training tasks that closely reproduce the physical loads of the MDP, while aligning them with players’ individual technical and tactical objectives. Moreover, by understanding player behaviors during and after the MDP, coaches can optimize training design to enhance performance during and facilitate more effective recovery strategies after.

While the present study provides valuable insights into the most demanding passages using multifactorial kinematic variables, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The dataset was drawn exclusively from a single tournament, the World Cup, which may limit its generalizability, as it does not account for contextual factors that could affect the measured values, such as competition level, team style, or environmental conditions. Additionally, the MDP was identified using transformed positional data, focusing solely on kinematic measures. This approach may not capture all high-intensity actions, particularly short, explosive efforts such as rapid changes of direction, physical contacts, or brief accelerations in congested areas, potentially underestimating the true physical demands. Moreover, contextual factors, including individual player characteristics, opposition strategies, and match location, may further influence these demands [54].

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that in the period after 1-min MDP there was a clear reduction in the players’ activity, especially regarding high-intensity running and sprinting distances. This suggests that players may assume a pacing strategy after these high intense periods, which may indicate clear behavior regardless of the position on the field. Our analysis revealed a substantial decline in all technical variables across all playing positions during the Post period. Notably, this reduction was most prominent in passes for WM and CF, and in shots for CF. Regarding tactical variables, a notable shift was observed across player roles. During the MDP, CD, FB, and CM appeared to perform more defensive movements. Furthermore, in the Post, WM and CF were positioned closer to the opponent’s goal.

Furthermore, this study provided valuable insights into MDP and Post during match play, supporting its value as a key metric for assessing physical match performance in soccer. Quantifying the differences between MDP and Post not only in physical, but also in technical and tactical performances provide deeper context to the data. Such contextualization allows coaches to better use the MDP data at the individual level, understanding how their physical, technical and tactical performance changes and supporting data-driven decision-making in training and competition planning.
