PPAC: Predicting Protein–Protein Affinity Changes Induced by Amino Acid Mutations Using Protein Large Language Models
Authors: Leilei Zhang, Xiaofei Zhou, Lu Liang, Jianping Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding the impact of amino acid mutations on protein–protein binding free energy is fundamental to drug design and functional biology. We propose a novel approach utilizing Protein Large Language Models (PLMs) to characterize both wild-type and mutant proteins, enabling accurate predictions of mutational effects. We employed three state-of-the-art PLMsEsm2, EsmC, and ProtT5to generate sequence-based representations. These representations were subsequently integrated into seven distinct model architectures. Through a rigorous 5-fold cross-validation, we selected the optimal model and feature combination before training on a large-scale data set. Our results show that this PLM-based method significantly outperforms traditional approaches, achieving state-of-the-art (SOTA) predictive performance. The final PPAC model was evaluated on a test set of 9,558 data points and applied to two case studies. The results demonstrate that the model not only provides high-precision predictions but also exhibits a significant advantage in identifying key residues crucial for protein interactions, highlighting its effectiveness in protein interaction modeling.

Introduction

Protein–protein interactions (PPIs) form a complex molecular communication network within cells and are fundamental to numerous biological processes. They play a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis, regulating metabolic pathways, transmitting signals, controlling the cell cycle, and coordinating immune responses, among other essential functions. − Moreover, dysregulation of PPIs is closely associated with the onset and progression of a range of diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and autoimmune conditions. − These diseases often arise from alterations in protein structure or function, particularly mutations in amino acid sequences, which can disrupt or imbalance PPIs. Consequently, a thorough understanding of the regulatory mechanisms that govern PPIs, and how these mechanisms are altered in disease, is essential for unraveling the molecular pathogenesis of these conditions. Such knowledge is critical for identifying potential therapeutic targets and developing innovative treatment strategies, with both scientific and clinical significance.

Binding affinity (ΔG), also known as binding free energy, is a key physicochemical property that characterizes protein–protein interactions (PPIs). The change in binding affinity induced by mutations (ΔΔG) is a critical parameter for assessing the strength and stability of PPIs, and it serves as an important metric in protein engineering and drug design. This change is typically calculated by comparing the binding affinities of mutant and wild-type proteins.

Currently, a variety of experimental techniques have been developed to accurately measure ΔΔG. However, these methods face several challenges, primarily due to the time-consuming, labor-intensive nature of the procedures, as well as the high financial costs involved. ,− More critically, the vast diversity of amino acids creates an enormous combinatorial space for mutations, making it impractical to conduct comprehensive mutation scanning through traditional wet-lab methods within a reasonable time frame. Therefore, the development of efficient computational methods to predict the impact of mutations on PPI binding affinities is of paramount importance. Such methods not only reduce dependence on experimental techniques and accelerate the drug discovery process but also provide significant scientific and practical value in advancing both basic research and applied applications.

In this context, the development of computational biology has provided new perspectives for a deeper understanding of protein–protein interactions (PPIs). By leveraging computational simulations and machine learning techniques, it is now possible to predict, at the atomic level, how mutations affect protein binding affinity. This can offer theoretical guidance for experimental design, thereby optimizing resource allocation and accelerating the pace of scientific discovery. Currently, computational methods are broadly categorized into three areas:1) Molecular Simulation: Based on molecular dynamics (MD) and quantum chemistry (QC) theories, molecular simulations track the movement and interactions of atoms or molecules, revealing the behavior of wild-type and mutated molecular systems at an atomic level. , 2) Energy Statistics: Classical mechanics or statistical potential functions are used to estimate free energy changes. Typical tools in this field include FoldX, ,, BeAtMuSiC, and Rosetta. , FoldX and Rosetta perform calculations by considering multiple energy terms, whereas BeAtMuSiC utilizes statistical potential functions for prediction. 3) Artificial Intelligence: Artificial Intelligence models predict protein interactions and stability using structural or sequence features. Structural-based models like mCSM use atomic distance patterns and physicochemical properties, GeoPPI employs Graph Attention Networks (GAT) and Gradient Boosting Trees (GBT), and DiffAffinity integrates Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) and Invariant Point Attention (IPA) for enhanced accuracy. Sequence-based models include INPS, applying SVMs with BLOSUM62 scores and hydrophobicity; SAAFEC-Seq, combining evolutionary information with GBT for predictions; and ACDC-NN-Seq, using CNNs for sequence features.

Although AI-based methods have demonstrated promising performance across various data sets, they still face several challenges. Structure-based models may suffer from reduced prediction accuracy due to conformational instability induced by mutations, while sequence-based models are less susceptible to this issue. The ability of protein features to represent structure and function accurately, as well as variations in data distribution across different scenarios, remains critical factors affecting model efficacy. Therefore, selecting appropriate algorithms and features to build models with strong generalization capabilities is essential for improving prediction robustness.

Method

Data Set

The data sets utilized in this study originate from commonly used protein–protein interaction databases, namely the SKEMPI 1.0 database and the SKEMPI 2.0 database. SKEMPI compiles experimental data on changes in thermodynamic and kinetic parameters due to mutations in protein–protein complexes, which have 3D structures stored in the PDB. From the SKEMPI 1.0 database, a nonredundant set of interface mutations was obtained, encompassing 114 protein complexes with a total of 1,131 single-point mutations and 195 double-point mutations. Among these, the 1,131 single-point mutations were integrated into a separate data set referred to as S1131, ,, SKEMPI 2.0 is an updated version of the SKEMPI 1.0 database, consisting of 7,085 single- or multipoint mutations across 345 complexes, including data from three other databases (AB-Bind, , PROXiMATE, and dbMPIKT. A subset of single-point mutation data was extracted, with unusable data removed, leaving a total of 4184 records, referred to as SKEMPI-Single (Supporting Information Table S1).

Lu et al. collected over ten million data points from 20 studies, covering 41 protein–protein complexes, named BindingGYM. We curated 31,772 single-point mutation data points from 23 protein complexes, and used the MMseq2 , software to remove data with more than 25% similarity to the training set. Finally, we retained 9,558 data points from 13 protein complexes,referred to as BindingGYM-Single.

Additionally, two external validation data sets were used for case studies, which are not present in the training set. The first is a data set of 19 single-point mutations in the interleukin-4 and its receptor alpha chain complex (PDB ID: 1IAR) collected from literature, − referred to as IR-19. The second data set includes mutations in the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein binding region with ACE2 (PDB ID: 7KMB), these mutations may affect the binding capacity of the virus to host cells, thereby having significant implications for the virus’s transmission and infectivity. , Guo et al. found that the S19P mutation in ACE2 enhances the binding affinity of RBD and may serve as a potential target for antibody development. Therefore, we extracted all mutation information at position 19 from the literature , and named it the SARS-ACE2-data set.

Data Partitioning

In all training processes of this study, the GroupKFold cross-validation scheme was adopted, with grouping based on the ECOD homology level (“H-level”) of proteins, ,, The specific steps were as follows: First, we uploaded the protein structures in the et to the ECOD server to determine the domain classification for each complex. Since the ECOD homology level (“H-level”) is a rigorous similarity metric that effectively clusters even distantly related protein domains, we chose this criterion as the basis for grouping. Next, we grouped the proteins according to their domain classifications, ensuring that samples from the same protein domain were not included in the same fold. This strategy was designed to minimize homology bias during the cross-validation process, thereby enhancing the accuracy and reliability of model evaluation.

Feature Extraction

First Set of FeaturesEsm

Esm2_t36_3B_UR50D (abbreviated as Esm) is an improved and optimized version, based on the Generative Pretrained Transformer architecture. This model leverages sequence information from a vast protein database to model protein sequences through self-supervised learning. It is capable of extracting highly representative feature vectors from protein sequences to capture the interactions and structural information between amino acids. Additionally, the model utilizes a Transformer architecture, which includes multiple self-attention mechanism layers, allowing it to adaptively focus on relevant information at different positions when processing protein sequences. Furthermore, the model introduces techniques such as residual connections and layer normalization to enhance its performance and training efficiency. In this study, protein sequence features are obtained by loading the Esm model, labeled as Ew (for wild-type proteins) and Em (for mutant proteins), both with a dimension of 2560.

In 2024, EvolutionaryScale launched the ESM3 series, along with the ESM C parallel series, which was open-sourced on December 4, 2024. The official description of ESMC states that it is “focused on obtaining hidden biological information on proteins through representation.” In this study, we used ESMC-600 M as the representation large model, ultimately obtaining a 1152-dimensional representation.

Second Set of FeaturesProt

ProtT5-XL-UniRef50 (abbreviated as Prot) is pretrained on large-scale protein sequence data, leveraging the advantages of self-supervised tasks and the Transformer architecture to capture high-level semantics and patterns in protein sequences. It uses 1024-dimensional embedding vectors to encode the residue information on protein sequences, thereby abstracting each position in the sequence into a vector representation. These embedding vectors are able to capture the interactions between residues and important features in the sequence, providing a rich representation of protein sequences. From these embedding vectors, biological properties such as function, structure, and interactions in protein sequences can be understood. This makes it an efficient tool for predicting protein stability and interactions. , In this study, the protein sequence features are acquired by loading the Prot model and are designated as Pw (wild-type proteins) and Pm (mutant proteins), both boasting a dimensionality of 1024.

This study overcomes the limitations of structural data by leveraging sequence features, addressing the prediction bias caused by the lack of high-resolution structures and conformational dynamics in protein–protein interactions (PPIs). Pretrained language models (PLMs) implicitly encode information such as sequence context, local conformation, and evolutionary conservation through tasks like “masked language modeling” and “residue contact map prediction.” By utilizing attention mechanisms, these models capture interactions between residues at the binding interface, compensating for the lack of spatial relationship modeling. When mutations occur, the model detects structural remodeling signals through changes in representation vectors (such as interface exposure and hydrogen bonding potential), allowing for the accurate simulation of the “mutation-structure change-affinity” cascade effect.

Model Architecture and Training

Due to the inherent feature complexity of large models, integrating more complex models may lead to high computational resource consumption and a higher risk of overfitting. Therefore, we have opted for a streamlined Artificial Neural Network (ANN) architecture, which not only enhances computational efficiency but also mitigates the risk of overfitting. This approach facilitates easier adjustment and optimization, enabling rapid validation of specific tasks. The ANN model employed is a multilayer perceptron (MLP) consisting of five fully connected layers, with the intermediate hidden layers having dimensions of 128 and 256 neurons. The final layer is used to generate the output predictions. Each hidden layer uses the ReLU activation function. This architecture progressively extracts features from the input data, gradually reducing dimensionality before generating the final predictions. Additionally, other models such as GBRT, SVM, RF, DT, KNN, and CNN were also explored. A detailed description of these models can be found in Supporting Information 1.

During the training process, the complete sequence of the protein is fed into the large models to extract features. Notably, both large models, Esm and Prot, are capable of accommodating protein sequences of varying lengths. The representation differences between the mutant and wild-type proteins (Em-Ew, Pm-Pw) are computed to capture the alterations resulting from mutations. These differences are subsequently input into the ANN for training. Model performance is assessed via 5-fold cross-validation, and the optimal parameters are selected based on the performance of the validation set. Detailed parameter ranges and results are presented in Supporting Information Table S2.

Definition of Binding Affinity Change (ΔΔG)

Binding affinity change (ΔΔG) refers to the difference in binding free energy between two different states. In the context of protein–protein interactions, ΔΔG is used to quantify the impact of protein mutations on the stability of the interaction between two proteins. 1 ΔΔG=ΔGmt−ΔGwt

A negative value of ΔΔG indicates an increase in binding affinity between the two proteins, while a positive value indicates a decrease in binding affinity.

Results of Cross-Validation on S1131

To further validate the stability and generalization ability of the ESM-ANN combination, we conducted 10-fold cross-validation under the same training strategy (Supporting Information Table S3). The results consistently showed that ESM features outperformed Prot and ESMC features in both RMSE and PCC, and ANN continued to outperform other models across all three feature representations. By increasing the number of folds, we ensured the model’s stability across different data splits and further eliminated the possibility of overfitting. Furthermore, we compared our approach with several existing methods (e.g., Topnettree, MuToN, DGCΔΔG, etc.), and the results show that our approach outperforms the others across all metrics (Supporting Information Table S4). Based on these validation results, we selected the ESM-ANN framework and proceeded with subsequent training. The superior performance of the ESM-ANN framework can be attributed to two key factors. First, its simple architecture mitigates the risk of overfitting and reduces parameter redundancy; second, it directly leverages the core biological knowledge encoded in the ESM-2 large model, enhancing predictive performance while maintaining efficiency.

Cross-Validation Results

We next conducted a 5-fold cross-validation on the larger SKEMPI-Single data set to assess the performance of our model. To begin, we obtained protein domain information using ECOD, and then divided the data set into five nonoverlapping subsets based on domain differences. This partitioning ensured that each subset was structurally independent, with no overlap in proteins between subsets (Data and code availability). This approach allowed for a more equitable evaluation of the model’s generalization ability.

In addition, we compared our model’s performance against other baseline models selected from various categories, including energy-based, sequence-based, unsupervised, end-to-end, and pretrained ΔΔG prediction models. These models included Rosetta, flex ddG, ESM-1v, ESM-IF, ESM2, PSSM, DDGPred, RDE-Net, DiffAffinity, and DDAffinity.

As shown in Table , among the baseline models, DiffAffinity exhibited the best performance in terms of both RMSE and PCC, with an RMSE of 1.288 and a PCC of 0.672. However, our model PPAC outperformed DiffAffinity on both of these key metrics, demonstrating its superior performance, with an RMSE of 0.732 and a PCC of 0.860. This result highlights the superior capabilities of our model and emphasizes the significant advantages of using large-scale models for representation.

Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we explored the effectiveness of large-model-based protein In this paper, we explored the effectiveness of large-model-based protein representations in predicting changes in protein–protein binding affinity due to mutations. By utilizing Esm2_t36_3B_UR50D, ESMC_600M_2024_12_v0 and ProtT5-XL-UniRef50 to extract protein sequence features and applying a variety of machine learning and deep learning models, we trained and validated our models on the S1131 and SKEMPI data sets, ultimately selecting the best-performing model. 5-fold cross-validation and further validation on the BindingGYM-Single data set demonstrated the model’s outstanding performance, with the highest PCC ranking. In case studies involving the IL-4/IL-4Rα complex in immune-related research and ACE2 mutations in viral research, our model successfully predicted the changes in binding affinity, excelling in sensitive site identification, thus validating its superior performance and generalization ability.

Although our model demonstrates strong performance, there remains potential for further enhancement. A primary direction involves integrating conformational data by combining sequence-based pretrained language model (PLM) features with structural information (e.g., atomic distances, interface interactions) derived from advanced methods like AlphaFold. Additionally, predicting the ΔΔG of multipoint mutations presents a significant challenge due to nonadditive effects, structural complexity and data scarcity. Extending the capability of the PPAC framework to handle multipoint mutations is another key direction. Finally, the future research will focus on the interpretability of the model, such as the biological information captured by the model embedding.
