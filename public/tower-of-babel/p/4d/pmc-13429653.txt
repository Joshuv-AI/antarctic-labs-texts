Uncertainty analysis in the simulation of hydrological processes in Euphrates River Basin with contrasting climates
Authors: Saleh Hammad AL-Hammad, Halah Kadhim Tayyeh, Ruqayah Mohammed, Ahmed Mageed Hussein, Brahim Benzougagh, Amnah Alasqah, Khaled Mohamed Khedher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change is causing significant changes in the hydrology of the Euphrates River Basin, which has traditionally been one of the most important water basins in the region, due to climate change, changing surface runoff patterns, increasing water demand, and changing hydroclimate patterns (i.e., rainfall) over time. The use of hydrological models, therefore, plays an essential role in providing information to support decision-making regarding the use of water resources, and also in understanding how climate and operational changes affect the hydrology of the Euphrates River Basin. In this regard, we used the SWAT (Soil and Water Assessment Tool) model within ArcGIS to simulate the hydrology of the Euphrates River Basin. This is the first time that this model has been applied at the scale of the Euphrates River. Our findings show that by using 16 key parameters to calibrate the model, we were able to attain a greater degree of accuracy in simulating monthly discharge from the Birecik Dam to the Haditha Dam on the Euphrates River. Evidence suggests that the parameters SOL_BD, RCHRG_DP, GWQMN, and ESCO were found to be relatively more sensitive when compared with the more commonly used CN2 parameter to simulate hydrology (contrary to earlier findings). Our results indicate that the model was able to achieve satisfactory performance indicators during both the calibration and validation stages of the project. The calibration results for R², NS, and RSR were 0.83, 0.77, and 0.46; whereas the validation results were 0.87, 0.83, and 0.23. The uncertainty indices were greater during the validation period (probability coefficient 81% and correlation coefficient 0.91) than during the calibration period (probability coefficient 74% and correlation coefficient 0.87), indicating an increase in the reliability of the hydrological forecasts during the validation period. Additional findings of this study were the accurate estimation of the average annual depth of spatial runoff over 29 sub-basins, including point-source discharges, which is a key aspect of advanced hydrological studies. Introduction

Hydrological models are frequently used all over the world to model different types of hydrologic processes (such as the quantity and quality of streamflow) within watershed boundaries1,2. The establishment of gauging stations at multiple locations to collect long-term streamflow (flow) measurements is cost, labor, and time-intensive, thereby making hydrological models an important tool to fill gaps in available data to help accurately analyze hydrologic trends where continuous streamflow measurements are not available3,4. Rainfall and runoff are interconnected events that impact sediment erosion projections. Hydrological Procedures (Hydrologic Models) simulate how hydrological processes change over time and across geographic areas; the types of Watersheds (Watersheds) and Watershed Size (Size) are the major types of Watershed Models. Hydrological Procedures are often based on Physical Models and some on Empirical Models, depending on their complexity, accuracy and the degree to which the model serves the user. The most important of these models is Soil and Water Assessment Tool (SWAT)5. A process-driven, distributed model for continuing hydrological research in river basins is SWAT6–8. Remarkably, it emerges as the most extensively employed instrument for mimicking the repercussions of managerial actions and climate shifts on hydrological dynamics in watershed magnitudes9,10. United States Department of Agriculture Research Service (USDARS) has enhanced its modeling skills over period of more than thirty years, which were utilized in the development of SWAT11–13. Model tuning is a critical step in evaluating the water balance of a watershed; in fact, it is a requirement for sustainable management of water resources14,15. As hydrologists have the goal of maximizing model adjustments to produce the most accurate models for simulating both water quantity and quality, this area of research has gained increased attention within hydrology. However, developing hydrologic models results in many uncertainties due to the large spatial heterogeneity and multiple parameters that need to be input across a large range16,17. These errors can result in both the overestimation and underestimation of the hydrologic processes, which may lead to incorrect choices18–20. Consequently, calibrating and addressing uncertainty in hydrologic models is necessary to enhance the accuracy of hydrologic model simulating capacity21–23. To achieve successful calibration and examination of uncertainties, various techniques exist. The calibration of hydrological models includes many different techniques and strategies that assess the uncertainties related to these models and confirm that the models are accurate and can be dependably relied upon. The majority of the techniques used to calibrate hydrological models include combining SUFI-2 (Sequential Uncertainty Fitting 2) with SWAT Calibration Uncertainty Procedures (SWAT-CUP). These two sets of methods represent the most widely accepted methodology for performing Sensitivity Analyses and analyzing Hydrology Parametrizations and Calendars, validating Hydrological Models, calibrating Hydrological Parameters, and analyzing uncertainties in Hydrological Models24–26. Therefore, because the SUFI-2 method provides a partly automated solution to the calibration of hydrological models, it streamlines the process of calibration and enables the process to be conducted within realistic time limits27. Furthermore, the inclusion of many parameters in a Hydrological Model makes the Calibration Process complicated and very time-consuming when conducted manually, providing a large amount of attention from the research community28,29. Sensitivity Analysis is a critical step to ensure effective and dependable predictive Hydrology Models. This step helps identify and rank the key variables that have the greatest effect on the results or output of interest to a Hydrology Model30–32. The techniques members used for sensitivity analysis within the framework for SUFI-2 include Local Sensitivity analysis using One-At-a-Time (OAT) approaches and/or Global Sensitivity analysis methods2. The OAT technique for sensitivity analysis involves the repeated single variation of model input parameters with each successive test of only one of the model parameters by a predetermined ratio, while the rest of the input parameters remain unchanged from their original, representative inputs, in order to determine how sensitive each of the individual input parameters is managing the model output response33–37 OAT sensitivity analysis techniques were viewed as being less credible since perturbing one parameter can cause it to deviate from its nominal parameter value and therefore introduce uncertainty into the outcome of the sensitivity analysis. Conversely, the Global Sensitivity analysis Techniques, which perturbation method takes into consideration how all input parameters have been changed/varied during the entire cooperative model simulation, allows users to make simultaneous changes to each model input parameter under consideration when assessing the interaction of multiple parameters and their effect on the model’s output results38,39. This allows for the ability to conduct a sensitivity analysis on the full range of values to which each model input parameter was subjected to; therefore it allows for thorough identification of relationships amongst model input parameters40. Both techniques yield dissimilar results41,42, stressing the necessity of properly and comprehensively conducting a thorough assessment of the sensitivity of model input parameters43. Many factors, including parameters sensitivity, are vital for effective SWAT modeling. Numerous factors influence the success of the SWAT model calibration, such as parameter sensitivity, simulation numbers, iteration counts, and parameter uncertainties. Due to the limited transboundary geographic representation of Turkey, Syria, and Iraq in previous studies, as well as the lack of quantitative integration of point-source discharges and parameter uncertainty assessment, this research addresses these important gaps through the development of an integrated SWAT modeling framework for the entire Euphrates River Basin. The research integrates dam operation, point-source discharges, and hydroclimatic variability and its effects on hydrological basin-scale processes within the Euphrates Basin. There have only been a few studies done before that examined the entire length of the Euphrates Basin from Turkey to Iraq and within the SWAT model with regulated flow conditions and uncertainty analysis as well.

The objective of this research is to develop and implement a SWAT model at the basin level for the transboundary Euphrates River Basin, incorporate data from dams and point sources discharging into the river in conjunction with the model, and also calibrate and validate the model using historical discharge records collected upstream from Birecik Dam and downstream from Haditha Dam. In addition, an extensive sensitivity and uncertainty analysis will be performed on key hydrological parameters that affect how runoff is produced, and the effect of hydroclimatic variability on estimating both spatially and annually distributed runoff volumes will be assessed in 29 sub-basins. This integrated modeling approach will improve the reliability of hydrological simulations and improve our understanding of SWAT model behavior for semi-arid, regulated river systems.

Site selection

In western Asia’s Euphrates River Basin (ERB), the present research was undertaken as described in Fig. 1. The Euphrates River is one of the longest rivers in Asia, measuring approximately 2,786 km in length. Encompassed within the Euphrates River Basin is an area of some 440,000 km2 in size. Starting from the Keban Dam in Türkiye and proceeding through the complete area of Syrian territory within the Euphrates River Basin and ending at the Haditha Dam in Iraq, the site of this research has been defined to be between the latitudinal limits of (34–37° north) and the longitudinal limits of (37–42° east), and has an array of different climates. As such, the area was divided into five climatic subsectors (i.e., semi-arid, arid, humid, dry sub-humid, and hyper-arid) based on the general characteristics of their climate and geography36 (Fig. 2). The watershed exists at elevations ranging from 97 m to 913 m above sea level. The average temperature during the summer months in the Euphrates River Basin are highly elevated and very little moisture from precipitation is received at the lower elevations, but the winter months are milder and they receive more precipitation than what is received during the summer. In the Iraqi portions of the ERB, a humid subtropical climate dominates, featuring semi-arid characteristics and hot summers that render these areas susceptible to downstream desertification. Rainfall across the basin displays variation, with yearly averages ranging from 930 to 1150 mm upstream, gradually diminishing to an average of 120 mm downstream44,45. In the river’s upstream section, the average annual maximum temperature stands at 15.5 °C, while the minimum temperature averages around 1.3 °C. On the other hand, downstream areas exhibit higher temperatures, with a low of 9.3 °C and an average yearly maximum of 27.5 °C.

Spatial data preparation

Physical modelling must be done using spatial databases to represent surface elevation, land use, and soil information. These watershed data are modelled within the ArcGIS and SWAT environments.

Digital Elevation Model (DEM)

Topography is one of SWAT hydrology model’s most important input data components. It is utilized for watershed delineation, enabling the model to determine drainage patterns and analyze the features of the land surface. DEM is a critical component of this topographic data, as it directly impacts the movement and direction of flow rates over the land surface46. For this investigation, A DEM with a (30*30) m resolution was employed. NASA supplied DEM data in (.tif) ESRI Format GIS (Geographic Information System), along with a World Geodetic System 1984 or WGS84 projection. The DEM has been utilized to generate surface slope data that can be used to determine flow direction in the basin47.

Land cover data

One of the most critical inputs into SWAT for modeling runoff is land use and land cover (LULC) data; because of this, having reliable, high-resolution spatial inputs for LULC was crucial to the success of the project. LULC data which were pre-labeled from 2024 Sentinel-2 imageries were used for this research as they are available through the proper agency and are capable of providing spatial information in resolutions as great as 10 m. Because this data set was already pre-labeled, no additional classification takes place within the research performed for this report; rather, the already-labeled dataset was used directly, resulting in an image of the area that meets the requirement specified by the reviewer. Previous research48,49 has shown that Sentinel-2 labeled products can exhibit accuracy rates as great as 95%, thus making them reliable for representing the distribution of spatial LULC patterns and justifying their use in hydrologic analyses.

Soil data

Basic soil characteristics play a vital role in determining how a River basin will respond hydrologically50. For this reason, it is important to know as much about the soils in order to model presumably runoff correctly with the ArcSWAT2012 model. To accurately portray the soils within each soil unit, ArcSWAT2012 requires many types of physical and chemical properties for the various layers in the soil, including texture, bulk density, saturated hydraulic conductivity, available water capacity, moisture content, and organic carbon content. To meet the requirements of the references, we explain that all these parameters were extracted directly from the FAO Soil Classification System database (2003). Soil units in the FAO map were first matched with their associated property tables, and then these properties were converted to the standard format required by the SWAT model (SWAT Soil Database Format). These values ​​were incorporated into the soil profiles used in the simulations to ensure a realistic representation of water movement through the soil profile, thereby improving the estimation of infiltration and surface runoff.

Hydro climate data preparation

The process of hydrological modeling requires the utilization of three essential datasets: a spatial dataset, daily climate data, and either monthly hydrological datum, which is employed for the purpose of calibration51.

Weather data

National meteorological agencies provide easy access to daily climatic data for different regions within ERB. However, the high collection costs have made it increasingly difficult to get meteorological data for some places52. Additionally, the varied land use and variances in energy transmission mechanisms are blamed for the subpar density of ground stations in some areas53. With funding from NASA Earth Science, researchers utilized virtual stations provided by NASA POWER (NP), as a workable substitute for dispersed and unavailable ground station data in order to address this data shortage issue54. These virtual stations, launched on 20 December 2018, proved an excellent substitute. Climate factors from 1982 to 2022 include precipitation, humidity, wind speed, solar radiation, maximum and lowest temperatures.

Hydrological data of dams, stations and point source

Monthly records were collected from the Hasiba Hydrological Station for the period 2000–2018. This station is an official station of the Iraqi Ministry of Water Resources and is considered one of the stable and reliable sites along the Euphrates River. The United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia (ESCWA) also provided additional point source data, including springs resulting from snowmelt in the upper mountainous areas of the basin, discharges from wastewater treatment plants after purification, and data on releases from the main dam reservoirs within the studied section. This data is contained in the SWAT model as point sources using .ptq files, which include location and monthly discharge values for each point source. This provides a realistic representation of the hydrological system within the data file. SWAT model framework

The SWAT model provides a semi-distributed, dynamic system for simulating large basins and assessing hydrological processes over extended time periods. The SWAT model accounts for the numerous spatial effects that different land cover types, soil types, and climactic factors (e.g., temperature, rainfall, wind speed, relative humidity, etc.) exert and is able to do this by using a range of input data types from remote sensing and GIS sources55. The SWAT model allows for a detailed representation of any given watershed using multiple analysis levels as well as a very efficient means of analyzing input data and providing effective representation of soil traits and land use types56. GIS software that has been used to support this process, particularly ArcGIS 10.5, has been invaluable in identifying hydrological characteristics and processing all of the various spatial datasets that are required to model a watershed within the ArcSWAT 2012 platform; the interface provides an excellent means of providing both a graphical and fast way to access this analysis and provide graphical outputs for prediction purposes. The SWAT model was applied to the Euphrates River Basin and used to simulate hydrological behaviours of the boardering area, including important hydrological processes (e.g., surface runoff, infiltration, groundwater, and basal runoff). Estimates of evapotranspiration (ET) formed a major portion of water balance and groundwater recharge calculations for SWAT estimates, especially since most semi-arid areas are extremely sensitive to ET estimates. Based on the physiological and climatic sensitivities associated with ET estimation methods, this study used the Penman-Monteith equation to help determine ET accurately since it has been proven to accurately predict the influence of numerous different climatic factors and determine the extent of surface runoff formation.

Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

The SWAT software application (SWAT-CUP) was used to carry out the analytical procedure once the basic SWAT model was established. This entailed performing sensitivity, calibration, validation tests, and addressing uncertainties to ensure the model’s reliability and accuracy.

Sensitivity analysis

The user’s objective is to identify parameters significantly impacting the model’s output results during the calibration process using SWAT-CUP. By conducting parameter sensitivity analysis, in order to simplify calibration and sensitivity analysis, the goal is to reduce quantity of input variables. Two main methods for sensitivity analysis are available: Automated Latin Hypercube method (LH-OFAT) and the global sensitivity analysis procedure58. Although the global sensitivity approach is more reliable at delivering findings, it was used for this investigation. Protocol of hydrological parameters selection

Flow simulations using the SWAT model were conducted at various time scales, covering 2000 to 2015. The first two years (2000–2001) were dedicated to warming the model to ensure stability. Selecting sensitivity parameters in SWAT involves defining the problem by identifying specific focus parameters. Determine the model outputs to concentrate on, such as runoff. Select the parameter pool by creating a comprehensive list of all possible factors, such as the physical features of the watershed, that can influence the selected model outputs, land use, soil properties, hydrological coefficients, and climate variables. Properly classify and group all parameters that might play a role in understanding model outputs, referencing relevant existing literature, studies, and expert opinions to locate parameters that have previously been shown to have an effect within similar regions or watersheds. Subsequently, carry out the Morris screening procedure to assess each parameter to find out if any could greatly influence model output; use the results from the screening process to choose a smaller representative set of parameters that are more likely to affect model output and reduce computation time for future evaluations on these selected parameters. Select a suitable sampling procedure for use to obtain samples based on the selected sampling procedure; then, run the SWAT model with the parameter values obtained from these samples. Use these model output values to calculate sensitivity indices (first-order (main effect) and total-order) for each parameter and rank by index value for each parameter. The performance validation stage of calibration evaluates the model’s robustness and reliability using a different climatic period than the calibration period. The goal of validation is to determine the model’s capacity to accurately replicate stream flow along all parts of a river under multiple climatic conditions. Uncertainty

Significant uncertainties in watershed modelling can be attributed to various sources, including observed and measured data, conceptual models, parameter uncertainties, and input variables utilized in the hydrological model. SUFI-2 technique is commonly employed to address these uncertainties, providing a 95% prediction uncertainty (95PPU) range from model outputs63. P- and the R-factor, which are necessary to get optimal model outcomes, define 95PPU range. P-factor, However, indicates proportion of observed data that falls within the 95PPU range, R-factor shows how consistent range. Ideally, there should be little substance in the majority of the observed data. For flow rate calibration, a P-factor of at least 0.7 and an R-factor of around 1 are recommended64. When compared to alternative methods, the Latin Hypercube Sampling methodology from SUFI-2 provides the best P-factor (prediction uncertainty ranges) and R-factor (relative coverage of measurements), hence it was utilized in this work for calibration and uncertainty evaluation65. This approach ensures a more robust and reliable handling of uncertainties in the watershed modelling process. Sensitivity of model parameters

The Euphrates River basin flow was modeled using the SUFI-2 Technique, and a parameter sensitivity analysis was performed. Before starting the analysis, the researchers used the hydrological model system’s parameter selection protocol to select 21 parameters carefully. The results in Table 2 illustrate the statistical characteristics of these parameters. The absolute values of the t-statistic ranged from 0.08 to 11.33, while the P-factor ranged from 0 to 0.81. Using an established p-value criterion of < 0.05 as the standard for determining parameter significance, 16 of the original parameters passed this sensitivity test and will be the focus of the next phase of the study. The following description explains how 16 parameters work to describe the performance of the model regarding the Euphrates River Basin. This is shown in Figure 6 from a Hydrologic view. Each of these Parameters has specific functions that govern its inclusion in the overall model. SOL_BD refers to the horizontal and vertical movement of Water through the Soil, ESCO regulates Evaporation from the Soil to the atmosphere, GWQMN and RCHRG_DP define where Groundwater will be located, GW_DELAY and ALPHA_BNK will establish how long Water will be in the base flow, and CN2, SLSUBBSN, and OV_N will produce Surface Water runoff from Precipitation events. SOL_K governs how the Water moves through the Soil and CH_N2 will provide the physical characteristics of the channel for conveying Channel flow. Due to their essential Functions, these Parameters play a significant role in providing accurate information regarding Flow dynamics in the study area66,67.

Analysis of the SWAT model for the Euphrates River basin, using SUFI-2 technology and parameter sensitivity analysis, revealed that climatic variations play a significant role in model performance and flow behavior across different sub-basins. By analyzing 21 hydrological parameters and selecting 16 based on sensitivity testing (p < 0.05), parameters such as CN2, ESCO, SOL_BD, GWQMN, and RCHRG_DP emerged as having the most significant impact on surface and groundwater runoff dynamics, which are directly influenced by climatic conditions such as rainfall, temperature, and evaporation. The performance of the overall model (as measured by R2, NASH, RSR and PBIAS) was determined to be acceptable based on both calibration and validation findings, but permanent contrast between wet and dry sub-basins has been discovered due to differences in rainfall and temperature over space affecting both surface runoff production and groundwater fluctuations. Therefore, the model systematically underpredicts the monthly volumes over most months of the year, particularly in the arid regions, indicating the impact of climatic variability on parameter variability and the manner in which the model responds to such climatic conditions.

Analyzing uncertainties in hydrological modeling

The SUFI-2 approach successfully captured monthly surface runoff patterns during calibration. Two rounds of simulations produced hydrographic charts for 95 PPU, i.e., 200 and then 1000 simulations. First, the results showed a high percentage of the observed monthly river flow data exhibiting significant agreement between the simulated and observed values, with 74% of the results falling within 95 PPU during calibration and 81% during validation. Second, the model’s reliability was confirmed by the R-factor values ​​of 0.87 and 0.91 for the calibration and validation tests, respectively as illustrated in Fig. 8. The simultaneous consideration of the R-factor and the P-factor provides a comprehensive view of both the calibration effectiveness and the assessment of uncertainties, given their inherent correlation. The data in this study indicate that an understanding of where uncertainty originates and how to evaluate the cause for bias is essential for the correct interpretation of model accuracy and reliability as well as its ability to simulate hydrological dynamics in each of the two study watersheds. The p-factor was shown to be within acceptable limits for both of these models, indicating that the level of uncertainty in each hydrological model will be reduced. The poor representation of the surface water dynamics within the sub-basins, as well as inter-basin water transfers, of the watershed systems may be the reason why both models produced very high r-factor values. Another point demonstrated by this analysis is that when changing the number of simulations under comparable conditions, a variety of results are obtained for uncertainty indices, which may affect how well the fit is assessed. Also, the use of two different simulations improved confidence in evaluating model uncertainty.

Spatial analysis of surface runoff

A comprehensive basin-scaled assessment of runoff in the Euphrates River Basin is achieved through an evaluation of hydrology at the basin level, as it reflects a spatially distributed approach to hydrology and takes into account inflows of tributaries, effects of reservoir regulation, impacts of point source discharges, and hydrologic response in 29 sub-basins using hydrology under different climate conditions. Including spatially-distributed temperatures and precipitation data into the simulation period greatly increases the ability to accurately represent hydroclimatic variability and is considered one of the most significant contributors to generating runoff in large semi-arid / trans-boundary basins44.

Figures 8 and 9 present the average annual runoff depth generated using ArcGIS and illustrate very well the effects of climatic gradients on the basin. For example, the humid / semi-humid sub-basins had average annual runoff depths of 415–640 mm, while the semi-arid sub-basins had average annual runoff depths of 205–301 mm. Similarly, the arid sub-basins had average annual runoff depths of 107–205 mm; however, the dry sub-basins had very low average annual runoff depths (1–54 mm). This pattern demonstrates the established non-linear relationship between precipitation inputs, ET demand and runoff production in terms of drought-prone environments, as a small reduction in the amount of precipitation can result in a larger reduction in the amount of runoff produced9.

Research conducted using the SWAT model in semi-arid regions has confirmed the trend of decreased runoff volume as a result of increased aridity, with rainfall variability and soil moisture dynamics being the primary controls on streamflow response68. Additionally, the incorporation of both reservoir releases and point-source discharges into the modeling framework resulted in a more accurate representation of the regulated flow regime, thus reducing the structural bias associated with many large-scale hydrologic models.

Conclusions

This study is the first to conduct a complete hydrological modeling of the Euphrates River Basin for the entire length of the river through Turkey (Birecik Dam) to Iraq (Haditha Dam). Hydrologic modeling was performed with an ArcGIS-enabled SWAT model, which was calibrated, validated, and assessed for hydrologic performance using 16 primary calibration parameters. It was determined in this investigation that the parameter choice for model calibration significantly enhanced the simulation accuracy of monthly discharge; however, model sensitivity analysis indicated that some parameters such as SOL_BD, RCHRG_DP, GWQMN and ESCO had a much more pronounced influence on generating runoff than the commonly recommended parameter CN2. These results differ from other studies’ comparison of parameter significance related to runoff development. The SWAT model yielded reasonable statistical accuracies in both calibration (R² = 0.83, NSE = 0.77, RSR = 0.46) and validation (R²=0.87, NSE = 0.83, RSR = 0.23), demonstrating substantial agreement between simulated and measured discharges.

The SWAT-CUP analysis uncertainty assessment indicates greater certainty for the validation period (p-factor = 81%, r-factor = 0.91) compared to the calibration period (p-factor = 74%, r-factor = 0.87). These findings support the validity of hydrological simulations as robust despite the considerable spatial size and hydroclimatic variability of the basin. The average annual spatial runoff depth of 29 sub-basins, including point-source discharges, was calculated using SWAT, and was improved by spatial integration with ArcGIS 10.5 for better interpretation and differentiation of runoff patterns. Additionally, the low runoff amounts observed during the calibration phase demonstrate how spatiotemporal variability of rainfall in addition to structural uncertainty in the models have a greater effect on hydrological projections in semi-arid regulated basins.

While the above contributions are significant, a few limitations should be considered in the analysis. First, the analysis was dependent upon the availability and spatial distribution of meteorological and hydrological monitoring data, which may have created instances of uncertainty for certain sub-regions of the basin. In addition, many micro-scale hydrological processes, such as localized evaporation processes or groundwater-surface water dynamic interactions, could not be adequately represented due to a lack of data. Finally, the assessment of potential future anthropogenic modifications to tributaries and reservoirs is also beyond the current modelling framework.

Future research should broaden the modeling framework to evaluate the long-term impacts of climate change using multiple climate scenarios and emission pathways. Including land-use change projections, along with human development scenarios, will enhance the accuracy of predicting runoff as well as performing water resource management planning. Additionally, improving the veracity of the model’s ability to simulate short-term variability in rainfall by improving the model will create a more reliable model for simulating extreme hydrological events, such as flash floods69–71.
