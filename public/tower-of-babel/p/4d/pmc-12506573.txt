A simple, rapid and cost-effective UHPLC–MS/MS method for simultaneous quantitation of seven antiepileptic drugs/metabolites in human serum
Authors: Xiaowei Fu, Xiangtang Li, Hui He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Quantifies seven anti-epileptic drugs (AEDs) in 20 µl serum in 4.5 min.•High accuracy and precision across all AEDs tested.•Enables same-day results for therapeutic drug monitoring.Keywords: Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS)

Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic non-communicable disease of the brain that affects around 50 million people worldwide [1]. During 2021 and 2022, about 2.9 million U.S. adults 18 and older [2] and about 456,000 U.S. children 17 and younger reported having active epilepsy [3]. It is estimated that up to 70 % of people living with epilepsy could live seizure-free if properly diagnosed and treated [1]. Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) are still the mainstay for treatment. With the newer generation of AEDs came the hope of not simply superior efficacy but also reduced adverse events and improved safety [4]. However, the narrow therapeutic ranges and the pharmacokinetics of the drugs show significant inter-individual variability. The use of combinations of different AEDs is also very common, especially in the pediatric population. About 1.5 million U.S. adults with active epilepsy have suboptimal seizure control [2], and many pediatric patients were not in the effective treatment range [5]. Therefore, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of AEDs is often necessary to optimize pharmacotherapy by maximizing efficacy and minimizing toxicity.

Various analytical methods have been used for AED monitoring, such as immunoassays [4,6], high-performance liquid chromatography with ultraviolet detection [7,8], liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) [9], and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) [5,10,11]. While immunoassays do not meet the detection requirements of AEDs, especially for new-generation AEDs due to cross-reactivity, the LC–MS/MS methodology has several advantages, including (a) higher selectivity, sensitivity, and throughput, (b) the ability to perform panel drug profiling by rapidly and simultaneously measuring many drugs and metabolites in a single sample, and (c) short analytical time and quicker turnaround time to minimize costs. These advantages ultimately contribute to improve the quality of patient care. As a result, LC–MS/MS used in combination with stable-isotope-labeled internal standards (ISs) is considered the gold standard nowadays [12]. LC–MS/MS instruments and methods have improvements compared to older published methods, e.g., simpler sample preparation, shorter run times, and improved sensitivity. The simultaneous, sensitive analysis of multiple drugs significantly reduces the required sample volume compared to other techniques, which is especially important among pediatric patients [12,13]. Levetiracetam (LEV), lamotrigine (LMT), lacosamide (LCM), zonisamide (ZON), oxcarbazepine (OXC) and clobazam (CLO) are among the most used AEDs in our Children’s Hospital. They are used alone or in combination to treat different types of epilepsy. OXC is indicated as monotherapy or adjunctive therapy in the treatment of partial and generalized tonic-clonic seizures in adults and children. Most of the absorbed OXC is converted into its active metabolite, 10-hydroxycarbazepine (OXC-M1). Herein, we report the development and validation of a UHPLC–MS/MS method to simultaneously quantify these AEDs and two metabolites, OXC-M1 and N-desmethyl clobazam (N-CLB, clobazam metabolite), with a simple sample extraction procedure (protein precipitation and dilution) and short analytical run time (4.5 min). Our method is accurate, simple, fast, cost-effective, and allows for routine therapeutic drug monitoring and dosing adjustments in short intervals.

Method

Chemicals

Levetiracetam (LEV), Lamotrigine (LMT), Lacosamide (LCM), Zonisamide (ZON), 10,11-Dihydro-10-hydroxycarbamazepine (OXC-M1), Clobazam (CLO), N-desmethyl clobazam (N-CLB, clobazam metabolite), and their corresponding isotope standards: Levetiracetam-D6 (LEV-D6), Lamotrigine-13C,15N4 (LMT-13C, 15N4), Lacosamide-13C, D3 (LCM-13C, D3), Zonisamide-13C6, (ZON-13C6), 10,11-Dihydro-10-Hydroxycarbamazepine-13C6 (OXC-M1-13C6) and Clobazam-13C6 (CLO-13C6) were all purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. GibcoTM dialyzed Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) One Shot Serum was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. All other common reagents and solvents, such as water and acetonitrile, etc. were mass spectrometry grade and were obtained from general commercial suppliers.

Calibrators, quality control samples, and internal standard solution

A stock solution containing 100 µg/ml LEV, 30 µg/ml LCM, 30 µg/ml LMT, 80 µg/ml ZON, 70 µg/ml OXC-M1 and 2 µg/ml CLO, 20 µg/ml N-CLB, was prepared in dialyzed FBS and was serially diluted to make calibrators at nine levels. Three levels of quality controls (QCs) were prepared from the stock solution in FBS. Detailed concentrations are presented in Table 1. Aliquots of calibrators and quality control samples were stored at −80 °C until use. On the day of analysis, calibrators and quality control samples were thawed and extracted as described below together with patient samples. An IS solution containing 0.1 µg/ml LEV-D6, LMT-13C, 15N4, LCM-13C, D3 and CLO-13C6, 0.2 µg/ml OXC-M1-13C6 and 0.8 µg/ml ZON-13C6, respectively, were prepared in acetonitrile and stored at −80 °C in aliquots until use.

As only fully anonymized patient samples were used that were not obtained specifically for use in this study through an interaction or intervention with living individuals, neither informed consent nor IRB review were required.

Sample preparation

20 µl of serum sample, calibrators and QCs were used for extraction. 80 µl internal standard solution in acetonitrile was added for protein precipitation. Protein was removed by centrifugation at 13,000 g for five min. 20 µl of the supernatant was diluted with 780 µl of HPLC water, vortex for five minutes, 2 µl was injected for UPLC–MS/MS analysis.

Ultra high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) conditions

The UHPLC system consisted of a SCIEX Exion LC™ UPLC system (AB SCIEX, CA, USA) with a high-pressure binary pump, autosampler, column oven, and a flow-through needle for injection. Separation of these AEDs/metabolites was achieved with a Synergi Fusion-RP 80 A LC column (4 µm, 50 × 2 mm, Phenomenex, CA, USA) and a Gemini C18 (4 µm, 4 × 2.0 mm) guard column (Phenomenex, CA, USA) with a 4.5-min LC program. The two mobile phases consisted of solvent A (0.035 % formic acid and 10 mM ammonium hydroxide (pH 8) in water) and solvent B (0.36 % formic acid in acetonitrile). The detailed gradient program can be found in Table 2. The flow rate was 0.75 ml/min. The column oven temperature was set to 30 °C, and the sample injection volume was 2 μl.

Tandem mass spectrometry conditions and analyte quantitation

Mass spectrometry analysis was performed with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) and electrospray positive ionization mode with a SCIEX Triple Quad tandem mass system 6500 (Citrine, AB SCIEX, CA, USA). Critical MS parameters such as ion spray voltage, collision energy and declustering potential were optimized to obtain the best possible sensitivity. Source-specific parameters and detailed information on compound-specific parameters can be found in Table 3. Data was collected and analyzed with Analyst 1.6.3. Calibration curves were built by plotting the peak area ratios of each analyte/isotope-labeled IS with a weighing factor of 1/x. AED/metabolites levels were quantified from the most abundant and reliable product ion for each analyte using MultiQuant MD 3.0.3 software.

Validation procedures

The assay was validated in accordance with the guidelines published by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA): Guidance for Industry, Bioanalytical Method Validation [15] and Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI): Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry Methods [16]. Method validation included assessment of selectivity, linearity, lower limit of quantification (LLOQ), within (intra) and between (inter) assay precision, accuracy, recovery, matrix effect, stability, and re-injection reproducibility.

Selectivity

Chromatograms of serum samples from multiple patients (n = 150) were analyzed for interfering peaks.

Linearity and LLOQ

Nine different levels covering the therapeutic range of each AED/metabolites were included in the linearity study. A stock mixture solution containing 100 µg/ml LEV, 30 µg/ml LCM, 30 µg/ml LMT, 80 µg/ml ZON, 70 µg/ml OXC-M1 and 2 µg/ml CLO, 20 µg/ml N-CLB was made from an individual stock solution of 1 mg/ml (N-CLO of 0.1 mg/ml), and then the stock mixture solution was serially diluted using FBS to nine levels (Table 1). Samples (n = 3 per level) were extracted as described above and analyzed in random order, and linearity was evaluated with EP Evaluator software release 12 (Data Innovations, VT, USA). Linearity was considered acceptable with a correlation coefficient (R2) > 0.99 and back-calculated concentrations within ±15 % of nominal values (±20 % for LLOQ). The LLOQ was established by meeting the FDA suggested attributes of a coefficient variation (CV) 20 % during the linearity validation process.

Intra and inter assay precision

Intra and inter assay precision were determined by analyzing quality control samples (three levels) together with calibrators. Samples (n = 20 per level) were extracted and analyzed in the same day to evaluate intra assay precision. Samples were extracted and analyzed in different days (n = 20) to evaluate inter assay precision. CV < 15 % was used as acceptable criteria.

Accuracy

An accuracy study was performed by analyzing CAP survey materials (n = 24) for LEV, LMT, LCM, ZON and OXC-M1. Because CAP surveys did not include CLO and N-CLB, patient samples (n = 27) were sent out to a reference lab using a LC–MS/MS method. Patient samples were also sent out for comparison with an enzyme immunoassay (EIA) method. Results were evaluated with EP Evaluator using the Method Comparison function with acceptable criteria of regression slopes within ±10 % and R2 > 0.98.

Recovery and matrix effects

Recovery and matrix effect were evaluated with FBS spiked with each AED/metabolite at three QC levels (n = 3 per level) (Table 5). Unextracted samples were prepared in the absence of matrix. Post-extracted samples were prepared by spiking analytes into extracted serum. Pre-extracted samples were prepared by spiking analytes into FBS followed by extraction. Recovery was evaluated by comparing the pre-extracted samples to post-extracted samples. Matrix effect was evaluated by comparing post-extracted samples to unextracted samples [17,18] with acceptable criteria of ±15 % of nominal values:

Stability studies and re-injection reproducibility

Stability was tested with QC materials at three levels (low, medium, and high concentrations) (n = 3 per level): stored at room temperature (RT) for 96 h, −20 °C with freeze, and thawed three times over 10 days. Re-injection reproducibility was evaluated in case the batch analysis was interrupted, and the samples had to be injected again after being kept in the sample manager (15 °C). Re-injection reproducibility was tested by running extracted calibrators and QC samples (three levels, n = 3 per level) stored for 96 h in the sample manager before re-injection. Both stability and re-injection reproducibility were evaluated by comparing the values (mean%) from the re-injected samples with the initial calculated values, with acceptable criteria of ±15 % of nominal values.

Interference studies

Three levels of QC samples (n = 3) were spiked into grossly hemolyzed, lipemic, and icteric serum samples and dialyzed FBS serum. Samples were analyzed as described above, and the results were evaluated by comparing the values (mean%) with the target values.

Results

UHPLC–MS/MS optimization

The proposed UHPLC–MS/MS method enables simultaneous quantification of seven AEDs/ metabolites from 20 µl of serum samples with an analytical run time of 4.5 min (Fig. 1). No peak was identified that could interfere with quantification in all patient samples analyzed (data not shown) and in a dialyzed FBS serum. Optimal compound-specific parameters and source-specific parameters for each AED/metabolite and isotope-labeled IS (Table 3) were determined with post-column direct infusion.

Representative chromatography of seven drugs/metabolites with corresponding IS.

Linearity and LLOQ

QCs (three levels) and therapeutic ranges of each AED/metabolite are summarized in Table 1. A 9-point calibration curve was designed to cover therapeutic ranges of each AED/metabolite with R > 0.99 on all analytes (Table 1). The calibration curves were prepared and assayed at final concentrations of 0.4–100 μg/ml for LEV; 0.12–30 μg/ml for LCM and LMT; 0.0078–2 μg/ml for CLO, 0.078–20 μg/ml for N-CLO; 0.28–70 μg/ml for OXC-M1; 0.32–80 μg/ml for ZON, respectively. No carry-over effect was observed.

Intra (within) and inter (between) assay precision

Quality control samples (three levels) were used for the assessment of precision. The intra assay coefficient of variations (CVs) ranged from 2.1 % to 6.8 % (n = 20), and the inter assay CVs ranged from 4.2 % to 10.9 % (n = 20) (Table 4).

Accuracy

CAP (College of American Pathologist) proficiency testing materials were included for accuracy study for LEV, LCM, LMT, OXC-M1 and ZON. Since CAP does not provide proficiency testing for CLO and N-CLB, patient specimens (n = 27) was sent out to a reference lab with a LC–MS/MS method for comparison. Results were evaluated with EP Evaluator using Method Comparison (Alternate/Quantitative) (Fig. 2). Statistical data, including concentration ranges, Deming regression slope, intercept, and R value are summarized in Table 5.

Our in-house UHPLC/MS-MS method was also compared with an enzyme immunoassay (EIA) method by sending the patient samples to a reference lab. Result comparison for LEV (n = 21) covered a concentration range of 2.78–45.75 µg/ml, yielded a Deming regression slope of 0.967, an intercept of −1.263 with R value of 0.9884 (Fig. S1). Our validated UHPLC–MS/MS method showed similar correlation with both LC–MS/MS and EIA methods for LEV.

Recovery and matrix effects

Recovery and matrix effects were evaluated at three different levels (QCs, n = 3 per level). The recoveries were 103 %–111 % for LEV, 100–108 % for LMT, 89–102 % for LCM, 92–106 % for ZON, 105 %–106 % for OXC-M1, 88–100 % for CLO and 94–99 % for N-CLB, respectively, which is acceptable. The matrix effects were found to be minimal with 95–105 % recovery (Table 6).

Stability studies, re-injection reproducibility and interference studies

The AEDs/metabolites in serum matrix were stable after freezing −20 °C, with freeze and thaw (F/T) three times over 10 days, and following 96 h at RT. Re-injection reproducibility was evaluated in case the batch analysis was interrupted, and the samples had to be injected again after being kept in the sample manager for 96 h (15 °C). Re-injection reproducibility was tested by running extracted calibrators and QC samples (three levels, n = 3 per level) stored for 96 h in the sample manager before re-injection. Both stability and re-injection reproducibility were evaluated by comparing the values (mean%) from the re-injected samples with the initial calculated values. Re-injection reproducibility studies indicate that the extracted samples can be stored at the sample manager for up to 96 h with all results within approximately 15 % of the originally measured concentration (Table S1).

The test results were not affected by gross hemolyzed serum (increased hemoglobin level), gross lipemic serum (increased triglyceride level), and gross icteric serum (increased bilirubin level), with all within 15 % of the originally measured concentration (Table S2).

Discussion

Seizure is one of the most common neurologic disorders seen in children, with the highest incidence in the first year of life [19]. Many pediatric patients are prescribed AEDs to manage various types of seizures. Therefore, timely monitoring of AED concentrations in blood is essential to ensure therapeutic efficacy and safety. However, due to the limitation of the capital budget for expensive UHPLC–MS/MS instruments and relatively smaller sample volumes of each test ordered in a tertiary academic children’s hospital, one of our main goals was to develop a cost-effective assay [12]. We validated the panel testing, including the most used AEDs in our Children’s Hospital, on the same UHPLC–MS/MS system that other clinical biochemical genetic testing, such as plasma acylcarnitine profile (flow injection) and serum free and total carnitine levels, are performed routinely to maximize the utilization of instruments. We validated this assay by using the same mobile phases and analytical column used for other clinical testing to maximize the use of common reagents and standard operating procedures. It also reduced the chance of human errors and potential contamination of the system. By multiplexing seven AEDs/metabolites into one assay, this validated method significantly reduced analytical run time when two or three AEDs are used simultaneously in one patient. It also allows add-on testing results to be released very quickly without the need to re-collect samples, repeat the testing, and avoid delay of dosing adjustment. Another benefit of this method is small sample sizes (20 µl of serum), which is important for the pediatric population.

Many published LC–MS/MS methods use negative mode to measure ZON and polarity switching has been used in order to monitor multiple AEDs [11]. However, positive mode for zonisamide without polarity switching has also been used [12]. We optimized our method for zonisamide in positive mode along with other AEDs/metabolites. This strategy helped avoid gradient stretching, which could prolong the method runtime. Our method further demonstrated that zonisamide could be measured using positive ion mode without compromising analytical performance, such as accuracy (Fig. 2) and sensitivity [20]. The simple sample preparation procedure (protein precipitation and dilution) and short analytical run time (4.5 min) of our method enable result release on the same day that samples are received. Sending samples to reference laboratories would substantially increase turnaround time since a delay of at least 4–5 days is common (shipping generally takes 1 day; reference laboratories generally release results 3–4 days afterward). A delay of 4–5 days will not only compromise therapeutic outcome, but also make it much more difficult for dose adjustment. The turnaround time has improved dramatically using our in-house validated method to the same day or second day after sample receipt by the lab, which in turn improves the quality of patient care. Lastly, by establishing an in-house assay, we are able to avoid the more expenseive reference laboratory send-out costs.

As shown in Fig. 2, most AEDs demonstrated Deming regression slopes within ±10 % and R2 values exceeding 0.98. However, CLO yielded a slightly lower R2 of 0.9632, which is marginally below the commonly accepted threshold. This result is likely influenced by the use of a single external reference laboratory for comparison and the analyte’s broad dynamic concentration range (1.07–849.3 ng/mL). Notably, the metabolite N-CLB exhibited excellent agreement, with an R2 of 0.9966. We believe that these minor deviations do not compromise the overall reliability of the method or its applicability in clinical decision-making.

In summary, the validated UHPLC–MS/MS method we describe herein simultaneously measures human serum drug levels for seven AEDs/metabolites most used in our Children’s Hospital. We optimized this assay for qualities desired by a typical children’s hospital laboratory, improving productivity, cost-efficiency, and the quality of clinical services while minimizing reagent and labor costs and maximizing the utilization of the only UHPLC–MS/MS system. The proposed method was successfully implemented and proved to be practical, reliable, and cost-effective with a quick turnaround time. The method has been successfully applied to routine therapeutic monitoring of these antiepileptic drugs in patient serum for three years. We have also been successful in extending the service to other adult hospitals and clinics in the region.
