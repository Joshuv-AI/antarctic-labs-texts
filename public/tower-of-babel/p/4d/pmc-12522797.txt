A survey on availability and composition of herbal products with beneficial health claims for chronic non-communicable diseases in Sri Lanka
Authors: Chathumi Weerakoon, Uthpali Mannapperuma, Priyanga Ranasinghe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHerbal medicinal products are becoming increasingly available worldwide. Most countries lack stringent guidelines for the regulation of herbal medicinal products. This study aimed to assess the availability and affordability of herbal products for non-communicable diseases (NCDs), as well as their compositions and health claims, in a developing country.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted by visiting 41 commercial outlets, including supermarkets, community pharmacies, and Ayurveda stores between August and September 2023. A total of 88 products were selected, and assessed for their ingredients, compositions, health claims, and other key labelling information. Availability of each product was recorded across the three types of commercial outlets. Affordability of herbal products was determined based on the minimum wage of an unskilled government worker in Sri Lanka.ResultsMajority of the products were available at Ayurveda stores (n = 66, 73.9%), followed by supermarkets (n = 65, 73.9%) and pharmacies (n = 50, 63.1%). The most common active ingredient identified was Camellia sinensis (L.) Kuntze (n = 19, 21.6%). None of the locally manufactured herbal products mentioned the composition of their ingredients. Some products had inaccurate label information, such as misspellings and duplications of scientific names for plants (n = 4, 4.5%). Many products lacked essential label information, such as side-effects (n = 79, 89.8%), warnings (n = 71, 80.7%) and contraindications (n = 69, 78.4%), special patient group information (n = 63, 71.6%), storage conditions (n = 57, 64.7%), Department of Ayurveda registration number of the product (n = 16, 18.2%), and Department of Ayurveda registration number of the manufacturer (n = 69, 78.4%). The affordability of the majority of herbal products (68.9%) was low.ConclusionDespite the higher availability of herbal products, the low affordability and misleading label information are a concern. Herbal products are often marketed with unsubstantiated health claims which can mislead consumers. Therefore, there is a need for stringent regulations to ensure the efficacy, safety, and quality profiles of herbal medicinal products in Sri Lanka.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05137-w.

Background

The exponential increase in the prevalence of chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in South Asia raises significant health challenges for public health and healthcare systems of the region [1]. NCDs are medical conditions that result from the combination of physiological, behavioral, environmental, and genetic variables, unlike infectious diseases, which are caused by pathogens [2]. Being a middle-income nation in South Asia, Sri Lanka is also facing a similar rise in prevalence of NCDs, placing an increased demand on the health systems for the treatment and management of these conditions. The latest estimates from the Global Burden of Diseases Study in 2017 (IHME, 2017) indicate that NCDs account for 81% of all deaths in Sri Lanka [3]. Furthermore, nearly three-quarters of these deaths are attributed to cardiovascular diseases alone [4]. Since many of these NCDs typically occur during middle-age, they will have a negative impact on worker efficiency and economic growth of the country.

Herbal medicines are usually defined as “finished and labeled medicinal products that contain active ingredient(s), aerial or underground parts of a plant or other plant material or combinations” [5]. According to estimates, approximately 80% of people in developing countries rely on traditional herbal medicines as a primary alternative or as a supplementary therapy in addition to conventional medications [6]. Patients often prefer herbal medicinal treatments due to the accessibility, affordability, perceived lower risk of adverse-effects, and claims of better safety [7]. Furthermore, the cultural acceptance and recognition of traditional herbal medicines play a significant role in influencing patients’ preferences. These factors have contributed to a growing trend in seeking better health outcomes by using herbal medicines for NCDs. Therefore, the demand for herbal medicinal preparations is increasing day by day [8].

As highlighted, there is an increase in public interest for traditional herbal medicines in both developing and developed countries. This growing trend has increased the availability and diversity of over-the-counter herbal products in pharmacies, food stores and supermarkets [8, 9]. In addition, products have entered the market in different brands and dosage forms, thereby increasing the competition in the market. This competitiveness has led to advertisements and product labels promoting herbal products with exaggerated and misleading therapeutic and non-therapeutic claims [9]. In addition, manufacturers of herbal medicinal products face challenges like procurement of low-quality raw materials, authentication of raw materials, adulteration of products, lack of proper standardized procedures for manufacturing formulations, and absence of standardized quality control parameters [8]. The primary root cause of these challenges is the lack of stringent regulations governing the crucial aspects of herbal product manufacturing. This includes advertising regulations, standardized manufacturing processes, stability testing, efficacy and safety evaluations, and implementation of quality assurance protocols [10]. Furthermore, the majority of finished herbal products for chronic NCDs are available as over-the-counter medications. However, there is a discrepancy between the widespread availability of these over-the-counter herbal products and the assurance of their quality. This discrepancy raises concerns regarding potential impact on consumers. In practice, consumers may encounter challenges in distinguishing real therapeutic advantages from misleading or unfounded claims. Therefore, at present there is a need to evaluate the exact impact of the problem, by studying the availability of over-the-counter herbal products for NCDs.

Similar to other developing countries, Sri Lanka has also seen a rapidly expanding range of herbal products being marketed with health benefit claims for chronic NCDs. This has necessitated the implementation of regulations to safeguard consumers. However, there is currently a lack of scientific data regarding the extent of the issue and areas that need to be prioritized for regulations. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the availability, composition, and labeling of herbal products marketed with health claims for chronic NCDs in Sri Lanka. The challenges and issues identified will be relevant to other developing countries in the region and globally, which also face a rapid increase in the availability of herbal medicines for NCDs.

Methodology

A descriptive, cross-sectional survey was carried out to determine the commonly available over-the-counter herbal products with health-related claims for chronic NCDs in the Sri Lankan market between August and September 2023. The study focused on collecting data through a structured market survey, assessing product availability, labeling information, and commonly used ingredients in the identified products.

Study setting

Data were collected in the two most populous districts of Sri Lanka, namely the Colombo and Kalutara districts (Fig. 1), home to nearly 5 million (∼25%) of the Sri Lankan population. In these districts including supermarkets, individual pharmacies, and Ayurveda drug stores, where herbal products are available over-the-counter were included in the market survey. Data collection was carried out separately in supermarkets, pharmacies, and Ayurveda stores. Convenience sampling was used in the selection of commercial outlets to ensure feasibility and accessibility withing the limited time frame. Visits continued in each category until product saturation reached 90% (i.e. until a subsequent visit to a particular store type reached < 10% in terms of newly identified eligible products). A total of 41 commercial outlets were visited in both districts (supermarkets: 17, pharmacies: 12 and Ayurveda stores: 12), and details are summarized in Table 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A product was considered eligible for inclusion if the label had made a claim as being suitable for treatment or management for chronic NCDs, including but not limited to diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, gastrointestinal disorders, urinary diseases (excluding infections), joint or muscle disorders, and cancer. Additionally, products making indirect claims related to NCDs, such as weight loss, appetite suppression, calorie, and fat burning, were also considered eligible, together with product labels containing images supportive of these claims. In addition, to be eligible >60% of the ingredients had to be of herbal in origin [11]. Products only claiming benefits such as metabolic boosters, immunity enhancers, and non-specific health advantages not related to chronic NCDs were excluded. Furthermore, any product including a registered pharmaceutical drug as an ingredient was excluded. In addition, products requiring prescription by Ayurvedic practitioners were excluded. In the present study, therapeutic claims refer to the product’s ability to cure or prevent a medical condition such as providing complete relief, or lack of adverse effects. Non - therapeutic claims were considered when they indicated the features of the products, such as 100% natural or sugar-free, that are not directly associated with its medicinal benefits [12].

Data extraction and statistical analysis

A pre-tested standardized data collection form was utilized to systematically gather information from eligible products by one investigator (CW) and the accuracy was checked by a second investigator (UM). Data acquisition was conducted through a comprehensive examination of product labels, and information displayed on the packaging. The data gathered included product name, physical state, price, active ingredients, and health claims. Additionally, information related to manufacture such as manufacturer details, batch number, manufacturing/expiration date, and certification authority(s)/registration number were also collected. Further details related to the product, such as dosage form, mode of administration, pack size, and package type (primary packaging or secondary packaging), were also recorded. Furthermore, information related to non-therapeutic health claims, duration and direction for use, adverse effects, overdose information, considerations for specific groups, and warnings and precautions were analyzed. In addition, language used on the product label, prescribed storage conditions, and other relevant details were also documented. Any discrepancies in the data extracted in this manner were re-checked and resolved by discussion with the involvement of a third investigator where necessary (PR).

The prices for the over-the-counter herbal products were determined considering the entire package rather than individual doses. Herbal products were sold in whole packages, incorporating several dose units (i.e. 15 or 20 tea bags, 30, 60, or 90 capsules, 30, 60, or 100 tablets, syrup bottles). For an individual product, the package associated with the lowest number of dose units was considered for the price assessment. The prices were recorded in Sri Lankan Rupees (SLR) at the data collection point and subsequently converted to United State Dollars (USD) for standardized comparison. Currency conversion was based on exchange rates published by the Central Bank of Sri Lanka. The currency value during the data collection period (August to September) was considered (1 LKR = 0.0031 USD) [13].

The affordability of each herbal product was determined using the following equation, according to the WHO/HAI survey methodology on affordability [14]. The cost of the monthly requirement of the herbal product was determined by considering the daily dose or recommended unites to be confused of the product. Therefore, the products that lacked a defined daily dose were not considered (i.e. gels and oils for topical administration and porridges) for this calculation. Moreover, as some of the tea products had not mentioned the dose or how many tea bags per day should be consumed, one tea bag per day was used for the calculation. Furthermore, the daily consumed dose for adults was considered for the study. Results

Through the market survey, the study identified a total of 88 individual products, with a similar product diversity between the two districts, with 76 products being commonly identified in both districts. The majority of these products (n = 66, 73.9%) were identified from Ayurveda stores, followed closely by supermarkets (n = 65, 73.9%) and pharmacies (n = 50, 63.1%) (Fig. 2). Figure 3 illustrates the overlap of product availability across Ayurveda stores, supermarkets, and pharmacies. Further analysis demonstrated that 29 products (33.0%) were available across all three types of sites, 35 products (39.8%) were shared between supermarkets and pharmacies, and 40 (45.5%) between pharmacies and Ayurveda stores. When examining the physical state (Fig. 4), the most common dosage form was tea (e.g., tea bags) (n = 38, 43.2%), followed by capsules (n = 18, 20.5%), tablets (n = 10, 11.4%), and liquid preparations (n = 6, 6.8%). Less frequent forms included solid pieces (n = 4, 4.5%), powders for porridge (n = 5, 5.7%), and mixtures (n = 4, 4.5%). Topical forms included oils (n = 2, 2.3%) and gels (n = 1, 1.1%) (Fig. 5). When considering the language, most products (n = 56, 63.6%) were labeled in all three official languages of Sri Lanka: English, Sinhala, and Tamil (Fig. 6). Six products (6.8%) had labeling only in Sinhala, while eight imported products (9.1%) were labeled exclusively in English. None of the products used Tamil as the only language on their label.

A total of 14 (15.9%) products consisted of only one ingredient, and 25 (28.4%) products contained 2 ingredients. There were 26 (29.5%) products identified with 3–5 different ingredients. Moreover, there were 12 (13.6%) products that had 6–10 different ingredients, and 11 (12.5%) products with more than 10 ingredients. None of the locally manufactured herbal medicinal products specified the composition of the herbal constituents used, compared to all imported products (n = 7, 100%) that clearly outlined the compositions or amounts of the herbal ingredients per dosage unit on their labels. Additionally, all imported products provided a complete list of both herbal and non-herbal ingredients, and the scientific names of the plants whenever required. There were total of 38 products (43.2%) that had used only the vernacular name in Sri Lanka, while 36 (40.9%) products had used both vernacular names and scientific names in the ingredient list, 7 products had used only the scientific nomenclature, and 7 products had used vernacular name in India. Ingredient list on the label of 4 products (4.5%) had errors such as scientific names with misspellings and instances where duplications of plant names with different terms (i.e. use of “Black seeds” and “Black cummin seeds (Nigella sativa (L.))” in the same ingredient list). Additionally, only 14 products (15.9%) explicitly indicated the specific part of the herbal plant utilized in the preparation such as black cumin “seeds”, Centella asiatica (L.) Urb “leaves”, Ranawara “flowers”, Lotus “stamens”, Bael “roots”, and Ash plantain “fruit”. A significant amount of ingredients out of total ingredients, comprising 106 (93.0%), were plant-based ingredients. There were products (n = 10, 11.4%) which had incorporated non-plant-based elements to their composition, such as synthetic/chemical compounds (i.e. Permitted preservatives (E210) (n = 1, 1.1%), Sodium lauryl sulphate (n = 1, 1.1%), Sodium methylparaben IP (n = 2, 2.3%), Sodium propylparaben IP (n = 2, 2.3%), vanilla flavor (n = 1, 1.1%) and excipients (n = 1,1.1%), and compounds of animal origin (i.e. bee honey [n = 5, 5.7%], and gelatin [n = 1, 1.1%]).

Therapeutic claims

Most of the products had direct therapeutic claims as being suitable for common NCDs such as diabetes (n = 27, 30.7%), hyperlipidemia (n = 21, 23.9%), and high blood pressure (n = 7, 8.0%). A total of 72 (81.8%) products were identified with claims for multiple conditions. Additionally, there were products indicated for gastrointestinal disorders (n = 11, 12.5%), joint pain and osteoarthritis (n = 4, 4.5%), and urinary diseases (n = 14, 15.9%). Particularly, there were 20 (22.7%) products specifically claiming natural weight loss and indicated for obesity which is recognized as a primary risk factor for numerous cardiovascular diseases. Further, there were products with health claims for liver disorders (n = 7, 7.9%), cancer (n = 6, 6.8%), and central nervous system and mental disorders (n = 7, 7.9%). Additionally, there were 5 products (5.7%) identified with claims for non-communicable chronic respiratory disorders, such as asthma (n = 3, 3.3%). Instructions on usage

Majority of the products identified during this study were intended for oral administration (n = 86, 97.7%), while others were for topical administration (n = 2, 2.3%). A considerable proportion, accounting for 61.4% of the products (n = 54) had provided directions for the use of the product. These instructions included the recommended dose per administration, the frequency of administration, and the optimal timing with respect to the main meals. Furthermore, 4.5% of the products (n = 4) indicated the specific duration for use. This information included the period required to achieve the therapeutic objectives of the product or the time frame within which multidose containers that should be utilized before expiration following the opening of the container. There was one product that suggested the usage of a maximum number of doses per day to achieve accelerated therapeutic results.

For products such as teas (n = 21, 23.9%) and porridges (n = 2, 2.3%), additional details were provided for the preparation of the product prior to consumption. There were products which have suggested specific conditions for consumption, such as consuming with hot water (n = 1, 1.1%), a clove (n = 1, 1.1%), or on an empty stomach (n = 2, 2.3%), for optimal therapeutic outcomes. Notably, there was only one product (1.1%) which provided information for patients regarding overdose. Warnings for use were found on labels of 19.3% (n = 17) of products (i.e. do not consume on empty stomach, do not exceed recommended dose, external use only). Furthermore, only 35.2% of the products (n = 31) had clearly specified the recommended storage conditions, of which none were with any special storage requirements (i.e. storing in a refrigerator).

Only 5.7% (n = 5) of the products stated potential adverse effects on their labels (i.e. mild laxative effect), while 4.5% of the products (n = 4) declared the absence of any adverse effects after consumption. The majority of herbal medicinal products, 89.8% (n = 79), had not mentioned any potential adverse effects in their labels. There were 21.6% of products identified (n = 19) displayed with contra-indications, and these included pregnancy (n = 14, 15.9%), children under 12 (n = 2, 2.3%), children under 18 (n = 1, 1.1%), and patients with diabetes (n = 2, 2.3%). 28.4% (n = 25) of the products were specifically recommended for special patient groups (i.e. patients with diabetes (n = 14, 15.9%), obesity (n = 5, 5.7%), heart disease (n = 2, 2.3%), urinary disorders (n = 2, 2.3%), asthma (n = 1, 1.1%) or fatty liver (n = 1, 1.1%).

Manufacturer details

All locally manufactured products (n = 81, 100%) had either displayed the Ayurveda Department registration number of the product or Ayurveda Department registration number of the manufacturer and the details of the manufacturer, including name, address, and contact details. There were only 72 products (81.8%) that clearly displayed the Sri Lankan Ayurveda department registration number for the product. There were only 19 products (21.6%) which included the manufacturer registration number on the label. None of the imported products exhibited any Sri Lankan Ayurveda Department registration number. A significant observation was the presence of locally manufactured products sharing identical product registration numbers (n = 2, 2.3%) for distinct products of the same manufacturer. Both batch numbers and lot numbers of the products were clearly identified in 77 (87.5%) products. All products (n = 88, 100%) were stored in primary packaging, including tea bags, glass bottles, plastic containers, cellophane covers, aluminum covers, paper bags, and blister packs. Only 24 (27.3%) products were accompanied by secondary packaging, often in the form of cardboard cartons.

In Kalutara district, highest mean percentage of availability was indicated from supermarkets; 16.7% (±0.2), following, Ayurveda stores; 15.5% (± 0.2) and least mean percentage was recorded from pharmacies; 15.2% (±0.3). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge this is the first detailed scientific study on the availability and composition of herbal products with beneficial health claims for CNCDs in Sri Lanka. Although limited only to CNCDs, our results show a significant number of products in different brands and manufacturer available for over-the-counter use in Sri Lanka. Therefore, it is evident that there is a significant increase in the utilization of traditional herbal medicines by Sri Lankan people as an alternative or non-conventional therapy. Many people around the world regularly use over-the-counter herbal products because of claims regarding efficacy, dissatisfaction with conventional medicines and their side effects, higher cost of doctor’s appointments and treatments with western medicines, difficulty in finding time for medical consultations, desire for self-medication, peer recommendations, social and cultural influence on treatment options [8]. These factors are further encouraged by the easy accessibility of these products in various commercial outlets such as supermarkets, pharmacies, and Ayurveda stores, as demonstrated in the present study. In addition, marketing strategies employed by manufacturers of herbal products, including exaggerated therapeutic and non-therapeutic claims regarding their effectiveness and safety, influenced patient decisions. Furthermore, the absence of stringent pre-marketing evaluation and regulations and standards in Sri Lanka, leads to marketing of these products without evidence regarding true efficacy, quality, and safety information.

There were many instances identified in the present study, where the product labels failed to provide crucial product label information as recommended by WHO guidelines [16]. An important finding of this study was the identification of a significant proportion of herbal products without information on potential adverse effects on their labels (n = 79, 89.8%). Even though the diversity of plant ingredients used in the products increases the likelihood of potential adverse effects, herbal products were often marketed as “free from adverse effects” because of their natural origin (n = 4, 4.5%). Similar to Sri Lanka, in many countries, especially in South Asian region herbal products are not subjected to stringent evaluation in terms of safety and toxicology although such claims regarding safety are often made [8]. For example, green tea is a widely consumed beverage worldwide that has been identified being potentially hepatotoxic when used beyond stipulated amounts. A review of cases conducted in Italy (1999–2008) identified 34 instances of hepatitis following the consumption of products containing green tea. However, this study also found that these products were often adulterated with synthetic medicines (i.e. diclofenac and paracetamol) which themselves have hepatotoxic effects. Nonetheless, green tea has been identified as the primary cause of hepatic damage in 80% of the reported cases in the above study [17]. Garlic (Allium sativum (L.)), another common ingredient in herbal products, is marketed for its hypertensive and anti-lipidemic effects. However, it is known to interact with anticoagulant medicines (i.e. warfarin), increasing the risk of bleeding [18]. Similarly, studies have found the interaction of ginger and licorice with anticoagulant medicines. Curcumin, which is a major constituent of turmeric, was found to inhibit monoamine oxidase activity in the brain during animal studies. Also, it is reported in the literature that turmeric should be used with caution in patients with gallbladder diseases [19]. These are only a few of the potential adverse effects reported for commonly known herbal ingredients. A study conducted in Malaysia stated that herbal medicines are not adequately regulated for quality and safety by the relevant authorities. The study specified that the limited evaluation contribute to increased herbal - drug interactions and adverse effects in traditional medicines such as ginseng (Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer) [20]. Therefore, it is unlikely that although claim of 100% safety is made, these products are free from any adverse effects. However, diagnosing adverse effects can be challenging when considering products with multiple ingredients. Furthermore, a study has identified that the pharmacovigilance system for herbal products is weak, as evidenced by the fact that 69% of the patients do not report nor attribute adverse effects linked to herbal products. Therefore, rare, and unpredictable adverse effects can occur, potentially causing severe impacts on patient health [21].

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) stipulates that herbal medicinal products, which do not undergo the same rigorous evaluations of efficacy as conventional medicines, should be classified as dietary supplements. In addition, they further recommend that such products should not be marketed with claims regarding beneficial effects in the treatment of specific diseases. Furthermore, herbal products should be evaluated for adulteration, and products with adulterants are forbidden to market [22, 23]. In Sri Lanka, the evaluation of herbal products is regulated under the authority of the Department of Ayurveda, since Ayurvedic products are considered as health supplements, food supplements, cosmeceuticals or devices by the Ayurveda (amendment) Act, No.19 of 2023 [24]. A study conducted in the USA has identified 52 disease claims for 19 products [23]. It has been recognized that this trend has generated confusion among consumers and prevented patients from acquiring proper medical attention from a healthcare provider. Moreover, the continuous consumption of herbal medicines for chronic diseases can increase the risk of developing complications. This study also identified a higher frequency of multiple non-therapeutic claims on the product labels. According to the review, most of these claims were not based on significant scientific literature. Therefore, the FDA had taken action to remove herbal supplements with non-compliance with regulations due to unsafe and misleading health claims [22, 23].

In the present study, a significant number of herbal products were identified with direct therapeutic indications, such as being indicated for diabetes, cancer, blood pressure and cholesterol. Consumers may rely on these products for therapeutic purposes, unaware of the actual efficacy and safety of these products, leading to serious consequences in the form of non-treatment. This concern is further complicated by the fact that even a single plant extract may consist of numerous active ingredients. A study from China has highlighted that ginseng (Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer) contains more than 28 different ginsenosides, similarly garlic (Allium sativum (L.) contains three active components such as allicin, allitridin and ajoene, and about 15 different bioactive compounds have been isolated from the plant Lei Gong Teng (Tripterygium wilfordii Hook.F). Therefore, when considering products that contain mixtures of multiple herbals, it becomes extremely difficult to identify the specific compound responsible for the claimed therapeutic effect [25]. Therefore, the health of individuals and society are harmed by falsified label information including unproven health claims. Similarly, misleading non-therapeutic claims such as “100% natural”, “100% organic”, “pure and fresh”, and “ancient Ayurvedic formula” can create confusion and potentially deceive consumers. Non-therapeutic claims are displayed on the label of the product for the purpose of advertising. These labels are often used to make an impression of natural origin, although the product could contain traces of synthetic ingredients used in the process. Similarly, a product labeled “ancient Ayurvedic formula” may not have any scientific evidence to support its effectiveness. These non-therapeutic claims can lead to false expectations among consumers and serious health impacts.

Furthermore, inadequate information on the label regarding direction for use, duration of use, contraindications, precautions/warnings, and other crucial details results in inappropriate self-medication. It could contribute to the development of drug resistance, delayed medical interventions, and increased healthcare costs, causing broader implications for public health. There are studies that have been conducted to determine the risks of using herbal medicines associated with pregnancy. According to a study conducted in Malaysia on women’s knowledge regarding the use of herbal medicines, 89.9% of women had minimal awareness of the content and effects of herbal products [26]. Also, 77.2% believed that herbal products are free from harmful chemicals and therefore safe and effective for used. According to the same study, pregnant women commonly used herbal products to facilitate labor (89.2%), to relieve pregnancy discomfort (10.8%) and to improve the health and intelligence of the child (5.7%) but did not disclose to healthcare providers that they were using herbal products. These results highlight the importance of providing sufficient labelling information, especially regarding specific patient groups [26]. A cross-sectional survey in Italy found that 71.8% of pregnant women consume herbal products during the pregnancy [27]. The study identified that the use of these herbal medicines was often based on the idea that they are safer than complementary treatments, suggestions from family members or advice from doctors, and mostly self-medication. This study identified a correlation between the use of licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra L.) including herbal products causing an increase in preterm labor, miscarriages, and smaller neonates [27, 28]. A systematic review based on a pharmacoepidemiologic studies in Yemen involving postpartum women and breastfeeding mothers highlighted a random clinical trial that studied the effect of Nigella sativa (L.) during pregnancy and breast-feeding stages [28]. Breast milk samples of women who were involved during this clinical trial were analyzed for 3 months to identify a correlation between N. sativa consumption and the development of atopic dermatitis associated with cytokine levels in breast milk [28, 29]. Cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum J.Presl.) is another common herb used during pregnancy. A study conducted in Palestine reported abortifacient and oxytocic effects of cinnamon [30]. Apart from that, adverse effects such as diarrhea, nausea, rashes, heartburn, abdominal pain, and dizziness were proclaimed. A correlation to the development of fetal malformations in mothers who consumed higher amounts of cinnamon-included products during pregnancy has also been identified by another study [31]. Moreover, this study recommended to avoid using fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum L.) during pregnancy based on the impacts on fertility, blood glucose levels and risk of abortion [31]. An observational and questionnaire-based study in Palestine has recognized certain herbal medicines including senna (Senna alexandrina Mill), ginger (Zingiber officinale Roscoe.), cinnamon (C. verum J.Presl), aloe vera (Aloe barbadensis Mill.), cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.), and Green tea (Camellia sinensis (L.) Kuntze), which should not be used during pregnancy [30]. Since many of these herbs are widely used concurrently, those multiple ingredients can interact and produce biochemical changes. Therefore, marketing of herbal products with the impression of safe during pregnancy should be prohibited without scientific evidence. Moreover, the mentioning of special patient groups like children, the elderly, renal disease, and liver disease patients is important as pregnant and postpartum mothers.

Furthermore, many products identified in the present study lacked information on the ingredient compositions on the label. Especially the lack of details about the composition of single dose units such as capsules and tablets result in potential negative effects such as inaccurate dosing. In contrast, imported products had provided comprehensive information on the composition per single dose unit.

There are numerous potential risks linked to inaccurate information on the labels of herbal products, which can have considerable effects on consumer safety. There were elongated herbal ingredient lists through duplication of plant names to hold the interest of consumers, and inaccurate scientific names with spelling-errors identified which create confusion, making it difficult for consumers to make informed decisions. The ingredient list on the label of the product can often be misleading. It makes an impression of 100% natural origin of the product, although many synthetic, chemical compounds, and heavy metals were utilized during the production as well as the cultivation of plants. A study conducted in China has highlighted that unclear nomenclature of herbal ingredients can adversely affect the quality and safety of herbal products. Despite having different chemical compositions, some herbal plants may have names that are similar or identical. “Mutong” (Akebia quinata (Thunb.) Decne.) which can refer to multiple plant species in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), includes plants such as “Mutong”, “Chuan mutong”, and “Guan mutong” which share a common name. Similarly, the name “Fangji” (Stephania tetrandra S. Moore) refers to species unrelated to “Mutong”, but has also been misused due to overlapping nomenclature, such as “Guang fangji (Aristolochia fangchi Y.C.Wu ex L.D.Chow & S.M.Hwang). This misidentification has led to harmful effects, including nephropathy in patients [32]. Another study conducted using 260 medicines in Asia reveals that 25% of the products identified were contaminated with heavy metals, and 7% of the products were contaminated with pharmaceuticals other than the approved and have intentionally been adulterated [33].

The study identified that 51 (68.95%) products out of total of 74 products considered for affordability evaluation require more than the daily wage of an unskilled government worker. A recent study conducted in Sri Lanka aimed to evaluate the affordability of certain essential medicines states that the monthly requirement of more than 67% of the medicines considered for the study can be afforded with less than the daily wage of a government worker [34]. Consequently, taking western medicines for chronic non-communicable diseases could be potentially more cost-effective approach when compared to herbal products, due to stringent evaluation of safety and efficacy.

A review has identified that the surveillance system on herbal medicinal products is not strong enough in USA, similar to Sri Lanka to report and investigate on herbal medicines, like other pharmaceuticals. Similarly, a study conducted in Thailand on the use of traditional medicines by cancer patients reported that these patients are rarely monitored [35]. Also, people are not aware of misleading advertising and label information, which conceal the low quality, safety, and efficacy of those products. Therefore, manufacturers of herbal products deviate from adhering to guidelines recommended by WHO for manufacturing, labeling, and marketing. This non-compliance causes risks to consumer safety and the public health of a country. Consequently, the regulatory authorities should promptly address these issues by installing standards, rules, and regulations governing misinformation about herbal products, to ensure the safety of the consumers.

Limitations

It is important to identify several limitations in the present analysis. The market survey was conducted during a period of 2 months due to time constraints. Although Sri Lanka does not experience seasonal variations such as summer or winter, it does have 3 main climatic seasons: southwest monsoon, northeast monsoon, and inter-monsoon periods. In certain markets the availability can change based on the time of the year, seasonal changes in disease patterns and new trends in the market. Therefore, certain herbal products such as cough remedies and immunity enhancers could be increasingly available during specific seasons. However, it is important to appreciate the lack of seasonality in Sri Lanka, and since the study considered products for NCDs, the impact of such variations is likely to be minimal. In addition, an availability bias may still persist, despite the consistent availability of branded products and relatively stable trends of the market throughout the year. However, a long-term study would be beneficial to obtain a comprehensive overview of the market, across the year. The study was limited to two districts in Sri Lanka. Districts were selected due to their high population density, strong marketplace for OTC herbal products, and accessibility to products during the study period. However, due to the limited geographic coverage results may not be applicable to the national market, especially in rural areas. A broader geographic scope is suggested to produce a generalized overview. For the study, supermarkets, community pharmacies, and Ayurveda stores were considered where over-the-counter herbal products are available for consumers. Online products were intentionally excluded from this study due to significant challenges in ensuring data accuracy and consistency. Online platforms in Sri Lanka often display incomplete or inconsistent product information, with variations in labeling, ingredients, and manufacturer details. Additionally, the heterogeneity of e-commerce sources, ranging from formal websites to informal social media marketplaces, makes standardization and verification difficult. Many listings lack regulatory approval or provide insufficient details to assess product legitimacy. In contrast, physical retail outlets allow for direct observation and verification, ensuring higher methodological rigor and data reliability. While this approach strengthens internal validity, we acknowledge that the exclusion of online products may limit the comprehensiveness and generalizability of our findings, particularly given the growing role of e-commerce in herbal product distribution.

Conclusion

The availability of herbal medicinal preparations for chronic NCDs was high in Sri Lanka. Despite the higher availability, the affordability of most of the over-the-counter herbal products is low. Misleading label information of herbal products is a significant concern, with unsupported claims regarding therapeutic efficacy and safety. In addition, product labelling lacked information on usage, adverse effects and contra-indications. This study emphasizes the importance of regulating label information, advertising, availability and affordability of over-the-counter herbal products and enables to identify those gaps in regulations governing herbal products in Sri Lanka.
