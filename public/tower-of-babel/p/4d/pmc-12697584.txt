Periprocedural Hypotension and Functional Outcomes in Ischemic Stroke Patients Undergoing Mechanical Thrombectomy
Authors: Maria Zuluaga, Juan David Tascón‐Romero, Jaime A. Ortiz‐Villegas, Eder Moreno, Valentina Mejía‐Quiñones, Natalia Llanos‐Leyton, David Vargas, Edgar Folleco, Pablo Amaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThere is limited information on systemic blood pressure goals and variability before and during mechanical thrombectomy and how it affects outcomes in large vessel ischemic stroke.METHODSA longitudinal cohort study of patients with acute ischemic stroke and large vessel occlusion who underwent thrombectomy at a comprehensive stroke center in southwestern Colombia (January 2017–January 2023). Two groups were defined: group H (hypotension before thrombectomy, defined as >30 mmHg systolic blood pressure drop between arrival and anesthesia induction) and no hypotension (NH) group. Primary outcomes included functional status (modified Rankin Scale score, 0–2 favorable; 3–6 unfavorable) and mortality (modified Rankin scale score = 6) at discharge and 90‐day follow‐up. Inverse probability weighting was used to reduce confounding.RESULTSA total of 167 patients (44.3% female), median age 66 (interquartile range, 53–76), underwent thrombectomy. Admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score and Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score were similar (16.5 versus 16; Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score 9 in both). Thrombolysis was used in 45.4% (H: 23.3% versus NH: 22.1%). Door‐to‐needle time: 56 minutes versus 47 minutes; door‐to‐groin: 138 minutes versus 129.5 minutes. Procedure duration was longer in H (100 minutes versus 85 minutes). Successful reperfusion was not significantly different (modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction≥2b) 73.05% (H: 76.6%, NH: 70%, P = 0.29). Unfavorable modified Rankin scale score at discharge: H: 70.1%, NH: 68.9% (P = 0.86). Mortality at discharge: H: 32.5%, NH: 16.7% (P = 0.2); at 90 days: H: 37.7%, NH: 18.9% (P = 0.02) with inverse probability weighting analysis showing higher mortality in H both at discharge (odds ratio [OR] = 2.16, 95% CI: 0.93–5.04, P = 0.07) and 90 days (OR = 2.93, 95% CI: 1.25–6.86, P = 0.01) only the latter with statistical significance.CONCLUSIONHypotension before thrombectomy in large vessel occlusion stroke was associated with increased 90‐day mortality. Worse functional outcomes were also observed, though not statistically significant. Early blood pressure control during anesthetic induction may improve prognosis.

Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) and intravenous thrombolysis are the 2 reperfusion strategies available for ischemic stroke (IS) with large vessel occlusions (LVO). Longer treatment window time have been reported for MT. For example, MR CLEAN, 1 and SWIFT PRIME 2 (Solitaire with the Intention for Thrombectomy as Primary Endovascular Treatment) had good outcomes of thrombectomy performed within 6 hours of last‐known‐well (LKW), REVASCAT (Randomized Trial of Revascularization With Solitaire FR Device Versus Best Medical Therapy in the Treatment of Acute Stroke Due to Anterior Circulation Large Vessel Occlusion Presenting Within 8 Hours of Symptom Onset) within 8 hours, 3 ESCAPE (Endovascular Treatment for Small Core and Proximal Occlusion Ischemic Stroke) within 12 hours, 4 DEFUSE 3 5 (Endovascular Therapy Following Imaging Evaluation for Ischemic Stroke 3) up to 16 hours, and DAWN (Clinical Mismatch in the Triage of Wake Up and Late Presenting Strokes Undergoing Neurointervention With Trevo) up to 24 hours of known deficit. 6 Studies like EXTEND‐IA (Extending the Time for Thrombolysis in Emergency Neurological Deficits – Intra‐Arterial) were stopped early due to better reperfusion rates with thrombectomy 100% versus 37% with alteplase. 7 However, a successful reperfusion is not always equivalent to good functional outcomes. 8 This is why other factors involved during the MT procedure need to be considered. Blood pressure (BP) variations during MT, for example, have shown a relationship with functionality and mortality in patients with IS. 9

Currently, the American Stroke Association and the American Heart Association recommend BP to be between 140 to 180 mmHg systolic blood pressure (SBP) and maximum 105 mmHg diastolic blood pressure (DBP) during and up to 24 hours after thrombolysis to prevent hemorrhagic transformation (HT) and ischemic core growth. 10 However, specific BP goal ranges during the MT that correlate with patient functional outcomes are yet to be defined. Perithrombectomy BP management studies have been discussed in a narrative review. They recommend preprocedural BP to be <185/110 mmHg but suggest lower limits for better outcomes, to avoid SBP <140 mmHg or mean arterial pressure (MAP) <70 mmHg during MT, and maintaining SBP >160 mmHg after MT. 11 However, most of these recommendations have risen from thrombolysis guidelines, without appropriate clinical trials.

Acknowledging that MT is increasingly available worldwide, 12 defining safe management BP goals that promote adequate functional outcomes in patients with IS is crucial. Very high BP values can lead to HT, and low values increase cerebral tissue hypoperfusion and infarct core, both hindering functional outcomes. 13 Scarce research exists regarding intraprocedural BP and their effect on the functional outcome of patients with LVO, 14 less so in Latin America. In Colombia, an ethnically unique country, there is limited high‐quality research regarding this topic. Accordingly, our aim is to determine the relationship between BP hypotension and functional outcomes at discharge and at 90 days in patients with IS undergoing MT at a referral center in southwestern Colombia.

METHODS

Data are available on request from the authors. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist was used. We conducted an observational retrospective longitudinal cohort study including consecutive adult patients with IS and LVO undergoing MT between January 1, 2017 and 2023 at a Colombian certified stroke center. Patients were obtained from a prospective stroke database and corroborated by MT procedure codes. Then patient data were obtained from clinical history drafted by expert hospitalist neurologists including neurological status at arrival by National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) (11 items scoring: 1–4 mild, 5–15 moderate, 16–20 moderately severe, >20 severe stroke 15 ) and baseline functionality score by the modified Rankin scale (mRS) at arrival, considering favorable (0–2) and unfavorable 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 scores. Due to institutional regulations in stroke code, BP gold standard intra‐arterial measurements are obtained in all patients with acute IS from hospital arrival with continuous recordings. Hemodynamic variables were extracted from intra‐arterial BP records and during thrombectomy anesthesia electronic records every 5 minutes. 16 Patients who were eligible for intravenous thrombolysis received alteplase due to national drug regulations, with hemodynamic monitoring according to national and American Heart Association guidelines. 10 , 17 We excluded patients with incomplete clinical history lacking LKW times, Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score (ASPECTS), NIHSS score, BP data every 5 minutes or mRS score at discharge and 90 days, or double procedure considered MT with any other intervention as stenting or lesion intervention. Based on an expected prevalence of 10%, a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample size was calculated using the standard formula for estimating proportions in large populations. The resulting sample size was 139 participants, ensuring sufficient statistical power to detect the estimated prevalence with the specified level of precision.

Hypotension was defined as a SBP decrease of more than 30 mmHg between arrival and anesthetic induction for MT and 2 groups were defined. Those with hypotension (group H), and those without NH (no hypotension group). Demographic variables included sex, age, comorbidities, urban or rural origin, and health insurance regimen (contributive indicating self‐paid for those employed and of higher income or subsidized indicating government‐covered for those with lower income according to Colombian health regulations). Furthermore, clinical variables included BP, finger glucose, and LKW times. Also, ASPECTS from 0 to 10 with a point removed for every hypodense zone on computed tomography by an expert neuroradiologist, door‐to‐needle time measured from arrival to thrombolytic bolus, door‐to‐groin (DTG) time from arrival to catheterization for thrombectomy, and thrombectomy duration in minutes were registered. For thrombectomy results the modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (TICI) score was used to measure intracranial perfusion by angiography (0 = no reperfusion, 1 = minimal reperfusion, 2a = partial filling <50%, 2b = ≥50% filling 2c = complete reperfusion with slow flow or few distal cortical emboli 3 = complete reperfusion, 18 considering successful reperfusion from 2b and above [2b, 2c, and 3]). Follow‐up variables included complications such as HT scored by the European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study classification (hemorrhagic infarction 1: petechial bleeding along the ischemic lesion periphery, hemorrhagic infarction 2: confluent petechial hemorrhage in the infarct core, parenchymal hematoma 1: real hematoma <30% of lesion volume with/without space occupying effect, and parenchymal hematoma 2: hematoma >30% of lesion volume with significant space occupying effect and peripheral edema). 19 These HTs were defined as symptomatic, if they caused neurological symptoms or nonsymptomatic if they did not.

Primary outcomes included discharge and 90‐day functionality assessed by mRS considering favorable (0–2) and unfavorable 3 , 4 , 5 scores, and mortality (mRS = 6). Patients (or principal caretakers) who had no 90‐day follow‐up visit institutionally were contacted telephonically for mRS grading. Statistical analysis included exploratory data analysis to identify missing data (excluded) or outliers (none identified). For numeric data variable normality was established by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Means and SDs were used for normal variables and median and interquartile range (IQR) and Wilcoxon test were employed if nonnormal. Qualitative variables were expressed as percentages with chi‐square tests. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05. An exploratory data analysis was conducted to identify and address missing data. To assess data quality, a random 10% sample of the data set was selected, and the entered data were compared against the source documents. As some inconsistencies were noted, the entire database was audited to avoid missing data and check outliers. To try to reduce a potential selection bias we attempted multiple follow‐up methods, including repeated calls at varying times, the use of secondary contact numbers, and coordination with outpatient and rehabilitation center when possible. Further, to reduce confounding and balance variables for the H and NH groups, a propensity scoring analysis with inverse probability weighting (IPW) was performed. Fourteen variables were included in the model (sex, age, urban residence, history of hypertension, atrial fibrillation and diabetes, admission systolic, diastolic and mean arterial BP, NIHSS score at admission, LKW, DTG, thrombectomy duration time, and reperfusion status (TICI). The main outcome measured was mortality (mRS score = 6) at discharge and at 90‐day follow‐up. For covariate balancing standardized mean differences prior and after ponderation were calculated considering an acceptability threshold of 0.1. A quasibinomial logistic regression was employed to correct variance and calculate adjusted odd ratios with a CI of 95% for the relationship between presenting hypotension during anesthetic induction prior to thrombectomy and mortality. Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, EE. UU) was used. Institutional ethics committee approval was obtained from the institution (approval number #1990).

RESULTS

Admission Clinical and Imaging Data

Of 193 patients who presented with LVO, 167 were included in our analysis. The median age was 66 years (IQR 53–76), 74 were female (44.3%). Compared with patients without hypotension (NH group N = 90), those with hypotension (H group, n = 77) had lower prevalence of prior history of arterial hypertension (H: 46.7% versus NH: 67.5%, total 56.3%), but no significant difference in obesity (20% versus 20.8%), coronary artery disease (17.8% versus 20.8%), congestive heart failure (H: 11% versus NH: 12%, total 11.5%), hypothyroidism (H: 6.5% versus NH 9.3%, total 8%), chronic renal disease (H: 2.2% versus NH: 2.7%, total 2.4%), and previous stroke (H: 9.8% versus NH: 12%, total 10.8%). No significant differences were seen in demographic variables including urban area of origin with n = 113 (67.3%; H: 71.1% versus NH: 63.6%) and health care regimen contributive n = 103 (61.7%) versus subsidized n = 53 (31.4%). Further, no significant differences in admission clinical data were seen except for SBP.

The H group had significantly higher values upon admission (SBP 156 mmHg [IQR 136–176 mmHg]; DBP 85 [IQR 74—92]; MAP 105.33 [IQR 92.67–119.33] versus the NH group (SBP 134 [IQR 119–146]; DBP 76 [IQR 67—92]; MAP 96.17 [IQR 867.67–108]) with a significance of SBP P = 0.001, DBP P = 0.048, and MAP P = 0.0007. Median admission NIHSS value was 16 (IQR 12–20) with LKW times of 149 minutes (IQR 80–313) and mRS baseline score of 0. Regarding imaging 100% of patients obtained noncontrast computed tomography with median ASPECTS of 8 (IQR 7–10) followed by angiography. Majority of occlusions were detected at the M1 portion of the middle cerebral artery (78.4%) with 10 patients presenting occlusions in the posterior circulation (6%). Glucometry at admission had a median of 120 (IQR 101–140) (Table 1).

Reperfusion Data

Intravenous thrombolysis was performed in 76 (45.5%) patients (H: 41.11% versus NH: 50.7%). The door‐to‐needle time, DTG time, and total thrombectomy time were higher in the H group, but only the thrombectomy duration was significantly different. Door‐to‐needle time was H: 56 minutes (IQR 41–76) versus NH: 47 minutes (IQR 36–64), DTG time was H: 138 minutes (IQR 100–175) versus NH: 129.5 minutes (IQR 97–168), and total thrombectomy duration H: 100 minutes (IQR 70–140) versus NH: 85 minutes (IQR 60–120) (Table 1). General anesthesia to perform MT was used in 97.6% of patients (H: 100% versus NH: 95.5%). Successful reperfusion (TICI 2b, 2c, or 3) was obtained in 73.05% of patients (H: 76.62% versus NH: 70% (χ2: 6.13, P = 0.29) (Table 1). Regarding complications, similar rates of HT were shown in both groups H: 36.36% versus NH: 32.2%. Regarding BP percentage change from arrival to thrombectomy, those patients with hypotension had 13% change (IQR 13.4 [1.5–21.7]) whereas those without hypotension had almost double the variability in BP (IQR 21.7 [20.6–39.2]).

A total of 26 patients were excluded due to complete reperfusion with thrombolysis who did not require MT at angiography (n = 19), incomplete anesthesia data (n = 5), and double procedures including another intervention with MT (n = 2).

Functional Outcomes

A total of 22 patients (38.5%) achieved functional independence (mRS score 0–2) at discharge, and 29.5% maintained this status at 3‐month follow‐up. No statistically significant differences were observed between groups at discharge; however, mortality in the H group was twice as high as in the NH group. Unfavorable functional outcomes (mRS score 3–6) at discharge were observed in 61.5% of the total sample—70.1% in the H group versus 68.9% in the NH group (χ2 = 0.03, P = 0.86). Mortality (mRS score = 6) at discharge was also higher in the H group: 32.5% versus 16.7% in the NH group (χ2 = 8.6, P = 0.2) (Figure 1). Propensity scoring analysis after IPW for mortality at discharge showed a higher mortality for the H group comparted to NH with OR of 2.16 (95% CI 0.93–5.04); however, these results were not statistically significant (P = 0.07). This IPW model balanced 13 of the 14 variables included (previous SBP was not with a small difference of 0.129) for 163 patients (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

MT successful reperfusion results (TICI scores 2b, 2c, or 3) do not always correlate with favorable functional outcomes. Hence other variables during the procedure such as variability of SBP should be studied. In this study patients with IS of LVO taken to thrombectomy, who experienced hypotension (a decrease of >30 mmHg in SBP) from triage to anesthesia induction showed a higher association with functional dependency (mRS score >2) and mortality at 90 days of follow‐up compared with those who did not (NH), independently of their reperfusion status (χ2 = 15.75, P = 0.028). The hypotension group had double the mortality rate at 90 days compared with the nonhhypotension group (H 37.7% versus NH 18.9%, χ2: 15.8, P = 0.02) (Figure 2). Propensity score analysis with IPW at 90‐day follow‐up also showed higher mortality in the hypotension group with an OR = 2.93 (95% CI 1.25–6.86) and statistical significance (P = 0.01). It was also higher at discharge with an OR 2.16 (95% CI 0.93–5.04) but no statistical significance (P = 0.07) with both methods (χ2: 8.6, p: 0.2). This suggests that hypotension during the initiation of thrombectomy may predict worse functionality and higher mortality long‐term. There were no differences in complication rates between the 2 groups (H: 36.4% versus NH 32.2%, χ2 0.32, P = 0.57).

Periprocedural hemodynamic management during MT remains an area with limited data, yet growing evidence suggests that hypotension during the procedure is associated with worse functional outcomes. Valent et al demonstrated that a 10% reduction in BP from baseline—often observed with general anesthesia during MT—was significantly associated with poorer neurological outcomes. 14 Moreover, both the magnitude and duration of hypotension have shown linear correlations with worse outcomes. Even brief hypotensive episodes may compromise distal perfusion beyond the occlusion, potentially leading to vascular collapse and larger infarct volumes. 14 , 20

There is scarce information regarding variability of SBP during thrombectomy. Among the previously mentioned trials, only the ESCAPE trial reported BP measurements during the MT procedure itself, specifying SBP goals >150 mmHg prior to reperfusion, but not accounting for its variability. 3 Ongoing trials, such as the Masterstroke trial, aim to induce systolic hypertension levels of either 140 or 170 mmHg to evaluate functional outcomes; results from these studies are still pending. 21 Furthermore, a systematic review and meta‐analysis sought to identify the most effective BP measurements in patients with stroke by assessing various metrics, including SBP, DBP, MAP, and their variability. This review found that SBP variability was directly associated with functionality and mortality in these patients, suggesting that early measurement—whether on a beat‐to‐beat, minute‐to‐minute, or hour‐to‐hour basis—could be beneficial. 9

What has been evidenced in studies of MT is that higher BP levels may increase the permeability of the blood–brain barrier but also increase the risk of HT in patients with IS. 13 Elevated SBP levels contribute to the reduction of ischemic tissue by maintaining collateral artery flow and when lost due to BP reduction, the ischemic zone enlarges. Consequently, some studies have reported that a decrease in BP levels is associated with greater severity in patient outcomes. 22 , 23 Ischemic brain tissue lacks autoregulatory capacity and has impaired vasodilatory responses. 24 This dysregulation often manifests as systemic hypertension during the acute phase of stroke, as the body attempts to preserve cerebral perfusion. In this context, a reduction in systemic BP—particularly before recanalization—is likely to result in decreased cerebral blood flow, risking infarct extension below the ischemic threshold, and ultimately contributing to worse functional outcomes. 20

There is, however, research concerning BP management after thrombectomy and after reperfusion. The BEST (Blood Pressure After Endovascular Stroke Therapy) and BEST II trials have investigated BP management 24 hours after thrombectomy. BEST I defined 158 mmHg of SBP as the limit to differentiate between bad and good functional outcomes after thrombectomy, with worse outcomes with higher values. 25 BEST II showed that SBP goals <140 mmHg or 160 mmHg did not meet futility goals and had low probability of benefit when compared with SBP goals of ≤180 mmHg after successful reperfusion. 26 Secondary analysis of these data showed increased BP variability (especially SBP variability) are associated with worse mRS score at 90‐day follow‐up. 27

Furthermore, lower SBP has been linked to impaired collateral blood flow, which plays a crucial role in maintaining perfusion during LVO. 28 A sustained drop in SBP may lead to the expansion of the ischemic core, especially in patients with poor collateral circulation. Higher admission BP has also been associated with poorer collateral status, possibly reflecting the need for elevated BP to sustain cerebral perfusion in these vulnerable individuals. 28 Although collateral status was not specifically assessed in our study, it likely contributes to the individual variability in tolerance to BP fluctuations. This reinforces the notion that absolute BP thresholds may be less informative than the magnitude of BP variability from baseline on a per‐patient basis.

Contrarily, a retrospective study that monitored vital signs during thrombectomy revealed that lower maximum SBP values during the procedure were predictive of favorable functional outcomes. 29 Dissimilar information may be influenced by various factors including the population's cerebral autoregulation capacity, collateral quality, and reperfusion rates. Not all patients, ages, and ethnicities have the same number of collaterals or cerebral blood flow autoregulation, making the evidence challenging to interpret and discordant.

In our study reported reperfusion rates were similar (73%) to others reported. 22 , 30 , 31 However, a decrease in SBP did show differences in functional outcome at the 90‐day follow‐up, with an increase in mortality (OR 2.93, 95% CI 1.25–6.86, P = 0.01). Ethnic variability may account for differences in collateral vessel quality. According to Anadani et al, having elevated SBP values at the end of thrombectomy with successful reperfusion is associated with worse functional outcomes, increased mortality, and higher risk of HT, but not for those without reperfusion. 31 Therefore, lower reperfusion rates may represent populations requiring stricter BP control with higher target values and avoidance of hypotension. However, in the study mentioned by Pikija et al, better outcomes were indicated with increases of >120% even with reperfusion rates of 73.8%. 30

Furthermore, studies with periprocedural measurements recorded before or after thrombectomy have demonstrated that lower BP values are associated with better outcomes. In the MR CLEAN clinical trial, it was shown that patients with SBP <120 mmHg before thrombectomy had better functional outcomes. 1 , 32 , 33 Similarly, a multicenter study found that SBP values <180 mmHg after thrombectomy were associated with better functional outcomes and lower mortality at 90 days. 33 This may be because patients starting with lower BP do not experience as abrupt changes during the procedure compared with those who were hypertensive at presentation and subsequently experienced a drop in BP.

This was reflected in our study with a statistically significant difference in baseline BP at hospital admission between the groups presenting hypotension during thrombectomy (group H) and the 1 that did not (group NH). Patients who later developed hypotension during thrombectomy had significantly higher baseline values: SBP H: 156 mmHg (IQR 136–176) versus NH: 134 mmHg (IQR 119–146), P = 0.000; DBP H: 85 mmHg (IQR 74–92) versus NH: 76 mmHg (IQR 67–92), P = 0.048; and MAP H: 96.2 mmHg (IQR 86.7–108) versus NH: 105.3 mmHg (IQR 92.7–119.3). However, the history of hypertension was not significantly different between groups, suggesting prior small vessel disease was similar between groups and that the elevation seen at admission may reflect an acute physiological response to stroke. One hypothesis is that patients with more severe occlusions may exhibit reflex hypertension at presentation due to multiple reasons: sympathetic response, increase intracranial pressure, pain or stress associated with hospitalization, and potential injury to autonomic control centers in the brain such as amygdala, cingulum, and hypothalamus. 34 However, this was not reflected in differences in ASPECTS, NIHSS scores, or confirmed vessel occlusions between groups.

Higher admission BP has also been associated with poorer collateral circulation, 28 which could have made this group more vulnerable to poor outcomes. Additionally, though the observed values were within acceptable ranges for intravenous tissue plasminogen activator or endovascular intervention, a higher admission BP might lead to more aggressive BP management intraprocedurally. This could increase variability and risk of hypotension, especially if anesthetic protocols target “normal” physiologic values without accounting for each patient's baseline BP. These findings highlight the importance of individualized BP targets during thrombectomy, particularly for patients presenting with elevated admission pressures and the need for further research in high BP measures at admission as a predictive measure of poor outcomes.

At admission, most patients had an initial mRS score of 0 and imaging findings that suggested a favorable prognosis, with mean ASPECTS of 9/10 (range 7–10), with no significant differences between groups. Additionally, the proportion of patients receiving intravenous tissue plasminogen activator, DTG times, and LKW times were not statistically different, indicating comparable recovery potential in both groups. However, total thrombectomy procedure times were significantly longer in the H group. This could have affected the outcomes in the H group. It may be attributable to differences in LVO sites leading to a greater autoregulatory response, as previously mentioned. In our study, the H group had no internal carotid artery occlusions, a higher frequency of posterior circulation strokes, and fewer proximal M1 occlusions. This suggests a higher proportion of distal M1 or proximal M2 occlusions, which are technically more challenging and may require longer procedural times. Nevertheless, total reperfusion times from LKW were not statistically different between groups.

In a study by Alawieh et al, which evaluated the impact of thrombectomy duration on patient outcomes, it was concluded that longer thrombectomy times were associated with lower rates of functional independence and higher rates of symptomatic hemorrhage post procedure. This happened particularly in thrombectomy beyond 30 minutes, which in this study both groups surpassed with a 15‐minute difference (H: 100 minutes versus NH: 85 minutes). Additionally, it was noted that thrombectomies exceeding 60 minutes or requiring more than 3 passes should prompt consideration of the procedure's futility and the associated risks of continuing. 22 However, this must be interpreted in context. Many middle‐income countries have fewer resources available and less infrastructure available for these procedures leading to longer thrombectomy duration times with reported ranges of 251–454 minutes from symptom onset to recanalization, although scarce data exist regarding thrombectomy times. 29

Our sample had a high rate of poor functional outcomes. Poor functional outcome was defined as mRS score 3–6, consistent with large thrombectomy trials such as the HERMES (Highly Effective Reperfusion Evaluated in Multiple Endovascular Stroke Trials) study. 35 Our study reported a 16.5% higher rate of poor outcomes compared with HERMES (mRS score 0–2 at 90 days was 46% in the HERMES thrombectomy group versus 29.5% in our cohort). This difference could be attributed to multiple factors. First, the HERMES trial was conducted in high‐income countries with ample resources and experienced centers—most performing >100 thrombectomies per year. 35 In contrast, our study was based in a middle‐income country in Latin America, at a center performing significantly fewer thrombectomies (35 in 2022, 55 in 2023). Operator‐dependent factors may therefore contribute to outcome differences.

Additionally, access to neurorehabilitation is more limited and less targeted in our setting. In high‐income countries, rehabilitation is more accessible and better integrated into poststroke care, which can significantly improve long‐term functional outcomes. 36 , 37 In our context, significant barriers to accessing neurologically focused rehabilitation may have contributed to poorer 90‐day outcomes. Many patients who might have achieved better mRS scores with appropriate therapy were unable to access it due to systemic limitations.

When comparing our results to other thrombectomy trials in similar Latin American socioeconomic contexts—such as the RESILIENT (EndoVascular Treatment With Stent‐retriever and/or Thromboaspiration vs. Best Medical Therapy in Acute Ischemic Stroke) trial in Brazil (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02216643)—the difference in favorable outcomes was smaller (35.1% in RESILIENT versus 29.5% in our study; a 5.6% difference). 38 Although center volumes and health care structures still vary across Latin America, shared challenges in access to specialized resources and rehabilitation may contribute to similar patterns in functional outcomes.

Mortality rates were notably high in our sample. When compared with the HERMES trial, our observed mortality rate was 8.5% higher (23.8% versus 15.3%). 35 However, when compared with the Brazilian RESILIENT trial, our mortality rate was slightly lower (23.8% versus 24%). 33 These comparisons may highlight the influence of regional health care infrastructure and socioeconomic context on long‐term outcomes. Limited access to postacute care and rehabilitation services in Latin American middle‐income settings may contribute to poorer long‐term functionality and increased mortality among patients with stroke undergoing thrombectomy.

Regarding anesthesia, worse functional outcomes have been reported with general versus local anesthesia 14 , 39 leading some countries to use only local anesthetics to prevent hypotension and ensure shorter door‐to‐puncture times. However, this view is challenged by new trials with tighter protocol guidelines and BP monitoring during the anesthetic procedure, favoring general anesthesia due to less movement from the patient and better control of vital signs. 40 , 41

In addition, a subanalysis of MR. CLEAN trial including patients with general anesthesia who had BP measurements (n = 60) evidenced that anesthesia induced changes of MAP of >10 mmHg had fewer chances of functional recovery (OR = 1.67). 42 Conversely, another study assessed ischemic lesion size on postthrombectomy imaging and functional outcomes, finding that patients with increases >120% of baseline SBP values during thrombectomy had a smaller increase in ischemic lesion size and better functional outcomes, with no differences in those with a decrease of 20%. 12

During thrombectomy, the highest risk of hypotension occurs during anesthesia induction due to the medications used. In our study, 97.6% of the thrombectomies were performed under general anesthesia, per institutional protocol. This was associated with more frequent use of vasopressors, either in bolus or infusion, to manage BP, an aspect not thoroughly explored in terms of functional outcome differences in the present study. The reason hypotension presented itself in our study could be multifactorial. However, a possible reason for a fall in SBP of 30 mmHg or more between arrival and anesthetic induction (included) is the use of general anesthetics and a potential iatrogenic reduction in those patients with extremely high SBP at arrival. In very high BP as can be seen during LVO (SBP <220 mmHg) an 80% reduction within anesthesia protocols could easily exceed 30 mmHg. This is why, anesthesia induction has been identified as an intervenable point in the endovascular treatment of IS that could improve functional outcomes by mitigating patient hypotension before starting the procedure and individualizing pressure goals.

Moreover, raising awareness among anesthesiologists about the early use of vasopressors to counteract the hypotensive effects of certain induction agents could be beneficial. Clinical studies on the best methods for vasopressor administration are also warranted. Some Latin American studies have attempted to determine the most suitable anesthetic inducers for thrombectomy. These studies, summarized in a systematic review, compared different anesthetic agents and their outcomes. 43

Limitations and Strengths

A retrospective study design limits the assessment of BP, as it was not feasible to control variables such as prethrombolysis or thrombectomy BP levels, medications administered upon admission and during anesthesia induction, and the timing of their use. However, adherence to institutional protocols ensured reliability of the recorded variables, aligning with international clinical practice guidelines for standard management.

Furthermore, objective measurement of collateral circulation via angiography was not performed. It is important to note that BP was analyzed upon admission and during anesthesia induction at 5‐minute intervals, which does not account for potential variations throughout the remainder of the procedure. Future studies should therefore consider longitudinal measurements during the entire procedure.

There was variability in the 90‐day patient follow‐up, with a mean follow‐up duration of 15 days.

We also acknowledge the potential for selection bias due to nonresponse in 18.6% of the initial sample lost to follow‐up. At 3 months, 31 patients were lost (H: 20.8% versus NH: 16.7%). However, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.9). Many of our patients come from rural areas where unstable housing and ongoing national security concerns contribute to frequent changes in residence, making consistent telephonic follow‐up particularly challenging. These structural and geographic barriers were evenly distributed between groups, suggesting that loss to follow‐up was not systematically biased. To mitigate this limitation, we attempted multiple follow‐up methods, including repeated calls at varying times, the use of secondary contact numbers, and coordination with outpatient and rehabilitation center when possible. Despite these efforts, retention was limited by factors beyond our control. To further reduce variability between groups IPW was run for mortality as one of the primary outcomes, evidencing balancing of variables between groups and ORs that reflect previous statistical analyses.

This study represents an initial exploration of hypotension's impact during thrombectomy on functional outcomes in a Latin American cohort using invasive arterial line measurements. It highlights intervenable points such as anesthesia induction and emphasizes the need for further investigation into vital sign dynamics during the procedure to comprehend their long‐term impact on functionality in patients with LVO stroke undergoing thrombectomy.

CONCLUSION

A drop in systolic SBP >30 mmHg at the start of thrombectomy, usually during anesthesia induction, is associated with worse functional outcomes and higher mortality at 90 days in patients with IS. These results are applicable to the southwestern Colombian population and not widely generalizable. SBP hypotension is a potential predictor and a future intervention point in the acute management of this condition to improve functional outcomes.
