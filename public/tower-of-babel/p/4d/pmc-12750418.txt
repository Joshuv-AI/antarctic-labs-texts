Functional Recovery in a Young Adult With Spinal Cord Injury Secondary to Vitamin B12 Deficiency and Substance Use: A Case Report
Authors: Fereshteh Kargar Bafrani, Nozima Akbarova, Paul Rutkowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVitamin B12 deficiency is a well-established cause of spinal cord dysfunction, often manifesting as subacute combined degeneration. However, in young adults, especially those with a history of substance use, this diagnosis can be delayed or misattributed to other causes. We present the case of a 26-year-old woman with a history of nitrous oxide and ketamine use who developed significant functional decline and was ultimately diagnosed with B12 deficiency-related spinal cord injury. Despite normal spinal magnetic resonance imaging, she exhibited profound bilateral lower extremity weakness and neuropathic pain. Intensive inpatient rehabilitation resulted in significant functional improvement. This case underscores the importance of early recognition and a multidisciplinary approach to recovery, even in radiographically silent presentations.KEYWORDS: Functional recovery, Nitrous oxide, Rehabilitation, Spinal cord injury, Subacute combined degeneration, Vitamin B12 deficiency, Young adult

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) deficiency remains a clinically relevant, yet underrecognized, cause of reversible myelopathy, particularly among young adults with substance use disorders. The neurologic sequelae of B12 deficiency, including paresthesia, gait disturbance, and subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord, are well-documented. However, cases where spinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings are negative and where polysubstance use—particularly nitrous oxide—is involved can present diagnostic and rehabilitative challenges. This report details a case of profound functional impairment in a young woman and her subsequent recovery following targeted treatment and comprehensive rehabilitation.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old woman with a medical history notable for post-traumatic stress disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, depression, anxiety, polysubstance use (including daily nitrous oxide inhalation), and a remote history of Lyme disease, presented with rapidly progressive bilateral lower extremity, severe burning neuropathic pain, and gait disturbance. The onset occurred approximately 17 days prior to hospital admission. She was initially evaluated at Baptist Hospital, where MRI of the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine was unremarkable. Toxicology was positive for benzodiazepines. Laboratory testing revealed a profoundly low serum vitamin B12 level of 72 pg/mL and elevated homocysteine level of 60 μmol/L.

She was diagnosed with functional spinal cord impairment because of B12 deficiency, exacerbated by chronic nitrous oxide abuse, and was transferred to West Gables Rehabilitation Hospital for further management.

Upon admission, the patient was alert, cooperative, and oriented. Neurologic examination revealed symmetrical weakness in bilateral ankle dorsiflexors and plantar flexors (3/5), diminished lower extremity strength, and impaired balance. Deep tendon reflexes were normal. She had difficulty initiating gait and required maximal assistance for ambulation. There was also evidence of ataxia, proprioceptive deficits, and neuropathic pain localized to the lower limbs.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report, and the hospital ethics committee approved.

Rehabilitation course

A multidisciplinary inpatient rehabilitation program was initiated, including physical therapy, occupational therapy, physiatric oversight, and psychiatric support. Treatment involved the following: (1) intramuscular cyanocobalamin 1000 μg weekly ×2 doses, followed by daily oral B12, (2) neuropathic pain management with gabapentin, (3) progressive gait training and neuromuscular re-education, and (4) ADL retraining and energy conservation techniques.

Within 9 days of rehabilitation, the patient demonstrated significant improvement. She progressed from maximal to minimal assistance for ambulation and transfers. By discharge, she was ambulating over 100 feet with a rolling walker and minimal assistance. Bed mobility and ADLs were nearly independent. Occupational therapy noted full independence with upper and lower extremity dressing, grooming, and hygiene.

Discussion

This case exemplifies the complexity of diagnosing B12 deficiency-related neurologic dysfunction in young adults with substance use history. Nitrous oxide, a known inactivator of vitamin B12, is increasingly misused recreationally and has become a leading cause of acute B12 deficiency among younger populations.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Importantly, this patient’s MRI findings were normal, illustrating that radiologic findings may lag behind clinical symptoms or be entirely absent in early or peripheral-dominant presentations.

Early diagnosis through biochemical testing and clinical suspicion is crucial. Functional B12 deficiency can present with normal B12 levels but elevated homocysteine and methylmalonic acid. In our case, prompt supplementation and structured rehabilitation led to substantial recovery.

The rehabilitation outcome in this case was particularly encouraging. Despite initial maximal assistance needs, the patient achieved near independence at discharge. This underscores the role of early, intensive, and interdisciplinary rehabilitation even in nontraumatic, toxin-induced spinal cord dysfunction.

Conclusions

Vitamin B12 deficiency remains a treatable but frequently overlooked cause of spinal cord dysfunction, particularly in young adults with nitrous oxide use. Normal MRI findings should not delay treatment if clinical suspicion is high. This case highlights the potential for significant functional recovery with timely intervention and comprehensive rehabilitation.
