Treatment outcome and associated factors of severe malaria among admitted children in the Gambella Region, Southwest Ethiopia: a retrospective study design
Authors: Addisu Animut, Teshome Bekana, Sabit Zenu Siraj, Keerati Ponpetch, Geremew Tasew, Esayas Kinfe, Zeleke Mekonnen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFor thousands of years, humans have been plagued by the deadly disease malaria, affecting millions of people and harming their physical, social, and economic well-being. Despite significant progress over the past two decades in reducing malaria-related morbidity and mortality, malaria remains a major public health threat, particularly in Ethiopia. It is important to assess the treatment outcome of severe malaria and the factors influencing them due to the high prevalence and epidemic nature of the disease in the Gambella region of Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution-based retrospective study was conducted at Gambella General Hospital among 417 children aged from one month to fourteen years who were admitted with severe malaria between May 2022 to May 2024. Data were collected using pre-tested data collection forms. The collected data were cleaned and entered into EPIData, which was again exported into statistical software for analysis. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to determine associations between determinants and the outcome variable. A significance level was set at p < 0.25 and p < 0.05 for bivariate and multivariable logistic regression, respectively.ResultsOf 417 children enrolled in the study, the mean age was 4.03 years (SD of ± 2.11 years). The overall case fatality rate for severe malaria was 9.11%. High case fatality rates were also observed among children presenting with severe anemia 31.8%, convulsions 27%, pulmonary edema 19.7%, Coma 18.5%, acute kidney injury 18.4%, and comorbid illnesses 16.9%. The majority of the children (91.7%) admitted to the hospital had Plasmodium falciparum, which accounted for 34 (89.5%) of the deaths. Among the predictor variables assessed, convulsion, severe anemia, and comorbid illnesses were significantly associated with the outcome.ConclusionIn this study, the overall case fatality rate of severe malaria in children was notably high. This underscores the pressing necessity of prioritizing intervention packages that include preventive strategies, early detection, and the prompt identification of features that indicate severity.

Background

Malaria is a profound parasitic illness that may advance to complications involving the nervous system, respiratory distress, renal injury, metabolic acidosis, and hypoglycemia, potentially leading to fatality in instances of delayed or inadequate treatment [1, 2].

Malaria remains a major global health problem, with an estimated 249 million cases and 608,000 deaths in 2022. Of these, an estimated 95% of deaths occurred in Africa, with children under the age of five accounting for 76% of these deaths [3]. The case fatality rate of severe malaria in children varies across studies and regions. According to the WHO, the global case fatality rate for severe malaria in children ranges between 10 and 30% [4, 5]. In the WHO sub-Saharan Africa region, an estimated 580,000 deaths occur annually, accounting for the highest number of malaria-associated deaths globally. Between 2000 and 2019, the case fatality rate in this region declined by 60%, from 143 to 57 deaths per 100,000 people at risk. It increased to 61 in 2020 and then dropped again to 56 in 2022 [3, 6].

Although malaria-related morbidity and mortality have significantly decreased over the past 20 years, the disease remains a serious public health threat, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa [7]. In Ethiopia, malaria is among the top 10 causes of death [8] and continues to be a major public health concern, ranking as one of the leading causes of hospitalization and mortality [9]. In 2022 alone, Ethiopia reported approximately 5.1 million cases and 10,570 deaths [6]. Malaria is widespread across 75% of Ethiopia’s land area and threatens approximately 52% of the population [10]. Malaria-related deaths decreased by 58%, from 510 to 213, between 2016 and 2019[11]. However, due to pandemic-related program disruptions, armed conflict, and population displacement, Ethiopia experienced 32.5% increase in confirmed malaria cases between 2021 and 2022, from 1.1 million to 1.5 million. Compared with the same periods in 2021 and 2022, reported malaria cases in 2023 increased by 150% and 120%, respectively [12].

Malaria accounted for 0.78% of total Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) in Ethiopia's and 1% of global malaria-related DALYs [13]. In the Gambella region, malaria ranked third in contributing to the overall DALY burden in 2019 across all age groups irrespective of sex [14]. Beyond causing significant morbidity and mortality among Ethiopian children, malaria also leads to additional consequences such as increased medical expenses and school absenteeism [15]. The economic burden of malaria on households and the national healthcare system has been substantial and far-reaching [15].

Malaria transmission remains high in the western border near to South Sudan and Sudan, even though the disease's morbidity and mortality have decreased significantly in Ethiopia since 2000 [8]. The Gambella region, located in the western lowlands of the country, has the highest malaria prevalence (6%) [16]. However, there are few studies that assess the prevalence and epidemic patterns of the disease in the region. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the treatment outcomes of severe malaria and associated factors among children aged 1 month to 14 years who were hospitalized between May 1, 2022, and May 1, 2024.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted at Gambella General Hospital, located in Gambella Town, approximately 768 km southwest of Addis Ababa. The region has an estimated population of 547,103 in the host community and an additional 370,000 people residing in the refugee camps. Gambella has a predominantly hot climate, situated at an elevation of 400–600 m above sea level. Annual rain fall ranges from 800 to 1200 mm, with about 85% occurring between May and October. The average annual temperature ranges from 17.3 to 28.3 °C [17]. Gambella General Hospital is the only general hospital serving the region. The community receives both preventive and curative services across multiple disciplines from the hospital. In relation to malaria, the hospital provides diagnosis, treatment, case management, and training for medical personnel. Of the hospital's 192 beds, 24 are located in the pediatric inpatient department, where an average of 66 children with severe malaria are admitted each year. Study design and sample size calculation

This facility-based retrospective study included 417 medical records of children admitted with severe malaria. The sample size was calculated using the EpiInfoStatcalc formula with the following assumptions: case fatality rate of hyperparasitemia of 29.4% based on a 2016 study conducted in a tertiary care hospital in India [18]; a 95% confidence level (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Z}\upalpha/2$$\end{document}Zα/2=1.96); margin of error(d) of 0.05; and a power of (80%). After adding a 10% (38) allowance for potential non-response, the final sample size was determined to be 418 children admitted to the pediatric ward of Gambella General Hospital.

Study population and sampling technique

All pediatric children with severe malaria who were admitted to the pediatric ward of Gambella General Hospital and recorded in the disease registration logbook during the study period served as the source population. The study sample consisted of randomly selected medical records of children aged 1 month to 14 years who were admitted with severe malaria between May 1, 2022, and May 1, 2024. The proportionate sample size for each period was determined based on the total number of children admitted to the pediatric ward over the two-year study period. Additionally, the required sample for each year was selected using a systematic random sampling technique. The inclusion criteria were complete medical records of children admitted with malaria between May 1, 2022, and May 1, 2024. Records with unclear clinical diagnoses of severe malaria, as well as those documenting children who left against medical advice, were excluded.

Data collection

Data were collected by trained general practitioners at the hospital. The data collection was conducted to the highest standards, supported by the Medical Doctors affiliated with this institution. A supervisor provided continuous monitoring and oversight throughout the data collection period.

Ethical clearance

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Mattu University’s College of Health Sciences (Reference No. RCSIL/342/16). This approval was presented to Gambella General Hospital to facilitate communication with the Medical Director. The study protocol adhered to the principles of Declaration of Helsinki. Data collection was conducted in accordance with ethical standards. As retrospective review, the study posed minimal risk to participants and ensured of their rights and welfare. Access to the children’s medical charts was granted by the medical records supervisor, and all data were stored securely to maintain confidentiality.

Variables of the study

Dependent variable: The treatment outcome, categorized as either dead or survived. Independent variables: Sociodemographic factors, clinical determinants, antimalarial drugs administered, Plasmodium species, and other relevant and laboratory factors. The operational definitions used in this study were adopted from previously published research [19-22].

Data analysis

Data were collected using structured questionnaires adopted from studies at Arbaminch General Hospital and Karat Primary Hospital, Ethiopia, enabling extraction of all relevant information from the children's medical records. A pilot study involving 10% of the sample (42 charts) was conducted before the main data collection. The checklist included three sections: socio-demographic information, clinical profile and other relevant data and treatment outcomes of severe malaria. Four medical doctors collected the data, with one serving as supervisor. Data quality was ensured through a well-organized checklist, proper training of data collectors, and checks for duplicate data entries and validation. Internal consistency was acceptable, with Cronbach's alpha of 0.71.

Data were entered into EPIDATA version 3.1 and exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Binary and multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess the effect of independent variables on the outcome. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in a multivariable model. Predictors with a p-value < 0.05 in the final model were considered significantly associated with the outcome. Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) was used to describe the strength of association between predictors and the outcome variable.

Results

A 99.8% response rate was achieved, with 417 children included in the analysis. The mean age of participants was 4.03 ± 2.11 years. The majority of the children were male (250; 60%), lived in urban areas (356; 85.4%), arrived within five days of the onset (331; 79.38%), presented with prostration (312; 74.8%), had Plasmodium falciparum infection (382; 91.6%), and stayed in the hospital for seven days or fewer (374; 89.69%) (Tables 1 and 2). Treatment outcome of severe malaria and anti-malarial drugs

All 417 hospitalized children received Artesunate (IV) as anti-malarial treatment. The overall case fatality rate was 9.11% (95% CI: 6.35%, 11.87%) among children admitted with severe malaria at Gambella General Hospital (Fig. 4). The highest mortality was observed in children with Plasmodium falciparum infection (8.2%), followed by those hospitalized for ≤ 7 days (7.7%), children with comorbidity (6.5%), and children presented with prostration (6.2%). Determinants of treatment outcome of severe malaria

The following factors were found to be significantly associated with treatment outcome of severe malaria in children: residence, coma, respiratory distress, convulsion, pulmonary edema, anemia, co-morbidity, acute kidney injury, hospital stay, and time to admission. Binary logistic regression analysis was first performed to identify variables with a p-value < 0.25 for inclusion in the multivariable analysis. Multivariate logistic regression analysis, controlling for potential confounders, showed that convulsions, severe anemia, and comorbidities were strongly associated with the treatment outcome of severe malaria.

The odds of death among children with convulsions were 4.13 times (AOR = 4.13, 95% CI: 1.72, 9.92) higher than the odds of death among children without convulsions. Children with severe anemia had 5.32 times higher odds of dying compared to those without severe anemia (AOR = 5.32, 95% CI: 1.94, 14.62). Similarly, children comorbidities had a 4.99-fold increased risk of death compared to those without comorbid condition (AOR = 4.99, 95% CI: 2.18, 11.40) (Table 3). Discussion

In endemic areas, severe malaria can be life-threatening in children, requiring immediate medical attention and parenteral treatment. The WHO emphasizes the importance of timely intervention to reduce morbidity, mortality, and the socioeconomic burden on infected children. Moreover, understanding the epidemiological factors that drive the disease can help implement effective control measures and improve treatment outcomes in vulnerable populations.

Since managing severe malaria in children remains a major public health challenge, particularly in high prevalence settings, it is essential to understand treatment outcomes and the factors influence them. Given that no recent study in Ethiopia has examined the treatment outcome of severe malaria and its associated factors among children aged 1 month to 14 years, this study evaluated children admitted with severe malaria to Gambella General Hospital between May 1, 2022, and May 1, 2024.

Among the 417 children admitted with severe malaria, 60% were male, yielding a male-to-female ratio of 1.49:1.This finding aligns with studies conducted in Ethiopia [23] and India [24]. However, it contrasts with a study from Zimbabwe, where the majority of participants were female [25]. These differences may be attributed to environmental and socioeconomic variations across study settings.

Plasmodium falciparum accounted for the majority of severe malaria cases (91.7%) among hospitalized children, consistent with findings from studies in Khartoum and Ethiopia [26, 27]. This similarity may reflect comparable climatic and environmental conditions across the study areas. In contrast, Plasmodium vivax caused only 9.3% of admissions, which differs from its global burden [28]. This lower proportion may be due generally less severe clinical presentation associated with P. vivax.

The case fatality rate of severe malaria in children varies across studies and regions. According to the WHO, the global case fatality rate for severe malaria in children ranges from10% and 30%( 29, 30). The current study conducted at Gambela General Hospital found a case fatality rate of 9.11%, which is comparable to global estimates. This finding is also consistent with studies conducted in Nigeria [2], India [24], Uganda [31], and Nigeria [32], which reported case fatality rates ranging from 8.2 to 12.3%. In contrast, a much higher rate (28.8%) was reported in Zimbabwe [25]. The relatively lower case fatality rate observed in this study may be attributed to the use of microscopy in all cases and improvements in the management of severe malaria due to the study period [33].

On the other hand, lower case fatality rates have been reported in several studies including, Cameron (3.7%) [34], Kenya (1.4–7%) [20], Uganda (1.3%) [31], a governmental hospital in Nigeria (1.4%) [30], Khartoum (5.5%) [27] and Jimma hospital (2.3%) [11]. The higher case fatality rate observed in the present study may be attributed to the strong association of a specific predictors such as convulsion, severe anemia, and comorbid illness with mortality from severe malaria in the region.

Apart from assessing the overall case fatality rate of severe malaria among admitted children, the case fatality rate for each predictor variable was assessed. In a study conducted in India [24], the CFRs for coma (61.9%), ARD (81.2%), shock (55.3%), hyperparasitemia (30%), and hypoglycemia 91.7% were reported. Similarly, a study from Uganda [31] revealed that coma and severe anemia contributed CFR of 33.3% and 19.5%, respectively. In the current study, CFRs were: coma (18.5%), convulsion (27%), acute kidney injury (18.4%), pulmonary edema (19.7%), severe anemia (31.8%), and comorbid illness (16.9%). The relatively lower CFRs observed here may be partly attributed to timely access to healthcare services during the study period.

In a study conducted in Nigeria [32], decompensated shock, acute kidney injury, and respiratory distress were significant predictors of mortality. Similarly, hypoglycemia in Uganda [31], female sex in Zimbabwe [25], delayed presentation to hospital in Zambia [29] study were all significantly associated with the death outcome. Even though acute kidney injury, respiratory distress, and delayed presentation to the hospital were found to have a significant association in the bivariate analysis of this study, they couldn`t sustain to be significant in the multivariable analysis. The mitigation of these independent predictors in the multivariable analysis of this study may be due to the effectiveness of the current treatment protocols and timing of presentations [34].

Convulsion was a poor predictor of severe malaria in this study, which is also in line with a study done in India [35]. Similar to the current study, a study carried out in Nigeria [30] indicated that comorbid disease was one of the poor prognostic factors of severe malaria in children. According to a hospital-based study carried out in India, severe anemia was the other poor predictive factor for severe malaria in children [36].

It is important to take into account the limitations of the present study when interpreting the data, as the study design is retrospective. First, since the data was collected from existing medical records which were not originally designed for research, there have been instances of missing information, inconsistent reports and incorrect registration. Due to these factors, the findings from the present study may not be generalizable to other populations or settings. Second, the study design has limitations in measuring or controlling for relevant predictor variables such as the family's socioeconomic standing, the educational attainment of the mother and father, and bed net usage patterns. This may lead to overlooking important variables or relationships that were not taken into account in the original data collection process.

Conclusion

Since treating severe malaria in children continues to be a major public health burden, particularly in areas where the disease is highly prevalent, it is imperative to comprehend treatment results and related determinants. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of treatment for severe malaria and related variables in children who were admitted to Gambella General Hospital's pediatric ward. 9.11% was the total case fatality rate linked to severe malaria. Convulsion, severe anemia, and concomitant disease were the three major determinant factors linked to the treatment result of severe malaria evaluated in this study. Since treatment outcomes and mortality rates can be greatly impacted by prevention strategies, early disease detection, and anticipating severe malaria severity features, it is important to emphasize the importance of planned comprehensive interventional packages in reducing the mortality and morbidity impacts of severe malaria among the study population.
