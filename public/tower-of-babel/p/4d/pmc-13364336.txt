A Rare Rescue Case of Post-biopsy Pseudoaneurysm Treated by Direct Percutaneous Glue Injection after Failed Embolization
Authors: Than Van Sy, Bui Trung Nghia, Duong Trong Hien, Nguyen Dinh Minh, Le Thanh Dung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Post-biopsy pseudoaneurysm is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication, particularly in hypervascular spinal lesions. We report a 67-year-old man with a hypervascular vertebral lesion in the right L2 transverse process who developed a retroperitoneal hematoma after computed tomography-guided biopsy. Initial transarterial embolization provided only temporary hemostasis. Due to technical constraints, including the presence of a spinal artery variant, repeat transcatheter arterial embolization was not feasible. Fluoroscopic computed tomography-guided direct injection of n-butyl cyanoacrylate-iodized oil into the lesion achieved definitive hemostasis. The patient remained stable without further transfusion. Histopathology confirmed solitary plasmacytoma. A follow-up computed tomography scan at 3 months demonstrated resolution of the hematoma without rebleeding. This case illustrates the utility of percutaneous n-butyl cyanoacrylate embolization as an effective rescue option in anatomically complex post-biopsy hemorrhage.

Introduction

Percutaneous biopsy of vertebral lesions is a widely used and generally safe diagnostic technique [1, 2]. Although bleeding is a recognized complication, most post-biopsy hemorrhages are self-limiting and require no intervention. However, in hypervascular spinal tumors, clinically significant hemorrhage may occur and can occasionally be life-threatening, necessitating prompt hemostatic management [3, 4].

Direct percutaneous embolization using NBCA has been described as a salvage technique for controlling arterial bleeding when transarterial access is technically restricted or incomplete [5-7]. Pseudoaneurysm formation after biopsy of hypervascular spinal lesions is rare, and reports describing the use of percutaneous NBCA injection in this specific setting remain extremely limited.

Herein, we present a case of massive retroperitoneal hemorrhage following CT-guided biopsy of a hypervascular vertebral lesion. After unsuccessful endovascular hemostasis, salvage percutaneous NBCA injection into the lesion achieved definitive bleeding control. This case highlights the feasibility and potential utility of percutaneous NBCA embolization in select post-biopsy vascular complications when conventional endovascular management is insufficient.

Case Presentation

A 67-year-old man with a hypervascular lesion involving the right L2 transverse process was referred for CT-guided biopsy (Figure 1A-C). Using a 16-gauge coaxial system (STARCUTⓇ; TSK Laboratory, Tochigi, Japan) (Figure 1D-F), two core samples were obtained from different regions of the lesion. During the second pass, the inner biopsy needle (which features a 25-mm throw beyond the coaxial tip) inadvertently traversed the anterior cortical margin (Figure 1E, dashed arrow). This was accompanied by an early hematoma surrounding the right iliopsoas muscle (Figure 1E, asterisk). Local hemostasis was performed by delivering a thick gelatin sponge slurry (manually prepared with normal saline) through the 16-gauge coaxial needle. The needle tip was maintained in a fixed position during injection to achieve immediate mechanical tamponade at the biopsy site (Figure 1F). The patient was monitored for 30 minutes in the interventional suite, during which no clinical or radiologic progression of bleeding was observed, and was then transferred back to the oncology department in stable condition.

Twenty-four hours later, he developed acute right flank pain and progressive abdominal fullness. Laboratory testing showed a declining hemoglobin level. Ultrasound demonstrated enlargement of the retroperitoneal hematoma, and contrast-enhanced CT revealed a pseudoaneurysm within the enhancing soft-tissue component of the L2 lesion at the biopsy site with extravasation to adjacent retroperitoneal space (Figure 2). Although CT could not definitively distinguish whether the bleeding originated from intralesional or adjacent retroperitoneal vessels, the continuity between the lesion, pseudoaneurysm, and hematoma suggested an active bleeding focus at the biopsy site. Emergency angiography was therefore indicated.

Contrast-enhanced CT 24 hours post-biopsy shows pseudoaneurysm formation (solid white arrows) at the biopsy site in axial (A) and sagittal (B) planes, with the targeted tumor in the transverse process (dashed white arrows). Associated contrast extravasation and a large right-sided retroperitoneal hematoma are also present.

Emergency angiography confirmed the presence of a large pseudoaneurysm from the right L2 lumbar artery, corresponding to the region of the prior biopsy (Figure 3). Super-selective embolization was performed using a 1:4 mixture of NBCA (HistoacrylⓇ, B. Braun Surgical, SA Rubí, Spain) and iodized oil (LipiodolⓇ Ultra-Fluide; Guerbet, Paris, France) (Figure 3). Given potential collateral perfusion, the right L1 lumbar artery was also prophylactically embolized with the same mixture of NBCA and iodized oil. Angiography of the right L3 lumbar artery demonstrated a radiculomedullary branch with a midline cranial course ascending along the spinal canal. This trajectory was anatomically consistent with a spinal artery, raising the possibility of anterior spinal artery origin (Figure 4A and B). Although cone-beam CT was not available, this angiographic morphology raised concern for spinal cord ischemia risk. Consequently, embolization of the L3 artery was avoided (Figure 4C).

(B) Selective DSA of the right L2 lumbar artery (black arrow) after embolization shows complete occlusion of the pseudoaneurysm, with no residual contrast opacification.

Despite initial improvement, the patient developed worsening abdominal fullness and right flank pain the following day, accompanied by a precipitous hemoglobin decline (to 6.6 g/dL). Follow-up ultrasound confirmed enlargement of the hematoma. A non-contrast CT performed prior to the rescue procedure demonstrated dense NBCA-iodized oil casting within the previously embolized right L1 and L2 lumbar arteries. While this indicated anatomical occlusion of the primary feeders, the persistent bleeding suggested a collateral-fed source. To avoid additional contrast load in a patient with significant anemia, a repeat dynamic CT was omitted, and an immediate salvage direct percutaneous NBCA injection was prioritized, followed by hematoma decompression (Figure 5).

CT-guided percutaneous NBCA embolization. (A) CT shows enlargement of the retroperitoneal hematoma despite prior transarterial embolization. (B) An 18G needle (black arrow) is advanced into the lytic lesion at the biopsy site under CT guidance; note the retroperitoneal drain catheter (white arrow). (C) CT fluoroscopy during glue injection demonstrates complete filling of the hemorrhagic cavity within the lesion. (D-E) Axial and sagittal images confirm the NBCA-iodized oil cast filling the lesion (dashed arrows), with a small amount extending anteriorly through the biopsy-induced cortical breach. (F) A follow-up CT scan at 3 months demonstrates significant reduction in the size of the retroperitoneal hematoma with residual stable organized components.

Under CT guidance (SOMATOM Scope, Siemens, Forchheim, Germany), an 18-gauge needle (2-1/2″ Green Surflo ETFE I.V. Catheter) was advanced into the lytic lesion of the right L2 transverse process, targeting the suspected pseudoaneurysm within the tumor (Figure 5B). Needle positioning was confirmed by spontaneous backflow of blood, after which 1 mL of 5% dextrose solution was injected. An 1:2 NBCA-iodized oil mixture (1.5 mL) was slowly administered under continuous real-time CT fluoroscopic guidance. The injection was halted once multi-planar imaging demonstrated satisfactory opacification of the cavity, which correlated with a perceived increase in injection resistance. The needle was withdrawn 20 seconds after completion without significant resistance. Post-procedural CT confirmed dense NBCA-iodized oil casting within the cavity, with minor extensions through the cortical breach and along the needle path that did not result in complications (Figure 5D and E).

Simultaneously, a 14F retroperitoneal drain (BT-PD2 Series, Bioteq Corp., Taiwan) was inserted percutaneously to manage the hematoma. The patient received two units of packed red blood cells, and post-procedural hemoglobin increased from 6.6 to 8.8 g/dL. Over the following 24 hours, the patient showed rapid clinical improvement, and hemoglobin levels stabilized at 10.0-10.2 g/dL without further transfusion.

Histopathological analysis confirmed a plasma cell neoplasm consistent with plasmacytoma.

The patient was discharged in stable condition 8 days after the biopsy and was referred to the hematology-oncology service for further management. Follow-up contrast-enhanced CT at 3 months demonstrated significant reduction in the size of the retroperitoneal hematoma with no evidence of rebleeding (Figure 5F).

Discussion

Post-biopsy hemorrhage is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication, especially in hypervascular spinal lesions. Transarterial embolization is typically the first-line approach to control active bleeding, owing to its minimally invasive nature and ability to precisely occlude arterial feeders. However, its effectiveness relies heavily on accurate localization of the bleeding source and safe endovascular access. In this case, rebleeding occurred despite initial embolization of the right L1-L2 lumbar arteries, prompting consideration of an alternative source or mechanism.

In this case, a pseudoaneurysm developed along the biopsy tract within a hypervascular soft-tissue component of the lesion. CT demonstrated a cortical defect through which hemorrhage extended into the retroperitoneum. Initial embolization of the L1-L2 lumbar arteries achieved only temporary control. Based on the non-contrast CT findings of dense NBCA-iodized oil casting within the L1-L2 arteries, which suggested complete anatomical occlusion, the persistent hemorrhage was hypothesized to originate from vascular channels not excluded by the initial TAE, such as intralesional or collateral pathways. The presence of a radiculomedullary branch arising from L3 further limited the safety of repeat embolization.

Angiography of the right L3 lumbar artery revealed a radiculomedullary branch with a midline cranial course, raising suspicion for anterior spinal artery origin. In the absence of cone-beam CT for definitive confirmation, embolization was deferred to avoid spinal cord ischemia. This limitation may have contributed to suboptimal control of potential collateral supply.

When endovascular access is restricted by anatomic variants or incomplete exclusion of the bleeding focus, direct percutaneous NBCA injection offers a valuable salvage option. NBCA provides rapid polymerization, controlled penetration, and durable occlusion, enabling precise filling of a confined hemorrhagic cavity when the target is safely accessible under CT guidance. Although underreported in the setting of post-biopsy vascular injuries of the spine, percutaneous NBCA injection has demonstrated efficacy in managing deep soft-tissue or visceral hemorrhage [5, 8]. In the present case, the favorable outcome was achieved through precise needle positioning under real-time guidance, which provided direct access to the bleeding cavity. The controlled use of high-concentration NBCA, monitored by continuous CT fluoroscopy and tactile feedback, allowed for immediate and definitive hemostasis despite the initial challenges of incomplete transarterial embolization.

This case highlights two important clinical considerations. First, direct percutaneous NBCA injection should be considered a viable salvage strategy when TAE is technically limited or fails to achieve hemostasis, especially in hypervascular tumors with complex vascular anatomy. Second, if bleeding is observed during or immediately after biopsy―particularly in lesions with potential communication to large anatomical compartments such as the retroperitoneum―close monitoring and early, definitive hemostasis should be prioritized. In such settings, gelatin sponge may provide only transient control, whereas NBCA may offer a more durable option.

The favorable outcome in this case was likely facilitated by two key factors: the precise imaging delineation of the pseudoaneurysmal cavity and the direct intralesional needle access, which together enabled a targeted embolization. Nevertheless, several limitations should be recognized. First, due to the clinical urgency and the priority of minimizing contrast load in a patient with severe anemia, a repeat dynamic contrast-enhanced CT was omitted prior to the rescue procedure. Consequently, the identification of specific collateral channels remains a clinical hypothesis based on procedural observations rather than an objectively verified finding. Second, our institution currently lacks a hybrid Angio-CT suite, which would have provided an optimal platform for simultaneous digital subtraction angiography and CT assessment. To compensate for these constraints, we integrated real-time CT fluoroscopy to determine the procedural endpoint. While this approach proved effective in this life-threatening situation, it remains a technically demanding strategy that requires significant experience.

In summary, this case illustrates the potential role of CT-guided percutaneous NBCA-iodized oil injection as a salvage technique for controlling intratumoral hemorrhage after failed or incomplete embolization. This minimally invasive approach should be considered in anatomically complex vascular injuries where endovascular access is limited or high-risk.
