Unequal Hearts: Racial Disparities Persist in Heart Transplant Outcomes
Authors: Juan Carlos Batlle, Katherine A. A. Clark
Published: 2025

For those patients with end‐stage heart disease, heart transplantation (HT) remains the gold standard treatment for improved long‐term survival. 1 , 2 Despite medical advancements and increased awareness of health equity, racial disparities continue to plague the landscape of HT. The inequality of care for these often critically ill patients challenges the seemingly fundamental principles that should underpin the provision of all care spanning both the pre‐ and posttransplant course. 3 , 4 , 5 For example, prior work has demonstrated that Black patients are less likely to be referred for advanced heart failure therapies, including transplantation, and often experience longer wait times on transplant lists, leading to increased waitlist deaths. 6 Further, in the posttransplant period, several investigations have noted disparities in graft survival, rejection rates, and the development of complications such as renal dysfunction and diabetes. 7 , 8 Although more recent studies suggest a possible narrowing of this gap in outcomes, following the policy changes made in 2018 by the United Network for Organ Sharing heart allocation update 9 , 10 ; these long‐standing complex disparities underscore the need for continued investigation and targeted interventions to ensure equitable access to HT and optimize outcomes for all patients, regardless of race or ethnicity.

In this issue of the Journal of the America Heart Association (JAHA), Hamilton and colleagues present a contemporary retrospective analysis describing racial disparities of posttransplant longer‐term outcomes using the SRTR (Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients). 11 The study includes 15 873 adult (aged ≥18 years) HT recipients, categorized into Black (23%), non‐Hispanic White (63%), or other racial or ethnic group including Hispanic, Asian, and other/mixed race (14%), from 2017 to 2022. The mean age was 53±13.0 years, 27% recipients were women, and Black patients were significantly more likely to have nonischemic cardiomyopathy compared with non‐Black patients. The study's primary aim was to evaluate disparities in long‐term graft survival, with secondary outcomes including acute rejection, progression of renal dysfunction, and new‐onset diabetes. The findings revealed that while 1‐year graft survival rates were comparable across racial groups (91.8% for Black recipients versus 91.1% for non‐Black recipients; P=0.21), Black recipients experienced a statistically significant reduction in 3‐year graft survival rates compared with non‐Hispanic White recipients (83.3% versus 85.7%; P=0.006). The lack of association at 1 year was unchanged after adjustment, but the disparities at 3 years persisted even after adjusting for several socioeconomic status and clinical confounders, including insurance status, education level, and preexisting conditions (adjusted odds ratio for socioeconomic status plus clinical variables for graft failure at 3 years, 1.30 [95%; CI, 1.13–1.49]), suggesting that factors beyond access to care and preexisting conditions contribute to these inequitable outcomes. Moreover, Black recipients also had significantly higher rates of durable ventricular assist devices and intra‐aortic balloon pump use at the time of transplant (35.6% versus 30.3% for durable ventricular assist devices, and 24.8% versus 20.1% for intra‐aortic balloon pump; P<0.0001), suggesting later referral or diagnosis. In regards to the secondary outcomes, Black recipients exhibited increased rates of acute rejection episodes (12.4% versus 10.2%; P<0.05), progression of renal dysfunction (40.9% versus 37.1%; P<0.05), and new‐onset diabetes (10.8% versus 7.1%; P<0.05) at 3 years after transplant compared with non‐Black patients. Of note, other races were not associated with risk of graft failure (adjusted odds ratio, 1.09 [95% CI, 0.92–1.29]).

The authors can be commended for this comprehensive review of a large contemporary data set and a focus on longer‐term posttransplant outcomes. This study provides an essential update of racial disparities in HT outcomes, which provides crucial information as clinical practices and organ allocation policies have evolved. Regarding pretransplant outcomes, Hamilton et al demonstrate that Black recipients demonstrated a greater need for mechanical support, including intra‐aortic balloon pump and durable ventricular assist devices before HT, potentially indicating later referral or diagnosis. 11 Furthermore, for posttransplant outcomes, prior studies were limited by shorter follow‐up periods, potentially missing the emergence of disparities in complication rates that manifest years later. Thus, by examining longer‐term outcomes, of up to 3 years after transplant, this study provides a more thorough assessment of multiple posttransplant outcomes, more clearly illuminating the challenges faced by Black HT recipients over time, affecting their quality of posttransplant life. 11 Notably, their findings further clarify the seemingly discrepant posttransplant outcome findings of the current body of literature. Their data suggest that, although initial 1‐year posttransplant outcome inequities may have narrowed across various races and ethnicities since the 2018 United Network for Organ Sharing heart allocation policy change, they actually diverge later in the posttransplant course and significantly impact long‐term posttransplant outcomes, despite adjusting for racial differences. Thus, Hamilton and colleagues demonstrate that the limited follow‐up periods of prior work likely were not able to reveal the cumulative effects of chronic complications differentially manifested across races and ethnicities, as the longer follow‐up period reveals significantly worse 3‐year graft survival outcomes among Black recipients. 3 , 9 , 11

Another notable strength of the study lies in its granular assessment of multiple posttransplant outcomes, extending beyond the traditional focus on graft survival alone. While prior studies have primarily examined single complications, such as acute rejection or renal dysfunction, this manuscript provides a holistic view by simultaneously evaluating disparities in graft survival, acute rejection, progression of renal dysfunction, and new‐onset diabetes, considering how these complications are potentially related. This broader scope highlights the multifaceted and interdependent nature of racial disparities in HT outcomes and underscores the need for comprehensive yet personalized screening and management to address these complex clinical challenges. For instance, this study found that Black recipients had a higher rate of progression to renal dysfunction, which may be related to a differential risk of rejection and required immunosuppression dosing, as compared with non‐Hispanic White recipients. These findings suggest the need for a more tailored approach to renal monitoring in routine posttransplant care. 12 , 13

Another key contribution of this work is its emphasis on the impact of biological factors on differential posttransplant outcomes. The authors conclude that their findings challenge the perception that disparities in HT outcomes can be solely explained by socioeconomic factors or preexisting comorbid clinical conditions, a common recent focus of the literature. To demonstrate this, the authors adjusted for a range of potential confounders in their multivariable regression models; however, significant disparities persisted, suggesting that other unmeasured factors, such as genetic predisposition, immune responses, or subtle differences in clinical management, may contribute to these inequities. 14 These findings suggest that more complex and nuanced pathogeneses of racial disparities calls for further investigation of biological and systemic factors that may be driving these such inequities.

As is the case with any observational study, these findings must be interpreted within the context of both selection bias and unmeasured confounding. While the authors adjusted for a range of socioeconomic and clinical factors, the potential for residual confounding remains. Unmeasured variables, such as differences in adherence to immunosuppressive medications, access to specialized care, or variations in individual immune responses, could contribute to the observed disparities. Furthermore, it is important to note that the study relies on broad racial categories that are self‐reported as Black, non‐Hispanic White, or other racial and ethnic groups (including Hispanic, Asian, and other/mixed race) which may mask the heterogeneity within these groups and overlook the influence of specific genetic ancestries. Finally, the SRTR database, while comprehensive, depends on the accuracy and completeness of data entered by clinicians, potentially introducing biases related to reporting practices. 11

The work by Hamilton and colleagues emphasizes the urgent need for further research to elucidate the mechanisms driving these persistent racial disparities. 11 Not only does this study make a loud and clear call for the importance of further work to examine even longer‐term outcomes of HT recipients to better understand the pathogenesis of differential outcomes, but it also takes a step in the right direction of explicitly focusing on exploring the role of genetic factors, immune responses, and specific clinical practices that may contribute to the observed differences. Concurrently, additional longitudinal studies with more specific data on medication adherence, access to care, and patient‐reported outcomes are crucial for identifying potential modifiable factors. Additionally, qualitative research exploring the lived experiences of Black HT recipients can provide valuable insights into the challenges they face and inform the development of culturally tailored support programs. Thus, understanding the intertwined yet specific biological, social, and systemic factors at play is essential for developing effective and equitable strategies to improve long‐term outcomes for all HT recipients, regardless of their racial and ethnic background.
