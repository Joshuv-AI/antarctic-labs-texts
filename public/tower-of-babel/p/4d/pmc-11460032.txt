Retrospective analysis of multiparametric MRI in predicting complete pathologic response of neo-adjuvant chemotherapy in bladder cancer
Authors: Daan De Maeseneer, Pieter De Visschere, Mats Van den Broecke, Felix Delbare, Geert Villeirs, Sofie Verbeke, Valérie Fonteyne, Charles Van Praet, Karel Decaestecker, Alexander Decruyenaere, Sylvie Rottey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMuscle invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) treatment combines systemic therapy and radical cystectomy (RC) or local (chemo-)radiotherapy. Response to systemic therapy is an important outcome predictor but is difficult to assess pre-operatively.MethodsWe analyzed multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) in consecutive MIBC patients receiving cisplatin-based neo-adjuvant chemotherapy at our institution. Two readers, blinded for pathological outcome, independently scored mpMRI before and after 2 and 4 cycles using both a qualitative 3-step method and nacVI-RADS. We analyzed accuracy of mpMRI scores to predict pathologic complete response (pCR) and inter-observer agreement.ResultsWe analyzed 46 patients receiving NAC, 6 patients did not undergo RC after NAC and were excluded. Eleven out of 40 (28%) patients showed a pCR. mpMRI could be assessed in over 90% of patients. Radiologic complete response (rCR) using both methods was significantly associated with pCR, with an overall specificity of 96% and sensitivity of 36% and a high inter-observer agreement. rCR as assessed by the 3-step score was significantly associated with disease free survival (DFS) benefit.ConclusionThe use of nacVI-RADS can predict pCR after NAC with high specificity but low sensitivity and a high inter-observer agreement. A 3-step score adds value in determining local residual disease, rCR assessed by this method could correlate with DFS benefit. mpMRI scores should be prospectively assessed in future trials of multimodal management of MIBC and can be a predictive asset in routine clinical management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-024-01441-y.

Take home messages

• mpMRI can be used to evaluate response to neo-adjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) in muscle-invasive Bladder Cancer using nacVI-RADS.

• Radiologic complete response (rCR) on mpMRI can accurately predict complete pathological response.

• Adding a 3-step assessment to nacVI-RADS, improves residual disease assessment and could be included in future versions of nacVI-RADS.

Background

Worldwide, bladder cancer is the 12th most diagnosed cancer. In Western Europe and Northern America age-standardized rate is 20/10,000 in men and 5/10,000 in women [1]. Age standardized rate of mortality is 5.2 / 100,000 in men (8th of all cancers) and 2.2/100,000 in women (14th of all cancers). Radical cystectomy (RC) with pelvic node dissection is the gold standard in treating muscle invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) [2]. A meta-analysis of neo-adjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) in MIBC showed a 16% reduction in overall death risk compared to locoregional therapy alone, especially in node negative patients [3]. NAC probably reduces the risk of peri-operative spill of tumor cells and lowers the burden of micro-metastatic disease, but in non-responders could also delay curative treatment. Response to NAC regimens depend on the type of cisplatin based regimen, 50–60% of patients with MIBC will have residual invasive disease at RC [4, 5]. The VESPER trial showed superior pathological response rates of dose dense cisplatin-methotrexate-vinblastin-doxorubicin (ddMVAC) compared to cisplatin-gemcitabine [6].

Radiological assessment of response to NAC in MIBC is difficult. Clinical staging including Computed Tomography (CT) imaging will understage MIBC in a significant number of patients [7]. In contrast, a recent study showed downstaging on CT after completion of NAC is a strong predictor of overall survival [8]. Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) of the bladder guidelines have been developed. mpMRI has a high accuracy in local staging of MIBC before treatment [9] and downstaging on mpMRI performed 24 h after NAC and before surgery showed promising results in early prediction of pathologic response [10]. A novel categorical staging system was proposed based on sequential Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System (VI-RADS) evaluation, the Neo-Adjuvant Chemotherapy VI-RADS (nacVI-RADS) [11]. The objective of this study is to assess the accuracy of mpMRI in predicting complete pathologic response after NAC in MIBC patients using both nacVI-RADS and a 3-step qualitative assessment first proposed by Necchi et al. [12].

Methods

Design, setting, and participants

We identified consecutive patients treated with ddMVAC for NAC for MIBC from 2013 to 2019 at our institution using an electronic chemotherapy database. All patients had pathologic confirmation of MIBC on transurethral resection (TURBT). Patient characteristics including age, sex, prior treatment for non-MIBC, smoking history and pathological results of TURBT were extracted from the database. Patients with predominantly variant (> 50%) histology at diagnosis were excluded, squamous differentiation was allowed. All patients included were cisplatin eligible with normal left ventricular function and adequate renal function. In concordance with our institution’s guidelines, mpMRI was scheduled before, after 2 cycles to detect early disease progression, and after 4 cycles of ddMVAC. Patients who ultimately did not undergo RC were excluded from this analysis.

mpMRI assessment

mpMRI were all performed on a 3.0T MRI system (Magnetom Prisma Fit, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany), with an external phased-array surface coil. In the patient preparation motion and susceptibility artifacts were reduced by intravenous administration of 1 ml Hyoscine butylbromide (Buscopan®). The imaging protocol consisted of 1 mm coronal three-dimensional spin echo (T2-SPACE) with 3 mm orthogonal reconstructions in the sagittal and axial planes, 4 mm sagittal and axial turbo spin echo T2-WI without fat suppression, 4 mm axial turbo spin echo T1-WI, 4 mm axial DWI EP2d with b values 50, 400, 800 and calculated b1200 and corresponding ADC map. 3,5 mm axial T1-WI (VIBE-TWIST) dynamic contrast enhanced series and 4 mm axial turbo spin echo T1-WI after Gadolinium. The entire pelvis was imaged from the aortic bifurcation to the inferior margin of the pubic symphysis, including the whole bladder, proximal urethra, pelvic nodes and in males the prostate.

Outcome measurement

MRI characteristics

All mpMRI were independently reviewed by two radiologists, one with more than 10 years of experience in BC imaging and one fifth-year resident. Both readers were blinded to the final pathology. The readers scored for VI-RADS and nacVI-RADS after 2 and 4 cycles of chemotherapy described elsewhere in detail [11]. Two radiologists independently scored mpMRI images pre-, during and post-treatment on a 5-point scale, after which a consensus scoring was reached. Scores 1 and 2 define radiologic complete response (rCR) in nacVI-RADS.

Additionally, the readers used a 3-step score to characterize the following parameters: residual disease at T1-weighted (T1W) and T2-weighted (T2W) images, presence of hyperintense spots within the bladder wall on DWI, and presence of pathological contrast enhancement on DCE MRI as depicted in Fig. 1 [12]. For step 1, “Yes” was considered when there was an intravesical nodule, mass or wall thickening with intermediate signal intensity to urine and muscle on T2W image with or without soft tissue nodularity along the serosal surface or perivesical fat stranding. No reference tissue for the signal intensity was used. For step 2, image analysis was performed qualitatively by means of visual assessment, whereby high signal on the high-b-value images corresponding to a low signal at ADC was defined as positive, i.e. suspicious for residual tumor tissue (‘yes’). The measurements were performed quantitatively by means of apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) measurements on the ADC maps. After independent scoring, in a second session both readers reached consensus about the scores of the discordant cases. rCR is defined by ‘No’ on all three questions.

When patients had more than one lesion, only the score of the lesion with the highest score was registered.

Histopathologic evaluation

All the RC procedures were carried out by the same experienced surgical team at our center. The cystectomy specimens were analyzed by an experienced genitourinary pathologist with more than 10 years of experience in bladder cancer. The pathology report served as the reference standard for all study cases and ypT0 was considered as pathologic complete response (pCR). Any tumor rest (including non-invasive carcinoma) on cystectomy was considered non-pCR (> ypT0).

Endpoint

The main endpoint of this study was the accuracy of rCR on mpMRI to predict pCR.

Statistical analysis

After anonymization of the database, descriptive statistics were used to describe patient characteristics and main findings in patients with a pCR and non-pCR. For the 3-step score each mpMRI step consisted of a dichotomous variable (yes or no), and the proportions of pCR of each question were calculated. Fisher’s exact test was used to determine statistical significance. A ROC analyses was performed using a multivariable model with output of accuracy results for negative answers on > = 1, >= 2, and 3 questions. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. nacVI-RADS was analyzed in the same manner, using score 1 and 2 for rCR. Inter-observer agreement was assessed using an unweighted Cohen’s Kappa method. Unadjusted Cox regression was used to calculate equality of survival, patients entered analysis on date of TURBT and were censored on reoccurrence, death or last follow-up staging for DFS and death or last patient contact for OS. Cox regression analysis was performed for pCR and both rCR definitions at different time points.

All statistical analysis was performed using STATA/IC 16.1 (StataCorp, Tx, USA).

Inter-observer agreement

Agreement between observers using the 3-step score was substantial (72%, Kappa 0.61). Agreement between the 2 readers on VIRADS was moderate to near perfect after C2 and C4. nacVI-RADS scoring showed agreement in 80–98% of cases, with substantial to near perfect Kappa values (Table 4).

Survival analysis

We performed an exploratory survival analysis. At time of analysis, median follow-up of survivors was 58 months (IQR 43–71). Disease reoccurred in 13 patients within first 24 months, of whom 8 had a local reoccurrence. Ten patients died during follow-up, only 1 patient without prior disease reoccurrence. Median overall survival (mOS) and disease-free survival (mDFS) was not reached for the whole study group. No disease reoccurrence and only 1 death was noted in the pCR group. Regarding mpMRI defined groups, only in the “yes, yes, yes” group mOS was reached (40 months, 95% CI 14 - NR). Using nacVI-RADS, mOS was only reached in group 5 evaluated after cycle 2 (39 months, 95% CI 14 -NR).

DFS in pCR patients is significantly higher compared to non-pCR patients (Cox p 0.01). Analysis of mpMRI defined groups showed a significant DFS benefit for rCR patients using the 3-step score (p 0.02), but not for nacVI-RADS (p 0.2) (Fig. 3). Again “yes-yes-yes” on 3-step score reached mDFS of 20 months (8.4 - NR). Using nacVI-RADS both scores 3 and 5 reached mDFS after cycle 2 and 4 (16 months and 27 months, resp), regression analysis however did not show significant differences in DFS according to nacVI-RADS grouping at both time points.

Discussion

We present the results of a retrospective study in a university hospital focusing on pCR prediction using two mpMRI scoring systems after NAC for MIBC.

Our results show that using mpMRI for response evaluation after NAC is feasible, less than 10% could not be evaluated using the three-step method, all included cases could be evaluated by nacVI-RADS. mpMRI could accurately predict pCR in 78% of patients with a specificity of 96% (3-step score) and 93% (nacVI-RADS), but low sensitivity. Inter-observer agreement was very high for both methods. pCR and rCR measured by the 3-step score (but not by nacVI-RADS) was significantly associated with DFS benefit.

In our series pCR was achieved in 28% of patients. This is lower than historical SWOG 8710 trial data (38%) and a more recent retrospective analysis (41%) by Peyton et al. [5, 13] Possible explanations are the strict definition of pCR (excluding ypTis), higher percentage of > = T3 tumors (68%), and high percentage of hydronephrosis (40%) which is a known negative prognostic factor [14]. Only one patient with hydronephrosis at diagnosis showed a pCR. The number of patients receiving > = 3 cycles of ddMVAC was very similar to the data from the large retrospective analysis of Peyton et al. (91%).

Using the 3-step score, we found a lower proportion of patients with a rCR on MRI (9.1%), compared to the analysis of data from the PURE-01 study evaluating neo-adjuvant pembrolizumab (31-44%) [12].

The value of interim assessment with mpMRI after 2 cycles is difficult to assess in our study. Significant disease progression on interim mpMRI frequently results in omission of cystectomy in our center and thus exclusion from this study. The question remains if de-escalation of NAC is possible in patients with no residual disease after 2 cycles, in our study with predominantly high-risk patients this was the case in 2 patients (5%).

Major limitations to this study are the limited number of patients in this single institution analysis. The retrospective nature of the study is partly mitigated by the blinding of the readers to the pathological results and subsequent treatment decisions.

Imaging guidelines propose mpMRI or CT scan before the start of NAC but not after completion of NAC. Historical data show a very poor correlation between clinical staging and definitive pathological staging at RC, with around 40% being upstaged [15]. Assessing tumor response on imaging has been become more prominent, especially in trials investigating bladder sparing strategies (RETAIN trial NCT02710734, ALLIANCE NCT03609216, BLADDERPATH trial). Compared to pathological staging using TURBT, imaging is less invasive, can be more standardized and is able to assess extravesical disease. In a large retrospective analysis of TURBT post-NAC, only one in three patients were accurately staged compared to the final RC specimen [16].

mpMRI in bladder cancer has been standardized using VI-RADS [17]. Prospective validation of the VI-RADS has shown to accurately differentiate between MIBC and non-MIBC [18, 19]. mpMRI characteristics have also been included in tools to predict response to NAC [20]. Some promising results in this setting have been reported, using DWI the ADC values appeared to be significantly more heterogeneous in non-responders [21], but they are insufficiently reliable.

Post-treatment interpretation of MRI is often challenging because previous TURBT and bladder instillations may cause edema, inflammation and fibrosis in the bladder wall and surrounding perivesical tissues, making it difficult to distinguish between residual BC and benign treatment-related effects [21–23]. However our study showed a high sensitivity in predicting pCR using both the 3-step method as nacVI-RADS.

After TURBT, DCE and even more DWI seem to improve the differentiation between TURBT related effects and residual tumor [24, 25]. After chemoradiotherapy, Yoshida et al. [26] reported that T2-WI alone had low sensitivity, specificity and accuracy to predict pCR (43%, 45% and 44%, respectively). DWI was significantly superior in specificity (92%) and accuracy (80%) as compared to T2 and DCE (18 and 33% respectively), but all sequences showed poor sensitivity for detecting residual disease (43–57%). Persisting early enhancement on DCE, or no increase in ADC from baseline on DWI are indicative of poor chemotherapy response [27].

RECIST 1.1 criteria fail to assess NAC for MIBC because often only bladder wall thickening is present without measurable nodular lesions [28]. Similarly, the five-point VI-RADS scoring system is mainly developed to suggest the likelihood of detrusor muscle invasion in untreated patients (or patients having received only diagnostic TURBT) [17]. In our study, many VI-RADS 5 patients at onset remained VI-RADS 5 after NAC although the size of the tumor was clearly reduced, and many VI-RADS 2 or 1 lesions (biopsy proven MIBC but not confirmed on imaging) remained VI-RADS 2 or 1 after NAC. The recently proposed nacVI-RADS more accurately scores for these changes. However, by introducing the 3-step scoring system local response evaluation was more reader-friendly, more accurate in assessing residual disease and turned out to be a better predictor of DFS. We propose to add this 3-step method to the nacVI-RADS evaluation of residual disease.

The observation of rCR with high specificity after NAC holds the potential implication of omitting further pathological staging before definitive treatment or de-escalating treatment. Especially in bladder sparing treatment strategies this would be advantageous for patients because risk of residual disease in case of rCR on mpMRI would be very low. A rCR on post-NAC mpMRI would also be a prognostic parameter, as it predicts DFS in our study and is significantly correlated with pCR following NAC which is associated with improved survival [29–31]. A prospective analysis of mpMRI characteristics before and after neo-adjuvant therapy in bladder cancer is necessary.

Conclusions

mpMRI can predict pCR after neo-adjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy with high specificity but low sensitivity. Adding a 3-step score to nacVI-RADS improves evaluation of residual disease, using this assessment rCR was significantly correlated with DFS. mpMRI should be included in future prospective trials of multimodal management of MIBC and can be an important predictive asset in routine clinical management.
