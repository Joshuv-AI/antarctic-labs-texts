Comparative Analysis of Lung Ultrasound, Computed Tomography, and X-ray in the Diagnosis of Common Pathologies among Critically Ill Mechanically Ventilated Patients—A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Ashraf S Al Tayar, Hosni A Salem, Eslam E Abdelshafey, Mohamed A Rashwan, Mohmed F Khalil, Hebah A Alwafi, Walid S Alhabashy, Shamekh H Altayar, Nazeh E Elfakhrany, Dina H Zidan, Prashant Nasa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsChest X-ray (CXR) and computed tomography (CT) are established imaging modalities for patients in respiratory distress, and lung ultrasound (LUS) has emerged as an efficient point-of-care alternative. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of LUS and CXR in critically ill patients, utilizing CT thorax as the reference standard.Patient and methodsA prospective observational study was conducted in a tertiary care intensive care unit (ICU) involving mechanically ventilated adult patients requiring CT thorax. Before the CT, patients underwent portable CXR and LUS. Diagnostic performance metrics were calculated using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and AUC-ROC for pneumothorax, pleural effusion, consolidation, collapse, and pulmonary edema for CXR and LUS compared to CT. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen's κ.ResultsA total of 110 patients were included in this study. Overall, the performance of LUS was significantly superior to CXR for diagnosing various pathologies. Lung ultrasound exhibited 100% sensitivity and 97% specificity, outperforming CXR (66.7% sensitivity) for the detection of pneumothorax. For pleural effusion, LUS achieved 100% sensitivity and specificity, reflecting perfect concordance with CT, especially for moderate and severe pleural effusions, while CXR had an accuracy of only 68.18%. Lung consolidation and collapse were identified with 100% sensitivity and PPV by LUS. Furthermore, the accuracy of LUS in diagnosing pulmonary edema was 96.4%, with a specificity of 95.3% and an NPV of 100%.ConclusionAcross five pathologies that were evaluated in this study, LUS consistently outperformed CXR in diagnostic accuracy and concordance with CT thorax.How to cite this articleAl Tayar AS, Salem HA, Abdelshafey EE, Rashwan MA, Khalil MF, Alwafi HA, et al. Introduction

Critically ill patients often present with respiratory distress, which necessitates rapid and accurate diagnostic evaluation of possible pathologies. Conventionally, CXR and CT thorax have been used to evaluate pleuro-pulmonary conditions in the critical care setting. Further, portable CXR plays a center stage in the daily patient assessment of patients in intensive care units (ICUs). Chest X-rays are frequently ordered before morning rounds in ICUs to facilitate the detection of potentially life-threatening conditions that could otherwise have been missed. Furthermore, scheduling CXRs before these rounds enhances logistical efficiency, contributing to a more streamlined workflow in the unit.1 However, several studies highlighted the limited efficacy of routine portable CXRs, even when optimally performed, high inter-observer variability in interpreting portable CXRs, and the associated risks of harm from inappropriate interventions based on inadequate results.2–4 Consequently, there has been advocacy for the restriction of routine CXR use in ICUs.2,5 Although CT is considered to be the gold standard for thoracic imaging, it can mitigate some of these limitations by providing unparalleled sensitivity and specificity in the diagnosis of various pulmonary pathologies.6 However, the need of transporting critically ill patients to radiology departments for CT scans introduces several risks, delays, and higher costs.7,8 Moreover, the exposure to ionizing radiations associated with repeated portable CXRs and CT scans, especially in long stay and trauma patients, could exceed the radiation safety thresholds.9,10

Lung ultrasound, as an integral part of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), has become increasingly prominent in critical care settings due to its ability to deliver real-time dynamic imaging at the bedside without exposing patients to ionizing radiation. Lung ultrasound could effectively assess pleural, parenchymal, and chest wall pathologies, as well as guide therapeutic procedures.11 In addition, LUS is readily available, cost-effective, and usually well-tolerated, including those patients who are hemodynamically unstable or requiring mechanical ventilation.12 Despite the rising adoption of LUS in critical care, there exists a paucity of real-world comparative data regarding its diagnostic accuracy in identifying common pulmonary pathologies relative to conventional imaging modalities among unselected critically ill patients.

This prospective study aims to evaluate the performance of LUS and bedside CXR in comparison with the reference standard CT scan for diagnosing common pleuro-pulmonary pathologies in critically ill patients undergoing mechanical ventilation.

Patients and Methods

This study utilized a prospective observational design to compare the diagnostic performance of standard portable CXR and LUS with plain axial CT thorax, in identifying five major thoracic pathologies–pneumothorax, pleural effusion, consolidation, collapse, and pulmonary edema in mechanically ventilated adult patients.

Adult patients admitted to the ICU of a tertiary care referral hospital between February 2022 and March 2023 on invasive mechanical ventilation, undergoing plain axial thorax CT scan based on clinical indications determined by the treating medical team, independent of the study's objectives, were randomly selected for this study. Patients scheduled for CT imaging were screened 1 hour before the planned procedure to assess the presence of any imaging signs of the aforementioned pleuro-pulmonary pathologies using standard portable CXR and LUS imaging. The patients were transferred for a CT scan as per the ICU protocol and checklist.

The ethical approval for this study was obtained from the institutional review board (IRB) of the hospital in accordance with local regulations and international standards for research involving human subjects (reference no: 231/2021/02). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional or national research committee and with the 2024 Helsinki Declaration or comparable ethical standards.13 Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives before enrollment in the study. The study protocol, including the purpose, procedures, risks, and benefits, was clearly explained to the participants, and they were given adequate time to ask questions and make an informed decision regarding their participation. Patient confidentiality and data protection were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Portable CXR imaging was performed using standard techniques in the supine position or as clinically indicated. Anteroposterior (AP) and lateral views were obtained to visualize the entire thoracic cavity. Chest X-ray images were acquired and processed by trained radiology technologists following institutional protocols. Chest radiographs were initially interpreted by the attending consultant intensivist as part of routine assessment during daily ICU rounds. To ensure diagnostic accuracy and consistency, all CXR findings were subsequently reviewed and confirmed by the radiologist responsible for official reporting.

Lung ultrasound imaging was performed by experienced intensivists who were formally trained and accredited in POCUS. The primary investigator holds the European Diploma in Echocardiography (EDEC) by the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine, which involves structured formal training, supervised practice, and the submission of 135 fully supervised POCUS cases, including LUS examinations. Furthermore, the primary investigator undertook additional POCUS clinical attachments at several European centers before obtaining the EDEC diploma, thereby broadening exposure to a wide spectrum of pathologies. Importantly, all participating clinicians had completed structured training programs and routinely performed LUS in clinical practice. The imaging was conducted using a portable ultrasound machine equipped with a high-frequency linear probe (7–12 MHz) or a phased array probe (1–5 MHz), depending on the clinical scenario and operator preference. The phased array probe, due to its smaller footprint, was particularly advantageous for accessing intercostal spaces and was frequently used when transthoracic echocardiography was performed concurrently, allowing continuity of imaging without switching probes. Conversely, the curvilinear probe, with its wider field of view and good depth penetration, was also utilized when appropriate for comprehensive pleural and subpleural assessment.14,15 Patients were positioned in a semi-recumbent or lateral decubitus position to facilitate optimal acoustic windows for imaging. The thorax was systematically scanned in multiple anatomical regions, including anterior, lateral, and posterior lung fields bilaterally, to assess for the presence of pleural-parenchymal abnormalities and other pathologies. Standard scanning protocols recommended by established guidelines, or protocols specific to the institution were followed to ensure consistency and accuracy in image acquisition.11,16 For LUS reporting, the Thoracic UltrasONOgraphy (TUONO) consensus guidelines were used.17

Pneumothorax was excluded by the presence of lung sliding and/or B-lines and confirmed in the absence of sliding with identification of a lung point, a highly specific sign where the area without lung sliding meets an area with preserved sliding. The absence of B-lines, with or without A-lines, was also considered supportive of pneumothorax. The lung consolidation and collapse were differentiated on LUS, based on the characteristics of air bronchograms. Consolidation was defined by the presence of dynamic air bronchograms, where hyperechoic punctate or linear structures within the consolidated lung moved with inspiration and diminished during expiration. Collapse was defined by static air bronchograms, in which these hyperechoic structures remained fixed and did not move with respiration.

A plain axial CT scan of the thorax was performed using multidetector CT scanners with appropriate protocols tailored to the clinical indication and patient characteristics. Images were acquired in the supine position with the arms raised above the head to minimize motion artifacts and optimize image quality. Axial slices were obtained at predetermined intervals (e.g., 5 mm or less) to provide detailed anatomical information of the thoracic structures. Radiation exposure was minimized by using low-dose CT protocols whenever feasible, while ensuring diagnostic quality and coverage of the entire thoracic cavity.

All imaging procedures were conducted by trained and certified healthcare professionals adhering to standardized guidelines and safety protocols. The primary investigator holds the European Diploma in Advanced Critical Care Echocardiography (EDEC), awarded by the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine, and serves as an EDEC supervisor. To ensure consistency and diagnostic reliability, the ultrasound images were regularly reviewed for quality control by the primary investigator. Images obtained from CXR, LUS, and CT scans were interpreted by qualified clinicians who were blinded to the findings of other imaging modalities. Specifically, LUS was both acquired and interpreted by intensivists; radiologists were not involved in any aspect of LUS acquisition or interpretation. Computed tomography scans were interpreted by consultant radiologists. CXR images were initially interpreted by attending intensivists during daily ICU rounds, with final interpretations subsequently confirmed by radiologists. In cases of diagnostic uncertainty or discrepancies, consensus was reached through multidisciplinary discussion, often guided by CT imaging and radiologist input. Quality assurance measures were implemented to ensure the accuracy and reliability of imaging findings throughout the study period.

Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for CXR and LUS, using CT scan as the reference standard. Inter-rater agreement regarding CXR interpretation was evaluated employing Cohen's κ statistic. Furthermore, diagnostic accuracy measures, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), were determined for both CXR and LUS. Descriptive statistics were utilized to summarize patient demographics and characteristics. Depending on the distribution of the data, continuous variables were presented as either mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Results

One hundred and ten patients, including 66 (60%) females with a mean age of 52 years, who underwent a CT scan, were included in this study. Reasons for initiating mechanical ventilation were respiratory failure (35, 32%), septic shock (30, 27%), polytrauma (22, 20%), altered mental status (15, 14%), and others (8, 7%). The primary pathologies diagnosed on CT scan included pneumothorax (9, 8%), pleural effusion (58, 53%), lung consolidation (69, 63%), lung collapse (61, 55%), and pulmonary edema (25, 23%).

For pneumothorax, LUS exhibited a sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 97%, with an overall accuracy of 97.27%, along with a robust agreement between LUS and CT scan findings [Cohen's κ coefficient (κ) = 0.842, p < 0.001]. On the other hand, the CXR had a sensitivity and PPV of 66.7% and an accuracy of 86.36% (Table 1). Lung ultrasound exhibited a comparable efficacy of CT scan for detecting pleural effusion with a sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 100% and near-perfect agreement (κ = 0.372, p < 0.001) between the two modalities. However, the accuracy of CXR was only 68.18%, with PPV and NPV of 55.17% and 78.05%, respectively (Table 2).

Discussion

In this prospective observational study, LUS demonstrated a comparable accuracy to CT scan for the detection of common pleuro-parenchymal pathologies in mechanically ventilated critically ill patients, such as pneumothorax, pleural effusion, lung collapse, and consolidations, as well as pulmonary edema. Moreover, LUS outperformed CXR for the accurate diagnosis of all of these conditions.

The strength of our study lies in its exclusive focus on mechanically ventilated critically ill patients, unlike most prior studies that included both critically and non-critically ill populations. In addition, the patient selection was random and not based on the lung pathology. Our findings demonstrate that LUS can serve as a reliable alternative to portable CXR, which is often limited by low sensitivity and specificity in this patient group. Moreover, LUS offers a practical bedside option when CT, the current gold standard, is not feasible due to the risks associated with transporting unstable ICU patients. These results provide important evidence supporting the integration of LUS into routine diagnostic pathways for mechanically ventilated critically ill patients.

There are inherent challenges of portable CXR in mechanically ventilated patients due to sub-optimal image in the absence of maximal inspiration, patient positioning, and movement and artifacts from overlapping medical devices.18,19 Although LUS has been demonstrated to be superior to CXR for the detection of pneumothorax, significant heterogeneity exists in the evidence based on the selection criteria of included patients, trauma vs non-trauma patients, and the type of probe used.20–23 The comparable accuracy of ultrasound to that of CT scans, coupled with an NPV of 100%, underscores its effectiveness in excluding pneumothorax. This capability of LUS reduces the potential of missing a pneumothorax in a mechanically ventilated patient and the need for transferring a critically ill patient to the CT suite, thereby optimizing patient management and outcomes. Moreover, point-of-care LUS provides real-time imaging, allowing for immediate assessment without the delay usually associated with CT imaging and CXR.24 This rapid diagnosis expedites decision-making and facilitates prompt treatment initiation, particularly in acutely ill critical patients.25 Conversely, CXR showed lower sensitivity, highlighting its limitations in accurately identifying pneumothorax, consistent with existing literature.21,22

In our study, LUS also exhibited perfect agreement with CT scan and its superiority over CXR in detecting pleural effusions. Lung ultrasound has been demonstrated to possess higher accuracy than CXR for the detection of pleural effusions, particularly in critically ill patients for whom upright CXRs may not be feasible.26,27 A meta-analysis comprising 12 studies reported that ultrasound exhibited a pooled sensitivity of 94% and a specificity of 98% in identifying pleural effusions.26 Furthermore, LUS exhibited a high level of concordance with CT in the quantification of pleural effusion, particularly in cases of moderate to severe effusions. Conversely, CXR was found to be less reliable for quantifying pleural effusion. This diminished accuracy can be attributed to the constraints of patient positioning, particularly in mechanically ventilated patients, and CXR being a two-dimensional imaging modality significantly impacts the diagnostic performance of CXR, unlike LUS.28

In patients with suspected pneumonia, CXR is frequently an initial imaging choice. The portable CXR avoids the intra-hospital transportation for a CT scan and reduces the risk of transmission.29,30 However, a recent meta-analysis involving patients requiring mechanical ventilation found a pooled sensitivity and specificity of CXR for diagnosing lung consolidation were only 53% and 78%, respectively.27 Therefore, a CT scan is a reference standard for the diagnosis of lung consolidation. Our study findings indicate that LUS demonstrates exceptional accuracy and a very high degree of concordance with CT scans in detecting collapse and consolidation when compared to CXR. A systematic review including four studies noted a higher sensitivity and specificity of LUS in diagnosing CT-confirmed consolidation. Nevertheless, authors concluded that further prospective studies are required on the routine use of LUS to diagnose consolidation to overcome the significant risk of bias and heterogeneity observed within the existing literature.31 The superior performance in detecting lung consolidation suggests that LUS may serve as a valuable tool for intensivists, in allowing for rapid assessment as a point-of-care diagnostic tool for patients with acute respiratory failure without the need for exposure to ionizing radiation or the logistical challenges associated with obtaining a CT scan.11

Finally, our study showed the superiority of LUS in sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy compared to CXR in detecting pulmonary edema. Conventionally, CXR has been the standard technique for diagnosing and quantifying pulmonary edema.32 The superior performance of LUS in detecting pulmonary edema or extravascular lung water could be due to the fact that it enables allowing for the direct assessment of fluid as reverberating artifacts referred by “comet tails” or “B-lines.”11,33,34 This direct visualization may enhance sensitivity and specificity compared to CXR, which relies on indirect and semi-quantitative assessment, such as Kerley B-lines, cardiomegaly, and blunting of the costophrenic angles. Lung ultrasound real-time imaging capabilities, lack of ionizing radiation, and excellent diagnostic performance make it an attractive alternative to CXR in clinical practice.11 Moreover, a strong correlation was found between B-lines quantification and the degree of extravascular lung water, as well as response to fluid-removing interventions such as diuretics or renal replacement therapy.35 However, a few challenges, such as operator dependence and limited penetration in obese patients, may affect ultrasound's utility in certain scenarios. Despite these limitations, ultrasound's ability to provide rapid and accurate diagnostic information underscores its value as a frontline imaging modality in pulmonary diagnostics.36

Limitations

Our study has some limitations, notably its single-center design, the inherent subjectivity associated with the interpretation of imaging modalities, and the risk of selection bias related to the randomization process. Additionally, inter-operator variation in reporting and technique employed may have influenced the interpretation of CXR and LUS. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that LUS was conducted exclusively by trained and certified healthcare professionals who adhered strictly to standardized reporting guidelines and study protocol. Moreover, CXR and CT scans were independently interpreted by qualified radiologists or intensivists who were blinded to the results of the other imaging modalities. Finally, a formal sample size calculation was not performed before study initiation. The sample size was determined pragmatically based on the number of eligible ICU patients undergoing thoracic CT during the study period. While this approach allowed for meaningful diagnostic comparisons, a prospective sample size estimation would enhance methodological rigor in future studies.

Conclusion

This study emphasizes the effectiveness of LUS as a significant imaging modality for critically ill patients undergoing mechanical ventilation. The findings indicate that LUS demonstrates a high degree of concordance with the reference standard of CT scan and exceeds the diagnostic capabilities of bedside CXR in evaluating common pleuro-pulmonary conditions. Additional research and clinical validation are necessary to investigate the optimal integration and application of these imaging modalities in replacing routine CXR in the ICU.
