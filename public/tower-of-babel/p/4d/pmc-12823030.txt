Extensive phenotyping and genome‐wide analysis of grain number‐related traits in a diverse wheat core collection
Authors: Zhuo Su, Shun Sakuma, Miyuki Nitta, Yasir S A Gorafi, Shuhei Nasuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most important staple crops globally. Grain number per spikelet (GPS) is an important yield component in wheat. It is influenced by floret number per spikelet (FPS) and floret fertility. Through three consecutive years of observation of FPS, GPS, and floret fertility per spikelet (FFPS) in a globally collected hexaploid wheat core collection and using an improved genotyping dataset, we identified 42 marker‐trait associations (MTA) for spikelet‐related traits through the single‐nucleotide polymorphism and the k‐mer‐based genome‐wide association studies. Those MTA explain 4.45%–24.45% phenotypic variations for the FFPS and GPS traits, indicating a substantial effect on spikelet‐related traits. We found significant negative associations between days to heading (DTH) and spikelet‐related traits, suggesting that DTH and spikelet‐related traits are subject to a certain degree of common genetic control. We identified several potential candidate gene models in the associated regions, including TraesCS2A03G0815500, which encodes a protein with a sterile alpha motif domain, and TraesCS2A03G0780600, which encodes a plastid‐targeted lipoxygenase. These genes may facilitate wheat breeding and help elucidate the mechanisms of wheat grain set.

INTRODUCTION

Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most important staple crops. Therefore, increasing its grain yield is the primary goal of wheat breeding (Pingali, 2012; Sakuma & Schnurbusch, 2020). Wheat produces an unbranched inflorescence, called a spike, consisting of many spikelets developing at the rachis. Each spikelet produces an indeterminate number of florets attached to a secondary axis, the rachilla (Koppolu & Schnurbusch, 2019; Sakuma & Koppolu, 2023). The fertility of each floret determines the number of grains per spikelet. A wheat spikelets can produce up to 12 floret primordia; however, >70% of these florets abort during development, leaving only a few florets that successfully produce grains (Guo & Schnurbusch, 2015).

The yield potential of wheat depends primarily on the number of spikes, spikelets per spike, the number of grains per spikelet, and the weight of the grains. Yield improvement in modern cultivars primarily results from increases in both grains per spike and grain weight. While some studies indicate a strong positive correlation of grain yield with grain number (GN) per spike but a weaker association with 1000‐kernel weight (TGW) in wheat (Sakuma & Schnurbusch, 2020; Shearman et al., 2005), GN often shows a negative correlation with TGW (Philipp et al., 2018; Sakuma & Schnurbusch, 2020). So far, only one gene controlling the number of florets and grains in wheat spikelet has been cloned, which is named Grain Number Increase 1 (GNI1), and individuals carrying the reduced‐function allele increased florets, GN, and the final yield (Sakuma et al., 2019). However, the mechanisms controlling GN and floret fertility in wheat spikelets remain largely unknown.

Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) have been a fast, reliable, and efficient method for the identification of genomic regions controlling the traits of interest, especially with the improvement of the wheat reference genome sequences, the affordable sequencing costs, and the establishment of new models (Ibrahim et al., 2020; Korte & Farlow, 2013). However, the ordinary single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)‐based GWAS method overlooks structural variations in the genome, such as deletions, insertions, and inversions (Tam et al., 2019). In addition, SNP calling based on a single reference genome is susceptible to inaccurate genotyping, which compromises the accuracy of candidate localization by GWAS and overlooks some potentially important genomic regions (Shi et al., 2023). The emergence of the k‐mer‐based GWAS overcomes these shortcomings; more variations, namely, single nucleotide variations and structural variations, are considered by directly creating a presence/absence matrix. Since the k‐mer matrix creation step does not rely on a specific reference genome, more variations are included in the population, decreasing the risk of genotyping error (Lemay et al., 2023; Voichek & Weigel, 2020). In addition, variations with up to k nucleotides can be captured in the same k‐mer, thus providing a method to evaluate phenotypic differences caused by haplotypes rather than single markers (Voichek & Weigel, 2020), which improves the reliability of the association between genotype and phenotype.

This study utilized a hexaploid wheat core collection, comprising accessions from Eurasia collected over the last century and preserved in the ex situ gene bank of the National BioResource Project (NBRP)‐Wheat, Japan (Takenaka et al., 2018). We evaluated days to heading (DTH), floret number per spikelet (FPS), and grain number per spikelet (GPS) for each accession in three consecutive years to (1) understand the genetic diversity of FPS, GPS, and floret fertility per spikelet (FFPS) in this core collection; (2) identify the phenotypic association, if any, between spikelet‐related traits at different spike locations in this diverse panel; and (3) identify marker‐trait associations (MTAs) associated with spikelet‐related traits through GWAS to detect potential candidate genes and explore the mechanisms underlying FPS and GPS for future wheat improvement.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and field trials

We used 188 hexaploid wheat core‐collection accessions (Table S1) obtained from the NBRP‐Wheat (https://shigen.nig.ac.jp/wheat/komugi), the Japanese wheat gene bank. These include mainly worldwide landraces covering six wheat subspecies, with the addition of modern cultivars frequently used for breeding in Japan and experimental standards. Most of the landraces NBRP‐Wheat possess were collected through several expeditions, the first of which was conducted by Dr. Hitoshi Kihara in 1955, during the pioneering era of world genetic resources exploration. Currently available rarely at the collection sites since replaced by modern varieties. Those landraces are ex situ conserved, hence avoiding the intended introgression of modern cultivars.

All accessions were evaluated at the experimental field of the Arid Land Research Center, Tottori University, Tottori, Japan (Latitude: 35°30′32.39″ N, Longitude: 134°10′10.80″ E) during three consecutive growing seasons (2019, 2020, and 2021). Each accession was planted in a single‐row plot of five plants, with 20 cm spacing between plants and 80 cm spacing between plots. While this design has limitations for controlling for micro‐environmental variation within a single season, the replication across three independent years is a key strength of our study, allowing us to assess the stability of traits and genetic effects across major environmental factors.

Phenotypic evaluation

The floret and grain number per spikelet (FPS and GPS, respectively) were measured in three parts of the spike: apical, middle, and basal, as described previously (Sakuma et al., 2024), and the average of the three parts was calculated. At least three individual plants per accession and three representative spikes per individual plant were examined. The FFPS was calculated as a product of FPS divided by GPS. DTH was recorded as the number of days from planting to when the first spikes fully emerged from the flag leaf sheath.

The statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2. The best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) and variance components for each trait across the 3 years were calculated using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). The BLUE with three replicates was calculated using the following formula: P = μ + Line + (Line × Year) + Rep (Year) + e, where P is the phenotype, μ denotes the overall mean, Line indicates the fixed effect of the accession, Year signifies the random effect of the environment, Line × Year represents the interaction effect between accession and environment, Rep (Year) denotes the random effect of the replication nested in the environment, and e is the random error term associated with the Line, Year, and Rep. The broad sense heritability (H 2) was calculated using the formula H 2 = VGVG+VGE/Y+Ve/YR , where VG, VGE, and Ve are the variances of genotype, genotype‐by‐year interaction, and residual error, respectively, Y is the number of years, and R is the number of replications.

To compare traits with different scales, we used a min‐max normalization transfer of the DTH, FPS, GPS, and FFPS traits in a range from 0 to 1 using the following formula: x′ = x−min(x)max(x)−min(x), where x is the real value and x′ is the transformed value. This method was chosen for its superior interpretability, as it projects all traits onto a unified percentage scale, allowing for intuitive visualization of relative rankings and enabling the calculated average normalized score (ANS) derived from the mean of the transformed FPS, GPS, and FFPS values to represent the “comprehensive relative performance” of an individual's floret and grain production ability per spikelet. Although min‐max normalization is sensitive to outliers, we quantified this compression effect by comparing the normal data range (Q1 − 1.5 × IQR to Q3 + 1.5 × IQR) to the full data range. We found that the impact was acceptable, with data compression exceeding 10% only for the FFPS apical trait (19.7%).

DNA extraction and genotyping

Total DNA was extracted from the second leaves of individual plants using the DNeasy Plant Maxi Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The purified DNAs were sent to Eurofins Genomics (https://eurofinsgenomics.jp/jp/home) for genotyping using the genotyping by random amplicon sequencing‐direct (GRAS‐Di) system (Enoki & Takeuchi, 2018; Enoki, 2019; Miki et al., 2020). The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Raw reads were then trimmed using fsatp v. 0.23.2 (Chen et al., 2018) with default parameters and subsequently mapped to the reference genome Chinese Spring v2.1 (Zhu et al., 2021), hereafter denoted as CS RefSeq v2.1. SNP calling was executed on GATK‐HaplotypeCaller and followed the combine and variants call by GATK‐GenotypeGVCFs and GATK‐GenotypeGVCFs steps (Van der Auwera & O'Connor, 2020). Markers with >20% missing rates or minor allele frequencies (MAFs) <5% were filtered out; a total of 38,124 SNPs were used in the following analysis.

The previously reported diversity arrays technology sequencing (DArTseq) genotyping data of this 6x core collection (Takenaka et al., 2018) were used in this study after being remapped to the CS RefSeq v2.1 through blastn. We removed the short reads that did not map to CS RefSeq v2.1 and ended up with 12,320 SNPs and 21,618 presence/absence variations (PAVs).

The filtered GRAS‐Di and DArTseq datasets were then merged into one dataset, referred to as the GRDA dataset, which includes 50,444 SNPs and 21,618 PAVs. Because the three accessions (6 × 162, 6 × 164, and 6 × 174) were sequenced only by GRAS‐Di, the GRDA dataset includes 185 accessions.

To examine marker distribution in each chromosome, we followed the definition of five wheat chromosome segments (R1, R2a, C, R2b, and R3) of the International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium (IWGSC et al., 2018). We counted and compared the SNP numbers in GRAS‐Di, DArTseq, and GRDA data sets on each segment of each wheat chromosome using R software.

Population structure and genome‐wide linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis

To assess the genetic diversity among the 185 wheat accessions with GRDA genotyping data, we utilized 25,557 high‐quality markers with a missing rate of <10%. We performed phylogenetic analyses using the RAxML‐ng package (Kozlov et al., 2019). A maximum likelihood tree with the general time reversible model (GTR + G) searched 200 random or parsimony trees to find the best tree. For rendering and annotation, we used an R script to generate an iTOL‐compatible tree (Letunic & Bork, 2024). Principle component analysis was performed using TASSEL 5 (Bradbury et al., 2007). ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 (Zhou et al., 2011) was used to estimate the population structure. The fivefold cross‐validation procedure was used to determine the ancestral population number K.

Using the GRDA genotype dataset, we first calculated genome‐wide LD using PLINK software v1.9 (Purcell et al., 2007). Then, we estimated the LD half‐decay distance using the non‐parametric regression procedure in R (Hill & Weir, 1988).

Genome‐wide association analysis

The GWAS was conducted using SNP‐based GWAS and k‐mer‐based GWAS. The SNP‐based GWAS used the GRDA dataset. It was performed using the EMMAX algorithm (Kang et al., 2010), with an identity‐by‐state kinship matrix. We used a threshold of −log10(p‐value) = 5 as a criterion for the significant association. If a marker was associated with the same trait across multiple years or associated with a single year and the BLUE value, it was classified as multi‐seasonal or stable.

In the k‐mer‐based GWAS, the raw reads of GRAS‐Di were used to generate k‐mers. We build the k‐mer matrix as described in https://github.com/steuernb/AgRenSeq (Arora et al., 2019). K‐mers (k = 51) were counted in trimmed raw data per accession using Jellyfish v2.3.0 (Marçais & Kingsford, 2011). K‐mers with a count of less than two in an accession were removed. Then, k‐mer counts from all accessions were integrated to create a presence/absence matrix with one row per k‐mer and one column per accession. The entries were reduced to 1 (presence) and 0 (absence). Monomorphic k‐mers were removed during the matrix construction. Then, the matrix was transformed to the ped format by local bash script and to the tped format by PLINK v1.90. The GWAS was executed in the same way as described for the SNP‐based GWAS. After performing the GWAS, k‐mers with a −log10(p) value larger than four were selected and mapped to CS RefSeq v2.1 using blastn to determine the k‐mer position on CS RefSeq v2.1. Some k‐mers were mapped to multiple positions, but some were uniquely mapped. To select k‐mer peaks and confirm their positions, we first retained uniquely mapped k‐mers and applied a threshold of −log10(p‐value) ≥ 7 to identify significant k‐mers. We then merged significant k‐mers within each 150 bp into a block, as they originated from the same short reads. Similar to SNP‐based GWAS, if a block was associated with the same trait across multiple years or linked to a single year and the BLUE value, it was classified as multi‐seasonal or stable.

Candidate gene selection

To identify candidate genes, we extracted annotated genes within the 1.87 Mb LD half‐decay distance of significant markers using the Chinese Spring reference genome v2.1 (IWGSC annotation database (https://urgi.versailles.inra.fr/download/iwgsc/IWGSC_RefSeq_Annotations/v2.1/). Genes located within 5 kb upstream or downstream of significant markers were designated as high‐confidence candidates. We then predicted the functions of these putative candidate genes by comparing their coding sequences against the eggNOG database (Huerta‐Cepas et al., 2019) using eggNOG‐mapper v2. Additionally, we retrieved their expression profiles across different growth stages in Chinese spring wheat from the hexaploid Wheat Expression Database (Ma et al., 2021; IWGSC Annotation v1.1; http://202.194.139.32/expression/wheat.html).

RESULTS

Phenotypic variation and trait correlations

Initially, we examined the correlation between the three seasons (2019, 2020, and 2021) for the three traits FPS, GPS, and FFPS across the three spike positions (apical, middle, and basal) and the entire spike (total). The traits in different years were all significantly correlated (p < 0.01) with low variations between the seasons (Figure S1; Table S2). We estimated the BLUE values of all traits using the three‐season data (Table S3). All traits followed a normal distribution except for the GPS‐related traits and FFPS in spike apical position and DTH. All traits showed high broad‐sense heritability (H 2), ranging from 0.66 to 0.82 (Figure 1A). High correlations between three spikelet positions in FPS, GPS, and FFPS traits and between FPS and GPS were observed. FFPS showed a higher correlation with GPS than FPS (Figure 1B). In addition, we found a significant negative correlation between DTH and all other traits, indicating that early heading accessions tend to retain more florets and produce more grains per spikelet (Figure 1B,C). The landrace or modern cultivars collected from Asia showed more FPS, GPS, and FFPS and shorter DTH (Figure 1C).

Considering that the core collection includes modern varieties and landraces, we tested the correlation between spikelet‐related traits and DTH in each group separately by comparing the ANS and normalized DTH values. We observed a significant negative correlation between ANS and normalized DTH in both cultivar and landrace groups (r = −0.72**; and −0.65**, respectively, Figure S2A). Genome‐wide markers and LD

The marker distributions and densities of the DArTseq, GRAS‐Di, and GRDA are shown in Figure S3A–C, respectively. In the DArTseq dataset, we found that markers were mainly clustered in the distal chromosome regions R1 and R3 (63.4%), with a sharp drop in the number of SNPs in the C regions of the chromosomes; only 1455, which represent 4.2% of the markers (Table 1). In contrast, GRAS‐Di showed a more even distribution of markers across the different chromosomal regions, and the marker numbers tend to be correlated with the physical lengths (Table 1; Table S4). We found more SNPs in the regions C (7499; 19.6%), R2a (7861; 20.6%), and R2b (12,656; 33.2%) in the GRAS‐Di than in the DArTseq. By merging the two datasets (GRAS‐Di and DArTseq), the GRDA dataset leverages the strengths of both methods, resulting in an enhanced marker distribution across the entire genome (Figure S3C). The LD half‐decay distance of the whole genome estimated from GRDA data was 1.87 Mb (Figure S3D). This distance was used for candidate gene selection in subsequent analysis.

Population structure and phylogenetic analyses

The genetic diversity and population structure of the core collection have been reported previously by Takenaka et al. (2018) using DArTseq markers. In this study, we used the GRDA marker data set to explain the population structure of the 185 core collection accessions. The principal component (PC), admixture, and phylogenetic analysis generally agreed with those of Takenaka et al. (2018). The admixture analysis revealed that the error leveled off at K = 6, indicating that the accessions have originated from six distinct lineages (Figure 2A; Table S5). The PC1, PC2, and PC3 explained 11.3%, 8.2%, and 6.7%, respectively. PC1 and PC2 divided the core collection into 3 clusters. They formed a triangular shape, which agrees with the results reported by Takenaka et al. (2018). The clusters calculated by ADMIXTURE analysis and phylogenetic tree both occupied similar positions in the PC1–PC3 plane (Figure 2A,B). The recorded geographic information of collection sites agreed with the genetic classification of most accessions. However, there were some inconsistencies, such that the two accessions, KU‐497 and KU‐4703 collected from South Asia, were clustered with European accessions (Figure 2B,C; Table S5). We examined the relationship between ANS and DTH based on genetic diversity. We found that ANS and DTH were correlated within clusters irrespective of the accessions breeding status, landraces, or cultivars (Figure 2A,D,E).

GWAS analysis

We detected 111 MTAs through SNP‐based GWAS, mainly distributed on chromosome 2A, which accommodated 46 MTAs. Only four MTAs were stable and detected in at least two seasons or one season and the BLUE (Figure 3A; Table S6). For FFPS, three MTAs were located on chromosome 2A and clustered in a 4.1 Mb region (564.9–569.0 Mb). For DTH, we found one MTA on chromosome 6B (Figure 3A). An MTA on chromosome 2A at 569 Mb was found to be associated with multiple traits, including FFPS apical in season 2021 and BLUE, as well as FFPS total, GPS apical, and GPS total in season 2021 (Table S6). The k‐mer‐based GWAS identified 17,532 significant k‐mers (Table S7). The Manhattan plots for each environment and year are shown in Figure S4, and the corresponding Q–Q plots are presented in Figure S6. After merging the adjacent k‐mers within the 150 bp block (Table S8), we ended up with 832 significant blocks, including all the significant k‐mers. Out of the 832 blocks, 782 were detected in only one cultivation season and were not considered stable associations (Figure 3B; Table S8). In the remaining 50 significant blocks detected in multiple seasons, 11 were associated with DTH and did not overlap with those detected by SNP‐based GWAS. The peak on chromosome 5A found in two seasons (2019 and 2021) and BLUE corresponded to the known vernalization gene Vrn‐A1 (TraesCS5A03G0935400, chr5A; 589,259,335 to 589,271,297, CS RefSeq V2.1), reflecting the power of k‐mer‐based GWAS (Figure 3A,B).

The other 39 significant k‐mer blocks were associated with FFPS apical, GPS middle, and GPS total. All of them were located on chromosomes 2A and 6A, except for three significant blocks associated with FFPS apical, which were located on chromosome 3A (Figure 3B; Table S8). The region on chromosome 2A from 533 to 564 Mb was occupied with MTAs for FFPS apical, FFPS middle, GPS middle, GPS total, and DTH. The region from 218 to 316 Mb, on chromosome 6A, accommodated MTAs associated with FFPS apical, FFPS middle, GPS basal, GPS middle, and GPS total that the SNP‐based GWAS did not detect. These two regions of MTA clusters represent high‐confidence regions for candidate genes.

Phenotypic effects of identified MTAs haplotypes

We compared the BLUE values of different haplotypes for each trait in the high‐confidence regions on chromosomes 2A and 6A (Tables S9 and S10) to check the haeffect of haplotypes. We excluded haplotypes or alleles represented in <10 accessions (MAF < 5%).

On chromosome 2A, although the significant region spanned by SNP (564.9–569.0 Mb) and k‐mer (533–564 Mb) does not overlap, the haplotypes showed the same haplotype pattern (Table S11). Compared to accessions with the “C‐A” (n = 17) or the “0011” (n = 18) haplotype (2AH2), those carrying the “AGC” or “1100” haplotype (2AH1, n = 158) exhibited significantly earlier DTH and significantly higher FPS, GPS, and FFPS values (Figure 4A,B).

On chromosome 6A, we identified 26 highly significant k‐mer blocks spanning 98 Mb (218–316 Mb), which formed only two haplotypes carried by >10 accessions (Tables S8 and S9). Accessions harboring the favorable haplotype “10101101111111111111111111” (6AH1, n = 32) exhibited significantly earlier heading and higher FPS, GPS, and FFPS values than those with unfavorable haplotype “01010010000000000000000000” (6AH2, n = 117) (Figure 4C). The accessions with favorable haplotype 6AH1 were mainly East Asian cultivars (28/32, Table S11), indicating that the phenotype difference may be due to environmental differences and selection between East Asia and other regions.

Additionally, we examined the combined effects of the 2A and 6A haplotypes from the k‐mer GWAS results (Table S11). These two haplotypes divided the population into three distinct groups: 2AH1_6AH1 (n = 32), 2AH1_6AH2 (n = 89), and 2AH2_6AH2 (n = 17). Accessions carrying the combination 2AH1_6AH1 exhibited the most favorable agronomic traits (Figure 4D). The 6AH2 haplotype was further stratified by the 2AH1 and 2AH2 haplotypes, resulting in two subgroups (2AH1_6AH2 and 2AH2_6AH2). Accessions with the 2AH1_6AH2 subgroups showed superior phenotype for heading time, GPS, and FFPS compared to those with 2AH2_6AH2 (Figure 4D), with statistically significant differences between the two groups for all traits except FPS (Figure 4D). These results suggest that the 2A and 6A haplotypes may act additively to regulate heading time and yield‐related traits, as the combined haplotypes showed a progressive improvement in phenotypes (2AH1_6AH1 > 2AH1_6AH2 > 2AH2_6AH2).

Candidate genes identification

To identify candidate genes, we focused on the most stable MTAs identified using the SNP and k‐mer GWAS. We identified 788 genes within the 1.87 Mb regions (representing the LD half‐decay distance) surrounding the GWAS peaks from CS annotation v2.1. 28 genes with significant markers located within 100 kb upstream and downstream (Table S12). Through orthologue comparison and function prediction, two and one genes associated with FFPS and GPS traits were evaluated as possible candidates (Ma et al., 2021; Table S12; Figure S7). The gene TraesCS2A03G0815500, located on chromosome 2A and associated with the apical FFPS trait, encodes a gene containing a sterile alpha motif (SAM) domain that exhibits the highest expression level in spikes during the Zadoks growth stages Z32, Z39, and Z65, as well as in grain at stage Z71 (Zadoks et al., 1974; Figure S7). The TraesCS2A03G0780600 gene on chromosome 2A is the homologous gene of maize tasselseed1 (ts1) (Acosta et al., 2009), which is located close to the MTA associated with the apical FFPS, middle, and total GPS. Unexpectedly, it has the highest expression in root and stem (Figure S7). TraesCS6A03G0470400, located on chromosome 6A and related to the traits middle and total GPS, belongs to the cytochrome P450 (CYP) family, with a peak expression level in spikes during stage Z39 (Figure S7).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated a diverse bread wheat collection and found a clear correlation between spikelet‐related traits in different spike parts. Although the spikelets in the middle part develop the highest number of florets and grains, the correlations between FPS and GPS in the different parts of the spike are very high in the 188 accessions (ranging from 0.88 to 0.94). The same was true for GPS and FFPS (Figure 1B), consistent with the previous study by Philipp et al. (2018). The high correlation and the relatively high broad‐sense heritability (H 2), ranging from 0.69 to 0.82, as to the spikelet‐related traits in 3 years (Figure 1A), indicate that spikelet‐related traits in different parts of the spike are controlled to some extent by common genetic factors. In addition, we found that all spikelet‐related traits were negatively correlated with DTH. This correlation exists in both cultivars and landraces, irrespective of geographical origin, indicating that spikelet‐related traits and DTH are at least partly determined by the same pathway that may affect wheat floret development. This argument contradicts the previous report that no floret or GN QTLs co‐localized with flowering time (Guo & Schnurbusch, 2015). This contradiction may be due to the differences in the plant populations or the growing environments. We noted that East Asian accessions, both landraces and cultivars, tended to have more florets and grains per spikelet and shorter time to heading, suggesting that the East Asian environment has implemented selection pressure on traits related to DTH and spikelet development. We assume that the selection might have been directly oriented to DTH to avoid the rainy season in early summer during wheat harvest in Monsoon Asia. A shorter growth time tends to result in a small number of tillers and, consequently, a limited number of spikes. The haplotypes increasing FPS or GPS have probably evolved later to compensate for the effect of the limited number of spikes on yield.

Takenaka et al. (2018) constructed the hexaploid wheat core collection through genotyping using the DArTseq method. Because the selection of endonucleases by DArTseq targets the transcriptionally active subtelomeric regions of the chromosomes, only a few SNP markers were obtained near the centromere regions (Sansaloni et al., 2020). Considering the effects of mutations and transposons in natural populations, the function of genes in pericentromeric regions may also affect the phenotypes. Thus, an increase in marker density using the GRAS‐Di method enables the detection of allelic diversity in those genes within the pericentromeric regions. For example, the pericentromeric MTA peaks we identified on chromosome 6A comprise a total of 102 markers, spanning the 218–261 Mb range. In comparison, the DArTseq method only provides 19 markers, most of which are PAV markers (17/19). The significant MTAs we identified in this region are all detected by the GRAS‐Di markers, indicating that improving marker density and their distribution across the chromosome enhances the power of GWAS in discovering genotype‐phenotype associations.

The two GWAS methods, k‐mer‐ and SNP‐based GWAS, detected the MTA peak on chromosome 2A. However, the peak on chromosome 6A and the vernalization gene Vrn‐A1 locus for DTH were only detected by k‐mer‐based GWAS, indicating that k‐mer‐based GWAS has a more robust power to detect possible associations. For the vernalization gene Vrn‐A1, we found that the most significant k‐mer was located at physical position 589,156,507 on chromosome 5A. The original VCF file included SNPs located at positions 589,161,616 and 589,161,619 but were filtered out during SNP calling process due to low mapping quality (< 40), and large symmetric odds ratio test (>3 in GATK). Moreover, the subsequent missing rate filtering excluded adjacent SNPs that passed the GATK filtering from the analysis. This indicates that some informative markers may have been removed during the variant calling and genotyping process. Our results suggest that the k‐mer‐based GWAS can substitute the SNP‐based GWAS and have a more substantial MTA detection power.

For GPS and FFPS, we found two major MTA regions: one located on chromosome 2A and close to the MTA region found by Guo et al. (2017) for GPS and Zheng et al. (2022) for spikelet number per spike. Notably, this region overlapped with a significant DTH locus identified in our study, exhibiting pleiotropic effects on DTH, FPS, GPS, and FFPS (Figure 3). The consistent haplotype patterns, in which 2AH1 correlates with earlier heading and higher FPS, GPS, and FFPS values, while 2AH2 correlates with later heading and lower values (Figure 4), provide strong genetic evidence for a shared regulatory mechanism underlying the observed negative phenotypic correlation. This suggests that this locus may coordinate spikelet developmental timing (heading date) with the extent of reproductive organ development (spikelet and floret formation). Strikingly, this region on 2A does not overlap with any known major heading date genes (e.g., Ppd‐1, Vrn‐1, and Eps loci [Cha et al., 2025; Shimizu et al., 2021]), indicating it is a novel locus that may coordinate inflorescence development and flowering time in wheat. We hypothesize that the gene(s) in this region act as a key genomic hotspot to synchronize spikelet initiation/floret development with the transition to flowering.

We traced the origin of the two haplotypes. The 18 accessions carrying the unfavorable haplotype 2AH2 included 13 from Europe, as well as nine Triticum spelta L. and three Triticum macha Dekapr. & Mebabde accessions (Table S11). In contrast, the favorable haplotype 2AH1 is more widely distributed geographically. This distribution pattern indicates that the unfavorable 2AH2 haplotype might have been purged by human selection during domestication and breeding. Despite its negative effect on yield, the 2AH2 haplotype serves as a valuable genetic resource for elucidating the regulatory mechanisms controlling spikelet initiation and floret development.

Within this key region on 2A, we propose two compelling candidate genes. The first gene is TraesCS2A03G0815500, which encodes a gene containing a SAM domain, like the LEAFY protein (Rieu et al., 2023). SAM domain proteins are involved in the growth of floral meristems in angiosperms (Denay et al., 2017). The second gene is the wheat homologue TraesCS2A03G0780600 of the maize ts1 gene that controls maize sex determination and floret abortion. The ts1 gene controls the abortion of pistil primordia in staminate florets by a jasmonic acid‐driven cell death process (Acosta et al., 2009). The functions of these genes make them strong candidates for simultaneously influencing flowering time and spikelet/floret development.

As far as we know, no previously reported QTLs or GWAS MTAs overlapped with the chromosome 6A MTA identified in this study, which may harbor a novel locus for the spikelet‐related trait. TraesCS6A03G0470400 in the chromosome 6A MTA region possibly encodes for a cytochrome P450 (CYP) protein. It was previously reported that the CYP78A9 gene regulates floral organ growth and seed development in Arabidopsis by promoting cell proliferation (Fang et al., 2012; Sotelo‐Silveira et al., 2013).

GNI1 controls FPS and GPS in wheat (Sakuma et al., 2019). Through a BLASTN search, we checked the position of the GNI1 gene in CS Refv2.1 and found two fully matched sequences at 637 Mb on chromosome 2A and on an unknown chromosome. This study identified an MTA at 646 Mb on chromosome 2A, related to both apical and total FPS (Tables S4 and S5), which may be linked to GNI1. However, this MTA was only found when we analyzed the BLUE values, indicating that environmental factors may influence the function of GNI1.

It is important to note that the single‐row plot design used in this study was primarily for efficient initial screening of a diverse germplasm panel. We acknowledge that this design may not control field variation as effectively as multi‐row, replicated plots typically used for precise breeding value estimation. However, the 3‐year replication of our field trials significantly enhances the reliability of our phenotypic data. The identification of stable MTAs across seasons underscores that the discovered loci have substantial genetic effects and are not merely artifacts of a single year's environment. This successfully fulfills our primary objective of resource discovery. The promising individuals identified here provide a foundation for future work. We are currently developing biparental populations from these lines, which will be phenotyped using more refined, replicated field designs to validate these QTLs and move toward direct breeding applications.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provided valuable and fundamental insights into the process of floret fertility and grain setting in wheat. After validation, the novel MTA identified in this study on chromosome 6A may contribute to the development of new wheat cultivars with enhanced grain numbers. Moreover, this study confirmed the potential of the Asian germplasm in providing new alleles and genes essential for wheat breeding.
