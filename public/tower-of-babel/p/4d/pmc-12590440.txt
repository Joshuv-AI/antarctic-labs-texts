Fecal intestinal inflammatory biomarkers in piglets: analytical validation, homogeneity and stability studies
Authors: Anna Pelegrí-Pineda, Raquel Pato, Raquel Peña, Júlia Suppi, Eudald Llauradó-Calero, David Solà-Oriol, Anna Bassols, Yolanda Saco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•LCN-2, MPO and ADA methods available for serum have been validated in porcine faeces with adequate results.•fCal, MPO and ADA are homogeneously distributed in the stools.•Fecal samples should be mixed before LCN-2 determination.•The stability of all biomarkers is good when feces are frozen.

Introduction

The use of non-invasive samples for biomarker detection (e.g., saliva, feces, and hair) has become popular, especially in veterinary medicine, because it reduces stress caused by blood collection and improves animal welfare. In particular, blood sampling is very challenging in piglets. Fecal samples appear as suitable for assessing gastrointestinal disorders and are also easy and fast to collect (Svoboda et al., 2024).

Gastrointestinal inflammatory disorders are common in pigs, especially during weaning. Weaning is a sudden and extremely stressful event for piglets (Campbell et al., 2013). Early intestinal inflammation can result in mucosal and enterocyte damage. This disrupts tight junction integrity, increasing gut permeability to both pathogenic and commensal bacteria. This compromised barrier function poses a significant threat to the immune system of young pigs. The proliferation of gastrointestinal pathogenic bacteria triggers diarrhea, which is the main cause of morbidity and mortality in swine production, requiring the therapeutic use of antimicrobials, including mainly antibiotics. Typically, the inflammatory process occurs before clinical signs appear and alters intestinal integrity. Thus, inflammatory biomarkers may enable early diagnosis of potential more severe disorders. The intestinal epithelium contains inflammatory cells that increase their activity and secrete inflammatory molecules in pathological processes. These molecules can be retained in intestinal contents and quantified in feces. It should be noted that over 70% of cells in the immune system that produce various biomarkers of inflammation and immune function are located in the gastrointestinal tract (Celi et al., 2019).

Developing objective, robust, validated, and noninvasive diagnostic tools is essential for predicting intestinal inflammatory processes and making appropriate management and/or therapy decisions. In human medicine, various analytical parameters have been adopted as routine biomarkers of intestinal inflammation (Alghoul et al., 2022; Bennike et al., 2014; Sands, 2015). Some of them have been proposed as promising biomarkers in porcine feces, including calprotectin, lipocalin-2 (LCN-2), myeloperoxidase (MPO), and adenosine deaminase (ADA) (Celi et al., 2019; Niewold, 2015; Soares et al., 2022; Svoboda et al., 2024). Calprotectin, a 36-kDa protein from the S100 family composed of S100A8 and S100A9 subunits, is principally found in neutrophils. It appears in feces due to the migration of neutrophils to the gastrointestinal tract during infection or inflammation (Pathirana et al., 2018). Fecal calprotectin (fCal) is used to diagnose several human pathologies, including Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, celiac disease, and colorectal cancer (McMahon & Chhabra, 2018). The immunoturbidimetric method (Bühlmann fCAL® turbo, Bühlmann Laboratories AG, Schönenbuch, Suiza) for Calprotectin has recently been validated in our laboratory for porcine and bovine feces (Pato et al., 2023). Lipocalin-2 (LCN-2), also known as neutrophil gelatinase associated-lipocalin (NGAL), is a glycoprotein mainly produced by neutrophils and other cell types, including myeloid and intestinal epithelial cells (Zollner et al., 2021). In humans, fecal LCN-2 is used as a biomarker for inflammatory bowel disease and Crohn’s disease (Zollner et al., 2021). It has also been measured in pig feces (Cheng et al., 2018; Choi et al., 2023; Li et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2020). Myeloperoxidase (MPO, EC 1.11.2.2) is an enzyme found in azurophilic granules and is released during inflammatory conditions due to neutrophil degranulation (Arosa et al., 2022; Burn et al., 2021). MPO catalyzes the reaction of H2O2 and chloride ions to form hypochlorous acid (HOCl), which plays an important role in microbial killing. MPO has also been measured in human feces (Edwards et al., 2024; Ekoff et al., 2024; Swaminathan et al., 2022) and in porcine saliva samples (Botía et al., 2023). Finally, adenosine deaminase (ADA, EC 3.5.4.4) is an enzyme involved in purine metabolism that converts adenosine to inosine, and 2′-deoxyadenosine to 2′-deoxyinosine. ADA plays an important role in the adaptive immune system by increasing the proliferation and differentiation of T lymphocytes. Increased ADA activity has been observed in the serum of individuals with in diseases involving with a high number of T-lymphocytes (Fontes Baganha et al., 1990; Zavialov & Engström, 2005), including several gastrointestinal pathologies in humans (Beyazit et al., 2012; Vrabie & Kane, 2014). ADA has been measured in porcine serum and saliva. It has been shown that lame pigs have increased salivary ADA compared to healthy pigs (Contreras-Aguilar et al., 2020; Sánchez et al., 2021). There is no evidence in the literature about ADA determination in feces.

Analytical validation of any parameter (precision, linearity under dilution and recovery) is necessary before using it in the laboratory (Franco-Martínez et al., 2020; Kjelgaard-Hansen, 2010). The main objectives of the present study were to validate the methodologies described for LCN-2, MPO and ADA in porcine fecal samples and to assess the effects of sample homogeneity and storage conditions on the determination of these biomarkers and fCAL. These objectives aim to standardize preservation and collection protocols and to facilitate data interpretation in animal science and medicine.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and fecal sampling procedure

All the assays were approved by the Ethical Committee for Animal Experimentation at the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona in accordance with European and Spanish laws on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes (code: 12568, project grant CPP2021-008315).

Feces from different farms were used, all of which followed the same procedures and management conditions based on commercial diets and feeding programs. Piglets were weaned at 21 days of age and moved into the nursery facilities. They were distributed to ensure equality in body weight and sex, and they were vaccinated against Porcine Circovirus 2b (PCV2b) and Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae. On all farms, piglets were distributed to have at least 0.21 m2 per piglet, resulting in pens of different sizes and number of animals per pen. All farms had pens equipped with commercial, non-lidded feeders and nipple drinkers, providing ad libitum access to feed and water. Rooms were maintained at 26–28°C during the first three weeks of post-weaning (PW), then gradually lowered to 24 °C. The piglets received light from 0700 h to 1800 h, with natural light entering the building through multiple windows in the walls, supplemented by artificial lighting. Ventilation was controlled by adjustable fans connected to temperature sensors, to ensure optimal airflow. The diet specifications included a net energy content ranging from 2638 to 3412 kcal/kg, ether extract ranging from 4.80 % to 4.85 %, neutral detergent fiber (NDF) ranging from 6.31 % to 9.36 %, crude protein from 17 % to 19 %, and standardized ileal digestible (SID) lysine from 1.30 % to 1.37 %.

Piglets were immobilized and fecal samples were collected via gentle anal stimulation by inserting a cotton swap into the rectum. The samples were immediately stored in Falcon tubes on dry ice during transportation, and kept at -80 °C until extraction time, except for the stability study (Section 2.7). A total of 152 piglets were involved in the study and 334 fecal samples were obtained. A reduced number were selected depending on the type of analysis to be performed (2.4, 2.6).

Fecal extraction procedure

Fecal samples were processed following the instructions described in (Pato et al., 2023) with slight modifications. For all assays, 50 mg of stool were weighted and diluted 1:50 (w/v) with buffer A (137 mM NaCl, 10 mM NaH2PO4, 2.7 mM KCl, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, 0.25 M citric acid, 2.5 M urea, 0.025 M CaCl2 and 1.25 % BSA, pH 8) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) (Holtund et al., 2001). Each tube was vortexed for one minute, incubated on a shaker for one hour at room temperature, vortexed again for 30 s and centrifuged at 4200 x g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant extracts were frozen in aliquots at -80 °C until analysis. Analysis were performed within a month.

Biomarker assays

Fecal calprotectin was determined using the immunoturbidimetric method (Bühlmann fCAL® turbo, Bühlmann Laboratories AG, Schönenbuch, Switzerland) previously validated in porcine fecal samples (Pato et al., 2023). Lipocalin-2 was determined using a commercial porcine ELISA assay developed for blood samples (Porcine Lipocalin-2/NGAL, Biotechne NBP2-62163, Novus Biologicals LLC Centennial, CO, USA). For ADA analysis, an enzymatic colorimetric method described for human serum was used (BioSystems, COD 23754, Barcelona, Spain).

Myeloperoxidase was determined spectrophotometrically using the o-dianisidine oxidation method (Bradley et al., 1982) with slight modifications. Briefly, the two reagents were freshly prepared daily: Reactive 1 (R1, 0.165 mg/mL O-dianisidine dihydrochloride, Sigma-Aldrich® diluted in 50 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)), and Reactive 2 (R2, 0.0005 % hydrogen peroxide 30 %, Sigma-Aldrich® diluted in distilled water). The autoanalyzer configuration was 270 µL R1, 20 µL R2 and 10 µL of the sample in kinetic method analysis at 460 nm wavelength. The MPO concentration was calculated according to the equation: [MPO(U/L)=((ΔA/min)xVtx106)/(11300x0.5xVs)], where Vt is the total volume in the reaction mix (µl), 106 is the conversion factor from mol to µmol, Vs is the volume of the sample (µL), 11300 (mol-1cm-1) corresponds to the extinction coefficient of o-dianisidine at 460 nm and 0.5 corresponds to the cuvette length (cm). The final factor is 4424.8 and the formula to calculate the activity is [MPO(U/L)=ΔA/minx4424.8].

The fCal, MPO and ADA methods were adapted for use with the Beckman Coulter® AU400 Chemistry Analyzer. The ELISA results for LCN-2 were read using a Multiskan Sky plate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Method validation

The validation studies, which included an assessment of precision, linearity under dilution, recovery, and detection limit, were conducted in accordance with the Quality Assurance and Laboratory Standards Guidelines defined by the American Society of Veterinary Clinical Pathology (ASVCP) (Arnold et al., 2019). Three individual piglet fecal samples were used for each biomarker to obtain different analyte concentration levels (high, medium and low).

Precision

Intra-assay precision was determined by analyzing twenty replicates of each sample extract in the same assay, using individual samples of the three concentration levels: low, medium, and high. For inter-assay precision, five replicates of each sample were determined on five different days, using three concentrations low, medium, and high. A new aliquot stored at -80 °C was used each day. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV, %) were calculated using the following formula: [CV = SD/mean concentration x 100].

Linearity under dilution

Linearity was determined by evaluating the parallelism of dilutions of fecal sample extracts with a high concentration of biomarkers at serial 2-fold dilutions to obtain five different dilution levels. Four replicates were performed for each dilution and the mean value was obtained. The sample dilution for each biomarker was chosen differently to cover the entire analytical range. The results were analyzed by linear regression.

Recovery

High, medium, and low concentration fecal extracts were selected. A fecal extract with a high known concentration (MPO: 401.8 U/L; LCN-2: 823.0 pg/mL; ADA: 68 U/L) was used as the spiking material. An aliquot of this material was spiked into the spike (+) samples, and the same volume of dilution buffer was spiked into the spike (-) samples. A control tube containing buffer A was spiked with the same spiking material. As recommended, the dilution of the original specimen did not exceed 10%. The assay was performed in duplicate for each concentration. The average results of the replicates were used to calculate the recovery using the following formula: Recovery(%)=(spike(+)−−spike(−))/control·100.

Detection limits

The detection limits were assessed for the methods adapted to the analyzer. One fecal extract with an analyte concentration close to zero was analyzed to estimate the limit of detection (LoD) and the limit of quantification (LoQ). Twenty replicate measurements were performed and the blank was calculated with twenty replicates of the dilution buffer. LoD and LoQ were calculated using the following formulas: LoD=[meanvalueoftheblank+ 1,65 x SD of a lowconcentrationsample]. LoQ=[meanvalueoftheblank+ 2 x SD of a lowconcentrationsample] (Arnold et al., 2019).

Myeloperoxidase inhibition assay

Other peroxidases in addition to MPO can oxidize the substrate and thus interfere with MPO determination. To evaluate this possibility in feces, an inhibition assay using 4-aminobenzoic hydrazide (ABAH) was performed. ABAH is an irreversible inhibitor of MPO activity but does not inhibit the other peroxidases (Kettle et al., 1995, 1997). The assay was performed on ten fecal samples, five in the low-measurable range (8 U/L) and five in the high-measurable range (125 U/L). The ABAH inhibitor was added to the samples before the reagents were added, and the assay was performed just after.

Homogeneity of the biomarker distribution in fecal samples

The distribution of the biomarkers may not be uniform in fecal samples, particularly in large samples. To study this heterogeneity, five large stools (>10 g) from different animals were selected and, from each of these, 5 samples (50 mg) were taken from different points on each stool. Once all of these portions were obtained, each original sample was mixed to homogenize it as much as possible. From the resulting mixture, five extracts (50 mg) were also generated. Statistical analysis

Linearity was evaluated using linear regression analysis. A dose-response curve for MPO inhibition by ABAH was plotted, and the IC50 value was calculated for low and high concentration samples.

To evaluate the distribution of biomarkers in fecal samples, descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation and CV) were calculated. A student T-test for paired samples was applied to analyze whether there were significant differences between conditions. Pearson’s correlation was used to measure the association between homogenized and non-homogenized samples.

Repeated measures ANOVA and the Bonferroni post hoc tests were used to evaluate changes in mean concentration after storage of stools and fecal extracts at different temperatures. Changes exceeding two intra-assay CV and showing significant differences (p < 0.05) over time relative to baseline concentrations (day 0 = 100 % concentration) were considered to indicate unacceptable stability funder the given storage conditions (Escribano et al., 2014; Gröschl et al., 2001).

All the statistical analysis were performed using SPSS 25 software (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Graphical representations were created in GraphPad Prism 8.1 software. Tables and data processing were executed using Excel (Microsoft, WA, USA)]. A value of p < 0.05 was used to indicate significance.

Stability study at different storage conditions

Fecal samples were stored under four different temperature conditions and measured at various times. As shown in Fig. 3A, no significant differences were observed among time points for all biomarkers when feces were frozen at -20 °C or -80 °C. A significant loss of activity was observed after the first 24 h when fecal samples were kept refrigerated or at room temperature: 28–31 % for MPO and (48–56 % for ADA). No significant changes in activity were observed from 24 h storage to 72 h storage at room temperature, or from 24 h to one week storage at 4 °C.Fig. 3Stability of fCAL, MPO, LCN-2 and ADA in (A) porcine fecal samples and (B) fecal extracts, preserved at different temperatures and times (n = 8).Fig 3

The stability of the four biomarkers was also studied in fecal extracts stored at -20 °C or -80 °C (Fig. 3B). After one month storage, there were no significant differences in LCN-2 and MPO when extracts were kept at -20 °C or -80 °C. fCal showed a slight but significant increase in concentration (p < 0.05) after one month in frozen extracts, whereas ADA showed a 62 % loss of activity at -20 °C (p < 0.05) but remained stable at -80 °C.

Discussion

Analytical validation of fecal biomarkers

Analytical validation is an essential step prior to using of any assay in a diagnostic laboratory. It ensures the correct interpretation of quantitative results, especially when the determination is performed on a biological sample different from the one for which the reagent was developed. Globally, the validation of the three biomarkers (LCN-2, MPO and ADA) in porcine fecal samples yielded satisfactory results with imprecision values below 20 % (FDA, 2018). Linearity under dilution was demonstrated, showing good correlation between observed and expected concentrations for all analytes (R2 > 0.99). Recovery values were within the acceptable range (80–120 %) (Andreasson et al., 2015). fCal has been validated in our laboratory with optimal results (Pato et al., 2023).

LCN-2 has been previously determined in pig feces using ELISA assays developed for blood samples from different manufacturers and different extraction methods (Cheng et al., 2018; Choi et al., 2023; Moturi et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2020), although no validation studies have been reported for their use in feces. The quantitative results of these studies ranged from ng/g feces to µg/g feces, with our results falling within the lower range (ng/g). These differences highlight the need to validate extraction procedures and biochemical assays, since reagents, extraction buffers, and sample dilutions affect research results.

MPO is a well-established inflammatory marker in human medicine. It is often measured in feces to diagnose gastrointestinal diseases (Edwards et al., 2024; Ekoff et al., 2024; Swaminathan et al., 2022). However, very few reports have been published on the use of fecal MPO as an inflammatory marker in pigs. Again, different extraction procedures and methods lead to different quantitative results (Li et al., 2021; Wellington et al., 2023). The o-dianisidine method offers several advantages over other commercial methods. It has a low detection limit, enabling the measurement of low activities in a minimal sample size. It is also significantly more cost-effective than other commercial kits, including ELISA and colorimetric activity assays. Additionally, it can be adapted to autoanalyzers, which simplifies the workflow and reduces analysis time. One drawback is that the reagents used in this method are unstable and require daily preparation. One problem with MPO is its lack of specificity, as other peroxidases may act on the same substrate. This issue has not been studied in feces, which can obviously contain other enzymes involved in the redox systems. Thus, an inhibition assay with ABAH was performed to demonstrate the absence of interference from other peroxidases. The calculated IC50 (7–25 µM) is similar to the range published for human MPO (1–16 µM) (Kettle et al., 1995). The assay results indicated that MPO is essentially the only peroxidase in feces, similar to the results described in saliva (Botía et al., 2023).

Since ADA plays a role in the differentiation of T lymphocytes (Fontes Baganha et al., 1990) and is an indicator of chronic inflammation in porcine serum and saliva (Cerón et al., 2022; Contreras-Aguilar et al., 2019; Gutiérrez et al., 2017; Sánchez et al., 2021), it has been proposed as a biomarker of cell-mediated immunity. However, no data is available for porcine fecal samples. In fecal extracts prepared using our protocol, the assay was sensitive enough to easily detect total ADA activity, meeting all the requirements for analytical validation.

Homogeneity of the biomarker concentration in fecal samples

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first report on the homogeneity of fCal, LCN-2, ADA and MPO in porcine fecal samples. A similar experimental design has been used to study human fCal, where the CVs for non-homogenized samples were higher than for homogenized samples, but the results were well correlated (Tøn et al., 2000). In our study, there was no difference in the CVs between homogenized and non-homogenized samples for fCal, MPO and ADA. These CVs were numerically low (12–18 %), and indicating that these markers are homogeneously distributed in the stools. In contrast, greater differences were observed for LCN-2, with CVs ranged from 20 to 75 % (mean 50.9 %) for non-homogenized samples and from 2.5 to 16 % (mean 10.2 %) for homogenized samples. This unexpected result may be explained by the fact that LCN-2 is produced during inflammation by various cell types besides neutrophils, including myeloid and intestinal epithelial cells (Zollner et al., 2021). These cells may be unevenly distributed or damaged depending on the specific region in the mucosa.

Stability of biomarkers at different storage conditions

The stability of fCal is satisfactory when stool samples are analyzed, even when the samples have been kept at room temperature for 120 h, similar to human stools (Røseth et al., 1992; Zollner et al., 2021). Human fCal remains stable in frozen stool samples (whether at -20 or -80 °C) (Tøn et al., 2000). MPO and ADA remain stable when samples are frozen, but enzymatic activity begins to decrease after 24 h when at room temperature or 4 °C. The stability of LCN-2 was good when samples are kept frozen; however stability at higher temperatures has not been tested due to the complexity of the analysis. Human fecal LCN-2 remains stable for up to 96 h when stored at room temperature or 4 °C (Zollner et al., 2021).

As expected, the stability in fecal extracts is poorer than in feces when they are preserved at -20 °C, since sample processing and lower protein concentrations could affect the protein stability (Arakawa et al., 2001). Nevertheless, all biomarkers remain stable for up to one month when stored at -80 °C.

In our study, changes that did not exceed two intra-assay CV of each assay nor show significant differences (p < 0.05) over time were considered to indicate acceptable stability under the given storage conditions (Escribano et al., 2014; Gröschl et al., 2001). Other authors have considered that changes in concentration or activity within ±20 % are not considered significant in stability studies due to the variability inherent in the extraction process and the measurement method (Zollner et al., 2021). The conclusions of the experiment remain the same regardless of the criteria used.

Limitations of the study

The main objective of this study was the analytical validation of these fecal biomarkers in a laboratory setting, in accordance with ASVCP guidelines. We also considered other aspects of sample collection and management that could significantly impact the results.

With this in mind, there are limitations to be studied in the near future. First, biological validation is necessary to determine the performance of these biomarkers (including fCal) in different conditions and management situations or pathologies. In this regard, establishing a 'threshold' limit for intestinal inflammation would be highly beneficial. Several studies are currently being conducted in our laboratories to address these issues. Furthermore, the present work has focused on weaning piglets, which are severely affected by gastrointestinal problems. The suitability of these biomarkers for pigs of different ages, genetics, conditions and pathologies must also be studied.

This work also emphasizes the importance of standardizing preanalytical factors that may affect results, such as sample collection, management and storage, as well as validating reagents and equipment between laboratories. The goal is to reduce variability between laboratories and promote the use of fecal biomarkers in veterinary practice.

Conclusions

The analytical features of the methods tested for determining MPO, LCN-2, and ADA are adequate and support their use in porcine feces. Three biomarkers (fCal, MPO, and ADA) are evenly distributed in stool samples; however, caution must be exercised when analyzing LCN-2 due to its heterogeneous distribution. Sample stability testing revealed that storage of fresh stool samples at -20 °C or -80 °C is advisable for maintaining biomarker integrity. These findings provide essential validation data for the implementation of intestinal health biomarkers in swine research and production settings, which is particularly relevant in the current context of antibiotic and zinc oxide restrictions in swine production.
