Multicenter analysis of change fatigue and job crafting among nurse leaders in China: latent profiles and associated factors
Authors: Xiaoya Wu, Xinyue Zhang, Mengya Guo, Liu Yuan, Ying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid and continuous organizational changes in healthcare systems may increase the risk of change fatigue among nurse leaders, potentially affecting both workforce stability and care quality. However, the heterogeneity of change fatigue and its associated factors among nurse leaders remain insufficiently understood.AimTo identify distinct latent profiles of change fatigue among nurse leaders in a multicenter sample, and to examine factors related to profile membership, particularly job crafting.MethodsThis multicenter cross-sectional study was included 1,769 nurse leaders recruited from 76 hospitals across Hubei Province, China. This study utilized a self-designed general information questionnaire, the Change Fatigue Scale, and the Job Crafting Scale. A latent profile analysis was conducted on nurse leaders’ change fatigue, and univariate analysis and logistic regression were used to identify the factors influencing the latent profile categories of change fatigue among nurse leaders.ResultsChange fatigue among nurse leaders was represented by three latent profiles: a low change fatigue-proactive adaptive profile (31.3%), a moderate change fatigue-tolerant adaptive profile (50.5%), and a high change fatigue-maladaptive profile (18.2%). Multinomial logistic regression indicated that health condition, type of hospital, experience of changing employment, income satisfaction, and dimensions of job crafting were correlates of profile membership. Specifically, poorer health condition and lower income satisfaction increased the probability of belonging to the moderate and high change fatigue profiles compared with the low profile (p < 0.01), whereas greater cognitive crafting was linked to a reduced probability of membership in higher fatigue profiles (p < 0.001).ConclusionNurse leaders generally reported a moderate level of change fatigue. Change fatigue showed clear heterogeneity, indicating that individuals in the moderate and high fatigue profiles warrant particular attention. In addition, change fatigue was negatively related to job crafting, suggesting that enhancing job crafting may help reduce change fatigue among nurse leaders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04977-2.

Introduction

The healthcare industry is undergoing rapid and continuous organizational transformation, including technological innovation, workflow restructuring, and infrastructure upgrades to meet the growing demand for healthcare services [1]. In such environments, nurses are frequently required to adapt to complex and dynamic workplace conditions. However, when organizational change becomes frequent, prolonged, and difficult to predict, it may impose substantial psychological and occupational strain on nursing staff, compromising their physical and psychological health and contributing to burnout, anxiety, depression, reduced job satisfaction, and turnover intention [2, 3].

One concept that has emerged to describe this phenomenon is change fatigue, which has been conceptualized as overwhelming stress, exhaustion, and reduced capacity to cope caused by repeated and ongoing workplace change [4, 5]. In nursing settings, change fatigue may manifest as emotional depletion, reduced professional vitality, disengagement, and passive responses to change [4, 6]. A report released by the International Council of Nurses (ICN) in 2025 highlighted the deepening global nursing workforce crisis, noting that persistently high workloads, nursing shortages, and rapidly evolving healthcare demands have significantly increased occupational fatigue and intentions to leave among nurses [7]. Amid ongoing healthcare system reforms, this issue may be particularly salient for nurse leaders, who occupy a pivotal position in the implementation of organizational change. In addition to adapting to changes themselves, nurse leaders are also responsible for coordinating teams, communicating change-related expectations, and ensuring the quality and safety of patient care during transitional periods [8–10]. As a result, they may experience a dual burden: pressure from organizational demands on the one hand and responsibility for supporting frontline nursing staff on the other [11, 12]. In the Chinese healthcare context, ongoing hospital reforms, accreditation requirements, digital transformation, and continuous adjustments to nursing service delivery have further intensified the pace of organizational change [13, 14]. These conditions may increase nurse leaders’ vulnerability to change fatigue and, in turn, affect staff stability and care quality.

Nurse leaders must balance organizational responsibilities with the quality of patient care [9]. Additionally, they face many of the same stressors as frontline nurses, including heavy clinical workloads, low pay, and a lack of social support. Furthermore, they face escalating pressure from conflicting priorities at both hospital and departmental levels. Nurse leaders often occupy central positions in hospital reforms, necessitating adaptation to structural, technological, and policy-related transformations [10]. These changes often lead to an accumulation of stress, heightening susceptibility to change fatigue. Despite its practical importance, research on change fatigue among nurse leaders remains limited.

Most previous studies have assessed change fatigue using total scale scores and variable-centered methods, which may obscure meaningful heterogeneity within the population. Nurse leaders may not respond uniformly to organizational change. Some may remain adaptive and proactive, whereas others may experience persistent fatigue and difficulty coping. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) is an individual-centered method designed to identify latent subgroups that exhibit similar response patterns [15]. LPA has been increasingly used in nursing research to examine heterogeneity in psychological and behavioral characteristics such as resilience, burnout, and work engagement [16, 17]. Applying LPA may therefore provide a more nuanced understanding of change fatigue among nurse leaders and help identify subgroups with different levels of vulnerability.

In addition, job crafting may be an important factor associated with how nurse leaders experience and respond to organizational change. Job crafting refers to the process by which individuals proactively redefine their work tasks, perceptions of their work, and workplace interactions to achieve a better alignment between professional demands and their own intrinsic needs, strengths, and values [18]. Existing evidence suggests that job crafting is closely linked to increased well-being, greater work engagement, and improved resilience in high-pressure work situations [19, 20]. However, there has been little research on the relationship between job crafting and potential signs of change fatigue among nursing leaders. Against the backdrop of repeated organizational transformation, nurse leaders who engage in greater job crafting may be more capable of reinterpret work demands, mobilizing social support, and making behavioral adjustments, thereby reducing their vulnerability to change fatigue [21, 22].

This multicenter cross-sectional survey aimed to characterize latent profiles of change fatigue in nurse leaders using LPA and investigate factors linked to profile membership status, with particular attention paid to the contribution of job crafting. By clarifying the heterogeneity in change fatigue together with its associated factors, this study seeks to provide evidence for developing targeted nursing management interventions to support nurse leaders in adapting more effectively to organizational change.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

This study was derived from a larger multicenter survey in Hubei Province, China, investigating nurse outcomes [23]. Between January 5 and January 12, 2024, a cross-sectional investigation spanning the entire province was implemented by combining stratified and convenience sampling methods. Each city included at least three tertiary hospitals and two secondary hospitals. According to the hospital classification system of the National Health Commission (NHC), hospitals are categorized into three tiers based on scale, technical capacity, and scope of services. Secondary hospitals are generally city- or county-level hospitals that provide comprehensive medical services within a defined regional area. Tertiary hospitals are classified as the highest tier and generally provide a broader range of services and undertake more teaching and research responsibilities. Stratified sampling was undertaken with stratification determined by hospital levels and geographic distribution throughout all 17 cities in Hubei Province, while convenience sampling was us ed to enroll nurse leaders across different departments in each hospital.

The necessary sample size was calculated according to the statistical techniques employed in this research, namely LPA and multinomial logistic regression. To meet the sample size requirements of both methodologies, the larger sample size criterion was adopted, resulting in an estimated total sample size of 300 participants [24, 25]. From this larger dataset, 1,793 nurse leaders from 76 hospitals from 17 cities were included according to the following criteria: (a) currently serving as a nurse leader; (b) holding a valid nursing practice qualification certificate. Nurse leaders on leave, not in position, undertaking an internship, or undertaking further education were excluded. During the data cleaning process, questionnaires containing outliers, duplicate submissions, or responses with excessively short completion times were removed. Finally, a total of 1,769 eligible questionnaires were included, representing a 98.66% response rate.

Procedure

The data were collected using the web-based platform WJX, operated by Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Company (Ranxing Tech). The questionnaires were distributed to the nursing directors of each participating hospital, along with instructions on how to complete them and the required format. The directors of nursing then distributed the questionnaires to the nurse leaders of each department that met the inclusion criteria. The questionnaires followed a standardized format and a standardized instruction sheet, explaining the aim of the survey and the significance of the study. Every participant was allowed to complete the questionnaire only once on one electronic device, and the completion time was limited to 10 min.

Measurements

Self-designed general information questionnaire

The questionnaire was structured into two categories: demographic information and work-related characteristics. The demographic characteristics covered age, gender, marital status, number of children, religious beliefs, education level, and health condition. The work-related characteristics included professional title, years of nursing experience, department, type of hospital, experience of changing employment, and income satisfaction. The city where the hospital was located, the hospital’s full name, together with the department were retained solely for reference in the source data document. Since this instrument primarily gathered demographic and background data, reliability assessment was unnecessary. Experts in nursing administration and clinical nursing were invited to evaluate the relevance of each scale item to the research topic. Content validity index analysis revealed satisfactory results at both the item and scale levels, indicating good content validity.

Change fatigue scale

The Change Fatigue Scale (Bernerth et al.) served as the measure of change fatigue [5]. The instrument is designed to assess employees’ experiences of stress, exhaustion, and burnout resulting from ongoing and frequent organizational change. The scale is single-dimensional and includes six items. Responses are made on a 7-point Likert format anchored by 1 (strongly disagree) and 7 (strongly agree). Possible total scores range from 6 to 42, where higher scores signal more severe change fatigue. The Chinese version of the Change Fatigue Scale has been validated in nursing contexts and has demonstrated acceptable psychometric performance [26]. In a Chinese nursing sample, the scale demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity, with Cronbach’s α of 0.918, Guttman split-half reliability of 0.911, test-retest reliability of 0.894, content validity indices within acceptable ranges, and a one-factor structure explaining 72.665% of the variance [26].

Job crafting scale

Job crafting was captured via the 21-item Job Crafting Scale (Dvorak, 2014; based on boundary shift theory) [18], as revised into Chinese by Zhu Shixiao [27]. The scale comprises three seven-item dimensions: task, cognitive, and relational crafting. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with elevated scores indicating more extensive job crafting. The Chinese version has yielded satisfactory reliability; the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the task, cognitive, and relational subscales were 0.84, 0.89, and 0.89 respectively, and 0.91 for the overall scale.

Data analysis

Data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using Mplus 8.3 and SPSS 27.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic data and survey results. Continuous variables were described using the mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

In the latent profile analysis, model fit was compared using the following criteria: (1) information indices: Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC); (2) likelihood ratio tests: the Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMRT) and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT); and (3) classification diagnostics: entropy and class size distribution. Smaller values of AIC, BIC, and aBIC signify better fit, while entropy approaching 1 indicates higher classification precision. LMRT and BLRT were employed to determine the number of latent classes, selecting a k-class model when it significantly outperformed a k-1-class model (p < 0.05) [28–30]. Profile meaningfulness was additionally considered in the selection of the final model. The association between change fatigue and job crafting was examined with Pearson correlation analysis. Demographic characteristics, change fatigue, and job crafting were compared across profiles through chi-square tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the Kruskal–Wallis H test. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was employed to check for multicollinearity among the predictors. Although age was highly correlated with years of nursing experience, both variables were converted into categorical groups before inclusion in the regression model to reduce potential multicollinearity. Variables were entered into the multinomial logistic regression model based on theoretical relevance, prior evidence, and their potential association with latent profile membership. Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess potential common method bias. To account for the large sample size and multiple statistical tests, effect sizes were reported to evaluate practical significance, with effects explaining at least 5% of the variance considered substantive. A conservative significance level of p < 0.01 was adopted for the final analyses, and Bonferroni correction was applied to post hoc pairwise comparisons where appropriate.

Ethical statement

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. TJ‑IRB202401089). Participants were briefed on the purpose and importance of the research, and all gave informed consent prior to enrollment. Involvement was entirely voluntary and anonymous, and individuals were informed that they could cease participation at any time without facing any negative repercussions. All data were stored securely, handled confidentially, and used solely for analytical purposes.

Results

Latent profiles of change fatigue

To determine the optimal number of latent profiles, one- to six-profile models were compared, and the corresponding fit indices are presented in Table 1. The values of AIC, BIC, and aBIC progressively declined as more profiles were extracted. The entropy values of the two- to five-profile models were all above 0.90, indicating good classification accuracy. In addition, Significant LMRT and BLRT values (p < 0.05) emerged for models with two to five profiles, suggesting that each provided a superior fit relative to its k-1 counterpart. However, both the 5- and 6-profile models contained classes with relatively small proportions, which reduced the interpretability of the results. Considering the model fit indices, entropy values, class proportions, as well as interpretability, the 3-profile model was identified as the optimal solution. In this model, the entropy value was 0.930, and the proportions of the three profiles were 31.3%, 50.5%, and 18.2%, respectively (Table 1).

Figure 1 presents the distributions of the six change fatigue scale items across the three latent profiles. Profile 1 showed the lowest scores on most items of change fatigue, indicating relatively low levels of fatigue in response to organizational changes. This group was therefore labeled the “low change fatigue-proactive adaptive profile”, accounting for 31.3% of the sample (n = 553). Profile 2 demonstrated moderate scores across all items, suggesting a moderate level of change fatigue and a relatively stable response to organizational changes. This group was defined as the “moderate change fatigue-tolerant adaptive profile”. It comprised 50.5% of the sample (n = 894), representing the largest proportion of participants. Profile 3 exhibited consistently higher scores on all change fatigue items compared with the other two profiles, reflecting a higher level of fatigue when facing organizational changes. Therefore, this group was identified as the “high change fatigue-maladaptive profile”, comprising 18.2% of all participants (n = 322).

Characteristics of the participants

Prior to the main analyses, potential common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. The first unrotated factor accounted for 40.889% of the total variance, below the commonly used threshold of 50%, suggesting that severe common method bias was unlikely to be present. Altogether, 1,793 nurse leaders responded to the survey. After excluding invalid questionnaires, a total of 1,769 were included. Participants averaged 40.90 years of age (SD = 6.14). Females represented 97.4% of the sample. Married participants accounted for 93.9% (n = 1,661), and 95.3% (n = 1,686) had children. 84.7% (n = 1,499) of participants held a bachelor’s degree, with intermediate and senior professional titles accounting for 95.5% (n = 1,689). Only 3.7% (n = 65) of nurse leaders reported having religious beliefs, and 3.1% (n = 55) perceived their health status as below average. Additionally, 76.2% of participants were from tertiary hospitals, with the majority (63.8%) possessing more than 15 years of experience in nursing. Significant differences across latent profiles were observed for health condition, type of hospital, experience of changing employment, and income satisfaction (all p < 0.01). The corresponding effect sizes, assessed using Cramer’s, ranged from 0.115 to 0.229. Additional details are presented in Table 2.

Correlational analysis of nurse leaders’ characteristics, change fatigue and job crafting levels

Descriptive statistics revealed that the mean values of change fatigue and job crafting were 22.35 ± 7.65 and 133.14 ± 21.13, respectively. Change fatigue was negatively correlated with overall job crafting (r = -0.344, p < 0.01), explaining 11.8% of the variance. Negative correlations were also observed between change fatigue and task crafting (r = -0.289, r² = 8.4%, p < 0.01), cognitive crafting (r = -0.315, r² = 9.9%, p < 0.01), and relational crafting (r = -0.282, r² = 8.0%, p < 0.01). These associations met the criterion for substantive significance. Although age and years of nursing experience were statistically associated with change fatigue (r = 0.079 and r = 0.072, respectively; both p < 0.01), these associations explained less than 1% of the variance and therefore had limited practical significance. After categorization, the VIF values for all variables fell within an acceptable range (VIF < 10), which indicated no significant multicollinearity was not a concern. Differences in job crafting across latent profiles

Differences in job crafting across the three latent profiles were examined using ANOVA, and the results are presented in Table 4. Nurse leaders in the low change fatigue-proactive adaptive profile (C1) had the highest scores on overall job crafting and all three dimensions, including task crafting (26.13 ± 6.01), cognitive crafting (30.52 ± 4.46), and relational crafting (31.20 ± 4.06). Participants in the moderate change fatigue–tolerant adaptive profile (C2) and the high change fatigue-maladaptive profile (C3) reported comparatively lower scores across these dimensions. Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons showed that C1 had significantly higher scores than both C2 and C3 in task crafting, cognitive crafting, relational crafting, and overall job crafting. No statistically significant differences were found between C2 and C3 under the more conservative significance threshold of p < 0.01. Effect size estimates indicated that these differences were substantively meaningful, with η² values ranging from 0.070 to 0.136.

Predictors of latent profile membership

Table 5 presents the multinomial logistic regression findings for factors associated with latent profile membership. With the low change fatigue–proactive adaptive profile (C1) as the reference group, nurse leaders with fair health condition (OR = 1.858, 95% CI: 1.425–2.422), experience of changing employment (OR = 2.270, 95% CI: 1.502–3.431), dissatisfied income satisfaction (OR = 3.034, 95% CI: 1.551–5.935), or neutral income satisfaction (OR = 2.661, 95% CI: 1.990–3.558) were more likely to belong to the moderate change fatigue–tolerant adaptive profile (C2). Higher cognitive crafting (OR = 0.881, 95% CI: 0.844–0.919) and relational crafting (OR = 0.923, 95% CI: 0.885–0.963) were associated with lower odds of C2 membership.

Compared with C1, nurse leaders with poor health condition (OR = 7.009, 95% CI: 2.242–21.913) or fair health condition (OR = 1.700, 95% CI: 1.217–2.373) were more likely to belong to the high change fatigue-maladaptive profile (C3). Nurse leaders working in tertiary hospitals were less likely to be classified into C3 (OR = 0.597, 95% CI: 0.405–0.880). In addition, those with dissatisfied income satisfaction (OR = 10.276, 95% CI: 5.053–20.897) or neutral income satisfaction (OR = 5.073, 95% CI: 3.535–7.280) were more likely to belong to C3. Higher cognitive crafting was associated with lower odds of C3 membership (OR = 0.905, 95% CI: 0.859–0.954).

When C3 was used as the reference group, nurse leaders with dissatisfied income satisfaction (OR = 0.295, 95% CI: 0.187–0.467) or neutral income satisfaction (OR = 0.525, 95% CI: 0.386–0.713) were less likely to be classified into C2, indicating a greater likelihood of C3 membership. Higher task crafting was associated with greater odds of C2 membership (OR = 1.067, 95% CI: 1.034–1.101), whereas higher relational crafting was associated with lower odds of C2 membership (OR = 0.939, 95% CI: 0.899–0.980). Only associations meeting the conservative significance threshold of p < 0.01 were interpreted.

Discussion

LPA was used to distinguish subgroups of change fatigue among nurse leaders. Based on the response patterns across the scale items three latent profiles of change fatigue emerged: a low change fatigue-proactive adaptive profile, a moderate change fatigue-tolerant adaptive profile, and a high change fatigue-maladaptive profile. These findings suggest that nurse leaders do not respond uniformly to organizational change; rather, they exhibit meaningful heterogeneity in fatigue experiences. This person-centered evidence extends prior variable-centered research and provides a basis for profile-specific management strategies to mitigate change fatigue.

From an integrated theoretical perspective, repeated organizational changes may gradually deplete nurse leaders’ cognitive, emotional, and occupational resources, which may be associated with greater vulnerability to change fatigue [5, 31]. Consistent with burnout theory, when work demands persistently exceed available resources and rewards are insufficient, individuals may experience reduced energy, impaired coping capacity, and maladaptive responses [22]. Job crafting may serve as a proactive resource-regulation strategy through which nurse leaders reinterpret work meaning, adjust task boundaries, and mobilize relational support [19, 20]. This interpretation is consistent with our finding that nurse leaders with higher job crafting scores were more likely to be classified into the low change fatigue-proactive adaptive profile.

Latent profile characteristics of change fatigue

The high change fatigue-maladaptive profile comprised 18.2% of the total sample, indicating that nearly one in five nurse leaders experienced persistently elevated fatigue in response to organizational change. This subgroup may reflect individuals who experience greater difficulty adapting to repeated change demands. Prior theory suggests that when change becomes prolonged, frequent, and unpredictable, employees’ cognitive and emotional resources may be depleted [5], increasing the likelihood of fatigue and reduced engagement [32]. Marks’ saturation effect theory proposes that there is a limit to the impact of change on employees [31]. Vries et al., further suggest that an overload of change initiatives can make periods of stability more attractive [33]. In nursing management settings, where professional responsibilities are often combined with administrative pressures and family demands [34], repeated organizational changes may further intensify this burden [35].

The moderate change fatigue-tolerant adaptive profile represented the largest subgroup (50.5%), suggesting that for many nurse leaders, change fatigue may manifest as a manageable but ongoing strain rather than an extreme state. This finding is important because moderate fatigue may be easily overlooked in management practice, despite its potential to accumulate over time and develop into more severe maladaptive responses if support is lacking [36].

The low change fatigue-proactive adaptive profile accounted for 31.3% of the sample. Compared with the other two profiles, individuals in this subgroup appeared to show better adaptation to organizational change. Rather than indicating an absence of challenge, this profile may reflect more favorable personal or organizational conditions that help buffer fatigue [35, 37]. Understanding this subgroup may inform supportive interventions for nurse leaders at higher risk of change fatigue. Overall, the profile distribution suggests that moderate to high change fatigue is common among nurse leaders, emphasizing the importance of customized managerial responses as opposed to a generic, standardized solution [38].

Influencing factors of latent profiles

In multinomial logistic regression analyses, health condition, type of hospital, experience of changing employment, income satisfaction, and dimensions of job crafting were associated with latent profile membership under the conservative significance threshold of p < 0.01. Among these factors, health condition, income satisfaction, and cognitive crafting showed relatively consistent associations with profile membership. Fair or poor health among nurse leaders increased the odds of classification into the high change fatigue profile. This result supports the stress-health model, which suggests that prolonged exposure to stressors such as organizational change can impair individuals’ physical and psychological functioning, thereby reducing their coping capacity [39]. In nursing management contexts, ongoing organizational change may interact with existing health burdens [40–43], making adaptation more difficult for those in poorer health [44].

Compared with the low change fatigue profile, nurse leaders with dissatisfied or neutral income satisfaction were significantly more likely to be classified into the high change fatigue profile. These findings indicate that perceived rewards are closely linked to how individuals experience and cope with organizational change. According to Maslach’s theory, insufficient rewards, whether economic, social, or intrinsic, may lead to burnout among nurses when persistently mismatched with work demands [22]. Numerous studies have reported that many nurses perceive their income as inadequate, and income satisfaction is a key predictor of turnover, while fair and reasonable compensation plays a crucial role in nurse retention [45]. In addition, inequitable benefit structures in hospital employment systems may further reduce both nurse and patient satisfaction [46]. In the context of ongoing change, high work investment without corresponding returns may lead to psychological imbalance, thereby exacerbating fatigue.

Job crafting also played an important role in distinguishing latent profiles, although the effects varied across dimensions. Cognitive crafting showed the most consistent protective association, suggesting that the ability to reinterpret work in a meaningful and constructive way may help nurse leaders better accommodate organizational change [47]. This result aligns with prior studies showing that cognitive crafting can strengthen work meaning, role identity, and emotional regulation [19, 20, 48–50]. In the multivariable multinomial logistic regression model, task crafting showed a more differentiated pattern and was significant in distinguishing the moderate and high change fatigue profiles. This may indicate that behavioral adjustments to tasks are relevant for differentiating moderate and high levels of change fatigue [51].

Relational crafting was associated with profile membership in a less uniform manner, implying that social interaction and relationship management may function differently across adaptation contexts [52, 53]. Taken together, these results indicate that job crafting should not be viewed as a single undifferentiated resource; instead, its dimensions may contribute to change adaptation through distinct psychological, behavioral, and interpersonal pathways [19]. At the organizational level, type of hospital showed a specific association with the high change fatigue profile. Nurse leaders working in tertiary hospitals were less likely to fall into the high change fatigue profiles than their counterparts in secondary hospitals. Experience of changing employment was associated only with membership in the moderate change fatigue profile. These findings should be interpreted cautiously and not overextended beyond the specific profile comparisons in which they were statistically significant.

Implications for future clinical practice

These findings provide practical guidance for nursing management. Because change fatigue among nurse leaders is heterogeneous, intervention strategies should be tailored to profile characteristics rather than applied uniformly. For nurse leaders in the high change fatigue profile, priority should be given to establishing a multidimensional support system, including strengthening communication mechanisms, conducting resilience training, promoting self-care, and fostering a high-trust team atmosphere, thereby enhancing their adaptive and coping abilities [54]. For those in the moderate change fatigue profile, early intervention may be especially important to prevent further escalation of fatigue. Strategies such as workload adjustment, increased participation in decision-making, and timely emotional support may be beneficial [55]. For nurse leaders in the low change fatigue profile, management efforts should focus on maintaining adaptive functioning and leveraging their strengths in change-related roles, such as peer support, mentoring, or participation in implementation initiatives [56]. Moreover, the findings highlight the practical value of job crafting in practice. Training programs aimed at strengthening cognitive crafting, while also supporting task and relational crafting in a structured way, may help nurse leaders develop more adaptive responses to organizational change. Finally, management attention should also be directed toward broader contextual factors, particularly income satisfaction, health support, and organizational support, as these factors appear to shape vulnerability to change fatigue.

Limitations

This study acknowledges that there are limitations. Firstly, change fatigue was assessed using self-reported questionnaires, which could introduce potential reporting bias and common method variance. Although Harman’s single-factor test suggested that severe common method bias was unlikely to be present, future studies should consider using multi-source data to further reduce this concern. Second, this study employed a multicenter cross-sectional design, limiting causal inferences regarding change fatigue and its associated factors. Given that change fatigue is a dynamic construct, future research should employ longitudinal designs to further explore its patterns over time. Third, although the present study included a multicenter sample from different regions of Hubei Province, the geographic scope remains limited. Larger-scale samples with more centers are needed in future research to strengthen the generalizability of the findings. Finally, there were some key organizational factors that were not included in the study. Indicators such as perceived workplace support, leadership style, and the frequency of change were not included in the measurement and should be incorporated in future studies.

Conclusion

This study provides person-centered evidence on change fatigue among nurse leaders using LPA. Three profiles were identified a low change fatigue-proactive adaptive profile, a moderate change fatigue-tolerant adaptive profile, and a high change fatigue-maladaptive profile. The findings indicate that nurse leaders experience organizational change in heterogeneous ways rather than as a uniform group phenomenon. Health condition, type of hospital, dimensions of job crafting, income satisfaction, and experience of changing employment were associated with latent profile membership, with income satisfaction, health condition, and cognitive crafting showing particularly salient associations. These variables may help in identifying nurse leaders who are more prone to change fatigue and point to directions that may assist them to respond more adaptively to organizational change.
