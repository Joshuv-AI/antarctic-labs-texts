Cost-effectiveness and public health impact of influenza vaccination strategies for Chinese patients with rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Kangjun Xu, Zhexin Xu, Yingqi Chen, Chenlu Fan, Chuanxi Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are at higher risk of influenza than the general population, and influenza vaccination can effectively prevent infection in this group. We assessed the cost‑effectiveness of influenza vaccination for individuals with RA in China. We used a Markov model to project outcomes for several vaccination scenarios in hypothetical Chinese RA cohorts over five years. We quantified cases and deaths averted, quality‑adjusted life‑years (QALYs) gained, and incremental cost‑effectiveness ratios (ICERs) to identify the optimal vaccination strategy. Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess model uncertainty. Compared with no vaccination, annual coverage levels of 2%, 30%, 60%, and 80% increased costs by US$230, US$165, US$96, and US$50 and yielded 0.006, 0.098, 0.198, and 0.064 additional QALYs, respectively. At 30% coverage, vaccination averted 203,199 influenza cases and yielded 0.49 million additional QALYs relative to no vaccination. Our findings remained robust across all sensitivity analyses. Influenza vaccination among RA patients in China was cost‑effective, with ICERs of US$38,312, US$1,687, US$486, and US$190 at coverage rates of 2%, 30%, 60%, and 80%, respectively. Increasing influenza vaccine coverage among individuals with RA is strongly recommended.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is one of the most prevalent chronic inflammatory diseases, characterized by progressive joint damage and extra-articular manifestations, often resulting in cartilage and bone damage and functional disability.1 The epidemiology of RA exhibits notable geographic and sex disparities. Prevalence is highest in developed countries, followed by India and countries in South America.2 In China, the age‑standardizedincidence rate for RA is 0.28%, with the total number of RA cases projected to reach approximately five million.3 Furthermore, due to hormonal factors related to menopause and reproduction, RA exhibits a marked gender disparity, with incidence rates in women being 1–2 times higher than in men.4–6

Underlying immune dysregulation and the use of disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) predispose RA patients to severe infections – particularly respiratory diseases – and elevated mortality rates relative to the general population.7–9 A U.S. retrospective cohort reported higher influenza incidence in RA than controls (409.33 vs 306.12 per 100,000 person‑years).10 A large administrative study in the United States found an influenza incidence rate ratio (IRR) of 1.2 (95% CI, 1.1–1.4) in the RA population, indicating a 20% higher risk than in the non‑RA population.11 A U.S. National Inpatient Sample analysis reported mean hospital charges of US$46,087 for influenza patients with RA versus US$42,201 for those without RA – an attributable difference of ~US$4,000.12 Seasonal influenza imposes a substantial public health burden on RA patients, increasing both clinical risk and health‑care expenditures.

Regular vaccination is the most effective means of preventing influenza. The World Health Organization (WHO), along with many national guidelines, recommends annual vaccination for people with RA.13–15 The Healthy China 2030 plan also strongly recommends that individuals at high risk of influenza, including those with RA, be vaccinated before the influenza season.16 However, influenza vaccination coverage in patients with RA remains unexpectedly low, with a substantial disparity across countries. A French study of 3,920 RA patients from 17 countries reported overall influenza vaccination coverage of 25.3% (95% CI, 23.8–26.5), ranging from 1% in Morocco and Egypt to 66.2% in Japan.17

Previous studies generally show that influenza vaccination is cost‑effective in the general population (e.g., children and older adults); however, evidence for the RA population remains limited.18–20 Here, we we used a static model to investigate the cost-effectiveness of influenza vaccination in the Chinese RA population under various vaccination strategies. These findings can provide evidence to inform public health strategies and may help reduce influenza‑related morbidity and mortality among patients with RA.

Methods

Model overview

We built a five‑year static Markov model to simulate no vaccination and coverage rates of 2%, 30%, 60%, and 80% in a hypothetical cohort of 5,000,000 Chinese RA patients, with vaccination administered annually (Appendix Figure 1). Coverage levels were chosen to reflect current practice in China and target levels typical of developing and developed countries. All individuals entered the model alive. We defined six health states to capture the severity spectrum of influenza. These states were: healthy, non-medically attended influenza, outpatient influenza, influenza-related hospitalization, disability, and death. We estimated influenza incidence, influenza‑related deaths, direct and indirect costs, and quality‑adjusted life‑years (QALYs) for each strategy. Although some studies report cross‑season protection, we assumed no carryover effect of prior vaccination on subsequent influenza risk in the model.21 We constructed this analysis from a societal perspective. Materials and methods

Model inputs were primarily obtained from published literature (Table 1). The influenza infection rate was obtained from a randomized clinical trial in Korea.22 The odds ratio of RA in males versus females was derived from a systematic review of the rheumatoid arthritis disease burden in China.23 Disability is a common sequela of RA; sex‑specific disability rates were estimated from the Global Burden of Disease 2010 study.24 In the absence of China mainland data, the mortality rate among RA patients and the proportion of outpatient influenza were obtained from a population-based cohort study in Taiwan, China.25 The outpatient influenza proportion was obtained from a single-center, retrospective cohort in Japan.26 In addition, the probability of hospitalization leading to death and the mortality ratio among the disabled were derived from the literature.12,27 We assumed a 1% probability that hospitalized influenza patients become disabled, based on expert opinion. The utility for RA was obtained from a study of the disease burden in urban China.29 Utilities for outpatient, inpatient, and disabled states were derived from a retrospective EQ‑5D‑3 L telephone survey in China.30 The utility decrement for non-medically attended influenza was obtained from a cost-effectiveness study conducted in China.20 We assumed the duration of outpatient care matched the duration of influenza, based on a modeling analysis from mainland China.31 The length of inpatient care (9.23 days; range 7.38–11.08) was taken from studies of U.S. RA patients with influenza.32 The authors further calculated QALY loss during inpatient or outpatient care by multiplying the relevant utility by the duration of care as a fraction of a year. QALY loss from death was calculated as age‑specific life‑years lost multiplied by RA utility, discounted at 3% annually. In China, the average age at RA onset is 45 years, and life expectancy thereafter is 26.3 years.33,34

We included direct (medical and non‑medical) and indirect (productivity) costs. Direct costs included vaccine purchase and administration and medical costs for influenza‑related illness. Additionally, productivity losses for patients and caregivers were the main component of indirect costs. The costs of the vaccination strategy were derived from China CDC regional immunization data across 15 provinces.36 Direct medical costs for outpatient and inpatient care were taken from a retrospective survey of influenza‑associated economic burden in China.35 Self‑treatment costs were estimated from a Chinese economic evaluation.20 Indirect costs were calculated using the human capital approach to quantify daily productivity loss. All costs from published literature and local sources were adjusted to 2022 values using annual inflation and then converted to U.S. dollars (US$1 = CNY 7.3).

Statistical analysis

Cost-effectiveness and sensitivity analysises were performed using TreeAge Pro 2022 (TreeAge Software Inc., Williamstown, MA, USA). In the base case, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of various vaccination strategies was calculated as: (Cost of intervention A – Cost of intervention B/Gain with intervention A – Gain with intervention B). As per WHO guidance, an intervention is considered cost‑effective if the ICER is less than three times GDP per capita.37 China’s 2022 GDP per capita was obtained from the National Bureau of Statistics; the willingness‑to‑pay (WTP) threshold was set at US$35,218 per QALY (three times GDP per capita).38

Multiple sensitivity analyses were employed to test rmodel robustness of and assess uncertainty. One-way sensitivity analyses (OWSA) were conducted for all parameters using the ranges in Table 1. For parameters with an unknown uncertainty range, the plausibility range was adjusted to be 20% of the base value except for utilities, which were adjusted by 10% due to unrealistic numbers caused by a 20% variation. OWSA results are presented in a tornado diagram. To examine joint uncertainty, probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) with 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations was performed; vaccine cost and effectiveness were set to hypothetical values, while other inputs were sampled from pre‑specified distributions. In these analyses, vaccine costs and effectiveness varied by ±20% around base values, using normal distributions.

Results

Health-related and economic impacts

We focused on comparing outcomes for a 30% coverage strategy versus no vaccination in the entire cohort. The five‑year health impacts, costs, and QALYs for each strategy – for the entire cohort and by sex – are shown in Table 2. Considering public health outcomes, improvements in vaccine coverage should lead to substantial reductions in influenza incidence. Over five years, influenza case incidence decreased by 4.0% (from 19.6% to 15.6% of the cohort), with similar relative declines in influenza hospitalizations and deaths. Discussion

We assessed the cost‑effectiveness of influenza vaccination versus no vaccination in Chinese patients with RA. For a cohort of 5 million RA patients, 30% coverage could avert 203,199 influenza cases and 3,445 deaths over five years. With an ICER of US$1,687 per QALY, vaccination was cost‑effective relative to the WTP threshold (US$35,218 per QALY, three times the 2022 GDP per capita). As coverage increased, the ICER versus no vaccination decreased. In sensitivity analyses, vaccine price and effectiveness were the two most influential drivers of the ICER. Further adjustment for these factors reveals that ICER values decrease as vaccine prices decrease or vaccine efficacy increases.

We are not aware of cost-effectiveness analysises of influenza vaccine in RA populations in China or Asia. However, a Bangladesh study estimated ICERs for vaccinating older adults with comorbidities, with results consistent with ours.39 A systematic review and meta-analysis of the immunogenicity of antirheumatic drugs and vaccines for rheumatoid arthritis showed that methotrexate (MTX) and tumor necrosis factor inhibitor (TNFi) treatment did not impair influenza vaccine response and there was no evidence of adverse effects on disease activity after vaccination.40 A case series study using data from clinical practice research also reported that influenza vaccination was not significantly associated with increased disease activity or adverse vaccine reactions in the majority of patients with RA, adding to the evidence to support influenza vaccination in RA.41 Despite evidence of safety and effectiveness, uptake remains low among RA patients.42

Despite the elevated infection risk in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients, vaccination uptake remains suboptimal. Several factors may contribute to low influenza vaccine coverage and vaccine hesitancy in this population, notably socio-environmental factors, individual factors, and vaccine-related factors.43,44 Socio-environmental factors such as healthcare policies and social media can influence patients’ perceptions of the pharmaceutical industry, authorities, scientists and the medical community. For example, in regions where influenza vaccination is publicly funded, vaccination rates tend to be higher in the population.17 Studies in Italy and Bulgaria show that uptake is strongly associated with physician recommendation.45,46 Additionally, individuals with RA frequently decline vaccination due to negative portrayals of the vaccine in the media or by peers.44,47 Another key factor affecting vaccination is the level of health literacy and understanding of disease prevention among RA patients, many of whom are unaware that RA and its treatment elevate infection risk.48,49 Among the vaccine-related impacts, patients’ lack of proper knowledge about vaccines, concerns about vaccine safety, side effects or adverse reactions (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis flare-ups), access to vaccines, and costs associated with vaccinations are thought to affect vaccine acceptance.49–51 China’s influenza vaccination program and accessibility studies for people with chronic diseases, including RA patients, are still in the development stage, and there is currently no unified national compulsory vaccination policy. Nevertheless, policy advocacy has progressed in recent years. The Technical Guidelines for Influenza Vaccination in China list patients with chronic diseases (including autoimmune diseases such as RA) as priority groups.52 Enhancing uptake among adults with RA will require more effective measures and strategies. Clinicians’ recommendations, particularly those of rheumatologists, play a crucial role in increasing vaccine coverage, while pharmacists’ involvement in vaccine promotion can further enhance awareness and uptake among RA patients. In certain regions of China, the influenza vaccine is offered free of charge to the elderly, yielding positive outcomes. Since 2016, Shenzhen City in Guangdong province has offered free influenza vaccines to seniors aged over 60 years anually, resulting in an increase in vaccination coverage from 1.5% to 27%.53,54 RA is highly prevalent among the elderly, and the government could consider expanding the eligible groups and regions for free vaccine provision. Additionally, patients with RA should be reminded and prioritized for vaccination during the influenza season to improve vaccine accessibility and enhance coverage.

This study has limitations. First, due to limited data accessibility, data such as the proportion of outpatient and hospitalized influenza values were extracted from foreign literature. Differences in demographic and health‑system contexts may limit generalizability to China. We used Chinese or Asian estimates where possible, and OWSA suggests that, at the current WTP threshold, these uncertainties do not materially affect the primary findings. As relevant data becomes available, further studies will be conducted to provide more accurate insights for policymakers. Second, we did not model indirect effects of vaccination. As coverage rises, infection sources decline, providing indirect protection to the unvaccinated. Thus, the real‑world health benefits of free influenza vaccination for RA patients may exceed our estimates. Third, high-dose and adjuvanted vaccines have been approved for use in the elderly in some countries, demonstrating superior vaccine outcomes.55 However, these were not modeled because they are not licensed in China. Despite these limitations, influenza vaccination was cost‑effective among RA patients in China, with robust findings across analyses. Consequently, our analysis provides evidence to inform immunization planning for the Chinese RA population.

Conclusions

We provide robust evidence that influenza vaccination is cost‑effective for Chinese RA patients. Furthermore, expanding influenza vaccination among RA patients could significantly reduce overall healthcare costs and enhance population health. We therefore recommend increasing coverage to at least 30% – and higher where feasible – through targeted measures.
