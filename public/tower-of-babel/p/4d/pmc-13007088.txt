Association of physical fitness and overweight with mental and behavioural diagnoses in a population-based cohort of 156 202 young men
Authors: Tuomas Tenkanen, Kai Pihlainen, Jani Raitanen, Tuomas Honkanen, Jani P Vaara, Heikki Kyröläinen, Tero Levola, Richard Lundell, Harri Sievänen, Tommi Vasankari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDeclining physical fitness, rising obesity and mental and behavioural diagnoses are growing public health issues in young adults. This study aimed to examine the associations between cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness and waist circumference with mental and behavioural diagnoses in a large, population-based sample of young men.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort analysis using data from the Finnish Defence Forces. Cardiorespiratory fitness (12 min running test), muscular fitness (muscular fitness index combining push-ups, sit-ups and standing long jump) and waist circumference were measured at the beginning of compulsory military service. These data were linked with physician-diagnosed mental and behavioural diagnoses (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision F-diagnoses) in individuals who interrupted their service. The sample consisted of 156 202 men (mean age 19.7 years), representing over two-thirds of the national male age cohort.ResultsIn total, 4034 men interrupted service due to mental and behavioural diagnoses. These individuals had lower levels of cardiorespiratory fitness (2250±390 m vs 2410±380 m, p<0.001) and muscular fitness index scores (10.4±2.5 vs 11.2±2.7, p<0.001) than those who completed their military service. After adjusting for age, body mass index and year of the service, lower cardiorespiratory fitness (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.88 to 0.89) and muscular fitness (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.89) remained significantly associated with service interruption. Greater waist circumference showed no general association, except in cases of hyperkinetic disorders.ConclusionsAmong young men, lower levels of cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness are universally associated with mental and behavioural diagnoses during military service. Co-occurrence between physical fitness and mental and behavioural diagnoses suggests that good physical fitness may be an important component of overall mental health in this population.Keywords: Obesity, Psychiatry, Aerobic fitness, Physical fitness

Introduction

Physical activity levels among adults and children have significantly declined over the past decades.1 2 In 2022, the global age-standardised prevalence of insufficient physical activity was 31%.1 Globally, up to 81% of adolescents aged 11–17 years are insufficiently physically active.2 Simultaneously, partly due to reduced physical activity and unhealthy eating habits, the prevalence of overweight and obesity has increased within the population, also influencing adolescents and young adults.3 4 As a consequence of this development, overall physical fitness levels have declined, and many non-communicable diseases have become more prevalent.4,6

Mental and behavioural diagnoses have also become a significant challenge globally.7,9 Their incidence has been growing, particularly in recent years.10 11 Almost one billion people worldwide are suffering from mental and behavioural diagnoses,12 and the risk factors include low socioeconomic status, drug and alcohol abuse and genetic predisposition.13,15 Recent population-based data have recognised a poorer physical fitness as a risk factor for mental and behavioural diagnoses among children and adolescents.16 With better cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular endurance and power, the risk of the onset of anxiety disorder, depression and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder may be reduced in this age group.16

The present study was conducted in a population-based setting in Finland, where all young healthy men undertake compulsory military service lasting from approximately six to twelve months if not exempted due to medical or ethical reasons. This setting provides a natural population-based experiment, where the personal operating environment changes and requires mental resilience. These changes include moving away from home, getting to know and operate with previously unknown individuals with different backgrounds, and adapting to new routines including increased physical activity and strong discipline. In this study, we investigated how cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular fitness and waist circumference are associated with mental and behavioural diagnoses in a large-scale population-based cohort of young men. To our knowledge, this information has not been previously reported in the scientific literature based on high-quality population-level data.

Methods

Sample size and study population

The present study was based on a retrospective database of the Finnish Defence Forces that contains information on 156 202 young men with a mean age of 19.7 years (SD 1.1) who started their military service between 2014 and 2021 in all garrisons around Finland (table 1). Our study data originate from a period during which the measurement and collection methods for body anthropometrics and physical fitness were standardised and consistent. Prior to compulsory service, young men undergo a health examination covering both physical and mental health. The preservice medical examination is conducted within 1–2 years prior to the commencement of military service, and medical screening is repeated at the beginning of the service. Subsequently, 20 000–25 000 young men (approximately 75% of the population of young men) undergo military service annually.17 18 Around one in four Finnish conscripts is a daily smoker.19 Additionally, nine out of ten have completed secondary education and perceive their financial situation as at least moderate.20 During the first weeks of the service, the young men underwent standardised cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness tests, and their body weight, body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference were measured. Moreover, any interruptions in service due to medical reasons were assessed by a physician of the Defence Forces. This information was recorded in the conscription register database.4 The present study used this comprehensive dataset for studying the associations of body anthropometrics and physical fitness with the incidence of mental and behavioural diagnoses (diagnostic codes beginning with F in International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10)21) that led to interruption of the service. Rare causes of service interruption were excluded (under 100 cases), as we considered the potential for statistical bias to be substantial in these subgroups.

Measurements

Waist circumference was used as the primary variable for overweight and obesity. This indicates the accumulation of adipose tissue around the waist area and is considered more precise in predicting the harmfulness of overweight than BMI or body weight.22 The waist circumference was measured during the initial medical examination for military service from the midway between the lowest rib and iliac crest to the nearest centimetre.23 A 12 min running test was used as the primary variable for cardiorespiratory fitness. The results of the running test were recorded with an accuracy of 10 m. The muscular fitness assessment comprised tests for 1 min push-ups, 1 min sit-ups and standing long jump. The results for the standing long jump were measured in centimetres, based on the longest jump out of three attempts. Young men participating in these tests were instructed to perform them with maximal effort. Moreover, they were informed of their right to voluntarily interrupt the tests at any time. In addition, the testing procedures and any possible risks of the tests were explained, and safety instructions were given to the participants before each fitness test.4 Eligible data were obtained for the 12 min running test from 149 868 men, for MFI from 150 010 men, and for waist circumference from 122 514 men. All the data were anonymised before use in research.

Statistical analysis

Means, SDs and frequencies were used to describe the data. The 12 min running test, MFI and waist circumference results between the men whose service was not interrupted and those who had their service interrupted due to mental and behavioural diagnoses were compared using an independent samples t-test. The associations of the outcome (interruption in service due to mental and behavioural diagnosis; 0=no, 1=yes) with the 12 min running test, MFI and waist circumference were examined using binary logistic regression models. We reported OR with their 95% CIs from unadjusted models, from an adjusted model for the 12 min running test and MFI controlled for BMI, age and year of conscription, and from an adjusted model for the waist circumference controlled for age and year of conscription. For better interpretability, we rescaled the 12 min running test by dividing the distance (in metres) by 100, and the waist circumference and MFI by 10. This transformation allows for interpreting changes in the odds of the event per 100 m, 10 cm and 10 units, respectively. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata V.15. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Equity, diversity and inclusion statement

Our study includes approximately two-thirds of all young Finnish men between years 2014 and 2021 who, under Finnish law, are mandated to perform compulsory military service.24 Exemptions from service may be granted on medical or ethical grounds, which implies that the study population consists primarily of individuals in good health at baseline. Women are not subject to the same conscription requirements.

Results

Out of 156 202 young men who began their military service, 4034 (2.6%) interrupted their service due to mental and behavioural diagnoses, while a total of 5405 conscripts interrupted their service due to other diagnoses than mental and behavioural diagnoses. The most prevalent diagnostic category was reaction to severe stress and adjustment disorders (ICD-10 code F43), which accounted for 19.1% of all mental and behavioural diagnoses related to interruptions. Depressive episodes (F32, 19.0%) and other anxiety disorders (F41, 18.7%) were almost equally common. Less frequent reasons for discontinuation included cannabis-related disorders (F12, 5.0%), recurrent depressive disorder (F33, 3.1%), hyperkinetic disorders (F90, 2.5%) and non-organic sleep disorders (F51, 2.4%). Table 2 shows that out of 149 868 young men who had performed the 12 min running test, 3536 (2.4%) interrupted their service due to mental and behavioural diagnoses. The distance of the running test was significantly shorter for individuals whose service was interrupted than those whose service was not (2250 m (390) vs 2410 m (380), p<0.001). Figure 1 shows that a lower 12 min running test result was associated with interruption of the service due to mental and behavioural diagnoses (OR 0.90; 95% CI 0.89 to 0.91, p<0.001). This result persisted when adjusted for age, BMI and year of the service (OR 0.89; 95% CI 0.88 to 0.90, p<0.001). The results of the 12 min running test were also significantly poorer when examining different diagnostic entities. Table 2 demonstrates that out of 150 010 young men who had performed the muscular fitness tests, 3637 (2.4%) interrupted their service due to mental and behavioural diagnoses. The result of the MFI was significantly lower in individuals whose service was interrupted than those whose were not (10.4 (2.5) vs 11.2 (2.7), p<0.001). Figure 1 shows that a poorer MFI value was associated with interruption of the service due to mental and behavioural diagnoses (OR 0.89; 95% CI 0.88 to 0.90, p<0.001). This was also true when adjusted for age, BMI and year of the service (OR 0.88; 95% CI, 0.87 to 0.89, p<0.001). The results of the MFI were also poorer when looking at different diagnostic entities. A poorer MFI result was statistically associated with service interruption in all individual diagnostic entities except for non-organic sleep disorders (OR 0.93; 95% CI 0.86 to 1.00, p=0.052).

Table 2 shows that out of 122 514 young men whose waist circumference was measured, 3214 (2.6%) interrupted their service due to mental and behavioural diagnoses. The mean waist circumference of young men who completed the service did not differ from those who interrupted due to any mental and behavioural diagnosis (84.7 cm (10.5) vs 84.7 cm (11.7), p=0.802). Figure 1 shows that there was no association between the waist circumference and interruption of the service due to mental and behavioural diagnoses (OR 1.00; 95% CI 0.96 to 1.03, p=0.80). This also persisted when adjusted for age and year of the service (OR 0.97; 95% CI 0.94 to 1.00, p=0.089). Regarding different diagnostic entities, a greater waist circumference was not significantly associated with service interruption except for hyperkinetic disorders (OR 1.22; 95% CI 1.06 to 1.41, p=0.004). In addition, a lower waist circumference was associated with interruption due to cannabis-related disorders (OR 0.84; 95% CI 0.73 to 0.96, p=0.008)

Discussion

The present population-based study of healthy young men showed that poor physical fitness is universally associated with mental and behavioural diagnoses during the compulsory military service. The most common psychiatric illnesses associated with physical fitness were stress and adjustment disorders, depression and anxiety disorder. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report that both poor cardiorespiratory fitness and poor muscular fitness were universally associated with mental and behavioural diagnoses in a large cohort of young men and therefore may compromise mental resilience under changed circumstances.

Our study found that lower levels of cardiorespiratory fitness were universally associated with mental and behavioural diagnoses, which is consistent with previous research. The association between cardiorespiratory fitness and mental and behavioural diagnoses has been investigated in previous studies.25,28 A recent cross-sectional study of adolescents with a mean age of 16 years suggested that cardiorespiratory fitness plays a more important role in protecting against self-reported mental health issues than muscular fitness, and the association is stronger among girls.25 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of adults in Western countries with poor and moderate cardiorespiratory fitness reported 47% higher risk of depression and 23% higher risk of anxiety disorders compared with individuals with good cardiorespiratory fitness.26 Beyond the direct benefits of improved physical fitness, mental health may also be positively influenced by social interactions occurring during physical training and by neurobiological mechanisms.29,31 Compulsory military service naturally involves a high degree of social interaction, which may contribute to improved mental health as the service progresses. Future studies should further investigate the role of social factors in mental health during military service.

To our knowledge, no previous population-based study has examined the association between muscular fitness and mental health with such a comprehensive scope, although the relationship between muscular fitness and mental health has been increasingly studied in recent years.32,36 Better muscular fitness has previously been associated with improved mental health, including reduced symptoms of depression34,36 and anxiety35 as well as enhanced perceived mental health.37 In the existing literature, handgrip strength is frequently used as an outcome measure of muscular fitness.32 33 35 Our study adds significantly to this topic, providing further evidence of the association of good muscular fitness with a lower risk of mental and behavioural diagnoses. In our study, muscular fitness was examined with multiple tests involving several muscle groups. Furthermore, our results are corroborated by consistent associations of objectively measured fitness levels and mental and behavioural diagnoses by physicians. Furthermore, our analyses include a wider range of mental and behavioural diagnoses beyond depression and anxiety, which are commonly studied, and present evidence on the connection between hyperkinetic disorders and poorer muscular fitness, for example.

Waist circumference was not associated with mental and behavioural diagnoses in our study, except for hyperkinetic disorders, where a larger waist circumference increased the likelihood of interrupting military service, and for non-organic sleep disorders, where a smaller waist circumference was associated with service interruption. Previous studies have found that overweight can predispose patients to the development of depression and other mental and behavioural diagnoses. However, the scientific evidence is not conclusive for all age groups.38,42 Overweight and obesity measured with BMI are suggested to be a risk factor for depression in adult population, whereas depression is found to increase the odds for developing obesity.42 Furthermore, overweight was not associated with an increased risk of depression in a population younger than 20 years.42 Psychosomatic symptoms and depression have also been demonstrated to be more common with individuals who are underweight or overweight measured with BMI.40 43 44 The association between waist circumference and mental and behavioural diagnoses has been studied less extensively, and such relationships have typically been examined using BMI, as in the studies referenced above. A study conducted of the US adult population investigating waist obesity and depressive symptoms indicated that a larger waist circumference was associated with moderate or major depressive symptoms.45 Our study did not find a connection between a larger waist circumference and depression, but a connection was found with hyperkinetic disorders. Although the evidence of the association between anthropometrics and mental and behavioural diagnoses is partly controversial, obesity should not be ignored in the prevention of mental and behavioural diagnoses.

Our study pertained to the compulsory military service environment, which in general requires mental resilience, moving away from one’s home, following orders given by someone who you do not know, meeting and interacting with large groups of earlier unknown individuals, getting used to new routines and learning new skills. It is plausible that many of these new life contents are likely to contribute to the onset of mental and behavioural diagnoses in individuals who are already predisposed. Naturally, such factors may also contribute to other environments than compulsory military service, such as moving to a new location, starting academic studies, or the beginning of new employment. Future research should continue to examine the incidence of mental and behavioural diagnoses in such settings among individuals with varying levels of cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness.

Based on the present findings suggesting poor cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness are universally associated with mental and behavioural diagnoses in young men, it may be relevant to invest in developing and maintaining both cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness of young adults to support their mental health. Although our study does not directly address causal relationships but rather associations, improving physical fitness could bring significant public health benefits also with respect to mental health. Those individuals who complete the military service may benefit from it as military service is known to increase physical fitness.17 A recent study demonstrated that good cardiorespiratory fitness in early adulthood protects against depression and anxiety later in life.46 Improvement in physical fitness during compulsory service could therefore potentially protect young men who complete their service from mental and behavioural diagnoses, as their fitness is likely to improve over the course of service.

In addition to physical fitness, increasing physical activity at the population level could contribute to improved mental health. A dose-response relationship between physical activity and depression was presented in a recent meta-analysis.47 An increase in the volume of physical activity equivalent to 2.5 hours of brisk walking weekly is associated with a 25% lower risk of depression, and a halved dose with an 18% lower risk compared with no activity at all. Approximately 11.5% of depression cases could be prevented if less active adults met the physical activity recommendations.47 Similar results regarding the association of mental health with physical activity have also been obtained in other studies.48,50 Consequently, increasing physical activity at the population level could reduce the overall risk of depression and improve mental health.

Clinical and research implementation

Based on our findings, both cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness in young men are universally associated with mental health and behavioural diagnoses at the population level. These results suggest that individuals with poor physical fitness may be at increased risk of mental health problems, and conversely, those with mental and behavioural diagnoses may be more likely to exhibit reduced physical fitness. This study provides a foundation for evaluating similar associations in other population groups, including individuals of different ages and sexes. Future research could explore how poor physical fitness contributes to the development of mental and behavioural diagnoses over longer follow-up periods, or alternatively, how mental and behavioural diagnoses influence physical fitness over time.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides population-level evidence on the universal associations between cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness and mental and behavioural diagnoses in the population of young men. This includes previously less studied diagnoses such as hyperkinetic disorders. Among the strengths of our study is the large cohort size representing approximately 75% of the population of young Finnish men. This provided a comprehensive database to assess the associations, also those involving rarer conditions. In addition, all fitness assessments and waist circumference were standardised and based on objective measurements, not self-reports. Further, the mental and behavioural diagnoses were based on ICD-10 classifications screened by medical doctors.

However, our study has some limitations. We included only male individuals since the military service in Finland is not mandatory for females, and consequently, their number in our data was small and therefore not included in the analyses. Additionally, chronically ill individuals or significantly obese individuals with other disorders do not undergo military service. Therefore, our findings cannot be generalised to young women or specified subgroups of young men. A limitation of our study is the lack of some background information, which is inherent to the nature of the dataset. Information on socioeconomic status, precise conscription site location and possible substance use—factors that may influence mental health disorders14 15 —is not available. However, information on substance use before military service would obviously exclude these men from the service. Additionally, as outlined in the Methods section, the conscript population in Finland is highly homogeneous, including with respect to these variables, which likely mitigates their potential confounding effects. The majority of the conscripts carry out their military service before studies at university and before engaging with working life. Moreover, our dataset does not include information on prior mental and behavioural diagnoses, which may have influenced the observed outcomes, and reverse causation could be possible (men with previous mental and behavioural diagnoses might be less likely to engage in regular physical activity and therefore be less fit). However, all young men undergo a medical examination prior to the onset of military service, during which individuals with active mental and behavioural diagnoses are typically excluded from service. Therefore, those who enter service are generally in good mental health at baseline. On the other hand, we do not have access to data from this health examination, including information on possible mild physical and mental symptoms which do not represent a disqualification for entering military service. The lack of this information represents a limitation of the study. Furthermore, fitness test results and waist circumference information were missing for some participants due to illness-related absence or incorrect measurement procedures, resulting in some missing data. In addition, social adjustment and functioning may impair a conscript’s ability to perform at the beginning of military service, and the lack of such information represents another limitation of our study. Nevertheless, the study includes data from the majority of young men, ensuring the reliability of the findings despite these limitations.

Conclusions

Based on a large population-based cohort of young men, this study indicates that both poor cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness are universally associated with mental and behavioural diagnoses during military service. While our findings do not establish causality, they highlight a strong co-occurrence between physical fitness and mental and behavioural diagnoses, suggesting that maintaining good physical fitness may be an important component of mental health in this population.
