Investigating the Antioxidant, Pro-Inflammatory Enzyme Inhibitory, Neuroprotective, and Antineuroinflammatory Bioactives of Osbeckia octandra Using In Vitro and In Silico Approaches: A Pilot Study
Authors: Hettiarachchige Dona Sachindra Melshandi Perera, Anton S. Perera, Jayanetti Koralalage Ramani Radhika Samarasekera, Shiroma Mangalika Handunnetti, Sisira Jagathpriya Weerasena, Qasim M. Alhadidi, Mpendulo Samkeliso Dlamini, Gavini D. Liyanaarachchi, Abilash Sundaresan, Zahoor A. Shah, Liyanaaratchige M. Viranga Tillekeratne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osbeckia octandra (Melastomataceae) is an endemic Sri Lankan edible plant traditionally used for liver health. Oxidative stress, pro-inflammatory enzyme-mediated hepatic inflammation, and neuroinflammation are interconnected conditions that exacerbate liver diseases. The study investigates the antioxidant, pro-inflammatory enzyme inhibitory, neuroprotective, and antineuroinflammatory potential of the bioactives from O. octandra. The leaves were sequentially extracted with petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, and ethanol using a cold extraction technique. Antioxidant activity was evaluated using DPPH free radical scavenging and oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) assays. Pro-inflammatory enzyme inhibition was assessed using lipoxygenase (LOX), xanthine oxidase, hyaluronidase, and nitric oxide production inhibition assays. Bioassay-guided fractionation was conducted using chromatography techniques, and the compounds were characterized using spectroscopic techniques. In silico investigations computationally validated the promising experimental findings. The ethyl acetate extract showed the highest LOX inhibition (IC50: 36.21 ± 1.09 μg/mL), the highest DPPH free radical scavenging activity (IC50: 42.29 ± 1.29 μg/mL), and the highest ORAC (470.33 ± 3.16 mg TE/g). The LOX inhibition-guided fractionation of the ethyl acetate and petroleum ether extracts led to the isolation of the compounds casuarinin (1) and lupeol (2), respectively. Casuarinin (1) showed strong, dose-dependent anti-LOX (IC50: 33.94 ± 0.59 μg/mL) and antioxidant activities. In silico investigation predicted that casuarinin (1) forms multiple hydrogen bonds at the LOX active site despite kinetically limited active site access. Casuarinin (1) exhibited dose-dependent (13.3–213.3 μM) antineuroinflammatory properties (>50%) in mouse microglia cells. It also demonstrated significant neuroprotective effects on H2O2-induced apoptotic SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells, with a notable increase in cell viability at 10.7 μM (73.74 ± 2.04%) and 106.7 μM (99.07 ± 1.43%). The bioactives from O. octandra exhibited significant antioxidant, LOX enzyme inhibitory, antineuroinflammatory, and neuroprotective properties, substantiating its therapeutic potential, positioning it as a promising candidate for phytotherapeutic applications.

Introduction

Osbeckia octandra belongs to the family Melastomataceae, which is one of the largest families of flowering plants. The genus Osbeckia comprises approximately 50 species, distributed in tropical Africa, Southeast Asia and South Asia, and Northern Australia, most of them found in Asia. Two Osbeckia species, namely O. aspera and O. octandra, are found in Sri Lanka, the latter being endemic to Sri Lanka.

Osbeckia octandra is a shrub with a branched stem that usually grows to 2 m in height. The stem bark is reddish-brown in color, with spreading hairs giving a texture of velvet. Leaves are three-nerved, hairless, 1.5–6 cm in length, and 0.5–2 cm in width with a rounded base and a pointed tip. Flowers are regular, bisexual, mostly seen in loose clusters, and pink to mauve or purple in color. In the traditional system of medicine in Sri Lanka, O. octandra is mainly used for the prevention and treatment of liver disorders.

To date, a range of in vitro and in vivo bioactivities including antioxidant, hepatoprotective, antiglycation, immunomodulatory, oral hypoglycemic, and antioral cancer activities of crude extracts of O. octandra have been reported, indicating its medicinal potential. − Yet, studies on the isolation and characterization of bioactive compounds from extracts of O. octandra and the mechanisms underlying their biological activities are limited to date. However, phytochemical investigations have revealed the presence of bioactive phytochemicals, including phenolics (gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, ellagic acid, osbeckic acid), flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol), terpenoids (norsesquiterpenoids, ursolic acid, betulinic acid), anthocyanidins (delphinidin, malvidin, cyanidin), and steroidal saponins (daucosterol), − indicating the potential use of Osbeckia species as natural sources of promising therapeutic agents.

Inflammation, together with oxidative stress, has been identified as an important causative factor in liver diseases. Oxidative stress is a condition that arises due to an imbalance between the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidants. Liver damage caused by ROS can induce the activation of pro-inflammatory genes. Expression of pro-inflammatory genes in turn prompts signal transduction in order to produce more ROS, propagating the cycle leading to liver diseases. The pro-inflammatory enzymes, including inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), lipoxygenase (LOX), hyaluronidase, and xanthine oxidase (XO), have been identified as potent mediators of inflammation. Moreover, lipoxygenases, mainly arachidonate-5-lipoxygenase (A5-LOX) and 15-lipoxygenase (15-LOX), contribute to hepatic inflammation, with A5-LOX playing a predominant pro-inflammatory role due to the significant role of leukotrienes (LTs) in the pathogenesis of liver inflammation. As enzyme inhibition is one of the prime targets in drug discovery, inhibitors of pro-inflammatory enzymes are considered potential anti-inflammatory drug candidates in the management and treatment of inflammatory diseases. Moreover, encephalopathy and brain edema are well-documented complications associated with liver disorders. Recent studies have shown that microglial cells become activated during liver failure, leading to neuroinflammation and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, including NO. Neuroinflammation is the coordinated response of the brain to tissue damage, characterized by microglial release of pro-inflammatory factors including cytokines, proteases, and free radicals. While acute neuroinflammation facilitates the removal of harmful stimuli and promotes recovery, if the response becomes prolonged or uncontrolled, then it can lead to secondary damage, causing neuronal dysfunction and death. Although acute neuroinflammation is protective, prolonged inflammation is detrimental and significantly contributes to neurodegeneration.

To date, there have been no studies reported on investigating the pro-inflammatory enzyme inhibitory, antineuroinflammatory, and neuroprotective potential of O. octandra.

In the present study, different solvent extracts/isolated bioactive compounds of leaves of O. octandra were systematically screened for their antioxidant, pro-inflammatory enzyme inhibitory, neuroprotective, and antineuroinflammatory potential. Furthermore, in silico approaches were used to gain insights into selected promising experimental observations.

Experimental Section

Collection and Preparation of Plant Material

Leaves of Osbeckia octandra (L.) DC. (Melastomataceae) were collected from Gampaha, Sri Lanka (7°8′7.7712″ N, 80°1′5.0556′′ E) in June 2013. Plants had been growing naturally under natural growth conditions (average temperature: 27 °C; humidity: 73%; natural light conditions; rainfall: 1500–2500 mm). A voucher specimen of O. octandra was identified by Dr. R. A. S. W. Ranasinghe and deposited at the National Herbarium, Peradeniya, Sri Lanka (voucher specimen no: OOL_001). Leaves were washed with running water, shade dried at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) under well-ventilated conditions (65%–75% relative humidity) for 72 h, and ground into a coarse powder using a mechanical grinder.

Extraction

Powdered leaves of O. octandra were sequentially extracted with petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, and ethanol using a cold extraction technique. Powdered leaves of O. octandra (100 g) were soaked in 500 mL of the respective solvents overnight, followed by stirring for 1 h using a mechanical stirrer at room temperature (RT) (25 ± 2 °C). The extracts were filtered through a Celite bed packed in a sintered funnel (diameter, 70 mm; porosity, 3; Buchner). The extraction was repeated three times for each solvent. The filtrates were combined and concentrated under reduced pressure at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator (Buchi rota vapor R-1141; Buchi water bath-B-480; Buchi vacuum pump v-700, Switzerland) to obtain solvent-free extracts. The extracts were stored in airtight glass containers at −20 °C until used.

Antioxidant Activity

The free radical scavenging activity of the extracts/compounds of leaves of O. octandra (0.15–500 μg/mL) was determined using DPPH free radical scavenging assay according to the method of Blois with some modifications. The oxygen radical absorption capacity of the extracts/compounds of leaves of O. octandra (1–100 μg/mL) was determined according to the method of Ou with modifications.

DPPH Free Radical Scavenging Assay

The DPPH free radical scavenging activity of the extracts/compounds was determined according to the method of Blois with some modifications. The extracts/compounds were tested within the assay concentration range of 0.15–500 μg/mL. DPPH solution (40 μg/mL, 200 μL) was incubated with 100 μL of sample at RT (25 ± 2 °C) in the dark for 10 min. 6-Hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchroman-2-carboxylic acid (trolox) was used as the reference standard.

The absorbance of the reaction mixture was recorded at λ = 517 nm. The DPPH free radical scavenging activity was calculated as follows: scavenging activity(%)=[(Abscontrol−Abssample)/Abscontrol]×100

Oxygen Radical Absorbance Capacity Assay

The oxygen radical absorbance capacity assay was conducted according to the method of Ou et al. with modifications. The assay was carried out at 37 °C and pH 7.4. Fluorescein (48 μM) and AAPH (4 μg/mL) solutions were prepared in phosphate buffer (75 mM, pH 7.4) prior to use. The extracts/compounds were tested in the assay concentration range of 1–100 μg/mL. Samples (10 μL), phosphate buffer (4 μL), and fluorescein solution (100 μL) were preincubated at 37 °C for 10 min, and AAPH solution (5 μL) was added. The decay of fluorescein was recorded at 1 min intervals for 35 min at the excitation and emission wavelengths of λ = 494 and λ = 535 nm, respectively. Trolox was used as a reference standard. The net area under the curve (AUC) of the plant extracts was calculated using the following equation. Results were expressed in terms of mg of trolox equivalents (mg TE)/g of extract using the calibration curve of trolox (y = 0.035x + 0.08, R 2 = 0.999). net AUC of samples=AUC of sample−AUC of control

Pro-Inflammatory Enzyme Inhibitory Activity

Lipoxygenase Enzyme Inhibitory Assay

The LOX inhibitory activity of extracts/compounds was evaluated according to the spectrometric method described by Tappel with modifications. The samples were tested in the assay concentration range of 10–1000 μg/mL. The samples (10 μL) were incubated with 110 μL of sodium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 8.0) and 55 μL of LOX (soybean, L7395, Sigma) enzyme solution at 25 °C for 10 min. The reaction was then initiated with the addition of a linoleic acid (0.08 mM, 25 μL) solution. The change of absorbance at λ = 234 nm was monitored for 10 min at 25 °C. The percentage inhibition of LOX was determined using the following equation. Baicalein was used as the reference standard. enzyme inhibition(%)=[(Vmax,control−Vmax,sample)/Vmax,control]×100 where V max = maximum reaction velocity

Hyaluronidase Enzyme Inhibitory Assay

The hyaluronidase inhibitory activity of extracts/compounds was evaluated according to the spectrometric method described by Sahasrabudhe et al., with modifications. The samples (100, 200, and 500 μg/mL) were incubated with hyaluronidase (bovine testes; H3506, type-1-S, Sigma, 42 000 U/mL, 10 μL) for 10 min at 37 °C. After 10 min, calcium chloride (12.5 mM, 20 μL) was added to the reaction mixture and reincubated for 10 min at 37 °C. The substrate solution of sodium hyaluronate (12 mg/mL, 50 μL) was added to the reaction mixture and incubated at 37 °C for 40 min. After the incubation, sodium hydroxide (0.9 M, 10 μL) and sodium borate (0.2 M, 20 μL) were added, and the mixture was heated at 100 °C for 3 min. After the mixture was cooled to RT, para-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde (PDMAB) (67 mM, 50 μL) solution was added to the reaction mixture and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. When color developed, absorbance was measured at λ = 585 nm. Percent enzyme inhibition was calculated as follows. Tannic acid was used as the reference standard. enzyme inhibition(%)=[(Abscontrol−Abssample)/Abscontrol]×100

Xanthine Oxidase Enzyme Inhibitory Assay

The xanthine oxidase inhibitory activity of extracts/compounds was evaluated according to the method described by Lee with modifications. The samples were tested in the assay concentration range of 25–250 μg/mL. The samples (10 μL), sodium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4, 150 μL), and 10 μL of XO enzyme (bovine milk; 4376x; Sigma) were incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. The reaction was then initiated with the addition of a xanthine solution (20 μL, 0.1 mM). The change of absorbance at λ = 295 nm was monitored at 25 °C for 15 min. The percentage inhibition of XO enzyme activity was determined by using the following equation. Allopurinol was used as the reference standard. enzyme inhibition(%)=[(Vmax,control−Vmax,sample)/Vmax,control]×100 where V max = maximum reaction velocity.

Nitric Oxide Production Inhibitory Activity and Viability of LPS-Activated RAW 264.7 Macrophages

Murine macrophage (RAW 264.7) cell lines were purchased from ATCC, VA, USA. RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with penicillin G sodium, streptomycin sulfate, amphotericin B, and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Cells were split twice a week.

Monolayer cells were seeded in 96-well microtiter plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and incubated in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 24 h. After 24 h, cells were treated with plant extracts (250 μg/mL) and incubated for 30 min, followed by the addition of bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 μg/mL) and incubation in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 24 h.

Nitric oxide production of macrophages was determined by measuring the nitrite concentration in the cell culture supernatant using Griess reagent. After 24 h of incubation with LPS, cell culture supernatant from each well was carefully aspirated and mixed with Griess reagent (100 μL) in a 96-well microtiter plate. A standard curve of serially diluted sodium nitrite was used to determine the nitrite concentration. Nitric oxide production inhibition was calculated in comparison to the control as follows. L-NMMA was used as a reference standard. Cell viability was determined using standard MTT assay. NO production(%)=[(Abscontrol−Abssample)/Abscontrol]×100

Nitric Oxide Production Inhibitory Assay and Viability of LPS-Activated Immortalized Mouse Microglia Cells (SIM-A9 Cells)

The immortalized mouse microglia cells (SIM-A9) were cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 5% horse serum, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin under humidified conditions with 5% CO2, 95% air atmosphere, and at 37 °C. Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After 24 h, cells were treated with compounds (5 μL) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL; 2 μL) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After 24 h, the cell culture supernatant (100 μL) was aspirated and reacted with Griess reagent (100 μL) in a 96-well plate and incubated at room temperature for 10 min in the dark. Absorbance was measured at λ = 540 nm. Nitric oxide production inhibition was calculated in comparison to the control. NO production(%)=[(Abscontrol−Abssample)/Abscontrol]×100

Neuroprotective Activity

The neuroprotective activity of compounds was studied according to the method described by Subedi with some modifications. SH-SY5Y cells were cultured in DMEM (Cellgro, Manassas, VA, USA) containing 10% cosmic calf serum (Hyclone) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Lonza). The cells were seeded into 24-well plates. After incubating at 37 °C in 10% CO2, 95% air, and 100% relative humidity for 24 h, the cells were treated with compound 1 and H2O2, followed by reincubation for 24 h at similar conditions. After 24 h, MTT solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The cell culture medium was carefully and completely removed by gentle aspiration, and cells were lysed by adding DMSO, followed by shaking on a microplate shaker for 10 min. Absorbance was read at λ = 560 nm, and the neuroprotective effect (%) was determined in comparison to the control (cells treated only with H2O2) in terms of cell viability.

Cytotoxicity of Compounds on Normal Cells

The cytotoxicity assay was conducted according to the method described by Oliver et al. The cytotoxicity of the compounds was tested against RPE cells and human gingival fibroblast cells. The cell lines were initially cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% cosmic calf serum (Hyclone) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were seeded (5 × 104 cells/mL, 500 μL) in 24-well plates, incubated at 37 °C in 10% CO2, 95% air, and 100% relative humidity for 24 h, and observed for proper cell attachment and confluency. The compounds (5 μL) were added to the cultured cells and reincubated at 37 °C in 10% CO2, 95% air, and 100% relative humidity for 48 h. After 48 h of incubation, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were incubated with saturated methylene blue solution in ethanol (1:1 v/v, 250 μL) for 30 min at RT. After 30 min, the excess of methylene blue was removed, and wells with attached, stained cells were carefully rinsed three times with distilled water and drained/dried on an absorbent paper. Hydrochloric acid solution (0.1 M, 100 μL) was added to each well to extract methylene blue taken by the viable cells. The extracted methylene blue in HCl was transferred into a 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at λ = 530 nm. Cell viability (%) was calculated in comparison to the control as follows: cell viability(%)=[(Abscontrol−Abssample)/Abscontrol]×100

Isolation of Bioactive Compounds

Column chromatography was performed using silica gel 60 (particle size: 0.035–0.070 mm), supplied by Fluka, Switzerland (220–440 mesh); silica gel, 230–400 mesh (Sorbent Technologies, GA, USA), and Sephadex LH-20-100 (Sigma Chemical Company, USA). For analytical thin layer chromatography (TLC), precoated silica gel plates (250 μm, 250 μM with UV254, Sorbent Technologies) were used. All procedures were carried out at room temperature. NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III NMR spectrometer with a CryoProbe (Germany). All NMR data were measured and reported in parts per million, and TMS (tetramethylsilane) was used as an internal standard. The proton (1H) and carbon (13C) frequencies are 600.2 and 150.9 MHz, respectively. Chemical shifts (δ) were presented in parts per million and referenced to the solvent peaks (δH at 3.31 and 4.88 or δC middle band at 49.0 for CH3OD and δH 7.26 and δC 77.23 for CDCl3). IR spectra were recorded on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5 FT-IR spectrophotometer (Madison, WI, USA). Bruker TopSpin 3.2 software was used for offline FID processing.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s multiple range test were used for the mean comparison of bioactivities. p < 0.05 was considered significant. Results were expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Theoretical Calculations

Theoretical calculations and computational simulations were performed to further elucidate the theoretical basis of the experimental observations, such as the antioxidant activities and the inhibition of LOX by casuarinin (1) and lupeol (2), and their potency was compared to baicalein. We utilized the same docking protocol that we reported in a previous work for A5-LOX using AutoDockFR (AutoDock for Flexible Receptors) with the protein crystal structure PDB ID: 6N2W − and extended the same approach to 15-LOX using the crystal structure PDB ID: 4NRE. This procedure was previously validated to produce results in good agreement with the experiments for A5-LOX, and it was therefore applied to both enzymes in this study. The structure preparation for proteins and ligands, docking site identification using AutoSite 1.1, and defining flexible residues using AGFR 1.0 were done according to the above-mentioned protocol.

Beyond molecular docking calculations, we extended our theoretical investigation focusing only on A5-LOX since the ultimate goal of this part of the study is to assess the inhibitory potential of casuarinin (1) and lupeol (2) on A5-LOX, which has a major pro-inflammatory role in hepatic inflammation. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the protein and protein–ligand complexes were performed using AMBER. CHARMM-GUI , was used as an aid to generate the input files for the MD simulations. Force field parameters for standard and nonstandard residues were based on CHARMM36m with CGenFF-compatible partial charges for the ligands obtained using the ParamChem server. The simulation box length for all simulations was maintained at an edge distance of 10.0 Å. TIP3P water with KCl at 0.15 M salinity was used to solvate the simulation boxes.

All systems were initially subjected to energy minimization with 5000 steps of steepest descent and conjugate gradient methods consecutively, followed by 50 ns simulation under NVT conditions with a 1 fs time step using the SHAKE algorithm , at 303.15 K maintained using the Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 1.0 ps–1. For data extraction, 50 ns long simulations under NPT conditions were conducted at 1 bar of pressure. The pressure control with 1 ps relaxation intervals was achieved using the Monte Carlo pressure coupling algorithm with isotropic position scaling. Three-dimensional periodic boundary conditions (3D-PBC) were applied for all systems with a cutoff value of 12.0 Å for nonbonded interactions.

Estimated binding free energies were computed using the molecular mechanics–generalized Born surface area (MMGBSA) method using the MD trajectories from the aforementioned simulations. GBSA calculations were performed with 2000 frames evenly distributed over 40 ns using the MMPBSA.py script as implemented in AMBER with “Onufriev–Bashford–Case” generalized Born model parameters.

The noncovalent interactions (NCIs) between the ligands and amino acid residues around them were computed using NCIPLOT 4.0 with “fine-level” accuracy parameters.

Binding pocket analysis was performed using the Caver Web 1.0 server and CAVER 3.0 , for docking along the ligand passage. Analysis and visualization were performed using the software packages Cpptraj, PyMol, VMD, UCSF Chimera, and Schrödinger Maestro academic edition and the Python libraries MDAnalysis , and Pytraj. ,

Physicochemical and pharmacokinetic characteristics of lupeol and casuarinin were predicted using the SwissADME web tool. The pkCSM platform was used to evaluate toxicological profiles.

Results and Discussion

Bioactivity-Guided Fractionation and Isolation of Bioactive Compounds from Osbeckia octandra

Bioactivity Screening

To identify the most potent extract and the most relevant enzyme target for bioactivity-guided fractionation, the sequential solvent extracts of leaves of O. octandra were screened for antioxidant and pro-inflammatory enzyme inhibitory activities using a range of bioassays. The ethyl acetate extract showed the highest antioxidant potential as evidenced by its significantly higher DPPH free radical scavenging and oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC), followed by the ethanol and petroleum ether extracts (Table ).

To access the pro-inflammatory enzyme inhibitory potential, the extracts were screened against four key pro-inflammatory enzymes: LOX, hyaluronidase, XO, and iNO synthase. Overall, all extracts demonstrated a preferential inhibition on the LOX enzyme compared to the other enzymes, indicating the dominant LOX inhibitory potential of the bioactive constituents in the leaves of O. octandra. Soybean lipoxygenase (plant derived 15-LOX) was used as a model enzyme to study the inflammatory role of A5-LOX enzyme, as there is a common mechanism of reaction between the different isoforms of LOX. Their oxygenation, catalyzed by LOX, shows an involvement of a similar pair of double bonds on the fatty acid substrate and its coupling with molecular oxygen. Moreover, due to the limited availability of sufficiently purified human enzymes (human-5-LOX), research studies investigating LOX inhibition as a potential anti-inflammatory strategy have been performed using soybean LOX, as in the present study.

Among the three extracts, the ethyl acetate extract showed the most pronounced dose-dependent LOX inhibition, followed by the ethanol and petroleum ether extracts. The ethyl acetate extract exhibited low inhibitions on hyaluronidase, xanthine oxidase, and iNO synthase and was the only extract that inhibited iNO synthase activity. In contrast, the petroleum ether and ethanol extracts showed low or no inhibitions on hyaluronidase, xanthine oxidase, and iNO synthase enzymes indicating their lower potency as inhibitors of the tested enzymes (Table ). This is the first report investigating the potential of O. octandra extracts in inhibiting pro-inflammatory enzymes, as well as the first study to highlight the promising LOX inhibitory activity of the ethyl acetate extract of O. octandra.

Based on these results, the ethyl acetate and petroleum ether extracts, which showed relatively higher anti-LOX activities, were selected for further purification and subjected to LOX inhibition-guided fractionation to isolate and identify compounds with promising LOX inhibitory activity.

Bioactivity-Guided Fractionation of Ethyl Acetate Extract

Since normal-phase TLC analysis of the ethyl acetate extract revealed the presence of predominantly polar compounds with poor separation, the extract was fractionated using size exclusion chromatography (SEC) on Sephadex LH-20, employing a mixture of methanol and dichloromethane (1:1), followed by 100% methanol as the mobile phase. This process resulted in 10 distinct fractions (DF1–DF10) with yields of 21, 1134, 406, 132, 49, 734, 8, 73, 42.2, and 7.9 mg, respectively. Notably, fractions DF1 and DF2 predominantly contained green and yellow pigments, suggesting the removal of chlorophylls, which may facilitate better separation and purification of the bioactive compounds in subsequent fractions.

The fraction DF3 (406 mg) was further fractionated by C18 reversed-phase chromatography, initially eluted with water (100%), followed by a gradient solvent system of methanol/water (1:19, 1:9, 1:4, 3:7, 1:1, 7:3, and 4:1 v/v), and finally with 100% methanol to obtain 26 fractions, which were combined into 6 fractions (EF1–EF6) based on proton NMR profiles. The fraction EF3, eluted with methanol/water (3:7 v/v), yielded compound 1 (15.2 mg, 0.018%) as light brown needles.

Bioactivity-Guided Fractionation of Petroleum Ether Extract

LOX inhibitory activity-guided fractionation of the petroleum ether extract using silica gel chromatography led to the isolation of a major compound with an Rf value of 0.58 (ethyl acetate/petroleum ether, 2:8 v/v). Spectroscopic analysis and comparison with reported data identified the compound as the triterpene lupeol (2).

Bioactivity Studies of Isolated Compounds

Antioxidant Activity of Compounds Isolated from Osbeckia octandra

The antioxidant activity of isolated compounds was evaluated by using DPPH free radical scavenging and ORAC assays. Casuarinin (1) showed the highest DPPH free radical scavenging activity (IC50: 5.37 ± 0.06 μg/mL), which was found to be significant and dose-dependent (0.15–10 μg/mL), comparable with that of the reference standard trolox (IC50: 5.54 ± 0.03 μg/mL) and eight times higher than that of the ethyl acetate crude extract from which it was isolated. The high radical scavenging activity of casuarinin (1) could be attributed to the presence of HHDP and galloyl groups in casuarinin (1). The antioxidant activity of casuarinin was found to be consistent with previous reports, further validating its potent free radical scavenging and oxygen radical absorbance capacity and supporting its potential as a natural antioxidant agent. The ORAC assay, which is based on a hydrogen atom transfer mechanism, is widely considered as one of the most reliable methods for assessing the chain-breaking antioxidant activity of a compound. In the ORAC assay, casuarinin (1) exhibited a higher ORAC value (1098.48 mg TE/g) compared to lupeol (2) (795.45 mg TE/g), indicating its promising antioxidant potential as an oxygen radical absorber. One possible explanation for the high ORAC value of casuarinin (1) is the presence of a galloyl moiety with three adjacent phenolic hydroxyl groups, which is known to stabilize oxygen radicals through hydrogen bonds to adjacent hydroxyl groups. However, further studies are needed to explore other potential mechanism(s). Furthermore, casuarinin (1) demonstrated a 2-fold higher ORAC value than the crude ethyl acetate extract (470.33 ± 3.16 mg TE/g), while lupeol showed a 10-fold higher ORAC value than the crude petroleum ether extract (75.76 ± 5.47 mg TE/g), suggesting that fractionation and purification of the crude extracts enhanced their antioxidant capacity. Therefore, casuarinin could be considered a potential antioxidant marker compound for leaves of O. octandra. Furthermore, the identification of casuarinin as a marker compound would facilitate the establishment of quality control parameters for O. octandra, particularly for validating antioxidant claims in pharmaceutical or nutraceutical applications.

Lupeol (2) also showed a notable DPPH free radical scavenging activity with an IC50 value of 18.56 ± 0.22 μg/mL, which is approximately 25 times higher than that of the petroleum ether crude extract from which it was isolated, though it remains significantly lower than that of the reference standard, as previously reported.

Pro-Inflammatory Enzyme Inhibitory Activity of Isolated Compounds from Osbeckia octandra

The pro-inflammatory enzyme inhibitory activity of the isolated compounds was evaluated by assessing their ability to inhibit LOX, XO, hyaluronidase, and NO production.

In the LOX enzyme inhibitory assay, the crude ethyl acetate extract showed a significantly higher LOX enzyme inhibitory activity compared to that of the crude petroleum ether extract (Table ). Similarly, casuarinin (1), which was isolated from the crude ethyl acetate extract, demonstrated a significantly higher and dose-dependent inhibitory activity (IC50: 33.94 ± 0.59 μg/mL) compared to that of lupeol (2) (IC50: 46.76 ± 0.05 μg/mL), isolated from the crude petroleum ether extract (Table ). However, upon fractionation, the LOX inhibitory activity of the crude ethyl acetate extract did not change significantly, while the activity of the crude petroleum ether extract was enhanced 2-fold. The LOX enzyme inhibitory activity of casuarinin (1) may be attributed to its marked free radical scavenging activity, as confirmed in the DPPH free radical scavenging assay. Free radical scavengers are known to inhibit LOX enzyme by scavenging free radical intermediates generated at the active site of the LOX enzyme during its catalytic function.

Antineuroinflammatory Potential of Isolated Compounds from Osbeckia octandra

Encephalopathy and brain edema are well-documented complications associated with liver disorders. Recent studies have shown that microglial cells become activated during liver failure, leading to neuroinflammation and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, including NO. Therefore, to investigate the antineuroinflammatory potential of the compounds, NO inhibition in microglia cells was measured.

Microglial cells were incubated with a range of concentrations of LPS (10–100 ng/mL) to determine the optimal assay concentration, which was found to be 100 ng/mL. Three controls were used in the assay: Control 1 (no LPS or DMSO) was used to assess the baseline physiological conditions of cells under experimental conditions; Control 2 (LPS only) was used to determine the maximum amount of NO released; and Control 3 (LPS and DMSO) was used to evaluate the effect of DMSO on the cells.

The LPS stimulation resulted in significant NO release into the culture supernatant (p < 0.05). Nitric oxide was quantified by measuring the nitrite content, a stable nonvolatile byproduct of NO catabolism, using a simple colorimetric assay based on the Griess reaction. In this assay, nitrites in the cell culture supernatant react with Griess reagent under acidic conditions, producing an intense pink color. The reduction in the pink color is indicative of and directly proportional to the inhibition of NO production.

The compounds were initially screened at two concentrations, 10.7 and 106.7 μM, to assess their NO inhibitory activity. Casuarinin (1) exhibited the highest activity at 10.7 μM (52.01 ± 0.52%), while lupeol (2) showed no activity at 10.7 μM but demonstrated weaker activity (1.81 ± 0.22%) at 106.7 μM (Figure A). Casuarinin (1) was further evaluated for its dose-dependent response in the concentration range of 6.67–213.3 μM (Figure B). The release of NO was significantly reduced in a dose-dependent manner from 13.3 to 213.3 μM, compared to Control 2 (Figure B). These findings align with previous studies that report ellagitannins exerting anti-inflammatory activity via inhibition of NO production. The high cell viability (>75%) observed in the MTT cytotoxicity assay confirms the nontoxic nature of casuarinin (1) at the tested concentrations (Figure B), excluding the potential confounding effect of cytotoxicity as a contributor to the observed NO inhibition.

The nitric oxide (NO) signaling pathways involve several key targets that could be inhibited by casuarinin. One major target is inducible NOS (iNOS), which is responsible for the NO production during inflammatory responses. Casuarinin could inhibit iNOS expression or activity, thereby reducing excessive NO production in microglia during neuroinflammation. Additionally, soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC), the primary receptor for NO, could also be targeted by casuarinin. NO binding to sGC activates the enzyme, leading to the production of cyclic GMP (cGMP), which is a critical signaling molecule. Inhibition of sGC by casuarinin would block cGMP synthesis, disrupting downstream signaling pathways. Furthermore, oxidative stress plays a key role in modulating NO signaling by generating peroxynitrite, which can impair NOS function, leading to the production of superoxide instead of NO. Casuarinin may also modulate oxidative stress due to its high antioxidant potential, preventing the dysregulation of NOS and subsequent disruption of cGMP production. Furthermore, the role of NO in neuroinflammation has been studied in animal models of excitotoxicity where glutamate accumulation and microglial activation occur. Nitric oxide released from the active microglia has been found to block glutamate reuptake at presynaptic sites, leading to NMDA receptor activation, which can promote neuronal death. , By targeting these components of NO signaling, casuarinin has the potential to reduce neuroinflammation and the associated neuronal damage. However, further studies are needed to completely understand the underlying mechanisms of NO inhibition.

Moreover, neuroinflammation-induced cell death is known to be driven largely by the long-term effects of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), leading to apoptotic cell death through oxidative or nitrosative damage to neurons. The brain is particularly vulnerable to oxidative stress due to an imbalance between its antioxidant defenses and its ability to produce oxidative species due to the low levels of glutathione (GSH) and moderate activity of antioxidant enzymes, including catalase, superoxide dismutases (SODs), and GSH peroxidase.

The potent antioxidant activity of casuarinin, combined with its ability to inhibit NO production, could work synergistically to alleviate neuroinflammation.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have reported on the NO inhibitory potential of casuarinin (1) in microglial cells. Therefore, this is the first report of the antineuroinflammatory potential of casuarinin (1) in microglial cells via NO production inhibition.

Neuroprotective Activity of Isolated Compounds from Osbeckia octandra

The compounds with high antioxidant potential are known to impart neuroprotective effects via the attenuation of oxidative stress caused by reactive oxygen and nitrogen species. Therefore, casuarinin (1), which showed promising antioxidant properties, was further studied for neuroprotective potential against H2O2-induced apoptosis in human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells in vitro. The cytotoxicity induced by H2O2 is a common method for the investigation of in vitro neuroprotective activity of antioxidants. − Hydrogen peroxide serves as an oxidative inducer by modulating the function of mitochondria, which play a pivotal role in the generation of ROS and apoptotic-programmed cell death. ,

The optimal H2O2 concentration was initially determined to identify the effective concentration of H2O2 that can cause significant cell cytotoxicity. The lowest H2O2 concentration that showed ≥50% cytotoxicity in the MTT assay was determined as 100 μM and selected as the optimal assay concentration (Figure X).

The cytotoxicity of casuarinin (1), which showed high antioxidant activity, was initially assessed at three concentrations: 1.07, 10.7, and 106.7 μM. It showed high cell viability, confirming its nontoxic nature. Casuarinin (1) showed significant increases in cell viability at 10.7 μM (73.74 ± 2.04%) and 106.7 μM (99.07 ± 1.43%) in comparison to the H2O2 treated cell control (53.24 ± 0.48%) (Figure Y), indicating its neuroprotective effects.

There are several mechanisms suggested for the neuroprotection imparted by phenolic compounds. The free radical scavenging mechanism is one such prominent mechanism of neuroprotection. Being a polyphenolic compound, casuarinin (1) also showed pronounced free radical scavenging activity in the DPPH free radical scavenging assay (section ), suggesting the observed neuroprotective action of casuarinin (1) may be partly attributed to its strong free radical scavenging activity. However, further studies, including ROS assays at the cellular level, are required to validate this mechanism. In addition to the scavenging of ROS, the attenuation of ROS production, the reduction of superoxide dismutase activity, a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential, and a decrease in ATP production may also be proposed as possible mechanisms for the neuroprotective action of casuarinin (1).

Cytotoxicity of Compounds on Normal Cells and Their Metabolic Stability

Casuarinin (compound 1) and lupeol (compound 2) exhibited no significant cytotoxicity on RPE and human gingival fibroblast cells (IC50: >100 μg/mL). Similarly, previous studies have reported that casuarinin showed no significant cytotoxic effect on normal human gingival fibroblast cells with IC50 values ranging from 177.9 to 243.5 μg/mL, as well as normal lymphocytes and normal Chang liver cells (IC50: >37.47 μg/mL).

The inhibition and induction potential of lupeol on various cytochrome P450 (CYP) isoforms, including CYP1A2, CYP2C11, CYP2D6, and CYP3A2, using rat liver microsomes, are reported to date. The results from the inhibition assays have demonstrated that lupeol has an inhibitory potential for CYP1A2, with an IC50 value of 59.42 μM. No significant inhibition was observed for CYP2C11, CYP2D6, and CYP3A2 (IC50 > 100 μM). Additionally, no significant induction or inhibition of CYP450 isoforms was observed, suggesting that lupeol does not substantially modify the activity of these enzymes.

The inhibition and induction potentials of casuarinin on various cytochrome P450 (CYP) isoforms have not been comprehensively studied to date. However, due to its large molecular weight and complex structure, its pharmacokinetics (PK) is quite different from those of typical small-molecule drugs. Casuarinin has poor absorption in the upper gastrointestinal tract due to its large size and high polarity.

Theoretical Calculations and Computational Simulations

We initially investigated two different binding sites of both A5-LOX and 15-LOX enzymes to gain a general understanding of possible inhibition modes, although the primary focus was directed toward the active sites. Results from docking (Table ) suggest that casuarinin (1) and lupeol (2) exhibit favorable binding at both enzymes but with a stronger preference for the active sites. When compared to the positive control baicalein, lupeol (2) consistently demonstrated markedly higher binding affinities with a comparable number of feasible binding poses, while casuarinin (1) displayed weaker binding affinities and fewer feasible modes in A5-LOX but a considerably higher number of poses in 15-LOX, indicating that structural flexibility at the 15-LOX site may accommodate this larger molecule more effectively. Importantly, the overall docking trends across both enzymes were highly similar, with lupeol (2) ranking as the strongest binder, baicalein showing moderate activity, and casuarinin (1) remaining the weakest, in turn validating our approach of only extending our theoretical investigation to A5-LOX.

At the alternate sites, the patterns were less consistent: casuarinin (1) showed unfavorable binding in 15-LOX despite having many poses, suggesting steric accommodation without productive binding, whereas lupeol (2) retained favorable binding in both enzymes but with fewer poses. Baicalein, the natural positive control, exhibited moderate binding in both enzymes with a reasonable number of poses, supporting its experimentally reported activity. In contrast, phthalic acid, the synthetic control, once again stood out at the alternate sites with a very high number of feasible binding poses, indicating a higher overall probability of binding through allosteric inhibition when probability distributions are considered. While docking results highlight the strong active site binding of lupeol (2) across both enzymes, they also underscore a recurring limitation of docking: the scoring functions largely emphasize steric fit and minimization of unfavorable contacts and thus may not fully capture experimentally observed inhibition trends.

Although active site residue flexibility and multiple GA runs resulting in different binding modes can roughly account for ligand–receptor interface flexibility, MD simulations can capture the dynamic flexibility of both the receptor and the ligand and the effect of explicit solvent–ion interactions more realistically. The authors further note that results from MD simulations of A5-LOX have been used to explain experimental trends observed experimentally, per the previously discussed rationale. MD simulations of A5-LOX with baicalein, casuarinin (1), and lupeol (2) at its active site were utilized to acquire an insight into the stability of the protein–ligand complexes, the free energy of binding, and intermolecular interactions. In terms of the stability of ligands through the trajectory, baicalein appears to be the most stable. It can be attributed to the smaller number of rotatable bonds leading to low conformational degrees of freedom. Both casuarinin (1) and lupeol (2) may switch between conformers, as indicated by the fluctuation behavior of ligand RMSDs in Figure a. However, when the protein in the bound state is considered, the baicalein–A5-LOX complex may undergo conformational changes in its secondary structure when compared to the protein–ligand complexes of casuarinin and lupeol. In the context of the flexibility of individual residues, all three complexes indicate similarities with regions of flexible and rigid areas, as represented by the RMSF profiles in Figure b and superimposed protein structures from all three complexes (Figure d). Lupeol (2), which has only one hydroxyl group, shows the least preference toward forming hydrogen bonds. The possibility of forming a large number of H-bonds can be attributed to the higher inhibition of LOX by casuarinin (1) compared to lupeol (2), which is not reflected by docking scores.

With all of the analyses discussed above, the ability of casuarinin (1) to better inhibit LOX than lupeol (2) is reasonably justified if the argument is exclusively restricted to the ability to bind at the active site pocket. Nevertheless, this alone does not rationalize why baicalein shows the best inhibition when compared to the test compounds. The answer to this question appears to be two-fold. Critical interactions formed between the ligand and the protein that are not captured through the scoring function used in docking and by the free energy estimation model. Since both docking and classical MD are based on Newtonian mechanics (do not involve explicit electrons as in quantum mechanics), they are unable to apprehend interactions due to delocalized π electrons, via dispersion interaction (π–π stacking) or electronic coupling. 2D ligand interaction diagrams indicate that baicalein can form multiple π–π stacking interactions between its phenyl aromatic rings and imidazole aromatic rings from histidine residues (mainly HIS 367) at the active site, whereas both casuarinin (1) and lupeol (2) are not able to do so due the unavailability of aromatic rings in their structures. Baicalein has a better chance of forming coordination or other metal interactions with the Fe2+ ion (Figure b, baicalein), which may also be implied by the stronger electrostatic contribution to the free energy of binding. When hydrogen bonding is considered, baicalein may also possess effective interactions despite the smaller number of hydroxyl groups when compared to casuarinin (1), as it could have unfavorable interaction overlaps due to its large size and many functional groups that may hinder its hydrogen bonding. Moreover, the NCI diagrams show that the active site of LOX with baicalein possesses sufficiently large areas that could facilitate electronic coupling. However, more electronic structure calculations at the active site are required to confirm this. Although many studies often directly correlate binding affinity to the inhibition of proteins, the binding at the active site is subjected to the successful (statistically favorable) entry into the active site. Both docking and trivial MD simulations fail to capture this aspect of ligand binding. Enhanced sampling methods such as replica exchange MD, steered MD, and umbrella sampling are able to address this but are computationally expensive to consider all possible entry mechanisms given the large size of the active site pocket. − Hence, herein we utilized a simpler but effective method to acquire an insight into the transportation of these three ligands from the outside environment into the protein active site. CaverDock, which was used here, is based on AutoDock Vina, similar to ADFR, which makes the docking scores comparable within the entire analysis. Figure presents the binding energies of the three molecules along the α tunnel, which is among the three most probable passages for molecules to enter the active site. This indicates that baicalein (green) has no activation energy barrier, possibly due to its relatively planar shape and smaller solvent-accessible surface area (SASA). Even though other passages may grant easier access to the active site, the probability distribution of molecules entering into the active site would likely favor baicalein when kinetics is considered; hence, it will have a better chance of inhibiting A5-LOX, despite the comparable binding affinity of lupeol (2) once it is situated within the pocket. Since casuarinin (1) is the bulkiest of the three, it has the least probability of entry into the active site.

Furthermore, the analysis of physicochemical and pharmacokinetic characteristics and toxicological profiles corroborates our experimental observations. Lupeol (2) shows drug-like properties, with a molecular weight of 426.72 g/mol, one hydrogen bond donor, one acceptor, and a low topological polar surface area of 20.23 Å2, and it passes Lipinski’s filters. It is highly saturated, contains one rotatable bond, and has a molar refractive index of 135.14 (Table S1). It is highly lipophilic with a consensus log P of 7.27, which exceeds the optimal range, and it is predicted to be poorly water-soluble across multiple models. Lupeol (2) is not a substrate for P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and is predicted to have no inhibitory effect on the main CYP450 enzymes (Table ), as confirmed by in vitro studies using rat liver microsomes. It is metabolized primarily via CYP3A4, posing a low risk for metabolic drug–drug interactions. Its bioavailability score is 0.55, suggesting moderate oral bioavailability. Compared to lupeol (2), casuarinin (1) has a complex structure with a high molecular weight of 936.65 g/mol, 16 hydrogen bond donors, 26 acceptors, and a low topological polar surface area of 455.18 Å2. It has four rotatable bonds and a high molar refractive index of 212.5 (Table S1). It is hydrophilic, with a log P value of −0.53. Casuarinin (1) is a substrate of a P-gp substrate, adding another barrier to its bioavailability. It is predicted to have no inhibitory effect on the main CYP450 enzymes (Table S2); this indicates minimal hepatic metabolism and low interaction. It has a low bioavailability score of 0.17. Although both compounds have probable hERG II inhibition, indicating a low risk of cardiotoxicity profiles, they are nonmutagenic and nonhepatotoxic and are not predicted to inhibit hERG I.

Conclusion

This pilot study investigates the bioactivities of leaves of O. octandra, focusing on its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective potential in vitro with particular attention to its ability to inhibit pro-inflammatory enzymes and alleviate inflammation. The ethyl acetate extract of O. octandra exhibited the highest inhibitory activity against LOX, together with a significant antioxidant potential. Bioassay-guided fractionation resulted in the isolation of two major bioactive compounds, casuarinin (1) and lupeol (2), with casuarinin exhibiting the strongest LOX inhibitory activity and promising antioxidant activity. In silico docking studies reinforced the experimental findings, revealing that casuarinin (1) interacts with the LOX active site through hydrogen bonding, although its ability to enter the active site gorge is kinetically constrained. Importantly, docking studies with both A5-LOX and 15-LOX revealed highly similar ligand binding trends, reinforcing the validity of the observed inhibitory patterns across the lipoxygenase isoforms. Casuarinin (1) further demonstrated significant antineuroinflammatory effects in immortalized mouse microglia cells (SIM-A9) and neuroprotective effects in human SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells exposed to hydrogen peroxide, indicating its potential for managing neuroinflammatory conditions. This represents the first preliminary investigation to screen the pro-inflammatory enzyme inhibitory, neuroprotective, and antineuroinflammatory potential of O. octandra leaf extracts, along with the identification of key bioactive compounds. Overall, the findings reveal the therapeutic potential of O. octandra and its bioactive compounds in the context of oxidative stress, chronic liver diseases, and neuroinflammation, warranting further comprehensive investigations on molecular pathway analysis, mechanistic characterizations, and in vivo validation to fully elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms and clinical applicability of these bioactives. Moreover, the findings of the study support the traditional uses of O. octandra and suggest its viability as a candidate for the development of novel phytotherapeutic agents.
