Compounding Disasters and Australian Youth Mental Health: A Time of Significant (di)stress
Authors: Amy D. Lykins, Melissa Parsons, Suzanne M. Cosh, Phillip J. Tully, Belinda Craig, Clara Murray, Donald W. Hine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimClimate change exposure is predicted to contribute to significant mental health problems for younger people; however, little research has examined this stressor in the context of other psychosocial stressors experienced by this age group.MethodIn the immediate aftermath of Australia's severe bushfire season of 2019/2020 and at the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, 712 Australian youth (M age = 21.07, range 16–25 years) completed standardised clinical screening measures of psychological distress, adjustment disorder, and substance misuse. Objective exposure to extreme drought, bushfire, and bushfire smoke (air quality index) was quantified using data from the NSW Department of Primary Industries and Energy. Subjective exposure was quantified by responses to questions about direct exposure to drought, bushfire, and bushfire smoke.ResultsIn contrast to expectations, neither subjective nor objective exposure to climate extremes meaningfully contributed to mental health outcomes, with little variance explained. However, common psychosocial stressors did account for significant amounts of variance in psychological distress (range: 2%–6%), adjustment disorder (range: 1%–3%), and substance misuse (range: 1%–2%) symptom endorsement.ConclusionThese results indicate—for the first time—that although extreme climate events may cause significant distress in some young people, other stressful events may have more of an impact on overall mental health.

Introduction

Adolescence and young adulthood are times of major transition and increased risk for experiencing mental illness (Solmi et al. 2022). Common life stressors such as un/under‐employment, financial stress, and changes in living circumstances frequently occur during this period of life (Núñez‐Regueiro and Núñez‐Regueiro 2021) and contribute to risk for mental illness (Vrshek‐Schallhorn et al. 2015). Further to these life stressors, the predicted (and now observed) effects of climate change on mental health and well‐being have received growing attention over the past decade and a half (Charlson et al. 2021). Impacts on mental health are vicarious via the awareness of climate change and its effects on the planet and its inhabitants (e.g., ‘eco‐anxiety’) (Berry et al. 2010) but may also arise from climate extremes that influence the frequency, magnitude, extent, and timing of extreme weather events (EWEs). Due to a host of physical, mental, and developmental factors, such as underdeveloped coping resources and exposure to parental stress (Crandon et al. 2022; Burke et al. 2018), children and young people are consistently identified as uniquely vulnerable to poor mental health outcomes related to climate change (Martin et al. 2022). Understanding these impacts within the context of other life stressors at these ages will inform the development of programmes to support distressed youth, as well as identify those at higher risk of negative mental health consequences for targeted intervention.

The mental health impacts of direct exposure to EWEs have been well documented. Although most youth survivors do not develop psychiatric disorder/s following direct exposure to a disaster, common consequences for those who do include symptoms of post‐traumatic stress disorder (PSTD), depression, anxiety, and substance abuse (Garcia and Sheehan 2016; Wang et al. 2013). Furthermore, a recent population‐based cohort study has shown that multiple EWE exposures are associated with greater mental ill‐health than single EWE exposure, which is exacerbated when the time between events is short (Li and Leppold 2025). Regarding awareness of climate change, large‐scale national and international surveys and reviews have reported youth to be experiencing significant concern, worry, and anxiety about climate change (Hickman et al. 2021; Leger‐Goodes et al. 2022; Lykins et al. 2023; Martin et al. 2022) at disproportionately higher levels than older populations (Swim et al. 2022). Emerging evidence suggests broader relationships between eco‐anxiety and mental ill‐health in young people, with climate change awareness associated with depression (Sciberras and Fernando 2022), and anxiety, sadness, anger, and fear (Leger‐Goodes et al. 2022). These results highlight the need for further investigation into the mental health sequelae of climate impacts in this population—particularly in the context of other stressors, which thus far have been largely sparsely investigated.

Climate Extremes in Australia

Australia is a stable, high‐income country with a relatively strong social safety net and universal healthcare, which can serve as protective factors for physical and mental health problems. However, Australia has one of the most variable climates in the world (King et al. 2020), which is becoming more variable due to climate change (IPCC 2021). Associated with climate extremes is an expected increase in the frequency, magnitude, and extent of floods, bushfires, storms, droughts, and heatwaves in Australia (IPCC 2021). When these EWEs become disasters, they can have substantial social, economic, and environmental impacts (Deloitte Access Economics 2021).

The 3 years prior to the data collection for this study were characterised by compounding EWEs, followed by the COVID‐19 pandemic (Figure 1). Most of southern Australia, including the state of New South Wales, was in severe or critical drought from 2017 through to 2020, with conditions in 2019 being hotter and drier than during any other drought in more than 120 years (NSW Government 2022). The 2019–2020 bushfire season lasted nearly twice as long as a typical bushfire season and was unprecedented in terms of severity, extent, and type of vegetation that burned (Collins et al. 2021; van Oldenborgh et al. 2021). Available models indicate that these extreme conditions are unlikely to have occurred without the influence of climate change (van Oldenborgh et al. 2021; Devanand et al. 2024).

Timeline of drought and bushfire extreme weather events affecting New South Wales (NSW), in relation to data collection and the declaration of the COVID‐19 pandemic. The World Health Organisation declared the COVID‐19 pandemic on March 11, 2020. The first lockdown in NSW occurred between March 29 and May 10, 2020, and restricted people from leaving their homes for non‐essential activities including school, work and socialising. Data collection for this study started on March 5 and closed on May 28, 2020. Most of the metropolitan respondents completed within the first 2 weeks of survey distribution, with the regional, remote, and very remote participant recruitment taking until May.

Australian youth have consistently shown concern and worry about climate change (Sciberras and Fernando 2022; Teo et al. 2024) and associated EWEs (Lykins et al. 2023). Such concerns are situated alongside other life stressors that are not climate change‐related, but the relative influence of both on the mental health vulnerability of Australian youth is unknown. In this study, we aimed to assess the relationships between common life stressors, subjective and objective exposure to EWEs, and youth mental health using validated and standardised clinical screening measures of depression, anxiety, stress, adjustment disorder, and substance misuse. In line with predictions from previous research (e.g., Leviston et al. 2023), we hypothesised that direct exposure (subjective, objective, or both) to drought and/or bushfire would account for a significant amount of variance in mental health outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Inclusion criteria required that participants be between the ages of 16 and 25 and living in the Australian state of New South Wales, which was heavily impacted by drought and bushfire (Figure 1). We performed targeted sampling of metropolitan and regional dwellers (as per ABS 2011a) on a 1:1 ratio. A total of 746 participants completed the survey between March 5 and May 28, 2020. Eight participants were removed from the dataset for living outside of the catchment area, and 20 were removed for not having completed all the mental health measures. Given the observed significantly greater mental ill‐health observed in non‐binary youth (ABS 2023), an additional 6 were removed for reporting a gender identity other than male or female. The final sample comprised 712 participants, 79.2% (n = 564) of whom identified as female, with a mean age of 21.07 years (SD = 2.81, range = 16–25). Fifty‐seven percent lived in a major city, whereas 43% lived in an area classified as inner regional (33.8%), outer regional (8.4%), or remote (0.8%).

Power and Sensitivity Analysis

To determine the sensitivity of the study to detect small associations between objective climate‐exposure indicators and mental health outcomes, we conducted a post hoc sensitivity analysis using G × Power. With a final sample size of N = 712 and three objective exposure predictors entered simultaneously, the study had approximately 80% power (α = 0.05) to detect an additional R 2 of roughly 2% (f 2≈0.02), with power exceeding 90% for effects larger than this. This analysis indicates that the study was well powered to identify small‐to‐moderate associations. As reported below, the objective exposure variables collectively explained less than 1% of the variance in mental health outcomes, suggesting that any undetected effects are likely to be very small.

Measures

Demographics

Participants were asked a variety of demographic questions, including age, gender identity, highest level of education obtained, and the postcode where they lived most of the year. Participants also answered a series of subjective yes/no questions about whether they had been directly affected by bushfire(s), bushfire smoke, and drought in the preceding 12 months (e.g., ‘Have you been directly affected by any of the bushfires over the past year?’).

Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scales (DASS‐21) (Lovibond and Lovibond 1995)

The DASS‐21 is a 21‐item clinical self‐report screening measure of depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms experienced over the preceding week, appropriate for use with people aged 14 and older. Due to the significant positive relationships among the three subscales and in line with guidance from the DASS instruction manual, the three subscales were combined to create a composite score of overall psychological distress (range 0–63).

Adjustment Disorder New Module 8 (ADNM‐8) (Kazlauskas et al. 2018)

The ADNM‐8 is a brief clinical self‐report screening assessment of adjustment disorder symptoms as defined by the International Classification of Diseases (11th ed.) (World Health Organization 2019). In part 1, participants are asked to indicate whether they have experienced one or more of 16 defined stressful life events (SLEs) that have occurred over the past 1–2 years that ‘burdened’ the individual over the preceding 6 months. If participants answered ‘yes’ to at least one of these items (n = 685), they were then presented with an additional eight items assessing impact. A score of 18.5 or higher has been shown to indicate a probable diagnosis of adjustment disorder (Ben‐Ezra et al. 2018).

UNCOPE Alcohol and Substance Abuse Screener (UNCOPE) (Hoffmann et al. 2003)

The UNCOPE is a 6‐item screening tool for the assessment of possible alcohol or substance abuse across the lifetime, appropriate for use in adolescent populations (Urofsky et al. 2007).

Exposure to the Effects of Extreme Weather Events

Exposure of participants to drought, bushfire, and bushfire smoke was objectively measured using environmental data. Monthly drought data for local government areas (LGAs) were obtained using the NSW Department of Primary Industries and Energy (NSW DPIE) combined drought indicator (NSW DPIE 2020a). The percentage of an LGA subject to intense drought (rainfall, soil water, and plant growth below the 5th percentile) was averaged between October 2019 and March 2020. Concordance between LGAs and postcodes was determined using ABS correspondence tables (ABS 2011b), and participant postcodes were assigned the drought intensity value for the corresponding LGA.

A map of land area burned during the severe 2019/2020 bushfires was obtained from NSW DPIE (NSW DPIE 2020b). A GIS was used to compute the percentage of land burned in each participant's postcode.

Daily air quality data were obtained from the NSW DPIE Air Quality Index (AQI) (NSW DPIE 2020c). The AQI is compiled from daily monitoring of pollutants; very poor to hazardous air quality is indicated when the index passes 150 (NSW DPIE 2020c). The number of days the AQI was 150 or over was tallied between October 1, 2019 and February 29, 2020, corresponding to the severe bushfire season of 2019/2020. Concordance between participant postcodes and the AQI reporting regions was determined by an experienced geographer (M. Parsons) by visually assigning postcodes to a corresponding AQI region using published coordinates and maps. The AQI does not encompass Western NSW because of low population density; therefore, three Western NSW postcodes were assigned to the nearest AQI region (n = 4 participants). The air quality variable used in this study represents a relative spatial approximation of extreme air quality deficits likely arising from bushfire smoke in participant postcodes, although air quality deficits during this period may have occurred concurrently with dust mobilisation associated with co‐existing severe drought conditions.

Procedure

Participants were recruited by Qualtrics to complete the online survey. Participants were presented with a page detailing information about the research, including contact information for support services. After providing implied consent, participants answered the demographic and EWE exposure questions, followed by each of the questionnaires presented in a randomised manner. Due to the data collection commencement date (i.e., before the pandemic was officially announced), no questions about the COVID‐19 were included in the survey. The survey had three randomly interspersed attention questions that participants had to answer correctly to continue. Participants received a small reward for their time (e.g., points towards gift cards) distributed by Qualtrics. The study was approved by the University of New England's Human Research Ethics Committee (HE19‐182). All analyses were run using SPSS v.27.

Four of the SLE variables significantly predicted respondents' DASS‐21 composite scores: higher total scores on the DASS‐21 were associated with family conflicts, financial problems, one's own serious illness, and being assaulted. These predictors explained 26% of the variance in the DASS‐21 composite scores. Four SLEs, all from the ADNM‐8, significantly predicted respondents' substance misuse scores: financial problems, being assaulted, family conflicts, and workplace conflicts. These predictors explained 17% of the variance in substance misuse. Finally, four variables, once again all from the ADNM‐8, significantly predicted adjustment disorder scores on respondents' total ADNM‐8 scores: family conflicts, financial problems, one's own serious illness, and assault. These variables explained 20% of the variance in adjustment disorder scores.

Subjectively reported exposure to bushfires and/or drought explained less than 1% of the variance in respondents' DASS‐21, UNCOPE, or ADNM‐8 scores. Thus, relative to the other SLEs we assessed, perceived exposure to EWEs was not a strong predictor of youth mental health symptoms.

Ancillary Moderation Analyses

Although objective and subjective indicators of direct exposure to EWEs did not, by themselves, predict youth mental health outcomes, their effects may be moderated by other factors. For example, possessing low levels of psychological resilience, exhibiting high levels of concern about climate change, and living in regional areas with poorer access to mental health support (Orygen 2020) (as well as a higher likelihood of experiencing EWEs than those living in cities), all could conceivably exacerbate the negative impacts of EWEs on mental health. To assess these possibilities, we ran a set of exploratory moderation analyses using the PROCESS macro v. 4.3 for SPSS (Hayes 2022) with 10 000 bootstrapped models and unconditional moderators in which: (1) objective and subjective exposure to EWEs served as the predictor variables; (2) trait psychological resilience (Smith et al. 2008), geographic remoteness (as per ABS (2011a) classifications, major cities vs. inner regional vs. outer regional and remote combined) and climate change concern (Jones et al. 2016) served as moderators; and (3) the three mental health indicators were used as the outcome variables. To avoid problems with multicollinearity and shared variance, objective and subjective indicators of exposure were assessed separately, as was each moderator. We did not correct for family‐wise error given these analyses were exploratory and the effect sizes were expected to be small. There was some evidence for moderation, with several objective and subjective stress indicators more strongly predicting negative mental health outcomes in regional areas. Otherwise, none of the moderation analyses approached statistical significance (see Supporting Information).

Discussion

The aim of the study was to assess the contribution of subjectively reported and objectively assessed exposure to climate extremes on young people's mental health symptoms in the context of other common life stressors experienced by this population—making this a novel contribution to the literature on climate change and mental health. Contrary to our expectations, exposure to these EWEs did not significantly contribute to the mental ill‐health outcomes measured in our sample. Neither subjective reports nor objective exposure data based on geographic location showed any meaningful contribution to participants' reported mental ill‐health symptoms. Furthermore, potential variables that might be expected to moderate these relationships—such as trait psychological resilience and climate change concern—had no consistent or meaningful impact on the outcomes of the analyses. What did significantly predict mental health outcomes were a range of psychosocial stressors, most importantly self‐reported family conflicts, financial problems, serious illness, and having been assaulted. We consider explanations for these unanticipated results below.

The extant literature, whether focused on the impacts of climate change consequences such as climate extremes (Burke et al. 2018; Garcia and Sheehan 2016; Wang et al. 2013), or the effects of climate change awareness (Hickman et al. 2021; Leger‐Goodes et al. 2022; Martin et al. 2022; Sciberras and Fernando 2022), is uniformly consistent in predicting that there will be clear relationships between climate change and youth mental health. Indeed, children and young people have been identified, unequivocally, as a disproportionately high‐risk group for negative mental health consequences of climate change (Burke et al. 2018). However, we did not find these anticipated relationships in our sample, despite being in the timeframe within which one would expect to see the strongest effects of exposure to EWEs on mental health (Wang et al. 2013), and climate change concern played no role in these relationships.

There are several potential explanations for these results. As noted above, most people exposed to EWEs do not subsequently develop mental ill‐health (Goldmann and Galea 2014). Bushfires and drought are characteristic of natural environmental variation in Australia, and youth are uniquely aware of climate change compared to other age groups (Swim et al. 2022). The anticipation of climate extremes may have provided some psychological resilience to these events in our sample. Related to this, the shifting baseline syndrome (Soga and Gaston 2018) proposes that members of each generation experience their environmental conditions as ‘normal’, not having had any different experiences by which to compare them. In this way too, the climate extremes experienced by our sample may have felt more normal than catastrophic, and thus had less of an impact on mental health and well‐being (especially given the majority of participants did not perceive that they had been directly impacted by drought or bushfires). Some recent work on this age group has also shown greater exposure to environmental hazards to be associated with greater climate change denial (Bakul et al. 2025), a seemingly paradoxical finding but one that may point to denial being used as a coping mechanism for climate distress. We did not assess climate denial in our sample, but it is possible that the impact of extreme weather exposure on our participants' mental health may have been tempered if they elected to use this strategy to cope.

Previous research has also indicated that disaster arising from natural hazards typically results in less of a mental health impact with respect to stress disorders than those that are man‐made, such as terrorism or war (Bromet et al. 2017). That said, even in the 2000s, researchers were beginning to question the ‘naturalness’ of climate extremes (Satcher et al. 2007), and if attribution for these events is relevant to psychological outcomes, this needs to be considered in future research.

Finally, a scoping review on youth mental health and climate change noted that most empirical studies have used non‐standardised measures of ‘worry’ and other negative emotions (Martin et al. 2022). In addition to potentially varying recruitment methods across these previous studies, which may have resulted in a more highly distressed sample of youth than those from the general population, when assessed by standardised clinical screening measures, the overall impact of these events on youth mental health may be negligible (at least relative to other stressors). Alternatively, the ubiquitous nature of indirect impacts of the 2019/2020 bushfires (e.g., direct exposure to extended periods of bushfire smoke across the region, near constant alerts and media coverage of the bushfires) may have limited the ability to detect differences between exposure groups, with all participants likely having experienced some form of indirect exposure to bushfires that summer; indeed, Macleod et al. (2023) reported high rates of distress across all participants in a large sample of adult Australian residents, irrespective of severity of individual impact. Thus, our results may highlight the critical role of indirect exposure for mental health, a hypothesis supported by the high levels of distress across our sample.

Reported SLEs did significantly predict reported mental health symptoms in our sample of young Australians. Although some SLEs may plausibly arise downstream of climate extremes (e.g., family conflict, financial strain), exploratory analyses indicated minimal overlap between exposure indicators and endorsement of these stressors, suggesting that indirect pathways were unlikely to account for the present findings. The link between SLEs and mental illness in young people is strong and known (e.g., Vrshek‐Schallhorn et al. 2015). Critically though, the dates of data collection (unintentionally) coincided with the first wave of the COVID‐19 pandemic and associated ‘lockdowns’ in Australia. As our survey launched in advance of the pandemic's official declaration and Australia's first lockdown, we were unable to modify it to include specific questions about the pandemic. However, participants were asked to identify their ‘most stressful’ event via open text in the ADNM‐8, and around 5% of our sample reported something associated with the pandemic (e.g., increased/decreased work because of COVID‐19, lack of access to normal leisure activities, isolation due to social distancing requirements, or just ‘COVID‐19’ itself). Previous research has reported the significant mental health impacts of the pandemic (Kauhanen et al. 2023), with young people having been disproportionately affected (Wilson et al. 2021). More broadly, these unique circumstances may have contributed directly to the types of stressors (e.g., financial problems, family conflicts) endorsed by our participants that did predict greater mental ill‐health and have been found to supersede mental health impacts of wildfire exposure on youth in the United States (Sugg et al. 2022). Some researchers have suggested that there is a finite pool of attention for threat, with evidence pointing to decreased attention to some threats when others take prominence (Sisco et al. 2023); it is possible that the all‐consuming nature of the pandemic onset may have masked any existing climate‐related effects on our mental health outcomes. Providing some support for the veracity of our results, though, Ogunbode et al. (2025) found that climate anxiety did not account for significant variance in mental health outcomes when assessed against other stressors (e.g., loneliness, financial anxiety, health anxiety), arguing for the need to consider the broader context of young people's lives when investigating relationships between climate change and mental health. Either way, it would be important to replicate these findings in a non‐pandemic context.

Strengths of this study include a relatively large sample, the use of standardised clinical screening measures, and good representation of both major city and regional geographic regions, making this a unique contribution to the growing empirical literature. However, the conclusions drawn from the results are limited by the study's cross‐sectional design, the over‐representation of female participants, known issues inherent to opt‐in online survey panel recruitment (e.g., non‐random sample, unknown participation rate), unknown socio‐economic status of our participants (which could have implications for the relative impact of SLEs such as financial problems and unemployment), and the use of a non‐dimensional subjective exposure measure. Future research would benefit from more equitable gender representation, a longitudinal methodological design, and rigorous clinical assessment and diagnostic procedures (e.g., semi‐structured clinical interviews). It would also be worth collecting data following different types of climate extremes (e.g., floods), capturing the severity of exposure to natural disasters, and replicating these findings now that the COVID‐19 pandemic has ended so that its potentially confounding influence is removed.
