Arterial Stiffness and Acute Kidney Injury in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) Study
Authors: Ester S Oh, Aditya L Surapaneni, Jessica B Kendrick, Hirofumi Tanaka, Morgan E Grams, Anna J Jovanovich, Kristen L Nowak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveAcute kidney injury (AKI) is associated with prolonged hospitalization and increased in-hospital mortality risk. However, the prediction of incident AKI is inaccurate, and additional predictors of AKI are strongly needed. Arterial stiffness, as measured using carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV), is associated with kidney function decline and may serve as a plausible predictor of AKI. Acute kidney injury (AKI) commonly occurs in US adults; the rate of hospitalization with AKI was 62.1 admissions per 1,000 person-years in 2020.1 AKI is associated with prolonged hospitalization and an elevated risk for in-hospital mortality.2 The major risk factors for AKI include age, chronic kidney disease (CKD), heart disease, hypertension, and diabetes3; however, predicting who will develop AKI is still inaccurate. Although several risk models of AKI have been developed, these models are often poorly validated and used only in specific clinical conditions, such as cardiac surgery.4 Thus, additional associating variables that may in part explain the potential risk of AKI are strongly needed, especially in individuals at high risk.

Arterial stiffness is determined in part by the properties of the arterial wall, including the elasticity and compliance of the artery5, and significantly contributes to the ability to conduct blood flow and mitigate the high blood pressure that travels through to end-organs.5 Higher arterial stiffness is associated with an elevated risk for kidney function decline as well as cardiovascular disease (CVD).5,6 The gold-standard measure of central (aortic) arterial stiffness is the carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV).7 We previously demonstrated a strong association between cfPWV and the risk of AKI in individuals at high cardiovascular risk who participated in the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT).8 However, the SPRINT study included only a small number of AKI events in a shorter follow-up period (ie, in total 20 AKI events over a median of 453 days), which may have limited the power of the analysis. Thus, we herein examined the association of arterial stiffness (cfPWV as well as 5 other pulse wave velocity [PWV] measures) at baseline with the risk of AKI in community-dwelling older adults who participated in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study. We hypothesized greater arterial stiffness is associated with higher AKI risk.

Methods

Study Design

The ARIC study is a community-based prospective cohort study that recruited a total of 15,792 participants aged 45-64 years at study entrance from 4 ARIC field centers in the United States (Washington County, MD; Forsyth County, NC; Jackson, MS; and Minneapolis, MN) between 1987 and 1989 when participants attended the baseline clinic examination (visit 1).9 Four subsequent visits occurred, including visit 2 between 1990 and 1992; visit 3 between 1993 and 1995; visit 4 between 1996 and 1998; and visit 5 between 2011 and 2013. In our current analysis, visit 5 was identified as the baseline because this was when PWV was first measured. As such, the participants in our analysis were considered older adults at baseline (visit 5) because their mean ± SD age was 75 ± 5 years. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of all participating centers and all participants provided written informed consent.

A total of 6,538 participants were able to attend visit 5 (baseline for the current study, which included measurements of PWV). In the current analysis, we used follow-up data until December 31, 2019 (follow-up data from Jackson Center were only through December 31, 2017). Participants with measurements of PWV and without end-stage kidney disease were included. A total of 2,293 participants were excluded from the current analysis because of missing data for the primary predictor variable (cfPWV, n = 1,423) and/or covariates (smoking, n = 58; diabetes, n = 24; CVD, n = 86; hypertension, n = 48; systolic blood pressure, n = 7; diastolic blood pressure, n = 7; body mass index, n = 12; total cholesterol, n = 31; high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, n = 31; estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR], n = 24; urinary albumin creatinine ratio [ACR], n = 563; ankle-brachial index, n = 113), resulting in a final analytic sample of 4,245 participants (Fig S1). The demographics in the currently analyzed cohort were similar to those of the entire ARIC cohort (Table S1).

The current analysis was conducted in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.10

Study Variables

The primary predictor in this analysis was cfPWV at baseline (ie, ARIC visit 5). The secondary predictors were heart-femoral PWV (hfPWV), heart-carotid PWV (hcPWV), heart-ankle PWV (haPWV), brachial-ankle PWV (baPWV), and femoral-ankle PWV (faPWV). PWV was measured using a vascular testing device VP-1000 Plus (Omron, Kyoto, Japan).11 For ha PWV and hfPWV, PWV from the heart was determined by measuring the heart sound using phonocardiogram.12 PWV methodology for ARIC study has been previously reported in detail.11,13 Briefly, trained technicians measured PWV according to a standardized protocol. Before testing, participants abstained from caffeine and fasted for ≥ 3 hours and each subject rested supine for 5-10 minutes in a quiet, temperature-controlled room. A previous study reported that repeatability is acceptable for all PWV measures in ARIC.14 The intraclass correlation coefficient (95% confidence intervals) were 0.70 (0.59, 0.81) for cfPWV, 0.84 (0.78, 0.90) for baPWV, and 0.69 (0.59, 0.79) for faPWV. The minimal detectable change between repeat measures within an individual was 411.0 cm/s for cfPWV, 370.6 cm/s for baPWV, and 301.4 cm/s for faPWV. The minimal detectable difference for 2 independent samples of 100 per group was 139.3 cm/s for cfPWV, 172.3 cm/s for baPWV, and 100.4 cm/s for faPWV.

The primary outcome was time to AKI. Intervening hospitalizations for ARIC participants were determined by semi-annual telephone follow-up starting in 2012 and through active ongoing surveillance of community hospital discharge list, local newspaper obituaries, state death lists, and death certificates.15 International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) or Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes were recorded from hospital discharge billing or death certificate. Incident AKI was defined by codes 584.5 to 584.9 in ICD-9-CM and codes N17.0 to 17.9 in ICD-10-CM.15 The use of ICD codes for defining AKI in the ARIC study has previously been validated (∼90% positive and negative predictive values, despite low sensitivity of 17% and high specificity of 95%).16

Confounders potentially related to vascular stiffness and AKI, all measured at visit 5, were selected a priori as covariates for the analysis. Demographics (age, sex, and race) and smoking status were self-reported.13 Body mass index was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by height squared (m2). Blood samples were collected following a standardized venipuncture protocol, and ARIC central laboratories performed assays for total and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol.17 Certified technicians measured the blood pressure following a standardized protocol and using a validated automatic sphygmomanometer OMRON HEM-907 XL (Omron).18 Prevalent hypertension was confirmed if systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg, and/or participants used antihypertensive medications in the past 2 weeks.19 Prevalent CVD included a history of myocardial infarction, coronary bypass, and angioplasty of the coronary artery. Prevalent diabetes was defined as meeting one of the following criteria: self-reported physician’s diagnosis of diabetes mellitus; use of hypoglycemic medications; nonfasting serum glucose levels ≥ 200 mg/dL; or fasting serum glucose level ≥126 mg/dL.19 Urinary albumin was measured by an immunoturbidometric method using ProSpec nephelometric analyzer (Dade Behring GMBH, Marburg, Germany).20 Urinary creatinine was measured by an enzymatic method (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN) using Roche Modular P Chemistry Analyzer (Roche Diagnostics).20 eGFR was calculated using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) 2021 equation.21 Ankle-brachial index was assessed using the same tool which was used for PWV measurement.18

Statistical Analyses

Covariates were summarized by mean ± SD or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and number (n) and proportion (%) for categorical variables. To examine if there were nonlinear associations of cfPWV and 5 other PWV measures with AKI risk, we employed cubic spline curves. Because there was a J-shaped relationship, cfPWV and 5 other PWV measures were modeled as a categorical variable in quartiles (Q) with the lowest quartile, Q2, serving as the reference category. Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to evaluate the longitudinal association of cfPWV and 5 other PWV measures with time to AKI. Model 1 was adjusted for demographics (age, sex, and race); model 2 was additionally adjusted for traditional CVD risk factors (smoking status, prevalent diabetes, prevalent CVD, prevalent hypertension, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, body mass index, total cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol); model 3 was further adjusted for markers of kidney function (eGFR and log-transformed urinary ACR); and model 4 was further adjusted for a marker for peripheral artery disease (ie, ankle-brachial index). Using the Fine and Gray method, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in which all-cause mortality was evaluated as a competing risk for AKI. The association between quartiles of cfPWV and AKI risk was also examined in prespecified subgroups by demographics (sex, race [African American vs White], older age [>75 years vs ≤75 years]), risk factors for AKI (ie, prevalent coronary heart disease [CHD], diabetes, and CKD). Interactions of prespecified subgroups × cfPWV were evaluated in the fully adjusted model (model 4). A 2-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using R v.4.1.3 and Stata v.17.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among the 6,538 participants enrolled at visit 5 (baseline), a total of 4,245 participants were included in the final analytic cohort. The mean ± SD eGFR was 66 ± 18 mL/min/1.73 m2, age was 75 ± 5 years, 44% (n = 1,860) were male, 77% (n = 3,269) were White, 29% (n = 1,208) had prevalent diabetes, and 15% (n = 615) had prevalent CVD (Table 1). Over a median follow-up of 7.1 years (interquartile range 5.4-7.8), there were 639 AKI events. The AKI event rate was highest in Q4 of cfPWV, and the event rates in Q3 and Q4 were higher than in Q1 and Q2 (Fig 1A).

The cubic spline curve demonstrates a J-shaped association between cfPWV and the fully adjusted hazard ratio (HR) of AKI (Fig 1B). A J-shaped (hfPWV, hc PWV, and ha PWV) or an inverse (baPWV and faPWV) association was observed between other PWV measures and the fully adjusted HR of AKI (Fig S2).

In other PWV measures, only ha PWV and baPWV showed a J-shaped association with AKI risk in unadjusted and adjusted models (Figs S3 and S4, Tables S2 and S3). There was a J-shaped association of hfPWV and hc PWV with AKI risk in the unadjusted model, but this was not evident when fully adjusted (Figs S3 and S4, Table S2 and S3). An inverse association between faPWV and AKI risk was observed in the unadjusted model, but this association was not evident in the fully adjusted model (Figs S3 and S4, Tables S2 and S3).

In the subgroup analysis, the highest quartile of cfPWV (Q4), as compared with reference cfPWV (Q2), was associated with a significantly elevated risk of AKI in those aged > 75 years, both sexes, White participants, those without CHD, and those with prevalent CKD (Fig S5). However, none of the interactions were statistically significant.

Discussion

In community-dwelling older adults who participated in the ARIC study, we demonstrated that a higher cfPWV (ie, the gold-standard measure of central arterial stiffness) was associated with a greater risk of AKI over a median follow-up of 7.1 years (interquartile range 5.4-7.8 years). Interestingly, after fully adjusting for demographics, CVD risk factors, and markers for kidney disease and peripheral artery disease, there was a J-shaped relationship between cfPWV and AKI risk, suggesting a nonlinear association between central arterial stiffness and AKI risk in older adults.

Arterial stiffness is associated with a decline in kidney function.18 Increased arterial stiffness reduces arterial elasticity and compliance, which contributes to the transmission of high-pressured forces to end-organ microvasculature.2 Given these vascular beds are not designed to endure high pressures, the high-pressured forces resulting from increased arterial stiffness could damage microvascular beds.2 The kidney is a highly vascularized organ that receives a fifth of the cardiac output22; thus, the kidney could be affected by the high blood volume flow and the low resistance in the setting of greater arterial stiffness.2 Consistent with this pathophysiology, in our current analysis of ARIC participants, we demonstrated there was a higher AKI risk in participants with cfPWV in Q3 and Q4 as compared with reference cfPWV (Q2) after adjusting for kidney function markers (ie, eGFR and urinary ACR) and peripheral artery disease markers (ie, ankle-brachial index). Of all the PWV measures, cfPWV (the gold-standard index of aortic stiffness) was the strongest predictor variable of AKI risk. Our previous findings in SPRINT participants also demonstrated that cfPWV is strongly associated with AKI risk, independent of kidney function markers (ie, eGFR and urinary ACR).8 Combined, our previous and current findings suggest that a higher arterial stiffness may increase the risk of AKI in older adults (mean ± SD age: SPRINT 72 ± 9 years; ARIC 75 ± 5 years).

The J-shape association of cfPWV and AKI risk (ie, a higher AKI risk in participants with cfPWV in Q1, Q3, and Q4 as compared with reference cfPWV [Q2]) is of interest. A potential explanation for this observation is the cfPWV in the lowest quartile may reflect a lack of the ability of the arteries to sufficiently react and contract (ie, appropriate autoregulation) to mitigate the changes in cyclical pressures generated during the cardiac cycle, which ultimately reduces the amount of blood flow to the end-organ microvasculature including the kidney. This may lead to an increased risk of AKI. However, this explanation is speculative without sufficient evidence to support it. However, our observation is biologically plausible given a previous study demonstrating a U-shaped association between serum calcium levels, which affects vascular tone and arterial stiffness23,24, and the risk of in-hospital AKI.23 Moreover, a U-shaped association is observed between ankle-arm index (ie, an indication of arterial stiffness) and mortality in a large community-dwelling older cohort (ie, Health, Aging, and Body Composition study; n = 2,886; aged 70-79 years).25 Taken together, these results suggest both lower and higher arterial stiffness may indicate poor health and possibly increase AKI risk. Future studies are warranted to test our hypothesis and investigate the underlying mechanisms of this nonlinear relationship between cfPWV and AKI risk.

Studies of patients with CKD support an independent role of arterial stiffness (assessed by cfPWV) in kidney function decline in established CKD.26,27 In adults without known CKD (N = 4,838), cfPWV is associated with 1.84-fold greater risk of incident CKD.28 In patients who have undergone coronary artery bypass graft surgery (N = 137), cfPWV is associated with 1.5-fold greater odds of AKI events.29 We observed similar findings in our previous SPRINT study.8 However, the reason why we observed a J-shaped association in our current ARIC study, but not in SPRINT study8, may be due to several inherent differences between the study cohorts. First, the sample size was much greater in the current ARIC study as compared with the SPRINT study (ARIC, n = 4,243; SPRINT, n = 613), as were the number of AKI events (ARIC, 639 events; SPRINT, 20 events). The follow-up duration of the current ARIC study is longer than the follow-up in our previous SPRINT study (ARIC, median 7.1 years; SPRINT, median 453 days). Moreover, although the SPRINT participants were individuals with hypertension at high cardiovascular risk, they were recruited to participate in a randomized controlled trial, whereas the ARIC participants were community-dwelling older adults. Accordingly, our current findings in the ARIC study are more generalizable to a community-dwelling older population.

Although the gold-standard measure of central arterial stiffness was associated with AKI risk, we did not observe associations of other PWV measures of central arterial stiffness (ie, hfPWV, hc PWV) and peripheral arterial stiffness (ie, faPWV) with AKI risk in the fully adjusted model. In the subgroup analysis, no interaction of cfPWV with demographics (ie, age, sex, and race) and AKI risk factors (ie, prevalent CHD, diabetes, and CKD) was observed in predicting AKI risk. These nonsignificant interactions could result from limited power in the subgroup analyses. These results also could suggest that cfPWV does not differentially associate to AKI risk in those subgroups, and thus, results are generalizable in community-dwelling older adults. Accordingly, our results support the potential utility of cfPWV, the gold-standard measure of arterial stiffness, on AKI risk prediction across various demographics and AKI risk factors.

There are several limitations in the current study. Most of the participants were White, and the African American participants were mainly recruited from a single study site (Jackson, MS); thus, caution is needed when generalizing the results to other races. We adjusted for numerous covariates, but the results may be subject to residual confounding factors, which may be unmeasured or unknown at this time. In addition, AKI in the ARIC study was defined based on the ICD codes, which is a less robust index for defining AKI because of limited sensitivity, lack of adjudication, and inability to identify the cause and severity of AKI.30 However, the use of ICD codes for defining AKI in the ARIC study has previously been validated.16 Moreover, our current ARIC study showed similar findings as our previous SPRINT study that adjudicated AKI events.8 A major strength of our analysis is the use of a large, diverse, population-based cohort of community-dwelling older adults who participated in the ARIC study, which allowed us to expand and confirm our previous findings in SPRINT participants8 who are at high cardiovascular risk into a community-dwelling older adult population. Moreover, the current study includes carefully collected data on PWV, including the gold-standard measure of arterial stiffness (cfPWV).

To conclude, we observed a J-shaped relationship between cfPWV and AKI risk. Higher arterial stiffness was associated with an increased risk of AKI in older adults. These findings are concordant with our previously reported analysis of an older population at high cardiovascular risk who participated in the SPRINT study.8 We also found lower arterial stiffness was associated with higher AKI risk in the ARIC cohort, suggesting impaired autoregulation could possibly increase AKI risk in community-dwelling older adults. Our findings have important clinical implications, suggesting central arterial stiffness, as measured by cfPWV, may be useful to understand the biologically plausible mechanisms carrying the risk of AKI in older adults. However, future studies are warranted to confirm this nonlinear relationship in other community-dwelling populations and examine its underlying mechanisms.
