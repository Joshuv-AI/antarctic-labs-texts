The role and application prospects of glycolysis-related non-coding RNAs in lung cancer
Authors: Qingting Zhang, Haoxiang Wang, Zongkai Jiang, Yuanshan Yang, Yulun Li, Wei Wang, Xiaochun Peng, Jinbai Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic disorders, especially those affecting glucose metabolism, are hallmarks of lung cancer and other tumors. Cancer cells rely on glycolysis to rapidly generate energy from glucose, pyruvate, and lactate, fueling accelerated tumor growth. Recent studies have revealed a close association between aberrant non-coding RNA (ncRNA) expression and glycolysis.Main BodyThis review explores the regulatory mechanisms of glycolysis-associated ncRNAs in lung cancer, including competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) mechanisms, epigenetic modifications, and protein interactions. By acting on glycolytic enzymes, transcription factors, signaling pathways, and metabolic crosstalk in the tumor microenvironment, these ncRNAs influence lung cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, immune evasion, and therapeutic response, thereby playing critical roles in the glycolytic process of lung cancer. Furthermore, this review discusses the potential value of these ncRNAs in lung cancer diagnosis, prognosis assessment, drug resistance, and radiosensitivity, and summarizes current research progress in ncRNA-based therapies, along with the challenges facing their clinical translation.ConclusionGlycolysis-associated ncRNAs not only serve as central hubs in the metabolic regulation of lung cancer but also offer new avenues for the discovery of novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Although clinical translation remains challenging, the continued convergence of emerging technologies and metabolic research is poised to position the ncRNA–glycolysis axis as a cornerstone of future precision medicine in lung cancer.Keywords: miRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, ncRNA, Lung cancer, Glycolysis, Therapy

Introduction

Lung cancer, among the most prevalent malignant tumors, is a leading contributor to cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Lung adenocarcinoma, the most common type of primary lung tumor, disproportionately affects female non-smokers and is often missed during early-stage lung cancer screening [2]. Due to the tendency for lung cancer to be diagnosed at advanced stages, affected individuals often succumb to the disease. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of lung cancer, the identification of highly sensitive detection methods, and the development of corresponding targeted therapies are urgently needed.

Metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark of cancer, is a distinct biochemical characteristic of cancer cells. This reprogramming is defined by a preferential shift toward glycolysis as a source of energy, even in the presence of oxygen [3]. Glycolysis is a less efficient method of energy production compared to oxidative phosphorylation. Under normal physiological conditions, cells only use glycolysis when oxygen is limited, a process known as anaerobic glycolysis. However, cancer cells utilize glycolysis even when oxygen is plentiful—a phenomenon called the Warburg effect [4]. This energy production method rapidly meets the energy demands of DNA replication, proving crucial for tumor growth and proliferation. Like other solid tumors, lung cancer utilizes aerobic glycolysis for bioenergetic processes, and the survival of lung cancer cells is regulated by glycolysis and its key enzymes [5]. Aerobic glycolysis has been shown to promote tumor cell growth and proliferation. Elevated aerobic glycolysis increases lactate levels within the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby inhibiting immune cell infiltration and polarization. This process ultimately facilitates tumor immune evasion and contributes to drug resistance [4]. Targeting glycolysis may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for cancers such as lung carcinoma.

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) are distinctive RNA transcripts widely found in eukaryotic genomes. They play critical roles in cancer progression, acting as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors, and hold significant potential for cancer therapeutics [6]. Research indicates that the aberrant expression of ncRNAs—including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs)—is closely associated with glycolysis [7]. A substantial proportion of ncRNAs are specifically expressed in lung cancer and play pivotal roles in glycolysis by interacting directly with sugar transporters, key enzymes, cancer-suppressing genes, transcription factors, and diverse signaling pathways within the cell [8] (Table 1), ncRNA dysregulation can either enhance or inhibit glycolysis. Current research suggests that glycolysis-associated ncRNAs are critical in lung cancer. However, the specific molecular mechanisms and integrated networks by which ncRNAs regulate glycolysis in lung cancer are not fully understood. Therefore, this review aims to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of ncRNAs in glucose metabolism, focusing on their emerging roles in lung cancer. This effort seeks to provide insights into the initiation and progression of lung cancer and to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Regulatory mechanisms of ncRNAs targeting key glycolytic molecules and signaling pathways in lung cancer

Research advances in glycolysis in lung cancer

Generally, differentiated cellular energy production relies primarily on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, while tumor cells predominantly utilize aerobic glycolysis, tending to convert glucose into lactate [33]. Metabolic reprogramming allows tumor cells to rapidly generate ATP and accumulate intermediates necessary for proliferation-dependent synthesis pathways. These intermediates include acetate-CoA, essential for fatty acid synthesis; glycolytic intermediates, key for non-essential amino acid synthesis; and ribose, crucial for nucleotide synthesis. Ultimately, these products promote lung cancer cell proliferation, enhance invasiveness, and contribute to drug resistance [34] (Fig. 1). Targeting glycolysis in lung cancer represents a potential therapeutic strategy.

Aerobic glycolysis significantly impacts lung cancer progression by influencing tumor proliferation, migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and immune evasion. Furthermore, enhanced glycolysis in stromal cells can mediate anti-tumor immunity. Key glycolytic enzymes, transcription factors, and signaling pathways modulate aerobic glycolysis, making them potential therapeutic strategies for lung cancer treatment

Glycolysis regulates tumor progression

Research indicates that the Warburg effect and cancer cells’ reliance on it are influenced by both intracellular and extracellular environments. Lung cancer cells often have mutations in genes and pathways such as the PI3K (Phosphoinositide 3-kinase)-AKT (Protein Kinase B, PKB)-mTOR (Mechanistic target of rapamycin) pathway, as well as in oncoproteins like RAS, c-Myc (cellular myelocytomatosis oncogene) and HIF-1 (hypoxia-inducible factor 1), and in the tumor suppressor gene TP53. These genes and signaling pathways regulate cellular glycolysis and metabolism, thereby influencing tumor progression [35]. As a common oncogenic driver in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), KRAS gene mutations increase the activity of OXPHOS (Oxidative phosphorylation)(mitochondrial complex subunits), improving nutrient uptake and glycolysis in lung cancer cells to enhance invasion [36]. Hypoxia or hypoxic conditions, as hallmark features of the TME in solid tumors, influence the stability of HIFs—key factors in metabolic reprogramming [37]. In cancer cells, HIF-1α increases the expression of glycolytic enzymes and suppresses pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), which in turn enhances lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) activity. This promotes an acidic TME, fostering proliferation and angiogenesis in NSCLC cells [38]. Concurrently, hypoxia fosters dispersal and colonization in NSCLC cells by enhancing glycolysis (through the lactylation levels of the transcription factor SOX9) [39]. However, research indicates that exposure to hyperoxia (60% oxygen) inhibits the MYC/MCT1 (monocarboxylate transporter 1) axis, leading to lactic acid accumulation and intracellular acidification, thereby slowing tumor growth and metastasis [40]. Glycolysis enhances tumor angiogenesis. Studies in NSCLC tissues show that increased expression of phospholipase PLA2G2D (phospholipase A2 group IID), an immune and metabolism-related molecule, correlates with both VEGF expression and glycolytic signaling pathways. PLA2G2 appears to mediate NSCLC cell survival and angiogenesis through the glycolytic pathway [41].

Upregulation of key enzymes in the glucose metabolic pathway intensifies abnormal metabolic processes in tumor cells. For example, studies have shown that in lung cancer, eight out of ten glycolytic enzymes are upregulated, including HK2 (hexokinase 2), PKM2 (pyruvate kinase 2), and PDK1 [5]. In glycolysis, HK2 is a key enzyme subject to positive regulation by several oncogenic pathways. Specifically, the transcription factor OCT1 (octamer-binding transcription factor 1), as well as the BARX2 (BarH-like homeobox 2) and FOXA1 (Forkhead box A1) transcription complex, can directly target HK2 expression in lung cancer. This targeting drives glycolytic flux and promotes cellular proliferation [42, 43]. One of the key regulators of glycolytic flux, phosphofructokinase (PFK), exhibits upregulation considered a hallmark of malignant tumors. The platelet-derived PFK (PFKP) phenotype, revealed in clinical samples, may predict prognosis in lung cancer [44]. Moreover, PFKP stability is regulated by ubiquitination. Specifically, the E3 ligase HRD1 (HMG-CoA reductase degradation protein 1) inhibits glycolysis by mediating ubiquitin-dependent degradation of PFKP. Therefore, targeting the HRD1-PFKP pathway may inhibit the Warburg effect and suppress lung cancer progression [45].

Glycolysis promotes immune cell infiltration

Tumor cells can foster an immunosuppressive microenvironment by metabolically reprogramming their surroundings, with the glycolytic metabolite lactate playing a central role. High levels of lactate create an acidic TME and impair immune cell function. Tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (TDEs) activate NF-κB, which enhances glycolysis and, consequently, lactate production. This process not only increases PD-L1 expression in lung cancer cells but also polarizes resident tissue macrophages toward an immunodeficient phenotype in the immediate vicinity, ultimately promoting tumor metastasis [46]. SLC16A3 (also known as MCT4), acting as a transporter mediating lactate efflux, promotes lactate production and efflux. By suppressing CD8 T cell function, it diminishes tumor responsiveness to anti-PD-1 inhibitors. Targeting SLC16A3 can ameliorate the immunosuppressive TME and enhance antitumor efficacy through anti-PD-1 blockade [47]. Glycolysis can alter macrophage phenotypes, promoting immune infiltration and contributing to drug resistance. For instance, lung cancer patients with high glycolytic activity show increased infiltration of M0 and M1 macrophages. This macrophage infiltration affects lung adenocarcinoma prognosis and diminishes immunotherapy response, potentially by inducing Th2 cell infiltration [48]. NDRG1 (N-myc downstream-regulated gene 1) enhances glycolysis and promotes lactate accumulation by inhibiting LDHA ubiquitination. This process induces M2-type macrophage reprogramming, compromises CD8 T cell functionality, and upregulates immunosuppressive genes, thereby promoting immunosuppression [49]. Research indicates that glycolysis plays a role in STING (stimulator of interferon genes)- dependent anticancer activity in dendritic cells (DCs) from NSCLC patients. Specifically, STING activation promotes HIF-1α-mediated glycolysis, creating a positive feedback loop that enhances DC-driven antitumor immunity [50]. The research suggests that targeted intervention in the glycolytic pathway is a promising therapeutic avenue for overcoming resistance to anti-cancer immunotherapies.

Therapeutic strategies targeting glycolysis

Targeting glycolytic metabolic pathways, as well as the expression of critical enzymes and transporters, shows considerable promise for lung cancer treatment. For example, the recombinant adenovirus Ad-apoptin suppresses glycolysis, motility, and invasion in pulmonary carcinoma cells through targeted inhibition of the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway [51]. Glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1) interacted directly with phosphorylated p-EGFR and inhibited EGFR protein degradation via proteolysis mediated by ubiquitin [52]. This study suggests that using GLUT1 inhibitors to make pancreatic cancer cells more susceptible to the EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) gefitinib may improve treatment outcomes. Furthermore, because cancer cells can compensate for impaired glycolysis by increasing their reliance on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, targeting oxidative phosphorylation could disrupt this adaptation and produce an antitumor effect [53]. As an intrinsic regulatory factor in NSCLC tumors, HVEM interacts with the N-terminal amino acid segment 1-383 of the MPRIP protein to inhibit its downstream glycolytic signaling pathway. It further modulates macrophage polarization, thereby suppressing the metastatic progression of NSCLC cells [54]. Given glycolysis’s pivotal role in immune suppression and subsequent drug resistance, targeting this metabolic pathway can inhibit tumor growth and enhance immunotherapy efficacy. Bufotenin (CB), the bioactive component of bufotenin injection, demonstrates potential antitumor effects by modulating macrophage activation. Studies suggest that CB inhibits M2 macrophage-mediated pro-tumor activity by targeting HIF-1α and glycolytic pathways, thereby shifting the TME towards an antitumor state [55]. In lung cancer, protein branched-chain ketodehydrogenase kinase (BCKDK) promotes aerobic glycolysis by upregulating c-Myc transcription, which subsequently increases HK2 expression. Simultaneously, elevated lactate levels, a byproduct of glycolysis, upregulate ABC (ATP-binding cassette) transporters, promoting resistance to trametinib [56]. Target-directed intervention of the BCKDK/MYC/HK2 pathway offers a high-promise strategy for lung cancer treatment, capable of both inhibiting tumor progression and overcoming trametinib resistance.

Regulatory mechanisms of ncRNAs and glycolysis in lung cancer

The modulation of glycolysis by ncRNAs is not a simple linear cause-and-effect relationship, but rather a highly integrated, complex network [7] (Fig. 2). ncRNAs participate in metabolic pathways and can affect lung cancer progression by targeting key enzymes and signaling nodes in glycolysis through multi-level intervention, including epigenetic modification, transcription factor activity, mRNA stability, and protein function (Fig. 3).

Regulation of the transcription of key glycolytic genes

Epigenetic modifications

ncRNA can function as a scaffold or guide, recruiting chromatin-modifying complexes to the promoter or enhancer regions of key glycolytic genes (e.g., HK2, LDHA, GLUT1). Through modifications such as histone methylation and acetylation, ncRNA can activate or repress their transcription [57]. Epigenetic modifications, such as DNA methylation and histone modification, can determine the effects of ncRNAs on glycolysis. Chromatin regulatory factors, such as histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1), HDAC2, and silence information regulator 1 (SIRT1), have been reported to undergo nucleolar translocation, inducing chromatin remodelling that disrupts global transcription. The p53-regulated lncRNA EPB41L4A-AS1 facilitates the nuclear translocation of HDAC2. This, in turn, increases HDAC2 occupancy at the VHL and VDAC1 promoter regions, enhancing glycolysis [58]. LINC00184 stimulates PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog) promoter methylation by recruiting DNMT1 (DNA methyltransferase 1), thereby boosting mitochondrial OXPHOS activity and utilizing the PTEN (Phosphatase and TENsin homolog)/AKT axis to mediate glycolysis in cancer cells [59]. However, the precise mechanisms by which ncRNAs recruit chromatin regulatory factors to specific chromatin sites, subsequently influencing glycolysis, remain largely undefined.

Interaction with transcription factors

Transcription factors can directly enhance glycolytic gene expression, thereby influencing the Warburg effect. ncRNAs can also modulate glycolytic gene networks globally by interacting with transcription factors, affecting their stability, activity, or DNA-binding capacity [57]. lncRNA FTO-IT1 recruits the ILF2/3 (Interleukin enhancer-binding factor 2/3) complex to the 3’ UTR of FTO mRNA, inducing FTO mRNA stability and reducing N-6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) modification by the demethylase N6-methyltransferase 6 on glycolysis-related genes, thereby leading to c-Myc overexpression. Concurrently, c-Myc binds its promoter region under low-glucose conditions, regulating FTO-IT1 expression to form a mutual feedback circuit between c-Myc and FTO-IT1 [60]. ncRNAs can also directly modulate the expression of transcription factors, thereby affecting their roles in glycolysis. For example, the transcription factor SIX1 upregulates numerous glycolytic genes. However, microRNA-548a-3p suppresses SIX1 expression, thereby modulating the Warburg effect and inhibiting tumor growth in both in vitro and in vivo experiment [61]. Although overexpression and knockdown experiments have demonstrated this regulatory mechanism, a single transcription factor in vivo may be regulated by multiple ncRNAs [26, 27], the corresponding targeting strategies need to be validated in clinical trials.

The transcription factor HIF-1 is closely associated with hypoxia and the Warburg effect [57]. HIF-1α plays a pivotal role in regulating cell proliferation, particularly in hypoxic environments. In NSCLC cells, a diminished expression of miR-199a is linked to elevated HIF-1α levels and accelerated proliferation. This correlation stems from miR-199a’s inhibitory effect on HIF-1α, which typically restrains hypoxia-induced cell proliferation and prevents the upregulation of PDK1 [25]. ncRNA can also indirectly modulate the activity of Factor Inhibiting HIF (FIH), an inhibitor of HIF-1α. For instance, miR-31-5 targets and reduces FIH expression, consequently promoting HIF-1 signaling. This cascade leads to an upregulation of aerobic glycolytic gene expression. In a mouse lung cancer xenograft model, this mechanism has been shown to enhance cell proliferation by stimulating glycolysis [31]. Furthermore, hypoxic conditions themselves can influence ncRNA expression. Hypoxia-induced AC020978, for example, facilitates the translocation of PKM2 into the nucleus. Once in the nucleus, PKM2 functions as a transcriptional co-activator, thereby enhancing the transcriptional activity of HIF-1α, forming an AC020978/PKM2/HIF-1α positive feedback loop that continuously drives the expression of glycolytic genes and tumor invasive phenotypes [15].

Affecting the stability and translation of glycolysis-related mRNAs

Regulating mRNA stability through direct binding

As post-transcriptional gene regulators, miRNAs are significant modulators in cancer. By binding to the 3’UTR (3’-Untranslated region) of mRNA targets, miRNAs interfere with translation, leading to degradation or translational suppression of the associated mRNA [62]. In lung cancer, miRNAs can target the mRNAs of various enzymes and related genes, influencing glucose metabolism within cancer cells. miR-206 downregulates HK2 expression by targeting its 3’UTR, thereby reducing NSCLC cell survival and proliferation [9]. In clinical lung cancer samples, miR-128 expression negatively correlated with PFKL mRNA levels. miR-128 regulates PFKL mRNA by suppressing the feedback loop in the AKT signaling pathway within pulmonary carcinoma cells, thus reducing glucose uptake and increasing intracellular ATP content. This may serve as a future research direction regarding the utility of metabolic modulators in treatment, but further clinical validation is needed [21].

LncRNAs and circRNAs also play a direct role in regulating mRNA stability. For instance, the lncRNA HOTAIR binds to HK2 mRNA, positively influencing its stability. This interaction subsequently promotes glycogen synthesis and proliferation in lung cancer cells by upregulating HK2 transcription [12]. Sevoflurane inhibits HOTAIR’s stabilizing effect on HK2 mRNA in an m⁶A -dependent manner, blocking this oncogenic pathway and suppressing glycolysis and cell proliferation in lung cancer.

Interaction with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs)

RBPs are pivotal in dictating mRNA’s fate, influencing its splicing, nuclear export, localization, stability, and translation [63]. The intricate interplay between ncRNAs and RBPs can directly modulate the stability and translation of mRNAs involved in glycolysis [64]. In the context of lung cancer, several RBPs have been identified for their interactions with ncRNAs in regulating glycolysis. Human Antigen R (HuR), an RBP known for stabilizing mRNA, binds to various RNA molecules to modulate protein expression patterns [65]. For instance, circDCUN1D4, which is notably downregulated in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tissues, directly interacts with HuR. This interaction facilitates HuR’s translocation to the cytoplasm, leading to the stabilization of thioxanthine-interacting protein (TXNIP) mRNA. TXNIP is a crucial negative regulator of glucose metabolism, and its stabilization consequently inhibits glycolysis and glucose transport in LUAD [66]. This research suggests that targeting circDCUN1D4 could offer therapeutic benefits for LUAD, particularly in patients with metastatic forms of the disease, although further comprehensive investigation is required.

As the most abundant chemical modification in eukaryotic mRNAs, m⁶A plays a critical in regulating mRNA stability, splicing, and translation [64]. Its influence on mRNA stability and translational efficiency is mediated by a sophisticated system involving “writers” (e.g., methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3)), “erasers” (e.g., FTO), and “readers” (such as IGF2BP and YTHDF). While these molecules are fundamentally RBPs, their recognition of mRNA is indirect, relying on the presence of m⁶A modification sites on the mRNA [67]. IGF2BP2, a key RBP involved in metabolic reprogramming, directly stabilizes the mRNA of metabolism-related enzymes and transcription factors, thereby influencing cancer progression [68]. Studies indicate that circDHTKD1 binds IGF2BP, while PFKL binds IGF2BP2. circDHTKD1 promotes glycolysis in NSCLC by recruiting IGF2BP2 to stabilize PFKL [69]. METTL3, acting as a core writer, stabilizes the expression of metabolism-related mRNAs, such as HK2 and GLUT1, thereby promoting glycolysis and ATP production in cancer [64]. The lncRNA DLGAP1-AS2 is significantly upregulated in lung cancer and associated with a poor prognosis. When regulated by METTL3, its stability increases, and it synergistically stabilizes c-Myc mRNA with YTHDF1. This enhances c-Myc-mediated transcription of glycolytic genes and promotes tumor growth [26]. These findings reveal the critical role of m⁶A modification-dependent regulatory networks in metabolic reprogramming in NSCLC, suggesting that targeting m⁶A may represent a potential therapeutic strategy.

As a component of the regulatory network for ceRNA

ceRNA, as proposed by Salmena et al., denotes transcripts that achieve post-transcriptional mutual regulation by competitively sharing the same miRNA [70]. Research suggests that ceRNAs regulate the expression of glycolysis-related genes, such as LDHA, HK2, and ENO1, by competitively binding to miRNAs. This competitive binding prevents miRNAs from inhibiting their mRNA targets (Table 2). The ceRNA network, therefore, establishes critical functional links between protein-coding mRNAs and non-coding RNAs, playing a vital role in glycolysis. Specifically, lncRNAs and circRNAs can bind to oncogenic miRNAs, suppressing tumor growth, or bind to tumor-suppressing miRNAs, stimulating tumor proliferation [71].

lncRNAs are an important class of ceRNAs that regulate glycolysis through the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA axis. LncRNAs act as sponges, adsorbing miRNAs and preventing them from binding to their target mRNAs. lncRNA HOXA11-AS intricately linked to adverse prognosis in lung cancer, positively modulates the expression of PKM2—a key glycolytic enzyme—by binding to miR-148b-3p, thereby driving tumor growth and metabolic reprogramming [73]. Although many studies on ceRNA mechanisms emphasize a precise “one-to-one” regulatory relationship governing miRNA-mRNA interactions, the complexity of cancer necessitates a diversity of regulatory mechanisms. For instance, Mir100hg can target both miR-15a-5p and miR-31-5p to regulate glycolysis in cancer cells, enhancing metastasis. However, overexpression of miR-15a-5p or miR-31-5p individually did not suppress the increased metastatic capacity induced by Mir100hg in lung cancer cells. Only the simultaneous targeting of both miRNAs altered their metastatic potential [81]. In the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory axis, a “one-to-many” regulatory network offers a more comprehensive understanding of tumor progression.

The circRNA-miRNA-mRNA axis also participates in this finely tuned regulatory network. Moreover, due to their stable closed-loop structure, circRNAs can function more efficiently and specifically as miRNA sponges, conferring greater stability to this network [88]. The circular RNA circRUNX1, overexpressed in NSCLC, enhances glucose metabolism and lactate production. It achieves this by binding to miR-145, which reduces miR-145’s inhibitory effect on its target gene, HK2. The subsequent buildup of lactate then promotes Treg cell infiltration and differentiation within the TME, ultimately aiding tumor cells in evading immune evasion [79]. In a related mechanism, PKM2 functions as a coactivator for HIF-1α transcription, influencing metabolic reprogramming in NSCLC cells. Exosomes from lung cancer cells release circSHKBP1, which regulates PKM2 expression by sponging miR-1294. This interaction leads to increased proliferation, migration, invasion, and glycolysis in NSCLC cells. Furthermore, circSHKBP1 accelerates macrophage polarization and recruitment, thereby acting as a tumor promoter in NSCLC [80].

The ceRNA mechanism, the most common functional form of lncRNA/circRNA, remains somewhat controversial. Under physiological conditions, the expression level of a single ceRNA is typically far lower than the combined abundance of its target miRNA and all other competing targets. Stoichiometric limitations therefore make it difficult for its sponge effect to exert significant regulatory influence [89]. Furthermore, a single miRNA typically targets hundreds of genes, which easily dilutes the competitive effect of any single ceRNA. Most current studies involve transient transfection to overexpress ceRNAs (far exceeding endogenous levels), which does not reflect true physiological conditions and may overestimate their physiological significance [90]. Subsequent validation studies should utilize genetic tools such as CRISPR to achieve endogenous-level knockouts or knock-ins, combined with absolute quantification.

Targeting glycolytic enzymes and signaling pathways

Directly linked to key enzymes in glycolysis

Due to their extensive sequences and intricate secondary structures, lncRNAs and circRNAs can adopt specific three-dimensional conformations, allowing them to directly bind to and regulate the function of enzymes involved in glycolysis [8]. For instance, circRARS is overexpressed in NSCLC and correlates with patient outcomes. Li et al. [91] showed that circRARS binds to LDHA, thereby upregulating the Warburg effect mediated by LDHA and promoting NSCLC cell proliferation. Similarly, circP4HB binds to PKM2, enhancing its tetramer formation. This increases PKM2 enzyme activity, accelerates glycolysis in LUAD, and promotes M2 macrophage phenotype conversion, ultimately regulating tumor progression [16]. Currently, research on the direct binding of lncRNA/circRNA to glycolytic enzymes primarily relies on evidence from RNA pull-down or RIP techniques, often lacking an in-depth analysis of the binding interface.

Targeting signaling pathway proteins

Beyond direct interaction with glycolytic enzymes, ncRNAs can globally reprogram cellular metabolic states by influencing core signaling pathways. For example, miR-214 and miR-21 modulate the Akt/mTOR pathway by directly targeting PTEN, which upregulates HK2 and PKM2 expression, thereby promoting NSCLC cell proliferation and glycolysis [28, 92]. ncRNA can simultaneously target glycolytic enzymes and associated signaling pathways. miR-124 not only directly suppresses the expression of glycolytic rate-limiting enzymes GLUT1 and HK2, but also negatively regulates AKT1/AKT2. Furthermore, AKT overexpression counteracts the inhibitory effects of miR-124 on both glycolysis and cell proliferation [93].

Adenylate cyclase-activated protein kinase α (AMPKα), a negative regulator of the the Warburg effect, can be directly bound and downregulated by miR-301a-3p. The application of AMPKα agonists attenuates the Warburg effect induced by miR-301a-3p in non-small cell lung cancer cells, leading to decreased expression of GLUT1, HK1, and LDHA [30]. However, this study was validated only at the cellular level and has not been verified in vivo.

HIF-1 is crucial for cellular adaptation to hypoxia, making the HIF-1α signaling pathway a significant therapeutic target in NSCLC. Combination therapy with bevacizumab and anlotinib demonstrates synergistic anti-angiogenic and antitumor effects in NSCLC through HIF-1α inhibition [94]. Conversely, circAGFG1 activates the HIF-1α signaling pathway by binding to miR-28-5p, thereby neutralizing miR-28-5p’s direct inhibitory effect on HIF-1α. This activation leads to accelerated glycolysis and promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of NSCLC cells while suppressing their apoptosis [95].

Indirect regulation of glycolysis via post-translational modifications

The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) stands as the principal pathway for protein degradation within eukaryotic cells. This intricate process involves tagging target proteins with ubiquitin, thereby marking them for subsequent degradation by the proteasome [96]. E3 ubiquitin ligases are pivotal players within this ubiquitination system. ncRNAs can modulate the ubiquitination levels and degradation rates of glycolytic enzymes indirectly. They achieve this by influencing either the activity or the substrate recognition capabilities of E3 ubiquitin ligases, ultimately leading to the activation or inhibition of the glycolytic pathway [97].

The TRIM family stands out as one of the most extensive families of monomeric E3 ubiquitin ligases. For instance, Gu et al. [98] demonstrated that TRIM50 impedes glycolysis in cancer cells. This inhibition occurs through TRIM50’s mediation of PGK1 ubiquitination and degradation, which in turn curtails cell proliferation. In a related context, the observation that circPTK2 and TRIM16 expression levels are low, while miR-94 expression is high in NSCLC tissues, is particularly significant, especially given that circRNAs can function as ceRNAs to modulate E3 ubiquitin ligase expression. Subsequent research elucidated that circPTK2 suppresses glycolysis by regulating the miR-942/TRIM16 axis, thereby diminishing NSCLC resistance to cisplatin. This suggests that upregulating miR-942 or inhibiting TRIM16 could potentially counteract the effects of cyclin PTK2 overexpression, offering a new therapeutic avenue for NSCLC [99]. Along similar lines, circ_0006324 positively influences TRIM59 expression by targeting miR-496. Since TRIM59 is primarily involved in degrading tumor suppressor proteins, circ_0006324 functions as an oncogenic promoter in NSCLC via the miR-496/TRIM59 axis, promoting cell proliferation and glycolysis while simultaneously hindering apoptosis [85]. However, none of the above studies have definitively excluded the possibility that TRIM16/59 exerts an indirect influence on glycolysis through the degradation of other metabolism-related proteins.

Mediating metabolic communication within the tumor microenvironment

Exosome-mediated remote regulation

Metabolic reprogramming in tumors is associated not only with cancer cell characteristics but also with the TME. Exosomes secreted by tumor or stromal cells, enriched with specific ncRNAs, can remotely control metabolic reprogramming.

Exosomes, a prominent class of extracellular vesicles, transport ncRNA, proteins, and lipids, facilitating the intercellular transfer of functional molecules [100]. Exosomes secreted by stromal cells within the TME contain specific ncRNAs that function as long-range messengers, regulating glycolysis and promoting the progression of lung cancer. CAF (Cancer-associated fibroblast)-derived exosomes target the 3’-UTR of kruppel-like factor 13 (KLF13) in NSCLC cells via miR-3126-5p, downregulating its expression, which transcriptionally activates Src homology 2B adaptor protein 1 (SH2B1). SH2B1 interacts with insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1), which then activates the PI3K/AKT pathway to enhance glycolysis, accelerating NSCLC progression [101]. Macrophage-derived exosomes (MDEs) are key factors influencing cisplatin chemotherapy resistance in lung cancer, exhibiting heightened expression levels of multiple miRNAs. miR-3679-5p, conveyed to lung cancer cells via MDEs, downregulates expression of the E3 ligase NEDD4L—a pivotal regulator influencing c-Myc stability—thereby promoting glycolysis and driving chemotherapy resistance in lung cancer [102]. Concurrently, MDEs deliver miR-6879-5p to recipient cells, where it targets and inhibits TRIM26, an E3 ubiquitin ligase. This inhibition subsequently diminishes TRIM26-mediated ubiquitination of the PKM enzyme, thereby promoting aerobic glycolysis [103].

Lung cancer cells not only receive signals from the TME but also secrete exosomal ncRNA to reshape it. Lei et al. [81] discovered that the lncRNA Mir100hg, exhibiting high expression within lung cancer stem cells, is capable of being transferred to non-stem lung cancer cells via exosomes. Mir100hg regulates ALDOA expression through direct targeting of miR-15a-5p and miR-31-5p, which enhances glycolysis in non-stem cells and consequently promotes their metastatic potential. In contrast, Mir100hg acts as a protein scaffold, facilitating the interaction between ALDOA (Aldolase, fructose-bisphosphate A) and the deubiquitinase OTUD4 (OTU Domain-Containing Protein 4). This interaction inhibits ALDOA modification, leading to increased protein stability and abundance. The resulting elevation in lactate levels further enhances H3K14 lactylation, thereby upregulating the transcription of genes associated with metastasis [104].

Regulating the metabolism of stromal cells

ncRNAs exert direct regulatory control over the metabolism of stromal cells within the TME, consequently exerting an indirect influence on the progression of lung cancer. CAFs, for instance, demonstrate elevated glucose uptake and lactate production compared to normal fibroblasts. The metabolic byproducts secreted by these CAFs, particularly lactate, drive cancer cells to adopt a metabolism predominantly characterized by oxidative phosphorylation. This phenomenon, termed the “reverse Warburg effect,” furnishes cancer cells with an ample supply of metabolic fuel, thereby augmenting their proliferative and invasive capabilities [105]. Research indicates that a reduction in miR-181c expression within CAFs alleviates its inhibitory action on HK2, leading to an increase in the glycolytic activity of CAFs [106]. Furthermore, CAFs exhibiting high levels of miR-21 expression display a highly glycolytic state. Nevertheless, this metabolic profile is disrupted when these CAFs are co-cultured with a miR-21 inhibitor [107]. While these investigations underscore the pivotal function of ncRNAs in the metabolic restructuring of stromal cells, the precise degree to which this impact triggers metabolic alterations in vivo warrants further investigation.

Clinical applications of glycolysis-related ncRNAs in lung cancer

Current lung cancer research focuses on developing biomarkers for diagnosis and detection, prognostic indicators, and therapeutic technologies. ncRNAs that regulate tumor glycolysis are emerging as key factors influencing lung cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment [88] (Fig. 4).

As a biomarker for diagnosis and prognosis

Currently, there remains no specific method available for the early diagnosis of lung cancer, and accurate prevention and treatment remain elusive [108]. Therefore, identifying early diagnostic biomarkers for lung cancer is crucial. Several ncRNAs have shown promise as biomarkers in studies examining tumor cell suppression and glycolysis. Advanced diagnostic technologies now offer cancer patients sophisticated methods like liquid biopsy and quantitative detection of exosomal ncRNA. For instance, research indicates that miR-210, a ncRNA linked to glycolysis, is expressed at significantly higher levels in NSCLC tissues compared to normal tissues (p < 0.001). Its presence in serum and sputum also holds considerable diagnostic promise, with area under the curve values of 0.82 and 0.81, respectively [109]. Furthermore, elevated expression of specific ncRNAs, such as HOXA11-AS and FAM83A-AS1, in lung cancer has been shown to regulate tumor metabolism and foster tumor progression, positioning them as potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers [73, 110, 111]. Nevertheless, significant variability exists in the levels of these upregulated ncRNAs found in easily accessible blood samples from NSCLC patients. This variability is compounded by discrepancies arising from different detection platforms and experimental protocols, leading to inconsistencies across studies. Current validation frequently relies on RT-qPCR. Future advancements should prioritize absolute quantification methods (e.g., ddPCR) to establish more standardized and reliable workflows. Additionally, the inherently low abundance of certain circulating ncRNAs presents a challenge for detection sensitivity; therefore, exploring highly sensitive nanobiosensors could offer a feasible direction [112]. Ultimately, for these ncRNAs to become dependable biomarkers for clinical decision-making, a comprehensive series of challenges, spanning both technical and clinical validation, must be successfully addressed.

ncRNAs associated with glycolysis that are upregulated in lung cancer can serve as prognostic markers for patients. For example, the lncRNA-AC020978, which is upregulated in NSCLC, correlates significantly with advanced TNM stages and poor clinical outcomes. Mechanistically, it regulates PKM2 and enhances HIF-1α transcriptional activity, thereby conferring an invasive phenotype to NSCLC. Consequently, lncRNA-AC020978 functions as an independent prognostic predictor in lung cancer [15]. The ongoing development of prognostic models, facilitated by advances in bioinformatics, has the potential to predict disease progression, enable early intervention and prevention, and provide novel insights to inform initial treatment decisions in cancer therapy [113]. Lung cancer patients exhibit elevated LDHA gene expression levels, which correlates with poorer prognosis and overall survival. Research by Nema et al. [114] utilized bioinformatics to identify two key genes co-expressed with LDHA. Employing the ceRNA network mechanism, they constructed a TMPO-AS1-let-7c-5p-LDHA RNA network to predict prognosis in lung cancer patients, suggesting a potential regulatory role in oxygen-dependent glycolysis within LUAD. Beyond single biomarkers, multi-omics integrative analysis offers improved predictive performance. In a prospective study, multi-omics factor analysis integrating plasma cfDNA methylation and exosomal miRNA profiles defined molecular subtypes associated with immunotherapy survival outcomes in NSCLC patients, with validation in an external cohort [115]. This approach highlights the potential of incorporating glycolysis-related ncRNAs into multi-omics frameworks to enhance predictive accuracy.

Modulating drug resistance and radiation sensitivity

Accumulating data indicate that ncRNAs play a role in two critical facets of cancer progression: metabolic reprogramming such as glycolysis, and the mediation of drug resistance. Specifically, ncRNAs involved in glycolysis can influence resistance to cancer chemotherapy by affecting tumor cell malignancy when glycolysis is induced. For example, research has revealed that the lncRNA RP11-544M22.13 is elevated in cisplatin (DDP)-resistant NSCLC tissues. It primarily promotes lung cancer cell resistance to DDP and malignant behavior by upregulating GLUT1 expression through direct interaction with GLUT1 mRNA [76]. Similarly, circAKT3 reduces the sensitivity of lung cancer cells to cisplatin by regulating glycolytic balance via the miR-516b-5p/STAT3 axis [116]. Conversely, circFOXO3, which is highly expressed in lung cancer, increases DDP sensitivity in NSCLC cells. There is currently a paucity of research regarding alterations in ncRNA expression within chemotherapy-induced non-small cell lung cancer. Expanded research in this domain could position ncRNAs as viable biomarkers for tracking chemotherapy effectiveness. Furthermore, targeting ncRNAs involved in glucose metabolism could facilitate the creation of small-molecule drugs for combination chemotherapy, particularly for patients with advanced, chemotherapy-resistant disease, thereby potentially enhancing their immune microenvironment.

Radiation-resistant cells demonstrate elevated glycolysis compared to their radiation-sensitive cells [24]. Suppressing PKM2 expression through shRNA (short hairpin RNA)-PKM2 treatment has been shown to augment radiosensitivity in both NSCLC cell lines and xenografts [117]. This suggests that modulating glycolysis could be a strategy to increase the radiosensitivity of cancer cells. Research shows that miR-449a expression is downregulated in lung cancer. However, its expression increases in cancer cells as radiation doses escalate. MiR-449a interacts with the 3’ UTR of LDHA mRNA, suppressing LDHA expression and subsequent glycolysis. This process enhances the radiation sensitivity of lung cancer cells [17]. Studies have shown that radiation-resistant cancer cells exhibit significantly increased expression of miR-21 and decreased expression of miR-133b. This is because miR-21 upregulates HIF-1α, which in turn promotes the expression of key glycolytic enzymes. Furthermore, inhibiting HIF-1α restores radiation sensitivity in these cancer cells, suggesting that miR-21 may regulate radiation resistance through the upregulation of HIF-1α [24]. Conversely, miR-133b targets PKM2, inhibiting glycolysis mediated by this enzyme and rendering cancer cells sensitive to radiation [14]. These studies demonstrate that ncRNA-driven alterations in gene expression affect glycolysis, thereby influencing the radiation sensitivity and drug tolerance of lung cancer cells. Therefore, targeting ncRNAs represents a novel approach to developing anti-radiation drugs.

ncRNA as a therapeutic target

An important consideration is the impact of anticancer drugs on glycolysis. However, because nearly all cells undergo glycolysis, directly targeting this pathway necessitates a high degree of tumor specificity. Several studies have explored the role of anticancer agents in controlling glycolysis by altering non-coding RNA expression. For example, β-elemene, a bioactive diterpene isolated from the traditional Chinese medicinal herb Curcuma wenyujin, has demonstrated inhibitory effects on the progression of various cancers, including lung cancer [118]. Research has revealed that β-elemene suppresses chemotherapy resistance by downregulating oncogenic miR-301a-3p or lncRNA LINC00511, thereby activating AMPKα or inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, ultimately inhibiting glycolysis [30, 32]. Epigenetically modified ncRNAs exhibit altered metabolic effects in cancer [119]. Sevoflurane reduces lncRNA HOTAIR stability via epigenetic transcription (m⁶A) modification, leading to decreased expression of the glycolytic key enzyme HK2 and inhibiting tumor proliferation [12]. In addition to its anesthetic properties, propofol can suppress malignant tumor progression. It achieves this by downregulating circTADA2A or circ-ERBB2, which then releases miR-455-3p or miR-7-5p. These microRNAs inhibit FOXM1 (Forkhead Box M1), a downstream glycolytic factor. Both intracellular and extracellular studies suggest that propofol disrupts the cellular energy metabolism of lung cancer cells, thereby suppressing tumor growth [85, 120]. Furthermore, dauriporphine—the primary component of Manispernum daericum DC.— induces high expression of miR-424-5p in lung cancer cells, thereby suppressing its target MAPK14 and weakening cancer cell metabolic activity and growth capacity. In vitro studies indicate dauriporphine holds potential for drug development against lung adenocarcinoma [121]. These studies demonstrate that targeting key glycolytic genes or pathways via drug-mediated delivery of ncRNA suppresses lung cancer growth. This offers a novel approach to developing antimetabolic drugs that specifically target glycolysis.

The studies mentioned previously (involving β-elemene, sevoflurane, propofol, among others) all utilize small-molecule compounds characterized by intrinsic transmembrane permeability and in vivo stability. Investigations employing gene editing to knockout or silence oncogenic ncRNAs, alongside oligonucleotide-based therapy (ONT) strategies such as small interfering RNA (siRNA) or shRNA, have demonstrated the antitumor efficacy of targeting glycolysis-related ncRNAs in vitro [122]. For example, silencing circSHKBP1 significantly reduces glucose uptake and lactate production in NSCLC cells, concurrently downregulating PKM2 expression and the transcriptional activity of HIF-1α. However, this particular study did not establish a clinical protocol for systemic administration [80]. This limitation stems from a fundamental challenge in using ncRNA itself as a therapeutic agent: in vivo delivery. Unprotected ncRNA molecules are highly susceptible to degradation by nucleases in the bloodstream. Furthermore, following systemic administration, they cannot passively enter target cells, which leads to off-target effects [123]. Consequently, they necessitate delivery vectors. Thus, achieving efficient, specific, and safe targeted delivery remains the primary challenge in this field.

Research into ncRNA therapeutics has predominantly focused on chemical modifications and nanoparticle delivery systems, with numerous clinical trials and preclinical studies already conducted in lung cancer (Table 3). Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) currently represent one of the more established nucleic acid delivery systems. Nevertheless, many LNPs exhibit potential toxicity, lack active targeting capabilities, and tend to accumulate in the liver [124]. For example, MRX34, an LNP-encapsulated miR-34a mimetic, was withdrawn from a Phase I clinical trial due to severe immune-related adverse events. This outcome may have been partly attributed to non-specific distribution resulting from passive targeting [125]. To address this issue, one study employed modified LNPs (surface-conjugated with the ligand ephrin-A1) to deliver the tumor suppressor gene let-7a to lung cancer cells expressing the EphA2 receptor for active targeting; let-7a can indirectly downregulate glycolysis by regulating c-Myc [126]. While this approach enhances delivery efficiency, it compromises particle stability. Moreover, whether this modification strategy can be applied to deliver miRNAs that directly target glycolytic enzymes has not yet been reported and requires further validation. Cell-derived vesicles, such as exosomes, have emerged as promising carriers due to their inherent low immunogenicity and lung tropism. However, they remain limited by various technical obstacles concerning isolation, purification, scale-up, and drug loading techniques [127]. Therefore, exosome-lipid hybrid nanoparticles (ELNs) emerge as a highly promising next-generation delivery system, synergizing the inherent targeting capabilities of exosomes with the high delivery efficiency characteristic of LNPs [128]. However, the feasibility of this model has not yet been validated and requires further optimization.

Drug delivery systems and clinical trials targeting ncRNAs

In summary, although therapeutic strategies targeting glycolysis-associated ncRNAs have shown promise in vitro and in animal models, their clinical translation faces significant obstacles, including targeting efficiency, immunogenicity, and the scalability of in vivo delivery systems. Currently, no drugs specifically targeting glycolytic ncRNAs have entered clinical trials for lung cancer. Therefore, emerging delivery systems warrant further investigation, focusing on the core question of how to efficiently, safely, and specifically deliver tumor-suppressing ncRNAs to lung cancer cells to inhibit glycolysis.

Conclusion and outlook

Glycolysis is a central area in cancer metabolism research with ncRNAs, playing a crucial regulatory role in tumor glycolytic pathways. These ncRNAs regulate glycolysis in lung cancer at transcriptional (epigenetic modifications, transcription factor interactions), post-transcriptional (mRNA stability, ceRNA networks, RNA-binding protein interactions), and post-translational levels (enzyme binding, ubiquitination-mediated degradation), as well as through metabolic exchanges in the tumor microenvironment (exosomes, stromal remodeling). These interconnected mechanisms form a complex network influencing lung cancer proliferation, metastasis, and immune evasion.

Despite extensive research on glycolysis-associated ncRNAs, the field is in early stages with unresolved fundamental questions. Most studies rely on unvalidated overexpression/knockdown assays, lack rigorous stoichiometric validation for ceRNA mechanisms, and provide insufficient detail on ncRNA protein-binding sites. Functional redundancy and compensatory mechanisms among ncRNAs are scarcely explored, and current cellular and animal models fail to accurately reflect the lung cancer microenvironment, necessitating in vivo validation. While ncRNAs offer advantages as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets due to their unique regulatory mechanisms, clinical translation is hindered by a lack of standardized detection protocols and uncertainties in in vivo delivery system targeting and stability. Though no ncRNA-targeting drugs for lung cancer are in clinical trials, preclinical studies and delivery strategies offer valuable insights for future trials.

Emerging technologies are expected to address these research gaps. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics can reveal ncRNA expression heterogeneity across cell subpopulations and niches, preserving spatial localization to elucidate region-specific glycolytic regulation. Spatial metabolomics can directly localize ncRNA-associated metabolic changes, linking molecular regulation to metabolic phenotypes. High-throughput CRISPR-based functional screening can systematically dissect ncRNA roles in glycolysis and cancer progression, more accurately reflecting the in vivo metabolic environment. Artificial intelligence and machine learning will integrate multi-omics datasets to predict core ncRNA nodes, construct patient-specific regulatory networks, and identify combination therapy targets. These combined technologies will reshape our understanding of ncRNA-glycolysis networks and accelerate their application in precision oncology.

In summary, glycolysis-associated ncRNAs offer not only a crucial window into uncovering the metabolic reprogramming in lung cancer but also new avenues for developing novel diagnostic biomarkers and targeted therapeutics. Although there are currently many challenges to clinical translation, with the integration of RNA biology and metabolic research, targeting the ncRNA-glycolysis axis holds promise as a key component of next-generation precision therapies for lung cancer.
