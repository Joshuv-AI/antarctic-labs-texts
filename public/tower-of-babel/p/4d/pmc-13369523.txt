Performance of a Monthly, Reusable, Soft Contact Lens in Participants Who Previously Dropped Out of Contact Lenses
Authors: Andrew D. Pucker, Gina Wesley, Shane Kannarr, Kristopher May, Jason Miller, Morgan Bromley, Quentin X. Franklin, Elyse Rayborn, Chris Lievens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo refit contact lens (CL) dropouts who dropped out of CLs because of dryness or discomfort into lehfilcon A (TOTAL30®) monthly, spherical CLs to determine the frequency that they can be comfortably refitted into CLs.MethodsA 6-month, four-visit, multi-centre, open-label, single-arm, six-site study was conducted in the United States. Adults, 18- to 40-year-old, past CL wearers who dropped out of reusable CLs at least 3 months but not more than 2 years ago because of discomfort or dryness were enrolled. Participants were required to have no significant dry eye symptoms (SPEED scores ≤3). All participants were then fitted into lehfilcon A CLs. The participants were evaluated with the SPEED, a comfort visual analogue scale (VAS; 0–100 scale) and a Likert questionnaire.ResultsThis study enrolled 61 participants who had a median (interquartile) age of 28 (7) years (78.7% female). The participants had a right eye median (IQR) refractive error (sphere/cylinder power) of −1.50 D (2.50 D)/−0.50 D (0.50 D). This study had a CL retention rate of 98.4% at 1 month and 65.6% at 6 months. The VAS yielded a median comfort score of 81 (40) at 6 months. The Likert questionnaire found that 80.7% of participants would recommend the CLs to a friend at 6 months. SPEED scores increased statistically after resuming CL wear (p = 0.0001) at 1 month, yet this symptom change was unlikely to be clinically meaningful.ConclusionsThis study found that previous CL wearers who had dropped out of CLs could be comfortably refitted with lehfilcon A monthly replacement, silicone hydrogel CLs, with most of them continuing to wear the CLs after 6 months of daily wear. The results suggest that the monthly soft CLs with water gradient technology can be a viable option for many CL dropout sufferers.

Refitting contact lens dropouts with lehfilcon A lenses enabled most participants to resume wear successfully, supporting alternative material selection as a strategy to reduce contact lens dropout rates meaningfully.Compared with prior daily disposable studies, monthly lens wearers showed slightly lower 6-month retention and satisfaction, suggesting replacement schedule and lens care complexity may influence long-term comfort and adherence.Despite daily wear times with the study lenses being below traditional benchmarks for successful wear, participants demonstrated functional improvement, transitioning from lapsed status to having several hours of comfortable wear.

Introduction

Contact lens (CL) discomfort is a complex phenomenon that has been defined by the Tear Film and Ocular Surface Society in their seminal report on CL discomfort as a condition that results in ‘episodic or persistent adverse ocular sensation related to lens wear, either with or without visual disturbance resulting from reduced compatibility between the CL and the environment, which can lead to decreased wearing time and discontinuation of CL wear’ [1]. While CL dropout occurs in about 20% of habitual wearers annually, with the top reason for ending CL wear being ocular discomfort [2], the majority of CL wearers who cease wearing their lenses have a desire to return to wearing CLs [3], and it is important to give interested patients the opportunity to resume CL wear as it may afford improved quality of life and satisfaction versus those who only wear spectacles [4–6]. CLs can specifically offer improved cosmesis and self-perception, especially among patients with high refractive errors [5–7], and can provide better visual freedom for those with active or physically demanding lifestyles [8]. Thus, providing CL dropouts with an alternative option, which may be more comfortable than their past CLs, could be a means to help reduce lens dropout and improve the lives of struggling CL wearers.

The authors’ recent work found that when 60 participants who had previously dropped out of CLs were given the opportunity to be refitted with a daily disposable CL, 100% of them were still wearing the new CL at 1 month and 98% of them were still wearing their CLs at 6 months [9]. While these data suggest that a CL dropout can thrive when being refitted into a daily disposable CL, the literature currently lacks a targeted study aimed at understanding how CL dropouts will respond to being refitted with a monthly replacement, daily wear CL. One option in this category is lehfilcon A (TOTAL30®; Alcon, alcon.com), which is a monthly replacement, silicone hydrogel CL with water gradient surface technology. This CL material incorporates biomimetic properties that give the CL the potential to mimic the structure of the corneal surface; past work has shown that this CL can provide a comfortable wearing experience for the full life of the lens and for the full CL wear day [10, 11]. The purpose of this study was to refit CL dropouts who lapsed from CL wear because of dryness or discomfort into lehfilcon A monthly replacement, spherical CLs to determine the frequency that they can be comfortably refitted with CLs at 1 and 6 months.

Methods

Participants

This was a 6-month, multi-centre, open-label study that was conducted at six sites in the United States. The primary outcome was evaluated at 1 month. One month was the primary outcome time point because Young et al. found that most participants who dropped out of CLs dropped out by 1 month (23% dropout) [3]. The study was approved by the Southern College of Optometry’s Institutional Review Board (IRB), and it complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was first submitted to ClinicalTrials.gov on February 21, 2025 (NCT06967298). Adults, 18- to 40-years-old, past CL wearers who had a best-corrected visual acuity of 0.00 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) or better were recruited (Snellen equivalent of 6/6). Participants were required to have worn reusable CLs for at least 1 year before they became a CL dropout. Participants over the age of 40 years were excluded to avoid presbyopia-related vision issues. All participants were required to have dropped out of CLs within the past 2 years because of discomfort or dryness (self-report) [9]. This determination was made by listing Young et al.’s reasons for dropping out of CLs and asking the participants to indicate their top reason [3]. Participants were considered a CL dropout if they had not worn CLs in the past 3 months. Participants were required to have scores of ≤3 on the Standard Patient Evaluation of Eye Dryness (SPEED) questionnaire (no significant dry eye symptoms) [12]. The SPEED questionnaire was selected because it has been validated in both CL and non-CL wearers [13]. Clinical Testing

After reporting to the baseline visit (Fig. 1), participants were asked to complete the informed consent process after being pre-screened by the investigator with an IRB-approved eligibility survey. All enrolled participants were then screened with the SPEED questionnaire to understand their initial eye comfort (scores ≤3 required) [13]. All relevant participants’ demographics were collected via a questionnaire developed by the investigators (age, sex, race, ethnicity). Participants were then asked to complete a manifest refraction including binocular balance, where the investigator determined the participants’ refractive error with a phoropter and best-corrected visual acuity with a high contrast Bailey-Lovie logMAR chart. No more than 1.00 D of sphere was added beyond the initial blur balance starting point. The investigator then used a slit-lamp biomicroscope to document normal and/or remarkable findings of the anterior eye structures: eyelashes, eyelids, conjunctiva and cornea. The participants were then fitted with the study CLs. The CLs were evaluated for centration, movement, coverage and power. CL power adjustments were only made if they improved visual acuity. A hydrogen peroxide-based care system (CLEAR CARE® PLUS; Alcon, alcon.com) was dispensed to every participant. Both the study CLs and care system were provided to the participants at no cost for the duration of the 6-month study. Participants were then asked to return to clinic 1 week later for an initial compliance and safety check. Participants were evaluated with the SPEED questionnaire and their high contrast visual acuity while wearing the study CLs. The investigator then used a slit-lamp biomicroscope to assess ocular health as described above. The study CLs were evaluated for centration, movement and coverage, and CL power adjustments were only made if they improved visual acuity. If a participant needed a CL adjustment, they were required to wait a full month before their 1-month follow-up visit. Participants were then asked to report for a visit scheduled about 1 month after they started wearing the study CL. During the 1-month follow-up visit, participants were asked to complete the SPEED questionnaire, a slit-lamp biomicroscope examination and a CL evaluation as described above, and they were also asked to complete a series of investigator-developed questions to evaluate the CL wearing experience. The questionnaire included both Likert questions as well as a visual analogue scale (VAS) question that used a 0–100 scale where 100 represented the best possible comfort, 0 represented the worst possible comfort and 50 represented neutral [10]. The participants were then compensated for their time, and they were released from the clinical segment of the study, at which time they were instructed to return to their normal care provider. Participants were later electronically contacted 6 months after the baseline visit, and they were asked the same VAS and Likert questions to determine their long-term CL status. CL retention was defined as participants who were still wearing the CLs at a given time point.

Sample Size and Statistical Analysis Plan

All data were collected with Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap; Vanderbilt; project-redcap.org) [14, 15], and the data were analysed with Stata/BE 18 (StataCorp LLC; stata.com). This study aimed to understand if CL dropouts could be comfortably refitted into lehfilcon A CLs. The primary outcome of this study was the VAS comfort scores at the 1-month visit. This study did not have a formal sample size calculation, given that the study’s primary outcome was based on a single arm and was not comparative. Thus, a sample of 60 participants was recruited because it was deemed a reasonable and feasible number of participants who could be recruited over a 6-month period. The number of participants was likewise deemed sufficient to begin to understand the qualitative experience of lapsed CL wearers due to discomfort who were being refitted with new CLs based upon a past study conducted by the investigators [9].

The data were assumed to be non-normally distributed; therefore, continuous variables were summarised with medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical results are summarised as percentages. Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to compare continuous variables across the life of the study. Comparisons were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

This study enroled 61 participants who had a median (IQR) age of 28 (7) years, and 78.7% of the participants were female (Table 2). The participants identified as Asian (16.4%), Black or African American (9.8%), White (67.2%), More than One Race (4.9%) or Unknown or Not Reported (1.6%), with 1.6% of the participants identifying as Hispanic. Only one participant was lost to follow-up before the 1-month visit, and only two additional participants were lost to follow-up at 6 months. The participants had a median (IQR) refractive error (sphere/cylinder power) in their right eyes of −1.50 D (2.50 D)/−0.50 D (0.50 D) and −1.75 D (2.75 D)/−0.50 D (0.50 D) in their left eyes. The participants had right and left eye baseline, best-corrected visual acuities of 0.00 (0.10) logMAR and 0.00 (0.10) logMAR, respectively. The participants had finalised CL powers of −1.75 D (2.25 D) and −2.00 D (2.25 D) in their right and left eyes, respectively, at baseline, and only one participant required a CL power change of 0.25 D at the 1-week visit in one eye. The participants had right and left eye baseline CL visual acuities of 0.00 (0.10) logMAR and 0.00 (0.10) logMAR, respectively. Visual acuity with CLs did not change significantly over the course of the study (all p ≥ 0.64). At 1 month, the participants indicated that they wore their CLs a median of 10.0 (4.0) h/day over the past week, that they had a median of 8.0 (2.0) h/day of comfortable wear over the past week and that they wore their CLs for a median of 5 (2) days over the past week. At 6 months, the participants indicated that they wore their CLs a median of 9.5 (12.0) h/day over the past week, that they had a median of 6.0 (10.0) h/day of comfortable wear over the past week and that they wore their CLs for a median of 4 (6) days over the past week. All the participants who were still in the study at 1 month were still wearing the CLs, and the overall CL retention rate at 1 month was 98.4% (60/61 participants). However, by 6 months, 18 of the 58 participants (three participants lost to follow-up) who completed the survey were no longer wearing the CLs. This resulted in an overall CL retention rate of 65.6% (40/61 participants) at 6 months.

The study’s primary outcome was CL comfort at the 1-month visit as measured with the same questions in two different formats. The first approach used a 0–100 VAS, which yielded comfortable median comfort scores at both the 1-month (85 [15] units) and 6-month (81 [40] units) timepoints. The second approach used the same question in Likert form, with 79.3% of participants indicating that they were either very satisfied or satisfied with their CL comfort over the past week, and only 10.4% of the participants indicated that they were either very unsatisfied or unsatisfied with their CL comfort over the past week at 1 month (Table 3). These data are supported by the participants having a baseline SPEED score of 2.0 (3.0) with SPEED scores of 3.5 (5.5) and 4.0 (5.5) at the 1-week and 1-month visits, respectively; nevertheless, SPEED scores did get significantly worse after resuming CL wear (p = 0.0001). This study also found that 43% of participants had comfortable SPEED scores (scores ≤3) at 1 month [12]. Additionally, the participants reported that they were very satisfied or satisfied with their vision (87.1%) and CL comfort at the end of the day (75.4%) over the past week at 1 month. Likewise, they indicated that they were very likely or likely to continue wearing their CLs on a daily basis (72.4%) and 87.0% of the participants indicated that they would recommend the study CLs to a friend at 1 month. The participants indicated that they strongly agreed or agreed that the study CLs allowed them to participate in fitness activities more easily (49.4%), allowed them to use digital devices more easily (49.1%), allowed them to be able to complete general daily activities more easily (63.7%), allowed them to feel more self-confident (57.2%), allowed them to feel less eye fatigue (46.8%), allowed them to feel happier (50.7%) and allowed them to feel less eyestrain (52.0%) at 1 month (Table 3). Discussion

This study describes the frequency of past CL wearers who can be comfortably refitted into reusable, daily wear, monthly replacement, silicone hydrogel soft CLs. These data provide credence for fitting struggling soft CL patients, or patients who stopped wearing CLs, into an alternative CL, which could help curb the frequency of CL dropout and provide patients with an opportunity to continue wearing CLs. This study found that participants could be refitted in the study CL, with only three participants being lost to follow-up over this 6-month study and with the majority of participants indicating that they were satisfied with their CL comfort over the past week at 1 and 6 months. Likewise, this study found an overall high CL retention rate at 1 month that decreased at 6 months. While not every participant in this study was able to achieve comfortable CL wear until they removed the CLs, this study’s results are promising, given that all participants were former CL wearers. Anecdotally, it is not uncommon for practitioners to defer to daily disposable CLs when managing patients at high risk of dropout or already dropped out due to discomfort. This study suggests that patients who previously discontinued reusable monthly replacement CLs can be comfortably refitted with lehfilcon A CLs.

CL dropout has been a recognised issue for decades [16–20], and unfortunately the frequency of patients electing to stop wearing CLs has been stable over this time period, even with the advent of optimised CL materials, daily disposable CLs and the development of new pharmaceutical agents [2, 21]. One of the most promising studies related to curbing CL dropout was conducted by Young et al., who refitted 236 CL dropouts who dropped out of CL at least 1 year ago into any alternative CL brand that was suitable to the participant’s needs [3]. This study included a combination of CL materials (silicone hydrogel, hydrogel, rigid gas permeable) and CL designs (sphere, toric, bifocal). Interestingly, the authors found that 77% of participants could be successfully refitted into CLs at the 1 month visit, and 74% of participants were still wearing the CLs after 6 months [3]. More recently Lievens et al. refitted 60 CL dropouts with the same entry criteria as the current study into a single brand of daily disposable CLs (Dailies TOTAL1®, delefilcon A; Alcon, alcon.com) and found that 100% of participants were able to wear the study CLs successfully for 1 month, and all but one participant were still wearing the CLs after 6 months [9]. The current investigation came to a similar result as these past studies, with the current investigation finding that 98.4% of participants were able to wear the study CLs for 1 month and 65.6% of participants were still wearing the study CLs at 6 months. Some potential reasons for why there was a lower CL retention rate in monthly CL wearers compared to daily disposable CL wearers could be related to the fact that daily disposable CLs offer a fresh CL every day or because daily disposable CLs are simpler to manage compared with monthly CLs (e.g., no care system is required for daily disposable CLs).

Furthermore, the current study found that the monthly CL wearers were slightly less satisfied with the comfort (75.4 vs. 78.0%) and with the vision in the study CLs (87.1 vs. 93.2%) at 1 month compared to Lievens et al.’s work using delefilcon A daily disposable CLs [9]. A similar trend was also noticed when comparing the 6-month data. Interestingly, satisfaction and the participant’s willingness to recommend the study CL remained high, even though only about 65% of the participants were still wearing their CL at the 6-month time point. While it is hard to say why this discordance between CL retention and satisfaction occurred, it could be because the participant’s perceived the study CLs to be better than their past CLs. The current study also found that SPEED scores got significantly worse over the first month after reinitiating CL wear compared to baseline, yet this worsening was unlikely to be at a clinically significant level, given that the median changes were only 1.5–2.0 units and because the SPEED scores remained low at 1-month [12]. The likely reason for the difference between the current findings and Lievens et al.’s work is that the current study evaluated a monthly, reusable CL while Lievens et al.’s work evaluated a daily disposable CL [9]. These results suggest that previous soft CL dropouts should be closely monitored during the first 6 months of wear after being refitted, particularly when being refitted into a monthly CL. If monthly CL wearers are unhappy, they may benefit from being switched to a different CL or, more specifically, a daily disposable CL.

The current study likewise evaluated CL wearing time as a measure of successful wear. At 1 month, participants were able to wear their CLs a median of 10.0 (4.0) h/day over the past week, had a median of 8.0 (2.0) h/day of comfortable wear over the past week and wore their CLs for a median of 5 (2) days over the past week. These results were similar at 6 months, though the values were shorter for each metric. Terry et al. suggested that a successful CL wearer should be able to wear their lenses for at least 12 h/day for at least 6 days per week [22]. While the median participant in the current study did not meet these criteria, the participants did have a dramatic improvement in their ability to wear CLs compared to their past CLs, as highlighted by the participants going from being lapsed CL wearers to being able to wear CLs again on a regular basis

While this study had several strengths, which include the standardisation of offering the same monthly CL, enrolling a robust number of CL dropouts and evaluating the CL wearing experience with multiple instruments, this study is not without limitations. Although using a single CL material that was provided by the study at no cost to the participant is a strength in that it provides consistency among participants, it is also a limitation because the study results can only be applied directly to the material and design tested, and the free CLs may have slightly biased the results in the positive direction. Therefore, a similar study could be repeated with alternative CL brands and/or designs to make the results more generally applicable. A registry study could also be conducted to track CL dropout better. Likewise, this study only evaluated a single CL replacement schedule, and because of this issue, a future study should be completed to assess how daily disposable and monthly CLs compare with regard to allowing CL dropouts to return to wearing CLs. The last limitation is that this study only evaluated participants who dropped out of CLs because of discomfort or dryness. While these two issues are the most common reasons for habitual yet lapsed CL wearers, the community would benefit from more data evaluating refitting patientswho dropped out of CLs for other reasons (e.g., vision, handling). This work could also include neophyte CL wearers who dropped out of CLs during the first year of wear.

Conclusion

This study found that many of the participants who dropped out of CLs because of dryness or discomfort could successfully resume CL wear for at least 6 months with lehfilcon A monthly replacement CLs. This success is supported by the current study determining that 79.3% of the participants were satisfied with their CL comfort and that 87.0% of participants would recommend the CLs to a friend after 1 month of wear. A similar positive result was found at 6 months, with the current study determining that 59.7% of participants were satisfied with their CL comfort and that 80.7% of participants would recommend the CLs to a friend. These data are important because they demonstrate that past wearers can resume CL wear if given the opportunity to try a new lens material. Refitting with a novel CL material may benefit CL dropouts or patients at high risk of dropout, without requiring a change in replacement schedule. During the study period, participants reported a median of 6–8 h of comfortable wearing time per day and were able to wear the CLs for a median of 4–5 days per week. Nevertheless, when data with the monthly CL in this study are compared to similar work that evaluated a daily disposable CL, more participants with a history of CL dropout were satisfied with the CL performance if refitted with daily disposable compared to monthly CLs [9] While the results of this study are promising, more work is needed to determine directly how CL dropouts would respond to a different brand or CL wearing schedule.
