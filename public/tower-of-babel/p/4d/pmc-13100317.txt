The efficacy of disease-modifying therapies in patients with clinically isolated syndrome: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Yanisa Chumpangern, Yanika Seawsirikul, Panisara Keatbundit, Leeyaporn Thongtem, Ekdanai Uawithya, Roongnapa Khampang, Tatchaporn Ongphichetmetha, Sasitorn Siritho, Naraporn Prayoonwiwat, Jiraporn Jitprapaikulsan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) is the initial clinical presentation that may indicate the development of multiple sclerosis (MS). While significant advancements have been made in the treatment of MS, there remains no definitive consensus on the effective treatment of CIS. Our study aims to evaluate the outcomes of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) in the progression of CIS to clinically definite multiple sclerosis (CDMS) and to assess their efficacy in the treatment of CIS. A literature search was conducted through March 2024, focusing on immunotherapies that can treat CIS. The control group consisted of patients who received a placebo. The primary outcomes were the number of patients who converted to CDMS over the course of the study and the progression of the severity of disability in patients with MS using the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS). The secondary outcomes included improvement in CIS symptoms based on MRI lesions, including gadolinium-enhancing (GDE) and T2-weighted (T2W) lesions. A total of 9 studies (8 randomized controlled trials and 1 post hoc analysis) included 3,339 patients diagnosed with CIS who received immunotherapy. The patients had a mean age of 31.4 ± 7.8 years and were followed for a mean duration of 35.7 months. Cladribine showed the strongest evidence in lowering CDMS conversion (HR 0.37; 95% CrI 0.23–0.59; SUCRA 85.40 followed by GA (HR 0.50; 95% CrI 0.34–0.73; SUCRA 62.10), IFN beta-1b (HR 0.51; 95% CrI 0.35–0.74; SUCRA 60.60), teriflunomide (HR 0.59; 95% CrI 0.37–0.92; SUCRA 59.20), and IFN beta-1a (HR 0.62; 95% CrI 0.50–0.77; SUCRA 39.50), respectively. DMTs, including cladribine, teriflunomide, IFN beta-1a, IFN beta-1b, and GA, showed evidence of reducing conversion to CDMS compared with placebo. Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) affects more than two million people worldwide and impacts quality of life by impairing both physical and emotional functioning1,2. Patients with untreated MS may present with decreased mobility, abnormal posture, numbness from sensory neuropathy, and cognitive impairment3. Some patients exhibit symptoms suggestive of MS but do not fulfill the criteria for MS, termed clinically isolated syndrome (CIS)4,5. Managing CIS patients is important to prevent MS and its complications, as 70–80% of untreated CIS patients eventually progress to MS6,7.

In the past, Clinically Definite Multiple Sclerosis (CDMS) was a definitive diagnosis of MS. According to the Poser Criteria (1983)8, CDMS required at least two clinical attacks and objective neurological signs. These criteria rely primarily on clinical findings, supported by additional laboratory investigations such as CSF analysis or evoked potentials. In this criteria, MS can also be classified as Laboratory-Supported Definite MS, Clinically Probable MS, or Clinically Possible MS, depending on the degree of clinical and laboratory evidence available. Nevertheless, the McDonald Criteria have been widely used in recent years, enabling earlier diagnosis of MS, sometimes after a single clinical attack, when MRI findings demonstrate dissemination in time and space9.

Disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) are immunotherapies that have demonstrated efficacy in slowing the progression of MS by modulating or suppressing the immune system to decrease further inflammation, which can be categorized based on their efficacy levels and mechanisms of action10,11. However, the application of these therapies in CIS patients remains variable. Therefore, this meta-analysis aims to evaluate the efficacy of DMTs in the treatment of CIS12.

Methods

Literature review and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis protocol has been registered with the prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO) under CRD No. 42024522090. The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement13. We conducted searches in three databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE (via Scopus), and CENTRAL (via the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials). The search objectives were (1) to compare the efficacy between CIS patients who received immunotherapies and those who received a placebo, and (2) to assess the efficacy of immunotherapies in CIS patients. The search terms included “immunotherapy” and “clinically isolated syndrome” with no language restriction. The results were uploaded to Covidence (Melbourne, Australia), an online platform designed to streamline collaboration in meta-analyses. Two investigator teams (Team 1: Y.C. & L.T.; Team 2: Y.S. & P.K.) independently screened all citations, and discrepancies will be resolved through discussion with a third author (J.J.). The precise search terms are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were (1) patients 18 years or older previously diagnosed with CIS, (2) the studies must report a head-to-head comparison between each disease-modifying therapy and placebo of the outcomes of interest, and (3) the study design must be a randomized controlled trial (RCT). The exclusion criteria were (1) patients who have already met the diagnostic criteria for MS based on any version of the McDonald criteria used in each stud y9,14, (2) immunocompromised patients, including those who receive immunosuppressive drugs such as corticosteroids, and (3) a cohort, case–control, cross-sectional study, case series, case reports, and reanalyzed studies.

Outcomes

The primary endpoints of the studies include the number of patients who convert to CDMS and the progression of the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS)15. The secondary endpoint is improvement in CIS symptoms, assessed by the number of MRI lesions, including gadolinium-enhancing (GDE) and T2-weighted (T2W) lesions.

Data abstraction and risk of bias assessment

The extracted data elements include baseline characteristics and statistical measures for each outcome, including the number of CDMS conversions, the mean and standard deviation (SD) of EDSS score changes, and the mean and SD changes in the number of GDE and T2W lesions. The quality assessment for the RCT studies was conducted by two investigator teams (Team 1: Y.C. & L.T.; Team 2: Y.S. & P.K.) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool16. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion with a third author (J.J.). The included studies were assessed in several domains: (1) the randomization process, (2) adherence to the assigned intervention(s), (3) handling of missing outcome data, (4) measurement bias and (5) reporting bias. The overall risk of bias was classified as low, moderate (some concerns) or high. Publication bias was not assessed due to the limited number of included studies17.

Statistical analysis

Since clinical heterogeneity was assumed to exist in all included studies, a random-effects model was used for all analyses. The Bayesian network meta-analysis was performed using the R software (version 4.5.0) with the BUGSnet and gemtc packages18. The Bayesian network meta-analysis was modeled using four Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains, with 5,000 adaptation iterations, followed by 100,000 sampling iterations per chain, applying a thinning interval of 10, to generate posterior distributions. The Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA) was calculated to rank the probability of each DMT being the most effective for each outcome.

The Bayesian model assessment and the consistency assessment between direct and indirect evidence were performed in accordance with the National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence (NICE) Decision Support Unit guidance19. For the model assessment, trace and leverage plots are presented in Supplementary Figures S4 and S5, respectively. Points which lie outside the lines with c = 3 (indicated by a purple line in Supplementary Figure S5) were identified as contributing to the poor fit of the model. Consistency was assessed by comparing the estimates obtained from the consistency and inconsistency models (Supplementary Figure S6). Specifically, estimates closer to the 45° dashed line indicate a stronger agreement between direct and indirect evidence.

Results

Baseline characteristics and study selection

Of the 7,722 studies identified through the search, 700 duplicates were removed, resulting in 7022 studies for manual screening. Following this, 85 studies were selected for full-text review, and finally, nine studies20–24 (8 randomized controlled trials and one post hoc analysis) were included in our meta-analysis, comprising a total of 3,339 patients. CDMS conversion

A total of 7 studies (1 Cladribine, 1 GA, 3 IFN beta-1a, 1 IFN beta-1b, and 1 teriflunomide) were analyzed, involving 2,690 patients. All DMTs demonstrated strong evidence of a reduction in conversion to CDMS compared to placebo. Cladribine showed the highest probability of benefit (HR 0.37; 95% CrI 0.23–0.59; SUCRA 85.40), followed by GA (HR 0.50; 95% CrI 0.34–0.73; SUCRA 62.10), IFN beta-1b (HR 0.51; 95% CrI 0.35–0.74; SUCRA 60.60), teriflunomide (HR 0.59; 95% CrI 0.37–0.92; SUCRA 59.20), and IFN beta-1a (HR 0.62; 95% CrI 0.50–0.77; SUCRA 39.50), respectively.

EDSS progression

A total of 3 studies (2 IFN beta-1a and 1 teriflunomide) involving 747 patients were conducted to assess EDSS progression. Neither teriflunomide nor IFN beta-1a demonstrated clear benefits in reducing the progression of EDSS compared to placebo, as their credible intervals included the null value. However, higher posterior probabilities of reducing EDSS progression were observed for IFN beta-1a (SUCRA 73.50) and teriflunomide (SUCRA 51.40), compared to placebo (SUCRA 25.10).

Number of new GDE and hyperintense T2W lesions

A total of 5 studies involving 2243 patients that reported on GDE lesions (1 Cladribine, 3 IFN beta-1a, and 1 IFN beta-1b) and 5 studies involving 1772 patients that reported on T2W lesions (1 Cladribine, 3 IFN beta-1a, and 1 IFN beta-1band) were analyzed. There were no clear differences in posterior probabilities between cladribine, IFN beta-1a, and IFN beta-1b compared to placebo in preventing new GDE lesions. Nevertheless, IFN beta-1b, cladribine, and IFN beta-1a showed higher posterior probabilities of preventing new GDE lesions, with SUCRA values of 71.60, 64.60, and 50.00, respectively, compared to placebo (SUCRA13.70). For T2W lesions, IFN beta-1a showed strong evidence of preventing new lesions compared to placebo (MD − 2.11; 95% CrI − 4.24 to − 0.27; SUCRA 72.70). Meanwhile, cladribine and IFN beta-1b showed higher posterior probabilities of preventing new T2W lesions, with SUCRA values of 66.40 and 52.50, respectively, compared with placebo (SUCRA 8.50).

Model assessment

The trace plots show stable mixing and substantial overlap across all chains, and the density plots are nearly identical between chains (Supplementary Figure S4, left panel), indicating good convergence of the Bayesian model. The unimodal posterior distributions suggest consistent estimates of relative treatment effects, without evidence that any single chain or included study disproportionately influenced the results (Supplementary Figure S4, right panel). Furthermore, Bayesian leverage diagnostics did not show highly influential observations, as all data points were within the c = 3 contour (purple line in Supplementary Figure S5), indicating an adequate model fit.

Consistency assessment

The consistency plot was designed to compare the estimates from the consistency model and the inconsistency model. Since all estimates align with the 45° dashed line, there is strong coherence between direct and indirect evidence across all study endpoints. All consistency plots are illustrated in Supplementary Figure S6.

Discussion

This meta-analysis is the first to use a Bayesian network approach to compare DMTs, including GA, IFN beta-1a, IFN beta-1b, teriflunomide, and cladribine, in patients with CIS. Such DMTs showed strong evidence of reducing the conversion to CDMS compared to placebo. We also found that both cladribine and GA achieved the highest probabilities of reducing the incidence of CDMS conversion compared to placebo, with SUCRAs of 85.40 and 62.10.

Previous studies estimated the progression of CIS to MS to be between 30 and 82%25,26. Therefore, early diagnostics and treatment of CIS are crucial to improve patients’ quality of life27. Consequently, both the current 2017 McDonald and the revised 2024 McDonald criteria emphasize the aim to increase the sensitivity of CIS diagnosis, facilitating earlier identification and treatment9. Kolcava et al. reported that key predictive factors for MS progression included more than 10 baseline hyperintense lesions on T2W MRI, cerebrospinal fluid, oligoclonal band positivity, and abnormalities in evoked potential testing26,28.

This study aligns with previous systematic reviews, indicating that DMTs can delay MS development29. Cladribine is associated with a favorable effect on reducing conversion to CDMS, with an HR of 0.37 (95% CrI: 0.23–0.59). Preventing conversion to CDMS is a primary objective of early treatment in patients with CIS. Hence, our study strengthens the evidence that the indication for cladribine should not be limited to MS patients for relapse prevention but should be extended to cover the CIS population18,30,31. However, the effect of cladribine on preventing new or progressive MRI lesions was weaker in our study compared with previous studies in progressive MS32,33. Specifically, the included study of cladribine’s effect on MRI lesions demonstrated notable efficacy in reducing both GDE and T2W lesions, with the median number of new or progressive lesions remaining at zero over 76 months of follow-up. These low event rates may have limited the ability to detect a treatment effect using mean differences. Notably, ratio-based estimates showed a significant relative reduction in lesion activity (GDE: 0.090 [95% CI: 0.050–0.182] and T2W: 0.269 [95% CI: 0.191–0.378]), which could be pooled in future analyses once additional evidence becomes available before concluding the comparative efficacy of DMTs on new or progressive MRI lesions in the CIS population.

GA is also a promising alternative for CIS treatment. In our study, GA demonstrated high efficacy in CDMS conversion (SUCRA 62.10), but data on other outcomes are limited. Therefore, as with Cladribine, future studies are recommended to evaluate the efficacy of GA compared to other DMTs and placebo, focusing on EDSS progression and the prevention of new GDE and T2W lesions. A previous NMA by Armoiry et al.34 reported a reduction in the time to CDMS conversion in patients treated with GA compared to placebo (HR 0.55, 95% CrI: 0.40–0.76). However, the highest SUCRA score was observed in IFN beta-1a 40 μg SC thrice weekly (0.70), followed by IFN beta-1b 250 μg SC every other day (0.68), IFN beta-1a 30 μg IM weekly (0.62), and GA 20 mg once daily (0.5). In our NMA, GA demonstrated greater efficacy than both IFN beta-1a and IFN beta-1b, consistent with recent trials indicating greater effectiveness of GA in CDMS conversion. A possible explanation is the inclusion of a higher GA dose (30 mg) in our study, compared with the lower dose (20 mg) used in the previous NMA34.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, there were variations in the definition of inclusion criteria for MRI lesions in all studies. Some studies include only unifocal demyelinating events, whereas others include both unifocal and multifocal demyelinating events, which may affect the risk of conversion to CDMS, as the number of lesions is thought to influence disease severity. Second, in these studies, different drug doses and various routes of administration, including subcutaneous, oral, and intramuscular, were used, for which data suggest that dose may affect the outcome. Third, the experiment was conducted in different regions, including Europe, Asia, and the United States, where ethnic background is a significant factor in the incidence of multiple sclerosis. Fourth, there is moderate to high heterogeneity in these systematic reviews, with limited studies, so we cannot interpret the data precisely.

Clinical implications

This study highlights the efficacy of both cladribine and GA in reducing the risk of CDMS conversion in CIS patients, reinforcing their role as effective early treatment strategies. Their ability to lower the risk of CDMS conversion supports their use in preventing MS progression. Early intervention with DMTs may help preserve neurological function, making them a critical therapeutic option in CIS management. However, further studies included only CIS patients diagnosed according to the latest McDonald 2024 criteria, and incorporating safety endpoints of available DMTs is recommended to strengthen our findings.

Conclusion

Cladribine and glatiramer acetate showed high efficacy in lowering CDMS conversion. Early use of these therapies may help delay disease progression, preserve neurological function, and provide a critical window for intervention before CDMS development.
