Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics associated with retention in care among adults living with HIV and severe mental illness and reasons for loss to follow-up in Uganda: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Kevin Ouma Ojiambo, Juliet Nakku, Rachel Nante Wangi, Ivan Segawa, Regina Ndagire, Joanita Nangendo, Anne Katahoire, Fred Semitala
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the socio-demographic and clinical characteristics associated with retention in care and reasons for loss to follow-up (LTFU) among people living with HIV (PLWH) with a known diagnosis of severe mental illness (SMI).DesignWe conducted a parallel convergent mixed-methods study. The quantitative study was used to determine the proportion and factors associated with retention in care among PLWH and SMI. The qualitative study explored reasons for LTFU.SettingThis study was conducted at two the HIV clinics of two tertiary hospitals in Uganda.ParticipantsWe reviewed records of 608 PLWH who started antiretroviral therapy (ART) and included participants who had a documented diagnosis of SMI.OutcomesThe primary outcome was retention in care. Age, gender, religion, tuberculosis (TB) status, WHO clinical stage, functional status, cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) cell count, viral load and SMI diagnosis were among the predictor variables.ResultsWe collected data from 328 participants. Retention at 6 months was 43.3% compared with 35.7% at 12 months. Having an unsuppressed viral load (≥1000 copies/mL) (adjusted incidence risk ratio (IRR)=1.54, 95% CI: 1.17 to 2.03), being 36 years and below (adjusted IRR=0.94, 95% CI: 0.94 to 0.95), initial presentation at outpatient department (adjusted IRR=0.74, 95% CI: 0.57 to 0.96), having TB signs and symptoms (adjusted IRR=0.98 95% CI: 0.97 to 0.99) and being in lower WHO stages (I and II) (adjusted IRR=1.08, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.14) at ART initiation were significantly associated with retention in care at 6 and 12 months. Inadequate social support, long waiting hours at the clinic, perceived stigma and discrimination, competing life activities, low socioeconomic status and poor adherence to psychiatric medication were barriers to retention in care.ConclusionTwelve-month retention in care remains low at 35.7% far below the 90% WHO target. There is a need to design and implement targeted interventions to address barriers to retention in care among PLWH and SMI.Keywords: INFECTIOUS DISEASES, HIV & AIDS, Epidemiology, MENTAL HEALTH, PUBLIC HEALTH, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

In low-income and middle-income countries, HIV is a leading cause of death and disability. People living with HIV (PLWH) are approximately 38 million globally and 21 million in East and Southern Africa.1 In Uganda, approximately 1.5 million people are living with HIV, with a prevalence and cumulative incidence rate per 1000 population of approximately 6.2% and 3.5 %, respectively, among adults aged 15–49 years.1 Age-standardised prevalence for mental disorders is about 11 935 per 100 000 people in sub-Saharan Africa. Notably, the leading contributors to this burden are depressive disorders, bipolar disorder, anxiety disorders and schizophrenia.2 There is a dearth of aggregated estimates, particularly for severe mental illness (SMI). SMI has been defined as a prolonged and recurrent mental disorder that impairs activities of daily living and requires long-term treatment3 and comprises bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, psychosis, schizoaffective disorder and schizophrenia.4

In Uganda, the prevalence of HIV among people with SMI is approximately 11.3%.5 Although evidence suggests that HIV risk behaviour is associated with psychiatric disorders,6–10 HIV transmission risk in SMI populations is much more complex and occurs as an indirect result of multiple individual, social and system factors.11

Immense advances have been made in HIV prevention and treatment, and PLWH can expect to live a near-normal lifespan if they have access to and adhere to antiretroviral therapy (ART). This has significantly altered the course of the AIDS pandemic and improved the quality of life for many PLWH worldwide.12 ART aids in viral suppression and prevents virus transmission to sexual partners.13 However, the retention of all PLWH in care is critical to maintaining this achievement and moving towards the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) ambitious targets of 95, 95, 95 by 2030.14

Retention in care has been defined as consistency in HIV care appointment attendance over time15 and remains a challenge particularly among people with SMI,16 due to physical and social factors associated with SMI, such as disorganisation and impulsivity.17 PLWH and SMI are vulnerable and more burdened by HIV. Stigma, discrimination and psychological and sociocultural factors all make it harder to access interventions.18 One year after starting ART, less than half of PLWH and SMI remain engaged in care19 20 compared with 74.1% in the general population.21 22 This results in negative health outcomes for PLWH and SMI at every stage of the HIV care continuum, including ART failure, hospitalisation, AIDS-defining illness and death.13 23

This study drew on the social-ecological model to understand the complex relationships and interplay between social and structural factors, individual practices, the physical environment and health and how these factors influence retention in care for PLWH and SMI. The model states that people’s conduct is influenced by their knowledge, values, attitudes and social influences such as the people they associate with, the organisations to which they belong and the communities in which they live.24 It explains the complex relationships between social and structural variables, human practices, the physical environment and health by situating health and health behaviours in the context of physical, social and policy contexts.25

The component factors are organised according to levels of influence on health and use the heuristic device of the iceberg-type figure to depict the relationship between deep structural factors like public policy level factors which are the wider circumstances in which people live, for example, broader cultural values, local and national laws and policies that impact health; organisational and institutional level factors which refer to rules and regulations of organisations and institutions that can impact health behaviour; the interpersonal level factors that comprise the social environment including social networks like family, peers, coworkers, neighbours, living and working conditions. Finally, the intrapersonal level constitutes factors that are directly related to individuals themselves and are poised as the main drivers of health-related outcomes and health-related behaviours including knowledge, attitudes and behaviour25 26 (figure 1). This enabled an exhaustive understanding of the complexity and the interplay among factors that influence retention in care among PLWH and SMI. The goal of this study therefore was to determine the socio-demographic and clinical characteristics associated with retention in care and reasons for loss to follow-up among PLWH with a known diagnosis of SMI at Butabika National Referral Hospital and Mulago Immune Suppression Syndrome (ISS) clinic.

Methods

Study design

We used a parallel convergent mixed-methods design. The quantitative study was a retrospective cohort study that covered 4 years of records review at the Butabika National Referral Hospital HIV care clinic and Mulago ISS clinic from 2017 to 2020 to determine the proportion and factors associated with retention in care among PLWH and SMI. This resonated with the ‘test and treat’ policy in Uganda which was rolled out in 2016 and implementation started in 2017.27 The quantitative study was complemented by data from a qualitative study in which we conducted in-depth interviews (IDI) with PLWH and SMI and key informant interviews (KII) with clinical staff to explore factors influencing retention in care and reasons for loss to follow-up.

Study setting

Butabika National Mental Referral Hospital HIV clinic and Mulago ISS clinic were the sites of this research. Both clinics are supported by Makerere University’s Joint AIDS Program and are located in Kampala, Uganda’s capital city. They both provide free mental health and HIV care. Butabika Hospital is about 12 km east of Kampala city centre and is the country’s only national referral mental health institution. Since 2004, this HIV clinic has served around 350 people per day and provides free HIV services that are not limited to individuals with mental illnesses or those who have been admitted to the hospital. Between 2017 and 2020, around 400 PLWH with a history of SMI started ART at this clinic. Mulago ISS clinic is part of the Mulago hospital complex, which is around 3 km north of Kampala’s city centre. The clinic is Uganda’s largest HIV clinic, serving approximately 16 500 PLWH and enrolling over 1000 people on ART every year. Approximately 165 PLWH and SMI were diagnosed at the Mulago ISS clinic between 2017 and 2020.

Population

At the Butabika HIV clinic and Mulago ISS clinic, all clinical data of PLWH who started on ART between 2017 and 2020 and had a documented diagnosis of SMI were included. Furthermore, the study participants had to be at least 18 years old and had been followed-up for at least 12 months. The study excluded patients’ charts that had missing data on critical characteristics such as SMI diagnosis and ART initiation date from the available records and those transferred out before 12 months of follow-up. To be asked to participate in IDI, PLWH needed to (1) be 18 years and above, (2) have a verified SMI diagnosis, received HIV services at the study sites and were either retained or lost to follow-up (3) provide informed consent. The KII was conducted with clinical staff. IDI were not conducted with any patients who were mentally unstable at the time of the study.

Sample size calculation and sampling technique

Using Kish Leslie’s formula,28 we calculated an initial sample size of 575 with a design effect of 1.5 and the assumption that 47% of the PLWH and SMI were retained in care 12 months after starting ART.19 After scaling down and adjusting for missing data, a final sample size of 328 participants was obtained including 151 participants from Mulago ISS and 177 participants from Butabika HIV clinic. We chose all clinical records that met the eligibility criteria consecutively until the sample size was reached. We set the sample size for the qualitative study a priori at 30 participants as recommended by Creswell and Poth29 and Moustakas30,29 30 and this was adjusted depending on the point at which saturation would be reached. Interviews were conducted in rounds and data was analysed until the point of saturation. The KII participants were selected through purposive sampling and IDI participants were selected through the criterion sampling method. The qualitative sample of PLWH and SMI was a subset of the quantitative sample. The IDI participants were recruited through phone calls and were facilitated to come for an interview at the clinic while the key informants were approached at their workstations and asked to participate in the study.

Data sources and measurements

Data was extracted by trained research assistants using a pre-tested data abstraction tool. The original ART registers were used as the primary source of information to ensure reliability because computerised databases can be less accurate at the level of individual patient data due to the possibility of clerical error associated with the process of transcribing the data from the chart to the database.31 The accuracy and reliability of the abstracted data were tested in random samples, and double data entry was used to reduce errors.

We used interview guides piloted a priori to conduct KII in English and IDI in Luganda (local language) or English based on the preferences of the participants for an average time of 60 min. All interviews were conducted at the study site in private rooms. Participants who were lost to follow-up were traced using the telephone contact extracted from the ART register and facilitated to come to the clinic for the interviews. The interviews were conducted by the principal investigator and two research assistants who had a background in behavioural and social science research and were trained on how to conduct qualitative interviews. The training took place at the study site and was conducted by the principal investigator. The interviews were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim and later translated into English.

The primary outcome variable was retention in care, which was defined as consistency in HIV care appointment attendance over time measured as having two and four completed visits in 6 and 12 months, respectively.15 Otherwise, the current status of the patient was recorded as died (of any cause), lost or dropped. A participant was considered ‘dropped’ if they missed their next appointment for 1 or 2 months, and ‘lost’ if they missed their next appointment for 3 months or more in a row without returning.32 ‘Loss to follow-up’ (LTFU) included all patients who died, were dropped or lost.32 Age, gender, marital status, religion, educational attainment, tuberculosis status at baseline, WHO clinical stage of HIV, functional status (inpatient, outpatient), baseline CD4 cell count, viral load and SMI diagnosis were among the predictor variables. Data was collected between November 2021 and February 2022.

Quantitative data analysis

Data were extracted and entered into EpiData V.4.6.02 (EpiData Association, Odense M, Denmark) for cleaning before being exported to Stata V.15 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) for analysis. We summarised numerical baseline characteristics such as age and baseline CD4 cell count using median (with minimum and maximum) and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The proportions of participants retained in care at 6 and 12 months follow-up periods were estimated with 95% CIs using clustered robust SEs (study site as the cluster). A multilevel mixed-effects Poisson regression model was used to determine factors associated with retention in care among PLWH and SMI reporting incidence risk ratios (IRR). Age was categorised at its median of 36 years and baseline CD4 cell count at 500 cells/mm3 of blood, given a healthy immune system normally has a CD4 cell count of 500 cells per cubic millimetre of blood and above. The follow-up period (time variable) was analysed as a continuous variable due to the small sample size. Education level and marital status were excluded from the multivariate analysis due to missing data. Only factors with a p value<0.2 at bivariate analysis were considered for multivariate analysis. There was no collinearity or overdispersion. All statistical tests were two-sided, and factors were considered statistically significant when their p value was <0.05. The interaction was assessed using a likelihood ratio test to assess the significant difference between the reduced model (with only basic variables) and the full model (with both basic variables and their interaction terms). A factor was considered a confounder if it changed the IRR of the crude model by more than 10% and was included in the final model.

Qualitative data analysis

The qualitative data was transcribed, translated to English, coded and synthesised by two authors (KOO and IS) using OpenCode V.4.02 (Information, Communication and Technology (ICT) Services and System Development and Department of Epidemiology and Global Health, University of Umea, Sweden). We generated prominent themes using a thematic analysis approach.29 30 We used techniques such as data triangulation, member checking, field diaries to reflect on position and assumptions and an audit trail to record methodological decisions made for reflection at the interpretation stage to ensure that qualitative data was credible and reliable.33 An inductive approach to data coding and analysis was largely adopted, which was a bottom-up approach driven by what was in the data. Hence, codes and themes were derived from the content of the data themselves, so that what was mapped during analysis closely matched the content of the data. Factors like social support, social network, stigma, cost of transport, socioeconomic status, gender norms, cultural beliefs, social norms, distance to the clinic and clinical processes like the number of patients enrolled and waiting time were determined a priori from literature and helped in guiding the formulation of the broad themes during the coding process.34 Coding was done independently and later we compared codes and potential themes generated through discussion, and later recoded the data to generate the final themes for this study. The results from two data sets were merged at the presentation and interpretation stage during this study.

Patient and public involvement

We held consultative meetings with healthcare providers and data officers during the development of the study and tools and piloting of study tools.

Results

Description of study participants

The eligibility of 608 participants was determined. We excluded 280 participants because of missing SMI diagnosis data and/or because they were transferred out before 12 months. We collected data from 328 participants who were eligible for analysis (figure 2).

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

Overall, the median age of participants was 36 years (IQR=17; minimum 18, maximum 72), 225/328 (68.6%) were women, 24/328 (7.3%) had tertiary education, 24/328 (7.3%) were married or cohabiting, 161/328 (49.1%) were residents of Kampala district, and 241/328 (73.5%) were Christians. One hundred and thirty-three of the 328 participants (40.6%) had psychotic disorders, 175/328 (53.4%) were in WHO clinical stage III, and 232/328 (70.7%) had no signs and symptoms of tuberculosis (TB) at the time of ART initiation, 171/328 (52.1%) were inpatients, and 168/201 (83.6%) were suppressed (table 1). Retention in HIV care of PLWH and SMI

At 6 months, 43.3% (95% CI: 38.0% to 48.7%) of PLWH and SMI were still in care, and 35.7% (95% CI: 30.7% to 41.0%) were still in care at 12 months. During the follow-up period, 64.3% of the participants (95% CI: 59.0% to 69.4%) were lost to follow-up. There was no significant difference in the proportions retained at the Butabika HIV clinic 37.9% (95% CI: 31.0% to 45.3%) and Mulago ISS clinic 33.1% (95% CI: 26.0% to 41.1%) at the end of the 12-month follow-up period (table 3).

Factors associated with retention in care and reasons for loss to follow-up

The social-ecological model was used to present the factors associated with retention in care and reasons for LTFU. This model demonstrates that behaviour is the result of an individual’s knowledge, values and attitudes as well as social influences such as the people with whom they associate, the organisations to which they belong and the communities in which they live.24 It is used to explain the complex relationships between social and structural factors, individual practices, the physical environment and health by situating health and health behaviours in the context of physical, social and policy environments.25

Intrapersonal/individual level factors

At bivariate analysis, age, gender, religion and place of residence were not significantly associated with care retention (p>0.05 for all comparisons). Furthermore, having an unsuppressed viral load (crude IRR (cIRR)=1.46, p=0.01), having signs and symptoms of TB at baseline (cIRR=0.99, p=0.01) and lower WHO stages (I and II) (cIRR=1.14, p=0.01) were significantly associated with retention in care. Retention did not change significantly over time (table 4). In multivariate analysis, when we controlled for all other variables, retention did not change significantly over time. Being 36 years and below (adjusted IRR (aIRR)=0.94 p<0.01), having lower WHO clinical stages (aIRR=1.08 p=0.01), having an unsuppressed viral load (aIRR=1.54 p<0.01) being an outpatient (aIRR=0.74 p=0.02) and having signs and symptoms of TB at baseline (aIRR=0.98 p=0.01) were all significantly associated with retention in care (table 4).

Comorbidity with SMI; Healthcare providers reported that poor adherence to mental treatment, disappearance from homes and wards, pill burden and relapse of mental illness interfered with follow-up of PLWH and SMI. For example, one key informant stated,

…some patients stop taking mental drugs when they are stable, they relapse and wander away and miss clinic appointments. KI003.

…when they go without mental drugs for a while, they relapse, wander away, and finding them is difficult; sometimes police bring them back, sometimes they are lost completely. KI005.

Interpersonal level factors

Social and economic standing; PLWH and SMI usually suffer from unemployment and extreme poverty and these lead to a lack of transport fare, food and the inability to buy psychiatric medication. These economic constraints interfere with their ability to keep clinical appointments. If they can honour appointments and pick medication the lack of food sometimes interferes with adherence to both ART and psychiatric medication. This was reported by KII and corroborated by some participants as stated below,

…some patients are purely dependent; they don’t work and some are abandoned by relatives, so they can’t afford anything…those who stabilize, we encourage them to get something to do. KI004.

I have been jobless but I had to eat and had to come for my appointments and survive, sometimes People have been able to support me but they get tired of giving you everything especially financially and you remain on your own, which is tough because in my condition getting a job is not easy. IDI019 30-year-old male.

…If I swallow drugs without food I don’t feel well and just to get money for food or transport when you do not work is not easy, sometimes you beg and no one gives you. IDI021 female 34 years.

Competing life activities; PLWH and SMI cited demands from various aspects of life, such as having clinical appointments on working days, the need to care for their children and other social demands, as barriers to continuous engagement in care. Some participants usually have not disclosed their status at work and simply need to take a little time to pick up medicine and return to work, however, the long queues that cause delays interfere with this arrangement as stated by some of the participants.

Sometimes you leave your place of work thinking, let me rush to the clinic and pick up some medicine and then come back to work, but when you come here, you know that day is dead! IDI010 28-year-old female.

You’re not going to work, but you haven’t told anyone what’s wrong with you, so they’re wondering why you’ve missed work with no explanation. IDI020, a 29-year-old male.

Social support; PLWH and SMI stated that they usually have few friends, were unable to socialise, and some had been abandoned by their families and were staying alone, making it easy to forget scheduled appointments. For example, one study participant stated,

…I stay alone in the house and have nobody to remind me that I am supposed to swallow medicine or go to the hospital, so at times that causes me to fail to come on the right date. IDI004 42-year-old male.

I used to stay with my aunt but she later gave up on me and sent me away because she said I scare her children when I get a mental health episode. IDI002 female 20 years.

Community level factors

Distance to the clinic; when compared with those living within a radius of less than 30 km to the clinic, living more than 30 km (aIRR=0.90 p=0.02) from the clinic was significantly associated with poor retention in care. This was also reported in the IDI as participants stressed that sometimes lack of money for transport hinders them from honouring clinical appointments given they come from far away and cannot foot to the clinic. One participant reported,

…If I happen not to come to the hospital, then it could be because of a very serious reason; maybe because I have forgotten or I have failed to get transportation. I at times get such problems where I have no transportation and I am stuck. IDI017 25-year-old male.

Perceived stigma and discrimination; participants in the study reported experiencing stigma in their communities and among family members, which manifested as calling names, pointing fingers, being rejected by partners and community segregation. To some extent, the participants reported being looked at by family and friends as less important or as though their death was imminent.

…at times they call somebody else at home and start saying she doesn’t come for her psychiatric medication, she doesn’t come for her ARVs, that thing affected me so much because you might not have disclosed to other people, and they start calling you names and abusing you that you’re mad and have Silimu (HIV). IDI015, 32-year-old female.

Yes, they used to segregate me a lot, and even when I developed this mental illness, sometimes they would beat me up. They treated me badly. However, recently, I made friends with a doctor who is also my neighbour and owns many private clinics. I disclosed my status to him and there was a time when I developed an illness that almost took my life but I did not have any money for admission so he helped me. IDI013 30-year-old female.

When people find out that you have HIV, there is a way that they start looking at you, they see you like you are about to die anytime! One might even start insulting you. IDI023 22 year-old-female.

Organisational level factors

Procedures at the clinic; PLWH and SMI, among other study participants, reported long waiting hours and bad staff attitudes as a barrier to care-seeking. However, patient-friendly services such as reminders, patient handling, privacy and confidentiality were also reported to encourage care-seeking in various ways.

…Some patients say I am not going back to Butabika in case a staff treats them rudely. KI001.

…you come here in the morning thinking you leave early and instead leave at 4 or 5 pm because you have to see a doctor and pick drugs from the two pharmacies and every place there is a long line. IDI029 male 33 years.

…they call you when you have missed the day when you are supposed to come. IDI006, female 35 years.

In summary, social support, clinical processes, stigma and discrimination, competing life activities, socioeconomic status and SMI comorbidity were identified as factors influencing care retention (table A3, online additional file 1).

Discussion

Uganda has made significant achievements along the HIV care cascade.35 However, for these gains to be sustained, there is a need to tailor services to the different subpopulations of PLWH which may have unique needs. An estimated 1 in every 10 PLWH in Uganda has had a diagnosis of SMI.5 Quality HIV services for this vulnerable group can be achieved through improved long-term retention in HIV care. Retention is a key indicator of HIV treatment success and is important in achieving the 95, 95, 95 UNAIDS target.14 PLWH and SMI are faced with both social and physical barriers to HIV care.17 To achieve and sustain the UNAIDS target and control the HIV/AIDS pandemic optimal retention must be achieved for both the key populations and general population of PLWH.

In this study retention in care at 12 months follow-up was 35.7% among PLWH and SMI, which was lower than the WHO target of 90%36 and the general population in Uganda (75–82%), as reported in other studies.37 At 6 months’ follow-up approximately 43 in every 100 PLWH and SMI are kept in care higher than 35 of every 100 who remain in care 12 months after ART initiation. During the first 3 months, participants’ responses to treatment are required to be closely monitored, and patients are scheduled for monthly appointments. After 6 months in care, participants are given appointments once every 3 months which explains the increase in LTFU from 56.7% at 6 months to 64.3% at 12 months. Furthermore, mortality increased with an increase in the follow-up period from 5.5% at 6 months to 9.1% at 12 months, affecting retention in care, which may be due to the functional status given the majority were inpatients (52.1%) and in higher WHO clinical stages III and IV (59.8%) at ART initiation.

We found that approximately 55 in every 100 PLWH and SMI were at risk of negative health outcomes such as ART failure, hospitalisation, AIDS-defining illness and death at the end of the first year after ART initiation, putting a strain on the healthcare system. Due to population differences, retention in our study at 12 months follow-up was lower than that reported by Nachega et al,19 (47%), as the study included a double cohort of PLWH who presented with SMI at diagnosis and PLWH without SMI.19

When compared with those over the age of 36, participants at/or below 36 years were less likely to be retained in care. This could be because young people have poor health-seeking behaviour and are unlikely to keep clinical appointments, resulting in lower retention in care, similar to results from other retrospective cohorts that found a significant association between younger age and low retention in care in South Africa and Nigeria.21 22

PLWH and SMI who had not achieved viral suppression were more likely to be kept in care than those who were virally suppressed (≥1000 copies/mL of blood). At both study sites patients who are not virally suppressed are given adherence counselling, which encourages them to stay in care hence this could have contributed to low attrition in this group. This finding was consistent with a systematic review of 14 studies conducted in resource-limited settings, which found HIV disease progression as measured by viral load suppression to be a key factor influencing HIV care retention.17

Participants who came from home to receive care at clinics were less likely to be kept in care than those who were admitted to the ward. The clinics’ strict environment and close monitoring given to patients on the ward could have contributed to their high retention in care. Notably, issues to do with the cost of transport and long distance to the clinic could have contributed to missed appointments among the outpatients. This however, contradicts the findings of a study conducted in Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia, which found that those with poor functionality, such as those who are bedridden, are less likely to remain in care.21 This could be because of the difference in population given that these studies were done in a population of general patients.

PLWH and SMI who were suspected of having TB (had signs and symptoms) at the time of ART initiation were less likely to engage in care than those who had no signs and symptoms of TB at the time of ART initiation. This could be due to an increased risk of mortality from TB comorbidity which reduces retention in care, this was similar to findings from a nationally representative multistage retrospective cohort study of HIV-infected adults in Zimbabwe,38 which found that TB comorbidity as a predictor of high attrition. TB stigma could have also played a role in reducing retention in care among PLWH and SMI suspected of having TB at ART initiation since HIV-TB coinfection increases the level of HIV-related stigma which certainly hurts retention in care.39–42

Comorbidity with SMI was identified as a major cause of poor retention in care. Non-adherence to psychiatric medication and relapse of mental illness influenced continuous care-seeking. PLWH and SMI who relapsed wandered away from the ward or home and they could not be found by their relatives and as a result, were reported to have discontinued care-seeking. Psychiatric medication stockouts at the pharmacies caused patients to go without medication and given the high cost of these drugs in private pharmacies, many people failed to adhere to the prescribed treatment, resulting in relapse. This was similar to a retrospective analysis conducted on 561 adult patients with HIV with a history of mental illness enrolled at an urban HIV clinic in Johannesburg, South Africa,20 which reported psychiatric illness as a significant predictor of poor retention in care, and having active psychiatric illness at the last clinical visit being associated with not returning for care.

Participants who travelled far from the clinics (more than 30 km) were less likely to be retained in care than those who stayed close to the study clinics. The high cost of transportation to the clinic, which makes keeping scheduled appointments difficult given that the majority of patients were of low socioeconomic status could have contributed to missed clinical appointments in this group. This was consistent with findings from two cohort studies of 2835 and 870 PLWH in South Africa and Ethiopia, respectively, in which long distance to the clinic was associated with low retention in care.43 44 This finding was also reported in IDI by participants in this study further affirming the importance of distance to the clinic on retention in care.

Perceived stigma and discrimination among family members, friends and community members, manifested as calling names, pointing fingers, insults and segregation from social groups and gatherings, were reported to discourage PLWH and SMI from continuing to seek care. When patients disclosed private patient information, family members were reported to share it with others, which devastated and killed morale for continuous care. These findings are similar to those of a qualitative study conducted among care providers in Washington DC, which identified dual stigma against both HIV and SMI as one of the factors impeding care provision.45 Another study conducted among refugees in Uganda reported stigma as a reason for attrition.42

PLWH and SMI reported to have been abandoned by their families, living alone and thus lacking social support. Hence, they usually had no one to remind them of their scheduled clinical appointments, which resulted in disengagement from care. The eventual lack of emotional support, which would have otherwise been provided by friends and relatives, predisposed them to loneliness, which may have exacerbated the already excruciating SMI and resulted in dropout from care. This was consistent with the findings of an evaluation of a population-based programme in the USA to promote care engagement and ART among PLWH and a qualitative study in Uganda, which found that social support and welfare, play an important role in patient retention in care.42 46

Participants reported being of low socioeconomic status which limited their ability to attend clinical appointments due to a lack of transportation and also perpetuated poor drug adherence due to a lack of food, which eventually led to a relapse of mental illness and increased attrition from care due to an unstable mental state. These findings are consistent with previous qualitative research from Uganda and Ethiopia conducted among refugees and the general population, respectively, where economic constraint was identified as a reason for attrition.42 47 However, this study brings new evidence since it was done among the PLWH and SMI.

Poor clinical processes, such as long waiting times due to insufficient staff, having to collect drugs from two pharmacies (HIV pharmacy and mental health pharmacy), and an increased number of patients, frustrate and discourage PLWH and SMI from honouring clinical appointments as scheduled. After a patient missed an appointment, the follow-up process with reminders via a phone call was reported to occur which encouraged patients to come for treatment, but it was inconsistent due to insufficient resources. This is consistent with a study from Ethiopia, where dissatisfaction with services was reported as one of the reasons for poor retention in care.47

Scheduling clinical appointments on weekdays, combined with long waiting times, was reported to interfere with other life activities such as work and caring for children at home, discouraging PLWH and SMI from keeping clinical appointments. This resulted in increased attrition since it forced participants to choose between caring for their children and going to work, as well as honouring the clinical appointments as scheduled. This was similar to a study conducted in the USA, which found that the ease with which patients could honour clinical appointments was an important barrier to care access among PLWH.46

In general, the proportion of retention at the 12-month follow-up period reported in this study was lower than that reported in prior studies (47%)19 which can be attributed to earlier initiation of care as reported in previous studies.48 However, there is no discernible difference in factors associated with care retention reported in studies conducted before the implementation of the test and treat policy.

The strengths of this study are in the design and setting given the qualitative study supplemented the findings on factors that influence care retention. The two study sites are the largest providers of mental health and HIV care in the country. As a result, the patients come from all over the country hence the findings can be applied to public facilities in Uganda. The study however has several limitations as follows; we used a retrospective study design and non-research programme or clinical data, hence, some variables were discovered to be missing or data on them was not collected, however. This was anticipated and mitigated by adjusting the sample size to cater for missing data. Inaccurate clinical information documentation may have resulted in information bias. Some observations, for example, marital status, SMI diagnosis and education level were recorded differently in both the electronic data set and the ART registers, however, this constituted about 5% of the total number of eligible participants. In such cases of difference between electronic records and ART registers, the information in the ART register was considered accurate since the electronic data comes from the ART registers. The exclusion of PLWH who had missing information on their SMI diagnosis due to the loss of mental health files but were still classified as PLWH and SMI in the electronic data set may have resulted in selection bias. These, however, constituted a small (about 10%) proportion of the total hence, the influence on our findings was minimal. There were inconsistencies noticed in the flow of information in the IDI of participants even though they were deemed to be stable with regards to SMI hence this could have affected the validity and trustworthiness of the information collected. The KII conducted corroborated some of the information from the IDI hence the effect on our results was reduced.

Conclusions

In comparison to the WHO target of 90%, our study found that PLWH and SMI are poorly retained in care at 6 months (43.3%) 95% CI: (38.0% to 48.74%) and 12 months (35.7% 95% CI: 30.7% to 41.0%). Poor retention was associated with being 36 years and below, living more than 30 km from the clinic, presenting through outpatient department and having signs and symptoms of TB at the time of ART initiation. Being in a lower WHO clinical stage (stages I and II) and having unsuppressed viral loads, on the other hand, were found to be associated with high retention in care. Our inquiry also found that a lack of social support, perceived stigma and discrimination, competing life activities, low socioeconomic status, clinical processes such as long waiting times and SMI comorbidity were contributing factors for disengagement from care.

Institutions that provide integrated HIV and mental healthcare and HIV programmes should develop projects that encourage PLWH and SMI to stay in care, particularly younger patients, outpatients and those who present with signs and symptoms of TB at the start of ART. Addressing critical but manageable psychosocial barriers such as poor social support, stigma and discrimination as part of integrated HIV and mental healthcare will go a long way toward improving retention of PLWH and SMI as we aim to control the HIV pandemic. Future research in this group, particularly prospective studies, should be conducted to provide more robust evidence on clinical, social and behavioural factors associated with care retention.
