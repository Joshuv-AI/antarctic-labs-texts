A large-scale human plasma metabolite atlas from over 380,000 participants
Authors: Zhirong Li, Yating Miao, Lina Jin, Yanan Ma, Xinyao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge-scale metabolomics enables systematic profiling of circulating small-molecule metabolites and provides an integrative view of genetic, environmental, lifestyle, and microbial influences on human health, yet comprehensive evidence linking metabolites to diverse traits, diseases, comorbidity, prediction, and causality remains limited. Here, we analyze 251 Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-derived plasma metabolites in 212,751 UK Biobank participants, with independent validation in 177,013 individuals from a newly released data wave. Testing across 884 health-related traits, 722 prevalent diseases, and 1137 incident diseases using multivariable regression and stringent Bonferroni correction, we identified 67,505 metabolite-trait, 21,982 metabolite-prevalent disease, and 41,214 metabolite-incident disease associations, with replication rates of 76.1%, 83.3%, and 74.0%, respectively. Associations were most concentrated in endocrine, metabolic, and circulatory diseases. Unsupervised clustering of disease-metabolite profiles revealed 10 and 12 disease clusters (for prevalent and incident diseases, respectively) that span International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 chapters yet share convergent metabolomic signatures, providing a metabolic basis for clinical comorbidity. Metabolite-based predictive models showed stronger performance for short-term than long-term outcomes, with creatinine as the most frequently selected predictor across prevalent disease models. Bidirectional Mendelian randomisation (MR) identified 61 putative metabolite-to-disease causal effects and 558 disease-driven metabolic alterations. This study provides a systematically validated plasma metabolomic atlas (https://net-matebolic.vercel.app/) linking metabolites with human traits and diseases, offering insights into comorbidity, risk prediction, and potential causal pathways.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases

Introduction

The concept of comprehensive disease management has gained increasing attention. However, for many diseases, reliable early diagnostic biomarkers and personalized therapeutic targets remain lacking1–3. Metabolites, as intermediates or end products of biochemical processes, play diverse and essential biological roles4. Metabolomics enables systematic investigation of the strong associations between metabolites and disease phenotypes, driving a paradigm shift in clinical practice from treatment-oriented approaches toward preventive precision medicine5,6. Compared with genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, metabolomics provides a direct and rapid snapshot of physiological states and pathological mechanisms through comprehensive profiling of small-molecule metabolites7. Metabolites are more sensitive to environmental changes and exhibit faster turnover rates than proteins or DNA, offering a unique perspective for elucidating the metabolic basis of disease onset and progression8. Consequently, metabolites are considered ideal biomarkers for disease risk assessment and for uncovering physiological and pathological mechanisms.

However, research on the multifaceted mechanisms of metabolites in relation to diseases still faces several limitations. Firstly, most current metabolomics studies focus on single diseases, specific systemic diseases, or a limited number of predefined phenotypes9–11, lacking systematic analyzes of a wide range of diseases and health traits on a unified platform. This restricts our understanding of whether certain key metabolites exhibit consistent or opposing patterns of action across different diseases. Therefore, there is an urgent need to conduct large-scale, multidimensional studies to systematically evaluate the patterns and causal relationships of metabolites in various diseases. Secondly, studies linking plasma metabolites with clusters of diseases across organ systems are still quite limited. In fact, metabolic abnormalities often affect multiple organs and drive various diseases12. Thus, conducting such systematic analyzes not only aids in identifying metabolites that drive multimorbidity but may also uncover early comorbidity biomarkers, providing improved tools for disease warning and risk stratification, ultimately achieving more precise and systematic disease management. Thirdly, the application of metabolites in disease prediction primarily focuses on specific diseases or specific time windows13. Research systematically evaluating the short-term and long-term predictive capabilities of metabolites across multiple diseases remains relatively scarce. There is a lack of comprehensive comparisons of disease risk across different time scales, such as within 5 years versus over 5 years. Additionally, studies systematically comparing the predictive power of metabolites with traditional demographic variables are also lacking.

In this study, we systematically investigated the associations between 251 metabolic traits derived from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and 884 health-related traits in the UK Biobank, including 722 prevalent diseases and 1137 incident diseases. A total of 212,751 participants were included in the discovery cohort, with an additional 177,013 participants included in an independent validation cohort. Based on these analyzes, we constructed an open, transparent, and comprehensive metabolomic atlas (https://net-matebolic.vercel.app). Subsequently, we further explored the relationship between metabolites and comorbidities to identify useful biomarkers for various health conditions. The predicted disease outcomes were categorized into four groups: prevalent diseases, incident diseases, 5-year incident diseases, and over-5-year incident diseases, to assess the temporal differences in metabolite-disease associations and determine optimal predictive models. Additionally, we integrated genetic data to perform bidirectional Mendelian randomization analyzes, aiming to elucidate potential causal pathways. Together, these findings provide important insights into the metabolic architecture of diseases and offer promising avenues for enhancing disease prevention strategies, personalized risk stratification, and therapeutic development.

Results

Phenotypes and participant characteristics

A total of 251 circulating metabolites were included in this study, comprising 170 concentration measures (67.7%) and 81 ratio measures (32.3%). Among the concentration measures, very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL)-related metabolites constituted the largest subgroup (N = 50), while VLDL ratios were the most prevalent among ratio measures (N = 29; Fig. 1A). After metabolomic data preprocessing, 212,751 participants were included in the discovery cohort and 177,013 participants in the validation cohort. In the discovery cohort, the mean age was 56.6 years, with 52.6% female and 47.4% male participants. The majority of participants were of White ethnicity (90.3%) (Fig. 1B, and Supplementary Data 1). Demographic characteristics of the validation cohort are presented in Supplementary Data 2.

Following integration of metabolomic data, we assessed associations with a wide spectrum of health outcomes, including 884 health-related traits, 722 prevalent diseases, and 1137 incident diseases. All outcomes had a sample size greater than 1000 and more than 100 cases. Health-related traits were categorized into 11 chapters according to UK Biobank definitions. The largest category was Diet and food preferences, comprising 171 traits (19.3%), followed by Health and medical history with 166 traits (18.8%). The mean sample size across chapters ranged from 21,914 to 212,135 participants (Fig. 1C, D). Prevalent and incident diseases were classified into 15 ICD-10-based chapters. For prevalent diseases, the number of phenotypes per chapter ranged from 13 to 98, with the Digestive system being the most represented chapter. The average number of cases per chapter ranged from 551 to 2593. For incident diseases, the Musculoskeletal system included the highest number of phenotypes (N = 148). Owing to the baseline age range of UK Biobank participants (approximately 40–70 years), pregnancy-related outcomes were rare, with only one incident endpoint recorded. Excluding the Pregnancy chapter, the mean number of cases per chapter ranged from 1208 to 5020, with a mean follow-up duration of 13.8 years (Fig. 1E, F).

Atlas of metabolite-health related trait associations

We tested associations between 251 circulating metabolites and 884 health-related traits, yielding 67,505 metabolite-trait pairs that passed Bonferroni correction (30.4% of 221,884 total associations) in the discovery cohort (P < 0.05/251/884/2 ≈ 1.13 × 10−⁷). Among these, the majority of associations (N = 51,401; 76.1% of all associations passing correction) were replicated in the validation cohort (Fig. 2A). To reduce the impact of potential cohort-specific findings, we primarily report associations identified in the discovery cohort and independently validated in the validation cohort, unless otherwise specified.

Across trait categories, the Brain structure chapter contributed the largest number of validated associations (N = 15,563; 30.3% of all validated associations), followed by Blood and urine assays (N = 11,049; 21.5%), highlighting the complex and widespread interplay between circulating metabolites with brain structural features and biological markers14. After accounting for the number of phenotypes within each chapter, glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA) emerged as the metabolite group with the highest relative association burden (weighted association count: 470.3%, N = 340), with most associations observed for traits categorized as Other traits. The top-ranking metabolites across other metabolite subgroups are summarized in Fig. 2B. Sex-stratified analyzes revealed substantial sex differences in metabolite-trait associations. For example, within the Mental health chapter, only 39.1% of associations passing multiple-testing correction were shared between men and women (Fig. 2C), suggesting the presence of sex-dependent metabolic signatures.

We next focused on associations between circulating metabolites and Brain structural features. Phospholipids in medium high-density lipoprotein (M-HDL-PL) exhibited the strongest negative associations, whereas creatinine showed the strongest positive associations. Both metabolites were predominantly associated with cortical regions, with 96 associations exceeding the significance threshold for each metabolite (Fig. 2D). The parahippocampal gyrus and postcentral gyrus displayed the most pronounced regional associations, indicating that specific circulating metabolites may be differentially related to distinct brain structures.

To further explore potential mechanistic links, we identified metabolites jointly associated with traits in the Mental and behavioral chapter and diseases in the Mental health chapter. Notably, GlycA, phospholipids to total-lipids in medium HDL percentage (M-HDL-PL%), and triglycerides to total-lipids in medium LDL percentage (M-LDL-TG%) were associated not only with mental and behavioral phenotypes but also with disorders, including mood disorders, depression, and anxiety disorders. In total, 30 metabolites showed associations with at least 7 of the 8 selected phenotypes after multiple-testing correction (Fig. 2F), indicating potential pleiotropic effects and shared biological pathways. Collectively, these results suggest that metabolite-trait associations may partially underlie observed metabolite-disease relationships, reflecting the complex interplay between intermediate traits and disease phenotypes.

Atlas of metabolite-disease outcomes associations

We examined associations between 251 circulating metabolites and 1859 disease phenotypes, including 722 prevalent and 1137 incident diseases, using logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models in the discovery and validation cohorts, respectively. In the discovery cohort, 21,982 metabolite-prevalent disease associations were identified after Bonferroni correction, accounting for 12.1% of the 181,222 tested associations (P < 0.05 / [251 × 722 × 2] ≈ 1.38 × 10⁻⁷; Fig. 3A). For incident diseases, 41,214 metabolite-disease associations passed the multiple-testing threshold (14.4% of 285,387 tests; adjusted P < 0.05/[251 × 1137 × 2]; Fig. 3B). Among these, 83.3% of prevalent disease associations (N = 18,304) and 74.0% of incident disease associations (N = 30,498) were replicated in the validation cohort.

Across both cohorts, metabolite-disease associations were strongly enriched within the Endocrine and metabolic and Circulatory disease chapters. Specifically, 2602 and 6408 associations were detected for prevalent diseases, while 5652 and 5047 were observed for incident diseases, respectively. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence highlighting the tight metabolic regulation within these systems. After weighting by the number of diseases per chapter, triglycerides to total-lipids in large ldl percentage (L-LDL-TG%) showed the highest relative association burden (403.8%, N = 328), with associations spanning 57.3% of Endocrine and metabolic diseases and 56.0% of Circulatory diseases (N = 47 and 51, respectively). Among metabolite concentrations, total cholesterol esters (Total-CE) exhibited the highest association rate (340.0%, N = 266; Fig. 3C). Notably, creatinine showed consistent associations with both gout and chronic kidney disease, while glucose (Glu) was robustly associated with type 2 diabetes, in agreement with previous reports (Fig. 3E)15–17.

Metabolite-disease associations displayed distinct patterns between disease prevalence and incidence. Although the Circulatory disease chapter showed the largest overlap between prevalent and incident associations (Nboth = 3943), this overlap accounted for 52.5% of all associations passing correction (Fig. 3D). Within this chapter, 353 inverse associations were observed, primarily involving major coronary heart disease events, myocardial infarction, and ischaemic heart disease (N = 90, 77, and 46, respectively). Additional inverse associations involving circulatory diseases, including cardiovascular disease, coronary atherosclerosis, and hypertension, are shown in Fig. 3F. These patterns reflect the dynamic nature of the circulating metabolome and suggest that metabolite-disease relationships may differ across disease stages, underscoring the need for longitudinal and mechanistic validation.

Sex-stratified analyses identified 15,260 metabolite-prevalent disease associations (5207 male-specific, 3413 female-specific, and 6640 shared) and 27,872 metabolite-incident disease associations (7035 male-specific, 7536 female-specific, and 13,301 shared). All associations were detected in the discovery cohort and replicated in the validation cohort after sex-stratified multiple-testing correction (adjusted P < 0.05/[251 × 722 × 2 × 2] = 6.90 × 10−⁸ for prevalent diseases; adjusted P < 0.05/[251 × 1137 × 2 × 2] = 4.38 × 10⁻⁸ for incident diseases). Only three sex-discordant associations were observed for incident diseases. For example, phospholipids in large HDL (L-HDL-PL) were positively associated with substance abuse in males [HR (95% CI): 1.11 (1.08-1.14)] but showed an association in the opposite direction in females [HR (95% CI): 0.91 (0.88-0.93)]. Similarly, cholesteryl esters to total lipids in medium VLDL percentage (M-VLDL-CE%) were associated with reduced risks of diabetic neuropathy and type 2 diabetes with neurological complications in males (HR = 0.68 and 0.67, respectively), but showed associations in the opposite direction in females (HR = 15.0 and 11.2; Fig. 3G). Several associations, including those involving cholesterol in large HDL (HDL-C) or L-HDL-PL and substance abuse, were observed in the discovery cohort but did not replicate. For prevalent diseases, 27 sex-specific inverse associations were detected in the discovery cohort; however, none were confirmed in the validation cohort.

In sensitivity analyzes, 88.3% of prevalent disease associations and 97.8% of incident disease associations identified in the discovery cohort remained robust after further adjustment for age², age × sex, age² × sex, and the first ten genetic principal components. To further address the potential for collider bias introduced by covariate over-adjustment, we additionally performed a sensitivity analysis restricting covariates to age and sex only. Under this sparse model, 80.9% of prevalent disease associations and 92.3% of incident disease associations remained robust, suggesting that the inclusion of additional covariates does not materially bias our findings.

Characteristic metabolites and disease clusters

Comparing metabolite signatures across diseases may provide insights into molecular differences between conditions with similar pathophysiology and help identify previously unrecognized connections. We performed unsupervised clustering of both prevalent and incident diseases based on their estimated effect sizes (ORs or HRs) derived from metabolite-disease association analyzes in the discovery cohort. The number of clusters was evaluated over a range of 10 to 40, and the optimal solution was selected using silhouette scores (Fig. 4A). This approach identified 10 clusters for prevalent diseases and 12 clusters for incident diseases. The largest clusters, Cluster 10 for prevalent diseases and Cluster 11 for incident diseases, comprised 212 and 250 diseases, respectively (Fig. 4B).

We next investigated associations between 251 circulating metabolites and disease comorbidity across these clusters for both prevalent and incident diseases in the discovery and validation cohorts. Comorbidity was defined as the presence of two or more diseases within a given chapter. Logistic regression models were used to assess associations between metabolites and binary comorbidity status, while ordinal logistic regression models were applied to evaluate associations with the number of comorbid conditions, excluding individuals without any disease.

For prevalent diseases, Cluster 3 was examined as an illustrative example. In the discovery cohort, 139 metabolites showed associations with Cluster 3 comorbidity in both logistic and ordinal logistic regression models (Fig. 4C, D). Of these, 98 metabolites were consistently retained in both analyzes after validation (adjusted P < 0.05/[251 × 10 × 2 × 2] = 4.98 × 10−⁶). We extracted these 98 metabolites and visualized their associations with diseases in Cluster 3 using a heatmap (Fig. 4E). Although the diseases originated from multiple chapters, their association patterns with metabolites were broadly similar. For example, the odds ratios for M-LDL-TG% ranged from 1.02 to 1.43 across diseases. In contrast, LDL-related measures, including LDL cholesterol (LDL-C), cholesteryl esters in LDL (LDL-CE), free cholesterol in LDL (LDL-FC), total-lipids in LDL (LDL-L), concentration of LDL particles (LDL-P), phospholipids in LDL (LDL-PL), leucine (Leu), cholesterol in medium LDL (M-LDL-C), cholesterol to total-lipids in medium LDL percentage (M-LDL-C%), cholesteryl esters in medium LDL (M-LDL-CE), free cholesterol in medium LDL (M-LDL-FC), total-lipids in medium LDL (M-LDL-L), concentration of medium LDL particles (M-LDL-P), and phospholipids in medium LDL (M-LDL-PL), showed broadly consistent protective associations, with a median OR (Q1, Q3) of 0.89 (0.80, 0.93), ranging from 0.60 to 1.03.

For incident diseases, Cluster 2 was selected as an example. This cluster included 82 diseases spanning 14 chapters. Association analyzes identified 124 metabolites that were consistently associated with comorbidity across all four models applied in the discovery and validation cohorts (Fig. 4F, G) (adjusted P < 0.05/[251 × 12 × 2 × 2] = 4.15 × 10⁻⁶). For average diameter for HDL particles (HDL-size), hazard ratios ranged from 0.97 to 1.34, with a median (Q1, Q3) of 1.09 (1.06, 1.11). Several large HDL-related measures, including cholesterol in large HDL (L-HDL-C), cholesteryl esters in large HDL (L-HDL-CE), free cholesterol in large HDL (L-HDL-FC), total-lipids in large HDL (L-HDL-L), concentration of large HDL particles (L-HDL-P), and L-HDL-PL, showed associations predominantly in the direction of increased risk, with a median HR (Q1, Q3) of 1.07 (1.03, 1.08) (Fig. 4H).

Predictive power of metabolites for disease outcomes

We evaluated the predictive performance of circulating metabolites for four categories of disease outcomes: prevalent diseases, incident diseases, 5-year incident diseases, and over-5-year incident diseases. For each category, outcomes with fewer than 100 cases were excluded to ensure model stability. This framework was designed to assess differences in metabolite-based prediction across short-term and long-term disease risk windows. For model construction, the top 30 metabolites ranked by feature importance were selected. Predictive accuracy based on metabolites was compared with that of baseline demographic features, including age, sex, ethnicity, education, Townsend deprivation index, body mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and systolic blood pressure. We further evaluated combined models incorporating both metabolites and demographic features. An area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) greater than 0.70 was used as a predefined threshold to indicate acceptable predictive performance.

We first assessed the predictive ability of metabolomic profiles across different disease time horizons. Among all outcome types, 597 diseases were shared across the four categories. After excluding 109 models with AUC values below 0.5, 488 diseases remained for subsequent analyzes. The most represented disease chapters were Digestive (N = 71) and Musculoskeletal (N = 69). Overall, metabolites showed stronger predictive performance for short-term outcomes, particularly prevalent and 5-year incident diseases, with 84 and 42 diseases, respectively, achieving AUC values above 0.70. In contrast, only 30 overall incident diseases and 22 over-5-year incident diseases exceeded this threshold (Supplementary Data 3), likely to reflect the greater short-term biological relevance and temporal variability of many circulating metabolites.

For prevalent diseases, metabolite-based models demonstrated particularly strong performance for Circulatory system disorders, with 35 outcomes achieving AUC values above 0.70. Notably, models for myocardial infarction and major coronary heart disease events reached AUCs of 0.885 and 0.849, respectively. For 5-year incident diseases, the highest predictive performance was observed for Endocrine and metabolic disorders, including type 1 diabetes (AUC = 0.842) and type 2 diabetes (AUC = 0.828), further underscoring the close metabolic basis of these conditions.

We next compared the predictive performance of metabolites, demographic features, and their combination for prevalent and 5-year incident diseases, encompassing 642 and 639 outcomes, respectively (Fig. 5A, B). For prevalent diseases, metabolite-based models outperformed demographic predictors for 83 outcomes (DeLong test P < 0.05 after FDR correction; Supplementary Data 4), most prominently within the Genitourinary (N = 28) and Circulatory (N = 20) chapters. Representative examples include type 2 diabetes (AUC: 0.892 vs. 0.790) and myocardial infarction (0.885 vs. 0.796). For 5-year incident diseases, metabolites showed superior performance relative to demographics for 46 outcomes (DeLong test P < 0.05 after FDR correction; Supplementary Data 5), largely concentrated in the Genitourinary (N = 19) and Endocrine and metabolic (N = 11) diseases. Examples include hypertensive renal disease (0.815 vs. 0.749) and chronic kidney disease (0.800 vs. 0.755). Although demographic features showed higher average predictive performance across all outcomes, combining metabolites with demographic variables consistently improved model performance. Specifically, 242 prevalent diseases and 192 5-year incident diseases exhibited higher AUC values in combined models than in models using either feature set alone (DeLong test P < 0.05 after FDR correction).

To identify metabolites contributing most strongly to disease prediction, we examined prevalent disease models with AUC values above 0.70, yielding 134 diseases. Analysis of the importance analysis showed that 211 metabolites (84.1% of the 251 measured metabolites) contributed to at least one predictive model. Creatinine was the most frequently selected metabolite, appearing in 131 models (97.8%), followed by glutamine (Gln) and glycine (Gly), which were included in 105 and 101 models, respectively (Fig. 5C). Similarly, among the 639 5-year incident disease models, 44 outcomes exceeded an AUC of 0.70, involving 164 metabolites. Notably, 3-hydroxybutyrate was included in 40 models, followed by creatinine and GlycA (39 models each), and albumin and Glu (38 and 35 models, respectively; Fig. 5D). These results highlight the prominent role of creatinine in short-term disease prediction and suggest more broadly that metabolite concentrations tend to contribute more strongly to predictive models than metabolite ratios.

Finally, all 1973 models with AUC values above 0.70 underwent external validation, with models trained in the full discovery cohort and evaluated in the validation cohort. Of these, 1761 models (89.3%) retained AUC values above 0.70, indicating stable predictive performance and further supporting the robustness of these findings.

Potential causal metabolites of diseases

To investigate potential causal relationships between circulating metabolites and disease outcomes, we conducted bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyzes. Genetic instruments for 249 of the 251 metabolites were obtained from previously published genome-wide association studies (GWAS)18. For the remaining two metabolites, glucose-lactate (Glu-lactate) and spectrometer-corrected alanine (Sc-ala), which lacked publicly available GWAS summary statistics, we derived genetic instruments using metabolomics data from UK Biobank participants, following the same GWAS pipeline applied in prior studies18. Among approximately eight million genetic variants tested, no single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) reached the conventional genome-wide threshold (P < 5 × 10−⁸). However, 7 SNPs for Glu-lactate and 18 SNPs for Sc-ala exceeded a suggestive threshold of P < 5 × 10−⁶. Genetic instruments for disease outcomes were obtained from the FinnGen study (https://www.finngen.fi/en/access_results) (release DF12). Of the disease endpoints included in previous analyzes, GWAS summary statistics were available for 908 diseases, which were carried forward into the MR analyzes. The remaining outcomes were not re-analyzed due to computational constraints.

In the forward MR analysis (metabolite → disease), we initially identified 6454 metabolite-disease associations exceeding the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold (P < 0.05/[251 × 908] ≈ 2.19 × 10−⁷). We then applied more stringent criteria for SNP inclusion (see “Methods”), resulting in 186 metabolites being retained for downstream analyzes. Under these stricter conditions, 61 metabolite-disease associations remained, meeting the adjusted threshold (P < 0.05/[186 × 908] ≈ 2.96 × 10−⁷) and passing tests for heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy (Fig. 6A). Given the known collinearity among Nightingale NMR metabolites, we assessed potential redundancy among these 61 associations. The 17 metabolites involved had an effective number of independent signals of M_eff = 6.2, calculated using the eigenvalue-based participation ratio method, indicating that these metabolites represent approximately 6 independent biological axes despite their nominal count of 17. To directly evaluate redundancy at the association level, we examined pairwise correlations among metabolites co-associated with the same disease outcome. No metabolite pairs satisfied both criteria for redundancy, high correlation (|r| > 0.8) and concordant effect direction, with the maximum observed |r| = 0.659 (M-LDL-TG% vs IDL-FC% for venous thromboembolism) and all other pairs showing |r| ≤ 0.507. These analyzes confirm that the 61 reported associations represent independent biological signals not attributable to metabolite collinearity. Among the 61 associations, triglycerides to L-LDL-C% showed the largest number of putative causal links (N = 11), followed by M-LDL-TG% (N = 9). For example, L-LDL-C% showed inverse associations with major coronary heart disease events, coronary atherosclerosis, and ischaemic heart disease (wide definition) (Fig. 6B). In contrast, 3-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate, and acetone showed associations consistent with increased risk of hypertension (Fig. 6C).

In the reverse MR analysis (disease → metabolite), diseases exhibited more extensive putative causal effects on metabolomic profiles. Initially, 77,116 disease-metabolite associations exceeded the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold (P < 0.05/[251 × 908]; Fig. 6D). After applying more stringent filtering criteria, 440 diseases were retained for analysis, yielding 558 disease-metabolite associations meeting the adjusted threshold (P < 0.05/[251 × 440]). At the chapter level, Infectious and parasitic diseases contributed the largest number of associations (N = 211), followed by Endocrine and metabolic diseases (N = 188). In total, these associations involved 26 diseases. Disorders of lipoprotein metabolism, unspecified, showed the broadest metabolic impact, with associations involving 181 metabolites, followed by diarrhoea and gastroenteritis of presumed infectious origin, which was associated with 105 metabolites (Fig. 6E). We also assessed redundancy among the 558 disease-metabolite associations. This reduced the 558 associations to 153 independent disease-metabolite pairs, representing a 72.6% reduction in nominally reported associations. The compression was most pronounced for disorders of lipoprotein metabolism (181→47 independent metabolite signals) and gastroenteritis-related infections (105→20 independent signals), consistent with the known clustering of lipoprotein subclass measurements into a small number of independent biological axes. Twelve diseases showed no redundancy (compression ratio = 1.0), indicating their associated metabolites represented genuinely distinct signals.

Additional results and interactive access

All analytical results have been compiled into an interactive web platform: https://net-matebolic.vercel.app/. This platform is publicly available. It is intended to facilitate reproducibility, peer review, and further hypothesis generation.

Discussion

This study makes a significant step forward in revealing the complex relationships between metabolites, health traits, and disease. By conducting a large-scale analysis of metabolomics data from 389,764 UK Biobank participants, we explored the million-pair associations between 251 circulating metabolites and 1859 diseases, as well as 884 health-related traits. Our validation analysis provides in-depth insights into the roles of metabolites in different health states. Additionally, through the analysis of metabolite-disease comorbidity associations, we uncovered significant differences in the association patterns and risk levels of specific metabolites with diseases across different disease clusters, particularly with respect to metabolites associated with LDL and HDL. Regarding the predictive capabilities of metabolites, they demonstrated superior performance in forecasting short-term diseases, with models incorporating demographic features significantly improving predictive accuracy, particularly in cardiovascular and metabolic disease predictions. This highlights the potential of metabolites in clinical risk assessment. Moreover, we identified 619 potential causal metabolite-disease associations, offering valuable perspectives for understanding the causal relationship between metabolites and diseases. Overall, the open-access metabolite Atlas (https://net-matebolic.vercel.app/) provided by this study comprehensively illustrates the associations between metabolites, health phenotypes, and diseases, further confirming the significant role of metabolites in a variety of complex diseases.

Through the comprehensive integration of human health and disease metabolic characteristics, our research is poised to offer valuable insights into the potential of metabolites for disease diagnosis, prevention, and treatment. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the most extensive to date, covering both newly developed and prevalent diseases across a large population. By combining discovery and validation cohorts, we identified 67,505 metabolite-trait pairs, 21,982 metabolite-prevalent disease pairs, and 41,214 metabolite-incident disease pairs. These findings further reveal the widespread associations between metabolites and various diseases and health phenotypes, including some previously unreported relationships, such as the potential association between metabolites and iron metabolism disorders, thus providing promising avenues for further investigation into the impact of metabolites on disease. Notably, we observed opposing effects of some metabolites between prevalent and incident diseases, suggesting that metabolites may play different physiological roles at different disease stages or during treatment. For example, LDL-TG and IDL-TG exhibit protective effects with prevalent hypertension in cross-sectional studies, but they may act as risk factors for the onset of hypertension. This phenomenon could be related to the fact that hypertensive patients often have a concomitant risk of hyperlipidemia and are frequently treated with statins19. Research indicates that statins can significantly reduce LDL-TG and IDL-TG levels in the blood20, thereby potentially influencing hypertension management. This finding underscores the complexity of metabolite mechanisms at different stages of disease and warrants further investigation.

Through the analysis of metabolite relationships with disease clusters and the predictive ability of metabolites over different time spans, this study reveals the important roles of metabolites in health and disease, particularly their potential applications in early disease prediction and clinical management. By using unsupervised clustering, nearly a thousand diseases were grouped into 10–12 clusters. These clusters contained diseases from different systems but exhibited highly similar metabolic association patterns. This phenomenon suggests that, although diseases may manifest clinically in distinct ways, they may share core metabolic pathways or pathophysiological mechanisms at the molecular level21,22. This “metabolic heterogeneity” explains why diseases from different chapters of pathology are often comorbid in clinical practice and offers an additional perspective on disease classification22. The study shows that metabolites outperform traditional predictors in predicting prevalent diseases and the risk of disease onset within 5 years. This may be due to the ability of metabolites to capture immediate changes in physiological status and environmental exposure, particularly in metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, where such changes are most evident. In feature importance analysis, creatinine was the most frequently appearing metabolite in prevalence disease prediction models, reflecting the fundamental diagnostic value of kidney function indicators in multi-system diseases23. It is also noteworthy that models combining metabolites with demographic features performed significantly better in the majority of diseases, indicating that metabolites provide distinct and complementary biological information rather than simple statistical redundancy.

Through MR analysis, we not only identified the potential causal effects of metabolites on diseases but also revealed the impact of diseases on metabolite profiles, providing valuable information for clinical applications and personalized medicine. In forward analysis, we found that metabolites such as triglycerides to L-LDL-C% were inversely associated with various cardiovascular diseases, suggesting that lipid metabolism may play a significant role in the development of coronary artery disease and other cardiovascular conditions22,24,25. Meanwhile, metabolites like 3-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate, and acetone were associated with increased hypertension risk, indicating that metabolic products may elevate hypertension risk by influencing energy metabolism and fatty acid oxidation25,26. In reverse analysis, the influence of diseases on metabolite profiles was more widespread, particularly with respect to lipoprotein metabolism disorders and infectious gastroenteritis, suggesting that diseases can alter metabolic networks through complex mechanisms, thereby influencing disease progression and prognosis. These findings provide further insights into the potential of metabolites in early disease prediction, intervention, and management, advancing the fields of metabolic medicine and precision healthcare.

The core strength of this study lies in the construction of what is, to our knowledge, one of the largest and most comprehensive metabolic landscapes to date, encompassing the most diseases and the largest number of participants. However, there are several limitations that warrant further exploration. First, although we have uncovered widespread associations between metabolites and disease/health traits, the underlying molecular mechanisms still need to be validated through cross-species experiments to gain a deeper understanding of the roles these metabolites play in disease pathogenesis. Second, while our findings have been validated through rigorous replication cohorts, as the data primarily come from the UK Biobank, with most participants being of European descent, future studies should replicate these findings in other ethnic groups with distinct metabolic profiles and disease spectra to ensure the generalizability of these results. Third, although the ICD-10 codes were used to define disease status, there are limitations, particularly with respect to the fact that the ICD-10 may not capture all stages of chronic progressive diseases27, especially those that progress slowly. Finally, the metabolomics platform used in this study has certain limitations. Of the 251 metabolic measures included, the vast majority (~200) represent lipoprotein subclass lipid quantities across 14 lipoprotein fractions (VLDL, IDL, LDL, HDL and their subclasses), with only a small number of non-lipid metabolites, including 10 amino acids, 6 glycolysis/TCA cycle-related metabolites, 3 ketone bodies, and a handful of phospholipid and inflammatory markers. As such, this platform captures primarily lipid and lipoprotein-centric biology and represents only a small fraction of the full space of circulating metabolites; large classes of biologically important compounds, including most amino acids, bile acids, acylcarnitines, xenobiotics, and the broader polar metabolome, are not measured. Future research incorporating broader untargeted metabolomics approaches would be important to provide a more complete and unbiased metabolic landscape of diseases and health traits.

In summary, this study systematically constructed a comprehensive plasma metabolomic landscape of human health-related traits and diseases through large-scale plasma metabolomic analysis. We further elucidated the key role of metabolomics in disease diagnosis and prediction, particularly its exceptional efficacy in short-term disease prediction. More importantly, we conducted large-scale identification of potential causal relationships between metabolites and diseases at the population level. This series of findings significantly deepens our understanding of the circulating metabolome as an integrative readout of biological state connecting genes, environment, and phenotypes, and provides a solid foundation for future precision clinical diagnostics, individualized risk prediction model development, and targeted intervention strategy formulation based on metabolomics, supported by robust molecular mechanism analyzes and powerful data-driven paradigms.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was based on data from the UK Biobank, a large-scale prospective cohort study that recruited more than 500,000 participants aged 33-73 years at baseline between 2006 and 2010 from 22 assessment centers across the United Kingdom. At enrollment, participants completed standardized touchscreen questionnaires collecting information on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and medical history. In addition, physical measurements and biological samples were obtained following uniform protocols. Detailed descriptions of the UK Biobank study design and data collection procedures have been published elsewhere1.

Metabolomic profiling using NMR spectroscopy has been conducted for a substantial proportion of UK Biobank participants. Earlier data releases included metabolomic data for approximately 270,000 individuals. In December 2025, the UK Biobank Data Release v20 expanded the availability of NMR-based metabolomic data to approximately 490,000 participants. In the present study, participants with metabolomic data released prior to Data Release v20 were defined as the discovery cohort. The validation cohort comprised participants whose NMR-based metabolomic data were newly released in Data Release v20. The study design is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

All participants provided electronic informed consent at the time of recruitment. No financial compensation was offered. The present analysis was conducted under UK Biobank application numbers 769193 and 1127192.

Plasma metabolite profiling

Plasma metabolic profiling in the UK Biobank was conducted using a high-throughput NMR platform, which quantified 251 metabolic traits. These included routine clinical lipids, lipoprotein subclasses, fatty acid profiles, and a range of low-molecular-weight metabolites such as glycolysis-related byproducts, ketone bodies, and amino acids, all expressed in molar concentration units. Among the 251 metabolic measures, 170 were absolute concentrations and 81 were ratios. The 170 absolute measures were classified into six major categories: very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL; N = 50), high-density lipoprotein (HDL; N = 36), low-density lipoprotein (LDL; N = 29), intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL; N = 7), total concentration traits (N = 8), and other metabolic traits (N = 40). The 81 ratios were grouped into VLDL ratios (N = 30), HDL ratios (N = 20), LDL ratios (N = 15), IDL ratios (N = 5), acid ratios (N = 9), and other ratios (N = 2). The metabolite and their abbreviations can be found in Supplementary Data 6.

Metabolite values were pre-processed separately in the discovery and validation cohorts prior to analysis. Extreme outliers, defined as values exceeding four times the interquartile range (4×IQR), were excluded. Missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) algorithm with k = 5. To reduce the influence of skewed distributions and to standardize scales across metabolites, all metabolite concentrations were transformed into probit scores.

Definition of diseases

Disease status was defined based on International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, surgical records, and death registry data. For each participant, the diagnosis date was determined as the earliest occurrence among these three sources. Baseline was defined as the date of attendance at the UK Biobank assessment center (Field ID: 53), and follow-up ended on 1 May 2023. Prevalent and incident diseases were defined according to whether the diagnosis occurred before or after the baseline, respectively. To ensure standardized disease classification and quality control, disease definitions were based on the latest FinnGen disease endpoint codes (Data Freeze 13, DF13). These definitions incorporated rigorous filtering criteria, including age, sex, specific inclusion and exclusion conditions (e.g., conditions, control_exclude, and control_conditions). Notably, while FinnGen uses five-character endpoint codes, the UK Biobank follows a four-character ICD-10 coding scheme. Therefore, for mapping purposes, the fifth character of the FinnGen code was truncated to align with the corresponding four-character category.

A total of 3840 distinct disease endpoints were initially identified. To ensure statistical stability in downstream modeling, we retained only diseases with at least 100 cases in both the discovery and validation cohorts after linkage with the metabolomic data. Only disease endpoints meeting this criterion in both cohorts were included in subsequent analyzes. This selection resulted in 722 prevalent diseases and 1137 incident diseases being analyzed. These diseases were further grouped into 15 major ICD-10 chapters: infectious and parasitic diseases; neoplasms; blood and immune disorders; endocrine and metabolic diseases; mental and behavioral disorders; nervous system diseases; diseases of the eye and adnexa; diseases of the ear and mastoid process; circulatory system diseases; respiratory diseases; digestive diseases; skin diseases; musculoskeletal disorders; genitourinary diseases; and pregnancy-related conditions (Supplementary Data 7).

Health-related traits

A broad set of health-related traits was included based on data fields curated in the UK Biobank. These traits were derived from multiple domains, including blood and urine assays, cognitive function, diet and food preferences, eye measurements, health and medical history, lifestyle factors, mental health, physical activity, physical measures, and working and living environment. Categorical variables with more than two unordered levels were encoded using one-hot encoding to generate multiple binary indicators for inclusion in subsequent regression analyzes. All variables were required to have a minimum of 1000 participants with available data in both the discovery and validation cohorts after linkage with the metabolomic dataset, and only variables meeting this criterion in both cohorts were retained. For binary variables, an additional requirement of at least 100 cases was applied to ensure sufficient variability.

In total, 884 health-related traits were retained, including 409 continuous variables, 264 ordinal categorical variables, and 211 binary variables. Linear regression, ordinal logistic regression, and binary logistic regression were used to evaluate associations between each trait and the metabolomic features, depending on the variable type.

Covariates

Four categories of covariates were included in this study:

Demographic factors: age (years), sex (male or female), ethnicity (White or other), and educational attainment (none, Certificate of Secondary Education [CSE], National Vocational Qualification [NVQ], college, or professional degree).

Socioeconomic status: Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI; continuous).

Lifestyle and anthropometric factors: smoking status (never, current, previous, or unknown), and body mass index (BMI; continuous).

Metabolite-related technical variables: fasting duration prior to blood collection (continuous), season of blood draw (spring, summer, autumn, or winter), sample storage duration (continuous; defined as the time interval between sample collection and metabolite measurement), and spectrometer ID (categorical; 1–8).

For covariates with missing data, imputation was performed as follows: continuous variables with ≤1% missingness were imputed using the mean, while categorical variables with ≤1% missingness were imputed using the mode. For categorical variables with >1% missingness, missing values were recoded as “unknown”. Notably, no continuous covariate had more than 1% missing values. Sex was included in models dynamically based on the sex specificity of the outcome variable.

Associations of metabolites with diseases and traits

All association analyzes were adjusted for the covariates described above. For prevalent diseases, associations between individual metabolites and disease status were assessed using logistic regression models. For incident diseases, Cox proportional hazards models were applied after excluding participants with the corresponding disease at baseline. To account for multiple testing, Bonferroni correction was applied separately for each analysis framework. For prevalent diseases, the correction factor was set to 251 metabolites ×722 diseases ×2 cohorts. For incident diseases, a correction factor of 251 metabolites × 1137 diseases × 2 cohorts was used. For health-related traits, association models were selected according to the data type of each trait: binary traits were analyzed using logistic regression, continuous traits using linear regression, and ordinal categorical traits using ordinal logistic regression. Bonferroni correction for trait analyzes was applied using a factor of 251 metabolites × 884 traits × 2 cohorts. Health-related traits included in the analyzes are listed in Supplementary Data 8.

For sex-specific outcomes, analyzes were restricted to the relevant sex. For outcomes that were not sex-specific, additional sex-stratified analyzes were conducted, and interaction effects between sex and each metabolite were tested by including interaction terms in the regression models. The statistical models remained otherwise unchanged, and Bonferroni correction was applied with a doubled adjustment factor to account for sex stratification.

Unless otherwise specified, results are primarily reported for associations that were identified in the discovery cohort and independently replicated in the validation cohort, defined as meeting the Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold in both cohorts with consistent directions of effect.

As a sensitivity analysis, all association analyzes were repeated with an extended set of covariates, including age², age × sex, age² × sex, and the first 10 genetic principal components. Sex-specific covariates were dynamically adjusted according to outcome type. To evaluate the potential for collider bias introduced by covariate over-adjustment, a second sensitivity analysis was conducted using a minimal covariate set restricted to age and sex only, with all other analytical parameters held constant. Bonferroni correction factors remained unchanged from the primary analyzes.

Comorbidity analysis by chapter and disease cluster

Hierarchical clustering was performed in the discovery cohort based on the association patterns between metabolites and diseases, using odds ratios (ORs) from logistic regression models for prevalent diseases and hazard ratios (HRs) from Cox proportional hazards models for incident diseases. The number of clusters was varied from 10 to 40, and the optimal cluster number was determined using Silhouette scores. This procedure identified 10 clusters for prevalent diseases and 12 clusters for incident diseases.

To evaluate metabolite associations with disease comorbidity patterns, subsequent analyzes were conducted using the optimal clustering structures derived in the discovery cohort. In the validation cohort, the same clustering assignments were applied to assess the robustness and reproducibility of metabolite-comorbidity associations. For each disease classification, comorbidity was evaluated using two approaches:

Binary comorbidity: individuals with ≥2 diseases within a category were considered comorbid, and logistic regression was used to test associations between metabolites and the binary comorbidity outcome.

Binary comorbidity: individuals with ≥2 diseases within a category were considered comorbid, and logistic regression was used to test associations between metabolites and the binary comorbidity outcome.

Comorbidity burden: excluding participants without any disease in the category, ordinal logistic regression was used to assess associations between metabolites and the number of co-occurring diseases within that category.

Comorbidity burden: excluding participants without any disease in the category, ordinal logistic regression was used to assess associations between metabolites and the number of co-occurring diseases within that category.

Comorbidity categories with fewer than 1000 participants or fewer than 100 cases were excluded from the analysis to ensure robustness. Multiple testing correction was performed using Bonferroni adjustment with a factor of 251× the number of evaluated comorbidity categories ×2.

Model training

To comprehensively evaluate the predictive value of plasma metabolites for disease risk, we developed four types of predictive models based on the timing of disease diagnosis: (1) Baseline Diagnosis Model, (2) Full Follow-up Prediction Model, (3) 5-Year Onset Prediction Model, and (4) Over-5-Year Onset Prediction Model. For the Full Follow-up Prediction Model, individuals with the target disease at baseline were excluded. In the 5-Year Onset Prediction Model, baseline cases were excluded, and individuals who developed the disease more than 5 years after baseline were reclassified as non-cases. Conversely, in the Over-5-Year Onset Prediction Model, individuals who developed the disease within 5 years were excluded. Models were not developed for outcomes with fewer than 100 cases to ensure model stability.

Model development was conducted using LightGBM, a gradient boosting framework. The training process consisted of three stages: Stage 1: All 251 metabolites were used to train disease prediction models. Feature importance rankings were then used to identify the top 30 predictive metabolites, which were selected for further modeling. Stage 2: Using the selected top 30 metabolites, we retrained models and compared their performance with models based on (i) demographic variables only and (ii) a combined model that included both demographic variables and the top 30 metabolites. The demographic variables included age, sex, ethnicity, education level, Townsend Deprivation Index, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and systolic blood pressure (SBP). Model performance was assessed using several metrics: area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, Youden’s index, and F1 score. Stage 3: Differences in AUC between the three feature sets were statistically tested using the DeLong test.

Model training and internal validation were performed in the discovery cohort using stratified tenfold cross-validation, with stratification by geographic assessment center to enhance model generalizability. Distinct sets of predefined hyperparameters were applied in Stages 1 and 2 to optimize performance for each modeling scenario.

For diseases with an AUC of at least 0.70 in the discovery cohort, final models based on the top 30 predictive metabolites were retrained using the full discovery cohort and subsequently evaluated in the independent validation cohort. Model performance in the validation cohort was assessed using the same evaluation metrics described above.

Mendelian randomization

Genetic instruments were obtained for 251 circulating metabolites. Of these, instruments for 249 metabolites were derived from previously published GWAS. For the remaining two metabolites, Glu-lactate and Sc-ala, GWAS were conducted using the same analytical framework as the published studies. Genetic instruments for disease outcomes were obtained from the FinnGen study (release DF12), and GWAS summary statistics were available for 908 diseases overlapping with the present analyzes.

Instrument selection followed established criteria. SNPs associated with each exposure at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were selected. For exposures with no SNPs meeting this threshold, a more lenient cutoff of P < 5 × 10⁻⁶ was applied. Selected SNPs were clumped to ensure independence using linkage disequilibrium thresholds of r² < 0.01 within a 1 Mb window. Variants located within the major histocompatibility complex region (chromosome 6: 25.5-34.0 Mb) were excluded. To minimize weak instrument bias, SNPs with an F-statistic ≤10 were removed. Bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization analyzes were performed to investigate potential causal relationships between circulating metabolites and disease outcomes. For exposures instrumented by a single SNP, causal effects were estimated using the Wald ratio method. For exposures with two or more SNPs, the inverse variance weighted method was used as the primary analytical approach.

To further reduce pleiotropic effects, sensitivity analyzes were restricted to SNPs meeting a stringent association threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸), and variants associated with more than five metabolites or with established confounders, including body mass index, smoking behavior, alcohol consumption, educational attainment, and socioeconomic status, were excluded. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using MR-Egger regression, with the intercept term used to test for directional pleiotropy. Steiger filtering was applied to evaluate the directionality of the inferred causal relationships. MR-Egger and Steiger analyzes were performed for exposures with at least two valid instruments. To address collinearity among Nightingale NMR metabolites, we calculated the effective number of independent metabolites (M_eff) using the participation ratio of the eigenvalue spectrum of the Pearson correlation matrix (M_eff = (Σλᵢ)² / Σλᵢ²)28,29. For both forward and reverse MR associations, metabolites co-associated with the same outcome were considered redundant only if pairwise |r| > 0.8 and effect directions were concordant, retaining the metabolite with the smallest P-value per redundant cluster.

After applying all instrument selection and sensitivity filters, MR analyzes were conducted for 186 metabolites against 908 diseases and for 440 diseases against 251 metabolites. Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple testing, using correction factors of 186 × 908 for forward analyzes and 440 × 251 for reverse analyzes.
