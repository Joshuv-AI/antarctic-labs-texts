Sexual dimorphism shapes renal metabolic adaptation to a ketogenic diet
Authors: Miranda E Kelly, Lauren A Hoffner, Cuauhtemoc B Ramirez, Alexis L Anica, Joohwan Kim, Gregory Tong, Yeojin Kim, Vyshnavi Mannepalli, Wonsuk Choi, Ki-Hong Jang, Yasmine H Alam, Sunhee Jung, Johnny Le, Miranda L Lopez, Varvara I Rubtsova, Hosung Bae, Yujin Chun, Won-Suk Song, Ian J Tamburini, Victor L Schuster, Hoda Anton-Culver, Wei Ling Lau, Thomas F Martinez, Gina Lee, Cholsoon Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYWhile kidneys are essential for maintaining systemic metabolic homeostasis and exhibit sexual dimorphism, the effects of sex and environmental factors, such as diet, on renal metabolism remain unclear. Using kidney-specific arteriovenous (AV) metabolomics, in vivo isotope tracing, and transcriptomics, we discover profound sex differences in kidney metabolic reprogramming under ketogenic diet (KD) in C57BL/6J mice. Tissue metabolomics shows the accumulation of aldosterone and acylcarnitines exclusively in female kidneys under a normal chow (NC) diet, suggesting basal sex differences in sodium and fatty acid metabolism. Under KD, AV metabolomics reveals that only female kidneys activate ketogenesis and gluconeogenesis, supported by transcriptional sex differences in related rate-limiting enzymes and transporters. Given the widespread public and clinical interest in KD for treating epilepsy, metabolic disorders, and cancers, our findings underscore the importance of considering sex differences in kidney metabolism as a fundamental variable when interpreting KD’s diverse effects on pathophysiology and therapeutics.

INTRODUCTION

Kidneys have long been recognized for their critical role in filtering blood and excreting waste. However, kidneys play an equally important role in maintaining whole-body metabolic homeostasis by absorbing and releasing numerous circulating metabolites. For example, along with the liver, kidneys are a vital organ for gluconeogenesis and ketogenesis under certain pathophysiological conditions, including starvation and diabetes.1–6 Kidneys also synthesize critical amino acids such as arginine, serine, and tyrosine and provide them to other organs.7–10 Additionally, kidneys are the sole synthesizer of specific metabolites such as guanidinoacetate, the precursor of creatine, to support energy metabolism of skeletal muscle and heart.11–13 However, how sex or environmental cues such as diet affect these versatile metabolic activities of kidneys remains poorly understood.

Preclinical and clinical studies consider sex as one of the most important biological variables because sex critically influences drug responses in patients with various diseases, including renal diseases.14 For instance, it is well known that females exhibit a more significant response to thiazides, a Na+-Cl− co-transporter (NCC) inhibitor, for hypertension treatment.15–17 This is likely due to higher NCC expression and protein abundance in female kidneys than in males. Sex differences also account for variations in kidney function, which have been attributed to the sexually dimorphic kidney structure, sex hormone receptors, and gene expression.15,18–21 Indeed, the cell sex of proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTECs) affects kidney physiology, with male PTECs showing greater susceptibility to oxidative stress and apoptosis than female PTECs.22 Additionally, cuboidal cell transformation of the outer layer of the glomerular capsule is observed in 60% of males but only in 5% of females.23

Despite this established importance of sex in biology, women remain disproportionately under-represented in biomedical research.24–27 Few studies have illuminated the importance of sex differences in disease prevalence or drug efficacies.16,18,21,22,28,29 One pivotal example is the fact that women experience more severe side effects, such as ketoacidosis, when prescribed sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors to treat type II diabetes.30,31

Dietary intervention studies have also recognized the importance of sex as a key variable. Many studies have demonstrated a sexual dimorphic response to a high-fat diet or a high-fructose diet.32–37 Recently, ketogenic diet (KD), an extremely low-carbohydrate, high-fat-containing diet, has gained great scientific and public interest due to its mitigating effects on renal disease, metabolic disorders, neurological disease, and cancers.38–46 KD intervention studies, especially those using plant-based fats, have shown beneficial effects on kidney function in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) or polycystic kidney disease (PKD).47–50 While sex differences have been reported in adipose tissues under KD,51 how the metabolic activities of the kidneys are differentially regulated between males and females under KD remains elusive.

Here, by developing kidney-specific arteriovenous (AV) metabolite gradient measurements in mice, we discovered remarkable sex differences in kidney metabolic activity under both regular diet and KD conditions. While conventional omics studies have examined sex differences in gene expression, protein abundance, and metabolite levels, our AV analysis infers dynamic metabolic fluxes across kidneys. Unexpectedly, we found female-specific induction of renal gluconeogenesis and ketogenesis upon KD. We further identified previously unrecognized metabolites taken up and released by the kidney in a sex- or diet-dependent manner. These resources, which reveal the profound effects of sex and diet on renal metabolic flux, may provide insights into sex differences in renal disease prevalence and kidney-targeted drug responses. They also offer a rationale for considering sex-dependent renal metabolism as a key variable for interpreting the diverse pathophysiological and therapeutic effects of KD.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Sex-dependent and diet-induced changes in kidney metabolome

To investigate how sex and diet affect kidney metabolism, we performed global untargeted metabolomics in male and female kidney tissues from 10-week-old C57BL/6J mice fed either a normal chow (NC) or KD52–55 (Figure 1A). After 5 weeks of KD, circulating ketone levels were similarly increased in both sexes (Figure 1B).53,56 The mice were then sacrificed in the early morning without fasting, and the kidneys were immediately isolated and frozen by smashing the tissues with a liquid-nitrogen pre-cooled clamp for rapid metabolic quenching.57

Kidneys are metabolically heterogeneous organs, which was confirmed by imaging mass spectrometry (MS).58–61 However, this technology also has some limitations, including inaccurate annotation and limited metabolite coverage due to the lack of chromatographic separation of isomers, low sensitivity, and various matrix effects. Thus, in our study, we utilized high-sensitivity, high-resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to comprehensively elucidate the effects of sex and diet on kidney metabolome.

Using our established metabolomics platform with accurate m/z, retention time, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns for metabolite annotation, we identified 452 aligned rank-transformed (ART)-significant metabolites between the two sexes and diets (Figure 1C; Table S1). Volcano plots highlighting sex differences indicated that, under NC-fed conditions, 115 metabolites were significantly higher in female kidneys, while 67 metabolites were higher in male kidneys (Figure 1D). Intriguingly, aldosterone, a hormone that regulates kidney’s sodium and water reabsorption, was much higher in female kidneys than in males (Figure 1E). By comparing aldosterone levels in kidneys with or without adrenal glands and also dissecting the cortex and medulla, we confirmed that this higher aldosterone level in female kidneys is not due to the contamination of adrenal glands (Figures S1A and S1B). High circulating aldosterone levels in males compared with females have been observed.62–64 However, aldosterone levels can be affected by the menstrual cycle, with the luteal phase typically resulting in elevated levels of aldosterone in circulation in females65 and comparable levels to males during the follicular phase.66 Additionally, sodium intake and posture have been identified as an important variable that influences aldosterone via the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), which has also resulted in conflicting results between studies.67–69 Finally, human data confirmed that pre- and post-menopausal, hypertensive or normotensive women have higher aldosterone levels than men.70 Further studies will be required to identify the mechanisms and pathophysiological significance of high aldosterone levels in female kidneys.

To further identify sex differences in renal metabolism, we performed an unbiased classification of metabolites using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) (Figure 1D). This analysis revealed that many acylcarnitine species were more abundant in females than males in NC-fed conditions (Figures 1F and 1G). However, free carnitine levels did not differ between sexes (Figure 1H), suggesting that the increased acylcarnitine levels in female kidneys likely reflect elevated conjugation of fatty acids to carnitine for their enhanced oxidation.

Intriguingly, KD feeding overall suppressed sex differences in the kidney tissue metabolome (Figures 1C and 1I). For example, sex differences in acylcarnitines disappeared in KD-fed conditions (Figure 1J). Amino acids and organic acids that were distinct between sexes under NC were also no longer different between sexes under KD (Figures 1D and 1I; Table S1). In contrast, aldosterone was still high in female kidneys even under KD (Figure 1E and 1I), suggesting its important role regardless of KD. Altogether, these data uncover the potent impact of sex and diet on kidney tissue metabolome.

Metabolite production and consumption by kidneys

To investigate the mechanisms by which sex and diet influence metabolite abundances in kidneys, we conducted kidney-specific arteriovenous (AV) metabolomics and urine metabolomics to identify the sources and destinations of metabolites.12 From anesthetized mice, we sampled arterial (A) blood that feeds the kidneys, renal venous (RV) blood that drains the kidneys, and urine from the bladder. Then, we calculated metabolite concentration differences between the two blood samples (A vs. RV) and between the arterial and urine (A vs. urine) to quantify the kidney’s net uptake, release, and urinary excretion of metabolites (Figure 2A). Given the significant renal metabolomic changes after 5 weeks of KD feeding in both sexes (Figure 1C), we performed this assay in multiple time frames (0, 3, and 5 weeks of KD feeding) to understand the progressive metabolic rewiring of kidneys during diet switching (Figure S2A).

To verify our kidney-specific AV metabolomics technique, we first examined the well-known metabolite production and consumption by kidneys, such as the active release of guanidinoacetate, a creatine precursor synthesized solely by the kidney11,12,71 (Figure S2B). In addition, we verified the previously reported renal uptake of citrate, a fuel source that kidneys preferentially utilize to generate energy12 (Figure S2C). Importantly, these metabolic activities of the kidneys were preserved across sexes and during diet switching, suggesting their critical roles regardless of these variables.

AV metabolomics revealed that kidneys released many metabolites under NC conditions, but such activity was suppressed after diet switching to KD in both sexes (Figures 2B–2G; Table S2). In terms of metabolite uptake, the kidneys overall took up more metabolites than they released, regardless of sex or diet. Uptake of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates was most prominent (Figures 2B–2G and S2D), suggesting renal utilization of these metabolites as fuels or substrates for biomolecule synthesis. The data also captured previously unrecognized metabolic activities of kidneys, including the uptake of circulating glutathione and hypotaurine, which was consistently observed in both sexes and during diet switching (Figures 2B–2G and S2E). Kidneys may utilize these sulfate-containing metabolites to alleviate oxidative stress generated from mitochondrial fuel oxidation.72,73 In contrast, kidneys generally released select amino acids (e.g., glutamate and cystine) (Figure S2F), suggesting that kidneys contribute to the circulating pool of these metabolites for other organs’ usage.

Next, we have performed an unbiased correlation analysis between kidney-blood metabolomics and AV metabolomics to identify relationships between them (Figure 2H; Table S3). We previously performed a similar analysis in our AV metabolomics study of cystic fibrosis in pigs.74 Interestingly, fatty acids showed statistically significant positive biweight midcorrelations, which were validated using Pearson correlation (Figures 2I and 2J), highlighting that fatty acid trafficking by kidneys is driven by a concentration gradient (higher levels in kidneys than in the blood, greater release). In contrast, negative correlations may highlight metabolites taken up by the kidneys from circulation against concentration gradients, indicative of active renal uptake. For example, KD-fed mice exhibited a significant negative biweight midcorrelation (bicor = −0.51, p = 0.03) of sebacate (Table S3), a dicarboxylic acid that increases with KD in humans75 and is an energy substrate mainly made by fatty acid oxidation in the liver.76 NC-fed mice also exhibited a strong trend (bicor = −0.46, p = 0.05), suggesting that liver-kidney axis controls circulating concentrations of sebacate. Biweight midcorrelations for other metabolites are listed in Table S3.

To gain insights into the relationship between renal metabolite uptake and urinary excretion or renal consumption, we have performed urine metabolomics in the same animals (Figure S2G; Table S4) and conducted a correlation analysis between kidney AV gradient (log2[RV/A]) and urinary excretion (log2[urine/A]) (Figure 2K), as we performed previously.74 Intriguingly, the two amino acids (glutamate and aspartate) exhibited a strong biweight midcorrelation between kidney release and urinary excretion, which was verified using Pearson correlation (Figures 2L and 2M), indicating simultaneous release into both circulation and urine. Such glutamate and aspartate excretion would affect renal nitrogen balance, although our nitrogen balance analysis indicated their minimal impact (see below), likely because they are the least abundant amino acids in circulation. In contrast, hypotaurine exhibited a significant negative biweight midcorrelation (bicor = −0.35, p = 0.03), with more uptake by the kidney and more release into urine, consistent with the kidneys’ filtrating roles (Table S4). Biweight midcorrelations for other metabolites are listed in Table S4. Together, these correlation analyses suggested potential mechanisms of metabolite trafficking (concentration-dependent movement vs. active transport) by the kidneys and the fate of metabolites (renal consumption vs. urinary excretion).

Sexually dimorphic renal fatty acid uptake and ketogenesis upon KD

Given that metabolites with high blood concentrations are likely the major contributors to metabolic fluxes, we next determined kidney-specific trafficking of the 30 most highly abundant circulating metabolites (e.g., glucose, lactate, ketone, amino acids, and fatty acids). This analysis revealed a profound sex difference in response to diet. For example, diet switching to KD for 5 weeks induced the absorption of non-esterified, albumin-bound fatty acids (NEFAs) by female kidneys but much less so by male kidneys (Figures 3A and 3B) despite similar circulating concentrations of NEFAs between sexes (Figures S3A and S3B). Such NEFA uptake by KD-fed female kidneys was overall proportional to blood concentrations, with linoleic acid (C18:2) showing the highest uptake (~200 μM) and the highest blood concentrations (~750 μM) among NEFAs (Figures 3B and S3B).

The KD-elicited sex difference in kidney handling of circulating NEFAs was somewhat counterintuitive, given the disappearance of sex differences in tissue acylcarnitine levels by KD (Figure 1J). Thus, we hypothesize that this discrepancy may suggest a distinct metabolic fate of NEFAs after uptake by male and female kidneys. One possibility is renal ketogenesis from NEFAs,1,77,78 which is vital for other critical organs that use ketones, including the brain, muscles, and the heart. In fact, female kidneys actively released 3-hydroxybutyrate (3-HB) upon diet switching to KD for 5 weeks (Figure 3C), matching the timing of the increased NEFA uptake (Figures 3A and 3B). In contrast, male kidneys tended to take up 3-HB (Figure 3C) despite their shift toward uptake of linoleic acid (C18:2) (Figure 3B), suggesting that male kidneys use linoleic acid for other purposes, such as complete oxidation to CO2 or lipid synthesis, which we did not measure in this study. Thus, in response to KD, only females activate renal NEFA uptake and ketogenesis, likely contributing to the versatile health-beneficial effects of KD.

Sexually dimorphic renal gluconeogenesis upon KD

Under low-carbohydrate KD conditions, maintaining glucose homeostasis is challenging, and the liver and kidneys may boost glucose production via gluconeogenesis.1–6,79 Thus, we next examined potential sex differences in renal gluconeogenesis upon diet switching to KD. Only female kidneys net released glucose upon diet switching to KD (Figure 3D) despite similar blood glucose concentrations between sexes (Figure S3C). Importantly, the amount of glucose released by female kidneys is negatively correlated with circulating glucose concentrations (r = −0.5393, p = 0.0006), with more glucose being released when blood glucose levels are low (Figure 3E). This relationship suggests that female kidneys are sensitive to circulating glucose levels and adjust gluconeogenesis, reflecting homeostatic regulation mechanisms.4,37,79,80 Male kidneys also showed a significant yet weaker correlation (r = −0.3539, p = 0.0271) (Figure 3F).

To determine the mechanisms underlying the female kidney’s gluconeogenesis upon KD, we investigated the renal uptake of glycerol, glutamine, and lactate, the gluconeogenic substrates abundant in blood (Figures S3D–S3F). For glycerol measurement by LC-MS, we adopted a recently published method that used a glycerol kinase reaction followed by glycerol-3-phosphate quantitation.81 Overall, both male and female kidneys tended to take up all three gluconeogenic substrates, with lactate showing the weakest trend and decreased blood concentrations after KD feeding (Figures 3G–3I and S3F).

To directly test whether circulating glycerol and glutamine serve as gluconeogenic substrates in kidneys upon KD, we performed in vivo stable isotope tracing using 13C5-glutamine and D5-glycerol (Figure 3J). D5-glycerol can enter the gluconeogenesis pathway through glycerol 3-phosphate, generating [M+3] dihydroxyacetone phosphate and [M+3] glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (Figure S4A). These triose phosphate metabolites can subsequently be converted to [M+4] and [M+5] glucose by hydrogen exchange and the involvement of downstream pathways (e.g., the TCA cycle). As such, D5-glycerol oral gavage resulted in various labeled glucose in the blood (Figure S4B). Kidney AV gradient measurements indicated that only KD-fed female mice showed a significant release of labeled glucose from kidneys, although other groups also displayed some trends (Figure 3K). 13C5-glutamine provision also led to glucose labeling in the blood (Figures S4A and S4C), consistent with gluconeogenesis from glutamine. However, AV gradient measurements revealed minimal uptake of labeled glucose by kidneys (Figure 3L). Thus, KD-fed female kidneys utilize circulating glycerol for gluconeogenesis.

Sex-dependent and diet-induced carbon trafficking by kidneys

To obtain a more quantitative picture of kidney metabolic rewiring during diet switching to KD, we next determined the absolute concentrations of 30 major circulating nutrients across our experimental conditions using isotope-labeled metabolite standards (Table S5). We then calculated the renal uptake and release of total carbon concentrations carried by these abundant metabolites (e.g., six carbons for glucose and sixteen for palmitate; see STAR Methods for details) (Table S5).

This quantitative analysis uncovered that, in NC-fed females (week 0), the primary carbon carriers into the kidney are circulating glucose and lactate, accounting for ~80% of the total carbon influx (Figure 4A). Circulating citrate also contributes significantly to carbon influx (~10%). On the other hand, carbon efflux is carried by fatty acids and amino acids. Consequently, the total carbon influx and efflux are reasonably well balanced.

However, 3 weeks after KD challenge, female kidneys decreased carbon influx carried by lactate (Figure 4B). Instead, glutamine (~15%) joins glucose as a significant contributor to total carbon influx. Despite 3 weeks of KD, there was no net uptake of NEFA-derived carbons. In terms of efflux, the release of carbons carried by amino acids decreased. More considerable changes were observed in female kidneys at 5 weeks after KD challenge (Figure 4C). The female kidneys exhibited a substantial increase in carbon influx carried by circulating NEFAs. The quantity of such incoming carbons is greater than the carbon quantity released as 3-HB, suggesting that the majority of NEFAs are used in other pathways (e.g., oxidation, lipid synthesis, etc.) in addition to 3-HB production.

Compared to female kidneys, male kidneys showed quite different carbon trafficking patterns. For example, male kidneys at week 0 showed no net glucose uptake and ~30% of total carbon influx compared to that in female kidneys (Figure 4D). However, at 3 weeks after diet switching to KD, male kidneys increased carbon influx carried by glucose, 3-HB, glutamine, and linoleic acid (Figure 4E). At 5 weeks after KD challenge, male kidneys increased the uptake of carbons from unsaturated NEFAs as well as glucose and 3-HB (Figure 4F). Thus, kidneys exhibit sexually dimorphic carbon influx and efflux during metabolic remodeling in response to KD.

Sex-dependent and diet-induced nitrogen trafficking by kidneys

Kidneys play key roles in whole-body nitrogen homeostasis by producing and releasing amino acids.7–10 However, how kidneys regulate nitrogen metabolism in different dietary conditions and whether sex differences exist in this process are unknown. Thus, we next quantitatively analyzed nitrogen influx and efflux by male and female kidneys during diet switching to KD (Table S5).

Female kidneys on the NC diet (week 0) took up nitrogen as non-essential amino acids (NEAAs), such as glycine, alanine, and proline as well as essential amino acids (EAAs), mostly branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) (Figure 5A). In terms of nitrogen efflux, female kidneys utilized NEAAs as nitrogen carriers, which seems to be an efficient mechanism that avoids protein breakdown while preserving EAAs that tissues cannot synthesize. However, we recognized tryptophan as a unique EAA released by the kidneys (Figure 5A), which was also seen in male kidneys (Figure 5D). It was previously shown that kidneys are critical for regulating circulating tryptophan levels, and various kidney diseases alter systemic tryptophan metabolism.82–86 Thus, the physiological significance of tryptophan release by the kidneys merits further investigation.

At 3 weeks after diet switching to KD, female kidneys took up a substantial quantity of nitrogen as circulating glutamine (Figure 5B). In contrast, they decreased nitrogen release by ~5-fold. This shift created a significant imbalance between nitrogen influx and efflux. The missing nitrogen efflux may be partly explained by urinary excretion of aspartate, glutamate, and other nitrogen-carrying metabolites (Figures 2L and 2M; Table S4). Furthermore, female kidneys may release nitrogen through secreted proteins that facilitate inter-organ communications. Indeed, kidneys are a vital endocrine organ, releasing many critical protein hormones, including erythropoietin, renin, kinins, and gastrin.87–90 Alternatively, female kidneys may utilize nitrogen for protein synthesis to support their remodeling upon KD challenge. Intriguingly, at 5 weeks after diet switching to KD, female kidneys restored the nitrogen balance, primarily by increasing nitrogen release as NEAAs (Figure 5C). This restored nitrogen balance may implicate the completion of the metabolic adaptation, which can be reflected by active ketogenesis and gluconeogenesis (Figures 3C and 3D).

In contrast to the marked sex differences in carbon trafficking (Figure 4), male kidneys showed nitrogen trafficking patterns quite similar to those of female kidneys. Nevertheless, one notable sex difference was the release of nitrogen carried by methionine in male kidneys on week 5 after KD challenge (Figure 5F). Methionine synthesis has been reported primarily in the kidneys and liver.91,92 From our RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis, we found sex differences in gene expression of key methionine synthesis enzymes. For example, betaine homocysteine methyltransferase (Bhmt) was expressed exclusively in female kidneys, while the isozyme Bhmt2 was significantly higher in males than females (Figures S5A and S5B). Future studies are necessary to determine whether this sex difference in gene expression drives male kidneys’ release of methionine.

Differential effects of sex and diet on kidney transcriptome

We next aimed to determine whether our observed sexual dimorphism of kidney metabolic activities is transcriptionally regulated. RNA-seq analysis of kidneys in both sexes and during diet switching to KD identified 11,760 differentially expressed genes by two-way ANOVA (Figure 6A; Table S6). More specifically, we performed a two-way ANOVA to check the following: (1) whether the relationship between diet and gene expression is affected by sex (i.e., comparing changes in gene expression between KD- and NC-fed mice and then comparing whether these diet-effects differ between sexes; 1,447 genes were identified), (2) whether sex alone affects gene expression (i.e., comparing gene expression between all males and all females to identify sex-dependent changes regardless of diet; 10,614 genes were identified), and (3) whether diet alone affects gene expression (i.e., comparing gene expression between all KD- and NC-fed mice to identify diet-dependent changes regardless of diet; 2,945 genes were identified). Principal-component analysis (PCA) demonstrated that sex exerts a much greater impact (56.97%) than diet (7.19%) on variance in gene expression (Figure S5C). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of sex-dependent genes revealed various pathways differentially expressed between sexes (Figure 6A). For example, male kidneys highly express genes important for metabolism, such as protein processing, peroxisome, BCAA catabolism, and fatty acid degradation. In contrast, female kidneys highly express genes related to aldosterone synthesis and secretion (which we observed in Figure 1E), cancer, or infection. While this bulk RNA-seq data does not provide information about cell types or tissue regions, a recent single-nucleus RNA-seq identified that the proximal tubules of kidneys show strong sex biases in gene expression, with males showing greater expression of fatty acid metabolic pathways and females showing greater expression of pathways related to cancer.61

Because the GSEA analysis failed to pinpoint gene signatures that could explain the female-specific ketogenesis and gluconeogenesis upon KD, we hypothesized that the kidneys may alter only a select subset of genes to rewire metabolism, such as rate-limiting enzymes or transporters. Thus, we first examined rate-limiting enzymes for ketogenesis (hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2, HMGCS2) and gluconeogenesis (phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 2, PCK2). We found strong induction of both enzymes at the protein levels in 5-week KD-fed female kidneys, with both cortex and medulla showing similar induction (Figures 6B, 6C, S6A, and S6B). Consistently, RNA-seq of the whole kidney also showed similar induction of these enzymes (Figures 6D and 6E; Table S6). However, other rate-limiting gluconeogenic enzymes (Fbp1 and G6pc) exhibited no sex difference, and KD feeding induced changes only in Fbp1 expression (Figures 6D and 6E; Table S6). Similarly, KD did not induce transporters of 3-HB (Slc16a6), fatty acids (CD36, Slc27a1, and Slc27a4), gluconeogenic substrates (Slc16a family members, Slc38a1, and Slc1a5), glucose (Slc2a1), or long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase 1 (Acsl1) in either sex (Figures 6D and 6E; Table S6).

We then examined whether metabolic stress (ATP/AMP ratio), redox balance (NADH/NAD+, NADPH/NADP+, lactate/pyruvate) or mitochondrial functions (TCA metabolites as a proxy) may contribute to female-specific ketogenesis and gluconeogenesis. NC-fed female kidneys showed significantly higher ATP/AMP ratio than NC-fed male kidneys (Figure S6C). However, after KD feeding, female kidneys tended to decrease ATP/AMP ratio, which may signal the need for energy production, driving both ketogenesis and gluconeogenesis. In terms of metabolites indicating redox balance or mitochondrial functions, we did not observe consistent sex differences or diet effects (Figures S6D–S6G).

Altogether, these data revealed the profound effect of sex, yet the marginal impact of KD on the kidney transcriptome. Differential expression of the rate-limiting enzyme, such as HMGCS2 and PCK2 or energy stress may contribute to female-specific induction of ketogenesis and gluconeogenesis upon KD. Decreased renal ketogenesis and gluconeogenesis are well-known manifestations in patients with CKD,93,94 yet no study has carefully examined the contribution of sex differences in renal metabolism to disease outcomes and treatment efficacies. The resource generated by this study also identified various metabolites previously unrecognized as being secreted or taken up by the kidneys and their differential regulation by sex and diet. Such knowledge will provide insights into the clinical disparities between male and female patients with various renal diseases and patients treated with KD for mitigating epilepsy, metabolic disorders, and cancers.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. While we focused on ad lib-fed conditions in healthy, young mice, future studies can use the same approach to determine how fasting, re-feeding, and various disease conditions, such as diabetes, hypertension, CKD, and PKD, change renal metabolic activities in a sex-dependent and age-dependent manner. In the kidneys, the cortex and medulla are known to exhibit distinct metabolic activities.95,96 Single-cell RNA-seq analysis, proteomics, and spatial metabolomics will provide more important information such as cell types responsible for our observed metabolic activities. Kidneys may use circulating lipids,97,98 proteins, or other macromolecules stored in kidneys, which we did not measure in this study. Interestingly, our RNA-seq data indicated a substantial reduction of lipoprotein lipase expression upon KD (Table S6), suggesting a decreased contribution of circulating lipids to kidney carbon influx. Regarding the integration of urine and AV metabolomics, measurement of excreted urea would greatly benefit our investigation of renal nitrogen balance. Additionally, the female mice used in our study were not controlled for estrous cycle at the time of sample collection, which may explain some metabolic differences, like aldosterone accumulation. Lastly, our study used only one mouse strain while metabolically diverse mouse strains respond to dietary changes differentially.99,100 Future studies are thus warranted to determine whether the findings highlighted here can be generalized across mouse strains and, more importantly, in humans, which is critical for translational applications of this study.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Mice

All research complies with relevant ethical regulations set by the NIH and IACUC. Nine-week-old male and female C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and housed in a pathogen-free barrier facility with a 12-h light/dark cycle at the University of California, Irvine. All animal experiments were approved by the University of California Irvine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. At 10 weeks old, mice were fed either a ketogenic diet (Envigo, TD.160153, 90.5% kcal from fat, 9.5% kcal from protein, 0.0% from carbohydrate) or a control chow (Envigo, TD.2020X, 16% kcal from fat, 24% kcal from protein, 60% from carbohydrate). For stable isotope tracing experiments, 10-week-old male and female mice were fed either a ketogenic diet (Envigo, TD.160153, 90.5% kcal from fat, 9.5% kcal from protein, 0.0% from carbohydrate) or an ingredient matched control chow (Envigo, TD. 150345, 12.6% kcal from fat, 9.8% kcal from protein, 77.7% from carbohydrate).

METHOD DETAILS

Kidney AV blood sampling and tissue processing

At 8–9 AM, ad-lib-fed mice were anesthetized with isoflurane using the drop method and placed on a dissection tray with a nose cone. The abdominal cavity was opened with a small incision, and all abdominal organs were moved with fine surgical scissors to reveal the left kidney. The renal vein was identified and then punctured with an insulin syringe. ~20 μL of blood was slowly collected with the syringe, then the renal vein was clamped as the syringe was removed. Renal vein blood was transferred into an Eppendorf tube and stored on ice. Immediately after, the thoracic cavity was quickly opened by cutting through the ribcage and the aorta was cut with fine surgical scissors. ~50 μL of pooled systemic blood in the thoracic cavity was collected with a cut pipet tip, transferred to an Eppendorf tube and stored on ice. Blood collection was completed within 5 min post-anesthesia. For serum preparation, blood samples were placed on ice in the anticoagulant-free tube for 20 min, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was stored at −80°C and analyzed within one week. The kidney tissues were snap-frozen using a liquid nitrogen-cooled Wollenburg clamp. Tissues were put into 2 mL Eppendorf tubes with a pre-cooled 5 mm metal bead. The cryo-mill (Retsch, Newtown, PA) was pre-cooled before loading 2 mL tubes. Once pre-cooled, the tubes were loaded, and all tissues were milled at 25 Hz for 1–2 min. Metal beads were removed, and tissue powder was stored at −80°C.

Quantitative analysis of arteriovenous metabolite exchange

For quantification of arteriovenous metabolite exchange, total ion counts (TIC) were normalized by spiked isotopically labeled internal standard. Blank samples were run to subtract the background from experimental samples. Absolute metabolite concentrations were calculated using standard curves derived from the measurement of stable isotope-labeled tracers for 30 major circulating nutrients. AV calculations were adapted from Park et al.101 using arterial (A) and renal venous (RV) metabolite concentrations. Briefly, AV gradients of each metabolite were calculated by taking the difference between the RV and A serum abundances (RV-A); positive values indicate release and negative values reflect uptake. A two-way ANOVA test was performed on these AV values to identify metabolites with significant gradient exchange. Classifications according to the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) were applied to each metabolite and colored accordingly in volcano plots to better identify groups of metabolites. For carbon and nitrogen exchange, we calculated the median concentration for each of the 30 abundant circulating metabolites and multiplied these values by the number of carbon or nitrogen atoms present in the molecular formula.72

Soluble metabolite extraction and measurements using LC-MS

For tissue metabolite extraction, frozen and cryo-milled tissue powder (~20 mg) was mixed with 40:40:20 mixture of acetonitrile:methanol:water (extraction solvent) to make 25 mg of tissue/mL of solvent, vortexed, and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. 3 μL of supernatant was injected into LC-MS. For serum, 150 μL of extraction solvent was added to 5 μL of serum and processed the same as tissue extraction. Our extraction solvent effectively disassociates fatty acids from albumin. A quadrupole orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive; ThermoFisher Scientific) operating in negative or positive ion mode was coupled to a Vanquish UHPLC system (ThermoFisher Scientific) with electrospray ionization and used to scan from m/z 70 to 1,000 at 2 Hz, with a 140,000 resolution. LC separation was achieved on an XBridge BEH Amide column (2.1 × 150 mm2, 2.5 μm particle size, 130 Å pore size; Waters Corporation) using a gradient of solvent A (95:5 water: acetonitrile with 20 mM of ammonium acetate and 20 mM of ammonium hydroxide, pH 9.45) and solvent B (acetonitrile). Flow rate was 150μL/min. The LC gradient was: 0 min, 85% B; 2 min, 85% B; 3 min, 80% B; 5 min, 80% B; 6 min, 75% B; 7 min, 75% B; 8 min, 70% B; 9 min, 70% B; 10 min, 50% B; 12 min, 50% B; 13 min, 25% B; 16 min, 25% B; 18 min, 0% B; 23 min, 0% B; 24 min, 85% B; and 30 min, 85% B. The autosampler temperature was 5°C and the injection volume was 3μL. Metabolite identity was confirmed by authentic standard chemicals from Sigma or predicted by MS/MS profiles. Data were analyzed using the EI-MAVEN and Compound Discoverer software (Thermofisher Scientific). Classifications according to the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) were applied to each metabolite and colored accordingly in volcano plots to better identify groups of metabolites.

Glycerol measurements using LC-MS

Enzymatic derivatization of glycerol to glycerol-3-phosphate was conducted using ATP (Abcam, AB146525) and glycerol kinase (Sigma-Aldrich, G6142–1KU), adapted from the previously published protocol.81 Enzyme Mix was prepared by resuspending purified glycerol kinase from Cellulomonas sp in Derivatization Buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM ATP, and 10% sucrose) to a concentration of 40 U/mL. 5 μL of serum was combined with 2.5 μL Enzyme Mix and allowed to sit at room temperature for 10 min. Enzymatic reactions were quenched by adding 950 μL extraction solvent containing 0.1 M formic acid and immediately neutralized with 88 μL of 15% NH4HCO3. Samples were vortexed and centrifuged at 4°C for 10 min at 16,000 g. 5 μL was injected for LC-MS analysis in a quadrupole orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive; ThermoFisher Scientific) operating in negative ion mode and coupled to a Vanquish UHPLC system ThermoFisher Scientific).

13C5-glutamine and D5-glycerol stable isotope tracing in mice

9-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Mice were housed at 22°C with a 12h light/12h dark cycle, free water access, and a chow diet. At 10 weeks old, mice were fed either a ketogenic diet or a control chow. After 5 weeks of feeding, mice were administered a solution of 10 mg/mL 13C5-glutamine (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, CLM-1822-H-0.25) (100 μg/g BW) and 10 mg/mL D5-glycerol (DLM-1229–1) (100 μg/g BW) via oral gavage using a plastic feeding tube (Instech Laboratories). For blood timecourse, tail blood was collected from one KD-fed female mice before oral gavage (0 min) and again at 5, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. The blood samples were immediately placed on ice without anticoagulant and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to separate the serum. To capture renal gluconeogenesis, mice were sacrificed in the morning (9 a.m.) 15 min after receiving oral gavage. Following AV blood collection, kidney tissues were rapidly dissected and snap-frozen. Natural isotope correction was performed with AccuCor2 R code (https://github.com/wangyujue23/AccuCor2).

RNA-sequencing

RNA was isolated from tissues using Qiazol (QIAGEN) and an RNeasy kit (QIAGEN). Raw sequencing data was assessed for quality using FastQC and trimmed by adapter sequences using Trim Galore with the –paired setting. The trimmed paired end reads were aligned to the mm10 reference genome using STAR with the following settings: –outFilterMismatchNmax 2 –outFilterMultimapNmax 4-chimScoreSeparation 10 –chimScoreMin 20 –chimSegmentMin 15 –outSAMattributes All –outSAMtype BAM SortedByCoordinate. The resulting bam file was filtered for primary alignments using samtools with the flag 0X100. Multimappers were removed from the alignment file using samtools with setting -bq 255. Reads were quantified using HOMER’s (Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment) analyzeRepeats with the following settings: -count exons -strand + -condenseGenes -norm 1e7. Differential analysis was performed using HOMER’s getDiffExpression with the following settings: -AvsA −log2fold 0.5.

Western blot

Tissues were lysed by radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors (PhosSTOP and cOmplete Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail; Roche). Detergent compatible protein assay (Bio-rad) was used to measure protein concentration. Proteins were boiled for 10 min with Laemmli sample buffer. SDS-PAGE gels were used to separate proteins (15–60 μg) and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane (Amersham Biosciences). Membranes were then incubated with Odyssey blocking solution (LI-COR Biosciences), followed by incubation with primary and IRDye secondary (LI-COR Biosciences) antibodies. Immunoblot signals were detected and quantified by Odyssey imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences). Antibodies against Hmgcs2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-393256) and PCK2 (CST, 6924) were used. For each microdissected kidney analyzed (3 biological replicates), matched liver lysate was used as a positive control for protein detection and subsequently excluded from analysis (see Data S1).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analysis was performed using Graphpad Prism 9.0 and R software (rstatix). Global sex and diet effects were assessed using aligned rank transform (ART) models in R (ARTool). Heatmaps were generated using R software (gplots). To determine the significant uptake and release of metabolites, a one-sample Wilcoxon test was used to calculate p values, with p < 0.05 used to determine statistical significance. When two groups were compared, a two-tailed, unpaired Mann-Whitney was used to calculate p values, with p < 0.05 used to determine statistical significance. When >2 groups were compared, within-sex diet effects were assessed using Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test and sex differences at each diet timepoint were evaluated using two-tailed, unpaired Mann-Whitney tests. All metabolomics analyses were non-parametric and p-values were adjusted for accordingly. For correlation analysis, biweight correlations were performed using R software (bicor) and interesting correlations were checked with Pearson correlation test using Graphpad Prism 9.0 software.
