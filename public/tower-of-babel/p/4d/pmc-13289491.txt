Health-related factors and epigenetic alterations in metabolic comorbidities patients and smokers with carpal tunnel syndrome: a preliminary investigation of patient-derived samples
Authors: Abdulaziz M. F. Shayea, Asmaa Kh. AlRumaihi, Alalyaa Kh. AlSabah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is a common painful musculoskeletal disorder caused by inflammation and hypertrophy of the flexor retinaculum. Significant relationships exist between CTS, metabolic syndrome, smoking, and health-related factors, but the molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood.MethodsThis cross-sectional preliminary investigation was conducted at Al-Razi Hospital (Department of Orthopaedics), Kuwait, between October 2022 and September 2023. Tissue samples from the flexor retinaculum were collected following carpal tunnel release surgery from patients with symptomatic CTS. Seven groups were included: healthy control (n = 10), trigger finger (control for gene expression analysis; n = 10), CTS (n = 10), diabetic CTS (n = 9), hypertensive CTS (n = 7), dyslipidemic CTS (n = 7), and smoker CTS (n = 9). Expression levels of CTS-related genes (COL-I, COL-II) and inflammatory genes (TNF-α) were evaluated by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Participants completed the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Disability of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH), Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NRS), and Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) questionnaires. One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc correction and Pearson correlation analyses were used, with adjustment for confounders using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs).ResultsA total of 62 participants (33 males, 29 females; mean age 54 ± 6.9 years; mean BMI 28.2 ± 3.4 kg/m2) were enrolled. Hand dysfunction was significantly greater in metabolic CTS and smoker groups versus CTS group (p < 0.0001; 95% CI for DASH score: 15.224.6). Stress levels were significantly higher in smoker CTS (p = 0.024; 95% CI: 2.3–8.7) and hypertensive CTS groups (p = 0.013; 95% CI: 1.9–7.4) compared to CTS alone. The diabetic CTS group showed significant sleep disturbance (p < 0.0001; 95% CI: 3.5–9.2) and increased pain (p < 0.0001; 95% CI: 2.1–5.8) compared to CTS alone. Life satisfaction was significantly lower in diabetic and hypertensive CTS groups (p < 0.0001; 95% CI for SWLS score reduction: -12.4 to -5.8). COL-I, COL-II, and TNF-α gene expression significantly increased in metabolic CTS and smoker groups (p < 0.0001; 95% CI for fold-change: 1.8–3.4). Strong correlations were observed between gene expression and all health-related factors (r = 0.34–0.93, p < 0.01).ConclusionsThis preliminary investigation demonstrates that hand dysfunction, stress levels, pain severity, and reduced life satisfaction in CTS patients are strongly associated with increased inflammation (tumor necrosis factor-alpha) and collagen gene expression (collagen type I and collagen type II) in patients with diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia and smokers with CTS. These findings provide mechanistic insights into CTS pathophysiology. Further rigorous research with larger samples is needed to validate these preliminary findings.

Introduction

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is a common painful musculoskeletal disorder caused by inflammation and hypertrophy of the flexor retinaculum. Significant relationships exist between CTS, metabolic syndrome, smoking, and health-related factors, but the molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. The pathophysiology of CTS is complex. Several theories have been proposed to explain its symptoms, including mechanical compression, microvascular insufficiency, and vibration. The mechanical compression theory attributes CTS to compression of the median nerve at the carpal tunnel level [1]. The microvascular insufficiency theory proposes that reduced blood supply leads to depletion of nutrients and oxygen to the nerve, gradually impairing its ability to transmit impulses [2]. The vibration theory suggests that prolonged use of vibrating tools may affect the median nerve within the carpal tunnel [2]. Overall, the pathophysiology of CTS is intricate and results from the interaction of multiple processes [3]. CTS significantly affects hand function. Compression of the median nerve causes numbness and tingling in the thumb, index finger, middle finger, and part of the ring finger, making it difficult to perform tasks requiring delicate sensation or precision [4]. Weakness in the affected hand leads to challenges with gripping or performing strength-dependent activities [5]. Pain and discomfort in the hand, wrist, and forearm can limit repetitive movements or sustained activities [6]. Furthermore, CTS impairs fine motor skills, reducing hand dexterity and causing difficulties with tasks such as buttoning shirts or picking up small objects. Loss of coordination may also occur, affecting precise movements such as writing or playing musical instruments. The severity of these symptoms varies, highlighting the need for early diagnosis and appropriate treatment.

Metabolic dysregulation has been linked to worse patient-reported outcomes in chronic pain conditions. In fibromyalgia syndrome, metabolic syndrome and higher body mass index (BMI) have been associated with impaired quality of life and functional status [7]. This broader context supports the relevance of examining metabolic factors in CTS.

New studies have shown that obesity and CTS are strongly linked, and previous studies suggest a possible common genetic factor between the two conditions. CTS frequently occurs in individuals with diabetes [8]. The heightened vulnerability of peripheral nerve trunks to compression in diabetic patients is likely influenced by both biochemical and structural modifications within the peripheral nerve [8]. Although one study found a weak relationship between hypertension and CTS, it suggested that CTS elevates arterial pressure, causing mild vasodilatation and temporary relief of the median nerve via microcirculatory compensation [9]. However, this relief is short-lived because long-term hypertension can lead to small-vessel disease [1]. According to the microcirculation ischemic theory, smoking may also increase the risk of CTS [9].

CTS is one of the most prevalent entrapment neuropathies. The etiology of the majority of cases is considered idiopathic [10]. Tendons and connective tissue components within the carpal tunnel may be involved, and abnormalities in these structures may contribute to CTS development. Genetic variables affecting collagen fibrils, which are crucial components of connective tissues (tendons, ligaments, bone), also play a role [11]. Three genetic mechanisms have been proposed: altered collagen production, altered collagen breakdown, and impaired protection against oxidative stress in connective tissue [12].

Despite growing evidence linking metabolic comorbidities and smoking to CTS, no previous study has simultaneously investigated: (1) health-related factors (hand function, stress, sleep, pain, life satisfaction), and (2) epigenetic alterations in collagen (COL-I, COL-II) and inflammatory (TNF-α) genes in the flexor retinaculum tissue of CTS patients with metabolic comorbidities or smoking history. Recent studies have identified epigenetic modifications in diabetic tendinopathies [13, 14], but whether similar mechanisms operate in hypertensive, dyslipidemic, or smoking-related CTS remains unknown. Therefore, this preliminary investigation is needed to generate hypothesis-generating data on molecular and health-related factor profiles across these clinically important CTS subgroups.

Research gaps identified

The following gaps were identified from previous studies: (1) no previous study has compared COL-I and COL-II gene expression across diabetic, hypertensive, dyslipidemic, and smoking CTS subgroups simultaneously; (2) the relationship between TNF-α-mediated inflammation and patient-reported outcomes (hand function, stress, sleep, pain, life satisfaction) has not been examined in metabolic CTS; (3) health-related factor profiles (beyond pain) have not been systematically compared across these subgroups; and (4) no study has used flexor retinaculum tissue, the primary pathological site, to assess epigenetic changes in CTS patients with metabolic syndrome or smoking history.

Clinical significance

For clinicians, identifying associations between specific comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking) and distinct molecular/health-related factor profiles may enable more targeted treatment approaches. For researchers, these preliminary data provide a foundation for larger mechanistic studies of epigenetic modifications in CTS. For patients, understanding how their comorbid conditions affect CTS pathology may improve adherence to comprehensive management strategies that address both local symptoms and systemic health factors.

Objectives

The purpose of this study was to investigate the health-related factors associated with CTS in patients with metabolic syndrome and smokers and to evaluate the epigenetic changes in CTS-related (COL-I and COL-II) genes and inflammatory (TNF-α) genes in all groups.

Hypothesis

We hypothesize that metabolic syndrome and smoking-induced epigenetic modification are underlying determinants of CTS and that there is variation in health-related factors and gene expression among metabolic syndrome patients and smokers with carpal tunnel syndrome.

Materials and methods

Design of study and instruments

Study Design: This was a cross-sectional, preliminary, observational laboratory study. Study Duration: October 2022 to September 2023. Study Setting: Al-Razi Hospital (Department of Orthopaedics, Kuwait) for participant recruitment and sample collection; Kuwait University Faculty of Allied Health Sciences laboratories for molecular analyses.

This study included standardised outcome measures that includes the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), Disability of Arm, Shoulder, and Hand Questionnaire (DASH). These assessments served as verified outcome measures to show the effect therapies effectiveness. Although the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) measures stress during the previous month, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) evaluates sleep quality. The Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) was used to quantify pain during the previous month. The Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand Questionnaire (DASH) is employed to assess hand function [15–18]. Another tool Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) measures satisfaction of life.

Participant flow and sample size

A total of 78 individuals were initially screened for eligibility. Of these, 16 were excluded: 8 did not meet inclusion criteria (4 had prior CTS surgery, 2 had cervical radiculopathy, 2 had inflammatory arthritis), 5 declined to participate, and 3 had incomplete data. The final analytic sample comprised 62 participants: 52 who underwent carpal tunnel release surgery (providing both tissue samples and patient-reported outcomes) and 10 healthy controls, providing only patient-reported outcomes; no tissue samples. Trigger finger (TF) patients (n = 10) served as a disease control group for tissue gene expression analyses only; they also completed questionnaires.

This study enrolled patients with individual metabolic comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia) rather than patients meeting full metabolic syndrome diagnostic criteria (e.g., IDF or NCEP ATP III criteria requiring three or more of: waist circumference, triglycerides, HDL, blood pressure, fasting glucose). All diagnoses were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) as follows: carpal tunnel syndrome (G56.0), trigger finger (M65.3, stenosing tenosynovitis), type 2 diabetes mellitus (E11.9) for the diabetic CTS group, essential hypertension (I10) for the hypertensive CTS group, dyslipidemia (E78.5) for the dyslipidemic CTS group, and nicotine dependence (F17.2) for the smoker CTS group.

Procedure

Participant diagnosis

All participants were initially evaluated by board-certified orthopaedic surgeons (minimum 5 years post-specialty experience) at Al-Razi Hospital Orthopaedics Department. Carpal tunnel syndrome was confirmed by nerve conduction studies performed by a neurologist with 5 years of electrophysiology experience. Trigger finger was diagnosed by clinical examination by the same orthopaedic surgeons. Metabolic comorbidities were diagnosed by the participants’ primary care physicians based on laboratory and clinical criteria. Smoking status was confirmed by self-report. Participants were given the choice to finish the assessments prior to surgical procedure. Surgeons in the orthopedic department Al-Razi Hospital obtained samples from patients who had flexor retinaculum release because of CTS. The samples of patients were promptly sent to the lab for analysis after being classified and divided into groups.

Instruments

Assessment of DASH

A questionnaire called the Disability of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) evaluation is used to assess how upper extremity conditions affect a person’s capacity to perform daily tasks. The thirty items on the assessment cover a broad range of topics linked to upper-extremity function, including daily activities, sports and recreation, and work-related duties. The DASH assessment also contains a part on emotions like tingling and pain. A zero scale (no disability) to a hundred scale (most severe disability) is used to score the self-administered questionnaire. The DASH questionnaire has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.92 to 0.96) and test–retest reliability (r = 0.94) in musculoskeletal populations [16]. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for DASH in CTS patients is reported as 10–15 points [16, 19].

Numeric rating scale

The Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) is a popular and simple tool to measure the intensity of pain. The NRS is a zero to ten scale, zero indicating no pain at all and ten indicating the worst possible pain. On the scale, patients were given the choice to select the number that best represents their pain level. The NRS has shown high test–retest reliability (r = 0.86–0.95) and strong convergent validity with visual analog scales. The MCID for pain in CTS is approximately 2 points. [15, 19].

PSQI assessment

A self-administered questionnaire known as Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) evaluates the sleep habits of person and quality in a month. The questionnaire’s 19 items assess a number of sleep aspects that include latency, duration, disruption, and use of sleep aids. Higher scores imply worse sleep quality. The PSQI creates seven component ratings, summing them together leads to a global score between 0 and 21. The PSQI has demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.83) and a cutoff score of > 5 distinguishes poor from good sleep with 89% sensitivity and 86% specificity [17, 19].

PSS assessment

A self-administered questionnaire known as Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) was employed to evaluate in what way a stressed-out person feels regarding his life over the course of preceding month. The questionnaire comprised of ten items, assessing in what way unpredictable, uncontrollable, and stressful a person considers their life. A higher perceived level of stress is indicated by higher PSS scores. The total score varies from 0 to 40. The PSS-10 has acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.78–0.91) and good predictive validity for health outcomes [17, 19].

SWLS assessment

A brief test called the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) gauges respondents’ overall cognitive appraisal of their level of life satisfaction. It has five items based on 7-point Likert scale (1 being “strongly disagree,” and 7 being “strongly agree”) to indicate how the participants felt about their life. Higher scores indicate more life satisfaction on the SWLS, which generates a total score between 5 and 35. The SWLS has shown high internal consistency (α = 0.87) and test–retest reliability (r = 0.82) over 2 months [18, 19].

Real-time PCR

Regarding to tissue handling and RNA quality control, flexor retinaculum tissue samples were collected intraoperatively and immediately placed in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on ice. Samples were transferred to the laboratory within 2 h and stored at − 80 °C until processing. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Only samples with A260/280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.1 and A260/230 ratios > 1.8 were included. RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis showing intact 28S and 18S ribosomal bands. cDNA synthesis was performed using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All samples were processed in duplicate, and batch effects were minimized by processing all samples within a 2-week period using the same reagent lots.

Statistical analysis

Regarding to Sample size justification, as this was a preliminary, hypothesis-generating study with no prior published data on gene expression differences across these specific CTS subgroups, formal sample size calculation was not performed. The sample size (n = 62 total, n = 5–10 per group). was determined based on feasibility considerations and similar preliminary epigenetic studies in CTS [13, 19]. For assessment, one-way ANOVA with the Bonferroni post hoc correction was used using Prism 5.0 (Graph Pad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Effect sizes for between-group comparisons were calculated using Cohen’s d, interpreted as small (0.2), medium (0.5), or large (0.8). Published minimal clinically important differences (MCID) were referenced where available for clinical outcome measures (DASH and NRS), but we acknowledge that MCID values have not been established for PSQI, PSS-10, or SWLS specifically in CTS populations. The Pearson correlation was applied. If P values are less than 0.05, statistically significant differences were considered. Using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), possible confounders (sleep quality, stress, hand function, pain, and life satisfaction) were selected for adjustment according to current knowledge regarding their correlations with gene expression, hand function, and satisfaction of life. In regard to the DAG results, research on the relationship between gene expression and hand function and life satisfaction must take stress, pain, and sleep quality into consideration [19, 21].

Results

Sixty-two participants (33 males and 29 females) were enrolled in this study. Their ages ranged from 47 to 66 years, with a mean age of 54 years (SD = 6.924). Table 3 shows the demographic data of the participants. Ten healthy control participants were included in this study. A total of 7 (70%) of the healthy control respondents were male, while 3 (30%) were female. Among the 52 patients who underwent operations, 10 (16.1% of the respondents) had trigger finger, while 10 (16.1%) had carpal tunnel syndrome. A total of 32 of the participants were carpal tunnel patients with metabolic syndrome diseases; 9 (14.5%) were carpal tunnel patients with diabetes, 7 (11.3%) were carpal tunnel patients with hypertension, 7 (11.3%) were carpal tunnel patients with dyslipidemia, and 9 (14.5%) were carpal tunnel patients and smokers. A total of 42 carpal tunnel participants received corticosteroids; 17 (40.5%) were injected once, 14 (33.3%) were injected twice, and 11 (26.2%) were injected three times.

No significant differences were observed between the five experimental groups with respect to the primary research variables. However, the BMI/kg of the five experimental groups and the healthy control group differed significantly (p < 0.0001) (Table 4).

Comparisons between the five carpal tunnel groups (n = 42) and the control group (n = 10) are displayed in Table 5. The carpal tunnel groups had higher mean expression levels of the COL-I, COL-II, and TNF-α genes. Discussion

Carpal tunnel patients pain suffering from metabolic comorbidities and smokers with carpal tunnel syndrome

In this study, patients’ pain levels were measured using a numerical pain scale before the flexor retinaculum was surgically released. Prior studies validated the overall pain level findings of respondents. According to a 2021 study, persons with diabetes are significantly more likely to experience severe symptoms like discomfort [22]. According to the findings section, diabetic individuals with carpal tunnel syndrome got the highest score of all the categories, with a score of 10, which is regarded as indicating considerable pain. In fact, any of the researched groups may have carpal tunnel syndrome-associated pain. Recent research suggests that cross-linking between collagen molecules brought on by hyperglycemia may induce collagen to build up in the flexor retinaculum around the median nerve [23].

This discovery could explain why carpal tunnel syndrome-related discomfort is exacerbated in diabetic people. Diabetes and carpal tunnel syndrome have been linked in a number of studies. The first researcher that associated diabetes with carpal tunnel syndrome was W. E. A. Stocker in 1973 [24]. According to Deger and his associates, the pathophysiology of diabetic carpal tunnel syndrome is significantly influenced by aberrant collagen metabolism, which results in compression of the median nerve, which is a symptom of the condition, and the later stage of fibrous tissue [25]. Though it is believed that nicotine is linked with a high incidence of carpal tunnel syndrome, when there is pain severity, both smokers and non-smokers with carpal tunnel syndrome have same pain level [26, 27].

According to our knowledge, no molecular experiments have studied the association between smoking and carpal tunnel syndrome. The numerical pain scale, which is sensitive, accurate, and dependable, was used in this study to measure pain in smokers with carpal tunnel syndrome. Participants with carpal tunnel syndrome who smoked scored 6.8, indicating significant pain, as mentioned in the findings section. The first finding of this study is that both carpal tunnel syndrome patients and smokers with carpal tunnel syndrome had about the same pain severity score, despite the fact that numerous studies have demonstrated that nicotine causes irreparable damage to the hands [28].

Dyslipidemia, particularly high levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, is an identified risk factor for carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS). This association derives from the metabolic and inflammatory impacts of abnormal lipid profiles on peripheral neurons [25]. Kin and his co workers discovered that high LDL levels are risk factor for idiopathic CTS in the middle age, recommending that LDL-correlated median nerve expansion enhances the carpal volume [29].

Pain may be a result of this hypertrophy. These findings imply a link between dyslipidemia and carpal tunnel syndrome, however the particular processes behind this interaction are not well known. Xanthoma, a recognized disorder that arises when elevated cholesterol levels induce deposits to accumulate in the flexor retinaculum, is likely the origin of the intense discomfort experienced by certain people with dyslipidemia and carpal tunnel syndrome. Our data, which showed that patients with dyslipidemia and trigger finger had a pain score of 8, which is regarded as significant pain, corroborate this conclusion.

We note in the findings section that the combination of carpal tunnel syndrome and hypertension raises pain levels. On the numerical rating scale, patients with carpal tunnel syndrome and hypertension received a score of 7.7, which indicates significant pain. According to earlier studies, hypertension can lead to ligament inflammation and collagen buildup, which can cause pain. Additionally, atherosclerosis and plaque accumulation in the arteries brought on by hypertension may reduce the blood flow to the ligaments [30].

Dysfunction of hand in carpal tunnel syndrome patients with metabolic comorbidities and smokers with carpal tunnel syndrome

Several studies have indicated that smoking impairs the healing process in general and exacerbates a variety of medical problems [31, 32]. Surprisingly, compared with the other groups, the smoking group had a nonsignificant difference in DASH score, excluding the CTS group. Similarly, Samona et al. [33] reported that regardless of the bad results of smoking, their investigation did not strengthened the proposed hypothesis that smoking has a significant influence on the postoperative motion range in flexor retinaculum injuries.

The DASH assessment found substantial differences between carpal tunnel syndrome patients and carpal tunnel syndrome patients with diabetes. Diabetic individuals may have difficulties doing functional tasks due of impaired sensory function in their upper extremities, notably their hands [34]. Our findings are supported by a research by Casanova et al. [35] that included three tests that showed reduced hand function in diabetics.

The dyslipidemia group had the lowest DASH score of all the groups, with the exception of the CTS group, however there is insufficient information to draw a clear link between CTS and dyslipidemia. Relative HGS was found to be adversely correlated with the risk of dyslipidemia in an adult investigation examining the relationship between hand grip strength and dyslipidemia [36].

There was a relationship between hypertension and poor hand function in CTS patients. Ji et al. [37] study corroborated our results that study found a strong hand grip could be linked to a high hypertension risk.

Disturbance of sleep in carpal tunnel syndrome patients with metabolic comorbidities and smokers with carpal tunnel syndrome

The symptoms of carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) include interrupted sleep, excessive daytime sleepiness, and several nighttime awakenings due to numbness and pain. This happens because the median nerve in the carpal tunnel is compressed as you sleep with your wrist flexed, which exacerbates discomfort [38]. A Swedish study in Lund University explained that elevated blood sugar levels raises the likelihood of getting a medical problem. According to the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), our results showed a major variation in sleep patterns between individuals with CTS and those with CTS + diabetes.

As stated in [38, 39]. Night smoking is considerably connected with higher insomnia and duration of short sleep. Surprisingly, there was no discernible variation between CTS group and the CTS + smoking group when the PSQI was employed. Relative with the other groups, the CTS group obtained the lowest score. The sleep habits of a person with CTS and a smoker with CTS were practically comparable.

The short sleep period and insomnia were substantially connected with dyslipidemia incidence, whereas the connection to poor quality of sleep and obstructive sleep apnea risk was statistically inconsequential [40]. This suggests that it might impact sleep patterns, though not to the same extent as other illnesses. The claim that there is no discernible difference between patients with CTS and those with dyslipidemia is supported by the PSQI data.

In developed countries, poor sleep quality is on the rise and may be linked to a high risk of adult hypertension. Comparing to people who slept regularly, a higher PSQI score was substantially linked to a higher chance of prevalent hypertension [41]. Chen et al. [42] study that poor sleep quality individuals were 17% more possibility to have prevalent hypertension. These results support our findings because individuals with CTS + hypertension had the second-highest PSQI score, followed by those with CTS + diabetes.

Stress in patients with metabolic comorbidities, CTS patient, and smokers with CTS

The CTS + smoking group had the highest stress level in this study, followed by the CTS + hypertension group. Few investigations have studied the link between CTS and smoking. Moreover, majority of smokers think that their stress levels will be lower by quitting smoking. Smokers face high levels of stress than nonsmokers [43, 44].

Our bodies acted with stressful situations with release of stress hormones (cortisol and adrenaline) into the bloodstream and emotional distress in the CTS + hypertension group, rising heart rate and make blood vessels tighter, moving more blood to the body core instead of extremities, because the body plans to launch the “fight or flight” reaction. Blood pressure elevates quickly because of constriction of blood vessel and heart rate increase, though thus impact persists till passing of stress response [45].

Life satisfaction in carpal tunnel syndrome patients with metabolic comorbidities and smokers with carpal tunnel syndrome

There was a major decrease in life satisfaction in the CTS + hypertension group in our study. A study revealed that patients with Stage 2 hypertension are less content with life than those with less blood pressure levels are [46]. Also, a cross-sectional study revealed a statistically significant negative correlation between life satisfaction and blood pressure [47].

Healthy lifestyle choices and biological processes are closely linked to life satisfaction. The results of our analysis demonstrated a major drop in life satisfaction in the diabetic CTS group. This understand because life happiness is gained from the alignment of one’s set of life standards with life circumstances throughout time [48]. Petterson et al. [49] performed a study with thousands diabetic patients and reported that long duration of diabetes patients had lower energy levels and well-being and more severe depression. The DM patients were interviewed, among which 64% felt unhappiness with their lives [50].

COL-I, COL-II, and TNF-α gene in patients with metabolic comorbidities and CTS and smokers with CTS

This study offers the initial tentative findings on epigenetic variations in gene expression in carpal tunnel syndrome, metabolic syndrome, smoker carpal tunnel syndrome. Chronic inflammation could cause pathogenic effects because of overactivation of signalling TNF-α [51]. Our results show that TNF-α expression is significantly higher in all CTS groups. Sookoian et al. [52] demonstrated that TNF-α is implicated in the development of metabolic syndrome, explaining the significant increase in TNF-α levels in the CTS + metabolic syndrome group. Insulin resistance and Diabetes are linked with high levels of TNF-α in the blood [51]. Type 2 diabetes and obesity are linked to elevated TNF-alpha levels [53]. TNF-α mRNA expression is anticipated to be higher in smokers with CTS. According to Petrescu et al. [54] smokers’ serum has higher amounts of TNF-α, which suggests that exposure to tobacco smoke causes an imbalance between proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors.

Collagens like COL-II and COL-II, together with other protein structures, make up the extracellular matrix. Changes in the ECM produce fast alterations in its mechanical properties [55]. Our findings demonstrated a substantial rise in the mRNA expression of both COL-I and COL-II in the CTS groups. The development of human lung cancer may be predicted using COL1A1 genes as possible diagnostic biomarkers [56]. In diabetes with prolonged hyperglycemia, collagen and other extracellular matrix components are synthesized more often and are deposited irregularly [57]. Like our results, according to Cain et al. [13] study in 2020 found that prolonged hyperglycemia in diabetic carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) produces epigenetic alterations in tendons, leading in the increased mRNA expression of both Collagen Type I and Collagen Type II [8]. These results are in line with earlier research showing that the development and progression of diabetic tendinopathies like CTS are significantly influenced by epigenetic changes brought on by persistently elevated blood sugar levels [13].

Strengths of the current study

This study has several strengths: (1) it is the first investigation to simultaneously examine health-related factors and gene expression across five CTS subgroups (diabetic, hypertensive, dyslipidemic, smoking) using tissue from the primary pathological site (flexor retinaculum); (2) comprehensive assessment of patient-reported outcomes including hand function, stress, sleep, pain, and life satisfaction; (3) use of directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) for rigorous confounder adjustment; and (4) clear separation of healthy controls (for patient-reported outcomes) from trigger finger disease controls (for tissue gene expression analyses).

Limitations and future directions

Real-time limitations encountered: Difficulty recruiting healthy control flexor retinaculum tissue, necessitating use of trigger finger tissue as a control comparator. This may introduce bias as trigger finger itself involves collagen pathology. (2) Smaller than desired sample sizes for some subgroups (e.g., hypertensive CTS, n = 7) due to surgery scheduling constraints and patient refusal. (3) Inability to obtain tissue from non-CTS smokers without hand pathology. (4) Logistical delays between tissue collection and RNA extraction (maximum 4 h, which may affect RNA integrity). (5) Questionnaire completion challenges for participants with low literacy. (6) This study did not report minimal clinically important differences (MCID) for PSQI, PSS-10, or SWLS as validated CTS-specific thresholds do not currently exist in the literature. Future studies should establish disease-specific MCID values for these patient-reported outcomes in CTS populations. Future studies with large sample sizes are needed to obtain conclusive findings regarding the variance in health-related factors and epigenetic changes in CTS-related genes.

Conclusions and implications

This preliminary investigation demonstrates that hand dysfunction, stress levels, pain severity, and reduced life satisfaction in CTS patients are strongly associated with increased inflammation (TNF-α) and collagen gene expression (COL-I, COL-II) in patients with metabolic syndrome and smokers with CTS. These findings suggest that integrating health-related factor assessment with molecular profiling may inform a more personalized approach to CTS management. Further rigorous research with larger samples is needed to validate these preliminary findings.
