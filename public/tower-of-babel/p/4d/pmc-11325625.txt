Distribution and influencing factors of pulp stones based on CBCT: a retrospective observational study from southwest China
Authors: Wantong Zhang, Yao Wang, Lin Ye, Yan Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulp stones are a type of pulp calcification, the presence of which tends to hinder endodontic treatment. Thus, this retrospective study aimed to analyze the distribution of pulp stones in the population in southwest China and identify the influencing factors.MaterialsCone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans of 5066 teeth of 200 patients (91 males and 109 females) aged 16–45 years were evaluated. Pulp stones were marked as either present or absent when distinct radiopaque masses were found in the pulp cavity, then evaluated the occurrence of pulp stones with regard to tooth type, sex, age group, and contact it with tooth status. The Pearson chi-square test and nonparametric test were used for statistical analysis.ResultsPulp stones were detected in 49.0% of patients and 7.4% of teeth, respectively. The incidence in females was 1.9 times higher than in males (OR = 1.9, 95% CI = 1.1–3.3, p = 0.001). Pulp stones were most prevalent in patients 36–45 years of age. Furthermore, in the age range of 16–45 years, the likelihood of finding pulp stones increased 1.1 times per year with age (OR = 1.1, 95% CI = 1.0–1.1, p = 0.032). A higher incidence of pulp stones was observed in the maxilla and molars. Of the 5066 teeth studied, pulp stones were more common in non-intact teeth.ConclusionNearly half of the population in southwest China had pulp stones. Pulp stones were found significantly more often in females, maxilla, and non-intact teeth, and their frequency increased with age. For dentists, understanding the distribution of pulp stones is crucial for the proper design of root canal treatment (RCT).Trial registrationThis study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Stomatology, Southwest Medical University (certificate number: 20220818001).Keywords: Clinical study, Cone-beam computed tomography, Pulp stones, Prevalence, Root canal treatment

Background

The pulp is the only connective tissue that forms a tooth and is composed of two parts: pulp cells and the extracellular matrix. Dental pulp cells and odontoblasts are the primary cellular components that play crucial roles in pulp calcification. Dental pulp cells mainly contribute to the formation of non-tubular dentin, whereas odontoblasts mainly form tubular dentin [1]. After dentin synthesis is complete, odontoblasts still maintain their capacity of secreting dentine. When the pulp is stimulated or injured, tertiary dentin is formed, including reactionary dentin and reparative dentin [2, 3]. In addition, the pulp tissue can develop unusual calcifications, known as pulp calcification. It occurs as a physiological or pathological process and appears as diffuse calcification or pulp stones within the pulp cavity and its surrounding layer.

Pulp stones are calcifications that may occur in the coronal or root portions of the pulp. Moreover, pulp stones collected from the same patient’s teeth can exhibit different morphologies, structures, and chemical properties, indicating a high degree of heterogeneity [4, 5]. An individual may have more than a dozen teeth with pulp stones simultaneously, Sener et al. [6] and Deniz et al. [7] detected the presence of pulp stones in 12 or more teeth of 0.4% and 1.6% of the patients.

According to previous studies, the prevalence of pulp stones varies widely among populations [8]. These differences may be related to differences in observation methods, inclusion criteria, and research designs. Though its exact etiology is unknown, factors related to pulp stones have been proposed, which can be broadly classified into local oral factors, such as trauma [9], long-term irritants [10], and periodontal disease [11]; and systemic factors, such as cardiovascular diseases [12, 13], diabetes, and systemic stones [14, 15]. Blood bacteria, namely, calcifying nanoparticles, have also been linked to pulp stones [16]. Furthermore, pulp stones vary according to age [17], sex [8], and genetic susceptibility [6].

Many epidemiological studies have used radiological methods to detect pulp stones. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), as an imaging method with many advantages, such as high resolution, low radiation dose, minimal distortion, and good hard tissue imaging, can overcome the limitations of traditional two-dimensional imaging [18]. Furthermore, it can visually display the dental pulp cavity from three angles: coronal, sagittal, and axial, providing accurate images for identifying pulp stones.

Pulp stones can form in healthy and diseased teeth and occasionally cause trigeminal neuralgia. Generally, it does not affect the pulp vitality [19]; therefore, no special treatment is required. However, during root canal treatment (RCT), pulp stones may block the file in the unobstructed root canal, cause deformation of file tips, and hamper disinfection and irrigation procedures, thereby complicating endodontic treatment and even leading to RCT failure [20]. This study used CBCT to observe the prevalence of pulp stones in individuals aged 16–45, and explored their correlation with age, sex, tooth location, and tooth status to provide clinicians with valuable data to mitigate potential complications associated with pulp stones during RCT.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective observational study examined the CBCT scans of all patients who visited The Affiliated Stomatological Hospital, Southwest Medical University, between January and December 2023. CBCT scans were obtained for oral examination, diagnosis, and treatment planning. The imaging data were screened according to the inclusion criteria for the selection of research objects (Table 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A total of 200 patients and 5066 teeth were included in this study. These patients were in the age range of 16–45 years (29.0 ± 7.6), among which 109 (54.5%, 29.4 ± 7.8) were female and 91 (45.5%, 28.6 ± 7. 5) were male.

Radiographic evaluation

All patients were photographed using a KO-DAK 9500 CBCT machine (Carestream Health, Inc., USA) at 90 kV and 10 mA with an exposure time of 10.8 s, a voxel size of 200 μm, and a field view of 150 mm × 90 mm, following the manufacturer’s recommendation and As Low As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA) protocols to minimize unnecessary radiation exposure to patients. Three-dimensional images were then post-processed and generated using CS 3D imaging software (Carestream Health, Inc., USA), which was observed using a screen with 1920 × 1080 pixels in a darkroom. To avoid omissions, each plane of CBCT was viewed layer by layer, with the coronal plane from distal to mesial, the sagittal plane from buccal to lingual, and the axial plane from coronal to apical. The diagnosis of a pulp stone was confirmed when a characteristic radiopaque mass was discovered in the pulpal space (including both the crown and root) (Fig. 1) [21]. Simultaneously, when there was a transmission shadow or a high-density filling shadow that exceeded the enamel layer in the image, the tooth was either restored or affected by caries that damaged the dentin, in this case, the tooth status was recorded as non-intact (Fig. 2).

Examiner calibration and reliability

The study was conducted by a trained investigator and an endodontist (with more than 10 years of experience in endodontics), and prior to the formal study, a pilot study was conducted to check inter-examiner reliability by evaluating 50 randomly selected images, with a Kappa value of 0.88 (p < 0.001), indicating that the observation method was reliable. The investigators examined all the images. A random sample of 25% of all images (n = 50) previously examined was reread after a month. Cohen’s kappa analysis showed that the coefficient of agreement between the two evaluations was 0.92 (p < 0.001), indicating convincing results. Differences between the formal study and the sample study one month later were discussed until a consensus was reached.

Statistical data analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Pearson chi-square test was employed to compare categorical variables such as age and sex. One-sample chi-squared test was employed to assess symmetry. Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were employed in independent group comparisons of the numerical values. Logistic regression analysis was employed to identify the risk factors associated with pulp stones, the results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Prevalence and sex distribution of pulp stones

Pulp stones were detected in 49.0% of patients. Specifically, in the sex grouping (Table 2), the incidence of pulp stones was significantly higher in females (56.0%) than in males (40.7%) (p = 0.024). Sex was identified as a risk factor for pulp stones in all patients examined and the prevalence of pulp stones in females was 1.9 times higher than in males (OR = 1.9, 95% CI = 1.1–3.3, p = 0.001).

Distribution of pulp stones by age group

Age group distribution of pulp stones is presented in Table 2. Patients aged 36–45 years exhibited a higher prevalence (63.0%) than those aged 26–35 years (52.5%) or those aged 16–25 years (36.5%). The youngest patient was a 17-year-old female with pulp stones on her molar, appearing as a calcified radiolucency detected in a wide pulp cavity (Fig. 3). Prevalence of pulp stones was significantly different between age groups (p = 0.013). When all patients were included in the logistic regression analysis, the likelihood of finding pulp stones increased by 1.1-fold for each additional year in the age range of 16–45 (OR = 1.1, 95% CI = 1.0–1.1, p = 0.032).

Distribution of pulp stones and different tooth status

Among the 5066 teeth observed, 210 were non-intact, and pulp stones were found in 47 teeth (Table 3). Non-intact teeth presented a significantly higher presence (22.4%) of pulp stones than intact teeth (6.7%) (p < 0.001).

Distribution of pulp stones in different tooth types

Among the examined teeth, molars had the highest detection rate (21.8%). Compared to the anterior teeth (2.8%) and premolars (1.2%), the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Furthermore, significant difference was observed between the first (27.9%) and second molars (16.7%) (p < 0.001). Among the first molars (Table 5), the maxillary left first molar had the highest incidence (35.4%), followed by the maxillary right first molar (33.3%), mandibular right first molar (20.7%), and mandibular left first molar (18.5%). Furthermore, pulp stones predominantly occurred in the canines (54.8%) of the anterior teeth (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Research suggests that the developmental pattern of pulp stones resembles that of atrophic calcification. Certain components of the dental pulp, such as collagen fibers, damaged blood vessels, and necrotic cell remnants [22], undergo initial calcification. This may be age-related or due to local pathological changes in the pulp tissue. Subsequently, on the surface of the irregularly calcified core, the calcified material is gradually deposited in concentric or radial circles [23]. During this process, collagen and fiber bundles in the pulp act as structural scaffolds. The most striking feature of the formed pulp stones is their lamellar structure, which is supported by the observation of concentrically layered calcifications in some spherical and oval pulp stones in histological studies [24]. However, histological studies have revealed another type of pulp stone with no specific shape, diffuse areas, reefs, and a rough surface, possibly due to the fusion of the two structures to form a single stone [23].

Previous conclusions drawn from radiographic assessments of pulp stones have varied widely. A comparison of the recent studies using various methods is presented in Table 7. Studies [18] have found that CBCT has a superior validity and reliability for the diagnosis of endodontic diseases. One study [21] compared CBCT with panoramic radiography for the detection of pulp stones and found that some pulp stones detected on panoramic photography were not detected on CBCT, because the spatial resolution and 2D nature of the 2D imaging system could lead to the misinterpretation of pulp stones, thus overestimating or underestimating the incidence of pulp stones. CBCT has higher specificity and accuracy, overcomes overlapping limitations, and is a superior method for detecting pulp stones compared to conventional radiography.

Pulp stone research literatures in recent years

According to previous studies, the distribution of pulp stones in different regions is different and affected by various factors to varying degrees, but there are no reports on large sample sizes in southwest China. This study performed a clinical analysis of CBCT images to assess the distribution of pulp stones and to correlate it with various variables. In our study, 49.0% of the patients and 7.4% of the teeth had pulp stones. Some previous CBCT-based studies have not reached the same conclusion as ours. da Silva et al. [21] noted the prevalence in 31.9% of patients and 9.5% of teeth in Brazil; Sezgin et al. [33] reported the prevalence in 24.2% of individuals and 33% of teeth in Turkey; Mirah et al. [35] observed the prevalence in 24.2% of individuals and 33% of teeth in Saudis. Compared to our study, they all concluded a lower population incidence and a higher tooth incidence. Different results in the population may be due to race and region, and different inclusion criteria led to the inclusion of more molars in their studies, leading to a stronger conclusion regarding teeth. A higher prevalence was observed by Kaabi et al. [34], who reported that 98.3% of patients and 52.1% of teeth had pulp stones, their study included more teeth with caries and fillings. Hsieh et al. [27] studied individuals in northern Taiwan, China, and reported a prevalence rate of 83.3%, however, their research focused on a broader age range of 20–60 years old. Additionally, other studies reported findings similar to ours, Tassoker et al. [30] reported that pulp stones were noted in 52.0% of the patients and 7.7% of the teeth, and Patil et al. [29] reported a population prevalence of 50.93%. Some researchers have conducted histological examinations of pulp stones using isolated tooth sections and found that the true prevalence rate was significantly higher with histological testing [36]. However, this method can only be used in vitro and has certain limitations. Several studies have found that Artificial Intelligence (AI) significantly enhances the sensitivity and specificity of dental pathology diagnosis compared with human observers [37, 38]. In the future, more accurate data should be obtained from AI observations.

Our analysis indicated that the prevalence of pulp stones in females was 1.9 times higher than in males. Several studies [5, 6, 32] reported similar differences, that may be attributed to hormonal changes in women. Some researchers [7] have suggested that bruxism is more common in women as a long-term chronic irritation, that may cause pulp stones. Contrary to our findings, a few studies [29, 34] have found a higher prevalence of pulp stones in males or no significant association between sex and pulp stones [27, 30, 35].

When considering age, our analysis found that patients aged 36–45 years were more likely to have pulp stones than those in the other two groups. At the same time, in our study population, we found that the risk of developing pulp stones doubles with every additional year of age. This indicates that in the age range of 16–45, increasing age is a significant factor in the development of pulp stones. Some scholars [16, 39] have reached the same conclusion, that development of pulp stone is a chronic degenerative change related to age, which may be due to age-related changes in the dental pulp, such as shrinkage of the pulp chamber, a decrease in vascular and cellular elements, and an increase in fibrous elements, which may increase the likelihood of calcification. However, Kaabi et al. [33] reported that the incidence of pulp stones did not correlate with age. Compared to previous studies, our study included a younger population. In our study, 36.5% of the 16–25 years age group had pulp stones, and the youngest was a 17-year-old female, reminding us that, although older patients have a higher probability of developing pulp stones, their prevalence in younger age groups should not be underestimated. Pulp stones have also been found in developing tooth germs and primary teeth, suggesting that pulpal pathology is not the sole cause of pulp stone formation [5].

The present study also confirmed that the maxilla had a higher number of teeth with pulp stones than the mandible, with a similar distribution on the left and right sides. Pulp stones are more common in the molars, which can be attributed to factors that can lead to increased calcification precipitation, such as their larger size, high occlusal load, and complex blood supply. Most previous studies support our conclusions [21, 22, 24]. Among the observed molars, the prevalence in the first molar was higher than that in the second molar, possibly because the first molar erupts earlier and remains in the mouth for a longer period. However, a different conclusion was observed for premolars and anterior teeth compared with previous studies [21, 32]. A higher prevalence of pulp stones was observed in anterior teeth (2.8%) than in premolars (1.2%). Pulp stones were predominantly found in the middle and upper thirds of the root canals of canines. This may be because the canines have relatively wide and long root canals.

Previous studies [18, 34, 35] have already revealed that the pulp stones are more frequent in carious and restored teeth. Sener et al. [6] favored the possibility that pulp stones are a response to long-standing irritants. Similar conclusions were reached in this study. Both caries and restorations can lead to chronic inflammation of the dental pulp, which stimulates the pulp for an extended period, resulting in the formation of pulp stones [21]. In addition, tooth cutting during the restoration process may cause vascular damage [10], and microleakage around the restoration may trigger an inflammatory response in the pulp-dentin complex [33], both of which can lead to the development of pulp stones. This finding was validated histologically. Berès et al. [40] found higher levels of Zn and Cu on the surface of pulp stones than on dentin, which may be related to the inflammatory environment of the dental pulp. In addition, Demant et al. [41] found ectopic cartilage-like connective tissue in the pulp cavity of late-stage carious teeth, which was difficult to distinguish from pulp stones on imaging. This may have affected the conclusions drawn in this study. Collectively, because caries and restoration are long-term processes, further longitudinal studies are required to explore their association with pulp stones.

In this study, more than half of the affected teeth were symmetrical, which is a noteworthy finding despite the lack of statistical significance. Thus, when pulp stones are present on one side, clinicians should consider the likelihood of their presence in the contralateral side.

In our study, five patients had more than 10 teeth with pulp stones. The highest incidence was found in a 35-year-old female patient with pulp stones in 17 teeth. In addition, 11 affected teeth were identified in the image of a 22-year-old young female. The presence of multiple stones may be related to the genetic characteristics of the family. Previously, a dominantly inherited trait was identified in a family with multiple pulp stones [42]. A later study [43] suggested that generalized pulp stones in clinically normal teeth may be a variation of dentin dysplasia or a new, nonsyndromic dentin defect. Some genes such as FAM20A [44] and SMAD6 [45] may also be involved. None of the patients in this study had other physical symptoms or known familial genetic disorders. In these cases, further genetic investigations may help explain this phenomenon.

The presence of pulp stones may lead some difficulties to the endodontist while performing the endodontic treatments, hence knowing about its distribution will help with examination, diagnosis, and timely removal. According to the results of our study, female and elderly individuals are more likely to have pulp stones. Therefore, special attention should be paid to carefully identify them during endodontic treatment for such patients. Hard tissue that is not the pulpal wall should be considered as a possible pulp stone and promptly removed. In clinical practice, imaging technologies, ultrasound, combined with microscopy, are frequently used to remove pulp stones [46]. Case reports [47] have indicated that pulp stones can be effectively removed from teeth with narrow root canals using EDTA or sodium hypochlorite as a dissolving agent.

Furthermore, the number, distribution, and volume of pulp stones vary from person to person, and matching the imaging data of pulp stones in forensic science may help in the forensic identification of the deceased. Therefore, different patterns of pulp stones have the potential to serve as valuable personal characteristics for forensic identification [41].

Consequently, given the limitations of our study, including its single-center retrospective design and limited age range, the results do not represent the entire population of China. All these findings underscore the need for further investigation that involve multicenter surveys or longitudinal studies, wherein larger sample sizes will be needed to obtain more comprehensive and accurate data. Hereby, clinicians can enhance our understanding of pulp stone etiology and inform more effective preventive and therapeutic strategies in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study offers a detailed analysis of pulp stones in individuals aged 16–45 in southwest China. Our findings revealed a notable prevalence of pulp stones, with 49.0% of the population and in the 7.4% teeth affected. Pulp stones were significantly more prevalent in females, in maxilla, and in non-intact teeth, and their frequency increased with age. However, no significant association was found between the distribution of pulp stones and dental side.
