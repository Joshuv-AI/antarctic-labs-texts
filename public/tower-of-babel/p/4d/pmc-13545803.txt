Microglial mitophagy as an immunometabolic checkpoint in alzheimer’s disease: linking mitochondrial quality control to neuroinflammation
Authors: Mingyue Zou, Tengyu Zhao, Weidong Wu, Jian Zhang, Pengyu Pan, Jingdong Yan, Quan Li, Yanyan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AD is a complex neurodegenerative disorder characterized by chronic neuroinflammation. Microglia, the brain’s resident immune cells, centrally regulate AD pathophysiology. Recent studies have highlighted microglial mitophagy as an important interface linking mitochondrial quality control to innate immune responses.Intact mitophagy facilitates the timely clearance of damaged mitochondria, thereby limiting the release of mitochondrial DAMPs (e.g., mtDNA and mtROS) and helping restrain aberrant activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and the NLRP3 inflammasome.In the AD pathological milieu, however, factors including Aβ deposition, tau pathology, and genetic risk variants such as TREM2 and APOE4 disrupt mitophagy at multiple levels—from initiation and recognition to lysosomal degradation. This review systematically summarizes the molecular regulatory network of microglial mitophagy, with a particular focus on the mechanisms by which AD-associated pathological factors impair this process. We further discuss potential mechanisms through which mitophagic dysfunction may contribute to the amplification of neuroinflammation, including the release of mitochondrial DAMPs, the reprogramming of TBK1 signaling, and intercellular interactions. Finally, we outline current therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring mitophagy and discuss their potential to modulate neuroinflammatory responses and AD-related pathological processes, while highlighting the challenges and future directions in this emerging field.

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a primary neurodegenerative disorder of the central nervous system characterized by cognitive decline and memory impairment [1],and it represents the most common form of dementia worldwide, accounting for approximately 60–80% of cases [2]. The core neuropathological hallmarks of AD include senile plaques formed by amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition, neurofibrillary tangles resulting from abnormal tau phosphorylation, and progressive neuronal loss [3]. Aβ and tau have long been regarded as the central pathogenic factors in AD. However, multiple clinical trials targeting the clearance of Aβ and tau pathology have failed to significantly delay disease progression. This indicates that therapeutic strategies focused solely on the removal of a single pathological protein may be insufficient to modify the core driving mechanisms of the disease [4, 5]. AD may therefore be better understood not merely as a proteinopathy, but rather as a complex systemic disorder driven by the reciprocal interplay between chronic neuroinflammation and metabolic dysfunction.

Microglia are the resident innate immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS) and play critical roles in maintaining immune homeostasis, surveilling the microenvironment, and regulating synaptic plasticity [6]. Convergent evidence from multiple dimensions has established the central role of microglial immune responses in the pathogenesis and progression of AD. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have revealed that multiple AD risk genes are significantly enriched in microglia-associated pathways, including TREM2, APOE, PLCG2, and BIN1 [7]. In vivo molecular imaging studies using TSPO-PET have directly demonstrated that microglial activation levels are significantly higher in the brains of patients with AD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) compared with cognitively normal controls, and that the degree of activation correlates positively with Aβ deposition burden [8]. In the cerebrospinal fluid, levels of soluble TREM2 (sTREM2), a specific marker of microglial activation, are persistently elevated in AD patients, and their longitudinal rate of change is closely associated with the progression of tau and Aβ pathology [9]. These convergent lines of evidence from genetics, in vivo imaging, and fluid biomarkers point to a unified conclusion: the functional state of microglia is intimately intertwined with the pathological progression of AD. Elucidating the regulatory mechanisms governing the transition of microglia from a homeostatic to a pathogenic phenotype has therefore become a critical question in AD research.

The functional transiion of microglia is tightly coupled to their metabolic state. In the AD pathological milieu, chronic exposure to Aβ and tau drives a metabolic shift toward glycolysis, leading to the accumulation of mitochondrial damage [10]. In this context, mitophagy—the selective autophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria, emerges as a key regulator influencing the threshold of microglial innate immune activation. When mitophagy is functionally intact, it restricts the leakage of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and reactive oxygen species (mtROS), thereby suppressing excessive activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and the NLRP3 inflammasome [11]. Conversely, impaired mitophagy is associated with the persistent release of mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns, which may promote a self-reinforcing inflammatory state [12, 13].

Of note, mitophagy also operates in other neural cell types. In neurons, mitophagy maintains axonal and synaptic integrity and directly influences tau homeostasis [14]; in astrocytes, it contributes to metabolic support, glutamate homeostasis, and the regulation of reactive astrogliosis under Aβ-associated stress [15]. However, within the multicellular AD microenvironment, microglial mitophagy occupies a distinct immunoregulatory position. As the principal innate immune effector cells of the central nervous system, microglia possess immune-specialized signaling machinery (e.g., NLRP3, cGAS-STING) that can potentially couple mitochondrial damage to inflammatory output. More importantly, microglia are among the earliest cell types to respond to Aβ deposition in the temporal sequence of AD pathology—in vivo imaging studies have shown that newly formed Aβ plaques recruit microglia within 24 h [16], and human postmortem studies have demonstrated that activated microglial clustering occurs prior to the formation of dystrophic neurites [17]. Furthermore, as discussed later in this review, dysfunctional microglial mitophagy can propagate damage to neighboring cells via paracrine and intercellular signals. Therefore, while astrocytic and neuronal mitophagy are not dispensable, this review focuses on microglial mitophagy.

Accordingly, we propose that microglial mitophagy serves as an “immunometabolic checkpoint”—a conceptual node that integrates mitochondrial quality control, cellular metabolic status, and innate immune signaling to help determine whether microglia adopt a homeostatic or pro-inflammatory phenotype. This checkpoint potentially operates at the intersection of the PINK1/Parkin pathway, TBK1-centered adaptor signaling, and the TREM2-mTOR-TFEB axis. Its dysfunction, as discussed in the following sections, may contribute to the pathological amplification of neuroinflammation in Alzheimer's disease.

Functional transformation of microglia in AD

Functional transition of microglia in AD

Microglia account for approximately 10% of all CNS cells in the healthy brain. Beyond their classical immune surveillance functions, microglia participate in synaptic pruning during neurodevelopment, maintain synaptic plasticity throughout adulthood, and contribute to the clearance of apoptotic neurons [18, 19]. Under physiological conditions, microglia exist in a highly dynamic “resting”state, continuously extending and retracting their processes to survey the surrounding microenvironment and detect both endogenous and exogenous perturbations [20]. Microglia exhibit bidirectional regulatory roles during the course of AD. Considerable evidence indicates that in the early stages of the disease, microglia migrate toward Aβ plaques, encapsulate and compact plaque structures, limit their spread into adjacent tissue, and promote the phagocytosis and degradation of Aβ [21]. This response is generally regarded as a protective function of microglia. However, as the disease progresses, chronic exposure to Aβ oligomers and hyperphosphorylated tau contributes to microglia to gradually lose their protective phenotype and transition into a chronically activated state characterized by the robust release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, thereby fueling neuroinflammation and neuronal injury [22]. Moreover, activated microglia can further accelerate tau pathology and synaptic loss by promoting aberrant tau phosphorylation and intercellular propagation, as well as by aberrantly engulfing tau-containing synaptic structures [23]. Thus, microglia not only respond passively to AD pathology but also actively participate in and even amplify the vicious cycle between Aβ and tau pathologies [24].

What mechanisms determine the irreversible transition of microglia from a protective to a pathogenic phenotype? The classical view holds that this transition depends on the cumulative intensity of extracellular pathological stimuli, specifically Aβ and tau, reflecting a dose-dependent effect.. However, emerging evidence suggests that this explanation is incomplete. Even under comparable Aβ burdens, microglia from individuals with distinct genetic backgrounds or at different disease stages display markedly divergent functional states [25, 26]. This strongly implies the existence of an intrinsic regulatory node that integrates extracellular pathological signals with intracellular metabolic conditions to ultimately govern microglial functional output.

Increasing evidence points toward the metabolic state of microglia as this critical regulatory node. In parallel with the phenotypic transition, microglia undergo profound metabolic reprogramming during the progression of AD.

Immunometabolic Reprogramming and Glycolytic Shift in Microglia

Microglial functional activation is tightly coupled to their intracellular metabolic state. Under physiological conditions, homeostatic microglia primarily rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to generate ATP for their energy demands [27, 28].Upon inflammatory stimulation or under pathological conditions, microglia rapidly shift their metabolic profile from OXPHOS toward glycolysis, a phenomenon known as the “Warburg effect” [29]. In the context of AD, this metabolic reprogramming is not merely a cell-autonomous adaptation but is actively influenced by by pathological factors such as Aβ and tau. Studies have shown that Aβ oligomers promote the transition of microglia toward a glycolysis-dependent pro-inflammatory phenotype through activation of the mTOR-HIF-1α signaling axis [30]. Furthermore, microglia carrying TREM2 loss-of-function mutations or the APOE4 risk allele exhibit more pronounced metabolic imbalance, suggesting that genetic risk factors further exacerbate this process [31].

Glycolysis enables rapid ATP production and provides intermediates for the biosynthesis of nucleotides, amino acids, and lipids, thereby supporting microglial proliferation, inflammatory mediator production, and phagocytic activity [32]. However, compared with OXPHOS, glycolysis is less efficient in terms of ATP yield, and prolonged reliance on this metabolic pathway suppresses electron transport chain activity, disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential, and leads to excessive production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) [33, 34]. In the chronic inflammatory milieu of AD, microglia are persistently exposed to Aβ and tau and remain in a state of glycolysis-dominated metabolic stress, resulting in the progressive accumulation of damaged mitochondria that cannot be effectively cleared through physiological repair mechanisms [35, 36].

The accumulation of damaged mitochondria itself serves as a potent amplifier of neuroinflammation. When mitochondrial structural and functional integrity is compromised, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), normally sequestered within the mitochondrial matrix, and excessively produced mtROS leak into the cytosol. These mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (mitochondrial DAMPs) activate two critical innate immune signaling pathways. Cytosolic mtDNA is recognized by cGAS, which subsequently activates the STING-TBK1-IRF3 cascade, driving type I interferon responses [37, 38]. Concurrently, mtROS acts as a potent trigger for NLRP3 inflammasome activation, promoting caspase-1-mediated maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18 [39]. Thus, mitochondrial damage resulting from metabolic reprogramming may contribute to the amplification of inflammatory responses and may serve as an important source of inflammatory stimuli in microglia.

In this context, mitophagy, defined as the selective autophagic recognition and clearance of damaged mitochondria, emerges as a critical regulatory node that may shape the inflammatory threshold of microglia [40]. When mitophagy is functionally intact, damaged mitochondria are efficiently degraded, the leakage of mtDNA and mtROS is effectively curtailed, and the activation of the aforementioned inflammatory pathways is suppressed by mitophagy. However, under the pathological conditions of AD, mitophagy becomes impaired, and the persistent leakage of mitochondrial DAMPs may drive chronic activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and the NLRP3 inflammasome, potentially trapping microglia in a self-perpetuating state of inflammatory locking.Therefore, the functional status of mitophagy may influence whether metabolic reprogramming remains adaptive or shifts toward a state that favors persistent inflammatory amplification.

Disease-specific microglial states in AD

Single-cell transcriptomic studies have identified a distinct microglial activation state in neurodegenerative diseases, termed disease-associated microglia (DAM) [41]. Unlike the classical M1/M2 polarization paradigm, DAM represent a unique transcriptional reprogramming state driven by the AD pathological microenvironment [42].

The characteristic gene expression profile of DAM includes the downregulation of homeostatic genes such as P2RY12 and TMEM119, and the upregulation of genes including TREM2, APOE, CLEC7A, and LPL [43, 44]. These upregulated genes are not only involved in phagocytosis and lipid metabolism but are also significantly enriched in inflammatory signaling pathways, such as components of the NLRP3 inflammasome and downstream effectors of STING. This suggests that DAM represent a functional state deeply coupled to immunometabolic dysregulation [45, 46].

The formation of DAM is highly dependent on the integrity of TREM2 signaling [47, 48]. As a cell surface lipid sensor, TREM2 initiates downstream signaling cascades via the adaptor protein DAP12, thereby regulating microglial metabolic reprogramming and survival [18]. APOE acts as a key downstream effector of TREM2 signaling, mediating lipid metabolism and Aβ clearance [49]. Importantly, the integrity of the TREM2-APOE axis is essential for maintaining microglial mitophagy [50]. Importantly, the integrity of the TREM2-APOE axis is essential for maintaining microglial mitophagy. In carriers of TREM2 loss-of-function mutations or the APOE4 risk allele, DAM conversion is either impaired or biased toward a dysfunctional inflammatory subtype, which may be partly attributable to the persistent leakage of mitochondrial DAMPs and metabolic collapse resulting from defective mitophagy [51]. Thus, the DAM phenotype reflects not merely transcriptional alterations but also the functional status of mitochondrial quality control.

DAM exert complex bidirectional effects in AD. In the early stages of the disease, DAM limit pathological spread by encapsulating Aβ plaques, compacting plaque structures, and enhancing phagocytic function [52, 53]. As the disease progresses, however, DAM gradually lose their protective capacity and instead become amplifiers of neuroinflammation. Pro-inflammatory cytokines released by DAM, such as IL-1β and TNF-α, not only directly damage neurons but also further activate surrounding glial cells, thereby propagating inflammatory cascades [54]. More critically, the mitophagic capacity of DAM themselves is significantly impaired. Studies have shown that mitophagic flux is markedly reduced in DAM in AD, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria and the persistent leakage of mtDNA and mtROS, which in turn may sustain their own inflammatory activation via the cGAS-STING and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways [55]. These findings suggest that mitophagy deficiency may represent more than a secondary consequence of DAM dysfunction and could contribute to maintaining a persistently activated inflammatory state.

Moreover, the pathological consequences of defective microglial mitophagy extend beyond the cell-autonomous level. Recent studies have identified a subpopulation of microglia expressing PTPRG that, through PTPRG-CNTN4 ligand-receptor interactions, induces the expression of the m⁶A methyltransferase VIRMA in neurons. This in turn suppresses neuronal Parkin translation and blocks neuronal mitophagy, potentially leading to neuronal death [56]. This finding suggests that impaired mitochondrial quality control in microglia can be directly transmitted to neurons via intercellular communication, extending the pathological effects from immune cells to the neural circuit level.

In summary, DAM represent the cellular phenotypic manifestation of microglial immunometabolic dysregulation in AD, and their functional status is intimately coupled to the integrity of mitophagy. Mitophagy deficiency may contribute to the transition of DAM toward a pathogenic inflammatory phenotype and may facilitate the propagation of mitochondrial stress to neighboring neurons through non-cell-autonomous mechanisms. Molecular regulatory network of microglial mitophagy

In the AD pathological milieu, microglia undergo metabolic reprogramming and face the progressive accumulation of mitochondrial damage, positioning mitophagy as a central regulatory node that determines their functional trajectory. However, whether mitophagy in microglia—as the resident innate immune cells of the CNS—exhibits immune-specific regulatory features distinct from those in other cell types remains an open question. This chapter begins with the canonical mitophagy pathways and sequentially examines the unique regulatory logic of the PINK1/Parkin pathway, receptor-mediated mitophagy, the dual-function hub TBK1, the TREM2-mTOR-TFEB axis, and the coupling of mitochondrial dynamics in microglia, aiming to establish a molecular framework for microglial mitophagy as an “immunometabolic checkpoint”.

Canonical molecular mechanisms of mitophagy

Mitophagy proceeds via two principal pathways: the ubiquitin-dependent pathway and receptor-mediated (ubiquitin-independent) pathways [57].In the ubiquitin-dependent pathway, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential leads to the stabilization of PINK1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane, which subsequently recruits and activates the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin. Parkin catalyzes the ubiquitination of outer membrane proteins, and the resulting ubiquitin chains are recognized by autophagy adaptor proteins such as p62, OPTN, and NDP52, thereby linking damaged mitochondria to autophagosomes for eventual lysosomal degradation [58]. In receptor-mediated pathways, outer mitochondrial membrane proteins such as BNIP3, NIX, and FUNDC1 directly interact with LC3 via their LC3-interacting region motifs to mediate mitochondrial clearance [59, 60]. While these canonical pathways are highly conserved across diverse cell types, in microglia their functions may extend beyond mitochondrial quality control to actively participate in setting the threshold for innate immune activation.

Immunoregulatory functions of the PINK1/Parkin pathway in microglia

In microglia, the PINK1/Parkin pathway not only mediates the clearance of damaged mitochondria but also actively participates in setting the activation threshold of the NLRP3 inflammasome [61]. Loss-of-function of PINK1 or Parkin results in the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, which is associated with increased activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and the cGAS-STING signaling pathway [62–64]. Thus, in contrast to its role in neurons—where the primary objective is the maintenance of cell survival—the PINK1/Parkin pathway in microglia is functionally biased toward restricting the initiation and perpetuation of chronic inflammation. By facilitating the timely clearance of damaged mitochondria, this pathway limits the generation of pro-inflammatory signals at their source and helps maintain microglial innate immune activation within a physiological range. This functional property positions the PINK1/Parkin pathway as an integral component of the sensor layer of the microglial immunometabolic checkpoint.

Receptor-mediated mitophagy in the inflammatory microenvironment

Under conditions of inflammation, hypoxia, and metabolic stress, ubiquitin-independent pathways mediated by BNIP3/NIX and FUNDC1 play a compensatory role in microglial mitophagy [65]. Unlike the PINK1/Parkin pathway, these receptor-mediated mechanisms do not depend on mitochondrial membrane potential. Therefore, even in inflammatory contexts characterized by mitochondrial depolarization and instability, receptor-mediated mitophagy can sustain a basal level of mitochondrial clearance [66], serving as an adaptive compensatory mechanism under metabolic stress.

The expression of BNIP3 and NIX is regulated by hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) and is significantly upregulated under hypoxic conditions [67]. In the AD pathological milieu, microglia are chronically exposed to low-grade hypoxia and inflammatory stress. Inflammatory stimulation and increased phagocytic burden have been shown to moderately activate receptor-mediated mitophagy pathways [68]. In this context, BNIP3/NIX-dependent mitophagy may function as a buffering mechanism that delays microglial dysfunction. However, under sustained chronic inflammatory conditions, dysregulation of BNIP3/NIX signaling may shift mitophagy from an adaptive response to a maladaptive process, ultimately contributing to functional decline [69]. Similarly, the activity of FUNDC1 is tightly regulated by phosphorylation, and its dysfunction may further compromise mitochondrial clearance capacity under inflammatory stress, thereby exacerbating the pro-inflammatory phenotype of microglia [70]. This dual-system organization may enable microglia to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis across fluctuating inflammatory and metabolic conditions.

TBK1 as a central hub linking autophagy adaptors and inflammatory signaling

Autophagy adaptor proteins, including OPTN, NDP52, and p62, serve as key molecular bridges between mitochondrial ubiquitination signals and the autophagic machinery [71]. In microglia, these adaptors not only mediate mitophagy but also participate directly in innate immune signaling. Among them, TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) emerges as a central regulatory hub. TBK1 promotes mitophagy by phosphorylating OPTN and NDP52, thereby enhancing their interaction with LC3 [72]. At the same time, TBK1 functions as a core kinase in the cGAS–STING pathway, which mediates cytosolic DNA sensing and pro-inflammatory signaling [73].

The functional output of TBK1 depends on the intracellular signaling context. When mitophagy is functionally intact, TBK1 activity is primarily directed toward mitochondrial quality control. When mitochondrial damage accumulates and mitophagy is insufficient to clear damaged mitochondria, mtDNA leaked into the cytosol chronically activates TBK1 via the cGAS-STING axis [74–76], shifting its functional output from promoting mitochondrial clearance toward enhancing inflammatory signal transduction [77, 78]. This functional switch suggests that TBK1 may act as a molecular converter linking mitochondrial damage with innate immune responses.

In microglia, TBK1 functions not merely as a kinase but as a node that integrates mitochondrial quality information with the threshold for immune activation.Whether TBK1 signaling preferentially supports mitophagy or becomes associated with inflammatory signaling may depend, at least in part, on the integrity of mitophagic flux. This functional plasticity positions TBK1 as a key component of the “switch layer” within the microglial immunometabolic checkpoint, where it integrates mitochondrial damage signals and helps shape downstream pro-mitophagic or pro-inflammatory responses.

The TREM2–mTOR–TFEB axis as a microglia-specific regulatory pathway of mitophagy

The regulation of mitophagy in microglia is profoundly shaped by the interplay between immune receptor signaling and metabolic pathways, among which the TREM2-mTOR-TFEB axis constitutes a core regulatory network unique to microglia. [79]. TREM2 is a receptor for lipids and apoptotic cells expressed on myeloid cells. Through its adaptor protein DAP12, TREM2 activates the downstream PI3K-AKT pathway, leading to moderate upregulation of mTOR activity and thereby providing the energetic foundation for mitophagy initiation [80].Furthermore, TREM2 can regulate mitochondrial membrane lipid composition via nuclear receptors such as PPARs and LXRs, indirectly influencing the sensitivity of autophagosome recognition toward damaged mitochondria [81].

The balance between mTORC1 and TFEB constitutes a central regulatory node of the autophagy-lysosome system. Under physiological conditions, moderate mTOR activity supports cellular metabolic demands while maintaining partial nuclear translocation of TFEB, thereby ensuring basal expression of autophagy and lysosomal genes [82]. TFEB serves as a master transcriptional regulator of autophagy-lysosome biogenesis, controlling the transcriptional program of genes required for autophagosome formation and lysosomal function [82, 83]. When TREM2 signaling is intact, mTOR activity is maintained at a moderate level, allowing TFEB to enter the nucleus and initiate lysosomal biogenesis and the expression of autophagy-related genes, thereby sustaining the terminal degradation efficiency of mitophagy. For mitophagy, even if the PINK1/Parkin pathway successfully initiates mitochondrial tagging and autophagosome engulfment, impaired TFEB nuclear translocation leading to insufficient lysosomal biogenesis or reduced lysosomal function will still obstruct the final step of mitochondrial clearance [84].

The TREM2-mTOR-TFEB axis integrates extracellular immune sensing, intracellular metabolic status, and mitophagy-lysosome flux into a unified regulatory framework [85, 86]. The integrity of this axis determines whether microglia can maintain appropriate mitophagic activity and controlled inflammatory responses. This immune-specific regulatory module constitutes the tuning layer of the microglial immunometabolic checkpoint, setting the threshold for mitophagic flux by integrating extracellular danger signals (e.g., Aβ, lipids) and intracellular energy status.

Coupling between mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy

In microglia, the coupling between mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy carries important immunometabolic implications. Mitochondria are highly dynamic organelles that continuously undergo cycles of fission and fusion to achieve morphological remodeling, functional complementation, and damage segregation [87, 88]. Mitochondrial fusion is mediated by outer membrane proteins MFN1/2 and the inner membrane protein OPA1, facilitating content exchange and homogenization of membrane potential [89]. Mitochondrial fission depends on the recruitment of dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) to the outer mitochondrial membrane, where it cooperates with receptor proteins such as FIS1 and MFF to divide mitochondria into discrete fragments [90]. This dynamic balance not only preserves mitochondrial network architecture but also constitutes a critical quality control layer upstream of mitophagy.

In the canonical model of mitophagy, mitochondrial fission typically serves as a prerequisite for autophagic degradation. Upon localized loss of membrane potential or oxidative damage, DRP1-mediated fission segregates dysfunctional regions from the healthy mitochondrial network, generating smaller fragments amenable to autophagosomal engulfment [91]. When fission is impaired, damaged mitochondria cannot be effectively isolated even if the PINK1/Parkin pathway remains intact, resulting in markedly reduced mitophagy efficiency. Concurrently, MFN2 not only contributes to mitochondrial network formation but also functions as a recruitment receptor for Parkin, thereby directly linking mitochondrial dynamics to ubiquitin-dependent mitophagy [92].Although fusion can transiently buffer local damage, excessive fusion dilutes damaged components and delays their recognition and clearance. Therefore, the balance between fission and fusion is an important prerequisite for the selective delivery of mitochondria to the autophagic degradation pathway [93].

Upon microglial activation, increased fluctuations in mitochondrial membrane potential and enhanced fission activity facilitate the timely segregation and clearance of damaged mitochondria, thereby limiting mtROS production. [94].

When fission is enhanced but mitophagic clearance is insufficient, mitochondrial fragments accumulate and become a persistent source of mtDNA and mtROS, which in turn fuel the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and the NLRP3 inflammasome [14]. Thus, the coordination between mitochondrial fission and mitophagy may help sets the threshold for microglial innate immune activation. This coupled regulation represents a manifestation of the “immunometabolic checkpoint” at the level of organelle dynamics.

Based on the molecular pathways detailed above, we propose that microglial mitophagy operates as an immunometabolic checkpoint organized into three functional layers. The first is a sensor layer, in which PINK1/Parkin and receptor‑mediated pathways (BNIP3/NIX/FUNDC1) detect mitochondrial damage and determine whether damaged mitochondria are tagged for clearance. The second is a switch layer, where TBK1 functions as a molecular converter. When mitophagy is intact, TBK1 promotes autophagic adaptor activity, whereas when mitophagy is impaired, persistent mtDNA leakage may bias TBK1 toward STING-driven inflammatory signaling, thereby linking mitochondrial quality status to innate immune output. The third is a tuning layer, represented by the TREM2‑mTOR‑TFEB axis, which adjusts the setpoint of the checkpoint by integrating extracellular signals (e.g., Aβ, lipids, TREM2 ligands) and intracellular metabolic state, thus controlling lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic flux. The functional outcome of this checkpoint, namely mitochondrial homeostasis versus inflammatory locking, depends on the integrity of all three layers. Disruption of microglial mitophagy and pathological amplification in AD

The core pathological features of AD, including Aβ, tau, and the genetic risk factors TREM2 and APOE4, disrupt microglial mitophagy through three principal mechanisms: direct mitochondrial toxicity, inhibition of mitophagy initiation (via ubiquitin-dependent or receptor-mediated pathways), and impairment of the autophagy-lysosome system. As a consequence, defective mitophagy triggers a cascade of downstream effects, including inflammatory locking, impaired phagocytic function, and enhanced intercellular propagation.

Disruption of microglial mitophagy by AD Pathological Factors

Microglial mitophagy dysfunction arises from the combined action of multiple pathological factors rather than a single isolated cause. These include both the direct toxicity of pathological proteins such as Aβ and tau and immunometabolic dysregulation driven by genetic risk factors including TREM2 and APOE4.

Distinct mechanisms by which Aβ and tau pathology may impair microglial mitophagy

Aβ oligomers exert direct toxicity on microglial mitochondria. Similar to their effects in neurons, Aβ can disrupt mitochondrial calcium homeostasis and reduce mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby contributing to mitochondrial damage burden [95]. In addition, Aβ interferes with the stabilization of PINK1 and the recruitment of Parkin, effectively impairing the initiation of ubiquitin-dependent mitophagy [96]. These alterations are thought to contribute to sustained mitochondrial dysfunction and persistent inflammatory responses in microglia. Moreover, Aβ disrupts inter-organelle communication between mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum, and lysosomes, impairing both autophagosome formation and autophagosome–lysosome fusion [97]. Chronic Aβ exposure further compromises lysosomal acidification and degradative capacity, thereby hindering the terminal steps of mitophagy and contributing to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria [98]. Functionally, these defects manifest as impaired migration toward Aβ plaques, reduced phagocytosis, and defective post-phagocytic degradation in microglia [99].

Compared with Aβ, tau pathology may affect microglial mitophagy primarily through indirect and stage-dependent mechanisms. In early AD, tau pathology is largely confined to neurons, where mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal stress may generate secondary signals that activate microglia [100]. As disease progresses, extracellular tau species released from degenerating neurons can be internalized by microglia, where they induce mitochondrial stress, elevate mtROS production, and activate inflammatory signaling pathways such as TLR- and inflammasome-associated cascades [101, 102]. Mechanistically, tau-driven mitochondrial stress and inflammatory activation may converge on regulatory nodes of mitophagy, such as PINK1/Parkin signaling and lysosome-associated pathways. In this context, TLR-driven NF-κB signaling may sustain mTORC1 activation and impair TFEB nuclear translocation, whereas tau-induced mtROS accumulation may interfere with PINK1 stabilization and Parkin recruitment [103]. Consequently, disruption of mitophagy flux may compromise lysosomal degradation capacity and reduce the ability of microglia to clear pathological tau efficiently [104]. This impaired clearance, in turn, may further promote extracellular tau accumulation and sustained mitochondrial stress, creating a self-perpetuating feed-forward loop that traps microglia in a state of immunometabolic dysfunction.

Therefore, rather than acting through identical mechanisms, Aβ and tau pathology may influence microglial mitophagy through temporally and mechanistically distinct pathways. Whereas extracellular Aβ aggregates appear to exert relatively direct effects on microglial mitochondrial homeostasis, tau pathology may initially affect microglia indirectly through neuronal dysfunction, with direct interactions between extracellular tau species and microglia becoming increasingly relevant during disease progression. Despite these differences, both pathological processes may converge on mitochondrial stress and impaired mitochondrial quality control, thereby contributing to chronic neuroinflammation in AD.

TREM2–APOE4 axis–mediated immunometabolic dysregulation and mitophagy impairment

Genetic risk factors in AD critically shape the mitophagic capacity of microglia, with the TREM2–APOE axis representing a central immunometabolic regulatory node.TREM2 signaling regulates the overall flux of the autophagy-lysosome system through the mTOR-TFEB axis. In the AD pathological context, this regulatory network becomes imbalanced due to impaired TREM2 function. When TREM2 signaling is attenuated, mTOR becomes aberrantly and persistently activated. Hyperactivated mTORC1 phosphorylates the ULK1 complex to inhibit autophagy initiation, and concurrently phosphorylates the transcription factor TFEB, thereby preventing its nuclear translocation and suppressing the transcription of autophagy- and lysosome-related genes such as LC3, LAMP1, and CTSD [105]. As a result, lysosomal biogenesis is reduced and the terminal step of autophagic flux is obstructed, leading to the abnormal intracellular accumulation of mitochondria that have already been engulfed by autophagosomes but cannot be effectively cleared.

The presence of the APOE4 allele further exacerbates this defect. Microglia carrying APOE4 exhibit pronounced impairments in mitochondrial energy metabolism and elevated levels of oxidative stress [106]. APOE4 impairs microglial mitophagy through dual molecular mechanisms. It simultaneously downregulates the expression of Drp1, a key protein for mitochondrial fission, and upregulates the fusion protein Mfn1, resulting in an abnormally hyperfused mitochondrial network characterized by increased branching and network complexity with a reduced number of discrete individual mitochondria. In this state, damaged mitochondria cannot be properly fragmented into small pieces suitable for autophagic engulfment [107]. This abnormal morphology directly impedes subsequent autophagic flux, reduces the fusion efficiency between autophagosomes and lysosomes, and is associated with the massive intracellular accumulation of mitochondria with decreased membrane potential and impaired function that cannot be cleared [108].

Additionally, APOE4 can directly compromise mitochondrial homeostasis by disrupting mitochondrial membrane integrity or the structure of mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) [109], while APOE-induced lipid metabolic dysregulation indirectly suppresses the mitophagy process [110]. Dysregulation of the TREM2–APOE axis thus shifts microglial mitophagy from a protective quality control mechanism to an inefficient or even pathology-promoting state under Aβ burden [111].

A bidirectional deteriorative relationship exists between Aβ and the TREM2-APOE axis. Aβ-induced aberrant TREM2 signaling promotes excessive lipid droplet accumulation in microglia [112, 113]. Although this metabolic adaptation may provide transient energy buffering, chronic lipid overload further suppresses phagocytic function and amplifies inflammatory responses, thereby exacerbating mitophagy impairment. Collectively, dysregulation of the TREM2-APOE4 axis transforms microglial mitophagy from a protective quality control system into a pathological amplification loop, driving the progressive escalation of neuroinflammation under sustained Aβ stimulation.

Inflammatory locking and functional exhaustion

When mitophagy is disrupted by the aforementioned AD pathological factors, microglia may enter a self-sustaining pathological cycle characterized by mitochondrial damage accumulation, sustained inflammatory signaling, and altered functional states. This section delineates the core mechanisms underlying this cycle from two perspectives: mitochondrial DAMP-driven inflammatory locking, and the exhaustion of phagocytic clearance and synaptic pruning functions.

Mitochondrial DAMP–Driven inflammatory locking

The disruption of microglial mitophagy by AD pathological factors not only contributes to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria but also can initiate a self-sustaining inflammatory cycle centered on the persistent leakage of mitochondrial DAMPs [114]. The intractability of this cycle is rooted in the unique features of AD pathology. Aβ oligomers and pathological tau, as continuously accumulating pathological entities, constantly impose new damaging stimuli on microglial mitochondria, placing the mitophagy system under sustained burden [115]. TREM2 signaling deficiency and the APOE4 risk allele result in reduced lysosomal biogenesis and impaired degradative function, creating a structural blockade of the terminal step of mitophagy rather than a reversible functional inhibition. Consequently, even when upstream recognition and engulfment steps are initiated, damaged mitochondria cannot be effectively degraded, and the leakage of mtDNA and mtROS becomes a persistent event rather than a transient fluctuation.mtDNA leaked into the cytosol chronically activates TBK1 via the cGAS-STING axis. Under physiological conditions, the kinase activity of TBK1 serves the phosphorylation of mitophagy adaptors and autophagosome formation [71, 74]. In the AD pathological milieu, however, persistent mtDNA release may promote preferential engagement of TBK1 in STING signaling, thereby shifting its functional output from supporting mitochondrial quality control toward facilitating type I interferon responses [116, 117]. Concurrently, mtROS acts as a potent activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome, continuously promoting caspase-1-mediated maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18 [75]. This may contribute to the formation of a self-reinforcing cycle that is difficult to resolve in the AD environment: the structural blockade of mitophagy leads to persistent leakage of mtDNA and mtROS, the leaked DAMPs drive chronic activation of STING-TBK1 and the NLRP3 inflammasome, and the resulting inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α further impair mitophagic capacity by activating the mTOR pathway and suppressing the expression of autophagy-related proteins, thereby favoring the maintenance of a persistent pro-inflammatory state.

The formation of this inflammatory lock signifies a qualitative shift in microglial functional state. Under physiological conditions or in the setting of acute injury, mitophagy impairment and DAMP leakage are typically transient, and cells can restore homeostasis by enhancing autophagic flux and activating antioxidant defenses [118]. In the chronic pathological environment of AD, however, the persistent presence of Aβ and tau, the structural deficiency of the TREM2-APOE4 axis, and the positive feedback regulation of inflammatory cytokines together constitute multiple locking mechanisms that prevent microglia from exiting the inflammatory state even when external stimuli fluctuate [119]. Therefore, reprogramming of the mtDNA-STING-TBK1 signaling axis associated with mitophagy impairment may represent an important transition point through which microglia shift from adaptive immune responses toward chronic neuroinflammatory states in AD, and may reflect one manifestation of immunometabolic checkpoint dysregulation at the inflammatory level.

Exhaustion of phagocytic clearance and synaptic pruning functions

The inflammatory locking state may contribute to the progressive exhaustion of core microglial functions. Immune surveillance, phagocytic clearance, and synaptic pruning by microglia are highly dependent on mitochondrial ATP supply and an intact lysosomal degradation system. The accumulation of damaged mitochondria resulting from impaired mitophagy not only causes insufficient ATP production but also leads to persistently elevated levels of mtROS and mtDNA, which further compromise phagosome maturation and lysosomal acidification. A direct consequence is a marked decline in the capacity of microglia to phagocytose and clear Aβ and cellular debris [105, 120].

Reduced phagocytic clearance means that Aβ cannot be effectively removed, leading to a sustained increase in the concentration of Aβ oligomers surrounding plaques. Because Aβ itself is a potent inducer of mitochondrial damage and an inflammatory stimulus, its accumulation further exacerbates mitophagy impairment and reinforces the inflammatory locking state. Concurrently, microglia with defective mitophagy also exhibit impaired clearance of pathological tau. Uncleared tau not only imposes persistent metabolic stress but also propagates pathology through intercellular transmission, and dysfunctional microglia may even facilitate tau spreading [121]. The continuous accumulation of pathological proteins resulting from impaired Aβ and tau clearance in turn aggravates mitochondrial damage and mitophagy deficiency, further deteriorating the clearance function of microglia [55, 122].

Aberrant synaptic pruning represents another important manifestation of functional exhaustion. Mitophagy impairment may favor a shift from a homeostatic state toward a pro-inflammatory state, accompanied by increased expression of complement factors C1q and C3 [123]. Under physiological conditions, complement-mediated synaptic pruning is an essential mechanism for refining neural circuits. In the AD pathological environment, however, persistent metabolic stress and inflammatory signaling divert this process from physiological remodeling toward pathological excessive elimination, leading to aberrant synaptic loss and neural network dysfunction [124]. Moreover, synaptic debris released by excessive pruning can act as a novel inflammatory stimulus, activating surrounding glial cells and amplifying local inflammatory responses.

The functional exhaustion triggered by mitophagy impairment is not an isolated event but rather a pathological cycle component nested within inflammatory locking. Inflammatory locking deprives microglia of the metabolic environment required to restore normal function, while the loss of phagocytic clearance and synaptic pruning capacities provides a sustained source of pathological stimulation that perpetuates the inflammatory lock. This self-reinforcing vicious cycle explains why microglia, once trapped in a state of “immunometabolic checkpoint”dysregulation, cannot readily recover spontaneously.

Cell type-specific mitochondrial inflammatory signaling and intercellular propagation

The pathological consequences of impaired microglial mitophagy are not limited to cell-autonomous dysfunction. While microglia remain the primary focus of this review, accumulating evidence suggests that mitochondrial stress responses in neighboring CNS cell types may participate in reciprocal inflammatory interactions and thereby influence the persistence of neuroinflammation. In addition to microglia, astrocytes and neurons are increasingly recognized as active participants in these processes through distinct responses to mitochondrial perturbation. Microglia predominantly engage the NLRP3 inflammasome and cGAS–STING pathways, whereas astrocytes have been reported to activate NF-κB- and STAT3-associated reactive programs, and neurons may induce type I interferon responses through the TBK1–IRF3 axis. These cell type-specific signaling events raise the possibility that mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to interconnected inflammatory circuits within the AD brain.

Microglia–astrocyte reciprocal inflammatory interactions

Astrocytes are essential for maintaining neuronal homeostasis and regulating inflammatory responses in the CNS. Emerging evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction in astrocytes may contribute to the acquisition of reactive phenotypes through cell type-specific inflammatory signaling pathways. Excessive mtROS accumulation has been associated with activation of NF-κB and STAT3 signaling, while cytosolic leakage of mitochondrial DNA may engage cGAS–STING-dependent responses under certain conditions. Consequently, reactive astrocytes have been reported to exhibit increased expression of inflammatory mediators, including complement component C3, IL-6, and CXCL10, which may influence neighboring microglia and contribute to the persistence of neuroinflammation [125, 126].

Reciprocally, activated microglia release pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and complement-associated molecules, which have been implicated in the induction of reactive astrocyte states. Therefore, astrocytes and microglia may establish a positive feedback mechanism through which mitochondrial stress and inflammatory signaling mutually reinforce one another. These observations suggest that the consequences of microglial mitochondrial dysfunction may extend beyond microglia themselves and influence astrocytic responses through intercellular communication.

Besides inflammatory signaling, mitochondrial impairment may compromise the metabolic support functions of astrocytes. Impaired mitochondrial homeostasis has been associated with reduced expression of excitatory amino acid transporter 2 (EAAT2), resulting in defective glutamate clearance and increased extracellular glutamate accumulation [127]. Such alterations may exacerbate neuronal excitotoxic stress and indirectly amplify inflammatory responses, suggesting that astrocytic mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to both metabolic and inflammatory disturbances within the neural microenvironment.

Microglia–neuron feed-forward inflammatory loops

Neurons are increasingly recognized as active participants in neuroinflammation rather than merely passive targets. In the AD pathological milieu, mitochondrial damage induced by Aβ and tau pathology, together with inflammatory mediators from activated microglia, can trigger neurons to release danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including ATP, HMGB1, cardiolipin, and oxidized mitochondrial DNA [128]. These neuronal-derived DAMPs in turn engage pattern-recognition receptors and purinergic signaling pathways on neighboring microglia, thereby amplifying local inflammatory responses.

Emerging evidence also suggests that neuronal mitochondrial stress may induce cytosolic mtDNA release and activate cGAS–STING signaling, leading to type I interferon responses through the TBK1–IRF3 axis [129, 130]. The resulting production of chemokines, including CCL2 and CXCL10, may facilitate microglial recruitment and activation [131]. Conversely, inflammatory mediators released by activated microglia may further aggravate neuronal mitochondrial dysfunction and enhance neuronal susceptibility to injury [132]. Such reciprocal interactions may contribute to self-reinforcing inflammatory circuits that sustain chronic neuroinflammation in AD.

Interestingly, recent studies have identified a more direct mechanism linking microglia to neuronal mitochondrial quality control. In the AD pathological environment, a subset of PTPRG-positive microglia has been reported to interact with neurons through the PTPRG–CNTN4 ligand–receptor pair, resulting in increased expression of the m6A methyltransferase VIRMA in neurons. Elevated VIRMA expression suppresses Parkin translation and impairs neuronal mitophagy flux, ultimately promoting neuronal degeneration [56]. Although the functional significance and in vivo relevance of this pathway require further validation, these findings raise the possibility that mitochondrial quality control defects may be propagated through specific intercellular communication pathways rather than solely through soluble inflammatory mediators.

Collectively, these observations suggest that the impact of microglial mitophagy impairment may extend beyond cell-autonomous dysfunction and influence neighboring CNS cell types through reciprocal inflammatory interactions. Rather than representing isolated events, mitochondrial stress responses in astrocytes and neurons may participate in interconnected signaling networks that contribute to the maintenance of chronic neuroinflammation and thereby facilitate AD progression.

Mitophagy impairment and microglial cell death

Persistent mitophagy impairment may contribute to the establishment of inflammatory locking and functional exhaustion in microglia and may favor the induction of specific forms of cell death, thereby acting as a potential feed-forward amplifier of AD-associated pathological processes. Among these, ferroptosis and pyroptosis are two cell death modalities closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation. This section will respectively delineate the interplay between these cell death pathways and impaired microglial mitophagy.

Antagonistic imbalance between ferroptosis and mitophagy

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, and its occurrence is closely associated with the inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4, peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids, and abnormal mitochondrial morphology [133]. Under physiological conditions, mitophagy exerts an inhibitory effect on ferroptosis by promptly clearing damaged mitochondria and thereby reducing the release of mtROS and iron ions. In the AD pathological environment, however, the balance between mitophagy and ferroptosis is disrupted by multiple factors [134].

The APOE4 allele is an important contributor to this disruption. Microglia carrying APOE4 exhibit dysregulated lipid metabolism and decreased mitochondrial membrane integrity, leading to significantly elevated levels of lipid peroxidation. Concurrently, Aβ-induced mitophagy deficiency results in the accumulation of damaged mitochondria and the persistent release of Fe2⁺ and mtROS, which provide abundant substrates and catalysts for the lipid peroxidation chain reaction [135]. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have further identified a subpopulation of lipid droplet-accumulating microglia in the AD brain, in which the expression of ferroptosis-related genes such as ACSL4 and TFRC is upregulated, suggesting that this subpopulation is highly susceptible to ferroptosis [136]. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have identified lipid droplet-accumulating microglia (LDAM) in the AD brain. These cells exhibit upregulation of ferroptosis-related genes (e.g., ACSL4 and TFRC), suggesting a heightened susceptibility to ferroptosis.

The occurrence of ferroptosis is not an isolated terminal cellular event. Lipid peroxidation products and damage-associated molecular patterns released from dying cells can act as potent inflammatory stimuli, activating surrounding microglia and astrocytes and further amplifying local neuroinflammation. Therefore, impaired mitophagy not only increases the susceptibility of microglia themselves to ferroptosis but also exacerbates the deterioration of the AD pathological microenvironment through ferroptosis-associated inflammatory amplification.

Pyroptosis and the inflammatory amplification of mitochondrial DAMPs

Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death mediated by inflammasome activation and caspase-1, characterized by the formation of plasma membrane pores and the robust release of IL-1β and IL-18 [137]. Unlike ferroptosis, the core driving force of pyroptosis is not metabolic imbalance but rather the excessive activation of innate immune signaling. It is important to note, however, that inflammasome activation primarily drives the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18; the progression to full pyroptotic cell death typically requires sufficiently strong and sustained activation signals [138]. Therefore, while mitophagy impairment may promote inflammasome signaling, this does not inevitably lead to pyroptosis.

Mitophagy suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation by clearing damaged mitochondria, thereby limiting the leakage of mtROS and mtDNA. When mitophagy is compromised by AD-related pathological factors, persistent leakage of mtROS and mtDNA may promote NLRP3 inflammasome activation and caspase‑1 signaling. Under conditions of sustained or intense inflammatory signaling, this pathway may further engage GSDMD‑mediated membrane permeabilization and pyroptotic responses [139]. Importantly, post‑mortem human AD brain tissue has revealed cleaved GSDMD in plaque‑associated microglia, astrocytes, and a subset of CA1 pyramidal neurons [140]. Notably, only microglia expressed the complete set of canonical NLRP3 inflammasome proteins (ASC, NLRP3, caspase‑1), whereas GSDMD cleavage in astrocytes and neurons correlated with caspase‑8 and non‑canonical inflammasome protein caspase‑4, respectively, suggesting alternative, cell type‑specific pathways for gasdermin activation [141]. This study also reported that NLRP3‑positive microglia and cleaved GSDMD‑positive neurons in the CA1 region were associated with local neuronal loss, indicating a possible contribution of pyroptosis‑related pathways to AD neurodegeneration.

The occurrence of pyroptosis is not merely an endpoint of cell death but may also serve as a node for inflammatory amplification [142]. The large amounts of IL-1β and IL-18 released by pyroptotic microglia may further activate neighboring microglia and astrocytes, thereby contributing to local inflammatory amplification. Concurrently, the cytosolic contents released during pyroptosis contain mtDNA and oxidized mitochondrial components. These DAMPs can be taken up by surrounding cells and activate their innate immune pathways, thereby transmitting mitochondrial damage signals from dying cells to surviving cells. Thus, microglial pyroptosis may facilitate the propagation of inflammatory signals beyond the initially affected cells [143].

Compared with ferroptosis, the significance of pyroptosis in AD microglia may lie more prominently in the amplification and propagation of inflammatory signaling. If ferroptosis reflects the metabolic collapse resulting from impaired microglial mitophagy, pyroptosis may represent the innate immune dysregulation that can arise from this impairment under sustained inflammatory conditions. Together, these two processes could represent complementary metabolic and inflammatory dimensions of microglial dysfunction associated with mitophagy impairment. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the presence of cleaved GSDMD in human AD brain tissue does not by itself establish pyroptosis as a dominant mechanism of neurodegeneration; functional studies directly distinguishing pyroptosis from other gasdermin‑related processes remain limited.

Bidirectional imbalance of mitophagy and pathological amplification

The preceding sections have systematically delineated how AD pathological factors disrupt microglial mitophagy and trigger a cascade of downstream consequences including inflammatory locking, functional exhaustion, and cell death. However, this does not imply that simply enhancing mitophagy would yield beneficial effects in AD therapy. The regulation of mitophagic flux is strictly direction-dependent, and both insufficient function and excessive activation can lead to microglial dysfunction.

When mitophagy is excessively activated, the rate of mitochondrial clearance may exceed the capacity of mitochondrial biogenesis, resulting in a net loss of mitochondria and impaired energy metabolism in microglia [144]. Reduced ATP production and compromised OXPHOS not only weaken phagocytic clearance and inflammatory regulation but may also force microglia to rely excessively on glycolysis, precipitating maladaptive immunometabolic reprogramming [145]. Although mitophagy deficiency represents the predominant manifestation in AD, the risk posed by excessive activation underscores that therapeutic strategies must precisely modulate mitophagic flux rather than simply pursuing unidirectional activation or inhibition.

Thus, the significance of microglial mitophagy in AD pathology can be conceptualized as a regulatory node characterized by “bidirectional imbalance”. Mitophagy deficiency driven by Aβ, tau, and genetic risk factorsmay contribute to the maintenance of chronic neuroinflammation, yet any therapeutic intervention must strike a precise balance between restoring clearance capacity and averting excessive activation. Maintaining optimal mitophagic flux, rather than merely enhancing or suppressing this process, constitutes a key principle for targeting this node in AD therapy.

In summary, based on the evidence discussed above, disruption of microglial mitophagy by AD pathological factors is unlikely to represent an isolated organellar event but may instead contribute to a multi-level, self-reinforcing pathological amplification network. Aβ, tau, TREM2 dysfunction, and the APOE4 risk allele converge to cause a structural blockade of mitophagy through direct mitochondrial toxicity, inhibition of mitophagy initiation, and impairment of autophagolysosomal degradation. This disruption manifests as a coordinated failure across the three functional layers of the immunometabolic checkpoint: the sensor layer (impaired PINK1/Parkin and receptor-mediated pathways), the switch layer (TBK1 locked in STING-driven inflammatory mode rather than supporting mitophagy), and the tuning layer (TREM2-APOE4 axis defects compromising lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic flux).The resulting inflammatory locking state deprives microglia of the capacity to restore homeostasis, while functional exhaustion compromises their efficacy in clearing pathological proteins. Ferroptosis and pyroptosis push cellular injury toward irreversible endpoints from the dual dimensions of metabolism and immunity.Dysfunctional microglia can also propagate mitochondrial damage signals to the neural network through inflammatory mediators and direct intercellular communication, thereby extending the pathological effects from the single-cell level to the tissue level. These mechanisms are mutually embedded and reciprocally amplifying, constituting the cell biological basis for the intractability of chronic neuroinflammation in AD. Reconciling this checkpoint may require not simply enhancing mitophagy, but restoring its hierarchical integrity across all three layers. Reconciling mitophagy mechanisms with human genetics in AD

An important consideration when interpreting the role of mitophagy in Alzheimer's disease (AD) is that several canonical mitophagy regulators, including PINK1, PRKN, TBK1, and TFEB, have not been consistently identified as major susceptibility loci in large-scale AD genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [146]. In contrast, mutations in PINK1 and PRKN are well-established causes of autosomal recessive early-onset Parkinson's disease (PD), indicating a clearer genetic connection between canonical mitophagy pathways and PD than has been observed in AD.

This distinction suggests that the contribution of canonical mitophagy pathways to neurodegeneration may differ between PD and AD. In PD, inherited defects in PINK1/PRKN directly impair mitochondrial quality control and constitute established causes of familial disease. By contrast, major AD risk loci are predominantly associated with lipid metabolism, endosomal trafficking, and microglial immune regulation, including APOE, TREM2, ABCA7, PLCG2, and CD33 [147, 148].These observations raise the possibility that mitophagy dysfunction in AD is not commonly initiated by inherited defects in canonical mitophagy genes.

One possible explanation for this apparent discrepancy is that impaired mitophagy in AD may arise largely as a consequence of upstream disease-related stressors, including Aβ accumulation, tau pathology, APOE4-associated lipid dysregulation, and TREM2-dependent alterations in microglial state. These factors can converge on mitochondrial homeostasis and progressively compromise mitophagic capacity, even in the absence of mutations in canonical mitophagy genes.

Therefore, despite the relatively limited genetic evidence, mitophagy may still represent an important interface through which multiple upstream pathological processes converge.Rather, mitophagy may represent an important interface through which multiple upstream pathological processes converge to sustain neuroinflammation and immunometabolic dysfunction. This perspective may also help explain why strategies aimed at restoring mitophagy remain therapeutically attractive in sporadic AD, despite the relatively limited genetic evidence linking canonical mitophagy genes to disease susceptibility.

Therapeutic strategies targeting microglial mitophagy

Building upon the pathological amplification framework outlined above, microglial mitophagy dysfunction resides at the intersection of immunometabolic dysregulation and chronic neuroinflammation in AD. Restoring this critical mitochondrial quality control process holds promise for interrupting the pathogenic cascade at its origin. Based on the hierarchical levels of intervention targets, current strategies can be categorized into three groups: directly enhancing mitophagy to restore or compensate for impaired mitochondrial clearance; suppressing downstream inflammatory signaling to interrupt the inflammatory amplification driven by mitochondrial DAMPs; and targeting upstream immunometabolic regulation to re-establish microglial functional homeostasis. In addition, non-pharmacological interventions and emerging technologies such as nanoparticle-based delivery systems provide new avenues for achieving precise and spatiotemporally controlled modulation of mitophagy.

Strategies for directly enhancing mitophagy

Directly enhancing mitophagy aims to restore or compensate for the impaired mitochondrial clearance capacity in AD, thereby reducing the leakage of mtDNA and mtROS at their source. Based on the distinct intervention targets, these strategies can be categorized into four directions.

Targeting the PINK1/parkin pathway

Multiple natural products and small-molecule compounds have been demonstrated to activate the PINK1/Parkin pathway. 2,3,5,4'-tetrahydroxystilbene-2-O-β-D-glucoside upregulates PINK1 and Parkin expression via the AMPK-ULK1 axis, restores autophagic flux, and reduces mtROS accumulation, thereby suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation [149, 150]. Quercetin similarly promotes PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy, and its protective effects can be reversed by the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA, supporting a mechanistic link between mitophagy activation and neuroprotection [94, 151]. However, most of these studies are based on BV-2 cell lines or short-term animal experiments, and their efficacy and safety in human AD remain to be further validated.

Targeting receptor-mediated pathways and metabolic regulation

Urolithin A enhances mitophagic flux by promoting autophagosome-lysosome fusion while upregulating the expression of phagocytosis-related proteins in microglia, thereby improving Aβ clearance capacity [152].NAD + precursors such as nicotinamide riboside enhance mitophagy by activating the AMPK-ULK1 signaling axis, reducing mtDNA leakage and suppressing cGAS-STING pathway activation. These effects are accompanied by improved cognitive function and attenuated neuroinflammation in APP/PS1 mouse models [153]. It should be noted that NAD + metabolism exerts broad cellular effects [154], and caution is warranted in distinguishing microglia-specific contributions from indirect effects on other cell types when interpreting its mechanism of action.

Targeting the TFEB-lysosome axis

In a model of CKLF-induced microglial dysfunction, the mTORC1 inhibitor Torin1 restores TFEB activity, promotes lysosomal biogenesis, and re-establishes the clearance capacity for damaged mitochondria [155]. Since TREM2 signaling regulates autophagy-lysosome function via the mTOR-TFEB axis, TREM2 agonistic antibodies may also indirectly enhance mitophagy through activation of this pathway. Systemic administration of mTORC1 inhibitors may trigger off-target effects such as metabolic disturbances, highlighting the need to develop cell type-specific delivery strategies as an important future optimization direction.

Targeting transcriptional regulation and non-canonical pathways

The NRF2 agonist CDDO-Im simultaneously suppresses inflammatory gene expression and enhances autophagic flux in human microglial HMC3 cells, an effect achieved through direct binding to the regulatory regions of multiple autophagy-related genes [156, 157]. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses have further revealed that NRF2-mediated autophagy regulation plays a critical role in microglia within the brains of AD patients. When canonical pathways are impaired, the small molecule ALT001 restores microglial mitochondrial clearance capacity and Aβ phagocytic function by activating the ULK1/Rab9-dependent non-canonical mitophagy pathway [61], suggesting that activating alternative clearance routes may represent a more feasible intervention strategy.

Despite the promising prospects demonstrated by direct enhancement of mitophagy in preclinical models, these strategies still face several common challenges. Most compounds exhibit limited blood–brain barrier penetration efficiency and brain-selective enrichment capacity, and microglia and other neural cells may respond differently to the same compound. More critically, an optimal window exists for mitophagic flux, as excessive activation may lead to mitochondrial depletion and bioenergetic collapse. Achieving precise and spatiotemporally controlled regulation represents a core challenge that must be addressed for these strategies to progress from preclinical investigation to clinical application.

Strategies for suppressing downstream inflammatory signaling

Unlike directly enhancing mitophagy to reduce the leakage of mtDNA and mtROS at their source, suppressing downstream inflammatory signaling aims to block the innate immune activation driven by mitochondrial DAMPs that have already been released. These strategies primarily target two critical nodes: the cGAS-STING pathway and the NLRP3 inflammasome.

Targeting the cGAS-STING pathway

Mitophagy deficiency leads to the leakage of mtDNA into the cytosol, driving type I interferon responses via the cGAS-STING axis. The STING inhibitor H-151 and the cGAS inhibitor RU.521 have demonstrated efficacy in attenuating neuroinflammation in various CNS injury models [158, 159]. NAD + precursor supplementation indirectly suppresses excessive STING pathway activation by enhancing mitophagy and thereby reducing mtDNA leakage [160]. However, the cGAS-STING axis plays a crucial physiological role in antiviral innate immunity, and long-term systemic inhibition may increase the risk of infection. Therefore, developing microglia-specific delivery systems or adopting indirect regulatory strategies may represent more feasible approaches.

Targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome

MtROS is a potent activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome, driving caspase-1-dependent maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18. The NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 reduces Aβ deposition and improves cognitive function in AD animal models, effects primarily attributed to the suppression of the pro-inflammatory microglial phenotype [161]. The IL-1 receptor antagonist anakinra similarly exhibits protective effects. However, the NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-1β serve indispensable physiological functions in host defense, and complete blockade may carry immunosuppressive risks. Furthermore, the clinical development of MCC950 has been hampered by safety concerns, including hepatotoxicity.

Targeting the mtROS-NLRP3 axis

Metformin nanoparticles inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation by suppressing excessive mtROS production, a mechanism involving the downregulation of Beclin-1 and Atg5 and the upregulation of Sirt3, thereby promoting the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria [115, 162]. The implication of this strategy is that by modulating mtROS as an upstream signal for NLRP3 activation, inflammation can be suppressed while avoiding direct blockade of NLRP3 itself.

Strategies aimed at suppressing downstream inflammatory signaling face a core challenge rooted in the dual physiological functions of their targets. Both the cGAS-STING pathway and the NLRP3 inflammasome are essential components of host defense, and long-term inhibition may compromise immune surveillance function. Combining these strategies with enhanced mitophagy to reduce the reliance on downstream inflammatory signaling by decreasing DAMP release at its source may achieve a more favorable balance between efficacy and safety.

Strategies for restoring upstream immunometabolic regulation

Unlike strategies that directly target the execution steps of mitophagy, restoring upstream immunometabolic regulation aims to re-establish the physiological balance by which microglia sense and respond to pathological signals, thereby indirectly restoring mitophagy function. These strategies primarily center on the TREM2-APOE axis and cellular metabolic reprogramming.

Targeting the TREM2-APOE axis

TREM2 agonistic antibodies promote microglial clustering around Aβ plaques, enhance phagocytic function, and improve mitochondrial metabolism in preclinical AD models [163]. APOE4 structure correctors indirectly restore mitophagy capacity by ameliorating APOE4-associated lipid metabolic dysregulation and disruption of mitochondrial membrane integrity.The challenge facing this strategy lies in the spatiotemporal complexity of TREM2 signaling, wherein early activation may confer protective effects whereas sustained or excessive activation may promote inflammation [164]. Furthermore, the pleiotropic nature of APOE4 implies that single-target strategies may be insufficient to fully reverse its pathological effects.

Targeting immunometabolic reprogramming

Glutaminase 1 inhibitors improve mitochondrial function by inducing mitophagy and selectively suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby attenuating neuroinflammation and Aβ pathology in AD models [40]. The key implication of this strategy is that reprogramming the metabolic profile of microglia can indirectly restore mitophagy capacity while circumventing the risk of excessive activation that may accompany direct intervention in autophagy pathways. However, metabolic interventions generally lack cell type specificity, and long-term alterations in cellular metabolic state may trigger compensatory adaptations, necessitating careful safety evaluation in the context of chronic disease.

Strategies aimed at restoring upstream immunometabolic regulation face a core challenge rooted in the biological complexity of the targets and the spatiotemporal precision required for intervention. Both TREM2 and APOE exhibit pleiotropic functions, and their pathological effects vary according to disease stage and brain region. Metabolic reprogramming requires striking a balance between restoring oxidative phosphorylation and maintaining necessary glycolytic activity. Achieving microglia-selective regulation, optimizing the timing of intervention, and developing efficient blood–brain barrier penetrating delivery systems constitute critical prerequisites for the clinical translation of these strategies.

Non-pharmacological interventions and emerging delivery technologies

In addition to pharmacological strategies, non-pharmacological interventions and emerging nanoparticle-based delivery technologies provide complementary approaches for modulating microglial mitophagy. Non-pharmacological interventions regulate mitophagy through lifestyle modifications or physical factors, offering the potential advantages of high safety and feasibility for long-term implementation. Nanoparticle-based delivery systems aim to address two common challenges: blood–brain barrier penetration and cell type selectivity.

Non-pharmacological interventions

Treadmill exercise activates microglial mitophagy in traumatic brain injury models, suppresses excessive activation of the STING pathway driven by mtDNA leakage, reduces pyroptosis, and promotes a shift toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype [159]. Although direct evidence in AD models remains to be established, this finding suggests that exercise may attenuate neuroinflammation through enhancing mitophagy [165]. Molecular hydrogen, as a selective antioxidant, activates the NRF2 pathway and modulates autophagic flux, thereby ameliorating mitochondrial dysfunction, restraining microglial overactivation, and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine release in AD models [166]. Hydrogen offers the advantages of a favorable safety profile, multiple administration routes, and efficient blood–brain barrier penetration, facilitating its clinical translation [167]. Fisetin enhances mitophagy in brain microvascular endothelial cells, thereby inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reducing IL-1β release, and indirectly suppresses microglia-driven neuroinflammation by ameliorating the inflammatory microenvironment at the blood–brain barrier [168]. The core challenge facing non-pharmacological interventions lies in the need to further optimize parameters in the AD context, including the optimal intensity, frequency, and timing of exercise, as well as the optimal dose and inhalation duration for hydrogen.

Nanoparticle-based delivery systems

Prussian blue/PAMAM/Angiopep-2 nanoparticles efficiently penetrate the blood–brain barrier via Angiopep-2-mediated LRP-1 receptor transcytosis and are selectively taken up by microglia. Following internalization, these nanoparticles enhance mitophagy, promote the clearance of damaged mitochondria, restore mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production, and concurrently reduce ROS accumulation [165]. The autophagy inhibitor 3-MA attenuates their protective effects, confirming that mitophagy is the core mechanism underlying their action. The clinical translation of nanoparticle-based delivery systems still faces challenges including the optimization of drug loading capacity, long-term biosafety assessment, and the development of scalable manufacturing processes. Nevertheless, their unique targeted delivery capability provides an important technological platform for overcoming the common obstacles encountered by the aforementioned strategies.

The optimization of individualized protocols for non-pharmacological interventions and the feasibility of clinical translation for nanoparticle-based delivery systems constitute the core challenges facing these approaches. Future research should systematically evaluate the optimal intervention parameters for exercise and hydrogen in AD-specific models, while simultaneously advancing the safety evaluation and process scale-up of nanocarriers. Combining non-pharmacological interventions with pharmacological strategies, or utilizing nanoparticle-based delivery to achieve multi-target synergistic regulation, may represent important directions for the development of AD therapeutics. Conclusion

Recent evidence has reshaped our understanding of Alzheimer ‘s disease (AD), moving beyond the classical proteinopathy-centered view toward a broader framework that incorporates chronic neuroinflammation and immunometabolic dysregulation. Within this evolving landscape, microglia have emerged as central arbiters of disease trajectory, influencing whether the brain mounts adaptive responses or progresses toward pathological amplification. In this context, this review proposes that microglial mitophagy functions as an immunometabolic checkpoint, representing a conceptual node at which mitochondrial quality control, cellular metabolism, and innate immune signaling converge to shape microglial functional states.

Based on the evidence discussed herein, we propose a working model in which this checkpoint operates through three interconnected layers—sensor, switch, and tuning—that together govern mitochondrial quality control and inflammatory output. In the AD pathological milieu, characterized by Aβ accumulation, tau pathology, and genetic risk variants such as TREM2 dysfunction and APOE4, the integrity of this tripartite system becomes progressively compromised, favoring the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, persistent mitochondrial DAMP release, and sustained activation of cGAS–STING and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways. Collectively, these processes may give rise to a self-sustaining inflammatory locking state that perpetuates microglial dysfunction and facilitates reciprocal stress signaling among neighboring neurons, amplifying neuroinflammation at the network level.

Despite the integrative potential of this framework, several important questions remain unresolved. First, the spatiotemporal dynamics of mitophagic dysfunction across distinct disease stages, brain regions, and microglial subpopulations remain poorly understood. Second, the causal hierarchy between mitophagy impairment and inflammatory activation remains incompletely defined. While the evidence reviewed here supports a model in which impaired mitophagy functions as a self-sustaining amplifier of neuroinflammation in established AD, the extent to which it serves as an early driver versus a secondary consequence of initial pathological insults likely varies across disease stages and genetic contexts. Dissecting this temporal heterogeneity will require cell-type-specific in vivo models with precise temporal control of mitophagic flux. Third, translation of these mechanistic insights into clinical strategies faces dual challenges: achieving microglia-selective modulation of autophagy without disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis, and identifying optimal therapeutic windows that accommodate the evolving roles of mitophagy throughout disease progression. Fourth, much of the current understanding of microglial mitophagy is derived from experimental models, and the extent to which the underlying mechanisms are conserved in human AD remains incompletely understood. Bridging this translational gap will be essential for validating and further refining the framework proposed here.

Looking forward, successful therapeutic intervention will likely require a combinatorial rather than unidirectional strategy. Simultaneously restoring mitophagic flux, attenuating downstream inflammatory signaling, and targeting upstream immunometabolic regulation may collectively enable precise recalibration of this immunometabolic checkpoint. Emerging approaches, including nanoparticle-based delivery systems and cell-type-specific gene regulation technologies, may further facilitate spatiotemporally controlled interventions. Ultimately, the checkpoint model proposed here provides a conceptual framework and a potential roadmap for next-generation therapeutic development. By recasting microglial mitophagy from an isolated cellular event into a tunable node embedded within a complex multicellular inflammatory network, this perspective emphasizes dynamic recalibration rather than simplistic enhancement or suppression.
