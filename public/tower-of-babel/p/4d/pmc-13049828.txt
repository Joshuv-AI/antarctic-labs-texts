Association between dietary magnesium intake and diabetic kidney disease among American adults with type 2 diabetes: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xinhua Jia, Aichao Zhao, Xiaolin Chen, Shengyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) has a substantial impact on the quality of life and life expectancy of individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D). Magnesium plays a crucial role in regulating blood glucose levels and insulin sensitivity, yet the association between dietary magnesium intake and DKD remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the association between dietary magnesium intake and DKD among American adults with type 2 diabetes.MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey spanning the years 2005–2018. Multivariable regression models were employed to explore the association between dietary magnesium and DKD. Additionally, restricted cubic spline and threshold effect analysis were conducted. Stratified analyses and sensitivity analyses were performed to verify the robustness of the findings.ResultsThe study included 6,032 participants with T2D, of whom 38.4% (2,316/6,032) were concurrently diagnosed with DKD. Compared to the group with lower dietary magnesium intake (Q1, ≤ 180 mg/day), the adjusted odds ratios for DKD in the higher intake groups Q2 (181.0-250.0 mg/day), Q3 (251.0-341.0 mg/day), and Q4 (≥ 342.0 mg/day) were 0.86 (95% CI: 0.73–1.02, P = 0.086), 0.71 (95% CI: 0.59–0.86, P < 0.001), and 0.70 (95% CI: 0.55–0.90, P = 0.005), respectively. The association between dietary magnesium intake and DKD exhibited a non-linear curve (non-linear, P = 0.029), with an inflection point approximately at 300 mg/day.ConclusionsThis cross-sectional study reveals that dietary magnesium intake was inversely associated with DKD, with an inflection point approximately at 300 mg/day. Further research is necessary to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26754-1.

Background

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a metabolic disorder characterized by chronic hyperglycemia, involving disturbances in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both [1]. Approximately half of individuals with diabetes will progress to diabetic kidney disease (DKD) [2]. DKD stands as the most prevalent cause of renal failure and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) among diabetic patients [3], substantially heightening the risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes [4] and truncating the life expectancy of those with T2D [5, 6]. Current therapeutic strategies for DKD primarily revolve around meticulous control of blood glucose, management of hypertension, and addressing hyperlipidemia [7]. Prior investigations suggest that dietary factors may serve as a potential avenue for treating diabetes or delaying the onset of chronic complications associated with the condition [8, 9]. Consequently, dietary modification emerges as a conceivable approach in preventing the chronic complications of diabetes.

Magnesium is an essential trace element required for various enzymatic activities within the body. Dietary sources of magnesium in the human body include green leafy vegetables, nuts, legumes, and whole grains [10]. Previous research has indicated that magnesium may enhance glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity [11, 12], and an increased intake of dietary magnesium has been associated with a reduced incidence of T2D [13]. Currently, there is a lack of research on the relationship between dietary magnesium intake and DKD. The objective of this study is to investigate the association between dietary magnesium intake and DKD, aiming to contribute insights for the formulation of dietary strategies for individuals with diabetes.

Methods

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database is accessible to the public at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/. NHANES represents a significant initiative led by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), operating under the umbrella of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The NCHS is entrusted with the generation of vital health statistics for the nation. The NHANES database integrates comprehensive interviews and thorough physical examinations. The interview component of NHANES encompasses inquiries related to demographics, socioeconomic status, dietary habits, and health-related factors. The examination component involves meticulous medical, dental, and physiological measurements, complemented by laboratory tests administered by highly skilled medical personnel. Data in the NHANES database are collected biennially, with approximately 5,000 participants each year selected from diverse regions across the United States, ensuring nationally representative samples. Prior to participation, all NHANES participants provide written informed consent, and the study is ethically approved by the NCHS Institutional Review Board [14].

This study is a cross-sectional investigation utilizing NHANES data from 2005 to 2018, incorporating a wide range of variables obtained from questionnaires, physical measurements, and laboratory examinations. We included individuals with T2D aged over 20 years, excluding pregnant individuals and those lacking essential variables.

Data collection

The diagnostic criteria for T2D are based on the standards established by the American Diabetes Association [15] and self-reporting. Diagnosis is confirmed if any of the following criteria are met: (1) fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (2) glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%; (3) plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L 2 h post oral glucose tolerance test; (4) current use of oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin for blood glucose control; (5) self-reported previous diagnosis of diabetes. For individuals identified with T2D, the diagnosis of DKD requires meeting the following criteria: the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) ≥ 30 mg/g and/or the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² [16]. ACR is obtained from the ratio of urinary protein to urinary creatinine, and eGFR is calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration algorithm [17].

The objective of the dietary interview section is to obtain detailed information on dietary intake from NHANES participants. Dietary intake data is used to estimate the types and quantities of foods and beverages (including all types of water) consumed in the 24 h preceding the interview (from midnight to midnight) and to estimate the intake of energy, nutrients, and other food components from these foods and beverages. All NHANES participants are eligible to participate in two 24-hour dietary recall interviews. The first dietary recall interview is conducted in person at the Mobile Examination Center, and the second interview is conducted by phone 3 to 10 days later. Dietary data were assessed using the first 24-hour dietary recall interview from the NHANES dataset. The first-day data was selected for the primary analysis to ensure the largest possible sample size and because it represents the standard methodological approach for numerous NHANES-based studies [18, 19]. A review of medication data confirmed no use of magnesium-containing laxatives in the study population, thus magnesium intake was considered to originate exclusively from diet and supplements.

We evaluated various potential variables based on the literature [20–24], including age, sex, race/ethnicity, family income, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, hypertension, hyperuricemia, caloric consumption, protein consumption, fat consumption, dietary calcium, dietary phosphorus, dietary zinc, dietary copper, family history of diabetes, duration of diabetes, use of glucose-lowering drugs and insulin, blood pressure, FPG, HbA1c, 2-hour plasma glucose, total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), hemoglobin, serum creatinine (sCr), serum uric acid (SUA), urinary creatinine, and urinary albumin. Race/ethnicity was categorized as non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, and other (including Hispanic and other races). Family income [14] was divided into three groups according to the poverty income ratio (PIR): low (PIR ≤ 1.3), medium (PIR > 1.3 to 3.5), and high (PIR > 3.5). Physical activity [25] was quantified by calculating metabolic equivalent (MET) minutes per week (MET-min/week) based on self-reported intensity, duration, and frequency of physical activity for one week, and participants were categorized into three groups: low (< 600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–3000 MET-min/week), and high (≥ 3000 MET-min/week) based on the International Physical Activity Questionnaire scoring criteria. BMI was calculated based on weight and height. Smoking status [14] was classified as never smoked, former smoker, and current smoker. Data on hypertension, family history of diabetes, time of initial diabetes diagnosis, and use of glucose-lowering drugs and insulin were obtained from questionnaire survey data. The duration of diabetes was calculated by subtracting the age at the time of the interview from the age at the initial diagnosis of diabetes. The presence of hyperuricemia [26] was determined based on SUA level (> 420 µmol/L for males or > 360 µmol/L for females). All the above information is freely available on the NHANES website (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/).

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables are expressed as proportions (%), and continuous variables are presented as means (standard deviation, SD) or medians (interquartile range, IQR) depending on the distribution. Group differences were compared using one-way analysis of variance (normal distribution), Kruskal-Wallis test (skewed distribution), and chi-square test (categorical variables). We constructed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) based on existing literature and professional knowledge (Supplementary Figure S1) to derive the minimal sufficient adjustment set (MSAS) for estimating the total effect of dietary magnesium on DKD. Univariable logistic regression analysis was employed to identify variables potentially influencing DKD. In our multivariable logistic regression analysis, we employed two models to determine the odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for the association between dietary magnesium intake and DKD. Model 1 controlled for the MSAS, including age, sex, BMI, family income, physical activity, fat consumption, protein consumption, dietary calcium and phosphorus intake. Model 2, the fully adjusted model, further considered race/ethnicity, smoking status, hyperuricemia, dietary zinc and copper intake, family history of diabetes, duration of diabetes, glucose-lowering drugs, insulin use, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and HbA1c, TC, LDL-C, and hemoglobin levels. Dietary phosphorus intake, identified as a necessary component of the MSAS in our pre-specified DAG, was retained in the final model despite exhibiting moderate multicollinearity [Variance inflation factor (VIF) = 5.175]. This decision was made a priori to minimize confounding and prioritize unbiased estimation of the causal effect of dietary magnesium.

In addition to adjusting for variables in Model 2, we employed restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression to evaluate linear relationships and scrutinize dose-response curves related to the association between dietary magnesium intake and DKD. Simultaneously, smooth two-piece logistic regression models were employed to examine the potential threshold association between dietary magnesium intake and DKD. The inflection point was ascertained through likelihood ratio tests and bootstrap resampling.

Furthermore, potential modifiers influencing the relationship between dietary magnesium intake and DKD were evaluated, encompassing age (20–65 years vs. ≥ 65 years), sex (male vs. female), physical activity (low vs. moderate or high), hypertension (no vs. yes), hyperuricemia (no vs. yes), and HbA1c (< 7.0% vs. ≥ 7.0%). Multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to scrutinize heterogeneity among subgroups, with likelihood ratio tests used to investigate interactions between subgroups and dietary magnesium intake. We opted to utilize the dietary interview data from the first 24-hours, to formally test the impact of day-to-day variation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis on the subset of participants with complete data from both dietary interviews. Additionally, to assess the robustness of our findings, another sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding participants with extreme energy intake, defined as < 500 or > 3500 kcal per day for women and < 800 or > 4000 kcal per day for men [27].

Data analyses were performed using the R Statistical Software (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and the Free Statistics Analysis Platform in Beijing, China. Two-tailed tests were conducted, and a significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data in the covariates were handled using multiple imputation. The missing-at-random (MAR) assumption was adopted for the imputation. The imputation model included all variables that were part of the final analytical model. Five imputed datasets were generated using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method in SAS MI.

Results

Participant characteristics

From a total of 70,190 participants in the NHANES 2005–2018 cycles, we identified 7,298 adults with T2D. After excluding 17 individuals who were pregnant, 791 with missing data for DKD, and 458 with incomplete dietary information, a final analytical cohort of 6,032 participants remained. Within this cohort, 2,316 individuals were diagnosed with DKD. A detailed participant flow diagram is provided in Fig. 1.

Basic characteristics of the study population

Table 1 presents the baseline features of all participants stratified by quartiles of dietary magnesium intake. The mean age of the study population was 61.1 (13.5) years, with 2,892 (47.9%) being female. The average daily dietary magnesium intake for the study population was 275.4 mg. In the group with the higher intake, individuals tended to be younger, predominantly male, with a higher proportion of Mexican American ethnicity, higher family income, and higher levels of physical activity. This group exhibited increased daily intake of calories, protein, calcium, phosphorus, zinc, and copper. Additionally, they had a lower proportion of hypertension and hyperuricemia, reduced use of insulin for blood glucose control, decreased SBP levels, elevated levels of hemoglobin, and better renal function indicators (such as sCr, SUA, ACR, and eGFR levels).

Association between dietary magnesium intake and diabetic kidney disease

To explore the relationship between potential variables and DKD, we conducted univariable logistic regression analysis. The results (Supplementary Table S1) indicated that factors associated with DKD include age; race/ethnicity; family income; physical activity; smoking status; hypertension; hyperuricemia; daily intake of magnesium, calcium, phosphorus, zinc, and copper; duration of diabetes; insulin use; as well as SBP, HbA1c, TC, LDL-C, and hemoglobin levels (all P < 0.05).

When modeled as a continuous variable, each 10 mg/day increment in dietary magnesium intake was associated with a significantly lower odds of DKD, both in the unadjusted (OR 0.98, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99; P < 0.001) and fully adjusted models (OR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98-1.00; P = 0.007) (Table 2). These results suggest that each 10 mg/day increase in dietary magnesium intake is associated with an approximately 1% reduction in the odds of DKD. When dietary magnesium intake was categorized into quartiles, a significant negative association persisted. Compared to the lower dietary magnesium intake group (Q1, ≤ 180.0 mg/day), the adjusted OR values for DKD in Q2 (181.0-250.0 mg/day), Q3 (251.0-341.0 mg/day), and Q4 (≥ 342.0 mg/day) were 0.86 (95% CI: 0.73–1.02, P = 0.086), 0.71 (95% CI: 0.59–0.86, P < 0.001), and 0.70 (95% CI: 0.55–0.90, P = 0.005) (Table 2), respectively.

Sensitivity analyses

A sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S2) restricted to participants with two 24-hour recalls (n = 5,314) yielded consistent results. Using the average magnesium intake from both days, the fully adjusted OR for DKD was 0.99 (95% CI: 0.98-1.00, P = 0.003), virtually unchanged from the primary analysis.

Moderate multicollinearity was observed for dietary phosphorus (VIF = 5.175). As a pre-specified confounder based on our DAG, it was retained in the model to minimize bias. A sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S3) confirmed that its inclusion did not materially alter the effect estimate for magnesium (OR change: 0.94 vs. 0.96), supporting the robustness of our primary finding.

After excluding participants with extreme energy intake, a total of 5,693 participants (Supplementary Table S4) remained, and the association between dietary magnesium intake and DKD remained stable. Compared to the lower dietary magnesium intake group (Q1, ≤ 184.0 mg/day), the adjusted OR values for DKD in Q2 (185.0-250.0 mg/day), Q3 (251.0-335.0 mg/day), and Q4 (≥ 336.0 mg/day) were 0.84 (95% CI: 0.71-1.00, P = 0.052), 0.73 (95% CI: 0.60–0.89, P = 0.002), and 0.68 (95% CI: 0.53–0.88, P = 0.003) (Supplementary Table S5), respectively.

Discussion

In this large, cross-sectional study of American adults with T2D, we identified a significant non-linear association between dietary magnesium intake and the prevalence of DKD. The relationship was characterized by a threshold effect at approximately 300 mg/day, below which magnesium intake was strongly and inversely associated with DKD, while above which no significant association was observed. Stratified analyses and sensitivity analyses both demonstrated the stability of this association.

Research indicates that the incidence of hypomagnesemia in individuals with T2D is tenfold higher than in the general population [28]. Hypomagnesemia is associated with insulin resistance, the latter of which increases the risk of developing T2D or exacerbating glycemic control in existing diabetic patients. Additionally, complications of diabetes may be further exacerbated by hypomagnesemia. Studies have shown a correlation between the reduction of both total magnesium and free magnesium in serum and the elevation of HbA1c levels and complications in diabetes [29]. Furthermore, there exists an association between lower serum magnesium levels and reduced dietary magnesium intake with an increased risk of developing chronic kidney disease and progressing to ESRD [30].

In a systematic review [12] conducted by N. Veronese et al., it was demonstrated that magnesium supplementation can reduce FBG levels in individuals with diabetes. Additionally, it can improve insulin sensitivity indices in diabetes-prone populations, such as homeostatic model assessment-insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). P.N. Brandão-Lima et al. also found, in their study [31], a negative correlation between magnesium intake (β = -0.007, P = 0.015) and HbA1c levels. Furthermore, a randomized controlled trial [32] indicated that after a 3-month supplementation with magnesium (250 mg/day), individuals with T2D showed improvements in HbA1c levels, insulin levels, C-peptide, and HOMA-IR. Consequently, it is concluded that oral magnesium supplementation can reduce insulin resistance and enhance glycemic control indicators in patients with T2D.

Magnesium can improve blood glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity through various pathways. Receptor kinases and autophosphorylation of all protein kinases in the insulin signaling cascade depend on magnesium. Magnesium may play a role in enhancing insulin secretion by pancreatic β-cells [29]. Furthermore, increasing magnesium intake has been associated with beneficial effects on systemic inflammation and gut microbiota. These factors contribute to explaining its favorable effects on improving insulin sensitivity and reducing the risk of T2D [33].

The pathogenesis of DKD involves impaired endothelial nitric oxide (NO) production, largely attributed to endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) dysfunction. As demonstrated by Mima et al., hyperglycemia inhibits the glomerular IRS-1/PI3K/Akt/eNOS axis, a key pathway whose impairment blunts insulin-stimulated NO production and links insulin resistance to diabetic microvascular complications [34, 35]. Magnesium deficiency directly aggravates this pathophysiology by inducing oxidative stress and activating signaling pathways (e.g., reduced Akt phosphorylation, increased MAPKs/NF-κB) that further suppress eNOS and NO release, culminating in endothelial dysfunction [36]. Given that endothelial cells, regulated by NO, are crucial for maintaining vascular homeostasis [37], we propose that higher dietary magnesium intake may preserve NO bioavailability and retard DKD progression via a dual mechanism: enhancing insulin sensitivity to activate the PI3K/Akt/eNOS axis and inhibiting PKCβ to alleviate eNOS suppression. This mechanistic model provides a compelling basis for future research. Our study provides epidemiological substantiation for a mechanistic link between diet, inflammation, and renal protection.

We found that higher dietary magnesium intake, which is the nutritional cornerstone of the Mediterranean diet [38], was associated with a lower risk of DKD. This diet is known to reduce the risk of diabetes and DKD [39, 40] and improve cardiometabolic and renal parameters [41–43], effects attributed in part to its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [44]. The series of studies by Mima et al. delineates a precise anti-inflammatory pathway: the insulin-PI3K/Akt-eNOS axis, for which magnesium is a key co-factor, generates nitric oxide to suppress NF-κB and NADPH oxidase, thereby countering vascular inflammation and insulin resistance [45, 46]. Our observation at the population level suggests that the benefits of the Mediterranean diet may be mediated significantly through magnesium’s role in activating this pathway. Thus, we posit that a magnesium-rich diet helps delay DKD progression by supporting this crucial anti-inflammatory axis, a hypothesis that consolidates mechanistic insights with population-level data [47].

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and can only demonstrate an association between dietary magnesium intake and DKD. Second, dietary assessment relied on a single 24-hour recall, which is susceptible to recall bias and within-person variation. However, our sensitivity analysis using the average of two recalls in a subpopulation yielded consistent results, strengthening confidence in this finding. Third, despite adjustments for a comprehensive set of confounders, residual confounding remains possible. A key point is that we could not adjust for specific cardiorenal-protective medications (e.g., sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists) due to a high proportion of missing data. To address this, we incorporated key clinical indicators (e.g., HbA1c, SBP) into our models. Furthermore, although we used multiple imputation for missing data, the potential for bias due to data not MAR, particularly for variables like LDL-C, cannot be fully excluded. Finally, our model exhibited moderate multicollinearity involving dietary phosphorus (VIF = 5.175). As it was a necessary component of our pre-specified DAG to minimize confounding, we prioritized causal validity, and a sensitivity analysis confirmed that this collinearity did not substantively bias the main association.

Conclusions

In the adult population with T2D in the United States, dietary magnesium intake demonstrated an inverse association with DKD, identifying an inflection point at approximately 300 mg/day which warrants validation in future studies.
