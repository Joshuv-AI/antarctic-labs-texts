Scalp Microbiome Alterations in Androgenetic Alopecia: Patterns and Emerging Mechanistic Insights
Authors: Aditya K. Gupta, Elizabeth M. Teasell, Amanda Liddy, Vasiliki Economopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAndrogenetic alopecia (AGA), the most common cause of hair loss in men and women, involves progressive follicular miniaturization and reduced hair density. Emerging evidence implicates the scalp microbiome as a potential modifier of this process. Preliminary evidence suggests alterations in microbial communities in patients with AGA compared to the general population, with the most robust finding being an enrichment of Cutibacterium acnes, particularly in males. Increases in Malassezia and reductions in Lawsonella and Corynebacterium have also been described, though results are less consistent across studies, while Staphylococcus displays variable patterns. These microbial shifts parallel alterations in scalp sebum composition, such as elevated triglycerides and palmitic acid, that may favor growth of lipophilic microbes. Mechanistic insights from in vitro studies and related inflammatory skin conditions indicate that C. acnes and Malassezia spp. are capable of generating free fatty acids and activating innate immune pathways, such as TLR2‐ and NLRP3‐associated signaling, leading to pro‐inflammatory cytokine production, potentially contributing to follicular stem cell apoptosis and hair follicle miniaturization. Although findings remain heterogenous, emerging evidence suggests microbial–lipid–immune interactions may contribute to AGA pathogenesis, providing a rationale for exploring treatments like ketoconazole or other lipid‐modulating interventions.

Introduction

Androgenetic alopecia (AGA) is one of the most common forms of hair loss in both men and women, and its prevalence increases steadily with age [1, 2]. In men, AGA often begins in adolescence or early adulthood, affecting roughly 30% of middle‐aged men and more than 55% of older men [2, 3]. In women, where it is referred to as female patterned hair loss (FPHL), onset is generally more gradual and tends to appear later in life, with an estimated 55% of women over the age of 70 years affected [4]. Pattern hair loss is characterized by progressive shortening of the anagen phase, with earlier and more frequent transition to catagen and a relative prolongation of telogen phase, ultimately resulting in hair‐follicle miniaturization and a gradual reduction in hair density [5, 6, 7]. These changes are often accompanied by alterations in follicular architecture, including arrector pili muscle atrophy and sebaceous gland hyperplasia, both of which may heighten scalp oiliness and foster a pro‐inflammatory microenvironment [5, 8].

Although AGA is highly prevalent and its association with genetic susceptibility and androgen activity is well established, the condition's underlying pathobiology remains only partially understood [5]. Increasing evidence suggests that features of the scalp and follicular microenvironment, including sebum composition, low‐grade microinflammation, and shifts in resident microorganisms, may also influence the hair miniaturization process, either as contributing factors or secondary changes (Figure 1) [7, 9, 10]. In this context, the scalp microbiome, consisting of diverse commensal and opportunistic microorganisms, has emerged as an area of growing interest as alterations in bacterial and fungal communities may interact with sebum lipids, immune pathways, and barrier function to modulate AGA development and progression.

Overview of the factors contributing to androgenetic alopecia (AGA). Hair follicle miniaturization is influenced by microbiome alterations, sebaceous gland and lipid changes, follicular microinflammation, genetic susceptibility, and androgen/hormonal activity, which interact to drive disease progression. Bidirectional interactions highlight a multifactorial network, in which disruptions in one component may propagate dysregulation across others, contributing to progressive follicular miniaturization and hair loss.

This review examines the microbial taxa most consistently altered in AGA and discusses potential mechanisms linking these fluctuations to disease pathophysiology.

The Scalp Microbiome in Androgenetic Alopecia

A healthy scalp hosts a diverse microbial community, composed primarily of bacterial species such as Cutibacterium (formerly Propionibacterium), Corynebacterium, and Staphylococcus, as well as fungi including Malassezia [11, 12, 13, 14]. Hair follicles, sebaceous glands, and sweat glands create localized microenvironments with varying oxygen levels, promoting the selective colonization of certain microbes [11]. Approximately 25% of the skin's bacterial population resides within hair follicles, highlighting their role as a key microbial reservoir [15]. The relative abundance and diversity of these microorganisms on the scalp and within the hair follicle are variable between individuals and is shaped by intrinsic factors such as genetics, sex, age, and sebum production, as well as extrinsic factors including hygiene practices, cosmetic use, and environmental exposures [16, 17, 18]. In this balanced state, the microbiome supports follicular health, prevents colonization by pathogenic organisms, and maintains a low level of immune activation, creating a microenvironment conducive to normal hair growth and scalp function.

Current evidence describing scalp microbial alterations in AGA compared with healthy individuals remains variable and, in some cases, inconsistent across studies (Table 1). Reported differences in bacterial and fungal composition differ by cohort and study design, likely reflecting substantial methodological heterogeneity and differences in disease severity and treatment status. Within the landscape of AGA, high‐level assessment of community‐wide microbial differences between AGA and healthy scalps, such as alpha and beta diversity, is often not reported or finds no statistically significant differences. However, a limited number of studies have described increased alpha diversity, a measure of microbial richness, in both men and women with AGA, which has been interpreted as reflecting the incorporation of additional, potentially non‐resident scalp taxa [17, 19, 20]. Beta diversity, a measure of microbial community composition between samples, further indicates distinct clustering among AGA samples compared to healthy controls, suggesting global shifts in community structure rather than isolated taxonomic changes [7, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Alterations in microbial composition have been shown to increase with the severity of AGA and may extend beyond the localized areas of hair loss [19, 24].

Studies have examined specific taxa associated with AGA progression. Notably, Cutibacterium acnes is frequently reported as enriched in the scalp and follicle of male patients with AGA compared to those with healthy scalps, suggesting a potential role in microbial shifts observed in the disorder [9, 21, 22, 25]. Women with AGA have shown elevated C. acnes relative abundance in the follicle [18], with decreases on the scalp surface [17, 23], implying that microbial dynamics in the follicles of FPHL may differ from those on the scalp.

Alterations in Staphylococcus species have been observed in AGA, but the direction of change varies across studies, with some studies reporting an increase in relative abundance [21, 23, 25] and others reporting decreases [20, 22]. Reduced S. epidermidis occurrence, in particular, has been associated with a higher C. acne: S. epidermidis ratio in AGA scalps, reflecting a shift in the balance between these key commensal taxa [22].

The relative abundance of Corynebacterium and Lawsonella has been associated with sex‐specific patterns [18, 20]; Lawsonella presence is notably lower in males with AGA compared with healthy controls than in females with FPHL relative to their healthy counterparts [20]. A reduction in Corynebacterium relative abundance has been reported in multiple male and female AGA cohorts compared with healthy controls, [9, 17, 18, 19] though increases in Corynebacterium have also been described in other studies [18, 20].

While most studies have focused on bacteria, fungi may also play a key role in AGA. Among them, Malassezia has been associated with hair shedding [26, 27] and may change more markedly than bacteria with disease progression [19]. Malassezia has been reported at higher occurrence on the scalps of individuals with AGA [9, 17, 25, 28]. Marked increases in Malassezia relative abundance are observed with aging in AGA, whereas relative abundances remain relatively stable in non‐AGA scalps [9]. Elevated Malassezia relative abundance has been detected in hair bulbs of AGA patients compared with controls [9]. However, not all studies support increased presence of Malassezia in AGA [22]. Other fungi, particularly the typically pathogenic genus Alternaria, have also been linked to AGA severity [19].

Environmental Factors Shaping Scalp Microbial Shifts in AGA

In light of reported microbial alterations observed in AGA, it is relevant to consider how changes in the scalp microenvironment may drive these shifts. Several studies have reported higher scalp sebum levels in both males and females with AGA, with sebaceous gland activity generally correlating with disease severity [17, 29]. In addition to changes in total sebum output, differences in sebum lipid composition have been described, including an increased triglyceride‐to‐free fatty acid ratio in individuals with AGA compared with unaffected controls [9, 30]. Elevated levels of certain lipid components, such as palmitic acid, have been reported in the scalp sebum of individuals with AGA [9]. As palmitic acid has been shown in other biological contexts to engage inflammatory signaling pathways, including toll‐like receptors 2 and 4 (TLR2/4), nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB), NOD‐, LRR‐, pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3), and reactive oxygen species (ROS generation) [31], these observations raise the possibility that alterations in sebum composition may contribute to a scalp environment permissive to low‐grade inflammatory signaling. However, the extent to which these lipid changes directly influence inflammation in AGA remains to be established.

These changes in the biochemical milieu of the scalp may selectively favor lipophilic microorganisms [32], such as Cutibacterium and Malassezia, potentially altering the overall microbial balance [9]. Consequently, alterations in sebum may both reflect and contribute to the microbial shifts observed in AGA. Supporting this interpretation, Corynebacterium relative abundance negatively correlates with the triglyceride‐to‐diglyceride ratio, whereas Cutibacterium positively correlates [9], implicating shifts in scalp lipid composition as a potential ecological driver.

Reduced oxygen availability within miniaturizing follicles has been proposed in both in vitro and in vivo models, potentially arising from decreased vascular supply or increased mechanical tension of the scalp [33, 34]. While unproven in AGA, lower oxygen tension is generally understood to favor microbes adapted to lipid‐rich, low‐oxygen niches, such as C. acnes, and may disadvantage taxa that depend on more aerobic conditions.

Microinflammation within the follicular unit, a recognized feature of AGA [35], could also influence microbial ecology. Inflammation can disrupt epithelial tight junctions and impair barrier integrity, leading to altered desquamation patterns [36, 37]. Such barrier changes may modify microbial attachment sites and increase permeability to opportunistic organisms, potentially creating conditions that favor shifts in the local microbiome [38]. However, these processes have not yet been demonstrated in the context of AGA and should be interpreted cautiously.

How Microbial Shifts May Contribute to Hair Loss in AGA

Although direct evidence remains limited, accumulating data suggest that alterations in the scalp microbiome may contribute to disease progression by modulating the follicular microenvironment. Mechanistic insights from acne vulgaris, seborrheic dermatitis, and other inflammatory scalp and skin disorders indicate that microbial immunologic activity can shape the local inflammatory milieu, providing a biologically plausible framework through which similar processes could influence hair loss progression in AGA [39, 40, 41, 42]. Inflammatory infiltrates have long been documented in AGA [43], and early perifollicular inflammation suggests that immune activity may contribute to disease progression [44]. Thus, microbial shifts that promote pro‐inflammatory signaling have the potential to exacerbate an already primed inflammatory environment within the follicular unit.

One plausible mechanism involves AGA‐associated shifts in microbial functional pathways, including upregulated porphyrin‐related pathways and increased fatty acid biosynthesis [18], which may reflect microbial composition and metabolic activity rather than direct pathogenic effects. The increased abundance of lipophilic microbes, such as C. acnes and Malassezia spp., correlates with increased production of lipases that hydrolyze triglycerides in sebum [45, 46, 47], generating free fatty acids, notably palmitic acid. These fatty acids can activate TLR2 and the NLRP3 inflammasome in sebocytes and keratinocytes, resulting in the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines including interleukin‐1β (IL‐1 β), IL‐6, IL‐8, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α) (Figure 2) [31, 48]. This localized inflammation has been shown in experimental and inflammatory scalp models to influence the anagen phase, potentially favoring premature transition to the catagen phase and contributing to hair‐follicle miniaturization [49]. Moreover, AGA‐associated shifts in microbial functional pathways include upregulated porphyrin‐related pathways and increased fatty acid biosynthesis [18], which may reflect microbial composition and metabolic activity rather than direct pathogenic effects, and may be linked to the elevated abundance and metabolic capabilities of Cutibacterium and other lipid‐dependent microbes.

Proposed Mechanisms Linking Microbial Alterations to Androgenetic Alopecia (AGA). Microbial shifts observed in AGA, including increased relative abundance of Cutibacterium acnes and Malassezia spp., promote hydrolysis of sebum triglycerides and production of free fatty acids that activate TLR2 and NLRP3 inflammasome signaling in follicular keratinocytes and sebocytes. Resultant cytokine release (e.g., IL‐1β, IL‐6, IL‐8, and TNF‐α), reactive oxygen species generation, and apoptosis contribute to shortened anagen duration and progressive hair‐follicle miniaturization. These pathways may amplify the underlying perifollicular inflammatory milieu characteristic of AGA.

Beyond lipid‐driven effects, Cutibacterium spp. and Malassezia spp. can also directly activate immune receptors to drive cytokine production. Malassezia spp. triggers NLRP3 inflammasome activation via SYK‐dependent signaling and induces pro‐IL‐1β production in antigen‐presenting cells, but not keratinocytes [50, 51]. Similarly, C. acnes promotes IL‐1β, IL‐6, and IL‐8 secretion through TLR‐2 and TLR‐4 activation in keratinocytes [52] and induces the expression of NLRP3 [53, 54, 55]. In parallel, C. acnes produces porphyrins which activate the innate immune system (NLRP3) and promote further IL‐1β release (Figure 2) [56, 57]. One study reporting higher relative abundances of C. acnes within the hair follicle of patients with AGA also identified increased transcriptomic expression of genes involved in microbial sensing and immune responses, including TLR4, DEFB1, interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1), the monocyte marker CD14, and the Langerhans cell markers CD1a/CD207, in miniaturized compared with non‐miniaturized hair follicles [7]. Evidence suggests that C. acnes‐induced inflammation may critically rest upon NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL‐1β [51]. Elevated expression of IL‐1 has been associated with inducing catagen phase onset, suggesting that microbial induction of IL‐1β via TLR2/4 or NLRP3 pathways may contribute to early anagen‐to‐catagen transition in AGA [58]. Additionally, pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α can generate ROS [59] and induce apoptosis of the hair‐follicle stem cells and hair bulb keratinocytes, providing a plausible link between pro‐inflammatory cytokines and follicular regression [60, 61]. However, much of this evidence derives from in vitro studies or non‐AGA inflammatory skin conditions, and well‐controlled in vivo studies in AGA are still needed to establish causality and clinical relevance.

Collectively, these microbial activities may contribute to the perifollicular inflammatory microenvironment characteristic of AGA, acting as modulators rather than primary drivers within a multifactorial disease process, creating a milieu in which hair‐follicle stem cells and keratinocytes are increasingly susceptible to apoptosis. As apoptosis within miniaturized follicles correlates with local inflammatory infiltrates in AGA, sustained microinflammation may accelerate anagen shortening, promote premature catagen transition, and reinforce progressive hair follicle damage [62]. Together, these observations suggest that microbially driven inflammatory signals may act as reinforcing contributors within the broader and androgen‐driven pathophysiologic network of AGA, rather than isolated or causal determinants. Importantly, most scalp microbiome studies in AGA are cross‐sectional in design and are subject to confounding by coexisting scalp conditions, sampling methodology, and hair or scalp treatments, which limits causal inference.

Therapeutic Modulation of the Scalp Microbiome

In AGA, the interaction between microbes, lipid metabolism, and inflammation may provide a conceptual framework for understanding the potential relevance of certain therapeutic approaches, rather than established treatment strategies. Much of this rationale is informed by mechanistic insights from acne vulgaris [63] and seborrheic dermatitis/dandruff [64], where C. acnes and Malassezia have been more extensively investigated. While these conditions differ fundamentally from AGA, they offer a useful starting point for considering how microbial and lipid‐associated pathways might influence scalp inflammation in androgen‐sensitive follicles.

Given this background, analogous strategies, such as modulation of sebum production, microbial burden, and microbial metabolic activity, could be explored in the context of AGA, although direct evidence that supports their efficacy for hair loss remains limited. Strategies including strain‐targeted approaches, metabolic inhibition, or alteration of scalp lipid availability have not yet been systematically evaluated in AGA and should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than therapeutic recommendations.

Medications such as clascoterone, an androgen receptor inhibitor [65] which has recently been approved for treatment of acne at 1% and is now in clinical trials for AGA at higher concentrations (5% and 7.5%) [66, 67], may help regulate sebum levels via reduced sebaceous gland activity and may also exert anti‐inflammatory effects (Figure 3) [65].

Potential Therapeutic Options for Androgenetic Alopecia (AGA) and Their Proposed Mechanisms.

Approaches that reduce microinflammation or block microbial activation of TLR2 and NLRP3 may mitigate follicular stress. Among FDA‐approved agents for AGA, minoxidil may exert ancillary anti‐inflammatory effects [68, 69]. Molecules shown to suppress C. acnes‐induced inflammation in other skin diseases may have relevance in AGA [54, 55, 70]. These agents appear to act by inhibiting peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) activation, suppressing intracellular ROS production and NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, and reducing caspase‐1 activation and IL‐1β secretion [54, 71]. Such pathways may represent complementary therapeutic targets beyond androgen suppression, offering opportunities to address the inflammatory and microbial components of AGA.

Ketoconazole has multiple mechanisms of action that may explain why it may benefit in AGA: antifungal activity, anti‐inflammatory effects, and reductions in sebum production, potentially through anti‐androgenic effects [27]. Ketoconazole also inhibits C. acnes growth and lipase activity in a dose‐dependent manner, which helps reduce production of free fatty acids and thus inflammation [70]. Investigating the microbial and lipidomic response to ketoconazole in AGA may clarify whether microbiome normalization accompanies clinical improvement.

Future Research Perspectives

An important next step is to determine whether the microbial changes observed in AGA are truly implicated in disease pathology or are merely incidental consequences of altered scalp physiology. Addressing this question will require methodologically rigorous and standardized study designs that reduce the substantial heterogeneity present in existing studies. Currently, interpreting microbiome findings in AGA is complicated by the considerable biological and environmental variability in scalp and hair follicle microbial compositions, making distinguishing changes that truly reflect disease mechanisms from those representing normal variations difficult.

Future studies should prioritize a standardized sampling protocol, including consistent scalp sites, depth of sampling, and timing relative to hair washing or treatment use. Reliance on scalp surface swabs, [72] which dominate current literature, may preferentially capture transient microbes rather than those residing in the follicular niche. Targeted sampling approaches, such as cyanoacrylate follicular biopsies [7], may better capture biologically meaningful microbial dynamics, particularly those linked to sebaceous activity and inflammation [18]. Additionally, rigorous control of confounding factors will also be essential, such as including detailed reporting of product use and treatment status to improve reproducibility and cross‐study comparability.

To move beyond the association, longitudinal study designs will be critical. Repeated sampling of the same individuals over time, ideally across different stages of disease progression or in response to therapeutic intervention, would allow discrimination between stable, disease‐associated microbial features and transient fluctuations driven by environmental or behavioral factors. Such designs may also help clarify whether microbial shifts precede, accompany, or follow changes in hair density, follicle caliber, or inflammatory activity.

The integration of multi‐omics strategies, such as transcriptomics, metabolomics, lipidomics, and profiling of secondary metabolites, represents another key priority that may help clarify whether microbial shifts contribute to inflammatory signaling, lipid metabolism, or other pathways relevant to follicular miniaturization [11]. Correlating microbial features with local cytokine expression, lipid species, or oxidative stress markers within the follicular niche may provide more direct evidence of biological relevance than taxonomic associations alone.

Beyond species‐level composition, strain‐level variation represents another critical yet largely unexplored dimension. Strains of the same species differ markedly in metabolic capacity, inflammatory potential, virulence factors, and interactions with host lipids [57, 73]. Loss of clonal diversity, particularly within C. acnes, has been implicated in other skin disorders [74], raising the possibility that AGA could involve overrepresentation of certain strains or a reduction in strain‐level heterogeneity. To our knowledge, strain‐level characterization of scalp or hair follicle microbes in AGA has not been performed, representing an important gap in our understanding of how microbial heterogeneity may contribute to disease‐specific inflammation or metabolic shifts.

Together, these considerations underscore the need for methodologically rigorous, functionally oriented, and anatomically precise microbiome investigations. Such approaches will be essential for determining not only whether microbial alterations are biologically meaningful contributors to disease‐associated inflammation or metabolism and whether they may ultimately inform biomarker development or adjunctive therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

Given the emerging evidence base, indications of microbial alterations in AGA should be interpreted cautiously; nonetheless, these preliminary findings raise the hypothesis that microbial changes could influence disease development and progression through pro‐inflammatory mechanisms. Altered bacterial and fungal communities, particularly enrichment of C. acnes and Malassezia spp., correspond with sebum compositional changes and activation of TLR2‐ and NLRP3‐mediated pathways that promote microinflammation and follicular stress. These findings expand the current understanding of AGA beyond androgen‐driven mechanisms, emphasizing the potential importance of microbial–lipid–immune interactions. Translationally, targeting these pathways through modulation of sebum composition, inhibition of microbial‐induced inflammatory signaling, or restoration of microbial balance may offer new adjunctive treatment strategies. Future clinical and mechanistic studies will be critical to determine whether the link between microbial shifts and inflammation is clinically supported and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of microbiome‐based interventions in AGA.
