Artificial intelligence prognostication of liver disease using imaging
Authors: Manil D Chouhan, Kate McLean, James A Thomas, Jason Dowling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prognostic tools in patients with chronic liver disease (CLD) have the potential to improve clinical outcomes and reduce health care costs. Imaging studies of CLD patients analysed using artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms for segmentation, detection and classification tasks have the potential to inform and improve prognostic models for liver-related outcomes. In this narrative review, we provide an overview of the strengths, weaknesses and approaches to inclusion of AI in prognostic models that use ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). We then use a prognostic endpoint-based approach to examine AI-based US, CT and MRI prognostic models in chronic liver disease (CLD). We highlight how AI has been applied to extract imaging features or build predictive models directly, and assess the limitations that currently hinder clinical translation. We also outline key challenges specific to prognostication in CLD and propose directions for future research.

Background

The global burden of chronic liver disease (CLD) continues to increase, accounting for approximately 4% of all deaths annually worldwide.1 Prolonged liver injury from any aetiology can result in chronic inflammation and progressive fibrosis eventually leading to the architectural disturbance that characterises cirrhosis. This late stage of CLD can be prevented by early recognition and management of its cause(s). The underlying process, usually occurring over years to decades, is often asymptomatic and therefore undetected. The initial phase of cirrhosis, termed compensated, is also usually clinically silent. The insidious development of portal hypertension follows cumulative liver damage with a rapid escalation in mortality associated with the complications that herald the clinically abrupt transition from compensated to acutely decompensated (AD) advanced CLD. These complications reflect advanced portal hypertension and liver cell damage manifesting as ascites, variceal haemorrhage, jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy, sepsis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which in combination with liver-related death are collectively referred to as liver-related outcomes (LROs). Importantly, the potential reversibility of the underlying CLD reduces with advancing damage.2 Although the pattern and aetiology of liver disease varies around the world, the disproportionate impact on young and working-age individuals regardless of aetiology is a significant global driver of rising liver disease-related healthcare costs3. These include the direct costs of supportive secondary and tertiary hospital services but also the sizeable indirect costs of income and productivity loss, as measured by disability-adjusted life years1,4,5.

The use of accurate prognostic tools to identify patients at risk of developing LROs at earlier stages of disease, could enable preventative strategies, highlight those likely to be benefit from closer clinical surveillance and inform clinical decision making in acutely hospitalised patients, thereby improving clinical outcomes and reducing health care costs6,7.

Prognostic models in chronic liver disease

Modern hepatological practice has an established tradition in the use of prognostic models. The Child-Turcotte-Pugh (CTP) score, Model for End-stage Liver Disease (MELD), and variations of these are well embedded into existing clinical algorithms, albeit with recognised limitations in the prediction of long-term clinical outcomes8. Invasive parameters such as the hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) have well-validated prognostic value in cirrhotic patients but require technical expertise available only in selected specialist centres. Non-invasive tests (NITs) are therefore appealing because of greater patient acceptability, lower cost and potential for more widespread use in stable community-based patients. The reliable and cost-effective prognostication of asymptomatic advanced liver disease in primary-care before the development of LROs is a huge unmet need. The refinement of accessible and accurate NITs offers potential for earlier prognostication and therefore prophylaxis at the population level9,10. Recent guidelines have incorporated composite serum markers [eg, the enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) test, FIB-4 index, aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index (APRI)], clinical parameters with liver and splenic stiffness for the diagnosis of liver fibrosis and portal hypertension. The prognostic value of these measures however remains unclear11,12.

Imaging studies are routinely used NITs in patients with stable and decompensated chronic liver disease. Ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrate well documented features including changes in hepatic lobar/segmental volume, liver surface nodularity, parenchymal coarsening, splenic enlargement, formation of portosystemic collaterals and development of ascites13–16, to name a few of the features that progress with the severity of underlying liver disease, and therefore could inform prognostic models.

Artificial intelligence and prognostic modelling

Artificial intelligence (AI) methods are increasingly being applied to prognostic model development in medicine, including liver disease, due to their potential to identify complex patterns and improve predictive accuracy beyond traditional statistical approaches17. AI encompasses a range of computational techniques, including machine learning (ML) and its subset, deep learning (DL). Traditional ML approaches typically rely on hand-crafted features derived from curated imaging datasets and employ supervised learning with labelled data. Deep learning methods, which form a subset of ML, differ in that they automatically learn hierarchical feature representations directly from raw data, reducing the need for manual feature engineering. While DL methods can be trained using supervised, unsupervised, or self-supervised learning paradigms, supervised learning remains the most commonly used approach in current medical imaging applications. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), currently the most widely used DL architecture in medical imaging, are particularly suited to processing image-based inputs18.

AI algorithms lend themselves to image analysis tasks suitable for integration into prognostic modelling approaches (Figure 1). These include: (1) Segmentation, where AI driven contouring of selected organs and structures (eg, liver, spleen, blood vessels, body compositional volumes, etc) can extract volume/dimension measurements for prognostic model inputs; (2) Detection, where AI algorithms detect adverse imaging features (eg, ascites, portal venous thrombus, etc), for prognostic model inclusion; (3) Classification, where AI image analysis is directly linked to prognostic outcomes. In these models, AI can: (i) build a prognostic model using imaging data combined with other clinical or non-invasive test (NIT) features, or (ii) analyse imaging data alone, with outputs integrated into a broader prognostic model using traditional statistical methods. Classification algorithms include prognostic models reliant on radiomics (quantification of textural information from images)19 and other quantitative imaging parameters including measures of organ stiffness or MR properties (eg, organ T1 or ADC values)17,18, 20. In practice a single prognostic model has the potential to include AI algorithms that incorporate a variety of tasks (eg, AI-based segmentation for organ volumetry, detection of adverse imaging features and use of imaging textural features for radiomics-based approaches).

AI algorithms can handle raw imaging data to complete tasks that include (i) segmentation, (ii) detection of imaging features and/or (iii) classification whereby imaging features are linked directly with prognostic outcomes. The output from these algorithms can either be combined with clinical parameters/other NITs or passed straight into the model building process which can take place using traditional statistical or AI-based methods. The final prognostic model could be presented in a number of forms to the clinical end-user.

In this review, we cover imaging-inclusive prognostic (not diagnostic) AI models published before the July 25, 2025. PubMed/MEDLINE databases were queried for studies linked directly with future liver-related outcomes, rather than those designed to diagnose fibrosis, portal hypertension or clinical features such as varices that may have prognostic implications (formal search strings and inclusion/exclusion criteria can be found in supplement 1). Initially screened-in studies which on secondary review met exclusion criteria were removed by consensus (investigators XX and XX). We aim to provide a modality and prognostic endpoint-based overview of existing imaging-inclusive AI models for prognostication of CLD, discuss the challenges that need to be overcome to make these models usable in routine clinical practice, before concluding with a discussion of future directions.

Ultrasound

US can be performed in a wide range of healthcare environments from primary through to intensive care settings (Table 1). It is an established NIT in hepatological clinical management pathways11,12, so extraction of additional prognostic information from standard-of-care US imaging would come at reduced cost. Collection of large volume datasets required to develop AI prognostic models is therefore much more feasible than for other imaging modalities17.

Computed tomography

As the workhorse imaging modality for acutely unwell patients, CT enables opportunistic collection of imaging data in the acute setting at no additional cost, at a potentially clinically meaningful time point for prognostication. CT data acquired for non-liver related indications with incidental upper abdominal coverage also provides opportunistic imaging data at no additional cost51. Consequently, larger volume datasets are also more available than with MRI, making CT a better suited modality for AI prognostic model development17 (Table 1).

The main application of AI in CT prognostic studies has been the use of automated DL/CNN-based segmentation tools for extraction of liver and splenic volumes (11 out of 17 studies, 65%), body compositional data (9 out of 17 studies, 53%) and segmented organ-specific/body compartment imaging features for radiomics studies (7 out of 17 studies, 41%). Less than half of the studies reviewed utilised AI for classification (7 out of 17 studies, 41%), mainly opting for ML classification methods, usually in the setting of radiomics studies (4 out of 7 studies, 57%). The remaining studies opted for traditional multivariate regression approaches to assimilate models based on imaging, clinical and combined imaging and clinical parameters. All studies included imaging and clinical parameters in their final proposed model (Table 2).

Magnetic resonance imaging

A major attraction of MRI is the wealth of data each study can provide52. Acquisition of more complex MR data however, extends scanning time and complicates comparison of data from different scanners/centres. These limitations have limited implementation of MRI as a NIT in existing clinical pathways11 (Table 1).

Despite these challenges, AI prognostication of chronic liver disease with MRI holds tremendous potential. Of the three true prognostic MRI studies identified, two studies used DL/CNN-based segmentation and classification, with one study using a DL/CNN-based model for both segmentation and monitoring tasks. Only one study developed and validated a purely imaging-based model, without inclusion of clinical parameters (Table 2).

Prognostic endpoints in CLD

Prediction of death/transplant free survival

US SWE ROI-based data in acutely decompensated predominantly chronic hepatitis B (HBV) aCLD patients was predictive of 1 year mortality (C-index of 0.808), exceeding the performance of prognostic scores such as the chronic liver failure consortium acute decompensation (CLIF-C AD) score (C-index 0.713), although the model was developed using a small sample size (n = 288), with data from a single centre and using a single imaging device22. US hepatic parenchymal echogenicity, contour nodularity, paraumbilical vein recanalization and spleen and liver length in a multi-centre study of patients with biopsy-proven MASLD (n = 457) was predictive of long-term death/transplant free survival (within 14 years; AUROC 0.79; sensitivity 75%, specificity 80%)25.

CT body compositional parameters in compensated and decompensated aCLD patients such as psoas muscle index (multivariate HR 0.86, P = 0.005)26, total muscle, VAT, SAT, bone mineral density parameters (C-index 0.71)29 and subcutaneous adipose tissue index in patients with compensated chronic HBV CLD (HR 0.49, P < 0.001)35 have been associated with long-term (>5 year) mortality/transplant free survival in models developed using larger sample sizes (n > 5000), tested predominantly in stable cirrhotics. CT body compositional parameters have also been combined with CT textural/radiomics features to predict 10 year mortality, in compensated and decompensated predominantly large cohort of MASLD patients (n = 4614, C-index 0.71)34 and death/transplant free survival within 4 years in compensated PSC patients (AUC 0.97), albeit with a small sample size (n = 80)39.

CT volumetry of the liver and spleen has been associated with long-term mortality in single-centre study on compensated HBV aCLD patients (within 10 years; HR 0.8, P < 0.001)28, and when combined with textural/radiomics parameters in a smaller scale but multicentre study, predictive of short-term (30 day) mortality in acutely decompensated aCLD patients (AUROC 0.80, sensitivity 80%, specificity 50%)32.

Finally, MRI-based 1-year change in liver: spleen volumetric ratio using a separately developed segmentation model53 was found to be associated with mortality/transplant free survival for up to 15 years in compensated aCLD patients (n = 186, sHR 0.46, P = 0.026) and decompensated aCLD patients (sHR 0.61, P = 0.026). Baseline liver: spleen post-gadoxetic acid enhancement was also associated with decompensated aCLD mortality/transplant free survival (n = 62, sHR 0.67, P = 0.027)43 (Table 3).

Prediction of decompensation/gastro-intestinal bleed

US spleen area was associated with the development of AD over a 3-year period in a small cohort of compensated MASLD patients with varices (n = 135) both with (HR 1.073, P = 0.02) and without (HR 0.890, P = 0.001) splenomegaly23. In a prospective but single-centre study in patients with aCLD and high-risk varices without previous non-selective beta-blocker treatment, baseline and 3-month follow-up liver and splenic SWE, in combination with heart rate was predictive of acute GI bleed or endoscopic variceal response at 1 year (n = 153, AUROC 0.91, sensitivity 94%, specificity 97%)24. In a multicentre study of patients with biopsy-proven MASLD followed-up for upto 14 years, US parenchymal echogenicity, contour nodularity, paraumbilical vein recanalization and spleen and liver length were also predictive of AD (n = 457, AUROC 0.90, sensitivity 88%, specificity 78%)25.

CT body compositional parameters in combination with radiomics/textural parameters were predictive of AD within 10 years in large cohort of compensated and decompensated predominantly MASLD patients (n = 4614, C-index 0.80)34, within 4 years in a small cohort of compensated PSC patients (n = 80, AUC 0.97)39 and within 2.5 years in a small cohort of compensated aCLD patients (any aetiology n = 243, AUC 0.729)42. CT body compositional parameters and spleen volumetry were associated with the development of AD within 10 years in a larger cohort single-centre study in patients with compensated chronic HBV CLD (n = 2169, visceral adipose tissue index HR 0.57, P < 0.005; and spleen volume HR 2.34, P < 0.001)35.

CT spleen volumetry using a separately developed segmentation model50 was also predictive of AD within 5 years in a multicentre cohort of compensated viral hepatitis aCLD (n = 689, AUC 0.91, C-index 0.87)30, in combination with platelet count (sensitivity 88%, specificity 69%)27, in combination with liver volume (AD within 10 years HR 0.71, P < 0.001)28, and in the short-term (3 months) for compensated aCLD patients (any aetiology) with oesophageal varices (AUC 0.818, sensitivity 65.2%, specificity 72.7%).

MRI-based liver: spleen volumetric ratio (sHR 0.71, P = 0.01), liver: spleen post-gadoxetic enhancement (sHR 0.81, P = 0.034) and change in 1 year liver: spleen volumetric ratio (sHR 0.54, P = 0.002) were associated with the development of AD within 15 years in small cohorts of compensated (n = 186) and decompensated aCLD patients (n = 62; any aetiology)43. MRI-based post-contrast delayed phase liver textural features in large duct PSC patients were (excluding clinical parameters) predictive of AD within 1 year (AUROC 0.84, accuracy 70%), in a small (n = 169) but multicentre internationally validated model (Table 4).

Prediction of post-treatment outcomes and other prognostic endpoints

CT L3 level body compositional parameters were predictive of post-TIPS 90-day mortality (n = 122, AUC 0.84)41 and hepatic encephalopathy within 1 year (n = 130, AUC 0.83, sensitivity 60%, specificity 75%)31. New CT-diagnosed sarcopaenia after liver transplant was associated in a small sample retrospective study (n = 107) with 2-year post-transplant survival (HR 3.34, P = 0.04) and post-transplant diabetes (HR 4.93, P = 0.02)33. CT tumour and liver remnant textural/radiomics following HCC resection were predictive of long-term recurrence in a small cohort single centre study (n = 214, 9.7 years; AUC 0.79, precision 82%, accuracy 72%)43. MRI post-contrast delayed-phase liver volume and textural features were predictive of the development of chronic DILI within 6 months of an acute episode (AUROC 0.88), albeit in a small sample-size single centre retrospective study (n = 168), raising the possibility of over-fitting45.

Finally, the development of diabetes in a large cohort but single centre study of compensated chronic HBV CLD patients was associated with CT spleen volume (n = 2169, HR 1.35, P = 0.004) and CT visceral adipose tissue index (HR 1.34, P = 0.001)35 (Table 5).

Challenges

Imaging-inclusive AI prognostication models in CLD face common challenges, broadly stratified into those pertaining to (i) the generation of AI models themselves, (ii) the generation of prognostic models and (iii) studies in CLD patients (Figure 2).

AI prognostication of liver disease using imaging faces a number of challenges that can be broadly split into those pertaining to (i) AI modelling itself, (ii) the prognostic modelling process and finally (iii) difficulties that arise from the development of prognostic models in patients with CLD.

AI modelling

Robust DL/CNN model generation requires large volumes of primary data to be credible and generalisable for clinical implementation18. Most existing models have been developed using relatively small datasets (only four of the reviewed studies had sample sizes exceeding 1000 subjects). More recent developments such as transfer learning, self-supervised learning and data augmentation have reduced this requirement. Data augmentation (eg, image flipping, rotation, or scaling) to artificially expand the training data, and cross-validation or bootstrap sampling can improve robustness and try to reduce the risk of overfitting during model training. Medical imaging AI also benefits from strong inductive biases (such as systematic organ position from cross-sectional imaging) that arise from preset domain knowledge (such as modality specific imaging contrast) and task-specific architectures (such as splenic segmentation in the setting of established liver disease). These combine to allow model development on smaller datasets, but larger and more diverse datasets remain important for generalisability particularly in prognostic tasks with high class imbalance (ie, low outcome rate in relation to the sample size, of particular relevance to CLD aetiologies such as with MASLD).

A common approach to mitigate this issue has been to pretrain the CNN using a large volume open-access non-medical imaging data set such as ImageNet. CNNs such as ResNet or InceptionV3 are examples of such off-the-shelf CNNs that have been fine-tuned with a significantly smaller medical imaging datasets in some of the reviewed studies, to generate the final model18,54. The foundational use of non-medical imaging data to develop these CNNs however does raise questions about overall model integrity. More recently medical imaging datasets such as RadImageNet have been developed55, but none of the reviewed studies have utilised this source.

As a general rule, prognostic model sample size should be informed by the number of variables being evaluated, with recommendations for at least ten prognostic endpoint events/non-endpoint events (whichever is the smaller in the overall sample) per variable being evaluated during model development56. Radiomics studies in particular highlight this challenge, as the inherent methodology requires the evaluation of hundreds–if not thousands–of variables–to define the final model57. Despite published transparent reporting of multivariable prediction models for individual prognosis/diagnosis in AI (TRIPOD+AI) guidelines58, none of the reviewed studies have presented sample size calculations, which remains a broader issue for AI in healthcare as a whole59.

A further challenge is how AI models are reported. The so-called “black box” phenomenon (where models demonstrate strong statistical performance but offer little insight into their internal decision-making) remains a significant barrier to clinical adoption. This is compounded by inconsistent reporting standards, with many studies providing limited and inconsistent details on model architecture, training data and validation process, making independent replication and validation difficult. Steps to improve model explainability have been made60,61, but none of these tools have been used in any of the studies reviewed. The Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM) reporting standards introduced in 2020 and updated in 202462, have helped improve overall study reporting standards, and while many studies reviewed have used either nomograms, listed a final model, or made the CNN developed available online, too many studies simply describe their final CNN performance without giving other researchers the opportunity to test, validate or falsify their findings independently. Finally, variations in how final model performance is reported remains a challenge. This review has highlighted the difficulty in assessing comparative performance of AI models developed from different populations, albeit designed to perform similar tasks.

Prognostic modelling

Many of the reported AI models in this study have been developed/validated within a single centre and/or worst still, using the same imaging device/scanner. Most of the prognostic studies reviewed have also lacked a proper control group of either non-CLD patients or normal volunteers. Most of the studies reviewed are retrospective; few include well-planned mixed retrospective and prospective designs. A priori definition of the prognostic time frame is too often dictated by data availability (particularly for retrospective studies) rather than clinical need. Of note, many excluded studies were diagnostic (rather than true prognostic studies). High quality true prognostic studies where the association between NITs and LROs are needed.

While most of the reviewed prognostic models were generated with imaging and clinical parameters, a third (8 of 24 studies reviewed) failed to use an established non-imaging prognostic model output as a foundational input for the development of an imaging-inclusive model to predict the same clinical endpoint. This represents a missed opportunity–established non-imaging prognostic models including CTP scores, MELD and CLIF scores will have widespread multi-institutional validation, clinical acceptance and incorporation into existing clinical care pathways17. Addition of imaging data to these models to demonstrate measurable incremental prognostic performance improvement would improve clinician acceptability and would be more amenable to independent external validation. Finally, none of the models reviewed have received formal medical device regulatory approval (eg, through the US Food and Drug Administration, UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Authority or Australian Therapeutics and Goods Administration), which would be essential for widespread translation into day-to-day clinical practice.

Prognostication in patients with CLD

The population used to establish any proposed imaging-inclusive model is significant source of bias, as there is substantial geographical variation in the overall burden of CLD aetiology3. Moreover, issues of accessibility, patient acceptability and clinical feasibility of a given imaging modality for a particular patient cohort will vary with healthcare context, setting and geography. The successful development and validation of a prognostic model in a specific CLD cohort with a given imaging modality (when reported properly), should be amenable to independent external validation at other centres in other patient cohorts by different researchers.

Despite globally rising prevalence, challenges with sample size loom large in CLD studies. We have reviewed a few multicentre studies, but appropriately powered international multicentre externally validated studies will be essential to the development of clinically translatable models.

Finally, imaging-inclusive DL/CNN models need to be accessible for independent external validation. In the academic setting, model source code publication in open-access repositories in combination with anonymised source data publication are essential. In the clinical setting, web-based tools are ideal to ensure early-adopter access. Ultimately, international consortia supported by specialist gastroenterological/hepatology societies will be required to provide the momentum for implementation into existing clinical pathways63.

Future directions

An increase in the number and quality of well-designed true prognostic studies linking imaging and non-imaging NITs with meaningful liver-related outcomes represents an important first step to developing this area. Standardised reporting of AI model performance, with use of tools to improve model explainability61 will improve transparency and overall end-user confidence. Uncertainty quantification, whereby AI models express a metric of overall confidence alongside each final result would also improve end-user trust, allowing clinicians to re-evaluate cases where the model does not have enough information to derive a valid result64. Improved access to the proposed AI models will also pave the way for much needed independent external validation on the road toward clinical translation. Efforts such as the ongoing EU Horizon-funded ARTEMIs project, which aims to integrate prognostic AI models in clinical decision support systems holds promise in this area65. Additionally, longitudinal AI image analysis across two or more serial studies, would seem an intuitive means of improving prognostic performance, but has to date had limited exploration (only 1 of the models reviewed24).

AI prognostication of CLD with imaging also stands to benefit from some of the newer approaches to model development. Multimodal foundation models, which leverage diverse data including radiology reports, clinical notes, results from other NITs with self-supervised learning, has the potential to yield more comprehensive models through holistic use of data66. Alternative DL architectures including the use of transformers (used for the prediction of cardiovascular events in MASLD patients67), recurrent neural networks (RNNs, of particular relevance to models utilising serial imaging data)68, generative adversarial networks (GANs)69 and hybrids of these approaches could represent alternative approaches to CNNs that have yet to be applied to CLD prognostication.

Federated learning (FL) enables distributed AI model training without partner-centres needing to transfer patient data between institutions. In this approach, each centre trains a local model on its own data, and only model parameters or updates are shared with a central server for aggregation70. This allows the CNN to benefit from much needed large data volumes that are difficult to garner from CLD patients even at the largest specialist centres, while avoiding some of the complexities surrounding data governance that can arise from multi-institutional collaborations.

AI-based prognostic model development could also be aimed at predicting events other than traditional liver-related outcomes, particularly for more indolent forms of CLD such as MASLD (eg, fibrosis progression or development of steatohepatitis). Large-scale population-based studies for example, have demonstrated benefit of liver imaging in the prediction of cardiovascular disease events, which have wider public health implications beyond liver disease65,67. Diseases are such as MASLD have also been linked with increased extrahepatic cancer rates, and hepatic and opportunistic extrahepatic imaging here too also has the potential to inform better surveillance strategies in this cohort71.

In summary, imaging-inclusive AI prognostication of CLD is a rapidly growing area of research that remains in its relative infancy. Imaging has the potential to address shortcomings of existing prognostic models and deliver much needed improvements in overall risk stratification. Imaging can be incorporated into prognostic models using AI algorithms for segmentation, detection and classification, for the prediction of short and long-term LROs. This review has summarised the existing state-of-the-art but credible, generalisable prognostic models are still lacking. These will need to arise from carefully designed studies that are able to develop and validate their AI models at scale and within multiple centres.
