The value proposition of wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet surgery in the upper extremity
Authors: Hilary Campbell, Omri Ayalon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet (WALANT) surgery offers a unique clinical and economic approach to delivering high-quality upper extremity care.Purpose:This article explores the evoluation, techniques, clinical efficancy, financial advantages, and future directions of WALANT as it establishes is growing role in value-based orthopedic care. In doing so, it outlines the value proposition of WALANT for patients, providers, and the hospital system.Methods:The authors conducted a comprehensive PubMed search to identify studies evaluating the clinical and economic outcomes of WALANT surgery in upper extremity procedures. Eligible studies included prospective and retrospective research, as well as case studies and case series, comparing WALANT with traditional anesthesia. The studies examined focused on metrics such as functional outcomes, patient satisfaction, and cost savings for the patient and the hospital system.Results:Current literature shows that hand and upper extremity surgery performed under WALANT yields similar functional outcomes and even demonstrates equal or higher patient satisfaction compared with traditional anesthesia. Regarding cost savings, WALANT's minimalist approach to procedures can lead to significant waste reduction and notable financial benefits for the patient, the provider, the hospital, and the wider healthcare system.Conclusions:WALANT surgery offers a compelling value proposition for the patient, the provider, and the healthcare system at large. Through efficient use of resources focused on minimizing waste and optimizing patient experience and outcomes, WALANT offers an opportunity for value-based surgical care. Further research into more sophisticated clinical uses and large-scale cost-savings analyses is necessary for WALANT procedures to be further validated and more broadly adopted.Level of Evidence:Level V.

Introduction

Wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet (WALANT) upper extremity surgery, as the name suggests, is performed with the patient wide awake without sedation. This allows the physician and patient to interact during the surgery, including asking the patient to demonstrate digital motion or describe their sensibility, and review rehabilitation protocols. For pain control, local anesthesia is provided using site-specific extravascular lidocaine, often with epinephrine, and no tourniquet is used. Without a tourniquet, there is an even lower need for analgesics and anxiolytics. With this unique setup, WALANT surgery presents a compelling opportunity for improved value-based care in upper extremity surgery. Value, defined as outcomes over costs, can be created in several ways; there must be optimal patient clinical outcomes and satisfaction, low costs, and/or both. As healthcare systems globally seek to reduce costs while improving patient outcomes, surgical innovations such as WALANT have understandably gained traction.

Historical Evolution of Wide-Awake Local Anesthesia No Tourniquet

The origins of WALANT surgery trace back to longstanding concerns about the dangers of epinephrine in relation to tissue necrosis. Sterling Bunnell, widely regarded as the father of hand surgery, published Surgery of the Hand in 1944. In this text, Bunnell1 made a formal recommendation against the use of epinephrine in the digits of the upper extremity, citing the rationale that epinephrine causes vasoconstriction in the terminal arteries and can, therefore, lead to digital necrosis. Over 50 years later, Keith Denkler2 investigated the validity of this commonly held belief and, in 2001, published a landmark article that comprehensively reviewed the literature on epinephrine use in the hand. In this study, Denkler and his team reviewed 120 years of literature for cases of epinephrine-induced gangrene of the digits. Over all this time, he was able to find only 21 cases of digital gangrene that involved epinephrine. Of those 21 cases, there were no case reports of digital gangrene after use of commercial lidocaine with epinephrine, which was introduced in 1948.

A few years later, Don Lalonde published a prospective study of over 3000 patients who were injected with low-dose epinephrine in the hands and fingers, demonstrating that none experienced digital tissue loss.3 In this article, he also reviewed Denkler's previously described 21 cases of digital necrosis, which included epinephrine use, and found that all cases were also injected with cocaine or procaine. His explanation for this finding was that procaine left on the shelf turns acidic, reaching a pH of 1. Since there were no expiration dates required by the FDA before 1978, it seemed likely that expired procaine, rather than epinephrine, led to the few cases of necrosed digits. This large, multicenter prospective study effectively discredited the myth of epinephrine as a cause of digital necrosis when used in the hand. Since this publication, WALANT has been more broadly adopted on a regional and global scale, with an increasing number of use cases and studies examining the clinical and economic outcomes.

Principles and Technique of Wide-Awake Local Anesthesia No Tourniquet

Wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet is characterized by 3 core features: local anesthesia, epinephrine-induced hemostasis, and the elimination of sedation and tourniquet use. The procedure typically involves subcutaneous injection of buffered lidocaine with epinephrine using a fine (27–30 gauge) needle. The injection is performed slowly, under the dermis, and in a tumescent fashion to ensure both pain control and hemostasis. An essential component of a successful technique involves advancing the needle while injecting, such that approximately 1 cm of anesthetic always precedes it. The skin is pinched before injection to reduce discomfort, and subsequent injections are administered in areas already anesthetized to avoid patient discomfort.4

The WALANT environment also differs from traditional sterility protocols. Minor field sterility—such as using a clean back table and gloves rather than a full sterile field—has been proven safe for many minor procedures, with several studies demonstrating equivalency in infection rates when applied appropriately.5,6 This approach reduces setup time, instrument use, and waste, further adding to WALANT's value.6

Clinical Outcomes

Research on clinical outcomes comparing functional outcomes and patient satisfaction for surgeries performed under WALANT vs. traditional anesthesia has increased in recent years. Carpal tunnel release (CTR) is the most common nontraumatic hand surgery performed in North America and is a relatively low-complexity procedure. For this reason, a meaningful portion of the WALANT outcome research uses CTR as a model. To date, numerous studies have demonstrated equivalent or superior outcomes with WALANT.7,8 A prospective cohort study performed by Davison et al.7 looked into patients' overall perioperative experience and evaluated how patients felt about CTR performed under WALANT vs. traditional anesthesia. They compared 100 CTR studies performed under WALANT with 100 CTR studies performed under IV sedation. Over 90% of patients in both groups reported that they would choose the same anesthesia they had the first time if they underwent surgery again.7 Of note, narcotics were used by only 5% of WALANT patients and 67% of sedated patients.7 In addition, sedated patients spent more time in the hospital, underwent more extensive preoperative testing, and had greater preoperative anxiety.7

Tulipan et al. compared 230 patients who underwent CTR either under WALANT (81) or monitored anesthesia care (MAC) sedation (149) and found that there was no statistical difference in quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH) scores, complications, reoperations, or pain scores.8 Both groups reported high rates of patient satisfaction, and the authors, therefore, made a conclusion in support of the equivalency of the 2 techniques.

Wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet has also demonstrated comparable outcomes and similarly low complication rates in other lower complexity hand surgeries, such as extensor tendon repair, trigger finger release, and flexor tendon repair.9,10 For flexor tendon repair, a comparative study by Townsend et al.11 of 65 evaluated zone I and II flexor tendon repairs evaluated outcomes including quick DASH scores, range of motion, grip strength, rupture rates, and pain levels and showed no statistically significant difference between WALANT and traditional anesthesia groups.

Patient satisfaction has been consistently high with WALANT.12,13 The ability to remain awake, avoid fasting, and return to normal activity more quickly contributes to high satisfaction rates. In addition, the ability to test repair tensioning in real time is appealing to the surgeon and patient alike. However, no studies to date have demonstrated functional improvements because of this technique.

Economic Impact

The economic argument for WALANT is persuasive. By avoiding the main operating room, sedation, anesthesia personnel, and preoperative evaluations, as well as by decreasing medical waste, WALANT can dramatically reduce the cost of surgery for both the patient and institution alike.

Alter et al.14 conducted a cost analysis of CTR performed under WALANT vs. sedation and found an average cost savings of approximately $1320 per CTR performed under WALANT. When considered in the context of the hundreds of thousands of CTR surgeries performed per year, this finding represents a significant opportunity for savings. In addition, the same study reported that the recovery room time was 7 minutes on average for WALANT procedures as opposed to 84 minutes for patients who underwent MAC, further demonstrating WALANT's potential to reduce indirect costs.

In another cost analysis, Chatterjee et al. reported an on-average net loss of $650 for hospital-based CTR cases, while office-based cases generated an on-average net profit of $1186.15 Looking beyond CTR as a model, Rhee et al.16 performed a prospective cohort study of 100 consecutive clinic-based WALANT hand surgery procedures and found that CTR, A1 pulley release, and de Quervain release achieved cost savings of 85%, 70%, and 84%, respectively, when the procedures were performed in clinic under WALANT compared with the main operating room.

In addition to financial savings, WALANT is more environmentally sustainable. McKnight et al.13 reported that using premade hand packs for back table setup saved their institution more than $65,000 and reduced over 16,000 kg (18.4 tons) of waste for 1001 consecutive patients undergoing hand procedures. Le et al. quantified the waste and related emissions associated with WALANT CTR procedures performed across 3 clinical settings—OR, procedure room, and office—and concluded that WALANT CTR generates significantly less solid waste in clinic and ambulatory settings compared with the main operating room.14 This study highlights the opportunity to improve sustainability and lessen environmental impact through thoughtful surgical setting selection.

The operating room remains one of the most expensive cost buckets of the hospital, with costs driven by fixed overhead and specialized personnel.17 By shifting routine hand procedures to the clinic, institutions can reduce costs and increase throughput without compromising care. From a systemic perspective, WALANT frees up operating room resources for higher acuity cases, improving hospital efficiency. As reimbursement models evolve to favor bundled payments and outpatient procedures, WALANT offers a model for aligning clinical and financial incentives.

Expanding Indications and the Future of Wide-Awake Local Anesthesia No Tourniquet

Future research and innovation in WALANT will likely focus on identifying additional procedures amenable to the technique, refining anesthetic protocols, and expanding its use in academic training programs. Wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet principles are increasingly being applied to more complex hand and upper extremity procedures, such as distal radius fracture fixation, infection care, tendon transfers, wrist arthroscopy, and even regional soft-tissue flaps.18–22 While these studies are currently largely limited to small case reports and case series, they demonstrate an interest in using WALANT technique more broadly.

There have also been reports on the use of WALANT-like techniques for procedures outside the field of hand and upper extremity, such as in orthopedic trauma, spine surgery, foot and ankle, and sports surgery.23–26 In these settings, WALANT has demonstrated feasibility, safety, and patient satisfaction. These findings suggest that the core principles of WALANT—simplicity, safety, and efficiency—may translate across subspecialties.

The WALANT model is also increasingly important in low-resource settings, where access to operating rooms and anesthesia services may be limited. Organizations seeking global health equity have begun adopting WALANT as a cost-effective and scalable approach for essential surgical care.27

It is important to note that WALANT is not without potential pitfalls. For one, WALANT surgery in the upper extremity carries a steep learning curve, requiring surgeons to develop proficiency in local anesthetic dosing, injection technique, and intraoperative patient communication, which can initially prolong operative times. The awake nature of the procedure may necessitate additional nursing or support staff to monitor patient comfort, anxiety, and hemodynamic status throughout the case. In select patients, discomfort, anxiety, or inadequate analgesia can arise despite appropriate technique, creating the potential need to convert to regional or general anesthesia mid-procedure. Such intraoperative conversion can disrupt workflow, increase operative time, and introduce logistical challenges, particularly in settings not equipped for rapid anesthetic escalation.

One concern with adopting the WALANT technique is the time required after injection of epinephrine to achieve maximal hemostatic benefit (25-30 minutes). Depending on the perioperative environment and the number of operative patients, this delay may be logistically difficult and impose a significant additional time burden on the system involved. Schulman et. al evaluated patient tolerance of location anesthesia with a pneumatic tourniquet (LA-T) and found that short, wide-awake hand procedures using a tourniquet were well tolerated and accepted by patients.28 In fact, the authors reported that the local anesthetic injection was considered to be more painful than the pneumatic tourniquet.28 While this is not to say that tourniquet use is without pitfalls, this study demonstrates how wide-awake surgeon can be adapted based on institutional needs.

Together, these factors underscore that WALANT may not be universally applicable and requires thoughtful patient selection, institutional resources, and surgeon experience to be implementeed safely and efficiently.

Conclusion

Wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet offers a compelling value proposition to patients, physicians, and hospitals: it is clinically efficacious and cost-effective; results in high patient satisfaction; is compatible with trends in hospital economics; and presents a real opportunity for innovation within and beyond hand surgery. Through efficient use of resources, elimination of unnecessary sedation, and preservation of surgical efficacy, WALANT exemplifies value-based surgical care. As research continues and clinical applications broaden, WALANT has the potential for use in more complex procedures and those outside the field of hand and upper extremity surgery, enabling enhanced global surgical access.
