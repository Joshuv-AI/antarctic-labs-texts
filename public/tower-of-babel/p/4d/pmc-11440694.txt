Pan-cancer analysis reveals that TK1 promotes tumor progression by mediating cell proliferation and Th2 cell polarization
Authors: Zhecheng Li, Zhaoyi Wu, Xing You, Neng Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTK1 (Thymidine kinase 1) is a member of the thymidine kinase family and has been observed to be significantly upregulated in a variety of cancer types. However, the exact roles of TK1 in tumor progression and the tumor immune microenvironment are not fully understood. This study aims to investigate the comprehensive involvement of TK1 in pan-cancer through the utilization of bioinformatics analysis, validation of pathological tissue samples, and in vitro experimental investigations.MethodsThe expression profiles together with diagnostic and prognostic role of TK1 in pan-cancer were investigated though TCGA, TARGET, GTEx, and CPTAC databases. The single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and single-cell sequencing datasets were used to examine the relationship between TK1 and immune infiltration. The expression of TK1 were verified in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) through qPCR, western blotting and immunohistochemical assays. The proliferative capacity of HCC cell lines was assessed through CCK-8 and colony formation assays, while cytokine levels were measured via ELISA. Furthermore, flow cytometry was utilized to analyze cell cycle distribution and the proportions of Th2 cells.ResultsTK1 was overexpressed in most cancers and demonstrated significant diagnostic and prognostic value. Among the various immune cells in pan-cancer, Th2 cells exhibited the closest association with TK1. Furthermore, the single-cell atlas provided insights into the distribution and proportion of TK1 in immune cells of HCC. In vitro experiments revealed an elevated expression of TK1 in HCC tissue and cell lines, and its role in influencing HCC cell proliferation by regulating G0/G1 phase arrest. Additionally, TK1 in cancer cells was found to potentially modulate Th2 cell polarization through the chemokine CCL5.ConclusionTK1 holds immense potential as a biomarker for pan-cancer diagnosis and prognosis. Additionally, targeting the expression of TK1 represents a promising therapeutic approach that can enhance the efficacy of current anti-tumor immunotherapy by modulating Th2 cell polarization and multiple mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-024-03515-x.

Introduction

Tumorigenesis and progression are not only the result of genetic mutations in cancer cells but also involve interactions between cancer cells and non-cancer cells and components in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [1]. The TME is composed of non-cancer cells and components present in the tumor, including immune cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, extracellular matrix, and signaling molecules [2]. The immunosuppressive TME, composed of inhibitory immune cells such as type-II T helper cells (Th2 cells), regulatory T (Treg) cells, and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), facilitates immune evasion of tumor cells through various pathways, thereby accelerating tumor progression [3, 4]. In recent years, the investigation into the tumor immunity has yielded numerous advantages for cancer treatment. For example, the implementation of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade has demonstrated a substantial increase in the survival rate of select patients diagnosed with advanced melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [5–7]. However, the benefits of these drugs are limited to only a few specific cancer types and a small fraction of patients. Hence, it is imperative to identify and study pan-cancer immunoregulatory genes to elucidate their collective therapeutic effects across diverse tumor types.

Thymidine kinase 1 (TK1) is a potential biomarker for cancer recurrence, treatment monitoring, and survival, as its upregulation has been found to be an early event in cancer development and its serum levels have been shown to be tied to cancer stage [8–10]. Research has revealed that TK1 plays a crucial role in cellular proliferation and the regeneration of thymidine nucleotides within the DNA salvage pathway [11–13]. Indeed, studies have also found that TK1, along with its methylation status, is associated with various tumor-infiltrating immune cells and immunomodulators in prostate cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma [14, 15]. However, there is currently a lack of research available regarding the oncogenic role of TK1 in pan-cancer and its specific mechanisms of influence on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME).

In this study, we have observed a negative correlation between the expression of TK1 and the prognosis of various cancers. In vitro experiments further validated the association between TK1 expression and tumor proliferation. Notably, we made an intriguing discovery that elevated TK1 expression promotes the polarization of Th2 cells. Our research findings suggest that TK1 could serve as a pan-cancer diagnosis and prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target to enhance the efficacy of existing anti-tumor immune therapies.

Methods

Gene and protein expression analysis of TK1

We obtained pan-cancer datasets from UCSC (https://xenabrowser.net/) [16], derived from TCGA, TARGET, and GTEx databases, which had undergone standardized processing. From this dataset, we extracted expression data for the ENSG00000167900 (TK1) gene across various samples. We further filtered the samples to include those from the following sources: Solid Tissue Normal, Primary Solid Tumor, Primary Tumor, Normal Tissue, Primary Blood Derived Cancer - Bone Marrow, and Primary Blood Derived Cancer - Peripheral Blood. Additionally, we performed a log2(TPM + 1) transformation on each expression value. Finally, we excluded cancer types with fewer than three samples within each individual cancer type, resulting in a final dataset comprising expression data for 34 different cancer types.

To assess the differential expression between normal and tumor samples within each cancer type, we utilized the R software (version 4.2.2) and conducted significance analysis using non-paired Wilcoxon Rank Sum and Signed Rank Tests. For evaluating the expression differences of TK1 across various clinical stage samples within each cancer type, we employed analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multiple group sample differential testing.

We utilized the UALCAN portal (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis-prot.html) for a supplementary validation of TK1 protein expression across various cancer types, based on the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) data [17].

Survival, prognostic, and diagnostic analysis

Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, employing the Kaplan-Meier plotter and the log-rank test through the R “survival” package, utilizing the data obtained as mentioned above.

Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) in order to ascertain the potential association between TK1 and various survival outcomes, including Overall Survival (OS), Disease-Specific Survival (DSS), Progression-Free Interval (PFI), and Disease-Free Interval (DFI). The HR values, along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals and log-rank p-values, were computed through univariate survival analysis. Statistical significance was determined with a threshold of p < 0.05. The PROC package and timeROC package in R software were used to draw the ROC curve of diagnosis. The Survival and Survminer packages were used to establish the nomogram model analysis and calibration curves.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) [18] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [19] pathway analyses were conducted by “clusterProfiler” [20], “org.Hs.eg.db”, “ggplot2” as well as “GOplot” [21] packages. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [22] was performed to determine the pathways associated with TK1 expression in multiple cancers utilizing the “org.Hs.eg.db”, “clusterProfiler”, “ggplot2”, and “enrichplot” packages in the R software. Adjusted p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune cell infiltration was determined using ssGSEA [23], which relied on marker genes specific to each immune cell type [24]. Lollipop charts were generated to investigate the correlation of TK1 with 24 types of immune cell infiltration in tumor samples from different cancers, using Spearman correlation analysis. Simultaneously, violin plots were created to compare the levels of immune cell infiltration between the high and low TK1 expression groups of patients, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05 using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. TISCH (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/home/) was utilized to unveil the expression pattern of TK1 across major lineage cell types in LIHC at a single-cell level.

Co-culturing system

Transwells (0.4 μm, Corning) were utilized and settled in a 6-well plate. The upper chambers were filled with 1.5 mL Jurkat cells (3*105 cells/ml) and the lower chambers with 2.5 mL HCC cells (2*105 cells/ml). Cell culture plates were put in a cell incubator for 24 h.

Flow cytometry analysis

For the HCC cell lines, 1 × 106 cells were inoculated into each well of a 6-well plate. Once the cells were collected, the plates were incubated with a combination of 1 ml of DNA staining solution and 10 µl of osmotic stabilization solution for a duration of 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the samples were immediately detected by flow cytometry.

The Jurkat cells, co-cultured with HCC cells, were subjected to flow cytometric analysis through the following concise steps: Initially, the Jurkat cells were centrifuged and resuspended in 2 ml of PBS, followed by a further centrifugation for 5 min. This centrifugation-resuspension process was repeated three times to ensure purity. Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in 100 µl of PBS for staining with a fixable live/dead cell dye from eBioscience (65–0866). Following this, various surface markers were applied. Specifically, anti-human CD3 (APC-Cy7, Sigma-Aldrich, 586519) and CD4 (FITC, Sigma-Aldrich, 617788) antibodies were used to detect extracellular proteins characteristic of Th2 cells. Subsequently, the cells were fixed using either the eBioscience Fix/Perm or BD Fix/Perm buffer kit. For intracellular staining, an IL-4 (PE-Cy7, BD Pharmingen 569088) antibody was employed to detect intracellular proteins associated with Th2 cells. Finally, the proportions of Th2 cells were accurately measured and analyzed using FACS Calibur and FlowJo software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and diagrams were made through SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 8. We utilized Student’s t-test for pairwise significance analysis and performed analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparing differences among multiple groups of samples. A two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

The expression of TK1 in pan-cancer

Firstly, we analyzed the expression of TK1 across 34 cancer types, utilizing data from the TCGA, TARGET, and GTEx databases. As depicted in Fig. 1A and B, TK1 expression was significantly higher in the majority of cancer types, both in non-paired samples and paired tumor samples, including liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), thyroid cancer (THCA), bladder cancer (BLCA), and others. However, we observed a considerably lower expression of TK1 in kidney chromophobe (KICH) compared to normal tissues. Moreover, we also observed that in nine different cancer types, including LIHC, the expression levels of TK1 generally increased with the advancement of T staging (Fig. 1C). In the CPTAC database, the expression level of TK1 at the protein level is also significantly upregulated in cancers such as LIHC, LUAD, and breast cancer (Fig. 1D).

The prognostic and diagnostic value of TK1 in pan-cancer

Subsequently, to delve deeper into the potential link between TK1 expression and prognosis, we conducted a Cox regression analysis, revealing a statistically significant positive correlation between TK1 expression and hazard ratios for OS, DSS (Fig. 2A and B), PFI, and disease-free interval (DFI) (Supplementary Fig. 1A and 1B) in patients diagnosed with LIHC, kidney papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), LUAD, and other cancer types.

The prognostic value of TK1 in pan-cancer. (A) Cox regression analysis of the OS in pan-cancer. (B) Cox regression analysis of the DSS in pan-cancer. The OS (C) and DSS (D) Kaplan-Meier survival curves

We subsequently employed prognostic data pertaining to 12 distinct cancer types from the TCGA database to generate Kaplan-Meier curves. The findings revealed that elevated TK1 expression is detrimental to the OS (Fig. 2C and supplementary Fig. 1C) and DSS (Fig. 2D and supplementary Fig. 1D) outcomes of patients diagnosed with LIHC, KIRP, pancreatic Cancer (PAAD), LUAD, mesothelioma (MESO), adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), glioblastoma multiforme, and lower-grade glioma (GBMLGG), as well as low-grade glioma (LGG) and other cancer subtypes. Conversely, TK1 was found to improve the outcomes of patients with cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC). Similar results were observed with regards to PFI as shown in supplementary Fig. 2.

In order to assess the value of TK1 as a pan-cancer diagnostic biomarker, we conducted ROC analysis, which revealed its excellent diagnostic performance across multiple cancers, such as LIHC (AUC = 0.962), LUAD (AUC = 0.944), oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) (AUC = 0.972), KIRP (AUC = 0.795), Kidney Clear Cell Carcinoma (KIRC) (AUC = 0.724), LGG (AUC = 0.720), ACC (AUC = 0.776), GBMLGG (AUC = 0.822), and other types of cancer (Fig. 3A).

Furthermore, nomograms were constructed to directly predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS outcomes of LIHC and other types of cancer patients, utilizing a combination of clinical characteristics and TK1 expression data. We observed that higher TK1 expression is associated with a poorer 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probability in LIHC patients (Fig. 3B), and this significant correlation is also evident in various cancer types, including LUAD, skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), ACC, GBM, KIRP, LGG, OSCC, and PAAD (supplementary Fig. 3). The calibrated 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival prediction curves demonstrated that the nomogram model had high accuracy in predicting the OS of LIHC (Fig. 3C). Moreover, the significance of TK1 as a prognostically valuable factor for LIHC was substantiated through both univariate and multivariate Cox hazard analyses (Table 1).

Functional enrichment analysis of TK1

Through GO and KEGG pathway analyses, we discovered that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with TK1 had significant regulatory effects on cell cycle, antigen binding, lymphocyte-mediated immunity, and DNA replication in LIHC (Fig. 4A). To further elucidate the biological functions of TK1, we conducted GSEA to identify the most relevant biological processes associated with TK1. Our results revealed that functions related to lymphocyte-mediated immunity, cell cycle regulation, and immune response-regulating signaling pathways were positively correlated with high levels of TK1 in LIHC, as shown in Fig. 4B. Similar functions, as well as cell cycle transition and regulation of immunity, were also observed in other types of cancer such as MESO, as depicted in supplementary Figs. 4, 5and 6.

To delve deeper into TK1 expression across various cell types at the single-cell level, we examined 7 single-cell sequencing datasets of LIHC. Our analysis revealed predominant TK1 expression not only in malignant cells but also in T proliferating cells (Tprolif), exhausted CD8 + T cells (CD8Tex), and DC cells (Fig. 4C and D).

Additionally, we analyzed the correlation between TK1 and several key checkpoint molecules in the cell cycle transition. The results indicated a significant positive correlation between the expression levels of TK1 and CDK1 (Cor = 0.74), CDK2 (Cor = 0.64), CDK4 (Cor = 0.45), CCNA2 (Cor = 0.69), CCNE1 (Cor = 0.62), CCNE2 (Cor = 0.43), and other molecules (Fig. 4E and F). Therefore, TK1 may significantly impact the regulation of cell cycle progression and contribute to the immunosuppressive microenvironment across a broad spectrum of cancers, particularly LIHC.

TK1 enhances the proliferation of hepatocellular carcinoma cells

Considering our earlier findings indicating elevated TK1 expression in LIHC and its close association with poor prognosis, we opted to conduct in vitro experiments in LIHC to validate the potential functions predicted by the bioinformatics analysis mentioned above. Firstly, we conducted quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), western blotting (WB) and immunohistochemical (IHC) assays to assess TK1 expression in HCC patients. The expression of TK1 was significantly higher in HCC tissues compared to normal tissues, both at the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 5A-B and supplementary Fig. 7A). This trend was also evident in HCC cell lines, as confirmed by the qPCR and WB results (Fig. 5C and supplementary Fig. 7B). Among the tested HCC cell lines, the Hep-3B cell line exhibited the highest TK1 expression level, while the Hep-G2 cell line displayed the lowest. Consequently, these two cell lines were selected for knockdown and overexpression experiments, as well as for further investigation (Fig. 5D and supplementary Fig. 7C and D).

Subsequently, through CCK-8 assay (Fig. 5E) and colony formation assay (Fig. 5F), we observed a significant reduction in the proliferative capacity of Hep-3B cells with TK1 knockdown compared to the control group. Conversely, Hep-G2 cells overexpressing TK1 exhibited significantly enhanced proliferative capacity. To further investigate whether TK1 promotes cell proliferation by regulating the cell cycle, we conducted flow cytometry to detect changes in the cell cycle of HCC cells after modulating TK1 expression. The results showed that knocking down TK1 in Hep-3B cells led to an increase in the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase and a decrease in the S + G2/M phase, indicating G0/G1 phase arrest (Fig. 5G). Conversely, overexpression of TK1 in Hep-G2 cells exhibited opposite results (Fig. 5H). Taken together, the aforementioned data indicate that TK1 is highly expressed in HCC tissues and cell lines, and it may promote the proliferation of HCC cells by regulating the G1/S phase transition of the cell cycle.

Immune cell infiltration associated with TK1 level

We employed ssGSEA [23] to assess the infiltration level of each immune cell type and subsequently examined the relationship between immune cell infiltration levels and TK1 expression across multiple cancer types. Notably, among all types of immune cells, Th2 cells displayed the strongest significantly positive correlation with TK1 expression across all 12 types of cancer. However, the infiltration levels of anti-tumor immune cells such as cytotoxic cells, NK cells, and CD8 + T cells were inversely correlated with TK1 expression in LIHC (Fig. 6A-F and supplementary Fig. 8A). Scatter plots specifically depict a significant positive correlation between Th2 cell infiltration levels and TK1 expression across various cancers, whereas total T helper cells do not exhibit such correlation with TK1 (Fig. 6G and supplementary Fig. 8B).

Additionally, when we divided patients into high and low TK1 expression groups based on the median value of TK1 gene expression across all samples, we observed a significant increase in the abundance of Th2 cells in the TK1 high-expression group across all cancer types including LIHC (Supplementary Fig. 9). These findings suggest that TK1 may play a critical role in promoting Th2 cell infiltration, thereby modulating the tumor immune microenvironment and contributing to the immunosuppressive microenvironment across various types of cancer, especially in LIHC.

TK1 in cancer cell promotes Jurkat cell polarization toward the Th2 phenotype

The Jurkat T cell line has been previously employed for the study of CD4 + T cell polarization due to its composition of uniform T lymphocytes, possession of membrane markers indicative of normal T lymphocytes [25], and its capacity to be induced to differentiate into various T helper (Th) cell subtypes [26, 27]. Considering the significant positive correlation between TK1 and Th2 cells in pan-cancer as observed in our previous results, we conducted a co-culture experiment involving Jurkat cells and TK1-manipulated HCC cells (Fig. 7A). The results showed that the concentration of Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-13 decreased after co-culture with shTK1 Hep-3B cells, while in contrast, the concentration of IL-4 and IL-13 displayed an increasing trend after co-culture with oeTK1 Hep-G2 cells (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, the mRNA levels of the Th2 cell marker GATA binding protein 3 (GATA3), as well as IL-4 and IL-13, were significantly lower in Jurkat cells co-cultured with shTK1 Hep-3B cells compared to their control group (Fig. 7C). Conversely, the expression levels of GATA3, IL-4, and IL-13 were significantly elevated after co-culture with oeTK1 Hep-G2 cells (Fig. 7D). Western blotting was then employed to validate the expression of GATA3 at the protein level in Jurkat cells co-cultured with shTK1 Hep-3B cells and oeTK1 Hep-G2 cells, respectively (Fig. 7E).

CCL5 is the critical chemokine induced by TK1 in HCC cells to promote Th2 polarization

To further investigate the mechanism by which TK1 influences Th2 cell polarization, we analyzed the correlation between TK1 and 41 chemokines in the TCGA-LIHC database. The results showed that the expression level of TK1 was significantly positively correlated with CCL5, CX3CL1, CCL3, CCL4, CCL18, and others, while it was significantly negatively correlated with CXCL14 (Fig. 8A). Subsequently, we selected seven chemokines potentially associated with Th2 polarization and performed qPCR validation in shTK1 Hep-3B cells and oeTK1 Hep-G2 cells. The results revealed that among all the chemokines tested (CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL8, CCL18, CCL20, and CCL25), the expression level of CCL5 exhibited the most significant difference (Fig. 8B). Consistently, the significant difference in CCL5 concentration in the culture medium of HCC cells in the lower layer of the co-culture system was also verified through ELISA experiments (Fig. 8C).

For further validation, we conducted “rescue” experiments. Exogenous CCL5 (50pg/ml) was added to the culture medium of Jurkat cells co-cultured with shTK1 Hep-3B cells. The results revealed a significant restoration of GATA3 expression levels in Jurkat cells (Fig. 8D). Consistently, flow cytometry analysis showed a significant increase in the proportion of Th2 cells after the addition of exogenous CCL5 (Fig. 8E). Collectively, these findings suggest that TK1 in cancer cells has the potential to promote Th2 polarization within the tumor immune microenvironment by regulating the secretion of the chemokine CCL5.

Discussion

TK1 belongs to the thymidine kinase family and is located in the cytoplasm, serving as a crucial enzyme during the cell cycle [28]. It is essential for DNA synthesis and cellular repair following DNA damage [10, 12]. In recent years, there has been a growing number of studies on TK1 in cancer, and many of them have demonstrated its diagnostic or oncogenic role in specific cancer types [14, 29, 30]. In this study, we found that the mRNA level and protein expression level of TK1 were significantly overexpressed in various cancer, and exhibited good diagnostic performance in multiple cancers such as LIHC, LUAD, KIRP, and LGG. Previous studies have also demonstrated that TK1 has advantages compared to validated markers such as Ki-67 [10]. Additionally, combining TK1 with HSP90α and AFP has been shown to enhance the diagnostic efficiency for HCC [31]. Furthermore, TK1 is more convenient for clinical practice as its expression level can be easily detected through serum [13]. In addition, TK1 is more closely related to the growth stage of cells and can more accurately reflect the proliferation status of tumors [32, 33]. In this study, we observed that high expression of TK1 positively correlates with more advanced clinical stages and predicts worse prognosis in various cancer, including LIHC, LUAD, KIRP, and LGG. To elaborate on the significance of TK1 as a prognostically valuable factor for LIHC and other cancers, we constructed nomograms and performed univariate and multivariate Cox hazard analyses based on the TCGA database. These findings align with the results of Jiang et al. in a multicenter real-world study [34]. Based on the above results, we can conclude that compared to other biomarkers, TK1 serves as a more accurate pan-cancer diagnosis and prognostic biomarker in a broader range of clinical practices across multiple tumor types.

Consistent with the critical role of TK1 in the cell cycle described earlier, our functional enrichment analysis results also demonstrate that TK1 is closely associated with DNA replication, cell cycle transition and regulation, and cell cycle checkpoints in LIHC and various other cancers. Additionally, given the close association between TK1 and cell cycle and cell proliferation, studies by McCarney and Malorni reported the significant potential of TK1 as a prognostic biomarker for patients with advanced hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer who treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors [35]. In our study, we found significant positive correlation between TK1 expression levels and key molecules regulating cell cycle checkpoints, including CDK2, CDK4, and Cyclin E, which regulate G1/S phase transition. Subsequent flow cytometry analysis confirming G0/G1 phase arrest induced by TK1 knockdown further corroborates these findings. The cytological experiments demonstrating the impact of TK1 on HCC cell proliferation may indeed be mediated through its regulation of the G1/S checkpoint. Previous studies have reported that TK1 can affect proliferative activity in pancreatic cancer [36] and proliferation and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in cervical cancer [37], consistent with our findings. However, our study is the first to propose that TK1 can influence HCC cell proliferation by affecting G0/G1 phase arrest. Meanwhile, a study by Li et al. found that TK1 can promote the progression of HCC by influencing metabolic reprogramming. This finding supports our conclusion that TK1 plays a crucial role in HCC progression and also highlights that oncogenes can promote tumor development through various mechanisms [38].

The TME plays crucial role in the progression of HCC [3, 39]. Intriguingly, we observed that TK1 is not only highly expressed in malignant cells but also in T proliferating cells (Tprolif), exhausted CD8 + T cells (CD8Tex), and dendritic cells (DC cells). The research findings by Fan et al. suggest that proliferating T cells exhibit a pre-exhausted state, which may evolve into T exhausted cells and correlate with poorer prognosis [40]. Exhausted CD8 + T cells are characterized by a diminished proliferative capacity and effector function, accompanied by the expression of multiple inhibitory receptors which are often closely associated with tumor progression [41]. The function of DC cells in cancer is dual-sided, DC cells can restrict the anti-tumor immunity of T cells by expressing V-domain immunoglobulin suppressor of T cell activation (VISTA), and promote Treg cell polarization by expressing CD31 [42]. Furthermore, in our pan-cancer analysis of the correlation between the infiltration levels of 24 types of immune cells and TK1, we found that Th2 cells, which are widely recognized as tumor-promoting cells [43], exhibited the closest association with TK1. On the other hand, the infiltration levels of anti-tumor immune cells [43], such as cytotoxic cells, NK cells, and CD8 + T cells, were inversely correlated with TK1 expression. These findings collectively suggest that TK1 is strongly associated with tumor immune resistance, especially in LIHC.

Considering the significant positive correlation between the infiltration levels of Th2 cells and TK1 in various cancers, we conducted in vitro co-culture experiments to investigate whether TK1 in cancer cells promotes Jurkat cell polarization toward the Th2. We validated the decreased expression of these representative Th2 phenotype markers in Jurkat cells co-cultured with HCC cells with TK1 knockdown, and conversely, their increased expression when co-cultured with HCC cells overexpressing TK1. This conclusion was further confirmed by flow cytometry experiments. In previous studies, TK1 and its methylation status have been demonstrated to correlate with immune infiltration in prostate cancer [14, 44]. Additionally, research by Huang et al. suggests a positive correlation between TK1 expression levels and Treg cell infiltration in Triple-negative breast cancer [45]. Meanwhile, TK1 has been shown to correlate with B cells, T cells, and dendritic cells infiltration in HCC [15]. The disparity between these findings and those of our study may stem from differences in the classification of immune cells and the algorithms used to assess immune infiltration levels. More importantly, our study is the first to report that TK1 predominantly regulates the tumor immune microenvironment by influencing the polarization of Th2 cells, which has been validated through in vitro experiments.

Chemokines and their corresponding receptors are closely related to the polarization and migration of T helper cells [46]. In this study, we validated through public databases, ELISA, and qPCR that the expression of TK1 in HCC cells is significantly correlated with the expression and secretion of various chemokines from a multi-omics perspective, with CCL5 being the most prominent one. Prior studies have reported that the receptor for CCL5, CCR3, is expressed on Th2 cells [47], and research by Zhang et al. found that in Luminal Breast Cancer, CCL5 can promote Th2 cell polarization by activating CCR3 [48]. Therefore, we conducted rescue experiments using exogenous CCL5 to validate the hypothesis of our study. Following the addition of exogenous CCL5, the expression of the Th2 phenotype marker GATA3 significantly increased in Jurkat cells co-cultured with shTK1 HCC cells, and flow cytometry confirmed a significant increase in the proportion of Th2 phenotype cells in Jurkat cells. This ultimately confirms that TK1 in cancer cells may influence Th2 cell polarization through the chemokine CCL5.

In conclusion, this study, for the first time from a pan-cancer perspective, explores the feasibility of TK1 as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for cancer. Building upon this foundation, using HCC as an example, we delve into the specific oncogenic mechanisms of TK1 and its regulatory role in the immune microenvironment, particularly its effect on Th2 cell polarization. Consequently, we can identify the immense potential of TK1 as a therapeutic target to enhance the efficacy of existing anti-tumor immune therapies.
