Predicting crop disease severity using real time weather variability through machine learning algorithms
Authors: Amit Bijlwan, Rajeev Ranjan, Manendra Singh, Rahul Purohit, Arun Jyoti Nath, Sumit Chakravarty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Integrating disease severity with real-time meteorological variables and advanced machine learning techniques has provided valuable predictive insights for assessing disease severity in wheat. This study emphasizes the potential of machine learning models, particularly artificial neural networks (ANN), in predicting wheat disease severity with high accuracy. The field experiment was conducted over two consecutive rabi growing seasons (2023 And 2024) using a randomized block design with four sowing dates to investigate critical weather-disease relationships for two key wheat pathogens: Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (yellow rust) and Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici (powdery mildew). Weekly assessments of disease severity were combined with meteorological data and analyzed using ANN and regularized regression models. The ANN model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy for yellow rust and powdery mildew, achieving R-squared values (R2 of 0.96 And 0.98 for calibration And 0.93 And 0.95 for validation, respectively. Random Forest (RF) models also exhibited robust performance with R2 values of 0.97 And 0.98 for calibration And 0.93 And 0.90 for validation for yellow rust and powdery mildew, respectively. In contrast, Elastic Net, Lasso, and Ridge regression models showed comparatively moderate predictive capabilities. Principal component analysis (PCA) explained the key meteorological variables influencing disease incidence, with evapotranspiration, temperature, wind speed and humidity emerging as critical factors. Disease prediction is an important aspect of developing a decision support system, and it makes farmers make informed decisions to optimize production.

Introduction

Wheat is an important global food crop, ranking as the third most-produced cereal after rice and maize and the second most-produced primarily for human consumption1. Wheat consumption is projected to increase by 11% by 2031, while the production area will expand by only 3%2. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) estimates that 20–40% of crops are lost globally due to pests and disease incidence every yer3. However, the actual loss in wheat yield is about 13%, mainly caused by pests and diseases4. Wheat yield loss is a global concern as new races of rust and powdery mildew pathogens are more virulent and evolve radially5–8. The yield loss caused by powdery mildew in Warsaw And Holland is 1–50% And 1–54%, respectively, in 50 cultivars9. In China, wheat yield loss from pests And pathogens ranges from 4.84 to 16.29%10. Spring wheat was reported to be susceptible to powdery mildew, which reduced the yield by 2.06–26.68% of 23 Egyptian cultivars11. However, under optimal climatic conditions, stripe rust And stem rust can lead to complete yield loss, while leaf rust may result in crop losses ranging from 45 to 50%. Overcoming yield loss with disease and pest resistant varieties takes many years, typically depending on the crop, disease causing pathogen and available technology12. Estimating yield reductions in wheat crops infected with rust and powdery mildew requires an updated understanding of specific disease progression curve elements for these complex pathosystems. Diseases such as wheat yellow rust (stripe rust), brown rust (leaf rust), and powdery mildew have been recognized as major threats to wheat production worldwide. Among these, the area under the disease progress curve (AUDPC), rate of disease increase (r-value), and final disease severity have been critically linked to wheat yield losses. Higher AUDPC values and faster disease progression rates have been strongly associated with greater reductions in grain yield13. Agriculture faces many challenges because of growing environmental concerns related to water use and temperature fluctuations14,15. It is projected that 22 metric tonnes of food grains will be deficient in South Asia by 2030. A recent freeze, followed by hot and dry weather, has adversely affected the winter wheat crop in the region16–18. Historical temperature trends indicate that wheat yield declined by 5.5% from 1980 to 2010, correlating with a 0.13 °C increase in decadal temperatures19. Meteorological data shows that mean Annual temperatures in regions where wheat, rice, maize, And soybean are cultivated have risen by approximately 1 °C over the last century and are projected to continue rising in the future20,21. The temperature increase is associated with An estimated global wheat yield loss of 6.0 ± 2.9% per degree Celsius. However, the spatial impacts of temperature increases are highly heterogeneous. Estimated wheat yield losses per degree Celsius are similar to the global average in the United States (5.5 ± 4.4%) and France (6.0 ± 4.2%), whereas in India (9.1 ± 5.4%) and Russia (7.8 ± 6.3%) are more susceptible to temperature increases22–24.

Powdery mildew is a critical foliar disease of wheat, prevalent in most wheat-growing regions. Infection with powdery mildew can lead to yield reductions of up to 40%, especially under humid rainfed and irrigated conditions25,26. Moreover, wheat blast, caused by the fungal pathogen, poses a significant risk to wheat production in warm and humid regions27. Recent studies have provided a more critical understanding of the factors influencing yield loss due to powdery mildew. Early disease onset has been shown to intensify severity by up to 82%, significantly contributing to yield reductions. In contrast, improved yields are associated with later disease onset, faster progression rates, earlier cultivar maturation, and optimal planting dates. Furthermore, favorable agro-ecological conditions such as increased rainy days in mid to late spring and warmer temperatures during autumn through spring can mitigate the disease’s impact and support higher yields28–30. Earlier disease onset, later sowing and maturity dates, lower cultivar resistance, increased rainy days, and higher wind speeds in spring are significantly associated with greater disease severity31–34. Climate change projections for 2040–2070 indicate that wheat blast will primarily spread in the tropics And southern hemisphere as temperatures And relative humidity rise, potentially leading to a 13% annual global loss in wheat production. Moreover, wheat blasts are expected to increase in the USA and Mexico. It can impact previously unaffected countries, including Uruguay, Japan, Italy, Spain, and New Zealand. Due to a warmer climate, leaf rust is a major cause of significant wheat yield losses, with occurrence and severity increasing in recent years. The global rise in leaf rust severity across all winter wheat production regions has been a concern35. Leaf rust, a prevalent disease that can occur under diverse climatic conditions, leads to substantial yield losses across large geographic areas36. Over the past decades, leaf rust has been the second most frequently reported disease, followed by powdery mildew in Asia, western and southern Europe and South America37,38. A positive correlation between erratic rainfall patterns and higher temperatures during wheat growing seasons affected the severity of leaf rust in wheat producing regions globally39–41.

The severity assessments of wheat stripe rust and powdery mildew are primarily conducted through visual observations42. However, accurately estimating and predicting the lesion area percentage on individual diseased wheat leaves remains challenging when relying solely on conventional severity grading standards for wheat stripe rust. AUDPC, disease onset time, maximum disease incidence and severity, and Gaussian model parameters (b, m, s), are significantly associated with stripe rust progression in wheat crops sown at different planting dates43,44. This way, forecasting models integrating meteorological data have emerged as valuable ICT tools, comparable to mobile applications, decision support systems, and remote sensing technologies commonly employed in modern agricultural extension programs to enhance disease management45,46. Informing farmers about upcoming diseases through integrating weather forecasts and crop disease severity in a particular region using machine learning models is vital to ensuring effective crop protection practices47,48. Disease prediction modelling techniques utilize deep data mining and probability tools combined with weather forecasting, serving as early warning systems for predicting disease and informing farmers in advance through mobile apps and farmers’ call centres to take adequate measures49. Machine learning (ML) encompasses statistical methods that identify patterns in complex datasets. Wheat crops are critically important to the global economy and food security. Thus, ML techniques help predict disease on a regional basis50,51.

Globally, climate change significantly contributes to wheat yield reduction and is influenced by diseases and pests. Moreover, changes in the plant host environment system caused diseases and pest incidence in unaffected regions globally52. Therefore, farmers can anticipate disease outbreaks and implement timely interventions by integrating meteorological data such as temperature, humidity, and rainfall into the current predictive model53–55. However, microclimate-based pest and disease management is an important aspect of developing decision support systems (DSS) to forecast the likelihood of disease occurrence56 enabling farmers to apply fungicides more judiciously and reduce unnecessary applications57. This practice minimizes the economic burden and mitigates environmental impact58,59. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of ANN and regularized regression models in explicating critical weather-disease correlations and predicting the most suitable multiple regression model to predict disease. Wheat yield loss ascribed by yellow rust and powdery mildew is a global concern. Thus, machine learning algorithms or digital technology offers a strategic approach for timely weather-related disease interventions. Disease prediction minimises crop losses and optimises resource use60. Disease prediction using weather data is an approach that enhances disease management and supports sustainable agricultural practices, ensuring long-term productivity and food security. Therefore, our research objective is to quantify the relationship between meteorological variables and wheat crop rust and powdery mildew epidemics.

Material and methodology

Study area

The study was conducted in two consecutive rabi growing seasons in 2023 And 2024 at the Crop Research Center of Govind Ballabh Pant University of Agriculture And Technology, Pantnagar. Geographically, it is located at a longitude of 29.0222oE And latitude of 79.4908oN at An altitude of 243.86 m above mean sea level in the Shivalik Himalayan foothills of the Terai region in Uttarakhand, India (Fig. 1). The region is weakly developed under the catena of mollic epipedons and grouped under Mollisols order. Parent material is characterized by loamy alluvial sediments with moderately coarse textures61,62. The area is characterised by a humid sub-tropical climate with hot And dry summers from the first week of March to June, May being the hottest month, And a cold season from November to February. Maximum temperatures reach about 45± 1.5 °C And 46± 1.5 °C during summer during the Kharif season, while a minimum temperature of 2.0 ± 0.5 °C usually occurs in the Rabi season. Frost generally occurs from December to February. The average Annual precipitation is about 1574.2 mm, And a maximum rainfall of 1450 mm (80–90%) ocurs during the monsoon season (July to September).

Experimental design and data curation

The field experiment followed a randomized block design with PBW-343 variety utilized with four distinct sowing dates: November 22nd, November 29th, December 6th, And December 13th, across the 2022-23 And 2023−24 growing seasons. The sowing dates were replicated in both growing seasons to provide various environmental conditions for disease progress evaluation and prediction. The study focused on two important wheat diseases: yellow rust and powdery mildew. Disease severity assessments were conducted every week by visual appearance, starting from the first appearance of the disease until maximum severity was observed. To quantify disease severity, standard rating scales were developed for yellow rust (Fig. 2) and powdery mildew (Table 1). Meteorological data includes maximum temperature (Tmax), minimum temperature (Tmin), maximum relative humidity (RHI), minimum relative humidity (RHII), sunshine hours (SSH), rainfall, wind speed (WV), and evaporation (Eva), were obtained from the Department of Agrometeorology at Pantnagar under the IMD recognition. Meteorological variables were categorized weekly using daily data to correspond with the disease severity observation dates over the two growing seasons. Four different sowing dates were incorporated in the experimental design to represent the wheat crop, which has a diverse range of environmental conditions. Thus, it is vital to observe the influence of meteorological variables on disease progression. This approach facilitated a comprehensive evaluation of the relationships between prevailing meteorological factors and the development of yellow rust and powdery mildew diseases in wheat under climatic conditions (Fig. 3). The objective is not to predict final severity from real-time weather alone, but to capture the relationship between weather conditions in the preceding weeks And the subsequent disease development trajectory. The total dataset consisted of 128 disease scores per year, providing 256 observations per disease.

Model development

Artificial neural network (ANN)

The ANN model was developed using the ‘nnet’ package in R. The dataset was split into 70% training And 30% testing sets. The model used 3-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning. Input features were ‘preprocessed’ by ‘centering’ and ‘scaling’. The model architecture was tuned using a grid search over different combinations of hidden layer sizes (1, 3, 5 neurons) and weight decay values (0.1, 0.5, 1). The final model used a linear output layer for regression. The training was performed using the ‘train’ function from the caret package, with the method set to “nnet” (Table 4).

Ridge regression

Ridge regression65 was implemented using the ‘glmnet’ package. The data was split into 70:30 for training And testing. The model used 3-fold cross-validation for ‘lambda’ tuning. Features were ‘centered’ and ‘scaled’. A lambda vector was created using 500 values ranging from 105 to 10−5. The alpha parameter was set to 0 for ridge regression. The ‘train’ function from caret was used with the method set to “glmnet”. The model was trained on the training set, using the predefined lambda vector for tuning (Table 4).

Elastic net (ENET)

The Elastic Net model66 also used the ‘glmnet’ package. Data splitting And preprocessing were similar to the Ridge model. The key difference was setting alpha to 0.5, balancing between ridge and lasso penalties. The same lambda vector (500 values from 105 to 10−5) was used for tuning. The model was trained using caret’s ‘train’ function with method “glmnet”, incorporating both L1 and L2 regularization (Table 4).

Gradient boosting machine (GBM)

The GBM model67 was developed using the ‘gbm’ package. After the 70 − 30 data split, features were ‘centered’ and ‘scaled’. The model was configured with 1000 decision trees, An interaction depth of 3, and a learning rate (shrinkage) of 0.01. Five-fold cross-validation was used within the GBM training process. The distribution was set to “gaussian” for regression tasks. The model was trained directly using the ‘gbm’ function, allowing for parallel processing across available cores (Table 4).

Lasso regression

Lasso regression68 was implemented similarly to Ridge regression, using the ‘glmnet’ package. The key difference was setting the alpha parameter to 1, which applies L1 regularization. Data splitting, preprocessing, and lambda vector creation were identical to the Ridge model. The ‘train’ function from caret was used with method “glmnet”, tuning over the same lambda vector but with alpha fixed at 1 (Table 4).

Random forest

The Random Forest model was developed using the ‘randomForest’ package69,70. After the 70 − 30 data split, 3-fold cross-validation was used for model evaluation. Unlike the other models, explicit feature scaling wasn’t necessary due to the nature of random forests. The model was trained using caret’s ‘train’ function with method “rf”. Default parameters were used for the number of trees and variables considered at each split, allowing the algorithm to optimize these based on the data (Table 4).

Principle component analysis

The Principal Component Analysis was conducted in this study to reduce the dataset’s dimensionality and explore the underlying patterns and relationships between wheat disease severity variables. By reducing the number of variables, PCA simplifies the interpretation of the data and helps identify the most important features driving the variation. Scaling was applied to the data before performing PCA to ensure that all variables were on a similar scale and to prevent variables with larger magnitudes from dominating the analysis. This study used standard scaling (z-score normalization), which subtracts the mean And divides by the standard deviation of each variable. This standardization ensures that all variables have a mean of 0 And a standard deviation of 1, making them directly comparable regarding their influence on the principal components.

The biplot was created, which visually represents the relationships between the independent variables and disease severity. The biplot was customized to include ellipses for each disease type, making it easier to visualize the separation between rust and powdery mildew.

The results showed that R2 between weather parameters And disease severity are strongly correlated with values of 0.96 And 0.93, respectively, obtained in calibration And validation sets. Discussion

Region-wise prediction of rust and powdery mildew based on meteorological data is crucial for effective disease management and optimizing crop yields72–74. Specific weather conditions influence these fungal diseases; meteorological data is crucial for forecasting outbreaks55. The study results indicate rust and powdery mildew infestations increase with average temperature and humidity. Similarly, previous studies also reported that powdery mildew flourishes in high humidity and moderate temperatures (15–20 °C), while rust is favoured by warm temperatures (20–25 °C) and prolonged leaf wetness75,76. A strong correlation between disease incidence and severity of winter wheat rust with weather data (r = 0.89 and r = 0.87, respectively) was reported using CART or decision tree analysis77. However, in our study, the coefficient of determination between weather parameters And disease severity strongly correlates with values of 0.96 And 0.93 during calibration and validation, respectively. Hence, it is best to describe the relationship between weather variables and winter wheat rust epidemics with some differences in weather variables32,78. Stepwise regression analysis for wheat rust showed that maximum relative humidity positively correlated with rust severity, while solar radiation and minimum relative humidity were negatively correlated in disease prediction79. Temperature-related variables are the most crucial for explaining and forecasting the occurrence of leaf rust, whereas a humid and warm environment better explains stripe rust. However, evapotranspiration is primarily a function of maximum air temperature80,81 whereas wind velocity and temperature also appear as important variables across different models82. Similarly, our results align with these findings as the most critical feature across all machine learning models.

Linear regression analysis relating leaf rust severity to maximum air temperature and humidity thermal ratio showed r2 values of 0.68 And 0.71, respectively83. The relationship between wheat leaf rust and weather variables was analysed by84 using the stochastic approach, including the Dennis model and Monte Carlo simulation. This approach emphasized the study of the combined effects of temperature, relative humidity, and rainfall on the infection of wheat by P. striiformis during continuous Hours over the critical period for the development of stripe rust incidence And severity in wheat. The results indicated optimal conditions for developing wheat stripe rust included a combination of relative humidity greater than 92%, temperatures between 4 °C And 16 °C for at least 4 continuous Hours, And rainfall of 0.1 mm or less. Shrinkage regression and machine-learning approaches envisaged that the ANN model is outperformed in calibration and validation to derive models for predicting epidemiological characteristics of white rust in mustard crops85. A decision tree classifier is utilized to predict stripe rust in wheat using limited weather variables And achieved a training And test accuracy of 75.70%. However, stem rust developed a prediction accuracy of 90.05% using 10-fold cross-validation86 In the present study, a six linear regression model showed the highest accuracy of r2 = 90.60, revealing that WV and relative humidity are important variables in predicting disease87. Similarly, features importance by our best-performing model ANN gives a high importance/weightage to WV for predicting yellow rust severity. In contrast, regression analysis by Lasso and Ridge shows that minimum relative humidity is negatively aligned with the prediction of yellow rust.

An empirical model study87 shows that wind is the primary weather factor influencing the powdery mildew epidemic, followed by sunshine and radiation. However, temperature is the main weather factor influencing disease severity88. Clam weather conditions create a humid environment that favors the development of powdery mildew severity89,90. The effect of wind on humidity is stronger than the effect of wind on dispersal. Low wind runs may still be sufficient to move the initial inoculum between fields. Our best-performing model, ANN also shows the importance of minimum temperature and sun shine hours for prediction of powdery mildew.

The stepwise selection method in logistic regression revealed that weekly mean temperature, relative humidity and sunshine are the key predictor variables for powdery mildew assessment at the high risk91. Odds ratio point estimates indicate that increasing weekly temperature (from 11.5 to 21.9 °C) and decreasing weekly relative humidity (from 65.6 to 41.8%) and sunshine hours (from 8.7 to 5.4 h) were key drivers for high powdery mildew severity. Cumulative logit models relating the development of wheat powdery mildew to six weather variables produced R2 of 84.1%. Rainfall and vapor pressure deficit were statistically significantly related to the disease score. The best model with temperature and humidity-related produced a generalized R2 of 72.4%. Greater average wind speed positively impacts the mildew epidemic29,92. Hence, the positive effect of wind on spore dispersal outweighs its negative effect on reducing humidity90,93. However, precipitation has a negative effect on spore survival due to the leaching of spores into deep soil.

Accurate disease prediction aids in implementing integrated pest management (IPM) strategies, combining cultural practices, resistant varieties, and targeted chemical applications94,95. Early warnings of disease incidence allow for proactive measures, such as adjusting irrigation schedules to reduce leaf wetness or optimizing planting dates to avoid peak disease periods96,97. Leveraging meteorological data for predicting wheat powdery mildew and rust is vital for enhancing disease management strategies98,99. It empowers farmers to make informed decisions, safeguarding crop health, ensuring food security, and promoting sustainable agricultural practices.

PCA conducted by32 to associate wheat leaf rust with weather And planting date revealed a tripartite structure in the leaf rust progress curves, collectively explaining 73% of the total variance. The first principal component (PC1) accounted for 33.8% of the variability, PC2, explaining 21.6% of the total variance, was predominantly associated with the mean monthly temperature And the frequency of days characterized by minimum temperatures ranging from 5 °C to 25 °C, coupled with maximum relative humidity exceeding 60%. The third principal component (PC3) elucidated 17.9% of the total variance and demonstrated a moderate positive relation for the mean monthly temperature.

Conclusion

The integration of ANN and regularized regression models with meteorological data demonstrates a powerful approach to predicting the severity of wheat diseases. The ANN model emerged as the most effective. Results indicate superior predictive accuracy and robustness, with low RMSE and MAE values for both diseases. The RF model also showed strong predictive performance, although slightly less accurate than the ANN model. The principal component analysis (PCA) identified evapotranspiration, temperature, and maximum relative humidity as the most critical variables influencing disease incidence, accounting for a significant portion of the variance in the data (87.18% for yellow rust And 88.36% for powdery mildew). The findings emphasize the importance of monitoring key meteorological variables, particularly evapotranspiration, temperature, and humidity, for effective disease prediction and management in wheat. The ANN and RF models provide valuable tools for farmers and researchers, enabling more accurate and timely predictions of disease outbreaks, which can inform proactive management strategies to mitigate the impact of these diseases on wheat production. Future research could explore integrating additional environmental factors and applying these models to other crops and regions to further enhance their predictive capabilities and generalizability. One limitation of our current methodology is the reliance on visual assessment of disease severity, which may not precisely capture the subtle or early-stage changes in disease progression and may introduce observer bias. Furthermore, while our models incorporate real-time weather data, they may not fully account for the latent period between infection and visible symptom development, potentially weakening the temporal alignment between weather events and disease severity. In future studies, integrating lagged weather variables, spore dispersal data, and high-resolution remote sensing or hyperspectral imaging for disease detection could enhance the temporal sensitivity and predictive power of disease models. Moreover, integrating mechanistic disease models with machine learning models that simulate the biological development of the pathogen in response to cumulative weather thresholds could offer a more robust framework for predicting disease outbreaks and severity.
