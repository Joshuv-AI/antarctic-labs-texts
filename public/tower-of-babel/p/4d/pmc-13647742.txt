Enhanced petroleum hydrocarbon degradation by biosurfactant-producing bacteria from Iko River estuarine sediments, Akwa Ibom State, Nigeria
Authors: Senyene Umana, Maria Bassey, Mfoniso Iwatt, Nsikak Abiaobo, Samuel Eduok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrocarbon contamination of estuaries poses a major environmental concern because of persistence and ecological toxicity, while nutrient availability often limit natural attenuation by indigenous microorganisms. This study evaluated the effects of glucose and sucrose biostimulation on petroleum hydrocarbon degradation by biosurfactant-producing bacteria from Iko River estuarine sediments, Akwa Ibom State, Nigeria, using standard physicochemical and microbiological techniques. Heavy metal concentrations of iron (1.43 mg/kg), vanadium (1.74 mg/kg), zinc (3.28 mg/kg), lead (3.01 mg/kg), cobalt (0.09 mg/kg), and nickel (0.43 mg/kg) were below sediment quality guideline thresholds. The mean heterotrophic bacterial abundance was 5.57 ± 0.9 log₁₀ CFU/g, which was 2.34 times higher than the 2.38 log₁₀ CFU/g recorded for hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria. Molecular characterization identified the isolates as Bacillus subtilis (MH145363.1), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MZ299002.1), Bacillus cereus (AJ277908.1), Micrococcus luteus (OP848034.1), and Bacillus luti (CP040336.1), showing 99.72–100% similarity to GenBank reference strains. Pseudomonas aeruginosa exhibited 64% emulsification activity and reduced surface tension to 31.2 mN/m, indicating strong biodegradation potential. Glucose supplementation increased bacterial growth, with optical density ranging from 0.15 to 1.17 at 20% glucose by days 30–40, compared to 0.10–0.20 in the control, suggesting strong stimulation of microbial proliferation. Sucrose also enhanced growth, with optical density ranging from 0.38 ± 0.01 to 1.01 ± 0.02 at 20% sucrose by day 50, compared with 0.15 ± 0.02 to 0.20 ± 0.02 in the control. Glucose stimulation at 15% improved the abundance of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, with values ranging from 5.30 ± 0.92 to 5.74 ± 0.62 log₁₀ CFU/mL by day 50, while the unstimulated control declined to 3.94 ± 0.45 log₁₀ CFU/mL at the same time point, indicating nutrient limitation. Sugar amendment enhanced hydrocarbon degradation, with the highest efficiency observed at 10% glucose, recording 59.56%, followed by 5% and 15% glucose at 57.13% and 55.03% respectively, compared to 31.11% in the control. Sucrose treatments also improved degradation, with values ranging from approximately 50% to 67%, although no clear concentration-dependent trend was observed. Simple sugar biostimulation enhanced microbial growth and hydrocarbon degradation, highlighting a cost-effective and environmentally sustainable strategy for the bioremediation of hydrocarbon-impacted estuarine environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12862-026-02561-5.Keywords: Estuarine sediment, Hydrocarbon degradation, Biosurfactant, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Bacillus sp., Biostimulation, Bioremediation

Introduction

The indiscriminate discharge of waste generated during oil exploration and production activities into aquatic ecosystem has contributed to the accumulation of recalcitrant environmental pollutants, including crude oil. This pollutant is often hydrophobic, lipophilic, chemically stable, and toxic, posing significant ecological and toxicological risks [1, 2] Crude oil is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons consisting primarily of alkanes, cycloalkanes, aromatic hydrocarbons, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), many of which are environmentally persistent and toxic [3, 4]. Alkanes, particularly low-molecular-weight compounds, can disrupt cellular membrane integrity and induce narcotic effects in aquatic organisms, whereas cycloalkanes are generally more resistant to biodegradation and may persist longer in contaminated environments than straight-chain alkanes. PAHs are of greater environmental concern because of their persistence, hydrophobicity, bioaccumulation potential, and well-documented carcinogenic, mutagenic, and teratogenic properties [5–7]. The strong adsorption of these hydrocarbons to soil and sediment particles, coupled with their low aqueous solubility, reduces bioavailability and makes remediation particularly challenging [7, 8]. Consequently, petroleum hydrocarbon contamination poses significant ecological and human health risks, and highlight the need for effective and sustainable remediation strategies.

Microbial biodegradation plays a vital role in the natural attenuation of hydrocarbon pollutants by transforming them into harmless compounds and facilitate environmental restoration [9, 10]. The efficiency of biodegradation is influenced by multiple factors, including hydrocarbon composition, concentration, nutrient availability, oxygen, pH, temperature, water content, and the number and type of indigenous microorganisms [9, 11]. Low molecular weight PAHs, such as naphthalene and phenanthrene, are relatively easier to degrade, while high molecular weight PAHs like anthracene and benzo[a]pyrene are highly recalcitrant because of low solubility and bioavailability [8]. Augmentation of the polluted environment with nutrient such as nitrogen and phosphorus enhance hydrocarbon degradation, although excessive concentrations inhibit microbial activity.

Despite the recognized potential of microbial biodegradation, the low bioavailability of hydrocarbons and limited nutrient in estuarine environments often constrain microbial growth, metabolic activity and reduce remediation efficiency [12, 13]. Biostimulation using simple sugars has emerged as a promising approach to enhance microbial proliferation and enzymatic activity to improve hydrocarbon degradation [14, 15]. Carbon sources such as glucose and sucrose modify the nutrient and physicochemical conditions of the polluted environment, increase biomass production and induce the synthesis of degradative enzymes. This study addressed critical gaps in understanding the role of simple sugar biostimulation on indigenous biosurfactant-producing bacteria in estuarine sediments for hydrocarbon degradation. While previous research has demonstrated the biodegradation potential of Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and Micrococcus species [12, 16], the combined influence of glucose and sucrose on microbial growth dynamics, biosurfactant production, and crude oil degradation in nutrient-limited estuarine ecosystem has remained underexplored. This study demonstrates how simple sugar amendments influenced microbial activity and hydrocarbon degradation efficiency, and provide insight into nutrient-driven optimization of bioremediation in estuarine sediments.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Biostimulating agents

Processed sugars (glucose and sucrose) were purchased from a laboratory supply store in Uyo, Akwa Ibom State, Nigeria. Bonny Light crude oil used was obtained from an oil production facility in the Niger Delta region of Nigeria. The glucose and sucrose concentrations were selected to evaluate the dose-dependent biostimulatory response of crude oil degrading bacteria (Pseudomonas aeruginosa) under controlled laboratory conditions.

Sediment sample

Sediment samples were collected from the Iko River in Eastern Obolo Local Government Area, Akwa Ibom State, Nigeria, using a metal grab sampler. Samples were collected from a depth of approximately 0–15 cm and transferred into sterile glass bottles. The samples were preserved in ice-packed coolers and transported to the laboratory, stored at 4 °C and analysed within 24 h of collection.

Heavy metal analysis of sediment sample

Heavy metals in the sediment were determined as described by Nkanang et al. [13]. A composite sediment sample (1 g) was transferred into a Teflon beaker and digested using a mixture of perchloric acid and nitric acid in a 1:1 ratio, followed by the addition of sulphuric acid. The mixture was heated at 200 °C for 30 min to ensure complete digestion, cooled to 28 ± 0.2 °C, and diluted to a final volume of 50 mL with distilled water. Concentrations of Fe, V, Zn, Pb, Co, and Ni were determined using an Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (Agilent AA55), with each metal measured at its respective wavelength. The instrument was calibrated using standard solutions prepared from analytical grade reagents (Merck, Germany). Heavy metal concentrations are reported as mg/kg of the net sediment weight.

Determination of microbial abundance in the sediment

The abundance of heterotrophic bacteria (HB) in the sediment was determined using the pour-plate technique as previously described by John et al. [17]. and Udosen and Umana [18], while the population of oil-degrading bacteria (ODB) was estimated using the vapour phase transfer technique [4, 19]. Appropriate dilutions of the samples were inoculated onto the selected culture media.

Nutrient agar was used for HB enumeration. Briefly, 1 g of composite sample was serially diluted and 1 mL of the appropriate dilution was aseptically transferred into sterile Petri dishes, and molten nutrient agar was poured into the plates, gently swirled to ensure even distribution, and allowed to solidify. The plates were incubated in an inverted position at 27 ± 2 °C for 24 h. Thereafter, visible colonies were counted and expressed as colony forming units per gram (CFU/g) of sample. For oil-degrading bacteria counts, aliquots of the appropriate dilutions were plated on Mineral Salt Medium (MSM) supplemented with sterilized Bonny Light crude oil as the sole carbon source. The inoculated plates were incubated at 27 ± 2 °C for 14 to 21 days. Colonies that developed on the crude-oil amended medium were counted and recorded as CFU/g of sample.

Morphological and biochemical identification of efficient crude-oil-utilizing bacterial

Pure cultures of the crude-oil utilizing isolates were characterized based on their morphological, physiological, and biochemical characteristics. Identification was performed by comparing the characteristics with standard descriptions in Bergey’s Manual of Determinative Bacteriology [20].

Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from the selected crude-oil degrading bacterial isolate using the ZR Fungal/Bacterial DNA MiniPrep™ Kit (Zymo Research, Hunan Province, China; Cat. No. D6005) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted DNA was used as template for amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene.

PCR amplification was carried out in a 25 µL reaction mixture containing PCR master mix, 0.5 µM of each primer, template DNA, and nuclease-free water. Universal bacterial primers 16 S-F (5′-GTGCCAGCAGCCGCGCTAA-3′) and 16 S-R (5′-AGACCCGGGAACGTATTCAC-3′) were used for amplification. The PCR reaction was performed in a GeneAmp 9700 thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, Massachusetts, USA) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 45 s, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 7 min.

The integrity of the genomic DNA and PCR products was confirmed by electrophoresis on 1% and 1.5% agarose gels, respectively. The expected amplicon size (~ 1500 bp) was verified by comparison with a molecular weight marker. PCR products were purified by ethanol precipitation to remove residual primers and nucleotides. The purified amplicons were rechecked on 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis at 110 V for approximately 1 h. DNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA).

Sequencing of the purified PCR products was performed using an ABI 3130xl Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA). Cycle sequencing was conducted using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Raw sequences obtained were edited and assembled using BioEdit software (version 7.2). The obtained sequences were analyzed for homology using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) available at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The nucleotide sequences were deposited in the GenBank database with accession numbers PZ585434-PZ585438.

Screening of biosurfactant-producing potential of bacterial isolates

Standardized seed inocula of the oil-degrading bacterial isolates were prepared in nutrient broth and adjusted to an optical density (OD₆₀₀) of 1.0. Thereafter, 5 mL of the seed culture was inoculated into 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 100 mL of mineral salt medium (MSM) supplemented with 2% (w/v) glucose as a readily metabolizable carbon source to enhance biosurfactant production. The cultures were incubated at 35 °C on a rotary shaker at 200 rpm for 5 days. After incubation, the cultures were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to obtain cell-free supernatant, which was used for biosurfactant screening assays. Biosurfactant production was evaluated using haemolytic activity, emulsification index (E24), drop collapse assay, oil spreading test and surface tension reduction.

Haemolytic activity

Haemolytic activity was evaluated as a preliminary screening test for biosurfactant production. Fresh bacterial cultures were streaked onto blood agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h under standard incubation conditions. The presence of clear zones (β-haemolysis) around the colonies was recorded as positive haemolytic activity, while absence of clear zones was recorded as negative.

Emulsification index (E24)

The emulsification activity of the isolates was determined following the method described by Akpan et al. [21]. Drop collapse assay

The drop collapse assay was performed using crude oil as the hydrophobic substrate as described by to Akpan et al. [21]. with slight modifications. A drop of crude oil was placed on a clean grease-free glass slide and a drop of 48-h culture supernatant was carefully added onto the oil surface. Spreading or collapse of the drop was recorded as positive for biosurfactant production.

Oil spreading test

The oil spreading technique was performed by adding 20 mL of distilled water into a clean Petri dish followed by a thin layer of crude oil on the water surface. A drop of culture supernatant was then added to the oil surface. The formation of a clear zone indicated positive biosurfactant production.

Surface tension measurement

Surface tension of the culture supernatant was measured at 24 h intervals for 5 days using a tensiometer (K11, Krüss, Germany). Isolates that reduced the surface tension of the medium to values below 35 mN/m were regarded as efficient biosurfactant producers.

Screening for crude-oil degrading potential of biosurfactants producing bacterial isolates

The crude-oil producing ability of the biosurfactants producing bacterial isolates was evaluated using the hydrocarbon overlay technique. Mineral salt agar was prepared by supplementing MSM with 15 g/L agar, sterilized, and poured into sterile Petri dishes. After solidifying, the agar surface was overlaid with 1% (v/v) sterile crude oil and allowed to equilibrate for 15–30 min. The test isolates were then streaked onto the surface of the prepared plates.

All inoculated plates were incubated at 27 ± 2 °C for 7–14 days and monitored periodically for growth. Colonies showing visible growth with clear zones were considered positive for crude oil degradation. The degradation potential was qualitatively ranked based on colony diameter and growth intensity using a grading scale of +, ++, and +++, indicating low, moderate, and high hydrocarbon-degrading potential, respectively, as described by Umana et al. [12].

In vitro evaluation of biostimulants (glucose and sucrose) effect on crude oil degradation

The effect of biostimulants (glucose and sucrose) on crude oil degradation by selected bacterial isolates from estuarine sediment was evaluated under laboratory conditions. Briefly, 2 mL of filter-sterilized crude oil was added to 100 mL of sterile mineral salt medium (MSM) in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. Each flask was inoculated with 1 mL of an active culture prepared from a 24-h nutrient broth culture of the most efficient crude-oil-degrading isolate.

Glucose and sucrose were separately added as biostimulants at concentrations of 5, 10, 15, and 20% (w/v), and the cultures were incubated aerobically on a rotary shaker at 27 ± 2 °C and 180 rpm. Each treatment was performed in triplicate (n = 3). An inoculated unstimulated control (MSM, crude oil, and bacterial inoculum without added biostimulant) was included to evaluate the effect of nutrient supplementation. An abiotic control consisting of sterile MSM and crude oil without bacterial inoculation was also included to account for non-biological hydrocarbon losses from volatilization and adsorption.

The experimental setups were monitored at 7 days intervals for 70 days to evaluate crude oil degradation. Degradation was assessed based on viable bacterial counts, turbidity and pH. Viable counts were determined using the pour plate technique, while turbidity was measured spectrophotometrically. Changes in pH were determined using a calibrated digital pH meter.

Direct assessment of petroleum hydrocarbon degradation by the test isolate

The degradation of petroleum hydrocarbons by the bacterial isolate was evaluated using extraction and column chromatographic analysis of crude oil before (abiotic control) and after microbial treatment. The methods described by Umana, et al. [1, 4, 12]. with slight modifications were followed.

Extraction of residual crude oil

After 70 days of incubation, the residual crude oil from each of the 10 Erlenmeyer flasks was extracted with dichloromethane, purified using column chromatography, and quantified in milligrams per litre (mg/L). Briefly, 100 mL of the culture broth was transferred into a separatory funnel, and 10 mL of dichloromethane was added. The mixture was vigorously shaken to allow complete extraction of hydrocarbons into the organic phase. The organic layer was collected through a chromatography column packed with glass wool, silica gel, and anhydrous sodium sulfate. Silica gel served to retain particulate debris, while anhydrous sodium sulfate was the dehydrating agent to remove residual moisture from the extract. The purified organic extract was collected into a clean vial for analysis.

Gas chromatographic analysis and quantification

Chromatographic separation and quantification of residual aliphatic hydrocarbons (C8 to C33) were performed using Gas Chromatography coupled with a Flame Ionization Detector (GC-FID), following modified guidelines of standard regulatory frameworks for extractable petroleum hydrocarbons. A 1 µL aliquot of the purified organic extract was injected using a hypodermic syringe into the GC inlet operated in splitless mode at an injection temperature of 280 °C. High-resolution separation of the petroleum fractions was achieved on a fused-silica capillary column (equivalent to a non-polar DB-5 / HP-5 phase; 30 m × 0.32 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness). Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL/min.

The optimized oven temperature program was initiated at 50 °C (held for 2 min), ramped at a rate of 10 °C/min to 300 °C, and held at 300 °C for an isothermal window of 8 min to ensure complete elution of the C33 fraction. The FID detector zone was maintained continuously at 320 °C. Hydrocarbon concentration was quantified using an external multi-point calibration curve derived from a certified C8 to C33 aliphatic alkane standard mixture, measuring total peak area integration against known mass concentrations. Final values were calculated and expressed as residual petroleum hydrocarbons in milligrams per litre (mg/L).

Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) procedures included the use of analytical-grade reagents, thoroughly cleaned glassware, instrument calibration before analysis, and the analysis of method blanks and duplicate samples to monitor contamination and analytical precision. Representative GC-FID chromatograms of the abiotic control (before biodegradation) and the treated sample (after biodegradation) are in the Supplementary information (Figures S1–S10) to illustrate changes in the hydrocarbon profile following microbial treatment.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3), and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to analysis, the data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance to confirm that the assumptions of one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were satisfied. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05, and differences were considered significant when p < 0.05, and not significant when p > 0.05. Statistical significance is reported in the text, while the figures and tables present the corresponding mean ± SD values.

Results

Heavy metal concentrations in estuarine sediment

The concentrations of heavy metals in the estuarine sediment were low: Fe (1.43 mg/kg), V (1.74 mg/kg), Zn (3.28 mg/kg), Pb (3.01 mg/kg), Co (0.09 mg/kg), and Ni (0.43 mg/kg) (Table 1). Among the metals analysed, Zn had the highest concentration, followed by Pb, V, Fe, Ni, and Co. Iron, vanadium, and cobalt were within reported natural background ranges. For metals with established guideline values, Zn, Pb, and Ni were all below the corresponding Threshold Effect Level (TEL) and Effects Range-Low (ERL) thresholds (Zn: 3.28 mg/kg vs. TEL 124 mg/kg, ERL 150 mg/kg; Pb: 3.01 mg/kg vs. TEL 30.2 mg/kg, ERL 46.7 mg/kg; Ni: 0.43 mg/kg vs. TEL 15.9 mg/kg, ERL 20.9 mg/kg).

Microbial abundance and characterization of hydrocarbon degrading bacteria in Iko River estuarine sediment

The mean heterotrophic bacterial count (THBC) was 5.57 ± 0.9 log₁₀ CFU/g and the mean hydrocarbonoclastic bacterial count (HBC) was 2.38 ± 0.5 log₁₀ CFU/g, which indicated that heterotrophic bacteria were more abundant than hydrocarbon-degrading bacteria (Fig. 1).

Bacterial abundance in Iko River estuarine sediment

A total of five hydrocarbon-degrading bacterial isolates were characterized based on their cultural, microscopic, and biochemical properties (Table 2). The isolates included both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, with rod-shaped forms predominating, while one isolate occurred as cocci in pairs. Among the isolates, three were identified as Bacillus species (Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus sp., and Bacillus cereus). These isolates were Gram-positive, rod-shaped, catalase-positive, motile, and spore-forming, with variable biochemical characteristics, including positive reactions for starch hydrolysis and citrate utilization. One isolate was identified as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a Gram-negative, rod-shaped, catalase- and oxidase-positive, motile bacterium, which exhibited variable carbohydrate utilization patterns, including acid and gas production in some sugars. The remaining isolate, identified as Micrococcus sp., was Gram-positive, cocci in pairs, catalase-positive, non-motile, and showed positive reactions for urease and citrate utilization, with differing carbohydrate fermentation profiles.

Biosurfactant producing potential of crude oil degrading bacteria

Among the isolates, Pseudomonas aeruginosa showed the highest biosurfactant production, indicated by a positive hemolytic reaction (0.3 mm), strong emulsification activity (64%), positive drop collapse and oil spreading tests, and a significant reduction of surface tension to 31.2 mN/m (Table 4). Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus luti and Micrococcus luteus reduced surface tension to 33.5 mN/m, 36.4 mN/m and 37.8 mN/m, respectively, and showed positive outcomes in the emulsification, drop collapse, and oil spreading assays. In contrast, Bacillus cereus showed no hemolytic activity, tested negative in both drop collapse and oil spreading assays, and exhibited low emulsification potential (28%), consistent with a relatively high residual surface tension of 48.6 mN/m.

Effect of biostimulants on crude oil degradation

Optical density

The impact of glucose supplementation on Pseudomonas aeruginosa culture growth was evaluated by monitoring optical density (OD) at 60 days (Fig. 2). In the control, OD remained low and relatively stable throughout the study, and ranged from 0.15 ± 0.02 at day 0 to 0.20 ± 0.01 at day 60, which indicated minimal growth. In contrast, the OD in glucose-amended cultures increased with time. At 5% glucose, OD was 0.83 ± 0.05 at day 30, which was 4.2-times higher than the control. Similarly, 10% glucose was 0.79 ± 0.01 at day 30, 3.9-times higher than the control), whereas 15% glucose showed sustained growth and peaked at 0.95 ± 0.03 on day 50 which indicated a 4.8-times increase. The growth at 20% glucose was 1.17 ± 0.02 at day 40, that was 5.8-times higher than the control. All glucose-amended treatments exhibited significantly higher OD values than the unstimulated control (p < 0.05).

Changes in growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa stimulated with glucose for hydrocarbon degradation

Sucrose amendment enhanced the growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa compared to the unstimulated control, where OD remained between 0.15 ± 0.02 and 0.20 ± 0.01. All sucrose-amended treatments exhibited significantly higher OD values than the unstimulated control (p < 0.05), with the greatest stimulation observed at 10–20% sucrose. The OD was 0.62 ± 0.03, 0.86 ± 0.03, 0.68 ± 0.05 and 1.01 ± 0.02 for 5, 10, 15 and 20% sucrose respectively at day 40. The result indicates 3.1, 4.3, 3.4- and 5.1-times higher growth relative to the control respectively (Fig. 3).

Changes in growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa stimulated with sucrose for hydrocarbon degradation

Effect of biostimulant on pH

The pH of the control remained relatively stable throughout the 60-day incubation, ranging from 5.0 ± 0.02 at day 0 to 4.8 ± 0.02 at day 60. In contrast, both glucose and sucrose induced acidification, with more pronounced decreases observed at higher sugar concentrations. For glucose-amended cultures, pH decreased progressively with increasing concentration. At 5% glucose, pH declined from 7.8 ± 0.02 to 4.7 ± 0.05 at day 40, corresponding to a 1.66-times reduction. At 10% glucose, pH decreased from 7.6 ± 0.02 to 3.9 ± 0.01, representing a 2.0-times reduction. Higher concentrations (15% and 20%) resulted in stronger acidification, with final pH values of 2.7 ± 0.01 and 2.8 ± 0.02, corresponding to approximately 2.9- and 2.8-times decreases, respectively (Fig. 4).

Changes in pH of Pseudomonas aeruginosa culture stimulated with glucose for hydrocarbon degradation

Sucrose supplementation showed a similar trend, with greater acidification at higher concentrations. At 5% sucrose, pH decreased from 7.9 ± 0.02 to 3.2 ± 0.05, while at 10% sucrose, it decreased from 8.0 ± 0.01 to 2.4 ± 0.01, corresponding to 2.5- and 3.3-times reductions, respectively. At higher concentrations (15% and 20%), final pH values were 2.5 ± 0.01 and 2.4 ± 0.07, respectively (Fig. 5).

Changes in pH of Pseudomonas aeruginosa culture stimulated with sucrose for hydrocarbon degradation

Abundance of Pseudomonas aeruginosa in culture stimulated with sugar

Glucose supplement in culture enhanced the growth of P. aeruginosa compared to the control, and the magnitude of stimulation increased with concentration. The control medium recorded 5.34 ± 0.62 log₁₀ CFU/mL on Day 0 and decreased to 3.93 ± 0.45 log₁₀ CFU/mL on day 50. At 5% glucose, bacterial abundance increased from 5.30 ± 0.92 log₁₀ CFU/mL at day 1 to 5.56 ± 0.72 log₁₀ CFU/mL at day 50, a 1.05-times higher than the control. At 10%, peak abundance of 5.70 ± 0.88 log₁₀ CFU/mL was reached at day 20 which was 1.08-times higher than the control. The 15% and 20% glucose produced 5.74 ± 0.62 log₁₀ CFU/mL and 5.60 ± 0.45 log₁₀ CFU/mL at day 50, and was 1.10 and 1.06-times respectively higher than the control (Fig. 6).

Effect of glucose stimulant on the growth of P. aeruginosa during hydrocarbon degradation

The bacterial growth stimulated by sucrose was concentration-dependent. The 5% sucrose induced viable bacterial abundance of 6.26 ± 0.77 log₁₀ CFU/mL at day 10, which was 1.18-times higher than the control. Bacterial abundance was 5.91 ± 0.00 log₁₀ CFU/mL at 10% sucrose on day 20 and indicated 1.12-times higher counts than the control. The 15% and 20% sucrose concentrations reached peak counts of 5.81 ± 0.62 log₁₀ CFU/mL at day 30 and 5.75 ± 0.33 log₁₀ CFU/mL at day 10, which represented 1.11 and 1.08-times higher abundance compared with the control, respectively (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Heavy metal concentrations and ecological risk in estuarine sediment

The low concentrations of heavy metals indicate a relatively uncontaminated sediment environment, which enhanced the activity and diversity of native microbial communities, including hydrocarbon- and cellulose-degrading bacteria. This is particularly important in aquatic environment, where microbial-mediated processes, including organic matter decomposition and pollutant degradation play a crucial role in maintaining ecosystem functioning, nutrient cycling, and ecological resilience [13]. Although Zn and Pb were the most prominent trace metals detected, the concentrations remain low and reflect minor anthropogenic inputs from industrial effluents or petroleum-related activities. Heavy metals influence microbial community composition and function. Zinc and lead, at higher concentrations exert selective pressure and induce metal-tolerant or resistant strains [22]. However, at the low concentrations detected in this study, such toxic effects are unlikely. Instead, essential trace metals, including Fe, Co, and Ni, played beneficial roles as cofactors in enzymatic reactions, support microbial metabolism and key biogeochemical processes such as hydrocarbon degradation.

Microbial distribution and identification of hydrocarbon-degrading bacteria in estuarine sediments

The high heterotrophic bacterial count compared to hydrocarbon-utilizing bacteria indicates that the microbial community was dominated by organisms capable of using a wide range of organic substrates, while only a smaller fraction possessed specialized metabolic capacity for hydrocarbon degradation. This observation is consistent with reports that hydrocarbon degraders typically constitute a subset of the heterotrophic bacterial population in contaminated environments [1, 9, 13]. The lower abundance of hydrocarbon-degrading bacteria reflects the selective requirements for hydrocarbon metabolism, including the presence of specific catabolic enzymes and pathways enabling the use of petroleum hydrocarbons as sole carbon and energy sources.

The isolation and characterization of five hydrocarbon-degrading bacteria demonstrate functional microbial diversity within the estuarine sediment. The predominance of Gram-positive, spore-forming rod-shaped isolates identified as Bacillus sp. reflects their ecological resilience in fluctuating and stress-prone environments such as estuaries. The ability to use multiple carbohydrates, including glucose, maltose, and fructose, further supports their identification as Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus cereus, and Bacillus luti during molecular analysis. The capacity to hydrolyze starch and metabolize diverse carbon sources indicates strong enzymatic potential, consistent with their known roles in the degradation of complex organic compounds such as crude oil and cellulose [1, 12, 23].

The Gram-negative rod isolate, which was catalase-, oxidase-, motility-, and citrate-positive, but negative for starch hydrolysis and spore formation, corresponds to the biochemical profile of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a metabolically versatile organism frequently associated with hydrocarbon degradation and organic matter transformation in aquatic environments [24]. Its occurrence indicates adaptation to organic pollutants commonly found in estuarine sediments.

The identification of Micrococcus sp. contributes to the diversity of the hydrocarbon-degrading consortium. Although generally less dominant in hydrocarbon mineralization, members of this genus are characterized by oxidative metabolism and tolerance to environmental stressors, including nutrient limitation, salinity fluctuations, and exposure to organic pollutants. These traits are linked to their metabolic versatility and ability to use diverse substrates [25]. Their presence indicates a potential role in organic matter turnover and nutrient recycling, and participate in hydrocarbon transformation processes [26]. This supports the view that estuarine sediments serve as reservoirs of functionally important bacteria involved in carbon cycling and environmental detoxification.

Gene sequence analysis showed high similarity (97.72–100%) with sequences in the GenBank database. The isolates were closely related to Bacillus subtilis (99.72%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (99.73%), Bacillus cereus (99.86%), Micrococcus luteus (100%), and Bacillus luti (100%). These similarity values (> 97%) confirm reliable species-level identification and indicate that Iko River estuarine sediments harbour a functionally relevant microbial community with hydrocarbon degradation potential. The predominance of Bacillus sp. is likely linked to their ability to form endospores that enhance survival under environmental stress conditions typical of estuarine systems. Members of this genus are also known to produce extracellular enzymes such as cellulases which support their involvement in organic matter degradation [14, 23]. Collectively, the dominance of Bacillus sp. together with the presence of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Micrococcus luteus, indicates a structured microbial assemblage adapted to dynamic estuarine conditions and capable of contributing to natural attenuation and hydrocarbon bioremediation.

Biosurfactant producing potential of crude oil degrading bacteria

The observed differences in biosurfactant production among the isolates suggest functional heterogeneity in their strategies for hydrocarbon utilization and environmental adaptation. Biosurfactant synthesis is a key trait that enhances hydrocarbon bioavailability by reducing surface and interfacial tension which facilitate uptake of hydrophobic substrates [27]. The presence of strong biosurfactant activity in Pseudomonas aeruginosa indicates a highly efficient hydrocarbon acquisition strategy, consistent with its ecological role as a dominant degrader in polluted aquatic systems [21, 28]. This functional advantage can contribute to its superior performance in crude oil utilization.

The moderate biosurfactant activity observed in Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus luti, and Micrococcus luteus suggests that these organisms possess auxiliary or inducible mechanisms for hydrocarbon access, which may become more effective under prolonged exposure to hydrophobic substrates [29]. Such variability is common among environmental bacteria and indicates differences in regulatory control of biosurfactant-associated genes as well as metabolic investment in extracellular production [30]. The limited biosurfactant activity associated with Bacillus cereus suggests a reduced reliance on surface-active compounds for hydrocarbon interaction, which may restrict its efficiency in environments where substrate availability is constrained by hydrophobicity. This probably indicate a preference for alternative organic substrates or a less specialized role in hydrocarbon transformation [21]. The difference in biosurfactant production highlights functional diversification within the estuarine bacterial community. This diversity likely enhanced ecosystem-level resilience, enable multiple complementary mechanisms for hydrocarbon processing, with different taxa that contribute variably to emulsification, substrate accessibility, and subsequent biodegradation processes in contaminated sediments.

Hydrocarbon degradation potential and growth dynamics of bacterial isolates from estuarine sediment

The variation in bacterial growth on crude oil over the 14-day incubation period reflects differences in hydrocarbon utilization potential among the isolates. P. aeruginosa exhibited consistently strong growth at both 7 and 14 days, consistent with its documented potential to efficiently degrade hydrocarbons through biosurfactant production and hydrocarbon-degrading enzymes [16, 21]. Micrococcus luteus also exhibited strong growth, although slightly lower than that of P. aeruginosa. The result indicates an appreciable ability to tolerate and use hydrocarbons, and support its role in the degradation of organic pollutants, albeit likely at a lower efficiency compared to primary degraders such as Pseudomonas. The Bacillus isolates showed variable growth responses. Bacillus subtilis exhibited a shift from weak growth at day 7 to moderate growth at day 14 which suggest an adaptive response and gradual acclimatization to crude oil substrates. This delayed but improved growth pattern may reflect the induction of hydrocarbon-degrading enzymes over time [30]. Bacillus cereus, however, maintained weak growth throughout the incubation period which indicate limited hydrocarbon utilizing ability under the conditions tested. In contrast, Bacillus luti showed no initial growth at day 7 but reached moderate growth by day 14, which indicate a lag phase in hydrocarbon adaptation followed by activation of metabolic pathways capable of supporting crude oil utilization. This delayed response highlights strain-dependent differences in hydrocarbon degradation efficiency within the genus Bacillus [31]. The variability in crude oil utilization among the isolates showed differences in metabolic adaptation, substrate specificity, and enzymatic capabilities [32]. P. aeruginosa emerged as the most efficient hydrocarbon degrader, with Micrococcus luteus and Bacillus luti that also showed potential for bioremediation of hydrocarbon-contaminated estuarine environments.

Effect of biostimulants on crude oil degradation

Optical density

The increase in optical density in glucose- and sucrose-amended cultures indicates that P. aeruginosa responds positively to readily available carbon sources, with both sugars that enhanced biomass accumulation relative to the control. The consistently low OD in the control suggests limited intrinsic growth under nutrient-restricted conditions and highlight the dependence of the organism on external carbon supplementation for enhanced proliferation in oligotrophic environment [4, 33]. The improved growth in glucose-amended cultures reflects efficient use of simple carbohydrates to support rapid energy generation and cellular biosynthesis. The variation in response across glucose concentrations suggests that growth was influenced not only by available substrate but also by concentration-dependent metabolic regulation, where optimal levels promote biomass accumulation while higher concentrations may affect metabolic efficiency and growth kinetics [14, 15].

A similar stimulatory pattern was observed in sucrose-amended cultures, indicating that P. aeruginosa effectively metabolize disaccharides, although with variable efficiency depending on concentration. This response suggests that carbon source complexity influences growth dynamics, because of additional enzymatic steps required for sucrose hydrolysis prior to uptake and metabolism [34]. The result demonstrates that supplementation with easily metabolizable carbon sources enhanced the growth performance of P. aeruginosa. This reflects flexible metabolic properties in using different carbohydrates, which may contribute to its ecological success in nutrient-variable environments such as estuarine sediments. The ability to respond to both simple and complex sugars also support its persistence and functional role in hydrocarbon-contaminated ecosystems where nutrient availability fluctuates. The findings indicate that sucrose stimulated microbial growth with optimal responses occurring at higher concentrations and between days 30 and 40.

pH

The pH trends indicate that carbohydrate supplementation influenced the metabolic activity of P. aeruginosa, leading to progressive acidification of the culture medium. The relatively stable pH in the control suggests limited metabolic activity in the absence of additional carbon sources and caused minimal production of acidic metabolic by-products over the incubation period [35]. Both glucose and sucrose-amended cultures showed marked decreases in pH, and indicate active carbohydrate metabolism and accumulation of acidic end products. The progressive decline in pH with increased glucose concentration suggests enhanced metabolic flux, driven by increased substrate availability [1]. However, the stronger acidification observed at higher concentrations also reflects overflow metabolism or accumulation of organic acids when carbon input exceeds cellular assimilation capacity [4, 21].

A comparable pattern observed in sucrose-amended cultures suggests that P. aeruginosa effectively metabolize both monosaccharides and disaccharides, although sucrose utilization likely requires prior hydrolysis before entry into central metabolic pathways. The pronounced acidification at higher sucrose concentrations indicates that increased substrate availability enhanced fermentative or oxidative metabolic activity and lead to greater accumulation of acidic by-products [36]. The results indicate that external carbon supplementation altered the metabolic output of P. aeruginosa, with higher sugar concentrations that promoted increased acid production and stronger acidification of the culture environment. This metabolic response shows the organism adapted to varying concentration of available carbon and highlights its capacity for intensive substrate conversion under nutrient-rich conditions. The results are consistent with previous reports which suggest that sugar supplementation induces microbial metabolism and associated physicochemical changes in culture media [14, 15]. The slightly higher initial pH in the sucrose-supplemented media reflects early metabolic activity and substrate use, while the subsequent stable pH during prolonged incubation is likely because of the reduced nutrient available and accumulated metabolic end-products.

Bacterial growth dynamics

Both glucose and sucrose were effective biostimulants for P. aeruginosa with enhanced proliferation and metabolic activity. Glucose produced more sustained stimulation, particularly at 15%, whereas sucrose induced earlier peaks that reflected faster initial metabolism. In spite of the higher sucrose concentrations (15% and 20%) that supported increased bacterial growth, the most effective hydrocarbon degradation was observed at 5% sucrose. The results suggest that optimal nutrient levels enhanced co-metabolic degradation of hydrocarbons, whereas excessive sucrose concentrations induced a preferential use of the readily available carbon source. The conditions downregulated the expression of hydrocarbon-degrading enzymes through carbon catabolite repression, and resulted in increased biomass without a corresponding high degradation efficiency. The low bacterial abundance at day 60 indicate the influence of environmental conditions such as nutrient exhaustion, metabolite and waste accumulation [33]. In addition, the results indicate that glucose was more stimulatory at 15% on day 50, whereas sucrose elicited its strongest effect at 5% on day 10. Furthermore, the type and concentration of sugar influenced the extent and timing of P. aeruginosa growth and offered valuable insights for the potential use of biostimulants in hydrocarbon-contaminated estuarine environments.

Effect of biostimulation on petroleum hydrocarbon degradation relative to controls

The inclusion of two controls (abiotic control and unstimulated control) provides a clear baseline for interpreting hydrocarbon degradation by P. aeruginosa under sugar supplementation. The abiotic control (medium + crude oil without bacteria) retained the highest hydrocarbon concentration, indicating that no measurable biological degradation occurred in the absence of microbial activity. This suggest that reductions observed in other treatments were attributable to microbial processes rather than physicochemical loss. The unstimulated control (medium + crude oil + bacteria without sugar) showed a marked reduction in total hydrocarbon concentration (31.11%) with inherent degradative ability of P. aeruginosa under nutrient-limited conditions. The result agrees with established bioremediation principles that hydrocarbon degradation is often limited by the availability of carbon sources, nitrogen, or phosphorus, and improved through biostimulation approaches [37].

Relative to this baseline, both glucose- and sucrose-amended treatments produced further reductions in residual hydrocarbon fractions across the C11–C33 range. In glucose-amended cultures, degradation efficiency ranged from 47.46% to 59.56%, with the highest value recorded at 10% glucose (59.56%), followed by 5% (57.13%) and 15% (55.03%). However, degradation decreased at higher glucose concentration (47.46%), suggesting that excessive glucose may reduce hydrocarbon utilization. This reduction is attributed to preferential metabolism of glucose over hydrocarbons, a phenomenon associated with carbon catabolite repression. The degradation levels in this study (47.46–59.56%) fall within the commonly reported range of approximately 40–70% hydrocarbon degradation for single bacterial strains under laboratory biostimulation conditions [1, 4, 21].

Sucrose supplementation also enhanced hydrocarbon degradation, with values of 66.81% and 63.36% at 5% and 10% concentrations, respectively. Higher sucrose concentrations resulted in reduced efficiency, with 54.44% and 50.98% at 15% and 20%, respectively. The enhanced performance at lower sucrose concentrations suggests that moderate nutrient supplementation promoted co-metabolic degradation, whereas excessive sugar levels downregulated hydrocarbon utilization. The degradation efficiency (50.98–66.81%) observed under sucrose stimulation is comparable with previous reports on hydrocarbon biodegradation by P. aeruginosa [1, 38].

Although glucose and sucrose effectively enhanced petroleum hydrocarbon degradation under controlled laboratory conditions, the concentrations in this study were selected to evaluate the dose-dependent biostimulatory response of P. aeruginosa and may not directly represent concentrations suitable for field-scale application. In environmental settings, excessive additions of readily biodegradable sugars could increase biochemical oxygen demand, reduce dissolved oxygen availability, alter the composition of indigenous microbial communities, and promote the proliferation of non-target microorganisms. These ecological trade-offs may compromise remediation efficiency and ecosystem functioning if carbon amendments are not carefully managed. Therefore, optimized carbon dosage and available oxygen under environmentally realistic conditions is essential to apply this biostimulation strategy at the field scale.

Fractional analysis showed that intermediate hydrocarbons (C12–C24) were more effectively degraded than heavier fractions (C25–C33), which persisted across most treatments. This persistence is attributed to higher hydrophobicity, lower solubility, and reduced bioavailability, which limit microbial access. This pattern is consistent with established biodegradation trends where shorter and mid-chain alkanes are more readily degraded than long-chain hydrocarbons [2, 39]. Enhanced degradation in sugar-amended treatments is attributed to increased microbial biomass, stimulation of biosurfactant production, and improved hydrocarbon bioavailability. In addition, P. aeruginosa produces rhamnolipid biosurfactants [21], which facilitate hydrocarbon emulsification and uptake for enhanced degradation in nutrient-amended systems.

Conclusion

The estuarine sediments from the Iko River harbour diverse indigenous bacteria with varying potential for hydrocarbon degradation and biosurfactant production. The low concentrations of heavy metals in the sediment indicate a minimal ecological risk and suggest limited anthropogenic inputs of heavy metal-containing waste. As a consequence, the sediment environment probably remained conducive to microbial metabolic activities involved in natural bioremediation processes. The isolation of metabolically versatile species such as Bacillus subtilis, P. aeruginosa, Bacillus cereus, Micrococcus luteus and Bacillus luti indicates the ecological importance of estuarine microbial communities in organic matter turnover and hydrocarbon degradation. Nutrient limitation is a key factor that hinder efficient biodegradation evidenced by the low growth in the control. Biostimulation with glucose and sucrose enhanced microbial growth, biosurfactant production and hydrocarbon degradation which indicates that carbon supplementation optimized microbial remediation processes. The high degradation efficiency at low sucrose concentrations suggests that moderate nutrient amendment promoted co-metabolic degradation, whereas excessive nutrient levels favoured biomass increase and down regulated degradation activity. The findings highlight the effect of simple and readily available carbon sources such as glucose and sucrose as cost-effective biostimulants to enhance microbial degradation of hydrocarbon, and provide valuable baseline information for the development of nutrient-based bioremediation strategies for petroleum-contaminated ecosystem.
