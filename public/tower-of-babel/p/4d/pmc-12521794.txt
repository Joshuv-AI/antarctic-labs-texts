Congenital Nasal Choristoma (Hairy Polyp) in a Term Neonate: A Case Report and Review of the Literature
Authors: Surasak Puvabanditsin, Ali G Saad, Arun Sangam, Anthony Poltronetti, Su Young Park, Amrryn Halari, Rajeev Mehta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe describe the case of a term Hispanic male neonate with a nasal septum/inferior turbinate choristoma. The presented case report includes a review of the historical, radiological, surgical, and histological features, and the literature on this entity. The lesion was identified in an asymptomatic infant and evaluated using flexible nasal endoscopy and MRI. The lesion was successfully surgically excised. Nasal septal/inferior turbinate choristoma is a rare, benign congenital mass that can vary in presentation depending on its size, location, and growth pattern.Keywords: choristoma, congenital, dermoid, hairy polyp, nasal polyp

Summary

Congenital nasal choristomas (hairy polyps), though rare and potentially asymptomatic, should be considered in neonates with nasal masses.

Introduction

The so‐called “hairy polyps” are mature bigerminal lesions that have sparked debate and curiosity among developmental biologists and physicians. These lesions have been most frequently documented in the nasopharyngeal area where they may manifest as a pedunculated polypoid mass at birth or early childhood and may be referred to as an otolaryngologic choristoma [1]. By definition, a “choristoma” is an accumulation of developing polygerminal tissue in an anatomical region where it does not belong. This name is often used in conjunction with “hamartoma” if such tissue is present in the mother's organ system. Choristomas can occur anywhere in the body, yet they are poorly understood and seldom investigated [1].

The terms choristoma, hairy polyp, and dermoid are often used interchangeably in the literature [2]. Histologically, they consist of mature stratified squamous epithelium with skin appendages, such as eccrine sweat glands, sebaceous glands, hair follicles (ectoderm), and mesodermal and ectodermal derivatives that are foreign to the nasal cavity, as in our case. They are the most primitive form of a teratoma [3]. Although the pathophysiology of choristoma polyps is unclear, there are a few possibilities. The nasal choristoma polyps are irregular masses located in unusual locations different from their usual placements. They may be derived from normal multifunctional tissue. Conversely, these nasal polyps are perhaps embryonic abnormalities due to aberrant first and second‐branchial arch embryogenesis [4]. Opinions regarding whether they are essentially a component of a family of neoplastic disorders or a range of well‐described congenital defects such as developmental aberrations or teratomas, currently, the literature is divided. We report a term male newborn with a nasal choristoma polyp and present a literature review.

Case Presentation

A 3670‐g male neonate was delivered by spontaneous vaginal delivery at 39 + 1 weeks of gestation to an 18‐year‐old Hispanic G2P1011 mother with an uncomplicated prenatal course. APGAR scores were 9 and 9 at 1 and 5 min, respectively. Physical examination revealed a mass completely obstructing the right nasal orifice, protruding through the nostril and extending onto the upper lip (Figure 1). No other anomalies were observed. The infant appeared comfortable and showed no signs of respiratory distress. However, the mother reported some difficulty during breastfeeding on the right breast, as the infant's only patent nostril (the left) would become compressed against the breast.

(a, b) Photos showing a broad‐based nasal polyp (1.3 × 1.1 cm) in the right nostril.

Examination/Investigation (Methods)

Non‐contrast CT of the facial bones revealed a 5 mm soft tissue mass in the right anterior nasal cavity. On day of life 7, nasal endoscopy was performed under general anesthesia. It revealed a broad‐based polyp containing cartilage. The lesion involved the anterior border of the inferior turbinate, extended superiorly to the upper nasal septum, and inferiorly to the mid‐nasal floor. The anterior right columella and nasal sill were also affected. A polypectomy was performed (Figure 2).

Post‐operative photo of the right nostril after the excision of 1.3 × 1.1 × 0.4 cm polyp with broad base involving right columella, nasal sill, superior portion of nasal septum, inferior mid‐nasal floor, and anterior border of inferior turbinate.

The patient was discharged at 9 days of age. He was doing well at 12‐month follow‐up.

Conclusion and Results

The histopathological examination revealed: a 1.3 × 1.1 × 0.4 cm polyp consisting of squamous cell epithelium, hair follicles, and sebaceous glands. A light microscopic examination of the nasal polyp revealed a squamous epithelium of ectodermal origin with adnexal structures (hair follicles, sebaceous glands) of mesodermal origin (Figure 3). The presence of the above‐mentioned adnexal structures distinguishes a dermoid from an epidermoid cyst, which does not have the same potential for transcranial extension. Unlike teratomas, choristomas (hairy polyps) do not contain any components of endodermal origin.

(a–c) A classic example of histopathology of a hairy polyp (Choristoma) showing mature tissue elements of both ectodermal and mesodermal origin, including stratified squamous epithelium (ectodermal origin), skin adnexa (mesodermal origin) (hair follicle, sebaceous glands), fibro‐adipose tissue, and muscle fibers [hematoxylin and eosin, ×100].

Discussion

Congenital nasal polyps are rare. A recent publication stated that 41 cases have been reported in 28 reports between January 2000 and October 2021 [5] (Table 1). For an easier differential diagnosis, management, and treatment plan, they can be classified into two groups. In the first group, the polyp originates from intranasal structures. The second group consists of an extra nasal polyp spreading from adjacent structures into the nasal cavity and presenting as a nasal polyp.

In infants, intranasal masses commonly present with respiratory distress and feeding difficulties. Prompt airway evaluation is essential. Management requires a multidisciplinary approach involving neonatology, otolaryngology, neuroradiology, and genetics. The complex germ‐layer lesions of the naso‐oropharynx—referred to as “dysontogenetic nasopharyngeal tumors”—were historically classified by Arnold (1870) into four types: dermoids, teratoids, teratomas, and epignathi [7]. Dermoids, including hairy polyps and choristomas, are bigerminal (ectoderm and mesoderm) lesions. They typically consist of skin or mucosal germ layer tissues covered with epidermal appendages, with the matrix formed of predominantly fatty tissue. In the literature, the terms dermoids, hairy polyps, and choristoma are often used interchangeably and they have been described as the most primitive form of teratoma, and some may have a stalk connecting with intracranial contents [1, 2, 8]. A choristoma is a more general term for a benign malformation consisting of derivatives of ectoderm (epithelium, hair follicles, sebaceous and sweat glands) and mesoderm (fibro‐adipose tissue, cartilage, and muscle fibers) found in an abnormal location in the body. Hairy polyp is specifically a choristoma found in the nasopharynx or oropharynx, characterized by its hairy appearance due to skin appendages like hair follicles. Dermoid is a specific type of choristoma that is formed by misplaced ectoderm, composed of epidermal and dermal structures including squamous epithelium and adnexal structures of mesodermal origin (i.e., hair follicles, sebaceous glands, and sweat glands). All dermoids are choristomas, but not all choristomas are dermoids; think of dermoid as skin‐based choristoma. Choristomas (Hairy Polyp) have been reported to be associated with other congenital abnormalities such as cleft palate, uvula dysplasia, auricular deformities such as microtia and low‐set ears, ankyloglossia, facial hemihypertrophy, hypospadias, left carotid artery atresia, osteopetrosis, bifurcation of the tongue, and branchial arch anomalies [8]. Teratoids, teratomas, and epignathi are trigerminal, containing derivatives of all three germ layers. Teratoids are poorly differentiated with immature tissue, whereas teratomas consist of mature tissue with histologically recognizable true organoid structures. Epignathi are mature tissue lesions, with a highly differentiated organoid presentation, often resembling parasitic fetuses (fetus‐in‐fetu). They share an axial alignment with the host and are generally incompatible with life [7].

Choristoma (hairy polyp) is the most common congenital tumor of the naso‐oropharynx, first described in 1784 [9] and later characterized by Brown‐Kelly in 1918 [10]. Its estimated prevalence is approximately 1 in 40,000 live births [11, 12], with a strong female predominance. Although typically identified in neonates, adult presentations are very rare, with only five cases reported in the last 25 years, two of which occurred in 2013 [13, 14, 15, 16]. Incidence beyond age 20 is considered unusual [17]. Approximately 37% of the cases involve newborns, with over half of them presenting at delivery or on the first day of life. In the pre‐PubMed period, Kelly et al. [18] reported that over 50% of these lesions manifested during infancy. However, our findings indicate this figure is closer to 36%, with neonates accounting for almost 73% of cases. This supports the theory that they are predominantly developmental abnormalities, as evidenced by a reported case of pregnancy termination following prenatal diagnosis of a hairy polyp [19], although this does not adequately explain their rare adult onset. Though suggestions have been proposed, the exact embryologic origin of naso‐oropharyngeal choristomas remains uncertain.

Clinically, these lesions often present within the first month of life with obstructive symptoms, including respiratory distress and feeding difficulties. Lesion distribution is as follows: 60% in the nasopharynx, 25% in the tonsil or tonsillar pillars, and 15% in the palate. Hairy polyps are 6.5 times more likely to occur on the left side and are 3.5–5 times more common in females. Unlike teratomas, they are not carcinogenic, with only one documented case of recurrence after surgical excision [2]. Complete surgical excision remains the standard of care. For neonates with suspected obstructing nasopharyngeal masses, both nasal and transoral endoscopy are considered the gold standard for diagnosis.

In conclusion, we described a congenital choristoma (hairy polyp), which is a bigerminal mass derived from ectodermal and mesodermal germ layers in a term neonate. Naso‐oropharyngeal hairy polyps are more frequently seen in female neonates and may present with respiratory obstruction and feeding difficulties and can be life‐threatening. In our case report, we have highlighted the clinical characteristics of bigerminal choristomas of the nasal vestibule and discussed their origin, morphogenesis, workup, and management.
