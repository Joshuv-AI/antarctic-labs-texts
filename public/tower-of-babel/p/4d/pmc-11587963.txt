Scorpionism in a fibromyalgia patient: First report
Authors: Jozélio Freire de Carvalho
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

Scorpionism is a common animal envenomation. In Brazil, in the period between 2015 and 2019, 615,631 cases of scorpionism were reported, with 645 deaths by SINAN, a Brazilian Information System for Notifiable Diseases, including animals.[1] The clinical picture of scorpion envenomation usually is a mild picture that includes local pain and paresthesia. However, there are descriptions of moderate and severe cases following this envenomation, including tachycardia, hypertension, hypotension, bradycardia, nausea, vomiting, myocarditis, and acute pulmonary edema secondary to heart failure.[2] The severity of pain may vary from mild to severe, needing analgesics, opioids, and local anesthetic blockade. It would be interesting to know the evolution of scorpionism in patients with chronic pain, like fibromyalgia (FM). The literature searching could not find any previous case of a patient with FM who suffered from scorpionism. The purpose of this article is to report an FM patient who suffered a scorpionism.

A 37-year-old female had a history of systemic arterial hypertension and fibromyalgia for 5 years. She was under amytriptiline 50 mg/day. She suffered a scorpion bite in her house. Although she did not bring the insect or any picture of it to the emergency department (ED), she reported that she saw it. She arrived at the hospital 1 hour later with severe local pain, with no systemic features. Her vital signs were normal. Local antisepsis was done, and she received intravenous analgesics, and a local anesthetic blockade was performed. She evolved with complete resolution of the pain and was discharged 6 hours later. After 24 hours, the patient returned to the ED, and she was very anxious with severe pain and paresthesia until the shoulder. Her systemic physical examination was unremarkable, with a blood pressure of 140 × 80 mmHg, a heart rate (HR) of 80 bpm, a respiratory rate (RR) of 16 ipm, an axillar temperature of 36.5°C, and peripheral oxygen saturation of 99% (ambient air). She had a local ecchymosis secondary to the previous blockage. Neurological examination demonstrated only increased levels of pain (visual analogic scale of 10; minimum 0, maximum 10). She was very preoccupied about the scorpionism. She received analgesic and local blockage again and was informed regarding the scorpion accident. She evolved with complete resolution of the clinical picture and was discharged.

The most common manifestation in scorpion envenomation is local pain and paresthesia.[2] The pain may vary from mild and moderate to severe with the need for anesthetic blockage as used in our case. Interestingly, the patient became asymptomatic after analgesics and anesthetics, and after 24 hours, she returned with pain. We believe that the patient read about the scorpionism and it induced to feel the symptoms again. FM is a good prototype of nociplastic or functional pain. It is characterized by hyperalgesia and allodynia, inconsistency and reversibility, and dynamic cross-system interactions with biological and psychobehavioral factors, as observed in our patient who returned to have pain after 24 h of becoming asymptomatic.[3] Scorpionism may be an example of an external agent that may worsen FM symptoms. On course of mild and moderate cases, in severe cases of scorpion sting, several systemic and serious signs and symptoms may develop in these patients. It was therefore a stressor for FM worsening. Furthermore, FM patients have catastrophizing thoughts. The education for her was crucial to solving the case, besides pain control.
