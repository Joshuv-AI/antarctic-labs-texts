Efficient bidirectional quantum frequency conversion between telecom and visible bands using adaptively phase-matched III-V nanophotonic waveguides
Authors: Jierui Hu, Hao Yuan, Joshua Akin, A K M Naziul Haque, Yunlei Zhao, Kejie Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantum frequency conversion (QFC) is essential for interfacing quantum systems operating at different wavelengths and for realizing scalable quantum networks. Despite extensive progress, achieving QFC with simultaneous high efficiency, low pump power, minimal noise, broad bandwidth, and pump-wavelength flexibility remains challenging. Here, we demonstrate efficient, low-noise, and bidirectional QFC between the telecom (1550-nm) and visible (780-nm) bands using unpoled indium gallium phosphide (InGaP) χ(2) nanophotonic waveguides, eliminating the need for a long-wavelength pump. Leveraging the large nonlinear susceptibility of InGaP together with adaptive phase-matching control, we obtain 27% (55%) internal efficiency in a 6-mm (2.5-mm)-long waveguide with a low pump power of 20 mW (50 mW) and a noise flux spectral density of 10−4 counts per second per hertz at signal–pump detuning of 20 nm. These results mark a significant advance in integrated nonlinear photonics for high-performance QFC, facilitating the development of versatile and scalable quantum networks.Subject terms: Nonlinear optics, Quantum optics

Introduction

Future quantum networks will rely on interconnected quantum nodes composed of matter-based qubits and optical fiber links that transmit quantum information via single photons. Quantum frequency conversion (QFC) enables photons to shift between wavelengths—for example, bridging the telecom and visible bands—while preserving essential quantum properties such as coherence and entanglement1–3. As a result, low-noise, high-efficiency QFC is indispensable for scalable quantum networks and hybrid quantum systems4–6. QFC also facilitates single-photon detection in otherwise inaccessible spectral ranges, such as the infrared7,8, where direct detection is difficult or requires complex conditions. Beyond quantum technologies, frequency conversion finds wide-ranging applications in telecommunications, spectroscopy, and laser systems.

QFC is typically realized through nonlinear optical processes driven by ancillary pumps, including sum-frequency generation (SFG)9–13 and four-wave mixing14–16, using χ(2) or χ(3) nonlinearities. While many demonstrations have relied on bulk nonlinear crystals17–20 or optical fibers21, integrated photonic platforms now offer a promising route to efficient QFC through chip-scale devices with strong mode confinement14,22–24. However, χ(3) nonlinearities are inherently weak and demand high pump powers, which in turn generate excessive noise. While optical resonators reduce pump power requirements, they introduce narrow operating bandwidths, limited wavelength tunability, and light-extraction challenges that limit overall end-to-end efficiency14–16,22,25–29. QFC using χ(2) nonlinearities has relied largely on periodically poled materials to realize quasi-phase matching, but poling disorder introduces statistically white noise from spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) of the pump30–32. This necessitates long-wavelength pumps31,33 and often multistage conversion schemes34,35, leading to degraded performance and increased system complexity. On the other hand, demonstrations of linear frequency conversion in unpoled χ(2) materials have been rare and, to date, limited to the classical regime with modest efficiencies25,26,28. Thus, achieving QFC with simultaneous high efficiency, low pump power, minimal added noise, broad bandwidth, and pump-wavelength flexibility remains an open challenge.

Here, we demonstrate high-performance QFC between the telecom (1550-nm) and visible (780-nm) bands using unpoled indium gallium phosphide (InGaP) χ(2) nanophotonic waveguides with adaptive phase-matching control, simultaneously addressing multiple critical requirements for QFC. The III–V semiconductor InGaP platform36–42 offers a large second-order nonlinear susceptibility (χ(2) = 220 pm/V), low optical loss, and a narrow Raman spectrum43, while employing noninvasive modal phase matching. We leverage these features to reduce pump power demands, suppress parasitic noise, and relax constraints on pump-wavelength configuration of QFC. We further implement adaptive phase-matching control to compensate thin-film nonuniformities and optimize both SFG and difference-frequency generation (DFG) for bidirectional conversion. Our device achieves low pump power for high conversion efficiencies among non-resonant QFC implementations. Importantly, the on-chip noise flux spectral density remains at a low level of 10−4 counts per second per hertz (cps/Hz)—even given the spectral proximity of the pump and signal—preserving the coherence and entanglement of input photons in our experiment. By combining efficiency, low power, bidirectionality, and noise suppression in a scalable integrated photonic platform, our approach addresses a critical need for advancing the development of versatile and hybrid quantum networks.

Results

Unpoled χ(2) waveguide with adaptive phase matching

The InGaP nanophotonic waveguide is designed to support the modal-phase-matched 1550-nm-band transverse electric (TE00) mode and 780-nm-band transverse magnetic (TM00) mode (Fig. 1). QFC between these modes occurs via SFG or DFG with an ancillary 1550-nm-band pump, satisfying the frequency and phase-matching conditions ω1550 + ωp = ω780 and k1550 + kp = k780, where ω1550 and k1550 denote the angular frequency and wavevector of the 1550-nm-band light, respectively, and similarly for the 780-nm-band light and pump. In the weak-pump limit, the simulated normalized SFG efficiency is ηSFG≡P780P1550PpL2=520,000%/W/cm2 for a lossless, perfectly phase-matched InGaP nanophotonic waveguide.

However, one major challenge in utilizing integrated photonic waveguides for χ(2) nonlinear processes lies in the thickness nonuniformity of the thin-film material44,45, which results in accumulated phase mismatch along the waveguide length and thus degraded conversion efficiency. To overcome this, we developed an in situ phase-matching tuning technique, using a nanoheater array integrated adjacent to the waveguide (Fig. 1b) (see the Supplementary Information Note 1 for device fabrication). By precisely controlling the temperature of individual waveguide segments with a voltage of only up to a few volts, we can counteract the effect of thickness variations and correct the phase-matching condition along the full waveguide length (Supplementary Note 4). The nanoheater array is programmable through the applied voltage to optimize the phase-matching conditions for different nonlinear processes and different wavelengths.

Optimized SFG and DFG

Figure 2a shows the SFG spectra of a 6-mm-long waveguide under a 1550-nm pump before and after phase-matching optimization, resulting in a near-sinc2 SFG transfer function at 1533 nm with a bandwidth of 31 GHz and a 3.2–fold improvement in conversion efficiency for the spectral peak. The tuning voltages of the array of 15 nanoheaters for the optimization are shown in the inset. The measured SFG conversion efficiency versus pump power after phase-matching optimization with a signal power Ps = 2.3 μW is displayed in Fig. 2b. The internal conversion efficiency is determined by η=PSFG/η780Psη1550ℏωsℏωSFG, where Ps (PSFG) is the off-chip signal (SFG) power and η1550(η780) is the fiber coupling efficiency for 1550-nm (780-nm) band light. The uncertainty of the measured conversion efficiency can arise from the fiber-coupling efficiency. We use adiabatically tapered fiber couplers with average efficiencies of 80% and 50% for the 1550-nm TE00 and 780-nm TM00 modes, respectively. The measured peak internal (external) conversion efficiency is 27% (11%), limited by the loss of the 780-nm TM00 mode of approximately 20 dB/cm (Supplementary Note 4), whereas the loss of the 1550-nm TE00 mode is approximately 1 dB/cm. This is consistent with the measured quality factor of 780-nm-band microring resonances (Q780 ~ 105)38 and a simulated group index of ng = 5.7 for the 780-nm TM00 mode (i.e., β = ngω/cQ). The experimentally inferred ηSFG = 70, 000%/W/cm2, by fitting the conversion efficiency curve via Eq. (1), is lower than the theoretical value, leading to a higher pump power (~20 mW) to reach the peak efficiency. This discrepancy arises from the residual phase mismatch at length scales shorter than those of individual nanoheaters and can be improved with more discrete nanoheaters (Supplementary Note 4).

Owing to the different waveguide losses for the 1550-nm and 780-nm modes and residual phase mismatch, the conversion efficiency of DFG with the same pump direction as the SFG is different from that of the SFG process and must be separately optimized. In contrast, the conversion efficiencies of DFG and SFG with opposite pump directions are the same due to time reversal (as highlighted in Supplementary Fig. 3). Fig. 2c shows the DFG spectra under 1532-nm pump before and after its phase-matching optimization for the same 6-mm waveguide and same pump direction as SFG, with a 2.1–fold improvement in conversion efficiency for the spectral peak. Fig. 2d shows the measured DFG conversion efficiency versus pump power after optimization, where the off-chip input signal power is 24μW. The peak efficiency and the corresponding pump power are both similar to those of the independently optimized SFG process.

To explore scaling behavior, we measured devices of different lengths. A 2.5-mm-long phase-matching-optimized waveguide exhibits approximately double the peak internal (external) conversion efficiency of the 6-mm device to 55% (22%), which is consistent with the exponential scaling with the waveguide loss (Fig. 2e), and a bandwidth of 50 GHz. The pump power (50 mW) required for peak efficiency does not follow the 1/L2 trend though, due to less phase mismatch accumulation in the short 2.5-mm device with inferred ηSFG = 175,000%/W/cm2 (Supplementary Note 6). This deviation is illustrated by the gray band in Fig. 2e, which plots pump power versus waveguide length for a range of ηSFG corresponding to various phase mismatches. We also measured the on-chip noise in the 1550-nm band of the 6-mm waveguide without signal injection (Supplementary Note 5), which is linear in pump power and reaches approximately 10−4 cps/Hz at a 20-mW pump power and at the wavelength separated from the pump by ±20 nm (∓2.5 THz) (Fig. 2f). The corresponding up-converted noise flux density is approximately 2 × 10−5 cps/Hz according to the calculation (Supplementary Note 5). For comparison, the signal flux spectral density of the entangled-photon source used in the up-conversion experiment is approximately 2 × 10−3 cps/Hz, which, nevertheless, is limited by the system efficiency. The linear noise is likely attributed to spontaneous Raman scattering of the pump in the waveguide (see the Supplementary Note 5 for the modeling). We note recent demonstrations of QFC between 1550 nm and 780 nm wavelengths using bulk poled waveguides and a long-wavelength pump exhibit comparable or lower noise background46–49 (10−6−10−5 cps/Hz), which might be due to different inherent material properties, large mode sizes, and larger signal-pump detuning. We also observed that, at wavelengths within approximately 8 nm of the pump, the noise scales quadratically with the pump power, which originates from the degenerate spontaneous four-wave mixing of the pump (Supplementary Note 5). Moreover, the QFC wavelength can be tuned by varying the pump wavelength or device temperature37, with a simulated SFG phase-matching wavelength range of >1000 nm for the fundamental mode and >30 nm for the SFG mode for the fabricated waveguide (Supplementary Note 3).

Single-photon up-conversion

We demonstrate that bidirectional QFC in InGaP nanophotonic waveguides preserves both quantum entanglement and single-photon coherence. First, we verify that the time–energy entanglement of photon pairs generated via SPDC is preserved after one photon is up-converted. The experimental setup is depicted in Fig. 3a. A commercial periodically poled LiNbO3 waveguide, pumped by a 780-nm-band continuous-wave laser, generates time–energy entangled photons in the telecommunications band. Non-degenerate signal and idler photons are spectrally selected via a tunable fiber Fabry–Perot filter with a 2.3-GHz bandwidth. The signal photon is coupled into the InGaP waveguide and up-converted to the 780-nm band via SFG. The signal photon rate before coupling into the device is approximately 4.3 Mcps. The up-converted signal rate and up-converted noise rate in the SFG bandwidth after the device are 151 kcps and 16 kcps, respectively. We perform time-resolved two-photon interference measurements to verify the time–energy entanglement. We first confirm the entanglement of the unconverted SPDC photons via a pair of glass Mach–Zehnder interferometers (MZIs) with a path delay of 1 ns, which is much longer than the coherence time of the SPDC photons. The phase of the MZI is controlled by temperature. Fig. 3b shows the two-photon interference fringe obtained by varying the temperature of one MZI, yielding a visibility of 83%, above the Clauser–Horne limit of 1/250. After up-conversion, the two-photon interference is measured via one MZI for the idler and one free-space Michelson interferometer for the up-converted signal. Fig. 3c shows the resulting fringe, and time–difference histograms for two interferometer phases are plotted in Fig. 3d. The visibility remains at 76%, confirming that entanglement is preserved after QFC. The two-photon fringe visibility before and after QFC are both limited primarily by the interferometer visibility. Here, the inherent time-filtering of the coincidence detection enhances the signal-to-noise ratio of the measurement, which surpasses the signal-to-noise ratio of the up-converted photon flux itself.

Single-photon down-conversion

In the down-conversion direction, we verify the preservation of single-photon coherence for a time-bin qubit. The experimental setup is depicted in Fig. 4a. A 780-nm-band time-bin qubit with a temporal delay τ = 1 ns is created by second-harmonic generation of an attenuated 1550-nm laser pulse (0.43 ns), followed by passing through a phase-tunable unbalanced Michelson interferometer. Each pulse contains approximately one photon before coupling into the device. The qubit is converted to the 1550-nm band via DFG in the InGaP waveguide. After the residual pump is filtered out, quantum state tomography is performed on the down-converted qubit (Supplementary Note 4). Figures 4b, c show the reconstructed density matrices for the +- and R-like states, before and after conversion. Fig. 4d summarizes the fidelity F=⟨ψi∣ρf∣ψi⟩ for six basis states—e, l, +, −, L, and R—with an average fidelity of F¯=96.2±0.6%. Noise in the bandwidth of the pulse is retained in the calculation of the fidelity. The dominant contribution to infidelity arises from limited interferometer visibility and phase errors, with uncertainty contributed from phase fluctuations and photon shot noise (Supplementary Note 4).

Discussion

In summary, we have demonstrated a high-performance QFC platform using unpoled nanophotonic waveguides. The combination of strong second-order nonlinearity, noninvasive modal phase matching, and adaptive phase-matching control enables high internal conversion efficiency with low pump power and minimal added noise. Figure 5 compares the peak internal conversion efficiency and corresponding pump power of non-resonant χ(2)-based QFC demonstrations. While the limited operating bandwidth of a cavity can be mitigated using thermal heaters to tune its operation wavelength14–16,22,25–28, non-resonant waveguides support broad bandwidths and can be continuously tuned over wide wavelength ranges, which significantly facilitates matching specific operating wavelengths within quantum networks. While heterogeneously integrated lasers have been developed for currently popular nanophotonic platforms, including silicon nitride and LiNbO3, the III–V semiconductor platform offers a potentially more straightforward way to integrate pump lasers with the QFC component toward a turn-key system56 and further the integration of quantum dot single-photon sources to serve as a monolithic quantum light source in the telecom band for quantum communication57. Furthermore, the cutoff wavelength of InGaP (~650 nm) is compatible with a wide range of quantum emitters38, including quantum dots, color centers, and neutral atoms, making this platform promising for facilitating scalable and versatile quantum networks and hybrid quantum systems.
