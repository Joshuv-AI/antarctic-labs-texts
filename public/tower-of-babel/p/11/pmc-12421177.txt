Perceived risk of human papillomavirus infection and cervical cancer: a qualitative study among Ethiopian women
Authors: Tadesse Fikre Lema, Annah Mosalo, Mary Moleki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe main objective of this study was to explore and describe perceived risk of women towards Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection and cervical cancer (CC) in Adama, Ethiopia. Perception of women towards CC screening was also investigated.DesignA qualitative exploratory, descriptive and contextual research design was employed. The health belief model (HBM) theoretical framework guided the study. Data were collected through focus group discussions (FGDs) using open-ended, unstructured questions and analysed using a theory-driven thematic analysis with the use of the key constructs of the HBM.SettingThe study was conducted from 1 to 30 October 2023 at three selected public health facilities in Adama, where CC screening service is being offered.ParticipantsThirty (10 from each public health facility) 25–49 years women were purposefully selected and participated in the FGDs until data saturation was reached. Information-rich women, believed to have some awareness about the topics to be discussed and could have the ability to clearly articulate their experience during the FGDs, were selected.ResultThere was low perceived susceptibility but high perceived severity of HPV infection and CC. Early detection of symptoms, getting treated in time and prevention of CC were the major perceived benefits recognised in the study. Fear of the result and negative influence from peers were perceived barriers identified. With regard to self-efficacy, all the participants had full confidence to be screened. Participants’ cues to action include recommendation from health workers and perceived bad experiences previously happened on others.ConclusionsThere was low perceived susceptibility but high perceived severity of HPV infection and CC. Awareness creation campaigns about HPV infection and CC are crucial.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the Papovaviridae family. Currently, there are over 218 different types of HPVs that have been isolated and found to infect human beings.1 Around 45 different HPV types have been linked to genital tract infections, while others cause skin diseases. HPV is a common sexually transmitted infection worldwide affecting at least 50% of sexually active individuals of both sexes at some point during their life.2 3 Based on the clinical prognosis of their associated lesions, HPVs can be divided into three subtypes: high-risk types (HR-HPVs) (HPV-16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 68), probably high-risk types (PHR-HPV) (HPV-26, 53, 66, 69, 73, 82), and low-risk types (LR-HPV) (HPV-6, 11, 40, 42, 43, 44, 54, 61, 70).4 5 HPVs are known to be implicated in the development of about 5% of all human cancers. They are now a well-established cause of cervical cancer (CC), and there is growing evidence of HPVs being relevant factors in other anogenital as well as head and neck cancers.6 7 Most women with HPV infections do not have any symptoms and do not go on to get cancer.8 However, some women will experience persistent infection with certain HPV genotypes that go on to cause abnormalities in infected cells. These changes are called ‘precancerous’ because they can develop into invasive CC. HPV-16 and HPV-18 genotypes together are responsible globally for 71% of cases of CC.9 More specifically, 60.6% of cases are attributed to HPV-16 and 10.2% to HPV-18.

The Catalan Institute of Oncology (ICO)/International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) Information Centre on HPV and Cancer (HPV Information Centre) World Summary Report 2023 estimates indicate that every year 604 127 women are diagnosed with CC and 341 831 women died from the disease in 2020. Annual estimates of new CC cases in the African continent reach 117 316 per 100 000 women per year. Of these, Eastern Africa countries share the highest burden of the region, 54 560 followed by Middle Africa countries, which is 15 646.6 According to the WHO report, CC is a leading cause of mortality among women.10 The annual number of new cases of CC has been projected to increase from 570 000 to 700 000 between 2018 and 2030, with the annual number of deaths projected to increase from 311 000 to 400 000.11 More than 85% of those affected are young, undereducated women who live in the world’s poorest countries.

CC is the second most common female cancer, and the second leading cause of cancer deaths in women aged 15 to 44 years in Ethiopia. About 7445 new CC cases were diagnosed, and 5338 CC deaths occurred annually in Ethiopia (estimations for 2020).12

The Ethiopian Ministry of Health started vaccinating schoolgirls aged 14 years using Gardasil-4 (a quadrivalent HPV vaccine that provides protection against highly prevalent HR HPV genotypes HPV-16 and 18, and low-risk oncogenic genotypes related to the appearance of 90% of genital warts HPV-6 and 11) since December 2018. Moreover, Ethiopia has been working on CC screening using VIA for more than a decade despite the fact that the services are not as widespread in the country as they should be. In addition, the services are not integrated with the HPV vaccination programme.

The effort of mitigating the impact of HPV infection and its sequelae CC in Ethiopia has to be supported by evidence-based scientific findings. In order to scale up the effort to make CC deaths no longer be a public health problem in Ethiopia, the country needs to have well-defined research-based evidence of HPV-related problems. Despite the fact that there is growing effort to conduct different research regarding HPV infection and CC across the country, there is no research done in the study area in this regard which describes perceived risk of women towards HPV infection and CC as far as the knowledge of the researcher is concerned. Even most studies done in the country are more of quantitative cross-sectional study types that do not reflect the risk perception of women towards HPV infection and CC. Therefore, the main purpose of this study was to explore and describe perceived risk of women towards HPV infection and CC. The information obtained from this qualitative study will add additional insight and achieve better understanding of perceived risk of HPV infection and CC among women in the study area. Furthermore, the output of the study will provide objective evidence that could be used to carry out evidence-based interventions and conduct further HPV-related research based on the findings and recommendations obtained from this study.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed exploratory, descriptive and contextual research design to explore and describe the risk perceptions of women towards HPV infection and cervical cancer. Perception of women with regard to cervical cancer screening was also investigated. Adama town is found in Ethiopia located at 99 km southeast of the capital city Addis Ababa. The town has five private hospitals, two non-governmental reproductive health clinics, more than 90 private clinics, one public hospital and eight public health centres. The study was conducted at three public health facilities in Adama where CC screening is carried out, namely, Adama hospital, Geda and Adama health centres.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Purposefully selected women aged 25–49 years who have resided in Adama town for at least 6 months and have attended the selected health facilities during the study period were included in the study. The selection was based on participants’ importance to the topic under study, whereas women beyond the aforementioned age range, seriously sick, mentally ill, unable to communicate properly and lacking the intended qualities for focus group discussions (FGDs) were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

The researchers formed focus groups each consisting of 10 members. A total of 30 participants took part in the study. A purposive sampling technique was used to select participants. The researchers, with the help of clinicians working at the selected CC screening centres, purposefully identified and recruited potentially eligible women who were readily accessible and had already visited the selected screening centres. In order to enhance the credibility of the study, the selection focused on information-rich women who believed to have some awareness about the topics to be discussed and could have the ability to articulate their experiences clearly during the FGDs. To reduce social desirability biases, homogenous participants who did not know the facilitator, the moderator or each other were recruited. Moreover, anonymity, not identifying participants by name, was also considered (table 1).

Data collection

The researchers employed FGDs to collect data from the participants. The focus group strategy was used because it is designed to obtain the perspective of the normative group, not individual perspectives. Moreover, because the researchers sought direction, explanation or in-depth dialogue, a FGD was a good fit for this study. One of the assumptions underlying the use of FGDs is that interactions among people can help them express and clarify their views in ways that are less likely to occur in a one-on-one interview. As a rule of thumb, research topics related to thoughts, beliefs and feelings work well in FGDs. The researcher was a facilitator of the FGDs and helped by a moderator who was well-experienced in this regard, to coordinate the technology, take notes and observe the behaviour of the participants during the discussion. Unstructured tool comprising initial open-ended questions for group discussion was applied to study the participants’ risk perceptions of HPV infection and CC and their perceptions regarding CC screening. The following questions were raised to initiate the FGDs:

What do you know about HPV infection? How does it transmit? What are the preventive aspects?

To check the validity of the open-ended qualitative questions, the researchers conducted a pre-test on a group of eight women in other health facilities to determine the target group’s reaction to and understanding of the initial qualitative questions: whether the questions were understandable, culturally appropriate, acceptable to the audience and appropriate for FGD. Accordingly, some amendments were made.

The discussion questions were forwarded to the study participants step by step. Probing questions, not leading questions, were used as a follow-up after an open-ended question to help the researcher understand why a participant responded in a certain way. Active listening, such as parroting back answers or asking for clarification, was applied to encourage participation.

Participants were asked for permission to audio-record and transcribe the interview. They were assured that all data would be de-identified and thus remain confidential. Totally, three FGDs, each lasting 62 to 69 min, were held to collect data from participants until saturation point was reached, meaning, until information redundancy occurred. All the audio recordings were cleaned and transcribed verbatim by the researcher and a moderator who have appreciable experience with it. Transcripts were double-checked for accuracy by the researcher. The FGDs were conducted in Amharic and translated to English by professionals in both languages. To reduce social desirability biases, homogenous participants who did not know the facilitator, the moderator or each other were recruited. Moreover, anonymity, not identifying participants by name, was considered, and video recordings were also avoided.

Data analysis

All the audio recordings were transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were double-checked for accuracy, and a theory-driven thematic analysis approach was employed to analyse the qualitative data with the key constructs of the health belief model (HBM). The HBM has been widely applied as a theoretical framework to explain health-promoting behaviours and guide researchers in their health behaviour interventions. Its reliability and validity have been previously approved to identify the beliefs in the field of CC prevention. A large body of research demonstrates the utility of the HBM in predicting vaccination and other preventive behaviours such as health screenings, making it an appropriate framework for this study. The six key constructs of the HBM (perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, self-efficacy and cues to action) were used as themes in the present study.

A six-step guide to thematic analysis, which is the most common form of thematic analysis, was provided by Braun and Clarke: familiarisation, coding, generating themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and finally the writing up were used.13 First, the researcher read the responses of the participants several times and became familiar with the data by compiling the participants’ open-ended responses to the unstructured questions. Preliminary notes were made about possible relations to the constructs of the HBM. Second, initial codes were generated throughout the data. Third, the codes reviewed for potential themes under each of the constructs of the HBM. Fourth, the researchers reviewed the themes to ensure that the codes fit within the themes and that the themes fit within the structure of the HBM. Fifth, the themes were named, defined and refined. Finally, the researchers related the themes and codes back to the study aims. The shorthand labels or codes in the process of this thematic analysis grouped around the domains of the HBM and subthemes developed from the participants’ responses.

Qualitative research reporting

The researchers used the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) to improve the quality of reporting the aspects of this research. To enhance the standard of this qualitative research reporting, COREQ—a 32-item checklist that the criteria included in it—was used to help researchers to report important aspects of the research team, study methods, context of the study, findings, analysis and interpretations.

Patients and public involvement

In this study, no patients were involved; neither patients nor the public were directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Risk perception towards human papillomavirus (HPV infection and cervical cancer

Risk perception of women towards HPV infection and CC in this research was regarded as the beliefs, attitudes or understanding of women towards the potential to be infected with HPV infection and develop CC. It was considered as the women’s subjective judgments about the likelihood of acquiring HPV infection and developing CC or the subjective judgement that women made about the characteristics and severity of HPV infection and CC. The risk perception incorporated here the two HBM constructs: the perceived susceptibility and the perceived severity, which are frequently combined and labelled as perceived threats (table 2).

Theme 1. Perceived susceptibility

Referred to as beliefs of study participants about the likelihood of contracting HPV infection that possibly led to CC development.

Sub-theme 1.1 Lack of information about HPV

With the exception of one person, every participant knew very little or nothing at all about HPV infection. They alluded to having heard about CC in the media and when people talk about it, but they did not have information about what causes CC. Only a few women knew that CC is caused by a virus, and among these women, only one mentioned HPV as a cause for CC.

I do not know what causes cervical cancer, except that it is fatal. I was told by a health worker that the infection occurs when having sex with many people. (FGD 3: Participant 1)

From what I understand, sometimes when it itches in the genital area or if there is something feels harden in the area, I think it is because of not being treated for this condition in time, it takes root, and I think this will be changed to cancer. Apart from this, I do not have information about what causes cervical cancer. (FGD 1: Participant 4)

Subtheme 1.2 Unaware of risk factors

Almost all participants had little information about the risk factors that predispose individuals to contract the HPV infection and develop CC. Only a few women were aware of one of the risk factors of HPV infection which was having many sexual partners, as they had heard this from health workers. Hence, the participants perceived that they were less susceptible to HPV infection and the following risks, such as CC development. This implies that a lack of information led participants to an improper impression that they were not susceptible to the problem.

I have heard about cervical cancer in the media, I have also heard when people were talking about it. But I did not have the information about how a person is exposed to this infection. I never thought that I was susceptible to the infection. (FGD1: Participant 1)

I do not know much about cervical cancer; I have heard health workers say that it can be caused by having sex with many men, and I had no such kind of relationship, so, I did not think I was susceptible to cervical cancer. (FGD1: Participant 10)

Theme 2. Perceived severity

Considered the study participants’ belief about the seriousness or severity of contracting HPV infection that would possibly complicate to CC development if the problem persisted.

Subtheme 2.1 Scare of the name cervical cancer itself

Most of the participants mentioned that they feel fear and anxiety even when they hear the name of CC itself. Participants’ perceived severity of CC was very high. This concern about the disease caused participants to be screened.

I fear of the name cervical cancer itself; I feel scared even when people talk about It., I have heard of people dying from cervical cancer. I got tested because I was afraid that this would happen to me. (FGD 2: Participant 3)

Cervical cancer, the name itself used to shock and scare me. I got tested, because I was afraid that the disease would happen to me. The health workers explained to me that if the infection is diagnosed and treated in time, it can be cured. I calmed down a bit. (FGD 3: Participant 5)

Subtheme 2.2 Feel stress and nervous

All the participants mentioned that CC is serious and fatal, and they also understood that it is treatable if it is identified and managed at its earliest stage of development according to the information the respondents got from health professionals. Study participants’ perceived severity of HPV infection and CC was very high.

I always worry and feel nervous about what would happen if I got the cancer, because I hear so often that people have died from it. (FGD 2: Participant 2)

I understand cervical cancer is deadly because I have heard and seen people die from cervical cancer up close. I feel stressed because I recently found out that an acquaintance of mine died of cervical cancer, a very serious and deadly disease. (FGD 1: Participant 10)

Perception about cervical cancer screening

Perception of CC screening in this research was regarded as the attitude or understanding of women towards the importance of getting screened for HPV infection and CC. It incorporated four constructs of HBM: perceived benefits, perceived barriers, Self-efficacy and cues to action (table 2).

Theme 3. Perceived benefits

Considered the participants’ opinions of the value or usefulness of getting the CC screening and associated positive consequences in avoiding HPV-related diseases, most importantly CC.

Subtheme 3.1 Remove (eliminate) worry

Most participants mentioned that they were freed from worrying about whether they had CC. They recommended others get tested and get rid of the worry of being diagnosed with CC like they did.

By getting screened, I have removed the worry of whether I have the problem or not. For the time being, I have confirmed that I am free of the infection, and I will protect myself from it in the future. As recommended by the health workers, I will come for checkups every two years. (FGD 2: Participant 8)

My screening took away my worry, because now I have been diagnosed and treated, I am doing well now. As advised by health workers, I am coming every time they gave me an appointment to monitor my health. (FGD 2: Participant 5)

Subtheme 3.2 Early detection of symptoms

The most important part of being screened for CC is early detection of pre-cervical cancer lesions if present. Study participants mentioned that they benefited from being screened for CC because it helped them to detect the problem before it got complicated.

I benefited from the examination because it helped me to know the presence of the disease before it took root. If I had not been screened, I would not have known until the disease worsened and I fell into bed. (FGD 2: Participant 4)

Getting screened is for own health, I am glad of being screened for cervical cancer. It helped me to know about the presence of the disease before it got complicated. (FGD 3: Participant 9)

Subtheme 3.3 Get treated (receive treatment)

The most important part of being screened for CC is early detection of pre-cervical cancer lesions, if present, and being treated in time. This benefits women to be treated early before the lesions turn to cancerous form.

The examination helped me to aware of my health condition and get treated in time. If I had not been examined and treated in time, I would have developed uterine cancer over time. (FGD 1: Participant 1)

I benefited from being screened because I got treatment in time, now I feel good health. (FGD 1: Participant 3)

Subtheme 3.4 Prevent developing cancer

All the women who participated in the study were aware of the benefit of being screened for CC. They already understood that if a woman is diagnosed with a precancerous cervical lesion, it is possible for her to prevent cancer development by getting treated early for the symptom before it worsens and turns into cancer form.

As health workers told me, a woman diagnosed with pre-cancer symptom can develop cancer within ten to fifteen years if not treated in time. I felt benefited because I have got treated in time before the symptom developed into cancer. (FGD 2: Participant 3)

I have learned that cervical cancer can be prevented if diagnosed and treated early, and that is what I did. I was treated in time before it turned to cancer. (FGD 3: participant 1)

Theme 4. Perceived barriers

Regarded as beliefs that had potentially negative consequences about the CC screening, including a lack of awareness towards the importance of having screened for CC.

Subtheme 4.1 Fear of the result

All the study participants had come to the health facilities for other health services, but accepted the invitation to participate in the study. Some of the participants expressed that they were afraid of what the results of the examination would be.

When they asked me if I wanted to be screened for cervical cancer, I was scared because I had no previous experience. It was scary for me to get screened because I had worried about what if I should have it (cervical cancer), but I got tested and I am glad I got an answer. (FGD 3: Participant 7)

Of course, I was afraid to be screened, afraid of being positive for the infection, but it is also necessary to be examined because it is for one’s own benefit. (FGD 1: Participant 4)

Subtheme 4.2 Negative influence from peers

The participants of the study did not experience serious barriers to getting screened. In fact, some of them expressed that some people discouraged them from getting tested because of fear of positive results.

I had come to the health facility to get rid of the contraceptive I was using, but when workers advised me to have cervical cancer screening, I immediately accepted the advice and decided to get screened. In fact, some people around me tried to scare me about the result, but I did not want to think twice, should not we pay for treatments when we get sick? (FGD1: Participant 9)

My friend tried to pull me back from getting screened, saying that there are so many other things to worry about and why you put yourself in trouble, but I got screened. (FGD 2: Participant 10)

Theme 5. Self-efficacy

Considered the study participants’ confidence and belief in ability to get screened for CC.

Subtheme 5.1 Enhance belief

All the participants of the study believed that it is possible to prevent cancer development in a woman who is already diagnosed as having a precancerous cervical lesion by receiving timely treatment before the lesion turns into cancer. This condition enhanced the women’s motivation to be tested for CC.

I was told that if women are diagnosed with the symptoms of cervical cancer and follow the treatment in time before it gets serious, they will be cured, and my heart believes that I will be cured, so I will do what I am told. For this reason, I have a plan for this. I have hope and I will implement it. (FGD 3: Participant 7)

The health workers told me that a woman can be cured from the infection if early symptoms are detected on examination and treated timely. This increased my interest to be examined. (FGD 1: Participant 8)

Subtheme 5.2 Feel confident

Most of the study participants felt confident they would not develop CC because they had already screened for CC. Those who were diagnosed as having precancerous cervical lesions received timely treatment. This helped women develop confidence in being screened for CC.

I am normally very afraid of cancer, but they told to me here that this is a sign, not a cancer. They explained to me that it can be cured and will disappear. I was treated, now I am calm and have full confidence that I will be cured. (FGD 1: Participant 1)

The health workers examined me and told me that there was a sign on the edge of my uterus. They explained to me that after ten to fifteen years it can be turned into cancer. They advised me to be treated and then I received the treatment. They told me that it would disappear. I have confidence, I will be cured. (FGD 1: Participant 3)

Theme 6. Cues to action

Regarded as triggers like advice from health professionals, illness of a family member or relatives, recommendations from organisations such as the Ethiopian Ministry of Health or media that prompt women to have CC screening.

Subtheme 6.1 Recommendation from others

Some of the study participants stated that they had heard about people dying of CC. Others mentioned that they knew women died of the same cancer, and this condition motivated them to get tested. Most participants explained that when they came to the health facilities for other services, health workers advised them to undergo precancer screening, and they did so. Some others explained that they had heard about CC screening in the media, and with this in mind, they were motivated to get screened.

I had information about cervical cancer screening before. Additionally, the health workers told me about it when I came to this health centre for other service, these motivated me to get tested. (FGD 1: Participant 6)

I did not have the idea to get screened before, because I did not have the Information about cervical cancer screening, but my friend knew about it and motivated me to get tested. (FGD 2: Participant 7)

Subtheme 6.2 Perceived bad experiences happened on others

The reason why some of the study participants decided to get screened was that they already knew women suffering from CC or heard about women who died of CC.

I recently knew that an acquaintance of mine died of cervical cancer, I was afraid that this would happen to me, so I was decided to be screened. (FGD 1: Participant 10)

I have heard that there were women who have died of cervical cancer, so I came here and got tested because I was afraid that this would happen to me. (FGD 2: Participant 6)

Discussion

The study found that almost all of the participants had poor/no information about HPV infection. Most of them reported that they had heard about CC, but they did not have information about the cause of the cancer. In addition, this study found that almost all of the participants were unaware of the main risk factors associated with the acquisition and persistence of HPV infection and CC development. This information gap caused most of the study participants to perceive themselves as less susceptible to the HPV infection and CC. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Iran,14 in the USA15 and South Africa.16 In contrast, a study conducted in Turkey revealed different findings that half of the participants worried about getting infected by HPV and CC.17 The discrepancy might be because of the sample size difference, the study area, the time of the study done and the screening method used.

The present study shows that women’s perceived severity of CC was very high. Most participants mentioned that they feel fear and anxiety even when they hear the name CC itself. This finding was consistent with studies done in the Philippines18 and in Johannesburg, South Africa.19 On the other hand, a study conducted in Argentina showed that most participants did not perceive that having HPV was a serious condition.20 The discrepancy might be because of difference in the study area, culture and difference in people’s perception about severity of a particular disease.

The participants’ major perceived benefits of being screened for CC were removing worry, early detection of precervical cancer lesions, being treated in time if present and prevention of CC development. This finding was consistent with studies done in Tanzania21 and Saudi Arabia.22

With regard to perceived barriers, this study reveals that fear of the result and negative influence from peers were barriers pulling back participants not to get screened for CC. The findings, except fear of results, were different from a study done in Australia that the most commonly stated barriers included lack of time, embarrassment, fear of results, irrelevance and male health professionals.23 Similarly, in Uganda, the most frequently reported barriers were fear of the screening procedure or outcome, residing in a remote or rural area, and limited resources/health infrastructure.24 In Ghana, the majority of the participants believed that screening is not important because there is no cure for cancer.25 In Nepal, the participants’ perceived barriers were sociocultural, including mistrust and gossip, negative experiences in previous meetings with service providers, the challenging geography of the country and financial limitations.26 The discrepancy might be because of the differences in the study area, the time of the study done, culture and the screening methods used.

Perceived self-efficacy in this study revealed that all the participants of the study had full confidence to be screened for CC. They believed that it is possible to prevent the development of CC through undergoing CC screening and getting treated in time before it turns into cancer form if precancerous lesions were diagnosed. The finding was consistent with studies done in Thailand27 and Khuzestan Province, Iran.28

Participants’ cues to action include recommendation from health workers and perceived bad experiences that happened to others, which motivated them to get screened for CC. This finding was similar to studies done in Uganda24 and in Southeast Asia.29

Strengths and limitations of the study

The information obtained from this qualitative study will add additional insight and achieve better understanding of perceived risk of HPV infection and CC among women in the study area. The study applied FGDs to collect data from participants. Since FGD is sort of an interview, the data might be influenced by social-desirability bias. To reduce this, the researchers recruited homogenous participants. Anonymity was also considered. FGDs are generally not considered externally valid due to their small sample sizes. There is much room for error in the data analysis and a high potential for the investigator’s dependency in drawing conclusions.

Conclusions

Almost all of the study participants had poor/no information about HPV infection. There was low perceived susceptibility and high perceived severity of HPV infection and CC. Early detection of symptoms, getting treated in time if present and prevention of CC development were the major perceived benefits recognised in the study. Fear of the result and negative influence from peers were perceived barriers that pulled back participants not to get screened for CC. With regard to self-efficacy, all the participants of the study had full confidence to be screened for CC. Recommendation from health workers and perceived bad experiences previously happened on others were cues to action to get screened for CC. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct community education programmes and awareness-raising efforts regarding the prevention of HPV infection and related risk factors. These can be achieved through different channels such as: developing TV and radio programmes, and brochures to enhance public awareness on HPV infection and its consequences. Moreover, transmission of messages through clubs and other existing community-level structures and organisation of community mobilisation events including workshops with key community and religious leaders and delivery of community-based health education, using health extension workers on HPV infection and its consequences, can help a lot to reach the community.
