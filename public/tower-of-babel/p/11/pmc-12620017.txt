Arterial Stiffness in Young Adults With Very Low Birth Weight: Modified by Gestational Age
Authors: Stephanie F Mayo, Christopher L Schaich, Andrew M South, Hossam A Shaltout
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDTo estimate the association of degree of prematurity with arterial stiffness in young adults using augmentation index corrected for a heart rate of 75 beats per minute (AIx@75) and pulse wave velocity (PWV).METHODSThis was a secondary cross-sectional analysis from a prospective cohort study of young adults born preterm with extremely low or very low birth weight (<1,500 g) and term-born peers with normal birth weight. Gestational age at birth was the exposure. Arterial stiffness outcome assessment was performed using radial artery tonometry (Sphygmocor) and a pressure wave transducer with an electrocardiogram (Colin). We used directed acyclic graph-informed multivariable linear regression to estimate the association of gestational age at birth on the continuous and binary scales with AIx@75 and PWV.RESULTSOf the 113 participants, 110 had AIx data and 97 had PWV data for analysis. AIx@75 was 5.07% higher in the preterm group. In adjusted analysis, each one-week higher gestational age at birth was associated with lower AIx@75 among the entire cohort (−0.38% per week, 95% confidence interval −0.74, −0.02). There was no statistically significant difference in PWV between the preterm and term groups.CONCLUSIONSThere was a higher AIx in young adults with increasing severity of prematurity amongst the entire cohort, but no difference in PWV. More studies are needed that directly assess the severity of prematurity with arterial wall stiffness and alterations in arterial wall structure to determine if they are associated with a higher risk of hypertension and cardiovascular disease long term in the premature population.

INTRODUCTION

Preterm birth has been shown to be associated with worse long-term outcomes, including a higher risk of hypertension.1,2 The underlying causes of this association remain inconclusive. Those born prematurely have altered growth and development, which may include alterations to the arterial system. Elastin and collagen are important tissue components that ensure appropriate arterial wall structure and function. Elastin allows for compliance and distensibility of arteries with increases in blood pressure, while collagen keeps the rigidity to allow recoil and prevent tearing. These two components gradually form in the vessels starting at the 12th week of gestation.3,4 The amount of elastin increases more significantly after the 25th week of gestation and continues until a few weeks post-birth.3,4 There is also a component of vascular remodeling after the 25th week of gestation, leading to a higher elastin–collagen ratio at birth.3,4

Aging has been associated with decreased elastin–collagen ratio due to elastin degradation and collagen accumulation in the arterial walls, which leads to increased arterial stiffness. This increase in arterial stiffness has been shown to be associated with hypertension in the adult population.5,6 Those born prematurely may be at higher risk for arterial stiffness compared to term-born adults due to this decreased elastin–collagen ratio from both prematurity related physiologic changes and aging. It remains unknown if those born more severely preterm have worse arterial stiffness compared to more advanced gestational age at birth.

Given this information, we hypothesize that (i) prematurity is associated with higher arterial stiffness (measured by augmentation index [AIx] and pulse wave velocity [PWV]); and (ii) the magnitude of this association will be greater with lower gestational age.

METHODS

Study design

This was a secondary cross-sectional analysis of data from a prospective cohort study of preterm-born individuals with very low birth weight (VLBW), called Prenatal Events-Postnatal Consequences (PEPC). Participants were first recruited at 14 years of age (PEPC1). They were assessed again between 18 and 23 years of age for two study visits (PEPC2). The preterm-birth group’s initial inclusion criteria were those born with extremely LBW or VLBW (<1,500 g) between 1 January 1992 and 30 June 1996 at a tertiary hospital in Winston-Salem, NC (Forsyth Medical Center). Term-born participants were those born at term ≥37 weeks’ gestation with birth weight ≥2,500 g between 1 June 1994 and 31 December 1995. Inclusion criteria for preterm-born participants were birth weight <1,500 g and follow-up at one year corrected gestational age. Exclusion criteria for both groups included multigestation, major congenital anomalies, birth defects, ward of the state, and health problems at the time of study visits. Term-born adolescents were further excluded if exposed to antenatal corticosteroids. An additional 28 young adults with VLBW, who met inclusion criteria but were not previously enrolled in PEPC1, were enrolled in the PEPC2 study to mitigate sample size attrition in the interim between PEPC1 and PEPC2. The outcome data for our current analysis were collected during visit 2 of PEPC2 (Figure 1).

Consort flow diagram demonstrating participant inclusion and exclusion criteria for the PEPC1 and PEPC2 studies and for the current analysis.

Data collection

Perinatal data were collected through review of medical records, review of research databases, and questionnaires administered to the parent or legally authorized representative, including gestational age at birth, birth weight, sex, mode of delivery, antenatal corticosteroids, maternal smoking status during pregnancy, and maternal hypertension disorder during pregnancy.

Anthropometric measurements

Height and weight were measured in triplicate per validated protocols, and body mass index was calculated. The average of three measurements was recorded during PEPC2 at the first visit.

Arterial stiffness measurements

AIx and PWV were the two measurements used to evaluate arterial stiffness at the second study visit. AIx was obtained using SphygmoCor PX (Model SCOR-PX, AtCor Medical, West Ryde, Australia), and PWV was obtained using Colin 1000/2000 (Colin Corporation, San Antonio, TX).

SphygmoCor

Trained research staff used a tonometric device to measure waveforms from the left radial artery. Each participant rested in the supine position for 10 minutes prior to measurements being taken. The radial artery waveform was measured by resting the tonometer over the radial artery on the left arm near the wrist until valid continuous waveforms were present. The waveforms collected were considered valid with an operator index greater than 75 as determined by SphygmoCor. The waveform was recorded once, and a repeat measure was made only if the operator index was below 75. This waveform was then calibrated with the systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure measurements to determine an aortic pulse waveform. SphygmoCor PX software transfer function was used to estimate the central aortic pressure waveform. The software then measured the aortic waveform’s systolic early peak and systolic late peak to generate the AIx using the calculation.(Δsystolicpeaks)(systolicbloodpressure−diastolicbloodpressure)7: The AIx is inversely proportional to heart rate and was standardized for a heart rate of 75 beats per minute (AIx@75).

Colin

The Colin VP 1000/2000 software was used to measure brachial-ankle PWV. Each participant had at least five minutes of rest prior to measurements being taken. Participants were placed in a supine position, and the blood pressure cuffs were placed on each extremity. An electrocardiogram was also placed with leads on the left wrist, right wrist, and over the right carotid artery. The blood pressure cuffs and electrocardiogram were used to measure blood pressure, heart rate, heart sounds, and arterial pulse wave from the brachial and tibial arteries using volume plethysmography. These measurements were taken once. A time delay was measured by the transmission time between the brachial and tibial pressure waves, and a distance was calculated from the participants’ height with a fixed regression equation. This was used by the machine’s algorithm (L(distance)pulsetransittime) to calculate PWV.8

Consent

Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and assent was obtained from participants. A modest compensation was provided to participants. The Wake Forest School of Medicine and Forsyth Medical Center Institutional Review Board approved this study.

Statistical analysis

We performed all statistical analyses in R 4.2.3 (2023) with a two-sided type 1 error of <0.05. Our primary objectives were to estimate the association of gestational age at birth on the continuous and binary (preterm <37 weeks’ gestation vs. term birth) scales with arterial stiffness on the continuous scale (AIx@75 and PWV in the right and left upper extremity). We also stratified our analyses by binary preterm vs. term-birth status for gestational age on the continuous scale and included an interaction term for preterm vs. term-birth status, with an interaction term P < 0.05 indicating statistically significant effect modification. For our exploratory secondary analyses, we classified gestational age at birth on a four-level ordinal scale based on the World Health Organization classifications (term birth, ≥37 weeks’ gestation; moderate preterm, 32 to <37 weeks’ gestation; very preterm, 28 to <32 weeks’ gestation; and extremely preterm, <28 weeks’ gestation), with term birth serving as the reference category.9 As this current project was a secondary analysis, we did not perform power and sample size calculations.

We used unadjusted and multivariable linear regression models to estimate the association of gestational age at birth on the continuous, binary, and ordinal scales with PWV and AIx@75 (both on the continuous scale). We developed graphical causal models of the relationships among our variables of interest using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) a priori to inform the minimally sufficient adjustment sets included in each multivariable model to close biasing, noncausal paths10,11 (Supplementary Figure S2). In each of our primary multivariable models, our minimally sufficient adjustment set included maternal hypertension disorder during pregnancy, systolic blood pressure, and body mass index. Since only a subset of participants contributed data for arterial stiffness measures, we additionally completed analyses with and without inverse probability censoring weighting to test assumptions about selection bias. Stabilized inverse probability weights were generated by logistic regression models conditioned on the presence of arterial stiffness data and our minimally sufficient adjustment set. Weighted and unweighted results were presented for each analysis.

Finally, DAGs indicated that maternal weight at delivery would ideally be included in the minimally sufficient adjustment sets. However, these data were available only for 23 of 80 (28.8%) of participants born preterm who were included in this analysis. Therefore, we conducted sensitivity analyses in which we estimated whether and the degree to which maternal weight at delivery potentially confounded these associations.12 These analyses were completed using the tipr (v1.0.2) package for R.

Of the 110 participants with AIx data, 37 were extremely preterm, 30 were very preterm, 10 were moderate preterm, and 33 were term.9 There was a statistically significant difference between term and each of the three preterm-birth groups in the unadjusted and unweighted model. This association was no longer statistically significant when adjusted and weighted, except for the very preterm-birth group compared to term (difference vs. term birth 5.80%, 95% CI 0.36, 11.2) as shown in Supplementary Table S5.

Of the 97 young adults with PWV data, 32 were extremely preterm, 24 were very preterm, 11 were moderately preterm, and 30 were term.9 There was no statistically significant difference between term and each of the three preterm-birth groups in either unadjusted, adjusted, unweighted, or weighted models (Supplementary Table S6).

DISCUSSION

In our cohort of young adults born premature with extremely LBW or VLBW and term-born peers with normal birth weight, we observed that both preterm birth and younger gestational age at birth, among the entire cohort, were associated with higher AIx@75 but not PWV.

As we age, the elastin in our arterial walls degrades secondary to normal mechanical stress of the cardiac cycle and decreased elastin turnover. As elastin is lost, it is replaced with collagen fibers to prevent breakage, thus decreasing the elastin–collagen ratio.4 These changes lead to arterial stiffening. In the premature population, infants are born with an already reduced elastin–collagen ratio as their vasculature did not reach full maturation prior to birth. One study found that abdominal aortic wall distensibility and whole-body arterial compliance were lower in stable VLBW preterm infants compared to term. This persisted at seven weeks post-birth, suggesting minimal changes to vascular maturation post-birth.13 Another study done on fetal porcine aorta found that elastin deposition occurs more frequently in the latter half of pregnancy. It also found that the largest postnatal changes occurred in the first 3 weeks after birth and subsided by 15 weeks after birth.14 These findings would suggest that vascular stiffness development may start earlier in the premature population compared to the general population. While the degree of prematurity may affect vascular stiffness, our study did not support this theory. Further studies on the vasculature in the aging premature population, with a larger sample size, are needed to better understand whether the severity of prematurity is associated with higher arterial stiffness risk over time.

Our results are similar to those of McEniery et al.15 who found a statistically significant difference in AIx in the extremely premature population but no statistically significant difference in aortic PWV. In this study, they used EPICure participants, a national cohort in the UK and Ireland, to assess the cardiovascular consequences of 11-year-old children born extremely premature. They assessed blood pressure, AIx, and aortic PWV amongst children born at 25 weeks, 6 days, or less and compared them to term-born peers. Another study by Hurst et al.16 also assessed long-term respiratory and cardiovascular outcomes using the EPICure participants assessed at 19 years of age with matched term-born controls. They also assessed AIx and PWV for cardiovascular outcomes using Sphygmacor. They found that AIx was higher in the extremely preterm group compared to the term-born group, but that there was no statistically significant difference in PWV. This demonstrated persistently higher AIx measurements in the extremely preterm group from the first study at 11 years, and in the follow-up study at 19 years of age. Similarly, we found a statistically significant difference between AIx between the preterm and term groups, but not PWV. Compared to these studies, however, our cohort had a larger preterm population (80 in our cohort vs. 68 at 11 years and 52 at 19 years in EPICure). Our cohort had a larger sample size and, in the preterm group, included participants born moderately preterm and very preterm. This contrasts with the EPICure study, which only included extremely premature infants (born less than 26 weeks’ gestation). Further studies are needed as these cohorts continue to age into middle and later adulthood to determine if clinically meaningful differences occur across different study populations with variable preterm-birth severity.

AIx and PWV are two noninvasive measurements used to assess arterial stiffness. PWV is considered a direct measure of large artery stiffness, while AIx, affected by other hemodynamic factors such as ventricular ejection, peripheral vascular resistance, and aortic wall distensibility, is considered a surrogate measure of large artery stiffness.17 Per the European Network for Non-Invasive Investigation of Large Arteries expert consensus, PWV is the gold standard for noninvasive arterial stiffness measurement.18 However, one study by McEniery et al. found that AIx may be a better predictor of arterial stiffness in the younger adult population (<50 years old).19 Thus, PWV may be a less accurate arterial stiffness measure in this study of young adults compared to AIx. Despite this finding, PWV and AIx have not become standard of clinical care for use in the youth and young adult population, given the lack of standardization and reference values to determine significance.20 Recently, after our study was completed, the Youth Vascular Consortium attempted to develop device-specific, age and sex-based reference values for aortic PWV in healthy people aged 1–40 years. They performed a retrospective cross-sectional analysis of PWV data pooled from numerous studies utilizing six different devices to measure PWV (29% used SphygmoCor).21 While participants were noted to be healthy, they included those who had elevated blood pressure or stage 1 hypertension (age ≥16 years) and overweight or approximately class 1 obesity (age <19 years), and preterm-birth status was not collected. These percentiles were based on the data from these studies and were not validated against any gold-standard measure of cardiovascular health or cardiovascular disease outcomes. In our study, both the preterm and term groups’ mean values were above the 90th percentiles developed by the Youth Vascular Consortium across all devices, but most notably for SphygmoCor. This discrepancy is unclear, but it is unlikely that the majority of our study population, particularly the healthy term-born control group, would have a high prevalence of abnormally high PWV. Although this is a step forward in understanding noninvasive measures of hemodynamics in the younger population and potential long-term risks, further studies are needed for young adults in determining these values and establishing generalizable reference values.

Arterial stiffness in the premature population is multifactorial. There are several studies assessing the causes of hypertension and arterial stiffness. One alternative hypothesis for our findings may be that birth weight, rather than prematurity, affects arterial stiffness and its correlation to hypertension.22 There are several studies assessing this hypothesis; however, the results have been inconclusive. In a study done by Cheung et al.,23 they assessed PWV and blood pressure between three groups at an average of 8.2 years of age: children born preterm and small for gestational age, children born preterm with weight appropriate for gestational age, and term-born children with weight appropriate for gestational age. They found that those born preterm and small for gestational age had higher PWV and blood pressure compared to the other groups. This suggested that birth weight was more influential than gestational age. In contrast, a study by Rossi et al.24 assessed PWV and blood pressure among 14-year-old former preterm and term-born infants. They grouped the participants as those born term with weight appropriate for gestational age, preterm with weight appropriate for gestational age, and term with weight small for gestational age. They found that the preterm group, rather than the small for gestational age group, had higher systolic blood pressure and PWV compared to the control group. Another alternative hypothesis includes the effects of postnatal corticosteroids on arterial stiffness and blood pressure. There are several studies assessing the effects of postnatal steroids on premature infants; however, there are limited studies on the effects of steroids on blood pressure, particularly long-term effects. Two studies assessed blood pressure at school age in a cohort of premature children who received steroids in the postnatal period. There was no association found between postnatal steroid course and blood pressure in either study.25,26 Despite these two studies, there is insufficient data on the long-term effects of postnatal steroid exposure on blood pressure and the vasculature, and this warrants further evaluation.

There are several limitations to our study. The cardiovascular measures required cuff measurements of blood pressure. Some participants may not have been able to complete these measurements due to inability to fit the cuff for accurate measurements, e.g., due to obesity. The AIx measurements were acquired using a radial arterial tonometric device, which can have substantial intra- and interoperator variability and thus potential measurement bias. Different machines and brands were used to measure AIx and PWV. Finally, this was a secondary analysis, so we were likely underpowered to observe statistically significant differences should they exist, particularly with PWV as evidenced by the wide CIs and for effect modification by preterm vs. term-birth status.

In a well-characterized birth cohort, we observed that those born preterm had higher AIx compared to term and that, among the entire cohort, younger gestational age at birth on the continuous scale was associated with higher AIx. Overall, we did not consistently observe statistically significant or clinically significant differences. It is possible that the changes in AIx that we observed would be magnified and more clinically relevant with a larger sample size that is adequately powered to assess for effect modification. As modern medicine evolves and improves, the degree of prematurity and infant survival will continue to increase. It will be important to continue to study the long-term outcomes of the premature population, particularly those aspects affecting cardiovascular health.
