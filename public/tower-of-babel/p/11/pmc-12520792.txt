Femoral head diameter varies widely in hips with developmental dysplasia and predicts acetabular component size in total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Songlin Li, Xingyu Liu, Wenwei Qian, Yiling Zhang, Qunshan Lu, Peilai Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe aim of this study was to explore the relationship between the femoral head diameter (FHD) and the degree of subluxation in developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) patients, and develop a machine-learning model for predicting acetabular component size in total hip arthroplasty (THA) according to demographic data and FHD.MethodsThe FHD of 469 DDH patients from Longwood Valley medical database was measured, after excluding those with severe femoral head destruction, bone grafting, or augments. Its distribution and difference across Crowe and Hartofilakidis classifications were also assessed. Five machine-learning algorithms were developed to predict the size of the acetabular component, and the best model was determined according to the mean square error (MSE), root mean square error (RMSE), and R-squared values. The accuracy of the best model’s cup size prediction was validated by comparing it with acetate templating and CT-based planning in a consecutive cohort from an independent institution.ResultsThe FHD gradually decreased with increasing Crowe and Hartofilakidis classifications. The Pearson correlation coefficient between FHD and the size of the acetabular component was 0.60, indicating a moderate correlation. In the test set, the random forest model outperformed the other four models in terms of MSE (0.904), RMSE (0.951), and R-squared (0.919). In the external validation, the accuracy of this model was not significantly different from CT-based planning (80.0% vs 87.5%, p > 0.05), but outperformed acetate templating (80.0% vs 52.5%, p < 0.05), particularly for Crowe Type IV (81.8% vs 27.3%, p < 0.05).ConclusionThe FHD decreases with increasing degree of subluxation in DDH patients. The machine-learning model constructed by combining demographic parameters and FHD demonstrates significantly higher accuracy in acetabular component size planning compared to templating methods. Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is an effective treatment for secondary osteoarthritis caused by developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH).1,2 However, due to the presence of acetabular dysplasia in DDH patients, acetabular bone defects and shallow acetabula, among other conditions, are common, increasing the complexity of acetabular reconstruction.3 Accurate preoperative planning for determining the appropriate cup sizes can minimize unforeseen intraoperative challenges while also reducing implant and instrument inventory and sterilization costs.4

2D acetate templating provides limited information, making acetate templating-based preoperative planning relatively inaccurate in predicting acetabular component sizes for DDH patients.5,6 CT-based preoperative planning allows the dynamic 3D evaluation of the acetabular morphology, displaying important information such as the true location and diameter of the acetabulum, factors known to improve component size prediction.4,7 However, 3D planning is resource-intensive and not universally available. Therefore, we explored whether a simple radiological measurement, combined with machine learning, could serve as a surrogate for 3D planning in predicting component size. Additionally, predictive models based on demographic data, regardless of the use of imaging data, have been developed to estimate acetabular component sizes and have demonstrated commendable accuracy.6,8-10 Nonetheless, the datasets used to train these predictive models are deficient in DDH data, and so their accuracy in predicting the size of the acetabular component in DDH patients remains unverified.

Previous studies have confirmed that the acetabular morphology differs among DDH patients with different degrees of dislocation. As a result, even with identical demographic data, the component sizes used in DDH patients may differ from those used in non-DDH patients. Mechanical forces are the primary stimulus for bone development; consequently, dislocated femoral heads may demonstrate abnormal size changes due to a lack of adequate stress stimulation.11 To our knowledge, few studies have investigated the correlation between femoral head diameter (FHD) and the degree of dislocation. Moreover, although the intraoperatively measured femoral head diameter is considered a potential predictor of acetabular component size, its predictive accuracy has yet to be validated in DDH patients.12,13

This study aimed to investigate the distribution of femoral head diameters among DDH patients with different degrees of dislocation, to develop a predictive model for the acetabular component size in DDH patients undergoing THA based on demographic data and FHD, and to validate the accuracy of this model by comparing it to the planned accuracy derived from acetate templating and 3D CT planning.

Methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study was conducted following approval from the ethics committee (JS2550). The data of patients who underwent primary THA from February 2018 to March 2024 were collected from the Longwood Valley medical database located in China.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) complete medical and surgical records; 2) complete radiological data; and 3) no postoperative dislocation or periprosthetic fractures. The exclusion criteria were as follows: severe preoperative femoral head destruction preventing measurement of the FHD with the best-fit circle method; and intraoperative bone grafting or the use of augments. Additionally, for patients who underwent bilateral surgeries, only data from the first procedure were included in order to avoid the influence of surgery for one side on decision-making for the contralateral side.

A total of 530 patients initially met the inclusion criteria. Among them, 38 patients were excluded due to severe preoperative femoral head destruction, 14 patients underwent intraoperative structural bone grafting, and nine patients required the use of augments. After these exclusions, 469 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1), and the demographic information is listed in Table I.

The distribution of patients according to their Crowe classification was as follows: Type I, 363 patients (77.40%); Type II, 54 patients (11.51%); Type III, 15 patients (3.20%); and Type IV, 37 patients (7.89%).14 The distribution of the patients according to the Hartofilakidis classification was as follows: Type A, 395 patients (84.22%); Type B, 32 patients (6.82%); and Type C, 42 patients (8.96%).15

Radiological measurements

All patients underwent standardized anteroposterior (AP) pelvic radiographs in the supine position with 10° to 15° internal rotation of the hips. Radiographs were obtained using a calibrated digital radiography system. To avoid errors from magnification, all measurements were performed on DICOM images using Bee DICOM Viewer software (SinoUnion Healthcare Inc, China) with automatic scaling based on the embedded radiological calibration marker. FHD was determined by fitting a best-fit circle to the femoral head contour on the AP view. The maximum diameter of this circle was recorded as the FHD. The Crowe classification was determined on the basis of the ratio of the dislocation distance to the pelvic height. The Hartofilakidis classification was determined on the basis of the degree of subluxation and its relationship with the false acetabulum. FHD and Crowe and Hartofilakidis classifications were jointly determined by two experienced physicians. In cases of disagreement on Crowe or Hartofilakidis classifications, a senior orthopaedic chief physician (PL) was consulted to reach a final decision.

Model development

The dataset was randomly split into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 7:3. Five machine-learning algorithms were used for training: multiple regression, back propagation neural network (BPNN), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector regression (SVR), and random forest. The candidate variables included sex, age, height, weight, FHD, Crowe classification, and Hartofilakidis classification. First, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to explore the correlations between the candidate variables and the actual cup diameter implanted intraoperatively. All model selection, hyperparameter tuning, and performance optimization were carried out exclusively within the training set using five-fold cross-validation to prevent biasing to the test set. Model performance in predicting the cup size in the test set was evaluated with the mean square error (MSE), root mean square error (RMSE), and R-squared, with smaller MSE and RMSE values and R-squared values closer to 1 indicating better model accuracy and a greater degree of fit.16

External clinical validation

Independent external validation was conducted using the data of 40 consecutive DDH patients who underwent THA at an institution not part of the Longwood Valley medical database between February 2023 and May 2024, and who had preoperative radiographs and CT scans. The demographic characteristics, FHD, and Crowe and Hartofilakidis classifications were input into the optimal prediction model to obtain the predicted acetabular component size. As acetabular component sizes are typically reported as even-numbered increments of 2 mm, the predicted value was rounded to the nearest even number. For example, if the predicted size was 50.6 mm, the planned cup size was set to 50 mm. Preoperative planning was performed with acetate templating according to the method described by Della Valle et al.17 Specifically, the acetabular component was placed with its inferior margin aligned with the tear-drop line and positioned near the ilioischial line, and the cup was placed in the location of the true acetabulum to ensure adequate coverage. 3D CT-based planning was performed with a validated artificial intelligence THA preoperative planning system (Longwood Valley, China), which dynamically determines the position and coverage of the acetabular component at three levels to determine the appropriate size.18 The planned component size was considered accurate if it matched the actual implanted size or differed by only one size (i.e. 2 mm). To avoid recall bias, all plans were finalized three months postoperatively, and patient-identifiable information was anonymized. The final decision on prosthesis size was jointly made by two experienced physicians (SL, QL) specializing in DDH reconstructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 27 (IBM, USA), and the prediction models were developed in Python (version 3.12.1, October 2, 2024). Continuous variables are reported as the means and standard errors (SEs), whereas categorical variables are reported as case numbers and proportions and 95% CI. The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to assess whether continuous variables followed a normal distribution. If the variables were normally distributed, analysis of variance was used to compare the data among the groups, and post hoc analyses were subsequently performed when statistically significant differences were detected. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, among-group differences were assessed with the Kruskal‒Wallis test. The chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test were used to assess differences in categorical variables among groups. When significant differences were found, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing. Statistical significance was considered if p < 0.05. Interobserver agreement was assessed with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Results

Model training and testing were conducted with the data of 469 patients with DDH, including 73 males and 396 females, with a mean age of 56.72 years (SE 13.92) and a mean BMI of 24.79 kg/m2 (SE 3.62). Among them, patients with Crowe Type I DDH were older at the time of the operation than patients with other types (p = 0.001, Kruskal-Wallis test). See Table I for details.

Distribution of femoral head diameters

The difference in the distribution of FHDs among the different Crowe and Hartofilakidis types was statistically significant. Overall, the FHD decreased as the degree of dislocation increased, i.e. it was greatest among patients with Crowe Type I DDH (mean 43.30 mm (SE 4.07)), followed by those with Type II (mean 41.26 mm (SE 4.51)), Type III (mean 36.42 mm (SE 6.36)), and Type IV DDH (34.85 mm (SE 5.91)). The FHD of Crowe type I was mainly between 34 and 52 mm, that of Type II was mainly between 36 and 48 mm, that of Type III was concentrated around 40 mm, and that of Type IV was mainly between 28 and 34 mm. Similarly, the femoral head diameter was greatest for Hartofilakidis Type A patients (mean 43.12 mm (SE 4.07)), followed by Type B (mean 40.72 mm (SE 5.20)) and Type C patients (mean 34.41 mm (SE 6.15)), and the differences among the types were statistically significant. Hartofilakidis Type A FHDs ranged between 34 and 52 mm, Type B diameters 36 and 46 mm, and Type C diameters 28 and 34 mm (Table II and Figure 2). The ICC of the inter-rater agreement in FHD measurement was above 0.84 (95% CI 0.81 to 0.87), suggesting almost perfect agreement.

Model performance

Pearson correlation analysis revealed that the size of the acetabular component was smaller for female patients, and negatively correlated with the Crowe and Hartofilakidis type, with a correlation coefficient of -0.61. Furthermore, height, weight, age, and FHD were positively correlated with the acetabular component size; the strongest correlation was with the FHD, with a correlation coefficient of 0.60 (Figure 3).

Pearson correlation matrix of variables related to acetabular component size in developmental dysplasia of the hip patients who underwent total hip arthroplasty.

As stated above, female patients tended to have smaller component sizes; the Hartofilakidis classification and Crowe classification were negatively associated with component size, and height, weight, age, and FHD were positively associated with component size in the linear regression model. Among these variables, the Hartofilakidis classification had the greatest impact, with a regression coefficient of -1.100, while weight had the smallest impact, with a regression coefficient of only 0.014, and was the only variable whose regression coefficient was not statistically significant at the 5% level. The variance inflation factor values for all variables were less than 10, indicating that there was no multicollinearity between the variables. The established linear regression model and its evaluation metrics are detailed in the Supplementary Material. Among the models, the random forest model demonstrated the lowest MSE (0.904) and RMSE (0.951), and its R-squared value was the closest to 1, indicating that it demonstrated the best predictive accuracy among the models (Table III and Figure 4).

External clinical validation

Overall, 40 patients independent of the medical database underwent three types of surgical planning. Of these patients, 16 had Crowe Type I, eight had Type II, five had Type III, and 11 had Type IV. At ± 1 size, the overall accuracy of the model predictions was 80.0% (95% CI 67.0% to 93.0%), greater than the 52.5% (95% CI 31.3% to 63.7%) of acetate templating but not dissimilar from that of 3D CT-based planning (87.5%; 95% CI 76.8% to 98.2%). The accuracies of the three types of planning were comparable for Crowe Type I (p = 0.143), Type II (p = 0.641), and Type III patients (p = 0.800). However, 3D CT-based planning and the predictive model were more accurate for patients with Crowe Type IV than acetate templating was (p = 0.001) (Table IV).

Discussion

Underdevelopment of the femoral head is one of the primary characteristics of DDH. However, to our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated the relationship between FHD and the degree of hip subluxation in patients with DDH. In this study, we found that FHD diminishes with increasing subluxation severity confirms that high-riding dysplastic hips tend to have smaller, hypoplastic femoral heads. Moreover, the FHD was moderately correlated with the acetabular component size. Compared with acetate templating, the predictive model for acetabular component size in DDH patients undergoing THA, developed based on demographic parameters and the FHD, demonstrated superior accuracy in an external validation set.

The acetabulum plays a crucial role in load transmission and bone remodelling in the hip joint. Pelvic loads are transferred through the acetabulum to the femur, thereby stimulating the development of its proximal part.19 In patients with dislocation, as the degree of subluxation increases, the contact area between the femoral head and acetabulum decreases, leading to a lack of normal mechanical stimulation, which inhibits the process of bone remodelling and, consequently, affects the development of the femoral head. In this study, we observed that the diameter of the femoral head progressively decreased as both the Crowe and Hartofilakidis type increased. Similarly, Chen et al4 reported that both the inner and outer acetabular widths decreased with increasing of subluxation. These findings suggest that the loading between the femoral head and acetabulum is reciprocal – a reduction in normal mechanical stimulation due to dislocation inhibits the growth of both the femoral head and acetabulum, leading to femoral head and acetabular dysplasia. However, this study included only Asian patients; because the distribution of FHD differs among different ethnicities, further clinical validation is needed to determine whether the findings of this study are applicable to DDH patients of other ethnic backgrounds.20

In THA for patients with DDH, selection of the correct acetabular component size is crucial for ensuring prosthetic stability and optimal patient function.21 Larger acetabular components have gained increasing popularity in THA because they can accommodate femoral heads of 32 mm or larger, thereby reducing the risk of dislocation and minimizing wear.22 However, the stability of cementless components largely depends on the bone mass remaining after acetabular reaming.23 Generally, at least 75% superior bone coverage is recommended, as it enhances prosthetic stability and improves surgical success rates.14 Excessive acetabular reaming can change the centre of rotation and offset, increasing the risk of impingement and dislocation. Such reaming is particularly problematic in DDH patients, where the underdeveloped true acetabulum often limits the available bone stock. Extensive reaming in such cases can significantly reduce bone mass, making it difficult to create a solid bone bed and leading to insufficient bone coverage for the acetabular component, subsequently compromising initial stability.14 Furthermore, an excessively large acetabular component can penetrate the inner wall of the ilium, causing complex acetabular fractures.14 Moreover, younger DDH patients undergoing THA may require revision in the future, which may be difficult if excessive bone loss occurs during the primary THA.24

Surgical planning based on radiographs alone is often unreliable, especially in cases of DDH with acetabular deformities.5,6 Traditional preoperative planning using acetate templating provides limited information in such cases, further reducing the accuracy of the surgical plan. Advancements in digital technology have allowed the use of 3D CT-based planning, significantly improving the precision of preoperative plans. However, CT requires specialized equipment, increasing costs as well as the preoperative waiting time and radiation exposure for patients.4,7 Predictive models based on demographic parameters offer a simple method for estimating component size; however, while these models are easy to implement and streamline the preoperative preparation process, their accuracy in complex DDH cases has not been fully validated.6,8-10 Previous studies have shown that the morphology of the acetabulum differs significantly with the degree of subluxation in DDH patients. As a result, even among DDH patients with identical demographic data, the component size may differ substantially from that used in non-DDH patients. In a study by Ben Lulu et al,13 which excluded DDH patients, a strong correlation was observed between the implanted component size and the actual diameter of the femoral head. In 92% of the patients, the diameter of the implanted cup was 2 to 4 mm larger than the excised femoral head. When the natural femoral head size was used as a guide for acetabular reaming, the total number of reamers needed for acetabular preparation was reduced. The authors suggested that adding femoral head measurements during preoperative templating could improve planning accuracy. In this study, we similarly found a significantly correlation between the FHD and the size of the acetabular component for THA (r = 0.60). However, this indicates that approximately 40% of the variance remains unexplained — likely due to factors such as pelvic width, acetabular depth, or bone stock. This limitation of linear prediction highlights the advantage of using machine-learning models, which can incorporate multiple nonlinear interactions beyond a single predictor. The predictive model developed from demographic parameters, the FHD, and the degree of dislocation offers a promising approach for improving the accuracy of preoperative planning in DDH patients undergoing THA. However, owing to the inability to accurately measure the FHD in patients with severe femoral head destruction, such patients were excluded from this study, which also limits the generalizability of the model to some extent.

Machine learning has been widely applied in various fields of medicine. By extracting features from large datasets, mathematical models can be established to optimize decision-making and improve predictive accuracy.25-27 Compared with traditional predictive models, machine-learning-based models can capture complex nonlinear relationships. Additionally, these models can be retrained and updated with new data, enhancing both their predictive accuracy and adaptability. The FHD-based ML model significantly outperformed conventional templating for component sizing. In severe deformities, the model was highly accurate, suggesting it could be a valuable tool for preoperative planning when standard templates are least reliable. The advantages of the predictive model over acetate templating were more pronounced in Crowe Type IV patients; one reason could be because determining the appropriate acetabular component size in Crowe IV-type DDH requires consideration of the 3D morphology of the acetabulum, which 2D templates cannot provide sufficiently. Owing to the insufficient number of Crowe Type III cases available for model training, the accuracy of the model in predicting the acetabular component size for these patients in the external validation cohort was poor. In addition to the small number of Crowe III cases available for model training, the reduced performance of our model compared to CT-based templating in this subgroup likely reflects the intrinsic advantage of 3D imaging in complex dysplastic anatomies. In Crowe III and IV hips, acetabular morphology is often severely distorted, and critical features such as bone stock, medial wall thickness, and component coverage are not easily captured by 2D parameters alone. CT-based planning provides a more comprehensive spatial understanding that improves accuracy in these challenging cases.

This study also has several limitations. First, the number of patients with different Crowe and Hartofilakidis classifications varied: notably, there was an insufficient number of Crowe Type III patients, limiting the improvements in predictive accuracy observed for the model over other methods. Additionally, the data for this study were sourced from a multicentre medical database; if a physician prefers selecting either oversized or undersized implants, this may result in slight variations in the final implant size. This also means that the model predicts what was actually implanted, which may differ slightly from an idealized anatomical size. Another limitation was the exclusion of patients requiring intraoperative bone grafting or the use of augments, to avoid confounding factors arising from altered acetabular morphology and customized reconstruction strategies, which might deviate from standard implant sizing practices.28 Furthermore, external validation was conducted at a single independent centre, which may limit the generalizability of the findings across institutions with different patient populations, imaging protocols, or surgical practices. Future multicentre validation studies are warranted to assess the model’s performance in more diverse clinical settings. Finally, owing to the black box nature of machine-learning models, their internal decision-making processes cannot be displayed as transparently as in linear regression, and they need to be employed within specialized programming environments. We are currently developing the predictive model from this study into a mobile application for easier accessibility.

In summary, the diameter of the femoral head decreases with the degree of subluxation in DDH patients. Additionally, the FHD is significantly correlated with the size of the acetabular component in THA. On this basis, a predictive model was developed using demographic parameters and the FHD to predict the acetabular component size, and its accuracy was confirmed through external clinical validation. The application of this prediction model can increase the accuracy and efficiency of preoperative planning for THA in DDH patients, providing a reference for appropriate reaming and prosthesis selection during surgery, and holds promise for optimizing preoperative manufacturer supply chain management and operating theatre efficiency.

Take home message

- Femoral head diameter decreases significantly with increasing developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) severity, and shows moderate correlation with acetabular component size.

- A machine-learning model based on this simple radiological measurement can accurately predict implant size in primary total hip arthroplasty for DDH, and incorporating this model into preoperative workflow could improve implant selection and inventory management in DDH hip arthroplasties.
