Unmasking iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Osman O. Radhwi, Omar M. Raslan, May A. Almoshary, Eman M. Mansory
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:This study investigates the prevalence of iron deficiency anemia (IDA) in Saudi Arabia, analyzing variations by gender, age, and geographical region.Methods:An observational, cross-sectional analysis was conducted from January 2014 to February 2024 using data from Al Borg laboratories, including ferritin levels, demographics, and routine hematological results.Results:The study included 420,956 individuals, with 73% being female and 94% over 15 years old. The IDA was found in 19.8% of patients, Non-Anemic Iron Deficiency (NAID) in 23.7%, and other anemias in 9.6%. Females represented 93% of those with IDA and NAID. All patients under 12 were anemic, with 45% having IDA. Among the elderly (65+), 29% had iron deficiency. Prevalence varied geographically, from 28% in Al-Jouf to 42% in Makkah, with female rates reaching 69%.Conclusion:The study reveals a high prevalence of IDA and NAID across Saudi Arabia, particularly among females. It underscores the need for targeted interventions to address this public health issue.

Anemia is a common health problem that affects approximately a quarter of the global population and impairs overall health.1 Although anemia can occur worldwide at any stage of life, it is more common during pregnancy and childhood.2 In many developing countries, anemia has become a major public health crisis. Iron deficiency, the most widespread micronutrient deficiency, accounts for half of all anemia cases. Malaria, parasitic infections, blood loss, hemoglobinopathies, and bone marrow suppression or replacement are other common causes, in addition to dietary deficiencies.3

As defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), anemia is characterized by hemoglobin levels below 120 g/L in non-pregnant women and 130 g/L in men.4 The global prevalence of anemia exhibits significant variation across different age groups and geographical regions. Particularly in Africa and Southeast Asia, anemia disproportionately affects preschool-aged children and women, with two-thirds and half of these populations, respectively, being affected.5 Globally, anemia impacts 12.7% of men and 23.9% of older adults.3

In Saudi Arabia, there is a considerable prevalence of anemia among preschool children and pregnant and non-pregnant women.3 A study by Al Quaiz revealed that approximately 37% of females in Riyadh are affected by anemia.6 Furthermore, several studies in Saudi Arabia have highlighted a significant prevalence of iron deficiency anemia (IDA) with various estimates (10-60%) among schoolchildren,7,8 teenagers,9 university students,10-13 and females of reproductive age.14,15 Non-anemic iron deficiency (NAID), characterized by low ferritin levels with a normal hemoglobin concentration, is likely even more common and has recently been considered a condition on its own.16 It also may have broader health implications than previously recognized. This hypothesis stems from iron’s essential role in numerous metabolic processes, including mitochondrial function, neurotransmitter synthesis, and muscle metabolism.17 Studies suggest a link between NAID and a range of symptoms, even in the absence of anemia, including fatigue, reduced quality of life, lower work performance, cognitive decline, mood disturbances, and restless legs syndrome with pica.18

Previous research on IDA and NAID in Saudi Arabia has been limited to specific regions and often involved underrepresented populations.13,19 This study aims to determine the nationwide prevalence of IDA and NAID and investigate their association with age, gender, and geographical location.

Methods

This is an observational, cross-sectional analysis of data from Al Borg Diagnostics Laboratories in Saudi Arabia, with over 72 branches distributed across all 13 administrative states in Saudi Arabia.20 Retrospective patient data was retrieved between January 2014 and February 2024. The collected data included demographic information (sex, age at testing, and geographical region), as well as routine hematological results such as ferritin and a fully automated complete blood count (CBC) analyzer. The study was approved by the Unit of Biomedical Ethics at Al Borg Diagnostics, approval number (No 02/24).

A total of 928,778 Ferritin samples were collected between January 2015 and February 2024. Of these, 370,599 repeat samples from the same patients were excluded, retaining only the sample with the lowest ferritin value. An additional 39 samples were removed due to missing demographic information of their patients. Patients without a concomitant CBC were also removed to avoid time-related bias, leaving a total of 420,956 eligible samples (Figure 1).

Although the most recent WHO guideline for defining iron deficiency recommended a ferritin cutoff of below 15 ng/mL for healthy individuals,21 we used various societies’ most recent updated recommendations for a ferritin cutoff below 30 ng/mL to define patients with iron deficiency.22,24 The sample was divided into patients with iron deficiency and those without. Anemia was defined based on the most recent WHO cut-offs based on age and sex,4 where the general population would have anemia if hemoglobin levels were below 120 g/L. Differences should be appreciated in children between 6-23 months (105 g/L), between 24-59 months (110 g/L), between 5-11 years (115 g/L), and adult males between 15-65 years (130 g/L). Patients with anemia and ferritin <30 ng/mL were defined as having “IDA”. Patients with normal hemoglobin for their age and sex and ferritin <30 ng/mL were labeled as “NAID”. Patients with low hemoglobin but normal ferritin were labeled as having “other anemia”. Finally, patients with normal hemoglobin and ferritin ≥30 ng/mL were labeled as “normal”.

The study data was provided in de-identified Excel files. Patient-specific information was replaced with unique research IDs. Multiple data points were collected for each individual. For a detailed overview of the analytic sample, please refer to Figure 1.

The ‘Region’ variable was recoded from a detailed 27-level city classification to a more generalized 13-level representation of Saudi Arabia’s administrative regions: Al-Jouf, Aseer, Al-Baha, Eastern Region, Hayel, Jazan, Al-Madinah, Makkah, Najran, Northern Border, Al-Qaseem, Al-Riyadh, and Tabouk.25

A descriptive data analysis was performed to summarize the study variables. Continuous variables were described using measures of central tendency (mean) and dispersion (standard deviation, range), along with the sample size (N). Categorical variables were summarized using frequency counts and percentages. The prevalence of iron deficiency and anemia was calculated and stratified by age, gender, and geographical region. To compare numerical variables between patients with and without iron deficiency, t-tests were employed. Chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables between the 2 groups. Statistical significance was determined at the 0.05 level using two-sided p-values. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA 18.26

Results

Iron deficiency

This study included 420,956 patients with ferritin levels and a concomitant CBC result. The age of patients at the time of testing ranged from 1 day up to 100 years, with most patients (94.4%) being above the age of 15 years. Females were the majority, comprising 73.1% of the sample size. 124,015 (29.5%) patients were anemic andiron deficiency comprised 67.2% of patients with anemia. Of the total population, 83,418 (19.8%) patients had IDA, 100,090 (23.7%) had NAID, 40,597 (9.6%) had other anemias, and 196,851 (46.7%) were normal samples.

Furthermore, and quite shockingly, all patients under the age of 12 were anemic, with 44.6% having IDA and 55.4% having anemia due to other causes. The CBC parameters

The platelet counts and MCV values showed statistically significant differences between the low and normal-to-high ferritin groups. Regarding platelet counts, the hypoferritinemia group had a mean platelet count of 311×10⁹/L, compared to 276×10⁹/L in the normal-to-high ferritin group. This difference was statistically significant (p<0.0001). As for the MCV values, the average was 80 fL vs. 84 fL in the low ferritin and high ferritin groups, respectively. The difference was also statistically significant (p<0.0001).

When looking at patients with IDA, 73% had low MCV. In patients with low ferritin levels (across all hemoglobin values), 51% had low MCV, 47.8% had a normal MCV, and a negligible proportion had a high MCV. Regarding samples with ferritin levels above 30 ng/mL, about 25.7% had a low MCV.

Discussion

In the present study, we determined the prevalence of iron deficiency with or without anemia in a large population sample obtained from a private laboratory, encompassing ten years’ worth of data from inpatients, outpatients, and healthy individuals. We included a heterogeneous sample with a wide age range, predominantly female participants but with a substantial number of males, and complete CBC results. The study reveals that the majority of females and children below the age of 12 are iron deficient. Our findings are within the range of published local and global reports regarding the prevalence of anemia and iron deficiency.3,10,27 Keeping in mind that most local reports have focused on specific subpopulations such as women of childbearing age, Saudi nationals, university students with lower age cut-offs, and children.1,11,13,19,28-32 The standardization of ferritin measurement is hindered by using diverse ferritin isoforms and antibodies across different laboratories.33 This variability, coupled with disparities in cut-off values, can lead to significant inter-laboratory differences in diagnosis and treatment of iron deficiency, potentially resulting in missed opportunities for timely intervention. Numerous labs use ferritin cut-offs <15 ng/mL according to WHO definitions for iron deficiency,21 despite recent evidence suggesting raising ferritin cut-offs to higher values (e.g., 30 ng/mL and 50 ng/mL).34 In this study, we chose a ferritin cut-off of <30 ng/mL as it has been shown to have a higher sensitivity (~92%) and high specificity (98%) for the diagnosis of iron deficiency.22,23 Our study has exposed the widespread issue of iron deficiency in our population, especially among female and pediatric populations. We call for the standardization of ferritin cut-offs across all laboratories in the Kingdom as an initial step in the proper detection and mitigation of this issue, which holds the promise of significantly improving the management of iron deficiency.

Approximately half of our samples had abnormal lab findings, either anemia, iron deficiency, or both. The most predominant abnormality was NAID, followed by IDA. As expected, most patients with iron deficiency (with or without anemia) were females aged ≥15 years. Surprisingly, all patients under the age of 12 were anemic and 44.6% of them were due to iron deficiency. A potential explanation for this observation is that younger patients tend to be referred for blood work and investigations when symptomatic or unwell. In contrast, older patients often voluntarily undergo basic investigations as part of routine check-ups or screening tests. Iron deficiency was less prevalent among patients above the age of 65, affecting only about one-third of the population. Most of these individuals were females. A plausible justification can be attributed to menopause in this age group.

Geographically, Makkah, Al-Baha, and the Eastern province had the highest prevalence of iron deficiency across all age groups and genders. Interestingly, when accounting for age and sex, Makkah and Al-Baha had the lowest prevalence of iron deficiency among females aged ≥15 years.

In a study conducted on female university students in Dammam, the prevalence of iron deficiency anemia (IDA) was reported to be 38.3%, with participants being females between 18 and 24 years old. They used a ferritin cut-off of <15 ng/mL.35 Our study, however, reports a substantially higher prevalence of 61.8%. Several factors could account for this discrepancy: first, our study encompassed all patients from the Eastern Province, not just the city of Dammam; second, we included a broader age range; finally, we employed a higher ferritin cut-off of <30 ng/mL. These methodological differences likely contributed to capturing a higher number of IDA cases. Another study from Makkah enrolling a larger number of patients of both genders reported a 47.1% rate of microcytic anemia. However, since ferritin levels were not available in this study, the specific rate of IDA cannot be determined.13

Despite a study reporting the lack of correlation between serum ferritin levels and platelet counts,36 our findings show a statistically significant difference in platelet counts between the 2 ferritin groups. These findings align with many published textbooks and distinguished guidelines.24,37 Our data also demonstrate statistically significant differences in MCV in patients with hypoferritinemia compared to patients with normal ferritin levels. We also note a significant proportion of individuals with no iron deficiency but low MCV (25.7%), which is likely a reflection of the high prevalence of thalassemia trait in the population, which is estimated at 12.9 thalassemia trait per 1000 according to the most recent data from the premarital screening program,38 while previous estimates were at 32.9 per 1000 in 2004.39 On the other hand, our data remind us that patients could be iron deficient yet not necessarily anemic or have a low MCV. All of this highlights the importance of always investigating new anemia thoroughly.

One limitation of our study is its retrospective design. However, this may not be of significant impact given that many of these patients were not referred for investigations, and a considerable number of the samples were electively drawn for general health screening. Additionally, we included only patients with complete data, further ensuring the reliability of our study.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the substantial burden of anaemia and, specifically, iron deficiency among the population of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. It offers a robust foundation for developing national strategies to manage and mitigate iron deficiency nationwide through social or dietary factors, especially in areas with the highest prevalence. We suggest a nationwide screening program for women of childbearing age, investigating the complete iron profile rather than relying solely on CBC values, and unifying the ferritin cut-off to <30 ng/mL, which can help standardize the diagnostic algorithm and limit the inter-institutional discrepancies in practice.
