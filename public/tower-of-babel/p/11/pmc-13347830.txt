A Longitudinal Cohort Study of the Costs of Adolescent Obesity Into Young Adulthood
Authors: Antonije Lazic, Sarah L R Crawford, Nichole Stanley, Michael R Thomsen, Clare C Brown, Ronald F Kahn, Joseph W Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAdolescent obesity is a strong predictor of adult obesity and increases the risk of costly comorbidities. Estimating the long‐term individual medical expenditures associated with adolescent weight status is critical for evaluating the cost‐effectiveness of prevention and treatment strategies.ObjectiveMeasure the association between adolescent weight status and health care expenditures in early adulthood.adulthood.MethodsThis longitudinal cohort study linked students' 10th grade body weight to health care expenditures (total medical, outpatient, inpatient, emergency department, and pharmacy) during the 30th year of life. A two‐part model was used to assess differences in costs within the extensive (having “any” spending) or intensive (amount of spending among those with spending) margins. Marginal effects were used to estimate the difference in health care expenditures at age 30 between individuals with higher weight status in the 10th grade (overweight, obesity, or severe obesity) compared with those with a healthy weight.ResultsIndividuals with obesity in the 10th grade had greater total annual expenditures at age 30 ($542, 95% CI $139–$945) compared to individuals with a healthy weight. Individuals with severe obesity had higher expenditures across all outcomes. Total medical expenditures were $874 more than individuals with a healthy weight, while inpatient expenditures were $159 more, outpatient expenditures were $387 more, and pharmacy expenditures were $222 more. Differences in inpatient expenditures were primarily at the intensive margin, while emergency department expenditures differed mainly at the extensive margin. Outpatient expenditures varied at both margins.ConclusionsHigher weight status in adolescence is associated with higher health expenditures in young adulthood, with the highest spending among 10th graders with severe obesity.Keywords: adolescence, health care expenditures, obesity

Introduction

The prevalence of adolescent obesity has risen rapidly in the past few decades with current levels (20%) almost four times higher than 40 years ago (5%) [1]. Adolescent obesity has become a major public health issue, as evidenced by one of the goals for Healthy People 2030 being a reduction in the proportion of adolescents with obesity [2].

The substantial rise in adolescent obesity is concerning given that adolescent obesity not only has immediate negative health impacts [3, 4], but also has potentially greater long‐term health impacts if left unchecked. Adolescent obesity is a good predictor of adult obesity, because 80% of adolescents who are obese remain obese into adulthood [5]. Obesity has significant economic consequences for the US. Recent estimates (2023) suggest that obesity is associated with an additional $1514 per employee in medical expenditures annually and accounts for more than $347.5 billion in economic burden within the civilian workforce alone [6]. These additional expenditures are largely attributed to obesity increasing the probability of clinical comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, liver disease, cancer, heart disease, and mood disorders [7].

Given the negative health and economic consequences of adolescent obesity, there is growing interest in the cost‐effectiveness of interventions, in particular obesity management medications, to combat adolescent obesity [8, 9]. A useful metric for evaluating the cost effectiveness of particular interventions is the future cost of adolescent obesity. There have been several attempts to estimate the direct medical costs associated with adolescent obesity [10, 11, 12, 13]. Most of the studies have shown higher costs for adolescents with obesity; however, the derived cost estimates are primarily from population‐based estimates rather than individual based data [10, 11, 12, 14]. Thus, previous cost estimates are not directly tied to the individual. In contrast to some of these earlier population‐based approaches [13], this study uses a unique dataset to evaluate the association of an adolescent's weight status on their health care expenditures into young adulthood (age 30) using a longitudinal population‐based cohort from the Arkansas BMI panel [15].

Materials and Methods

Participants

To assess the impact of adolescent obesity on health care expenditures into young adulthood, a longitudinal cohort was constructed using claims data from the Arkansas All‐Payer Claims Database (APCD) and 10th graders' BMI data from the nation's longest continuous statewide BMI screening program [15]. BMI data of individual 10th graders, measured between 2004 and 2008, were obtained from the Arkansas BMI Panel. This legislatively mandated annual screening program covers all K‐12 public schools in the state, reaching the majority of students enrolled in public education, except for those who were absent, declined the weight assessment, or were not measured due to reasons such as pregnancy [15, 16]. During the assessment, a student's height and weight were measured by a school nurse with the help of another adult recorder using a stadiometer and a digital scale. Additional details on the screening protocol are documented in the Arkansas Center for Health Improvement training document, BMI Screening Program: Height and Weight Measurement Training Manual [17].

The APCD contains membership information and insurance claims from payers in Arkansas, including commercial and Medicaid recipients [18]. The BMI data from the 10th graders was linked to their claims expenditure data at age 30 (i.e., the full year starting on their 30th birthday) from the Arkansas APCD (years 2016–2023) using a de‐identified personal key. Individuals who lacked a valid BMI measurement in 10th grade, could not be linked to the APCD, were not between the ages of 14 and 17 in 10th grade, lacked continuous medical or pharmacy coverage (at age 30), or would have been classified as underweight in 10th grade based on the growth charts provided by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) were excluded [27]. Additionally, individuals were geographically linked to their county‐level information (at age 30) to access variables from the Area Health Resource File (AHRF) for 2019 [19].

Outcomes

The primary goal of this study was to compare expenditures at age 30 among individuals from different weight categories during adolescence. All medical and pharmacy expenditures incurred between their 30th birthday and the day before turning 31 years of age were summed for each individual. Expenditures were defined as the total of both the insurer's payments and any out‐of‐pocket costs incurred by the enrollee, including copayments, deductibles, and coinsurance. Expenditure outcomes included outpatient, inpatient, emergency department, pharmacy, total medical expenditures, and total medical expenditures excluding pharmacy. Outpatient, inpatient, and emergency room visits were identified using definitions from the Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS) [20]. Additionally, expenditures were adjusted to 2023 dollars using the medical Consumer Price Index by the US Bureau of Labor Statistics to account for inflation [21].

Variable of Interest

The primary variable of interest in this study was an adolescent's weight status in the 10th grade. Weight status for 10th graders was defined using four categories based on age and sex‐specific BMI(weight(lb.)/[height(in)]2x703): (1) healthy weight (BMI ≥ 5th to < 85th percentile), (2) overweight (BMI 85th to < 95th percentile), (3) obesity (BMI ≥ 95th to < 120th percentile), and (4) severe obesity BMI ≥ 120th percentile). The measurements were based on growth charts provided by the CDC [22].

Covariates

Demographic covariates from the BMI panel included sex (male or female) and race/ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic, and other), and the date of birth (used to determine when an individual reached the age of 30). Information about insurance coverage came from the APCD (commercial or Medicaid). Finally, using the Area Health Resource File, models were adjusted for county‐level poverty (yes or no, defined by 2014 Persistent Poverty and High Poverty County Typology Codes), rurality (Rural/Urban Continuum Codes, or RUCA: metropolitan, urban, or rural), and access to health care providers (total number of medical doctors (MDs) per 1000 residents in each county, total number of hospitals per 1000 residents in each county, and the health provider shortage area (HPSA) variable (none, whole, or partial)).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to assess differences in covariates among individuals across the four weight categories. Unadjusted differences between the four weight categories were assessed using ANOVA and chi‐square tests. Because health care expenditures are often right skewed and exhibit increasing variance with larger values, a generalized linear model (GLM) with a gamma distribution and log link was employed to model the expenditures [23].

Additionally, health care expenditures often have a large percentage of zero or null values, making it challenging to distinguish whether observed differences stem from the proportion of individuals using a resource (extensive margin) or from changes in the amount of utilization among those using the resource (intensive margin) [24]. The proportion of non‐zero values was evaluated for each outcome to assess if the use of a two‐part model would be necessary. A two‐part model assesses overall costs and includes both the extensive and intensive margins. In the first part, the probability of an individual incurring any expenditures is assessed using a logit model. This probability is then multiplied by the expected expenditures for individuals who had expenditures greater than zero [24, 25].

After evaluating the proportion of zeros for each outcome, three separate models were constructed for each outcome. The first model used a two‐part model for the outcome. This model produced estimates that account for both the extensive and intensive margins and represented the overall dollar difference in health care expenditures in each weight category. However, to quantify and assess the significance of both the intensive and extensive margins separately, two additional models were constructed. Thus, the second model separated the effect of the extensive margin by using a logit that estimated the probability of having any expenditures. Average marginal effects expressed as percentage points were calculated to identify differences in utilizing each of the health care spending categories between each weight status group. The third and final model applied the gamma distribution and log link to each outcome, focusing on individuals with expenditures greater than zero for each of the outcome measures. The gamma model captures differences at the intensive margin, which represents the dollar difference in expenditures among individuals who utilized some services within each outcome. Finally, adjusted percentages were computed for each of the outcomes to estimate the percent change in adulthood utilization if individuals with higher weight statuses (as measured in adolescence) had instead been at a healthy weight in the 10th grade.

Data pre‐processing was conducted in SAS 9.4, and statistical analysis was completed in STATA 14. Estimates are reported as average marginal effects and calculated using the margins, dydx(*) command [26]. Standard errors are clustered at the county level.

This study was determined to be non‐human subjects research by the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences Institutional Review Board (#275173).

Results

There were 124,392 10th graders measured from 2004 to 2008 who had a valid BMI measurement. Of the initial sample of 10th graders 121,451 (97.6%) were between the ages of 14 and 17 in the 10th grade and had no missing demographic information. For these individuals, 96,204 (79%) were successfully matched to enrollment records in the APCD at age 30 and 31,577 (33%) of the matched records were continuously enrolled in both medical and pharmacy coverage for that year (Figure 1). Adolescents with a weight status of underweight were excluded because they were not representative of the population of interest and individuals with an underweight status represented only about 1% of the population (n = 413). Among the final sample, 18,366 (58.9%) had a healthy weight status in the 10th grade, 4174 (13.4%) had an overweight status, 5631 (18.1%) had obesity, and 2993 (9.6%) had severe obesity. Unadjusted differences indicate that the sample significantly differed by all covariates based on 10th grade weight status, except for the year in which one turned 30 (p = 0.167) (Table 1).

Most individuals had at least some outpatient (81.0%) or pharmacy expenses (72.4%), but a minority of participants had an emergency department (22.2%) or inpatient expense (8.9%) (Table 2). When examining the unadjusted differences in the percentage of individuals with at least some expenses across outcome measures, only emergency department (p < 0.001) and pharmacy expenses (p = 0.017) significantly varied by 10th grade weight status (Table 2). Notably, the higher the weight status in 10th grade, the greater the probability of incurring at least some emergency department expenditures (Table 2). Specifically, 20.4% of those with a healthy weight status in 10th grade had emergency department spending during the 30th year, compared to 23.9% of those who had an overweight status, 24.0% of those who had obesity, and 27.3% who had severe obesity. However, with pharmacy expenditures, the relationships were less clear because individuals who had obesity in the 10th grade had a lower probability of having pharmacy expenses than individuals who had a healthy weight in the 10th grade (71.6% obesity vs. 72.0% healthy weight). However, individuals who had severe obesity in the 10th grade had a higher probability of having had at least some pharmacy expenses compared to those who had a healthy weight in the 10th grade (73.5% severe obesity vs. 72.0% healthy weight) (p = 0.017) (Table 2).

To determine if the differences in the two‐part model were predominantly driven by the extensive or intensive margin (or both), two additional separate models were estimated for each outcome (Table 3). For total medical and outpatient expenses, significant differences in the extensive and intensive margins were observed for those who had obesity or severe obesity in the 10th grade compared with individuals who had a healthy weight in the 10th grade. The model assessing differences in the extensive margin indicated that the probability of having any medical or outpatient expense is significantly higher for individuals who had obesity (2% points (PP) more likely for both outcomes, total medical: p = 0.008 and outpatient: p = 0.003) or severe obesity (3 PP more likely for both outcomes, total medical: p < 0.001 and outpatient: p < 0.001) compared to individuals who had a healthy weight in 10th grade (Table 3). In the GLM models, including only individuals who had at least some expenses for each outcome, the models indicated that on average individuals who had had obesity or severe obesity had higher total medical (obesity $426, p = 0.005; severe obesity $652, p = 0.001) and outpatient expenses (obesity $257, p < 0.001; severe obesity $383, p < 0.001).

The two‐part model also indicated significantly higher emergency department expenses for all higher weight categories (overweight, obesity, severe obesity). However, the difference in emergency department expenses in adulthood appeared to be driven primarily by the higher levels of utilization among adults who had a higher weight status in 10th grade with each higher weight category having an additional percentage point greater probability of incurring at least some emergency department expense compared to individuals who had a healthy weight in 10th grade (overweight 2 PP (p < 0.001), obesity 3 PP (p < 0.001), and severe obesity 4 PP (p < 0.001) greater). Additionally, the two‐part model indicated significantly higher inpatient expenses for individuals who had severe obesity ($159; p = 0.039) compared with individuals who had a healthy weight in the 10th grade. However, the difference in inpatient expenses is driven by individuals who had at least some inpatient expenses. Individuals who had severe obesity in the 10th grade, if hospitalized, spent on average an additional $1715 (p < 0.001) in inpatient expenses at the age of 30 compared to individuals who reported a healthy weight in the 10th grade.

Finally, the two‐part model for pharmacy expenses indicated that individuals who had an overweight ($83; p = 0.024), obesity ($84; p = 0.048), or severe obesity ($222; p < 0.001) status in 10th grade had higher pharmacy expenses at age 30 than individuals who had a healthy weight in 10th grade. In addition, the model assessing differences in the extensive margin also indicated a greater probability of having some pharmacy expenses (at age 30) for all higher weight statuses in 10th grade (2 PP for overweight (p < 0.003) or obesity (p = 0.012) and 5 PP greater for severe obesity (p < 0.001)). However, only severe obesity showed a significant difference among those who had some expenses with a $233 (p = 0.002) higher expense (at the age of 30) compared to individuals with a healthy weight status in the 10th grade.

Using the extensive models, the adjusted means were calculated to show the adjusted percentage of individuals utilizing each service (Table 4). Discussion

This study used longitudinal data combining insurance claims data from a statewide APCD and the nation's oldest continuous statewide BMI screening program [15] to assess whether a student's 10th grade weight status is associated with their future health care expenditures at the age of 30. The results indicated that a student's 10th grade weight status was associated with their health care expenditures at the age of 30 with a general upward pattern, meaning the higher the weight status in 10th grade the higher health care expenditures for most health care outcomes.

Previous studies have estimated the future cost of adolescent obesity [10, 11, 12, 14]. However, this study has several advantages over previous reports. These include the use of a longitudinal cohort that generated health care expenditures in recent years (2016–2023) and the classification of obesity and severe obesity as separate categories. Most previous studies used census or national‐level data to simulate future expenses, but these estimates were calculated using data for the same individual in adolescence and young adulthood, enhancing confidence in the expenditure estimates associated with adolescent weight status [13]. The results indicate that adolescents who had severe obesity incurred even higher future expenses than adolescents with obesity when compared to adolescents with a healthy weight for all outcomes. Furthermore, previous use of only one category of obesity could have resulted in a null result for certain outcomes that would have been associated with severe obesity. For example, this study suggests no significant association between a 10th grader having obesity and their inpatient expenses at the age of 30. However, individuals who had severe obesity in the 10th grade had an average of $159 more inpatient expenses at the age of 30 than individuals who had a healthy weight status in the 10th grade.

In addition, the inclusion of severe obesity as a separate category showed a gradient trend in increased expenditures and utilization with increasing weight. For example, this study found even higher annual expenditures at the age of 30 associated with severe obesity compared with obesity in the 10th grade for total medical care ($874 vs. $540), total medical excluding pharmacy ($669 vs. $414), outpatient ($387 vs. $249), and pharmacy ($222 vs. $84).

Finally, this study generated marginal effects for both the two‐part model and the individual components of a two‐part model. The advantage of this evaluation approach and design is that it can determine whether the primary driver of cost differences is due to the greater number of individuals using a service (extensive) or due to the level of utilization among those using the resource (intensive) in the higher weight categories (overweight, obesity, and severe obesity) compared to individuals who had a healthy weight in the 10th grade. For example, the study results indicate that among emergency department expenses, most of the cost difference observed in the two‐part model was driven by the extensive margin. In other words, the primary reason emergency department expenses were higher for individuals who had higher weight categories (overweight, obesity, or severe obesity) is because a greater percentage of individuals in higher weight categories had emergency department care at least once at age 30. On the other hand, when looking at inpatient expenses, no significant difference was seen in the extensive margin, the proportion hospitalized, between individuals who had severe obesity or a healthy weight in the 10th grade. However, $1715 higher annual expenditures were seen at the intensive margin for those hospitalized. Put another way, there is not a significant difference in the percentage of individuals using inpatient services among weight classes, but among those who are using inpatient services, those who had severe obesity in the 10th grade will have higher spending than those who had a healthy weight.

Furthermore, this study shows that total medical and outpatient expenses at the age of 30 have significant differences in both the extensive and intensive margins for individuals who had obesity or severe obesity in the 10th grade compared with individuals who had a healthy weight. Thus, cost differences between the weight categories are driven by a greater percentage of individuals having outpatient expenses and due to individuals having outpatient expenses spending more on average. The same pattern is observed for total medical expenses, which is likely due to outpatient expenses being the primary drivers of medical expenditures at the age of 30. Finally, pharmacy expenses indicate significantly higher expenses annually for individuals with higher weight categories ($83 for overweight, $84 for obesity, and $224 for severe obesity). Most of the difference in cost is driven by a greater percentage of individuals (extensive margin) having at least some pharmacy expenses at the age of 30. However, for individuals who had severe obesity in the 10th grade, there was also a $235 higher annual spending among those who had at least some pharmacy expenses (intensive margin).

This study only examined individuals' health care costs at age 30. Adolescent obesity is associated with many diseases into young adulthood, including circulatory, endocrine, metabolic, and overall morbidity [27]. The differentials in disease progression observed at age 30 among individuals who had excessive weight in the 10th grade can be expected to increase over time [28] and when considered cumulatively. Ongoing assessments are needed to adequately provide differential costs associated with obesity to better inform and guide future interventions. These findings could inform cost‐benefit analyses for existing and new obesity treatment strategies.

Limitations

Arkansas has among the lowest health care costs per capita and one of the highest proportions of Medicaid enrollees in the nation [29, 30]. Therefore, these findings may not be generalizable to other populations and are likely to be lower estimates given the higher proportion of Medicaid insurance at age 30 among the adolescents with obesity and severe obesity. In addition, this analysis did not evaluate what drove the difference in cost within some of the outcomes. For example, this study did not investigate what factors (such as procedures, length of stay, or severity of illness) drove the observed $1715 difference in inpatient expenses (at the age of 30) among those who had some inpatient expenses for individuals who had severe obesity in the 10th grade compared to individuals who had a healthy weight in the 10th grade. For this 15‐year longitudinal cohort of 124,392 10th graders, 79% were successfully matched in the APCD at age 30, with 33% meeting continuous enrollment criteria, resulting in the potential of attrition bias. The distribution of demographics by weight categories for 10th graders included or not included are in Table S1. In addition, because this is an association‐based study, there is potential for residual confounding that may influence the results [31].

Conclusions

The higher the weight status in the 10th grade, the higher one's health care expenditures into young adulthood. Given that in 2021, the prevalence of adolescents who were overweight or had obesity exceeded 40% for all 50 states [32], there is great potential for health care expenditure reduction if weight reduction policies were successfully implemented during or before adolescence. Conversely, failure to limit increases in adolescent obesity portends increasing financial strain on the US. health care system. For these changes, the cost difference between weight categories may be driven by the extensive or intensive margin or both. Thus, given the clinical and cost implications of obesity into young adulthood, continued efforts should aim to address the epidemic rates of adolescent obesity.
