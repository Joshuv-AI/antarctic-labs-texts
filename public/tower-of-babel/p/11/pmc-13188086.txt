Closed reduction of acute posterior shoulder dislocation via intra-articular anesthesia: a case report and technical note
Authors: Andrea Redler, Alessandro Maggiori, Vito Caiolo, Cristiano Benelli, Damiano Caruso, Raffaele Iorio, Nicola Maffulli
Published: 2026

Acute posterior shoulder dislocations (PSDs) are rare, accounting for 2%-5% of all shoulder dislocations.15 The most common mechanism for unilateral PSD is an anterior-to-posterior trauma to an externally rotated and abducted shoulder.1 PSDs may also result from electric shocks or seizures due to imbalanced muscle contraction. This occurs when the internal rotator muscles contract with greater force than the external rotators, causing the humeral head to move superiorly and posteriorly.1

Acute PSDs are often missed in up to 50% of cases despite typical clinical and radiographic signs.16 Accurate clinical examination is mandatory in a suspected PSD; classic signs include a prominent coracoid process with posterior fullness and flattening of the anterior aspect. In addition, the shoulder is often locked in an internally rotated and adducted position.11,12,15

Imaging should traditionally include anteroposterior, Velpeau, and axillary view radiographs; indirect radiological signs include the lightbulb sign, the trough-line sign, and loss of the half-moon overlap sign.3,8,20 However, when a PSD is suspected, advanced imaging should be prioritized. A low-dose computed tomography (CT) protocol is currently considered the gold standard to confirm the diagnosis, accurately quantify the size of the reverse Hill–Sachs lesion, and rule out associated glenoid rim fractures, which are critical for determining the success of a closed reduction.10

Therapeutic options vary depending on the patient's age, comorbidities, and the presence of associated fractures. Closed reduction should be the initial choice to restore stability, while open reduction is reserved for failed closed attempts. Many methods are described for anterior dislocations, such as Hippocratic, Stimson, Fast Reliable Safe (FARES) method,22 Eachempati,7 Milch and Kocher techniques,2,5 although their success rate in PSDs is less defined. Traditionally, these maneuvers are performed under general anesthesia or deep sedation to overcome muscle spasm. However, recent evidence suggests that intra-articular anesthetic injections provide comparable success rates and patient satisfaction, with fewer complications and shorter emergency department stays.13

The present study reports a case of an acute PSD successfully treated in our emergency department using a modified version of the assisted self-reduction Kocher maneuver following intra-articular injection of 2% lidocaine. This technical note discusses the advantages and potential limitations of this approach compared to traditional techniques.

Case report and technique description

A 53-year-old road worker was admitted to our emergency department reporting a fall from a scaffold with a direct injury to his left shoulder. On physical examination, the left shoulder appeared slightly deformed, with a depression of the deltoid-pectoral groove “trench sign” (Fig. 1); his left arm appeared intra-rotated and adducted with limited mobility in external rotation and abduction (Fig. 2). The patient was unable to actively external rotate the arm and reported severe pain in the passive mobilization of the limb.

Evidence of “trench sign” in the affected shoulder.

Clinical presentation of the acute posterior shoulder dislocation. The left arm is locked in a position of internal rotation and slight adduction. Note the clinical deformity with flattening of the anterior shoulder profile and limited passive external rotation.

Plain radiographs including anteroposterior, scapular Y, and axillary views showed typical signs of a PSD with an associated engaged reverse Hill–Sachs lesion: (1) lightbulb sign; (2) half-moon overlap sign; (3) trough-line sign (Fig. 3, A-C). Although a low-dose CT scan is recommended as the gold standard for suspected PSD, in this specific case, the pathognomonic radiographic signs and the clinical presentation were considered sufficient to confirm the diagnosis and proceed with an immediate reduction attempt.

Pre-operative radiographic evaluation of the left shoulder. (a) Anteroposterior view demonstrating the "lightbulb sign" due to fixed internal rotation of the humeral head; (b) the "trough-line sign," appearing as a vertical line medial to the lesser tuberosity caused by an impaction fracture; (c) loss of the "half-moon overlap sign" between the humeral head and the glenoid, pathognomonic for posterior dislocation.

The first attempt to reduce the dislocation was made without the administration of any sedatives with a modified version of the assisted self-reduction Kocher maneuver (reverse Kocher maneuver). The patient was informed by the physician before the procedure that the reduction maneuver would be performed using a minimally invasive approach that required his cooperation. The maneuver was demonstrated first on the unaffected ted limb. The shoulder movements were checked in real-time using fluoroscopy. The patient lay supine, and the surgeon stood on the side of the dislocated shoulder. The elbow was kept in full extension. The procedure was performed in 3 steps: (1) the arm was adducted up to the chest; (2) the arm was slowly rotated internally; and (3) the arm was elevated in the sagittal plane. On the first attempt, the patient was unable to complete the procedure because of extreme pain.

After obtaining informed consent, we performed an intra-articular injection with 10 ml of lidocaine 2% using an ultrasound-guided technique with the transducer placed anteriorly on the shoulder and the beam parallel to the coracoid process.

A second attempt was made with the same maneuver. This time the patient reported minimal discomfort, and he was able to successfully complete the procedure under guidance but without any help from the physician. When the arm reached approximately 30 degrees of frontal flexion and 30 degrees of adduction, the shoulder relocated.

Following the reduction, the arm was gently internally rotated to position the forearm over the chest, and a sling-and-swathe orthosis was employed to keep the arm in a stable position. Postreduction radiographs and a CT scan were obtained to confirm the concentric reduction and to accurately quantify the humeral head defect (McLaughlin lesion). The CT scan was specifically indicated to assess the engagement of the reverse Hill–Sachs lesion and to rule out associated glenoid rim fractures, which are critical for determining the risk of recurrence. The CT confirmed the reduction and showed a McLaughlin lesion <30% of the articular surface (Figs. 4 and 5).

Post-reduction plain radiographs: (a) anteroposterior view sign, (b) axillary view, and (c) transthoracic view.

Post-reduction CT scan in axial view. CT, computed tomography.

The orthosis was maintained for 3 weeks. After the 3-week immobilization period, a structured physiotherapy and rehabilitation program began. The patient returned for follow-up at 3 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months. At the end of the follow-up, he had a stable joint and complete range of motion. He reported no pain in everyday activities and a Constant score of 92.

Discussion

PSDs are uncommon and difficult to diagnose. Diagnosis is often delayed because of subtle imaging findings, resulting in a posteriorly locked humeral head. The limited number of patients with locked PSDs precludes the establishment of a sizable patient cohort, making it impossible to derive an evidence-based treatment strategy from the existing literature.1 The current scientific evidence suggests several treatment strategies, each of which is based on classifications that have not yet been standardized. PSDs can be acute (<3 weeks), chronic (>3 weeks), or recurrent.4 PSD may also be classified as traumatic or atraumatic, acute or persistent, or recurrent voluntary.14 The humeral head defect can be classified as small (<25%), medicine (25% to 50%), and large (>50%). Although no classification is yet standardized, the size of the humeral head defect is critical for planning an optimal treatment strategy.

Many methods have been described for closed reduction of anterior shoulder dislocations and have often also been used for PSD in combination with general anesthesia. These can be divided into (1) traction-countertraction techniques, (2) leverage techniques, and (3) scapular manipulation techniques. Compared to these traditional approaches, which often rely on significant manual traction, our modified Kocher approach focuses on a gentle three-step path that anatomically guides the humeral head back into the glenoid fossa, potentially reducing iatrogenic risk.9 Turturro et al24 demonstrated that the assisted self-reduction technique for anterior shoulder dislocation achieved excellent results without the use of any type of medication. A systematic review showed that closed reduction should be attempted only in acute cases with a small humeral head defect,18 while Duralde and Fogle6 demonstrated excellent reduction results under general anesthesia in patients with acute PSD with a humeral defect of ≤30%. Rouleau et al20 described 2 methods of reducing PSD, both involving mechanical pulling by the operator or using weights.

Regarding intra-articular lidocaine injection (IAL) to reduce PSDs, the literature is very scarce. Socransky et al23 described a case of an elderly woman whose reduction was performed following IAL. A common concern regarding IAL is its efficacy in managing muscle spasm compared to general anesthesia. However, although lidocaine is not a systemic muscle relaxant, it effectively interrupts the pain-reflex arc. By eliminating the acute nociceptive stimulus, the secondary protective muscle guarding is significantly reduced, allowing for a controlled reduction.17 Waterbrook et al25 compared IAL and intravascular sedation in patients with anterior shoulder dislocation, showing no statistically significant differences in success rate and pain reduction. Significant differences were found in length of stay and cost, demonstrating that IAL is more cost-effective, and the shoulder reduction time is shorter.

Despite these advantages, some limitations must be considered. While IAL is generally safe, Piper et al19 examined the effects of local anesthetics on cartilage and noted that the risk of chondrolysis increases with longer exposure and higher concentrations. However, there are very little data on the long-term effects of a single intra-articular anesthetic injection. In our patient, we used a modified version of the assisted self-reduction technique described by Turturro et al24 based on the Kocher technique for acute PSD. Confirming Turturro's findings, the absence of pain during the procedure, following the lidocaine intra-articular injection, allowed our patient to reduce the dislocation almost independently and without any intravenous sedatives or general anesthesia. The use of IAL is usually not necessary in anterior shoulder dislocation reductions; however, in PSD it seems to be very useful in reducing the typically higher level of pain. Nevertheless, the efficacy of this technical modification cannot be fully established through a single case report. Its success may be limited in cases with larger engaged reverse Hill–Sachs lesions or in patients with extreme anxiety where systemic relaxation remains necessary.21 Further studies are required to statistically confirm the reliability and safety of this approach in broader clinical settings.

Conclusion

The diagnosis of PSD remains challenging. Emphasis should be placed on the correct interpretation of clinical signs, such as the depression of the deltoid-pectoral groove (“trench sign”), and pathognomonic radiographic findings. The assisted self-reduction technique reported here (reverse Kocher maneuver) may represent a viable alternative to the maneuvers previously described in the literature. In our experience, this approach, when combined with an ultrasound-guided intra-articular injection of lidocaine, allowed for a successful reduction without the need for systemic sedation. While potentially more cost-effective and faster compared to general anesthesia in an emergency setting, further large-scale studies are mandatory to statistically confirm the reliability, safety, and overall effectiveness of this technique before it can be broadly recommended as standard first-line treatment.
