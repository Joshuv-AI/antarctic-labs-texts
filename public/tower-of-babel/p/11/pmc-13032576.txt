The identification of gibberellin 2-oxidase gene family in cotton and functional analysis
Authors: Yating Wei, Ao Feng, Mingzhe Tuo, Shenglong Song, Yong Zhang, Shuaishuai Cheng, Xinyu Zhang, Yanjun Li, Jie Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGibberellin (GA) homeostasis is critical for plant growth and development, with gibberellin 2-oxidases (GA2oxs) functioning as key regulators by deactivating bioactive GAs. Mepiquat chloride (MC), a widely used plant growth regulator in cotton production, effectively suppresses vegetative growth to improve yield and fiber quality, yet its molecular mechanisms, particularly concerning GA catabolism, remain incompletely understood.MethodsIn this study, a genome-wide identification of GA2ox genes was performed in upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum), followed by systematic analysis of their gene structures, conserved protein motifs, chromosomal locations, and expression patterns. Virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) technology was used for perform functional validation of two candidate genes, GhGA2ox6 and GhGA2ox31. RNA-sequencing was used to explore the differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways in GhGA2ox6-silenced plants. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was further employed to explore the key gene modules and Hub genes associated with hormone pathways.ResultsA total of 39 GhGA2ox genes were identified and classified into three subfamilies: C20-GA2ox-I, C19-GA2ox-I, and C19-GA2ox-II. Expression profiling analysis revealed that tissue-specific patterns of GhGA2ox genes, and GhGA2ox6 and GhGA2ox31 were significantly up-regulated in response to MC treatment. VIGS-mediated silencing of GhGA2ox6 resulted in a marked increase in cotton plant height, while silencing GhGA2ox31 had no obvious phenotypic change. Further transcriptomic profiling analysis found that the height-promoting phenotype of GhGA2ox6-silenced plants was driven by a coordinated multi-hormone regulatory network involving gibberllins, auxins, and cytokinins. WGCNA identified 3 gene modules were significantly associated with hormone contents, and 36 Hub genes involved in ethylene, auxin, jasmonate, and gibberenllin pathways.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings indicate that GhGA2ox6 acts as a negative regulator of cotton plant height and is involved in MC-mediated growth regulation. The WGCNA further complemented the multi-hormone coordinated regulatory mechanism underlying GhGA2ox6 function. This study provides novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying MC-regulated cotton growth and offers a potential target gene for modulating cotton plant growth via genetic engineering techniques.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08332-0.

Introduction

Gossypium hirsutum L., also known as upland cotton, is one of the world's vital economic crops and serves as the primary natural fiber source for the global textile industry [49]. China is both one of the largest producers and the largest consumer of cotton, with its cotton industry holding a significant strategic position in the nation's economy and social development [54]. For a long time, China has been among the world leaders in both total cotton output and cultivation area, with the Xinjiang region being the core pillar of its cotton production. This region offers favorable ecological conditions for cotton growth, characterized by abundant sunlight, significant diurnal temperature differences, stable irrigation water sources, suitable soil, and an arid climate that reduces pest and disease pressures when complemented by reliable water supply. According to statistics, in 2025, Xinjiang's cotton planting area reached 2.59 million hectares, accounting for 86.9% of the national total, and its total output was 6.16 million metric tons, representing 92.8% of the national total [33]. This underscores Xinjiang's dominant role in China's cotton production and its crucial importance in ensuring the domestic cotton supply.

The indeterminate growth habit of cotton frequently leads to an imbalance between vegetative and reproductive growth, ultimately leading to yield losses. Coordinating these two developmental phases is therefore critical for achieving high and stable yield, as well as optimizing fiber quality [55]. To address this challenge, mepiquat chloride (MC), a widely used plant growth retardant, has been adopted in cotton production systems [55]. MC exerts its regulatory effects by suppressing excessive vegetative growth, including shortening internode length, reducing plant height, and enhancing root vigor, which in turn improves boll retention rates and promotes fiber quality parameters. Field experiments have confirmed that cotton yields are improved by 8–10% in central China and 15% in northwestern regions following MC application [45, 46, 48].

However, excessive MC application, particularly in vigorous cotton cultivars with inherent MC insensitivity, poses practical challenges, including increased labor input for management, potential mechanical damage, and suboptimal efficacy in regulating growth. The effects of MC on phenotypic and physiological aspects of cotton have been well-documented [2, 4, 45, 46, 48]. Beyond cotton, MC has been applied to other crops. For instance, in non-oilseed sunflower production, MC spraying effectively reduces plant height and lodging risk [44]. In maize, MC enhances lodging resistance by increasing actual stem strength and promoting lignin biosynthesis [25]. Similarly, timely and appropriate application of MC in alfalfa can inhibit plant height, shorten main-stem internode length, thicken internodes, and improve lodging resistance [47]. In soybean, MC has been found to inhibit plant growth by suppressing photosynthesis and cell-wall synthesis. Moreover, MC alters the biosynthesis and signaling of gibberellin (GA) and other plant hormones (e.g., zeatin, brassinolide), and promotes flavonoid accumulation, thereby enhancing drought resistance [45, 46, 48].

Despite these phenotypic and physiological insights, the underlying molecular mechanisms of MC action remain poorly understood. Current evidence suggests that MC inhibits gibberellin (GA) biosynthesis by down-regulating the expression of GA biosynthesis-related genes, such as CPS (ent-copalyl diphosphate synthase), KS (ent-kaurene synthase), and KO (ent-kaurene oxidase) [35]. Although GAs are widely present phytohormones in plants, GA homeostasis is crucial for normal plant growth and development [14].

The concentration of biologically active GAs is regulated by both de novo synthesis and metabolic degradation [18]. Inactivation of active GAs via gibberellin 2-oxidase (GA2ox) gene plays an important role in maintaining plant hormonal balance and modulating growth and development processes. The GA2ox genes encode 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase that catalyze the 2β-hydroxylation of active GAs (such as GA₄, GA₁ and GA₂₀) and their C20-type precursors (e.g., GA₁₂ and GA₅₃), irreversibly converting them into inactive forms (e.g. GA₈ and GA₃₄) to mediate GA catabolism (Liu et al., 2023) [12, 58]. The GA2ox gene family is divided into three categories: C19-GA2ox-I and C19-GA2ox-II primarily target C19-type active GAs to directly reduce endogenous active GA levels, while C20-GA2ox-I acts on C20-type GA precursors to block the biosynthetic pathway of active GAs [29]. The core function of this gene family is the negative regulation of endogenous GA homeostasis in plants [20]. The reduction of bioactive GA levels results in dwarfism, darker leaves, delayed flowering, and also enhances plant stress resistance [29, 53]. This regulatory mechanism forms a feedback loop with GA biosynthetic enzymes and holds important application value in optimizing plant architecture, regulating flowering time, and breeding stress-resistant cultivars (Liu et al., 2023) [6, 58].

The GA2ox genes has been identified in multiple plant species [18, 21–23, 29, 34, 35]. A recent genome-wide study of GA oxidases in Gossypium barbadense has further enriched our understanding of the composition of this gene family and its response to GA in cotton [57]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that GA2ox genes play an important role in regulating plant height and development [38, 58]. Overexpression of GA2ox genes in various plants has been found to cause reducing plant height [29, 41]. In our previous study, the stem of MC-insensitive cultivar XLZ74 and MC-sensitive cultivar SD1068 before and after MC treatment were collected for obtaining transcriptomic data, and several GA2ox genes were identified among the up-regulated differentially expressed genes [45, 46, 48], leading to the hypothesis that MC modulates plant growth not only by down-regulating GA biosynthesis-related genes but also by up-regulating GA2ox genes. In the current study, we systematically identified all GhGA2ox genes from the G. hirsutum genome and analyzed their gene structures, protein motifs, chromosomal distributions, and expression patterns. The virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) technology was used for functional validation of a candidate gene GhGA2ox6. This study aims to lay a theoretical foundation for clarifying the molecular mechanism by which MC regulates cotton growth and development, and to provide key target genes for modulating cotton growth via genetic engineering techniques.

Chromosome localization and classification of the GhGA2ox genes

The 39 genes are randomly distributed across 20 chromosomes of G. hirsutum (Fig. 1A), with 20 genes on the At subgenomes and 19 on the Dt subgenomes. Chromosomes A01, A08, A13, D01, D09 and D13 each harbor 3 members, A05, A06, A09, A10, D05, D06 and D10 each contain 2 members, while the remaining 7 chromosomes each contain 1 member. To predict the function of the GhGA2oxs, we collected previously reported GA2ox sequences from diverse plant species and constructed a phylogenetic tree of the GA2ox family (Fig. 1B). The GA2ox gene family members were classified into three subgroups: C20-GA2ox-I, C19-GA2ox-I, and C19-GA2ox-II. A total of 16 GhGA2oxs were grouped into subgroup C20-GA2ox-I, which are active against C20-GAs. Ten and eleven GhGA2oxs were classified into subgroups C19-GA2ox-I and C19-GA2ox-II, respectively, which are active against C19-GAs.Fig. 1Chromosomal localization of GhGA2ox genes and phylogenetic relationships analysis. Chromosomal location and synteny analysis of GhGA2ox genes

Gene duplication is widely recognized as a key driver of genetic diversification in plant genome evolution [13, 15, 16]. To investigate the expansion mechanisms and evolutionary patterns of the GA2ox gene family in upland cotton, we employed an integrated approach combining BLAST-based sequence alignment and genome-wide collinearity analysis using MCScanX software. Intra-genomic collinearity analysis identified 70 syntenic gene pairs within the GhGA2ox gene family (Table S2), indicating a significant role of segmental duplication in the expansion of this family. Notably, several genes (e.g., GhGA2ox2, GhGA2ox3, and GhGA2ox4) exhibited collinearity with multiple paralogs. For instance, GhGA2ox3 showed syntenic relationships with GhGA2ox6, GhGA2ox15, GhGA2ox23, GhGA2ox26, and GhGA2ox34 (Fig. 2A), suggesting they may represent ancestral loci that have undergone repeated duplication events. In addition to segmental duplication, a putative tandem duplication event was identified involving GhGA2ox10 and GhGA2ox11, which are located within a 100 kb interval on chromosome A08 and share high phylogenetic affinity. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that both segmental and tandem duplication events have contributed to the expansion of the GA2ox gene family in upland cotton. To evaluate the evolutionary constraints acting on the duplicated GhGA2ox genes, we calculated the ratio of non-synonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitution rates (Ka/Ks) for each syntenic gene pair. The Ka/Ks ratios for all 70 pairs ranged from 0.08 to 0.64 (mean: 0.21; Table S2), with all values significantly less than 1. This universal signature indicated that the GhGA2ox gene family has been subjected to strong purifying selection, suggesting that most duplicates genes have retained functional constraints and likely preserved critical ancestral functions following duplication.Fig. 2Intraspecific and Interspecific Collinearity Analysis of GA2ox genes. A Interaspecific Collinearity analysis between the At and Dt subgenome of G. hirsutum. B Interspecific collinearity analysis across three cotton species (G. hirsutum, G. arboreum and G. raimondii)

Intraspecific and Interspecific Collinearity Analysis of GA2ox genes. A Interaspecific Collinearity analysis between the At and Dt subgenome of G. hirsutum. B Interspecific collinearity analysis across three cotton species (G. hirsutum, G. arboreum and G. raimondii)

Comparative intergenomic collinearity analysis across G. hirsutum (AADD), G. arboreum (AA), and G. raimondii (DD) provided insights into the evolutionary history of the GA2ox gene family (Fig. 2B). Extensive synteny relationships were detected, with 97 collinear gene pairs between G. hirsutum and G. arboreum, and 94 collinear pairs between G. hirsutum and G. raimondii. A key evolutionary pattern emerged from the synteny analysis: multiple ‘one-to-many’ correspondences were prevalent between the allotetraploid G. hirsutum and its diploid progenitors. Specifically, a single GhGA2ox gene was often syntenic with several homologous genes in both G. arboreum and G. raimondii, reflecting gene retention following polyploidization. For example, GhGA2ox6 was syntenic with GaGA2ox3, GaGA2ox6, GaGA2ox16 as well as GrGA2ox4, GrGA2ox12, and GrGA2ox15. Similarly, GhGA2ox31 showed collinearity with GaGA2ox7, GaGA2ox12, GaGA2ox15 as well as GrGA2ox7, GrGA2ox13 and GrGA2ox16.

Conserved motifs and structural characterization of the GhGA2ox genes

Conserved motifs within the 39 GhGA2ox proteins were systematically characterized using the MEME suite under defined parameters (maximum motifs: 15; motif length: 9–150 residues). A total of 15 motifs (Motif 1–Motif 15) were identified (Fig. 3B), with motif counts per protein ranging from 4 to 12. The GhGA2ox18 and GhGA2ox37 genes were distinct due to their possession of only 4 motifs each (motif 3, 7, 2 and 4). All members in subgroup C19-GA2ox-II contained 11 motifs (motif 14, 10, 3, 6, 7, 8, 5, 2, 5, 4 and 13), with the presence of motif 3 distinguishing this subgroup from other groups. Subgroup C19-GA2ox-Ishared high motif composition similarity with C19-GA2ox-II, containing 10 motifs, differing only by the absence of motif 3. Subgroup C20-GA2ox-I exhibited distinct motif profiles compared to the two C19 subgroups. Most C20-GA2ox-I members contained unique motifs (motif 15, 9, 11, and 12) that were not detected in C19-GA2ox-I and C19-GA2ox-II. Additionally, the motif counts in C20-GA2ox-I varied greatly, ranging from 5 to 12 motifs. Protein domain analysis revealed that all 39 GhGA2ox proteins contain the 2OG-FeII_Oxy domain, a characteristic 2-oxoglutarate and Fe2⁺-dependent dioxygenase domain, which is widely conserved across diverse proteins belonging to the 2-oxoglutarate/Fe2⁺-dependent dioxygenase superfamily (Fig. 3C). Structural analysis of coding sequences (CDS) revealed variability in exon–intron organization among GhGA2ox genes, with the number of exons ranging from 2 to 4 (Fig. 3D). Notably, most GhGA2ox genes across all three subgroups exhibited similar exon/intron gene structure, consisting of 3 exons and 2 introns. Specifically, 5 genes (GhGA2ox11, GhGA2ox15, GhGA2ox37, GhGA2ox36 and GhGA2ox34) contain 2 exons and 1 intron, and 3 genes (GhGA2ox33, GhGA2ox23 and GhGA2ox3) possess 4 exons and 3 introns.

Cis-acting elements in the GhGA2ox Genes

To elucidate the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of GhGA2ox genes, the cis-regulatory elements within the 2kb promoter regions of 39 GhGA2ox genes were analyzed (Fig. 4). A total of 51 types of cis-regulatory elements were identified, including common ones such as CAAT-box and enhancer regions, as well as multiple other types of cis-regulatory elements. Based on their biological functions, these elements were classified into five categories: hormone-response elements, stress-response elements, light-response elements, growth and development elements, and MYB-binding site elements. Among 39 genes, GhGA2ox1 possessed the greatest diversity of cis-regulatory elements (20 types), while GhGA2ox6 contained the fewest (7 types). Most genes (31) contained elements from all five functional categories. Five genes (GhGA2ox8, GhGA2ox16, GhGA2ox17, GhGA2ox19 and GhGA2ox26) contained elements from four categories (lacking the MYB-binding sites), 2 genes (GhGA2ox12 and GhGA2ox15) contained elements from three categories, and 1 gene (GhGA2ox21) possessed only hormone-responsive and light-responsive elements. The results indicated that the majority of GhGA2ox genes are transcriptionally regulated by multiple factors, indicating hormones, light signals, and stress stimuli, reflecting their potential involvement in diverse physiological and developmental processes.Fig. 4Analysis of Cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of GhGA2ox genes

Tissue-specific expression profiles of the GhGA2ox genes

To elucidate the tissue-specific expression profiles of GhGA2ox genes in upland cotton, we analyzed the expression of GhGA2ox genes in different tissues (ovule, fiber, anther, petal, pistil, root, stem and leaf) based on publicly available RNA-seq data [21]. All the genes can be grouped into 5 classes based on their expression profiles (Fig. 5). The Class I including five genes (GhGA2ox32, GhGA2ox13, GhGA2ox21, GhGA2ox1 and GhGA2ox18) showed high expression in fiber and anther tissues. Additionally, the GhGA2ox1 in this class also exhibited a higher level in roots, and GhGA2ox18 was highly expressed in pistils. Ten genes (GhGA2ox30, GhGA2ox16, GhGA2ox35, GhGA2ox4, GhGA2ox19, GhGA2ox38, GhGA2ox17, GhGA2ox37, GhGA2ox7 and GhGA2ox36) in the Class Ⅱ were preferentially expressed in flower tissues, including anthers, petals or pistils, compared to other tissues. Notably, the GhGA2ox37 in this class also showed a higher expression in roots, and GhGA2ox7 showed a higher expression in 3-DPA (day post anthesis) ovlues, while GhGA2ox11 exhibited low expression across all tested tissues. Seven genes (GhGA2ox24, GhGA2ox9, GhGA2ox29, GhGA2ox20, GhGA2ox39, GhGA2ox2, and GhGA2ox22) in the Class Ⅲ were expressed at higher levels in ovlues, flower tissues, or both. All the genes (GhGA2ox6, GhGA2ox3, GhGA2ox26, GhGA2ox23, and GhGA2ox31) in Class Ⅳ were expressed at higher levels in stems. Among them, GhGA2ox6 and GhGA2ox26 were also expressed at higher levels in leaves, while GhGA2ox3 at a higher level in 25-DPA fibers. All the genes except for GhGA2ox25 in the Class Ⅴ were expressed at higher levels in roots. Among them, GhGA2ox12, GhGA2ox14, GhGA2ox15, GhGA2ox27, GhGA2ox10, and GhGA2ox33 were also expressed at higher levels in stems, GhGA2ox5 and GhGA2ox25 at higher levels in leaves and 25-DPA fibers, and GhGA2ox8 and GhGA2ox28 at higher level in petals.Fig. 5Tissue-specific expression patterns of GhGA2ox genes

The expression profiles of GhGA2ox genes in two varieties (MC-insensitive material XLZ74 and the MC-sensitive material SD1068) after MC treatment were analyzed based on previous trancriptomic data (Fig. 6). Discussion

The precise regulation of gibberellin (GA) homeostasis is fundamental to plant growth and development, with GA 2-oxidases (GA2oxs) playing a critical role in deactivating bioactive GAs to fine-tune this balance [52]. The composition and functional diversification of GA2ox genes in the economically vital allotetraploid cotton remained largely unexplored. Our study provides the first comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the GA2ox gene family in G. hirsutum. Phylogenetic analysis classified 39 GhGA2ox genes into three subfamilies (C20-GA2ox-I, C19-GA2ox-I, and C19-GA2ox-II), which is consistent with classifications in other plant species, suggesting evolutionary conservation of their functions [6, 37]. A core characteristic underpinning the function of all GhGA2ox proteins is the presence of the 2OG-FeII_Oxy conserved domain, which is the hallmark of the 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase superfamily, to which GA2oxs belong [6]. The 2OG-FeII_Oxy domain is responsible for binding the ferrous iron (Fe2⁺) cofactor and the 2-oxoglutarate cosubstrate, which are essential for the catalytic activity of these enzymes. During the reaction, 2-oxoglutarate is decarboxylated, providing the energy necessary for the dioxygenase to hydroxylate its specific GA substrate at the C-2β position, thereby rendering it inactive [17]. The presence of this intact conserved domain in all 39 GhGA2ox proteins validates their functional identity as bona fide GA-inactivating enzymes.

Cis-regulatory elements analysis revealed a complex regulatory network for the GhGA2ox family, with prevalence hormone-responsive (e.g., ABA, JA, SA, IAA), stress-responsive, and light-responsive elements. These elements indicated that the expression of GhGA2ox genes is tightly linked to both developmental and environmental signals, positioning these genes as Hubs for integrating internal and external stimuli to modulate growth via GA regulation [29]. Tissue-specific expression profiling corroborated this, showing distinct expression patterns of GhGA2ox genes across stems, roots, fibers, and floral organs. Notably, GhGA2ox6 was highly expressed in stems, implicating it may play a role in regulating plant height. Based on previous transcriptomic data, GhGA2ox6 was identified as being highly responsive to MC treatment, particularly in the MC-sensitive variety. These observations align with conserved GA2ox regulatory mechanisms across plant species. For example, salt stress induces SpGA2ox while GA3 suppresses it [38]; JA signaling activates rice GA2ox3/7 via MYC2 to balance defense and growth [24]; light signaling modulates GA2ox expression through DELLA-ABI4-HY5 module 51]; and low temperatures up-regulate GA2ox via CBF/DREB1 to enhance cold tolerance [28]. Collectively, the GhGA2ox family may act as a central regulatory node in GA metabolism, integrating hormonal, light, and stress signals to balance plant growth and stress responses.

In this study, silencing GhGA2ox6 led to a significant increase in plant height, confirming its role as a potent negative regulator of stem elongation in cotton. This phenotype is consistent with previous reports of GA2ox over-expression causing dwarfism in species like Arabidopsis thaliana (AtGA2ox7 and AtGA2ox8), switchgrass (PvGA2ox5 and PvGA2ox9) and Poa pratensis (PpGA2ox9) [40, 41, 50]. SilencingGhGA2ox6 resulted in a notable increase in GA15 accompanied by decrease in GA19 and GA53 within the C20-GAs (Fig. 8G), while an elevation in GA29 and GA51 and a reduction in GA1 and GA8 among C19-GAs (Fig. 8F). The alterations in GA contents following GhGA2ox6 silencing are consistent with its classification as a C20-GA2ox-I subfamily member. As C20-GA2oxs typically target C20-GA precursors to block active GA biosynthesis, GhGA2ox6 silencing led to accumulation of GA15 and reduction of downstream C20 intermediates (GA19 and GA53)(Fig. 8K). Concomitantly, the increase in C19-GA derivatives (GA29 and GA51) and decrease in bioactive GA1 and its catabolite GA8 indicate a compensatory shift in C19-GA metabolism, likely driven by feedback regulation of downstream GA homeostasis. This phenomenon resembles the metabolic compensation effect previously observed in Arabidopsis [29]. Notably, the lack of expected elevation in bioactive GA1 aligned with transcriptional reprogramming of GA pathway genes, including up-regulation of GA biosynthetic genes GA3ox1 and GA20ox1, complex modulation of DELLA repressors [7, 9] indicating a feedback loop to maintain GA homeostasis while enabling growth promotion.

The increased plant height phenotype in GhGA2ox6 silenced plants was further supported by integrated auxin and cytokinin (CK) signaling (Fig. 11). Hormone quantification showed reduced IAA-Gly (an inactive auxin conjugate) and increased indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), coupled with transcriptional up-regulation of 27 auxin-related genes in the plant hormone signal transduction pathway, including key regulators such as AUX/IAA, SAUR, and GH3 family members, and the auxin transporter LAX2. SAUR proteins are known to promote cell elongation by acidifying the apoplast [8, 30, 32, 36], providing a direct mechanism for the increased plant height in GhGA2ox6 silenced plants. Conversely, CK pathway suppression, including reduced trans-zeatin (tZ), elevated cis-zeatin riboside monophosphate (cZRMP), and down-regulated CKX/LOG genes likely relieved constraints on shoot elongation, as CKs typically antagonize apical dominance and cell expansion (Li et al., 2021) [19, 27, 56]. The CK pathway suppression likely released a constraint on growth, allowing auxin and gibberellin to act more concertedly in promoting stem elongation [1, 23] (Fig. 11). Collectively, these date demonstrated that GhGA2ox6 silencing drives plant height increase not through a single pathway, but via coordinated transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming of GA, auxin, and CK signaling. The WGCNA (Fig. 10) further complemented the multi-hormone coordinated regulatory mechanism underlying GhGA2ox6 function. This not only validates the functional role of GhGA2ox6 as a C20-GA2ox member but also provides insights into how MC-responsive genes modulate cotton plant height through interconnected hormone signaling cascades.Fig. 11Molecular mechanism model of regulating cotton plant height by silencing GhGA2ox6. In contrast, silencing GhGA2ox31 (a member of the C19‑GA2ox‑II subfamily predicted to target late‑stage, bioactive C19‑type Gas) did not result in a statistically significant change in plant height. This result aligns with reports in other species, indicating that members of this subfamily often mediate more subtle or spatially restricted effects rather than broadly regulating overall plant stature. For example, silencing HvGA2ox9 (a C19‑GA2ox‑II member) in barley did not alter plant height but increased root length by an average of 14% [5]. In rice, OsGA2ox1 is expressed specifically in the shoot apex and roots but not in leaves or stems [39], and CRISPR/Cas9‑mediated knockout of OsGA2ox9 enhanced panicle‑neck elongation and plant height specifically at the heading stage [42].

In this present study, VIGS-mediated silencing of GhGA2ox31 did not significantly affect cotton plant height, while that of GhGA2ox6 resulted in a marked increase in height. Therefore, this study provides insights into the function of GhGA2ox6 in cotton. However, the upstream and downstream regulatory mechanisms controlling its expression remain to be elucidated. To confirm its dwarfing effect and to directly evaluate its potential for shaping plant architecture through genetic engineering, generating transgenic cotton plants over-expressing GhGA2ox6 will be essential in the future.

Conclusion

This study presents the first comprehensive genome-wide identification and functional characterization of the GA2ox gene family in Gossypium hirsutum. We identified 39 GhGA2ox members and classified them into three evolutionarily conserved subfamilies: C20-GA2ox-I, C19-GA2ox-I, and C19-GA2ox-II. Expression profiling analysis revealed that tissue-specific patterns of GhGA2ox genes, and GhGA2ox6 and GhGA2ox31 were significantly up-regulated in response to MC treatment. VIGS-mediated silencing of GhGA2ox6 resulted in a marked increase in cotton plant height, while that of GhGA2ox31 did not lead to statistically significant difference. Further transcriptome analysis found that silencing GhGA2ox6 promotes stem elongation through a coordinated multi-hormone network involving gibberellin metabolic reprogramming, activation of auxin signaling, and suppression of cytokinin pathways. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into how MC regulates cotton growth—not only by inhibiting GA biosynthesis but also by activating GA catabolism via GhGA2ox6. The study highlights GhGA2ox6 as a central node integrating hormonal signals to modulate plant architecture.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Gossypium hirsutum (upland cotton) variety Shida 1068 (SD1068) sensitive to MC was obtained from the Cotton Research Institute of Shihezi University. Cotton seeds were soaked in warm water for 12 h and germinated at 28 °C. Uniformly germinated seeds (post-radicle emergence) were sown in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of nutrient soil and vermiculite, and cultured at 26 °C with 80% humidity and a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. Seedlings were subjected to agroinfiltration for VIGS (virus induced gene silencing) assays when two cotyledons were fully expanded.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from cotton stems and leaves using the Plant RNA EASYspin Plus Kit (Aidlab Biotechnologies Co., Ltd.). Following quality control, the total RNA was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA using the Easyscript® One-Step gDNA Removal andcDNA Synthesis Super Mix (TransGen Biotech). The reaction was conducted under the following conditions: 42 °C for 15 min, followed by 85 °C for 5 s.

Identification of GhGA2ox genes in Gossypium hirsutum

Genomic, CDS, GFF3, and protein sequence files of Arabidopsis thaliana, including GA2ox gene IDs and protein sequences, were retrieved from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR, https://www.arabidopsis.org/, accessed on 17 February 2024). Corresponding sequences of GhGA2ox genes were obtained from the CottonFGD database (https://cottonfgd.org/, accessed on 17 February 2024). Using A. thaliana AtGA2ox protein sequences as queries, BLASTP analysis was performed against the entire G. hirsutum protein database with an E-value cutoff of 1e-20. Additionally, the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) of the 2OG-FeII_Oxy domain (Pfam: PF03171) was downloaded from the Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/, accessed on 17 February 2024) to screen the cotton genome via HMMER 3.3.2. Candidate GhGA2ox genes identified by BLASTP and HMMER were further validated for domain integrity using CD-Search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi/, accessed on 17 April 2024) with a stringent E-value threshold (< 0.01), and sequences with incomplete domains were excluded to finalize the gene family members. Physicochemical properties of GA2ox proteins, including isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weiGHt (MW), were predicted using TBtools (accessed on 5 November 2024).

Phylogenetic tree construction, chromosomal localization and collinearity analysis

Chromosomal localization of GhGA2ox genes was determined by mapping their amino acid sequences to the G. hirsutum genome via BLAST (sequence similarity ≥ 98%), with localization data visualized as a chromosomal map using TBtools. The GA2ox proteins from G. hirsutum and A. thaliana were used to conduct multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis. A neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA11 with default alignment parameters, and branch support was assessed via bootstrap analysis (1000 replicates). The final tree was visualized using the Evolview tool (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview/#/treeview, accessed on 12 February 2025). Collinearity relationships among GhGA2ox homologous gene pairs were illustrated as Circos diagrams generated by TBtools.

Prediction of gene structure, conserved protein domains and Cis-acting elements

Gene structures (intron/exon organization) of GhGA2ox genes were determined by aligning their coding sequences (CDS) with the G. hirsutum genome using TBtools. Conserved domains and their positions in GhGA2ox proteins were analyzed via the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD). Conserved motifs in GhGA2ox proteins were predicted using the MEME online tool (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme, accessed on February 20, 2024) with parameters set as follows: 15 motifs, motif length ranging from 9 to 150 amino acid residues, and use default settings for other parameters. Gene structures (exons/introns) and protein conserved motifs/domains were visualized using TBtools. Promoter sequences (2 kb upstream of the start codon) of GhGA2ox genes were extracted from the G. hirsutum genome database and submitted to the PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be) for cis-acting element prediction. The predicted cis-acting elements were classified by their regulatory functions and visualized with TBtools.

Expression analysis of the GhGA2ox genes

Tissue-specific expression profile data of GhGA2ox genes (across roots, stems, leaves, petals, anthers, stigmas, receptacles, pistils, and stamens) was retrieved from the Cotton Omics Database (http://cotton.zju.edu.cn/10.rnasearch.html, accessed on February 20, 2024) by submitting gene IDs. For the expression analysis under mepiquat chloride (MC) treatment, Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) values were extracted from a previously published transcriptome dataset (BioProject PRJNA800117) [45, 46, 48]. The corresponding experimental design is briefly described as follows: the MC-sensitive parent SD1068 and the MC-insensitive parent XLZ74 were cultivated at the Second Company Experimental Field of the Cotton Research Institute, Shihezi University (44°27′N, 85°94′E). When the third true leaf was fully expanded and the fourth true leaf had not yet unfolded, seedlings were treated with either water or 80 mg/L MC. Young stem tissues adjacent to the apical growing point were collected at 0 h (pre-treatment) and at 1, 3, and 6 days post-treatment for transcriptome sequencing, with three biological replicates per treatment. Detailed experimental procedures followed the method described previously. Heatmaps illustrating tissue-specific expression patterns and MC-responsive expression profiles were generated using TBtools.

Protein homology modeling and molecular docking methods

The full-length protein amino acid sequences of GhGA2ox6 and GhGA2ox31 were retrieved from the CottonFGD database and saved in FASTA format. The 3D structural files of their predicted C19 and C20 gibberellin substrates were downloaded from the PubChem database and saved in SDF format. Homology modeling of GhGA2ox6 and GhGA2ox31 was performed using the SWISS-MODEL online server. After submitting the target protein sequences, the system automatically searched the protein database to identify suitable templates. Priority was given to previously reported crystal structures of plant‑origin gibberellin 2‑oxidases as modeling templates. Following model construction, the quality of each model was evaluated based on composite scores and Ramachandran plots; models that passed quality assessment were downloaded in PDB format for subsequent analyses.

Molecular docking was carried out using the CB‑Dock online server to predict substrate‑binding modes. The homology‑modeled structures of GhGA2ox6 and GhGA2ox31 were uploaded as receptors, and the SDF files of C19 and C20 gibberellin substrates were uploaded as ligands. The server automatically predicted potential active pockets of the proteins and performed rigid docking. Docking results were ranked according to binding free energy, and the pose with the best score was selected for further analysis. Visual inspection confirmed that the substrate molecule was positioned within a hydrophobic cavity containing key catalytic residues and that its C‑2β carbon atom was oriented toward the catalytic active center, yielding the final protein–substrate complex structures.

VIGS vector construction and cotton transformation

The TRV-based VIGS experiment in cotton was conducted as previous report [11]. The VIGS vectors pTRV1, pTRV2 and the positive control construct pTRV2:GhCHLI were kindly provided by Prof. Longfu Zhu (Huazhong Agricultural University). Targeted silencing sequence (300 bp) for candidate gene was obtained using the Sol Genomics Network online VIGS tool. The amplified target fragment was cloned into the tobacco rattle virus (TRV) vector pTRV2 by ClonExpress®Ⅱ One Step Cloning Kit(C112) (Vazyme). Recombinant plasmid pTRV2:GhGA2ox6 was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. pTRV2:GhCHLI was used as positive control, while empty vector TRV:00 was used as negative control. The bacterial suspensions and cotton cotyledon injection were conducted as described previously [11]. Injected seedlings were incubated in the dark at 23–25 °C for 24 h. Seven days post-injection, phenotypic observation of albinism in pTRV2:GhCHLI-treated plants was conducted to preliminarily confirm VIGS efficiency. The plant height (the length from the cotyledon node to the apical bud) of VIGS treated seedlings was measured with a ruler.

The qRT-PCR analysis

For further verify the gene silencing efficiency, qRT-PCR was performed to determine the expression level of the target gene. The GhUBQ7 (GH_A11G1110) served as the internal control. Each 20 µL reaction contained 300 ng cDNA and 10 µL 2 × PerfectStartTm Green qRT-PCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech), following the manufacturer's protocol. The PCR program was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Each sample included three biological replicates, with three technical replicates per biological replicate. Relative expression level of the target gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

cDNA library construction and transcriptome sequencing

Seven days after the onset of albinism, leaves were collected from gene-silenced plants (treatment group) and water-treated plants (control group), with three biological replicates per group. Total RNA from the samples was extracted using the RNAiso Reagent Kit from Takara. Transcriptome libraries were constructed using the Hieff NGS® Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai), and RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform by Nanjing Pisenno Gene Technology Co., Ltd. Raw data were quality-controlled with fastp to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads, yielding clean reads. Clean reads were aligned to the G. hirsutum reference genome TM-1_V2.1 using HISAT2 (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml), an upgraded version of TopHat2. Transcripts were assembled using StringTie based on alignment results.

Sample preparation and LC–MS/MS analysis for hormone contents

Ultra-low temperature-preserved samples were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen using a grinder (30Hz, 1 min). Approximately 50 mg of ground sample was weighed in liquid nitrogen, followed by addition of 10 μL internal standard mixture (100 ng/mL) and 1mL extraction solvent (methanol/water/formic acid, 15:14:1, v/v/v). The mixture was vortexed thoroughly for 10 min, centrifuged at 12,000 r/min and 4 °C for 5 min, and the supernatant was concentrated. The residue was reconstituted in 100 μL of 80% methanol/water, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and transferred to an injection vial for analysis [10, 31]. LC–MS/MS analysis was performed using an Ultra-Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) system (ExionLC™ AD, https://sciex.com.cn/) coupled with a tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) system (QTRAP® 6500 +, https://sciex.com.cn/). Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (1.8 µm, 100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d.) at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min at 40 °C. The mobile phases consisted of ultrapure water with 0.04% acetic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.04% acetic acid (B). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0 min, 95% A/5% B (v/v); 1.0 min, 95% A/5% B; 8.0 min, 5% A/95% B; 9.0 min, 5% A/95% B; 9.1 min, 95% A/5% B; 12.0 min, 95% A/5% B. Mass spectrometry detection were conducted using electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive and negative ion mode with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). The main parameters included the ion spray voltage, 5500 V in positive ion mode and −4500 V in negative ion mode; temperature, 550 °C; curtain gas, 35 psi.

KEGG enrichment analysis

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using the R package clusterProfiler. This analysis examines whether any KEGG pathways are statistically overrepresented in the DEG list compared to the whole genomic background. For each pathway, the analysis employs the hypergeometric test to calculate a P value, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. This process identifies the primary biological functions that the DEGs are most likely involved in.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

The gene expression matrix was processed using the "WGCNA" analysis tool on the Maiwei Cloud Online Analysis Platform (https://cloud.metware.cn) [45, 46, 48]. After module identification and screening, 6 co-expression modules were finally retained. Using a threshold of p-value < 0.01 and correlation coefficient > 0.7, 3 modules significantly associated with hormone content were identified. Then, differentially expressed genes were extracted from these 3 modules, and gene pairs with a correlation coefficient > 0.8 between genes were selected. The Advanced Multi-omics Correlation Network was used for visualization and network construction [3].

Data statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 23.6. Results are presented as mean ± standard error (SE), with differences among samples assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001.
