Wireless blood pressure sensor implantation in sheep: A detailed technique
Authors: David Santer, Paul Ecker, Judith Radloff, Katharina Heissl, Smruti Mirchandani, Sabine Leitner, Bruno Mora, Martin Czerny, Roman Gottardi, Michael Moore, Bruno K. Podesser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiomedical research and preclinical testing in large animal models are essential for developing and evaluating new medical devices. This ovine study presents a stepwise implantation protocol and initial experiences with a wireless blood pressure implant sensor, enabling continuous telemetric monitoring in sheep. Adult female Jezersko‐Solčava sheep underwent implantation of a wireless blood pressure sensor into the ascending aorta via the right carotid artery. The body of the device can was positioned subcutaneously in the lower neck region. After skin closure, a computed tomography scan was performed to verify sensor tip location. If malposition was detected, surgical revision was undertaken immediately. Wireless blood pressure monitoring was conducted over a 24 h period in a custom‐designed sheep barn. Twelve adult female sheep successfully underwent the implantation procedure, with no major complications reported. Sensor catheter revision was required in three animals (25%). The mean duration of the procedure (from incision to skin closure) was 36 ± 10 min. During the 24 h monitoring period, the average systolic and diastolic blood pressures were 93 ± 6 and 65 ± 5 mmHg, respectively. Large animal models, particularly sheep, are indispensable in cardiovascular device research due to their anatomical and physiological similarities to humans. This study demonstrates a rapid, minimally invasive protocol for wireless blood pressure sensor implantation in sheep, offering a valuable platform for chronic telemetric monitoring in the development of cardiovascular medical devices.

INTRODUCTION

Biomedical research and laboratory animal testing have become an inevitable approach to evaluate new medical devices in large animals. 1 Although ethical standards in animal experimentation have continuously improved, the influence of stress on animals' blood pressure and cortisol levels has been reported in various species. 2 , 3 Blood pressure in laboratory animals can be measured using direct and indirect methods; however, noninvasive monitoring has proven inferior to invasive techniques in lambs. 4 Due to the paramount importance of blood pressure monitoring in cardiovascular research, measurement technology has advanced significantly over the past decades. 1 Moreover, with the advent of modern therapeutic strategies, blood pressure regulation has become an even more prominent focus within biomedical research. To meet regulatory requirements and uphold the 3Rs (replacement, reduction, and refinement), 5 researchers request precise and reliable blood pressure monitoring options. Optimal laboratory conditions should provide natural environments to minimize external stressors that could bias study outcomes.

For the evaluation of novel cardiovascular devices, direct blood pressure measurements in conscious, unanesthetized animals represent the optimal setting for obtaining reliable data. This ovine study describes first experiences with the DSI M10‐F2 blood pressure implant (Data Science International [DSI], St. Paul, MN, USA) in sheep, enabling continuous arterial blood pressure monitoring. The presented data are part of a large chronic study in sheep to evaluate a new medical device.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Blood pressure‐sensor device

The DSI M10‐F2 pressure‐sensor implant (DSI) consists of an implant body (13.7 g, dimensions: 40 × 26 × 14 mm; Figure 1A), a wireless transmission antenna (Figure 1B), a pressure‐sensing catheter (outer diameter: 3 French, length: 35 cm; Figure 1C), a gel‐filled pressure tip (Figure 1D), and a radiopaque marker for imaging guidance (Figure 1E). The telemetry system includes the M10‐F2 blood pressure catheter (DSI) that is implanted via the neck, with the catheter tip positioned in the ascending aorta. Communication between the implant and the acquisition hardware is ensured using PhysioTel Digital TRX transceivers (TRX, DSI). All nonimplant hardware components are connected and powered by power over ethernet cables.

DSI M10‐F2 blood pressure implant (Data Science International [DSI], St. Paul, MN, USA). (A) Implant body, (B) antenna, (C) catheter, (D) catheter tip, and (E) radiopaque marker.

All recorded data are stored on a dedicated workstation (Windows 10, Microsoft Corp., Redmond, USA; ThinkStation P340, Lenovo B.V., Amsterdam, the Netherlands; Figure 2A), which is wired to the monitoring stable using a communications link controller (DSI) (Figure 2B) and paired transceivers (TRX 1 and TRX 2; Figure 2B). For continuous blood pressure measurements, these transceivers (TRX) are mounted on an overhead metal rack above the animals in the stable, in a 90° configuration (Figure 2C). During implantation, the workstation and two mobile transceivers (TRX 3 and TRX 4; Figure 2D) are transported into the hybrid operating theater on a cart to enable real‐time intraoperative catheter setup. The mobile transceivers are mounted on adjustable platforms, allowing flexible positioning within the operating room or husbandry to ensure optimal signal reception. Each transceiver can receive signals of the implants within a 5‐m radius. Real‐time pressure monitoring was also used for postoperative care during the acute recovery phase after anesthesia (Figure 2E). Sensor activation was performed using a hand‐held magnet (DSI; Figure 2F), with a magnetic field strength of 0.11 T, by swiping. To compensate for ambient pressure variations, an ambient pressure reference unit (APR‐2, DSI) was integrated into the system. The implants operated within a measurement range of −20 to 300 mmHg and provided an accuracy of +2/−4 mmHg and a sampling resolution of 500 Hz. Animal cohort

Female Jezersko‐Solčava sheep were housed at the Medical University of Vienna, Center for Biomedical Research and Translational Surgery, Himberg, Austria. Animals arrived at least 2 weeks prior to the experiments for acclimatization and were fed with pelleted feed once daily (LS 2 OGT p, Koenigshofer GmbH, Ebergassing, Austria), with unrestricted access to water and hay. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (protocol number: 2024‐0.419.773) and the Austrian Ministry of Science and Technology. All procedures adhered to the guidelines of the Federation of European Laboratory Animal Science Associations, ensuring humane care for all subjects.

Anesthesia

Each surgery was performed under general anesthesia to ensure a pain‐free intervention for the sheep. The day before the intervention, the animal's weight was measured. On the day of the intervention, hay and water were provided ad libitum. Twenty minutes before the intervention, atropinsulfate (1.0 mg/kg i.m. [intramuscular]), ketamine (3.0 mg/kg), and detomidine (0.07 mg/kg i.m.) were administered. After successful intubation and insertion of a gastric tube, anesthesia, 2% propofol (2.5–5 mg/kg i.v. [intravenous]) and fentanyl (0.1 mg i.v.), was induced and then maintained with 2% propofol (10 mg/kg/h i.v.) and fentanyl (0.01 mg/kg/h i.v.). The sheep were then positioned on their backs on a warming mat. The extremities were fixed to the operating table. To avoid corneal damage, ophthalmic ointment (OLEOvital Augensalbe, Fresenius Kabi Austria GmbH, Graz, Austria) was applied to the eyes. Arterial pressure was measured via the auricular artery. Electrocardiogram electrodes were placed on the thorax. Core body temperature was measured via a rectal or esophageal sensor.

Surgical procedure

After surgical disinfection, draping of the whole neck and a team timeout, a short skin incision (7 cm) was performed in the caudal third of the neck above the right carotid artery. The incision was made along the ventral edge of the sternocephalic muscle. At this time, the catheter was removed from its sterile packaging and placed in sterile saline for 30 min according to the manufacturer's instructions. Then a deep subcutaneous pocket for the implant body was prepared digitally, and a swab was provisionally inserted into the pocket. Then the tissue medial to the sternocephalic muscle was prepared layer by layer until the common carotid artery and the external jugular vein were visible. Attention must be paid to the vasosympathetic trunk, which accompanies the common carotid artery. Vessel loops (Primediloop, Primed, Halberstadt, Germany) were then placed at both ends of the carotid artery, and 5000 IU of heparin was administered intravenously (Figure 3A). Then, vessel loops were tightened, a small longitudinal incision of 2–3 mm was performed, and the pressure sensor tip was inserted into the vessel. After the caudal vessel loop was loosened, the catheter was advanced into the left ventricle (Figure 3B), retracted, and positioned as desired (Figure 3C). Finally, the purse‐string suture was tied and fixed around the catheter. The implant body was placed in the subcutaneous pocket and fixed with a nonabsorbable suture. After careful hemostasis, the wound was closed layer by layer. Skin closure was achieved using Donati‐stitches (Perma‐Hand, Johnson & Johnson Medical N.V., Diegem, Belgium; Figure 3D).

Catheter implantation procedure

Prior to insertion into the carotid artery, the viscous gel in the sensor tip must be completely free of air bubbles. If air is present, re‐gelling with biocompatible gel (Re‐gelling syringe, DSI) is performed to eliminate trapped air. The catheter tip should be positioned in the ascending aorta, aligned against the direction of blood flow. After carotid artery incision, the catheter was fully inserted (length: 35 cm) and advanced toward the left ventricle. Blood pressure signal of the implant was continuously monitored in real time throughout the procedure by surgeons and staff. If ventricular pressure signals were absent, the catheter was likely misdirected into the descending aorta. In such cases, the catheter was partially withdrawn and readvanced with the goal of crossing the aortic valve and entering the left ventricle (Figure 4A). Upon detection of a ventricular pressure waveform, the catheter was gradually retracted until a stable arterial pressure curve with a dicrotic notch was observed. From this position, the catheter was further retracted by 2 cm to maintain a safe distance from the aortic valve leaflets. The recorded pressure values were expected to correlate with systemic arterial pressure (Figure 4B). The catheter was then secured using a purse‐string suture. In most cases, an additional purse‐string suture was required to achieve a dry surgical field, and the catheter was anchored with this second suture as well. Finally, a nonresorbable stay suture was applied to the designated eyelet on the can, which was then rotated counterclockwise to coil any excess catheter length and inserted into the preformed subcutaneous pocket. After skin closure, a computed tomography (CT) scan was performed to confirm accurate tip placement (Figure 5A, B). Implantation was successful if the catheter tip was located within the ascending aorta or within 1 cm of the brachiocephalic trunk (“target zone”) (Figure 5C, D). For data acquisition, the sensor was activated using a standard clinical magnet, similar to those used for cardiac implantable electronic devices. 7

Postoperative pain management

Analgesic therapy was initiated in the awakening phase. In general, a fentanyl patch (2–3 μg/kg for ~72 h, starting from the second postoperative day) will be placed on the forelimb. To ensure proper analgesia before sufficient serum levels are reached, metamizol (40 mg/kg, s.c. [subcutaneous], every 8 h) was administered as soon as the animal was awake. Additionally, carprofen (4 mg/kg s.c., every 24 h) is applied the day of surgery and for at least three consecutive days. Further analgetic treatment depended on the clinical status. After surgery, animals were helped by trained animal keepers and veterinarians until they were able to stand freely. For postoperative intestinal motility, menbuton (Genabil, Boehringer Ingelheim Vetmedica GmbH, Ingelheim, Germany) was administered.

Data acquisition

Blood pressure data were obtained as follows: sheep were transferred into the monitoring stable (Figure 3B), and implants were activated via magnetic induction. Activation was confirmed via software or an external device (power‐on device, DSI). Data recording began once activation was verified and continued until the required measurements were obtained. Arterial blood pressure was recorded continuously for 24 h at a sampling frequency of 500 Hz, ensuring full resolution of the cardiac cycle. After the recording period, the implants were deactivated via software, and the animals were returned to their pens.

The DSI proprietary software algorithm identified individual cardiac cycles to derive systolic and diastolic pressures, which were then summarized in 10‐min mean intervals (144 mean data points per animal per day). To ensure data integrity, motion artifacts and signal noise were filtered using user‐defined thresholds: pressure limits were set between −50 and 500 mmHg, and heart rate limits between 20 and 300 bpm. Cardiac cycles were validated based on a minimum pulse height of 10 mmHg, a systolic validation time of 100 ms, and a nondetection interval of 70 ms to prevent false‐positive peak detection. Final analysis (statistical analysis, image creation) was conducted using Python 3.12 (Python Software Foundation, https://www.python.org). Outlier detection per animal was conducted using the interquartile range (IQR) method, whereby values falling below Q1–1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR were excluded, with Q1 and Q3 denoting the first and third quartiles, respectively. This procedure resulted in the removal of 22 systolic and 26 diastolic measurements across all 12 animals.

RESULTS

A total of 12 adult female sheep (body weight: 55 ± 12 kg) underwent the implantation procedure between November 2024 and June 2025. All surgeries were performed by an experienced cardiac surgeon. The mean procedure time, measured from skin incision to skin closure, was 36 ± 10 min, with a progressive reduction observed as surgical experience increased. The longest procedure without the need for revision lasted 50 min. In cases requiring surgical revision due to catheter malpositioning, the total procedural time more than doubled. Correct catheter tip placement was verified by postoperative CT scan. If the tip was found outside the predefined target zone, immediate surgical revision was performed. Postoperative complications

All animals survived the implant procedure and exhibited no serious complications. Postoperative wound seroma occurred in two (17%) animals, which was treated conservatively with soft compression. No device infections were observed.

Discussion

Large animal models play a crucial role in cardiovascular research. Sheep are frequently employed for chronic medical device testing because of their anatomical similarity to humans. The global market for cardiovascular medical devices is projected to grow from USD 53.67 in 2024 to USD 86.36 billion by 2030 (https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry‐analysis/cardiovascular‐devices‐market, accessed on August 1, 2025). Amid this technological growth, the need for biomedical testing is expected to increase in parallel. Therefore, robust, user‐friendly, wireless technologies for precise large animal monitoring are essential in medical device evaluation. Currently, invasive monitoring remains the gold standard. 8 To the best of our knowledge, this study describes the first implant procedure of the DSI M10‐F2 pressure sensor in adult sheep. In a cohort of 12 sheep, we observed a steep learning curve, progressively shorter procedural times, and a 100% survival rate without severe complications.

The first documented invasive blood pressure measurement was performed in 1731 by Stephen Hales, who measured pulsatile blood pressure after cannulation of the femoral artery of a horse. 9 , 10 Over the following three centuries, the demand for accurate invasive blood pressure monitoring has grown significantly. Noninvasive options are oscillometric sensors, which are easy to use on tails or limbs as well as Doppler ultrasound, which mainly allows systolic measurement. Both techniques lack accuracy compared to invasive intra‐arterial blood pressure sensors. 9 For continuous monitoring in large animal studies, implantable wireless telemetry systems are the established benchmark.

The DSI M10‐F2 has been designed for continuous invasive arterial blood pressure measurement in large animals. Similar to other DSI platforms (PA‐C10, PA‐C40, PA‐D70, and PhysioTel Digital L21), 11 , 12 it provides high‐resolution systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressure data in awake, freely moving animals and provides measurements without stress‐related artifact.

Comparable technologies, including the Telemetry Research TR43P 12 and the Transonic Endogear EG1‐V3S2T‐M2, 11 provide equivalent waveform fidelity, durability, and also suitability for chronic implantation. All systems have validated frequency response and dynamic performance characteristics necessary for precise arterial pressure waveform acquisition in large animals. Current‐generation radiotelemetry systems are known for their accuracy and reliability and recommended by the American Heart Association for both short‐ and long‐term investigations on blood pressure and its variability in experimental research. 1 Finally, platform selection is mainly guided by specific experimental requirements and available budget.

Our study utilized the PhysioTel Digital platform (DSI), which employs a fluid‐filled catheter coupled to an internal solid‐state sensor. 13 Alternative commercial systems include the easyTEL+ system (emka Technologies, Paris, France), which also transmits pressure to an internal sensor via a fluid‐filled catheter with a gel tip, 14 and the Stellar Telemetry system (TSE Systems, Berlin, Germany), which utilizes a miniature solid‐state sensor located at the catheter tip. In our hands, sensor implantation was straightforward, and the learning curve was steep. This approach enables reproducible, reliable invasive blood pressure monitoring in large animals and advances laboratory animal welfare.

The vertical offset between the catheter tip and implant body introduces a hydrostatic pressure artifact in fluid‐filled catheters. Because dynamic postural changes transform this artifact into random noise rather than systematic bias, applying a single correction factor would be erroneous. Although this variation is less critical for relative comparisons, it can be an important consideration depending on study endpoints. Consequently, these fluctuations are accepted as part of the measurement variation in the present study.

Wireless blood pressure monitoring has been demonstrated in lambs. 15 Guo et al. 15 reported femoral as well as left carotid access for monitoring abdominal aortic pressure with excellent results. If the main procedure does not involve the ascending aorta, we recommend implantation via the right carotid artery to facilitate placement into the ascending aorta. In a fetal sheep model, researchers implanted a pressure sensor via the fetal carotid artery, enabling telemetric fetal pressure monitoring in freely moving pregnant sheep. 16 Other more invasive techniques include the insertion of wired pressure catheters into the ascending aorta via sternotomy, 11 which are more traumatic and require a wired connection during recording. Additionally, wired pressure catheterization necessitates movement restriction, a major stressor. 17

Occupational radiation exposure increases cancer risk for medical staff. 18 Following the ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) principle, we performed a CT scan immediately after skin closure. However, the sensor's radiopaque marker is also visible with a conventional C‐arm. Besides the higher radiation exposure for staff, this would probably lead to reduced procedure times and avoid revisions. Using our implant protocol we confirmed sensor positioning via the pressure signal as described.

Postoperative intensive care is challenging in ovine models, as real‐time monitoring options are limited. In ovine cardiovascular studies requiring continuous pressure monitoring, wireless systems can also support postoperative critical care.

Our data confirm a steep learning curve when the procedure is performed by an experienced surgeon. The revision rate of 25% seems to be high; however, two of three revisions occurred within the first four implants. In addition, the procedure time decreased continuously to 20 min. Nevertheless, we would like to caution regarding pitfalls that may require or lead to serious complications.

Pitfalls

First, the sensor catheter is 35 cm long. Because of the length of sheep neck, implantation is recommended in the lower neck region. This ensures the catheter tip is advanced into the left ventricle; otherwise, intraoperative positioning requires an X‐ray.

Second, the catheter must be secured to the vessel with the purse‐string suture. Fixation must be confirmed by gentle manual tension. Without proper fixation, the catheter may dislodge during can placement or postoperatively, risking severe arterial bleeding.

Third, after the catheter is secured with the purse‐string suture, it should be moved in all directions to detect potential leaks.

Fourth, the implant body should be anchored in the subcutaneous pocket with a nonresorbable suture to prevent rotation and sensor retraction.

In conclusion, the DSI M10‐F2 pressure sensor is a reliable tool for real‐time blood pressure monitoring in sheep and a new alternative to existing platforms. 16 , 19 The rapid, minimally invasive implant procedure and telemetric monitoring further advance one of the 3Rs (“refinement”). 20 Finally, intuitive data recording promises to enhance cardiovascular studies in sheep and meet the needs of the growing medical device industry.
