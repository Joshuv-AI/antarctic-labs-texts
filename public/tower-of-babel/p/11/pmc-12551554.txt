Mechanical power is an early predictor of mortality in mechanically ventilated patients with COVID-19
Authors: Uday Rallabhandi, Clark Walker, Ann Davis, J W Hollingsworth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe inherent unpredictability of COVID-19 has compelled clinicians to seek early predictors of in-hospital mortality. This study explored the association between mechanical power and in-hospital mortality in mechanically ventilated COVID-19 patients and provides an evidence-based foundation for mortality prediction.Study design and methodsA multi-centre, retrospective analysis of 600 mechanically ventilated COVID-19 patients aged 18–98 years at 16 Texas Health Resources hospitals between 2020 and 2023 was conducted. Peak inspiratory pressure, static compliance, plateau pressure, driving pressure and mechanical power were analysed for association with in-hospital mortality. Mechanical power was further categorised to provide clinicians with a straightforward approach for mortality prediction.ResultsThe overall in-hospital mortality was 57% (53% to 60%). All five parameters were strongly associated with mortality after adjusting for confounders. In the first 24-hours of mechanical ventilation, 72 patients with mechanical power below 10 J/min had a mortality of 31% (20% to 42%), 159 patients with mechanical power between 10 J/min and 15 J/min had a mortality of 47% (39% to 55%), and 369 patients with mechanical power greater than 15 J/min had a mortality of 66% (61% to 71%). Patients still intubated on day 7 had a 2.1% increase in mortality for each J/min increase in mechanical power.ConclusionMechanical power in the first 24 hours of mechanical ventilation in COVID-19 patients is associated with in-hospital mortality and is useful for clinical prognostication.

Introduction

COVID-19 is a substantial contributor to intensive care unit (ICU) mortality in mechanically ventilated patients. Early in the pandemic, its course was highly unpredictable, with mortality reported as 30%–60% across ICUs.1 2 This variability compelled clinicians to seek predictors of in-hospital mortality to inform prognosis and facilitate communication with patients and their families. Historically, various metrics of lung physiology have been proposed to predict mortality in mechanically ventilated patients with respiratory disease, including peak inspiratory pressure, lung compliance, plateau pressure, driving pressure, etc.36 Although useful at the bedside, these factors were correlative—they did not link the underlying pathophysiology of ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) to mortality directly. Mechanical power, the theoretical energy transfer from the ventilator to the respiratory system per unit time, had originally been proposed as a mechanism for the development of VILI by Gattinoni et al.7 Derived from fundamental respiratory mechanics, mechanical power can be calculated from readily available ventilator parameters and has been extensively documented for its correlation with mortality in non-COVID-19 ventilated patients.8 However, mechanical power required in COVID-19 patients has been observed to be notably elevated compared with their non-COVID-19 counterparts9; consequently, previously established correlative studies between mechanical power and mortality may not be directly applicable to COVID-19 patients. The goal of this study was to explore the relationship between mechanical power and in-hospital mortality in COVID-19 patients and provide clinicians with an evidence-based foundation for quantitative mortality prediction at the bedside. Additionally, this study aimed to provide more empirical evidence for the role of mechanical power in the development of VILI.

Study design and methods

In this retrospective study, we analysed electronic medical record data from various urban and rural hospitals in the Texas Health Resource system in North Texas. These hospitals were categorised as each having one ICU with high-intensity staffing (24/7 intensivist coverage), low-intensity staffing (daytime intensivist coverage only), an open ICU (no dedicated intensivist) and telemedicine intensivist staffing. Patients aged 18–98 years with a positive COVID-19 PCR test and requiring mechanical ventilation between 1 January 2020 and 31 December 2023 were included in the study. Data analysis and visualisation were performed on Microsoft Excel, Visual Basic for Applications and Python’s statsmodels and matplotlib packages.

The following data were extracted from the electronic medical record for analysis: patient demographics, comorbid conditions, laboratory data, hospital discharge status, the earliest documented ventilator parameters within the first 24 hours of intubation and the earliest documented ventilator parameters within the first 24 hours after day 7 of intubation. Mortality was determined during hospitalisation. The full cohort was filtered for patients with adequate documentation of ventilator parameters to calculate mechanical power within 24 hours of intubation. As most of our patients were on volume control modes, most tidal volumes (TV) used for calculation were set tidal volumes, with the others being the tidal volume at the time of documentation. Plateau pressure (PP) was determined by an inspiratory hold manoeuvre, at which point compliance measurements were determined automatically by the ventilator software. Driving pressure (DP) was then calculated as plateau pressure minus the preset positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP), which was manually set by clinicians. Univariate analysis was performed for demographics, comorbidities, laboratory data and ventilator parameters during the first 24 hours for surviving and non-surviving patients. Surviving and non-surviving patient cohorts were extracted from this data, and a normal model was displayed for the frequency distribution of peak inspiratory pressure, static compliance (SC), plateau pressure, driving pressure and mechanical power using the means and SD of these samples. These parameters were then analysed for their correlation to mortality with logistic regression, both as an univariable regression and a multivariate regression with the demographic and comorbidity data that were statistically significant from the univariate analysis as confounders. The cohort was then analysed for temporal trends in mechanical power, observed mortality and predicted mortality during peak months throughout the study period.

The patients were then divided into categorical groupings of mechanical power and analysed for mortality. Mechanical power was then calculated on day 7 for all patients still intubated. The relationship between the change in mechanical power from day 1 to day 7 and mortality was examined by univariable logistic regression.

IQRs are reported in parentheses as the 25th and 75th percentiles. CIs are likewise reported in parentheses and were calculated at the 95% level. Confidence intervals for ORs were calculated using the Wald method. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. Continuous variables were compared using either the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, based on whether the data were determined to be normally distributed using the Anderson-Darling test. All tests were two-tailed, and a p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not directly involved in data collection, data analysis, study design and interpretation, or any other aspects of this study or the development of the manuscript.

Results

A total of 1563 patients from 10 urban and 6 rural ICUs were initially identified in the dataset as meeting the inclusion criteria. Of these, 1209 patients were from ICUs with high-intensity staffing, 282 from ICUs with low-intensity staffing, 45 from open ICUs and 27 from an ICU with telemedicine intensivist staffing. The hospital mortality for the entire cohort was 54% (52% to 57%). 600 patients had complete medical records with ventilator parameters required for calculating mechanical power within 24 hours of intubation and were included in the data analysis. Mortality in this cohort was 57% (53% to 60%). Univariate analysis demonstrated the following demographic and comorbid data as having a statistically significant difference between surviving and non-surviving groups: age, weight, male sex, asthma and chronic kidney disease (CKD) (table 1).

Among these 600 patients, 566 (94.3%) were on volume control or pressure-regulated volume control modes of ventilation. Normality of distribution for each ventilator parameter was assessed separately in surviving and non-surviving cohorts using the Anderson-Darling test, confirming a normal distribution for peak inspiratory pressure, static compliance, plateau pressure, driving pressure and mechanical power in both groups (p<0.02 for all). Statistically significant differences between survivors and non-survivors were identified for all five parameters using Student’s t-test: peak inspiratory pressure (p=0.001), static compliance (p=0.004), plateau pressure (p<0.001), driving pressure (p<0.001) and mechanical power (p<0.001) (figure 1). Results of logistic regression models further indicate that these parameters have a statistically significant association with mortality after adjustment for significant confounders identified in the univariate analysis (online supplemental figure 1; table 2). The continuous variable mechanical power was then converted to a categorical variable for purposes of analysis. Previous studies support mechanical power higher than 12 J/min to be associated with higher mortality in non-COVID-19 patients.8 Therefore, we chose cut-offs for mechanical power at 10 J/min and 15 J/min to establish three categorical groupings of low (<10 J/min), medium (10–15 J/min) and high (>15 J/min) mechanical power. There was a significant difference in mortality across these categories assessed by χ2 testing (p<0.001), with sample sizes of 72, 159 and 369 and mortalities of 31%, 47% and 66%, respectively (figure 2).

On day 7, 147 patients had been extubated alive, 181 had deceased, and 272 remained on mechanical ventilation. Of these, 207 patients had sufficient documentation of ventilation parameters to calculate mechanical power. These patients had a mortality of 56%, and their difference in mechanical power between day 7 and day 1 ranged between −18/3 J/min and 27.7 J/min with an average of −1.2±7.3 J/min. Mortality was 48% among 126 patients with decreased mechanical power and 68% among 81 patients with increased mechanical power (p=0.004). A logit analysis of the difference in mechanical power and mortality demonstrated an OR of 1.096 (1.048 to 1.145) and an average marginal effect (AME) of 2.1% (1.2% to 2.9%).

Discussion

This study focused on ventilator parameters previously established as predictors of mortality: peak inspiratory pressure, static compliance, plateau pressure, driving pressure and mechanical power.36 A strong association was confirmed between all five parameters and mortality in mechanically ventilated patients with COVID-19, independent of demographic and comorbid confounders. Mechanical power, which encompasses many other ventilator parameters, showed the broadest range of clinically relevant values and the most consistency in distribution between surviving and non-surviving patients. Consistent with prior publications in patients with COVID-19,6 our findings support mechanical power as a strong and useful predictor of mortality.

Qualitatively, our univariate and regression analyses suggest that age, weight, male sex and CKD are also independent predictors of mortality in COVID-19 patients, a result supported by previous studies.1012 Some laboratory results that were significant in the univariate analysis were not considered in the logistic regression due to either a high percentage of missing data or assumed multicollinearity of creatinine and potassium with the CKD comorbidity. Therefore, inflammatory markers such as D-dimer, ferritin and C reactive protein and signs of a lymphocyte response could also independently predict mortality, but were not accounted for in our study.

The timing of intubation of patients with COVID-19 has been of great interest. Due to the anticipated worsening of measurements of lung physiology during the natural course of disease, a lead-time bias may arise when comparing mechanical power and mortality. However, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated no significant difference in primary or secondary outcomes between early intubation and late intubation.13 While early intubation of COVID-19 patients may be associated with lower mechanical power, our study does not support that an early intubation strategy would result in improved clinical outcomes. Our findings do demonstrate a significant association between the severity of lung disease at the time of intubation and in-hospital mortality. Measurements of mechanical power can be a useful tool for clinicians to determine clinical prognosis early in the ICU course in this patient population.

Categorical stratification into bins of mechanical power on the first day of mechanical ventilation provides a quick and straightforward method to classify patients and predict mortality at the bedside. Repeat evaluation of mechanical power on hospital day 7 provided additional insight into clinical prognosis. An increase in mechanical power on day 7 was significantly associated with an increase in mortality predicted from day 1. Furthermore, by using the AME from the logit model, this change in predicted mortality can be easily quantified. Based on these observations, we propose a model to predict mortality in COVID-19 patients requiring mechanical ventilation. We suggest the calculation of mechanical power within the first 24 hours of intubation and categorisation of the patient as requiring low, medium or high mechanical power. For patients who remain intubated on day 7, determine the change of mechanical power from day 1 and adjust the predicted mortality by 2.1% for each J/min difference. This model provides a foundation for mortality prediction to help guide informed decisions for both patients and families, physician-patient communication, support modalities for patients and families, and resource allocation in high-volume ICUs.

There are several limitations to this study. This is an observational retrospective cohort in a single hospital system. Under-documentation of mechanical ventilator parameters during the pre-defined inclusion period limited the power of this study and the ability to identify additional risk factors for mortality. Our patients were COVID-19 positive on PCR testing and mechanically ventilated, but we did not identify if any patients had other primary or secondary causes of intubation besides hypoxaemic respiratory failure due to COVID-19. Additionally, we could not account for other acute comorbid pathophysiology contributing to the patients’ mortality. We did not capture vaccination status that could impact severity of disease. This study did not standardise for severity of disease, acute respiratory distress syndrome status, the strain of SARS-CoV-2, or use of antivirals or other treatment modalities besides mechanical ventilation. There was also no distinction between modes of ventilation, which has been shown to affect delivered mechanical power,14 although, as previously mentioned, the vast majority of our patients were on volume control modes. We acknowledge differences in mortality in each of the waves of COVID-19 during the pandemic. We observed that higher mechanical power was observed during waves with higher mortality (online supplemental file 1). Our findings do not establish a causal relationship between mechanical power and mortality but do reinforce their association, contributing to the growing body of empirical evidence linking mechanical power and VILI. Our findings can also guide clinicians with a meaningful prognostication tool. Further research is warranted to explore the causal relationship between ventilator parameters and mortality and to address the lack of standardisation for disease severity and treatment modalities in this study.
