Impact of atmospheric rivers on coastal oceanographic conditions off central-southern Chile
Authors: Yosvany Garcia-Santos, Diego A. Narváez, Gonzalo S. Saldías, Martín Jacques-Coper, Deniz Bozkurt, René Garreaud, Valentina Valdés-Castro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Atmospheric Rivers (ARs) are key drivers of extreme precipitation and hydrological variability along the west coasts of continents. While many studies have focused on their atmospheric impacts, the oceanographic response remains underexplored. Using remote sensing and in situ observations, this study examines how ARs affect river plumes, hydrodynamics and biogeochemical conditions in central-southern Chile (33–40° S). Synoptic atmospheric conditions associated to the passage of different oriented AR (i.e., Tilted and Zonal ARs) produce contrasting river plume dynamics, while TARs favor coastally trapped and southward-advected plumes by the northerly winds, ZARs generate dispersed and offshore plumes influenced by southerly winds. High resolution mooring data show that both TARs and ZARs initially enhance vertical mixing and currents by strong winds, followed by stratification due to the arrival of a river plume. Biogeochemical samplings before and after the passing of a series of ARs show that TARs increase surface nitrates, silicic acid and chlorophyll-a more than ZARs. The results provide novel insights into the response of river plumes and water column changes to the passage of ARs in the coastal ocean.

Introduction

The poleward transport of atmospheric moisture from tropical to mid-latitude regions is largely dominated by Atmospheric Rivers (ARs)—narrow, long corridors of intense water vapor fluxes in the lower troposphere1,2. These features can span over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} 2000 km in length and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} 1000 km in width, impacting vast geographical areas3,4, particularly along the west coasts of North and South America5,6. When ARs make landfall, they promote intense precipitation through orographic lifting7, convective processes, and interactions with upper-level jet systems8, contributing significantly to regional water resources where they are climatologically dominant9. However, extreme precipitation associated with ARs can also lead to hazardous hydrometeorological events, including floods and landslides10,11. In mountainous regions, ARs may promote substantial snowfall that contributes to seasonal snowpack12, which can lead to rapid snowmelt during warmer periods, further increasing the risk of downstream flooding13,14. Moreover, ARs are frequently associated with damaging wind events in various regions worldwide15, further amplifying their societal and environmental impacts.

Many severe flood events are closely associated with the inland penetration of ARs7,16. Globally, the presence of ARs has been associated with up to 80% increased likelihood of flooding, while their absence can increase the frequency of droughts by as much as 90%17. The intense precipitation associated with ARs significantly alters river discharge patterns, a phenomenon documented in various regions worldwide14,18,19. Notably, ARs contribute about 22% of global runoff, reaching over 50% in certain regions, including southern Chile17. This elevated river discharge has significant downstream implications, including the enhanced transport of sediments and nutrients into coastal regions20,21. This phenomenon can lead to substantial biogeochemical changes in coastal ecosystems, with important ecological implications22.

This freshwater input from rivers creates a low-salinity plume characterized as a thin surface layer of buoyant water. The dynamics of these plumes are primarily influenced by outflow inertia, buoyancy, tides, Coriolis, and wind stress23–26. Off central-southern Chile, freshwater plumes are particularly important in modulating water column stratification27,28, especially during winter when river discharges and plume extent reach their maximum29. During this period, nutrient export from rivers also intensifies, often leading to an increase in phytoplankton biomass, exceeding background concentrations by a factor of two in plume-influenced zones30. Major turbid plume events are more frequent primarily during warm phases of the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO), and negative phases of the Antarctic Oscillation (AAO), which shift storm tracks northward—thereby intensifying precipitation and runoff in the region31.

Central-southern Chile (33–40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}S) is one of the global hotspots for ARs15. In this region, ARs contribute up to 50% of the annual precipitation6, and are key drivers of extreme rainfall and associated hydrological impacts, including widespread flooding32,33. A crucial yet underexplored factor influencing these impacts is the orientation of ARs, which affects both precipitation distribution and flood intensity34–36. In this region, ARs can be categorized as Tilted Atmospheric Rivers (TARs), with a northwest-southeast (NW-SE) alignment, or Zonal Atmospheric Rivers (ZARs), which have an east-west orientation roughly perpendicular to the Andes19,32,33,37. TARs are generally associated with colder synoptic systems and tend to produce copious precipitation along the coast, often exceeding that of ZARs; whereas ZARs are often linked to warmer systems and produce stronger orographic precipitation enhancement over the Andes32,37. These differences are related to the distinct synoptic-scale conditions that favor each AR type: TARs are typically embedded in pre-frontal environments associated with the approach of deep extratropical cyclones and enhanced meridional moisture transport, while ZARs form under zonal flow between a subtropical high and a mid-latitude low, often linked to quasi-stationary fronts that enhance westerly moisture transport. Recent analysis of AR-driven ocean responses in this region shows that TARs enhance downwelling, increase sea surface temperature (SST), raise sea level, and generate southeastward currents, whereas ZARs promote upwelling, leading to cooling and sea level drops north of the AR axis38.

Despite advances in understanding the impacts of ARs on precipitation and river discharge, their influence on coastal ocean physical and biogeochemical processes remains poorly understood (e.g. Shinoda et al.39, Diaz et al.40, Garcia-Santos et al.38). We hypothesize that AR orientation not only modulates precipitation and river discharge distribution but also impacts river plume and shelf dynamics, influencing freshwater, sediment, and nutrient dispersal. Depending on prevailing wind patterns and synoptic conditions, ARs may either enhance or inhibit the offshore extent of river plumes, affecting water column stratification, salinity gradients, and nutrient availability. These interactions are critical for understanding how extreme weather events affect coastal marine ecosystems and economically important sectors such as fisheries and aquaculture. Results

Effects of ARs on precipitation and river discharge

This study focuses on central-southern Chile (33–40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} S), where ten representative rivers were selected to analyze the freshwater discharge associated with ARs (Fig. 1a). A strong river discharge event following a ZAR is shown in Fig. 1b. This region includes Coliumo Bay, where field campaigns were carried out during the winters of 2022 and 2023 to monitor the oceanic response to AR landfalls (Fig. 1b). ARs affecting this region exhibit distinct orientations (Fig. 1c); however, this study focuses specifically on TARs and ZARs. In this region, all ARs occur mostly during austral winter (June–July–August) (Fig. 1d), consistent with the climatology described by Viale et al.6. This seasonal pattern is driven by the equatorward displacement of the South Pacific Subtropical High (SPSH), which alters the storm track and increases the frequency of AR landfalls in the region41. When restricting to intense events (Integrated Vapor Trasport (IVT) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 500 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {kg/ms}$$\end{document}), both AR types remain more prevalent during winter. Given this seasonal dominance, the present analysis focuses exclusively on austral winter AR events.

TARs typically develop under more meridional flow regimes, associated with mid-latitude troughs and cyclonic activity that favor northwest–southeast moisture transport. The mean sea level pressure (SLP) field (contours) shows a trough southwest of the AR axis, responsible for steering the ARs in a clockwise path, allowing them to extend far north of their landfall latitude. In contrast, ZARs exhibit a pressure dipole, with high pressure to the north and low pressure to the south of the AR axis. This configuration generates a strong zonal (westerly) flow that constrains the AR trajectory and limits its latitudinal spread (Fig. 2a). Satellite-derived surface reflectance following AR landfall reveals a significant increase in river plume extent relative to winter climatological values (Fig. 3). After TARs, northerly to northwesterly post-landfall winds drive river plumes southward along the coast (Fig. 3a), promoting plume convergence and forming extensive bands of freshwater attached to the shoreline. In contrast, post-ZAR southerly winds favor offshore Ekman transport and northwestward spreading of river plumes (Fig. 3b). Consistent with precipitation and discharge patterns (Fig. 2c,d), reflectance anomalies north of 37.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} S are more pronounced following TARs than ZARs, indicating stronger plume expansion for rivers such as the Biobio and those farther north. South of 37.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} S, reflectance anomalies are typically stronger after ZARs, reflecting the increased discharge from southern rivers, leading to an extensive band of Rrs(645).

To evaluate the effects of ARs on the coastal hydrography and circulation, a field campaign was carried out during July 2022 off Coliumo Bay (Fig. 1b). A detailed description of atmospheric forcings during this period is provided by Garreaud et al.37. Six ARs (two TARs and four ZARs) made landfall in the study region during the field campaign. Among these, the events on July 9th (TAR 1), July 17th (ZAR 3), and July 26th (TAR 2) reached IVT values exceeding 500 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {kg/ms}$$\end{document}, classifying them as moderate ARs (Fig. 4a), following the AR scale of Ralph et al.42. All events were characterized by high precipitation and intensification of the meridional component of surface winds (Fig. 4b). In all cases, meridional winds were stronger than zonal winds. In both TAR and ZAR cases, orographic blocking induces a low-level northerly coastal flow, which enhances the meridional component of IVT; in contrast, ZARs are characterized by dominant upper-level westerlies that extend across the Andes37.

Time series of atmospheric variables show reduced solar radiation and limited air temperature variability during all AR events (Fig. 4c). This refers to the reduced diurnal temperature range during ARs, caused by thick cloud cover that limits daytime solar heating and traps longwave radiation at night. The two closest gauged rivers, Itata and Biobio, exhibited increases in discharge 1–2 days after peak precipitation (Fig. 4d). These discharge responses exceeded the July climatological means of 665 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {m^{3}/s}$$\end{document} for Itata and 1784 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {m^{3}/s}$$\end{document} for Biobio, with the exception of the ZAR event on July 23rd, which primarily affected the Biobio river.

The vertical structure of the water column revealed a thermal inversion, typical of winter conditions in this region43, with cooler surface waters (around 11.7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} C) overlaying warmer subsurface layers (around 12.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} C) (Fig. 4e). Most ARs induced mixing in the surface layer (0–10 m), while the mixing of the entire water column was observed during the TARs on July 9th and 26th. Anomalously low temperatures and strong thermal stratification occurred from July 14th to 17th. This phenomenon likely resulted from a persistent low-pressure system along the central coast of Chile from July 12th to 16th, which reduced the meridional wind component, solar radiation, and air temperature (Fig. 4b,c). Additionally, the strong river discharge after ZAR 2 could contribute to the enhanced stratification observed in that period. The ocean currents intensified during all AR events, with velocities reaching up to 0.2 m/s, primarily near the surface (5 m) and the bottom (20 m) (Fig. 4f). Toward the end of the observation period, high chlorophyll-a concentrations were observed near the surface, linked to the diurnal pulse of PAR and likely supported by an increase in terrestrial nutrients, particularly silicic acid. An increase in chlorophyll-a concentration was also detected from the extracted samples, particularly at 2 m, where values peaked at 1.2–1.3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {\mu g/L}$$\end{document} on July 11th and July 28th (Fig. 5d). The first peak coincided with an increase in Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR) (Fig. 6f), further supporting the role of light and nutrient availability in promoting phytoplankton growth during the post-AR phase.

Following TAR 2 (July 26–27), nutrient concentrations revealed similar trends, with a notable increase in nitrates and silicic acid concentrations (Fig. 5a,c), along with an increase in chlorophyll-a concentrations at the surface and the bottom (Fig. 5d). Unlike TARs, nutrient concentrations were generally lower following ZAR events. A noticeable increase in chlorophyll-a concentration was only observed after the passage of ZAR 3, reaching values up to 0.9 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {\mu g/L}$$\end{document}, likely due to the three-day delay between the event and the sampling, which allowed time for a phytoplankton response under optimal light conditions.

TAR 1 was associated with a synoptic-scale system consistent with an extratropical cyclone and cold front reaching south-central Chile during winter44. This system produced an accumulated precipitation of approximately 32 mm/day at the Dichato station and was accompanied by strong north-northwesterly winds, averaging around 6 m/s, that prevailed throughout the event. The study by Garreaud et al.37 provides a more detailed analysis of this event, showing a frontal cloud band that extends over southern Chile, oriented nearly parallel to the Andes Cordillera, with a north-northwest IVT impacting the study area.

The Wirewalker profiler allowed a high-frequency vertical characterization of physical (temperature, salinity) and biological-chemical (fluorescence, dissolved oxygen) variables before, during, and after the TAR 1 landfall (Fig. 6). The temperature profiles confirmed the thermal inversion observed by the temperature sensor array, with cooler water at the surface (around 11.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} C) and warmer subsurface water (around 12.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} C) (Fig. 6a). The salinity profiles revealed fresher surface waters (32 PSU) and progressively saltier, denser waters near the bottom (34 PSU) (Fig. 6b). Stratification was evident in the Brunt–Väisälä Frequency (BVF) profiles, with surface values near 40 cph during July 8 (Fig. 6c). Relatively high concentrations of dissolved oxygen (9.5–10 mL/L) and chlorophyll-a (2–2.5 µg/L) were confined to the surface layers prior to the AR’s arrival, while lower concentrations were observed in deeper waters (approximately 9 mL/L of oxygen and 1 µg/L of chlorophyll-a) (Fig. 6e,f). Turbidity remained low throughout the water column, consistently below 4 NTU (Fig. 6g). Nutrient concentrations (Fig. 5) were moderate before the event, with orthophosphate ranging between 1.4 and 1.6 µmol/L, nitrates between 10 and 12 µmol/L, and silicic acid concentration varying between 10 and 28 µmol/L in surface waters.

As the TAR impacted the area on July 9th, strong winds induced pronounced mixing of the water column (Fig. 6c). Notably, vertical mixing began a few hours prior to the official landfall of the AR, driven by the advance of strong winds associated with the approaching frontal system. This mixing disrupted the previously weak stratification, resulting in a homogenized water column with decreased temperature and salinity just below the surface, with values reaching 12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} C and 32 PSU, respectively. Following the AR-induced mixing, higher chlorophyll concentrations were also observed near the bottom (over 2.5 µg/L), though these levels declined in the subsequent hours due to reduced solar radiation during the AR passage.

Following the passage of the TAR, on July 10th, a pronounced decrease in surface salinity was observed, extending to a depth of about 10 m. This freshening confirms the arrival of runoff from nearby rivers, likely including the Itata River and adjacent tributaries, with minimum salinity values on July 11th. The freshwater input was associated with increased surface oxygen concentrations (up to 10.5 mL/L) and turbidity (up to 10 NTU). The shallow, buoyant freshwater plume enhanced vertical stratification, with BVF values reaching \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} 60 cph in the upper 10 m, indicating a sharp separation of salinity (and density) layers. As wind forcing diminished after the AR, there was a re-stratification which was further reinforced by the sustained arrival of the freshwater plume. This additional freshwater input led to a marked increase in FWC, surpassing the levels observed during the AR. Correspondingly, PEA values also increased, reflecting a progressive strengthening of water column stratification (Fig. 6d). Notably, the temporal evolution of FWC and PEA following the event showed a similar correspondence, suggesting that surface freshwater accumulation became the dominant driver of the vertical density structure during the post-AR period.

Oceanographic conditions following July 15th, including ZAR 3, ZAR 4, and TAR 2, were analyzed using a bottom-mounted SBE HydroCAT deployed at 20 m depth (Fig. 7, left panels). The instrument was deployed again in 2023 at the same location during additional AR events (TAR 3, ZAR 5 and TAR 4) (Fig. 7, right panels). During both periods, ARs triggered significant rainfall, particularly during ZAR 3 and ZAR 5, with precipitation rates reaching nearly 5 mm/h. Several additional rainfall events occurred in 2023 but were not classified as ARs. While the 2022 AR events were relatively short, the June–July 2023 period exhibited more persistent precipitation, including two consecutive ARs (TAR 3 and ZAR 5). This persistence likely contributed to cumulative hydrological effects, such as sustained freshwater input into the coastal system. Although meridional wind speeds reached up to 12 m/s even during non-AR events, AR periods were characterized by more prolonged and sustained winds, suggesting enhanced mechanical mixing over the inner shelf.

Temperature and salinity at 20 m depth exhibited similar responses to AR events, both decreasing as a result of vertical mixing induced by AR activity (Fig. 7b). These changes were more abrupt in 2023, likely due to the longer duration of the events during that period. Turbidity also displayed substantial variability (Fig. 7c). In 2022, three distinct turbidity peaks were observed, on July 17, 21, and 26, coinciding with episodes of intensified bottom currents (Fig. 4f). In 2023, turbidity responses were more directly associated with AR activity, showing a marked increase during ZAR 5, reaching up to 10 NTU (Fig. 7c). Discussion

The effect of ARs on the ocean remains relatively underexplored, with most studies focusing mainly on sea surface response (e.g., SST, SSH) to ARs, usually using reanalysis and numerical models39,45. Part of the difficulties in studying ARs is the operational challenges of deploying instruments and conducting field sampling during the passage of such extreme weather events. Our study provides novel results by characterizing the hydrological and oceanographic impacts of distinct ARs on coastal oceanographic processes such as river plume, hydrodynamics, and biogeochemical conditions. Previous studies have shown that the angle at which ARs intersect coastal topography plays a critical role in shaping the spatial distribution of rainfall34,35,37. Our results confirm that both TARs and ZARs significantly influence precipitation, but also show clear differences in the spatial extent and intensity of their respective impacts on river discharge.

TARs have a more pronounced effect on rivers located north of 37.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} S, while ZARs exert a greater influence in the southern regions. This spatial distinction likely reflects the interaction between AR orientation and the region’s complex topography37. The oblique orientation of TARs enables broader latitudinal reach, facilitating widespread moisture transport and concentrated rainfall across coastal and valley areas. This pattern aligns with the higher discharge observed in northern rivers, where terrain effectively channels runoff. In contrast, the zonal alignment of ZARs restricts their influence primarily to southern regions, where the orographic lift from the Andes enhances precipitation over mountainous areas, consistent with the discharge responses observed in rivers south of 37.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} S. This latitudinal pattern in river discharge driven by ARs seems to explain the previously documented pattern found by Rubio et al.46 and Saldías et al.31 using long-term analyses of hydrological variability and freshwater turbid plume respectively.

The influence of ARs on river plumes reveals that TARs and ZARs modify plume patterns differently. Our findings indicate that TARs lead to a more pronounced southward alongshore plume, particularly north of 37.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} S, driven by post-landfall northerly or northwesterly winds. In contrast, ZARs tend to generate river plumes that extend offshore, influenced by southerly upwelling-favorable winds. This pattern is consistent with the well-established dynamics of wind-driven plumes over sloping shelves24,47, where prevailing wind direction plays a crucial role in shaping the vertical structure and offshore dispersion of freshwater in the coastal ocean. Our results align with Saldías et al.29: during downwelling, river plumes are narrow, vertically mixed, and coastally confined, while upwelling leads to shallower, offshore-extending plumes with strong haloclines and enhanced stratification. In the case of TARs, as plumes are advected southward along the coast, plume coalescence events can occur, consistent with strong downwelling conditions that promote the merging of multiple plumes along the coast28,29,48,49. During such events, strong southward buoyancy-driven flows often dominate inner shelf circulation50. This pattern of winter buoyancy-driven circulation is consistent with poleward currents over the shelf following intense storms and plume merging events49,51.

Plume-to-plume interactions are common in coastal regions where multiple river mouths are closely located, and the dispersion and fate of riverine waters in these settings are strongly influenced by such interactions48,52. Merging plume events can have significant biogeochemical implications53, as smaller rivers may disperse their constituents over a larger area than a single river of the same drainage size52. This process facilitates the widespread distribution of key river-derived materials, including pollutants, nutrients, and sediments, across extensive coastal regions. In central-southern Chile, the likelihood and intensity of plume merging events are strongly influenced by climate-driven variations in river discharge. Warm ENSO phases and the negative phase of the Antarctic Oscillation (AAO) enhance precipitation and flow, particularly south of 37.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} S, increasing plume intensity and expanding their coastal and biogeochemical influence31,46,54.

The field campaigns conducted in July 2022 and June–July 2023 provide one of the few direct observations of AR-induced changes in coastal hydrography. These observations highlight the dual role of ARs in modulating surface ocean dynamics: first, as drivers of vertical mixing due to strong winds, and second, as promoters of stratification through freshwater arrival from river discharges. Temperature data reveal surface warming during the passage of ARs, accompanied by subsurface cooling caused by vertical mixing. In contrast, studies in the northeastern Pacific39,55 reported SST cooling during AR events, attributed to enhanced latent heat flux and wind-driven mixing. These contrasting results may be due to the fact that our study focuses on a specific location within a bay characterized by winter thermal inversion. However, a recent study conducted in a broader area encompassing our study region reported surface cooling along the AR axis in the open ocean38.

In our study, we observed a salinity decrease during the ARs, with strong winds enhancing mixing and reducing salinity even at the bottom. However, the most pronounced salinity drop occurred after the system dissipated, primarily due to the elevated river discharge and a progressive increase in FWC over the inner shelf. Similarly, Hoffman et al.56 reported that increased precipitation during the rainy season along the California Current System results in a progressive decrease in salinity. This response is modulated by both rainfall intensity and wind forcing. In the subpolar Southern Ocean, Edholm et al.57 found that daily accumulation of AR-driven precipitation provides a buoyancy gain to the surface ocean comparable to surface heat flux warming. Increased turbidity and dissolved oxygen were also commonly observed, indicative of sediment resuspension and vertical redistribution of oxygen-rich surface waters. In some cases, chlorophyll-a concentrations spiked shortly after AR passage, possibly due to resuspended benthic phytoplankton. Compared to non-AR events, ARs impose a more coherent and intense physical forcing due to their persistent wind fields and heavy precipitation. The intensity, orientation, and duration of each AR govern the extent of its impact, with effects observed even at 20 m depth. The depth of mixing appears to be more strongly controlled by the intensity and duration of AR-associated winds than by orientation alone. According to previous studies (e.g. Thadathil et al.58), the barrier layer, defined as the layer between the mixed layer depth and the isothermal layer depth, probably becomes deeper and more pronounced after the passage of ARs, likely as a result of the strong freshwater input.

Important biogenic effects have been reported in plume regions with elevated nutrient inputs, particularly silicic acid59, which promote eutrophication and influence plankton communities60. Our results show that both TARs and ZARs impact coastal biogeochemistry, though with differing magnitudes and biological responses. TARs led to a marked increase in surface nitrate and silicic acid concentrations, likely stimulating phytoplankton growth, as evidenced by a delayed rise in chlorophyll-a. In contrast, ZARs were associated with decreases in nutrient concentrations, and the resulting chlorophyll-a response was variable, with a notable rise after ZAR 3 but remaining low after ZAR 2, likely due to limiting factors such as light or vertical mixing61. Interestingly, orthophosphate exhibited a distinct pattern: the overall trend showed a progressive decrease over time. This suggests that, unlike nitrate and silicic acid, orthophosphate inputs following ARs may be more limited or subject to rapid biological uptake, potentially constraining its sustained availability for phytoplankton growth. Rivers also deliver phosphorus primarily in particulate form, which tends to settle quickly, while the dissolved fraction is rapidly consumed. These findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting that, while river plumes serve as important sources of silicic acid and nitrate62, phosphorus dynamics are often more complex and strongly influenced by both biological uptake and chemical processes59,63,64.

The study focused on winter because it is the season with the highest AR frequency. However, ARs also occur in other seasons, during which background ocean conditions differ from those in winter. In austral spring and summer, the water column in the study region is generally more stratified, with higher surface temperatures and without the strong thermal inversion typical of winter. Although AR-induced wind mixing is likely to erode this stratification and promote strong mixing, the reduced river discharge and the more sporadic occurrence of events suggest that the cumulative effects observed in winter would be weaker. Overall, our findings emphasize the significant role of ARs in modulating coastal biogeochemical cycles. TARs exert a stronger influence on nutrient availability and phytoplankton biomass, primarily through enhanced stratification and plume retention near the coast. In contrast, ZARs tend to promote offshore dispersion due to prevailing wind conditions, thereby diminishing the immediate biological impact in nearshore waters.

While this study provides novel insights into the oceanographic and biogeochemical responses to distinct AR orientations, several limitations should be acknowledged. The in situ hydrographic observations are based primarily on a single field campaign conducted off Coliumo Bay during winter 2022, with supplementary data from a shorter deployment in 2023. As a result, the observed physical and biological responses may not fully represent the spatial heterogeneity or interannual variability of AR impacts along the broader coastline. Moreover, although satellite and reanalysis products offer valuable regional context, direct in situ measurements of upper ocean currents and nutrient transport remain spatially and temporally limited. These constraints underscore the need for future observational efforts encompassing a wider range of AR intensities, seasons, and locations to improve the generalizability of these findings. More broadly, our results highlight the importance of continued investigation into the long-term implications of ARs for coastal productivity and ecosystem functioning—particularly in the context of future climate scenarios that may alter the frequency, intensity, and trajectories of AR events65,66.

Data and methods

AR catalog

The ERA5-derived global catalog was used to quantify ARs impacting south-central Chile (33–40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} S) from 1979 to 202367,68. This dataset provides a high-resolution view at 0.25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} x 0.25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}, with hourly updates. This catalog identifies ARs when IVT exceeds the 85th percentile and is above 100 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {kg/ms}$$\end{document}, with additional criteria of a poleward IVT component over 50 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {kg/ms}$$\end{document}, IVT directional coherence, and ARs having a length over 2000 km and a length-to-width ratio greater than 2. The AR tracking algorithm provides details on AR shape, axis, landfall, and statistics. The day of landfall was used to quantify the number of individual events impacting the study area. In this study, an AR event is defined based on the complete lifecycle of the system after its landfall in the study region. The variables used for this categorization were Zonal and Meridional IVT at Landfall Location (“lfivtx” and “lfivty”), as well as the track ID (“kid”) and IVT Direction at Landfall Location (“lfivtdir”, (degrees from north, clockwise)).

A total of 472 ARs were quantified in the period 1979-2023: 289 TARs and 111 ZARs, representing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}60 and 24% of all, respectively. TARs represent events with a landfalling IVT direction greater than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$310^{\circ }$$\end{document}, while ZARs encompass all events with a landfalling IVT direction between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$250^{\circ }-300^{\circ }$$\end{document} (based on the direction from which the IVT vectors come) (Fig. 1c). Events with IVT direction between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$170^{\circ }{-}250^{\circ }$$\end{document} (south-southwesterly) were excluded due to their infrequent occurrence (<2% of all). Significant events with IVT direction between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$300^{\circ }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$310^{\circ }$$\end{document} (14% of all) were excluded because, in this range, events with characteristics similar to TARs and ZARs may coincide, implying they could belong to either type of AR at different lifecycle stages38.

TARs and ZARs were accumulated over time and quantified by season to examine seasonal variations in event frequency and better understand their temporal distribution throughout the year. The seasons were defined as follows: summer (December–January–February, DJF), autumn (March–April–May, MAM), winter (June–July–August, JJA), and spring (September–October–November, SON) (Fig. 1d).

Precipitation and river discharge data

Large-scale atmospheric circulation during the ARs was characterized using the ERA5 reanalysis of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts69, with an hourly resolution on a 0.25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} × 0.25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} lat-lon grid and 137 vertical levels. Composite analyses were conducted to characterize the typical synoptic-scale meteorological conditions associated with each AR pattern, focusing on IVT magnitude, mean sea level pressure (MSLP), and wind fields. For each AR event, daily averages of these variables were calculated at every grid point for the day of landfall (designated as day 0). Composite means were then obtained by averaging these daily fields across all events within each AR pattern.

Daily precipitation data from various rain gauges were obtained from CAMELS-CL70. These data were used to quantify the accumulated precipitation between TARs and ZARs and to identify the areas most impacted by each AR type. For each event, daily precipitation was extracted over an 11-day window, spanning from five days before to five days after landfall. A composite average precipitation profile was then calculated for TARs and ZARs, respectively. Subsequently, accumulated precipitation was computed from day − 1 to day +2 relative to landfall. Day − 1 was included because the daily precipitation values were computed from 12:00 UTC of the current day to 12:00 UTC of the following day, following the standard of the Chilean Meteorological Service (DMC). This implies that rainfall occurring between 00:00 and 12:00 UTC is recorded as part of the previous day’s accumulation. Finally, the difference in accumulated precipitation between TARs and ZARs over the day − 1 to day +2 period was evaluated.

The discharge data from 1994 to 2023 was obtained from the Global Flood Awareness System (GloFAS) available from the European Centre for Medium-range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF)71. The recently updated version, i.e., GloFAS v4.0, provides gridded discharge information at 0.05\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} × 0.05\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} spatial resolution and daily temporal resolution. This dataset was processed similarly to the precipitation data, in order to compare the discharge associated with TARs and ZARs. The main difference in processing, compared to the precipitation data, was that in this case an average was calculated from day 0 to day +3, as it was possible to download hourly data in UTC format. Additionally, discharge data were obtained from flow gauges closest to the coast, maintained by the Chilean Water Directorate (DGA), and accessible through the CR2 Climate Explorer. These data were used to validate GloFAS in our study region, showing good correlation across all cases, with values exceeding 90% in some basins such as the Biobio, Itata, and Toltén. Additionally, a correlation between IVT and discharge reveals that the maximum correlation coefficient is reached after considering a lag of 2 or 3 days, indicating that the peak discharge typically occurs on day + 2 or day + 3 following the impact of the ARs.

High resolution daily data from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) onboard the Aqua satellite were analyzed for the 2003–2020 period. We used the surface reflectance centered at 645 nm (Rrs(645), MODIS band 1) as a proxy for freshwater turbid plumes because of the low penetration in the water column and its high coherence delineating river plumes31,72, and its correlation with surface salinity29. To assess the impact of TARs and ZARs on river plumes, we calculated composite anomalies of reflectance for each AR type using an 11-day window centered on each landfall. To capture the immediate post-event response, reflectance anomalies for days + 1, + 2, and + 3 were averaged. Anomalies beyond day + 3 were excluded due to the progressive weakening of the signal.

Field sampling—Coliumo campaign

During July 2022 and June–July 2023, oceanographic measurements were conducted in Coliumo Bay (Fig. 1b). At the seabed (20 m) of the same station, an RDI Teledyne 600 kHz ADCP (Acoustic Doppler Current Profiler) was deployed over the same period, providing continuous current profiles at 1 m vertical resolution from 5 m depth down to the seabed. A Seabird HydroCAT was also deployed on the seafloor during the second half of July 2022 and again in June–July 2023. The HydroCAT was equipped with sensors for temperature, salinity, turbidity, oxygen, and chlorophyll-a (fluorescence).

Additionally, a CTD-RBR Maestro mounted on a Del Mar Oceanographic Wirewalker was deployed for four days (8–12 July 2022) to collect data during the passage of a TAR (see Fig. 1b for location). The Wirewalker is a wave-powered profiling system equipped with sensors for temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), chlorophyll-a (fluorescence), and turbidity. This instrument measured water column properties from the surface down to approximately 20 meters, collecting between 20 and 40 profiles per hour from July 8 to 12, 2022, capturing the landfall of TAR 1. All oceanographic data were recorded at hourly resolution and presented as time series. These oceanographic observations were complemented by atmospheric data (air temperature, solar radiation, pressure, and wind speed) obtained from the Dichato meteorological station, provided by the Chilean Meteorological Directorate (DMC).
