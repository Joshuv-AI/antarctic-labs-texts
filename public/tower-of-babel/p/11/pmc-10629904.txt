Health Literacy and Health Care Utilization in Austria
Authors: Andreas Kvas, Gregor Kvas
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Efficient use of health care is crucial for health care systems; the literature reveals that health literacy (HL) plays an important role in health services utilization.Objective:This study aimed to analyze the relationship between HL and health care utilization in Austria.Methods:Registered data of 1,010 persons in the general population concerning costs of doctor visits, medication costs, and number, days, and costs of hospitalization were analyzed using a cross-sectional design for the observation period 2017 to 2019. HL was measured as general, digital, and navigational HL using the Austrian part of the European Health Literacy Survey between March 2020 and May 2020. Multivariable two-part hurdle regressions with generalized linear models, considering sociodemographic variables and chronic illnesses, were conducted to investigate the relationship between HL and health care utilization.Key Results:Navigational HL had a significant negative effect on the odds of causing costs at general practitioners, not adjusting (b = −0.02) and adjusting (b = −0.01) for sociodemographic variables and chronic illnesses. The effect was small, and no significant effect on any other type of analyzed health care was found.Conclusions:Further research on specific populations (e.g., persons with chronic diseases such as diabetes), rather than the general population, may reveal relevant effects of HL on health care utilization measured by registered data in Austria. [HLRP: Health Literacy Research and Practice. 2023;7(4):e197–e206.]

In the context of an aging society, efficient health care use is crucial for healthcare systems. Previous studies have indicated a relationship between individuals' health literacy (HL) and their extent of using healthcare services. HL is defined as follows:

“Health literacy is linked to literacy and entails people's knowledge, motivation and competences to access, understand, appraise, and apply health information in order to make judgments and take decisions in everyday life concerning healthcare, disease prevention and health promotion to maintain or improve quality of life during the life course”

(Sørensen et al., 2012, p. 3).

Recent publications have listed numerous studies indicating more efficient use of health care services among people with higher HL. Gerich et al. (2022) reported on research that showed lower medication costs, number of doctor visits, and hospitalization for people with higher HL. Moreover, Berens et al. (2018) cited studies showing higher health service use among individuals with lower HL.

In contrast, Gerich et al. (2022) indicated that, in the presence of low health-related knowledge, high HL can lead to more frequent doctor visits than low HL. They also referred to studies using registered data, which showed different results for different types of health services. Berens et al. (2018) also found inconsistent results when analyzing survey data on health care utilization. They reported more doctor visits by individuals with lower HL, but no corresponding effect on hospital and emergency department use.

Possible explanations for these contradictory results include variations in the tools used to measure HL, the data sources used to measure healthcare utilization, and the statistical methods used to calculate HL's possible effect on health services use.

The recently realized International Health Literacy Population Survey Project 2019–2021 (HLS19) was conducted by the World Health Organization Action Network on Measuring Population and Organizational Health Literacy. As part of HLS19, the national results of the Austrian survey (HLS19-AT) showed a lower number of self-reported doctor visits, emergency department services, and day-patient hospital services among people with higher HL (Griebler et al., 2021).

The current study aimed to analyze the relationship between HL, measured by survey data from the HLS19-AT, and healthcare utilization, measured by registered data of costs of doctor visits, medication costs, and number, days, and costs of hospitalization in Austria's general population. The hypothesis is that there is a negative relationship between HL and health care utilization.

Methods

Participants and Sample

The study included 1,010 participants of the HLS19-AT who provided informed consent to disclose their national insurance number to link their self-reported HL to their registered data on health care utilization for research purposes. The linked data were anonymized.

The HLS19-AT study target population comprised permanent residents age 18 years and older and living in private households. The survey was conducted between March 2020 and May 2020 under the coordination of the Austrian National Public Health Institute. Data were gathered via computer-assisted telephone interviews. A multistage random sampling procedure was used, reaching 3,535 net interviews, resulting in a response rate of 16%. For further details on the HLS19-AT survey, please refer to Griebler et al. (2021).

Measures

Health Literacy

The self-perceived HL of the Austrian population was retrieved from the HLS19-AT, which used the German translation of the English HLS19 Tool. In addition to general HL, specific digital HL and navigational HL were covered in the survey and used in this study.

General HL was represented by 12 items considering the content dimensions of health care, disease prevention, and health promotion. For each dimension, general HL measures the ability to access, understand, appraise, and apply health-related information. Digital HL was operationalized using eight items measuring the individual, social, technical, critical, and analytical ability to search for, access, understand, appraise, and apply digitally available health information. Navigational HL was gathered through 12 items. It considers the competence to cope with information concerning health care and the health system to access the right care in the right place at the right time.

For general, digital, and navigational HL, respondents had to rate the tasks comprising each particular HL on a scale from very easy to very difficult. Calculated HL scores determine the relative number of tasks for each kind of HL rated as very easy or easy, resulting in a value range of 0 to 100 with higher values representing higher HL. Griebler et al. (2021) provided further details on the HLS19-AT questionnaire and concepts of HL.

Health Care Utilization

Health care utilization was measured as costs of face-to-face doctor visits (not including mid-level practitioners), medication costs, number, days, and costs of hospitalization, and were gathered from registered data from Austrian social security institutions. The sum of the measures for each participant was calculated for the observation years from 2017 to 2019. The costs of doctor visits were restricted to data from the Austrian Social Health Insurance (SHI) fund (Österreichische Gesundheitskasse), which covers about 80% of Austria's insured population. The other measures were available from registered data of additional SHI funds. Together with the Austrian SHI fund, these funds cover about 99% of the insured population.

The costs of doctor visits were analyzed separately for general practitioners, medical specialists (excluding dental physicians), and dental physicians. Each type of doctor was further separated into panel and elective physicians, owing to the different payoff systems in these two groups. Panel physicians' cost data reflect the tariff costs of the physicians' service paid by the Austrian SHI fund. Patients must pay elective physicians out-of-pocket and are subsequently reimbursed for 80% of the applicable SHI tariff. Therefore, elective physician costs represented the fees paid by patients. Due to over 90% and over 80% of patients not using services by elective general practitioners and elective dental physicians, respectively, no analysis was carried out for the corresponding costs.

Hospitalization measures included inpatient and day-patient hospital admissions; no data were available from hospital outpatient departments or emergency care services. Hospitalization costs were measured by counting the Austrian diagnosis-related group (DRG) points assigned to each hospitalization according to the DRG payment system (Leistungsorientierte Krankenanstaltenfinanzierung). The more services the patient needs and the more severe the given diagnosis, the more points are attributed. In contrast to the defined euro value of a DRG point, this assignment is independent of the Austrian federal state level, on which hospital care financing is based.

The costs of prescribed medications were measured by the “pay desk” price, because it is the same across different pharmacies.

Sociodemographic Variables

Sociodemographic variables were obtained from the HLS19-AT. The highest educational level was measured using the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) 2011, which consists of nine categories (UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 2012). In the analysis, these categories were merged into “lower secondary education or below” (up to ISCED-2), “higher secondary education” (ISCED-3), and “post-secondary, short-cycle tertiary education, bachelor or higher” (ISCED-4 to 8). Whether participants had been trained in a health care profession was assessed by yes or no.

Financial status was measured by questioning how easy or difficult it is to pay bills in general at the end of the month, with options ranging from very easy to very difficult on a 4-point Likert scale. Employment status was gathered by several categories, then merged into the three categories “self-employed,” “unemployed,” and “nonworking.”

Degrees of urbanity were categorized as “predominately urban,” “intermediate,” and “predominately rural.” Geographical areas were considered for Austria's nine federal states: Vienna, Lower Austria, Burgenland, Styria, Carinthia, Upper Austria, Salzburg, Tyrol, and Vorarlberg.

Chronic Illness

In the HLS19-AT, respondents were asked if they have any long-term illnesses or chronic health problems that have lasted or are expected to last for 6 months or more. Possible answers were no, yes, one, and yes, more than one.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.1). Two-part hurdle models with generalized linear models were calculated as multivariable regressions in a cross-sectional design using the R package pscl (version 1.5.5). This method was chosen to model count and cost data, which are typically skewed with an additional overproportioned quota of zeros, describing participants without registered data in the observation period concerning the examined type of health service.

One part of the hurdle model analyzed HL's effect on the odds of using health care (0 vs. > 0), calculated using binary logistic regression. The other part analyzed HL's effect on the count data (>0) via a negative binomial distribution (i.e., number, days, and DRG points of hospitalization) and cost data (>0) via a gamma distribution (i.e., costs of doctor visits and medication costs), using a logarithmic link function. To consider heteroscedasticity, robust standard errors of the effect estimates were computed using the sandwich R package (version 3.0.1). To avoid confounding the model results through extreme values, the 2% of participants with the highest amount of health care utilization, regarding each outcome measure separately, were excluded from the analysis.

Gender, age, education, health care professionals, financial status, employment status, and chronic illness were used as control variables.

To consider the sample bias of the 1,010 available participants, the analysis reported in this study was additionally recalculated with weighted data. The weighting variables used were gender, age, education, federal state, and degree of urbanization. Weighting was conducted using the RIM method (Random Iterative Method) of the R package survey (version 4.1.1), ensuring that all weighting variables were considered. The reported, unweighted and the unreported, weighted results were analog.

Self-reported chronic illness had the most significant effect on all analyzed health care services, showing more health care use among participants with more chronic health problems. Reproduced analyses using the sample weighted by gender, age, education, federal state, and degree of urbanization showed no differences in statistical significance from the reported unweighted results.

Discussion

The present study included 1,010 participants in the HLS19-AT who disclosed their national insurance number. It analyzed the effect of general, digital, and navigational HL on health care utilization measured by costs of doctor visits, extent of hospitalization, and medication costs gathered from registered data from Austrian social security institutions.

In the unadjusted and adjusted HL effect models, the hurdle regression models showed lower odds of causing costs for panel general practitioners for participants with higher navigational HL. The effect was statistically significant (p < .05), albeit small (unadjusted, b = −0.02; adjusted, b = −0.01). Similarly, the HLS19-AT study (Griebler et al., 2021) showed fewer visits to general practitioners, but it was associated with higher digital HL. This study found no effect of HL for panel medical specialists, whereas the HLS19-AT study demonstrated a negative association between general HL and visits to a medical specialist. The HLS19-AT study did not distinguish between visits to panel and elective doctors. In line with HLS19-AT, no effect of HL was observed on hospital admissions. The present study used the same data regarding HL and sociodemographic variables as the HLS19-AT study. However, HLS19-AT measured health care utilization using a questionnaire, not registered data, calculated ordinary least squares regressions, and did not include any health status in the regression model. Moreover, it was not restricted to participants who disclosed national insurance numbers.

Another Austrian study used registered data and included health as a covariate but was restricted to data from the Upper Austrian sickness fund; it demonstrated fewer doctor visits and medication costs associated with high HL combined with high health-related knowledge (Gerich et al., 2022). In the present study, no HL effect was found on medication costs. In the HLS19-AT questionnaire, no health-related knowledge was available to compare these groups. Additionally, Gerich et al. (2022) listed several studies showing inconsistent results regarding HL's effect on the frequency and costs of health care utilization.

Berens et al. (2018) suggested a negative association between HL and self-reported visits to doctors and other health care professionals. The study was based on nationally representative survey data from Germany, which did not use registered data, and calculated ordinary least squares regressions. In line with their results, we considered health to be a predictor.

With the given data, it was not possible to analyze over- or under-utilization or the reason for service use. Several studies have shown a positive association between HL and medication adherence (Chima et al., 2020; Miller, 2016; Shah et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2019). In the present study, this could have resulted in under-utilization among participants with lower HL and, thus, no significant effects of HL on medication costs.

The Austrian National Public Health Institute's feedback on the presented results refers to a possible overlay of the reverse effects of HL, resulting in no significant effects. Griebler et al. (2021) indicated that HL is positively associated with health. Additionally, HL is thought to increase coping with one's chronic diseases. Higher measured HL leads to lower utilization in the first case and higher health care utilization in the second case.

Different studies demonstrated the positive effect of HL on disease-related knowledge among persons with chronic diseases, which consequently could result in better self-management skills (Al Sayah et al., 2013; Bailey et al., 2014; Mohd Isa et al., 2021; Mackey et al., 2016; Marciano et al., 2019; Shah et al., 2021). Focusing on persons with chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes) instead of the general population may reveal possible relevant effects of HL on health care utilization measured by registered data.

Strengths and Limitations

A particular strength of this study was the analysis of registered data instead of self-reported measures of health care utilization. Therefore, the outcome measure is objective and not memory-biased, as it could be when using questionnaire data. Additionally, the regression models used took the skewed and discrete distributions into account, including the overproportioned quota of zeros that are typical for healthcare utilization data. Another strength is the linkage to the HLS19-AT study and its multidimensional approach in measuring HL through not only different content dimensions of general HL, but also in gathering specific digital HL and navigational HL. Hence, no simple functional HL measurements were used.

This study has some limitations, including its cross-sectional design, which precluded proving causality. Additionally, since the present study was observational and required participants' consent to disclose their national insurance number, self-selection bias in any direction concerning the results is possible.

Due to the different payoff systems for panel and elective physicians, it was not possible to analyze the cumulative costs of both sectors. An association between participant characteristics and preference for visiting a physician could bias separate analyses. Unfortunately, a valid alternative for summing the number of doctor visits of both types was not possible because of the lack of existing data on the number of visits to elective physicians. Finally, all the predictor variables in the regression models, including the HL measure, were self-reported. Therefore, they may not reflect the exact value of education, financial or employment status, or chronic illness, and the HL measure is a subjective and not an objective, test-based measure. Using a self-reported HL measure could cause patients with a higher number of contacts to health care to rate their HL lower because they might face more complex situations. Therefore, self-reported chronic illness is included in the analyses (Berens et al., 2018).

Data collection on HL started during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. No correlation was found between the item on concern about the pandemic and general HL (Griebler et al., 2021). In Germany, data were collected before the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, Germany conducted an additional round of data collection in summer 2020 to assess the effects of COVID-19 on HL. No significant change was found in general HL and navigational HL. Only the portion of population with excellent digital HL showed a significant increase (Schaeffer et al., 2021). With these results it is assumed that the pandemic had no effect on HL at data collection between March 2020 and May 2020 in Austria.

Conclusion

The present study observed only small or no significant effects of HL on health care utilization, as measured by registered data in Austria. Further research focusing on persons with chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes) rather than the general population, with additional data on the reason for health service use, may reveal possible relevant effects of HL.
