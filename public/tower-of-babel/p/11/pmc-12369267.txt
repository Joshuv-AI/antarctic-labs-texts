FBXO42 promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression via mediating p57Kip2 ubiquitination and degradation
Authors: Bing Zhou, Shasha Wu, Shengqian Hong, Han Li, Zhi Jiang, Yong Sun, Jiannan Qiu, Lei Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), one of the most common malignancies, accounts for about 80% of all primary liver cancers. FBXO42 belongs to the F-box protein family and functions as a key component of the SCF (Skp1–Cullin–F-box) ubiquitin ligase complex, which is involved in the process of protein ubiquitination. The studies on the role of FBXO42 in different diseases are inadequate, especially in HCC.MethodsThe expression and the clinical features of FBXO42 in HCC were assessed using the HCCDB, TCGA, and ICGC databases. cBioPortal and SangerBox databases were used to analyze the genetic alterations and mutation profile of FBXO42. TIMER, TISIDB and GEPIA databases were used to examine the correlation of FBXO42 with tumor-infiltrating immune cells, tumor-associated fibroblasts and cancer stemness. CCK8 assay, clone formation assay and EDU assay were utilized to reveal the cell viability and proliferation ability. Meanwhile, transwell assay was used to assess the cellular migration. UbiBrowser and Co-IP revealed the FBXO42-interacting protein in HCC. The hTFtarget, ENCODE and CHEA databases were used to predict potential transcriptional regulators.ResultsOur data displayed that FBXO42 was highly expressed in HCC tissues and was associated with poor prognosis. Moreover, FBXO42 expression was correlated with immune cell infiltration and immune-related molecules, suggesting that FBXO42 may lead to a complex immune microenvironment in HCC. Meanwhile, our data revealed that the level of FBXO42 was positively associated with CAFs infiltration and cancer stemness. In addition, FBXO42 facilitated the malignant behaviors of HCC. Mechanistically, FBXO42 interacted p57Kip2, sequentially promoting p57Kip2 ubiquitination and degradation, ultimately leading to HCC progression. Finally, YY1 had been demonstrated to upregulate the expression of FBXO42 via transcriptional regulation.ConclusionsThis study revealed that FBXO42 promotes the malignancy of HCC through FBXO42-mediated p57Kip2 ubiquitination and degradation. Our findings underscore the possibility of FBXO42 as a potential therapeutic target for HCC.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03050-z.

Introduction

As is widely recognized, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors and one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality in the world [1, 2]. Unfortunately, the incidence of HCC is particularly excessive in China [3, 4]. Long-term and persistent hepatitis virus infection is a primary etiological factor for HCC. At the same time, both alcohol abuse and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease are emerging as serious risk factors for HCC [5, 6]. Due to the advanced stage at which many HCC patients are diagnosed, they often miss the opportunity for surgical intervention, resulting in a poor prognosis [7]. With advancements in modern surgical techniques, an increasing number of HCC patients are gaining access to treatment. Regrettably, high recurrence and metastasis rates continue to contribute to unsatisfactory postoperative survival outcomes [8]. Consequently, it is imperative to clarify the underlying mechanisms governing the oncogenesis and development of HCC.

F-box only protein 42 (FBXO42) belongs to the F-box protein family, characterized by the presence of an F-box domain. The F-box domain, typically situated at the N-terminus of these proteins, is a specific amino acid sequence that facilitates protein–protein interactions. F-box protein family members can be categorized into distinct subclasses according to their structural characteristics and substrate recognition properties. For instance, human F-box proteins are classified into subclasses, such as FBXL, FBXW, and FBXO, depending on whether they contain leucine-rich repeats (LRRs) or WD repeat units [9]. The involvement of the F-box protein family in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has been documented in various studies. For instance, FBXO7-mediated downregulation of PRMT1 expression results in diminished arginine methylation and subsequent activation of PHGDH, which in turn leads to impaired serine synthesis, increased accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and ultimately inhibits hepatoma cell proliferation [10]. FBXO28 inhibits the epithelial–mesenchymal transition of HCC cells via directly interacting with SNAI2 and functioning as an E3 ubiquitin ligase to degrade SNAI2 via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway [11]. The role of FBXO42 in tumor development had been reported. Such as, FBXO42 enhances neuroblastoma cell proliferation in a TP53-dependent manner [12]. FBXO42, identified as an RBPJ-interacting protein in leukemia, promotes global chromatin relaxation by facilitating the polyubiquitination of RBPJ at lysine 175, thereby enhancing the association of RBPJ with the chromatin remodeling complex [13]. However, the detailed roles and regulatory mechanisms of FBXO42 in the HCC progression remain to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the expression levels, prognostic significance and mutation profiles of FBXO42 in HCC using multiple public databases. We also examined the relationship between FBXO42 and infiltrating immune cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in the tumor microenvironment (TME), as well as tumor stemness. To elucidate the potential biological functions of FBXO42, we conducted a comprehensive series of functional assays to further assess its function in HCC malignancy. Mechanistically, we discovered that FBXO42 interacts with p57Kip2, facilitating its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation. This interaction leads to a reduction in p57Kip2 protein level, thereby promoting the progression of HCC. Furthermore, the upregulation of YY1 serves as a critical mediator in the enhanced expression of FBXO42 in HCC.

Materials and methods

Data sources and analysis

Utilizing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), we employed TIMER 2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org/) to evaluate the expression levels of FBXO42 in HCC and normal liver tissues [14], also we analyzed the expression levels of FBXO42 using the HCCDB database [15], which integrates data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE22058, GSE25097, GSE36376 and GSE63898) and the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC). Based on the data from TCGA, we evaluated the expression levels of FBXO42 in the HCC cancer tissues and the corresponding normal liver tissues, the relationship between FBXO42 expression and the T stage of HCC, survival analysis of HCC patients based on FBXO42 expression, and the relationship between FBXO42 expression and markers of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and cancer stemness. The correlation between FBXO42 level and the pathological stage of HCC was analyzed through the GEPIA database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/). Furthermore, the GEPIA database was utilized to illustrate the correlation between the expression levels of FBXO42 and two genes, including CDKN1C and YY1 in HCC.

The cBioPortal database (http://cbioportal.org) was chose to investigate genetic alterations of FBXO42. For subsequent analysis, we selected the Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (LIHC) data set from TCGA (Firehose Legacy). The mutation profile of FBXO42 in HCC, and the association between FBXO42 expression and ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore were evaluated using the platform available at http://sangerbox.com/Tool. In addition, the TISIDB database (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/) was utilized to investigate the correlation between FBXO42 level and a diverse range of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, immunoinhibitors, immunostimulators, chemokines, and receptors. The TIMER 2.0 database was utilized to assess the correlation between FBXO42 expression and the infiltrating abundances of regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), macrophages, and CAFs in HCC. In addition, the prognostic value of FBXO42 expression in conjunction with multiple immune infiltrating cells was evaluated. The UbiBrowser 2.0 was selected to predict the potential FBXO42-interacting substrates [16]. The hTFtarget, ENCODE, CHEA databases were used to predict the transcription factors that may activate FBXO42 transcription in HCC.

Cell culture and transfection

HCC cell lines (HepG2 and Hep3B) were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) located in Manassas, Virginia, USA. These cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic solution (streptomycin/penicillin), all sourced from Gibco, USA. The culturing process was conducted in a humidified incubator maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The lentiviral vectors encoding FBXO42 and CDKN1C (designated as LV-FBXO42 and LV-CDKN1C), short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting FBXO42 (designated as LV-shFBXO42), along with the corresponding empty vector (LV-vector and LV-shNC) were designed from Hanheng biological company (Shanghai, China). Lentiviral vectors encoding YY1 (LV-YY1), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting YY1 (YY1-siRNA) and their corresponding empty vector (LV-vector, si-NC) was designed from GenePharma Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All stably transfected HCC cell lines were generated through polybrene-mediated transduction and subsequently harvested following a 7-day selection period with puromycin.

CCK-8 assay

The CCK-8 Cell Counting Kit, obtained from Vazyme (Nanjing, China), was utilized to assess cell proliferation activity according to the manufacturer's protocols. All stably transfected cells were planted into 96-well (500 cells/well). At designated timepoints, 10μL of CCK-8 solution was supplemented to each well, diluted with 90μL of culture medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, after which the absorbance at 450 nm was measured for each well.

Clone formation assay

All stably transfected cells were planted into 6-well plates (500 cells/well) and incubated in 2 mL of medium for 14 days. The proliferating colonies were subsequently fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet (Servicebio, Wuhan, China).

Transwell assay

The transwell assay were conducted to evaluate cell migrative capacity. 1 × 10^4 cells resuspended in 200µL of serum-free DMEM were seeded into the upper chamber, while 800µL of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber to induce cell migration across the membrane. After 24 h of incubation, the chambers were carefully retrieved and the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After gently erasing the non-migrated cells on the upper surface with a cotton swab, the membranes were subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 15 min.

EdU assay

All stably transfected cells were seeded into 96-well plates (5000 cells/well) in 100 μl of medium. Following a 24-h incubation period, the cells were exposed to EdU (50 μM) for a duration of 2 h at 37 °C. Following this, the cells were fixed using a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 30 min and subsequently permeabilized with a 0.5% Triton X-100 solution. EdU staining was performed using the Apollo cross-linking reaction reagent for 30 min, followed by nuclear counterstaining with Hoechst33342. Ultimately, EdU-positive cells were visualized through a fluorescence microscope.

Animal experiment

Four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were provided by Jiangsu Huachuang Xinnuo Biotechnology (Taizhou, China). All mice were housed in a specific pathogen free environment with a 12-h circadian cycle of light and dark, and had free access to water and food. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Experimental Animal Management Committee of Soochow University. The transfected cells (1 × 10^6 cells/100μL) were inoculated into the subcutaneous area of the left axilla of BALB/c nude mice. Monitoring was performed every 4 days and euthanasia was performed after 28 days.

qRT-PCR

In this study, the total RNA was extracted from HCC cells using the Vazyme Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent (Vazyme). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted via using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer's standard protocols. The primer sequences utilized for PCR amplification in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays

The total protein extraction was dependent on the NCM RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail and 1% PMSF. All stably transfected cells were lysed for 30 min, during which the samples were agitated every 10 min. Following the addition of 5 × SDS–PAGE sample loading buffer, the lysate was heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Thereafter, the protein samples were subjected to separation via 10% SDS–PAGE and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. All PVDF membranes were blocked with NcmBlot blocking buffer for 30 min. The primary antibodies used in this study: FBXO42 (Invitrogen), GAPDH (Proteintech), p57Kip2 (Proteintech), anti-Flag (Proteintech), anti-HA (Proteintech) and YY1 (Proteintech).

All stably transfected cells were lysed on ice using 800µL of NP-40 lysis buffer supplemented with a 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysis procedure was conducted according to the manufacturer's protocol to ensure optimal cell lysis and protein preservation. Following lysis, we centrifuged the samples at 12,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. A 10% aliquot of the supernatant was reserved as an input control. The remaining supernatant (500 µL) was incubated with 5µL of primary antibody specific to the target protein overnight at 4 °C with gentle agitation. The following day, immune complexes were precipitated using Protein A/G agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and incubated overnight under the same conditions. After precipitation, the beads were washed extensively and resuspended in SDS loading buffer for subsequent Western blotting assay.

Ubiquitination assay

Following transfection, cells were incubated with 10 μM MG132 (Beyotime) for 8 h and subsequently harvested by pre-cooling RIPA buffer supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors, and lysed on ice. After sonication, the lysate was centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was then incubated overnight at 4 °C on a shaker with primary antibodies specific to the target protein and protein A/G-agarose beads. Upon completion of incubation, the immune complexes were washed with IP wash buffer and re-centrifuged. Finally, SDS loading buffer was added to the samples, which were boiled for 10 min, and the ubiquitination level of the target protein was analyzed by Western blotting.

IHC and TUNEL staining

Upon acquisition of the samples, they were promptly fixed in a 4% formaldehyde solution and embedded into the paraffin. Thereafter, tissues were sectioned into 4 μm slices. These slices were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with the antibody against Ki-67 (Servicebio). Following this, the slices were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) was utilized as the detection reagent to display the immunoreactivity.

TUNEL staining was conducted on paraffin-embedded tumor slices to evaluate the extent of apoptosis through the Klenow–FragEL DNA Fragmentation Detection Kit (Servicebio) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol.

Luciferase reporter assay

To investigate the interaction between YY1 and FBXO42 promoter region, the dual luciferase reporter assay was performed. After the cells from each group were cultured in 24-well plates to 70% confluence, they were co-transfected with 1 μg of FBXO42 promoter luciferase reporter plasmid, thymidine promotor-renilla luciferase reporter plasmid (for normalization) using Lipofectamine® 3000 (Invitrogen). Luciferase activity was measured 48 h post-transfection utilizing the dual luciferase reporter system (Promega, USA).

Statistical analysis

In this study, GraphPad Prism version 8.0 was used to calculate the p values, and the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For comparisons among two groups, Student's t test was employed. All experiments were replicated at least three times, and statistical significance was stated as follows: ns, not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

Results

FBXO42 is highly expressed in HCC tissues and is associated with an unfavorable prognosis

To investigate the potential effect of FBXO42 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we performed an evaluation of the differential expression of FBXO42. Through analysis of data from the TCGA data set via the TIMER 2.0 database, overexpression of FBXO42 was observed in the HCC tissues compared to the normal liver tissues (Fig. 1A). In addition, the overexpression of FBXO42 was identified in multiple HCC databases, including GSE22058, GSE25097, GSE36376, GSE63898, and ICGC database, through the analysis of HCCDB platform (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, evaluation of the FBXO42 mRNA level in 50 HCC tissues and their corresponding normal tissues was detected via analysis the data from TCGA database, it was observed that FBXO42 is obviously upregulated in HCC tissues (Fig. 1C). The expression level of FBXO42 was observed to increase significantly in correlation with the T stage of HCC (Fig. 1D), and exhibited a positive correlation with the pathological stage of HCC (Fig. 1E). In addition to disease specific survival, analyses of overall survival and progress free interval illustrated that elevated expression levels of FBXO42 were significantly associated with an unfavorable prognosis in HCC (Fig. 1F). Mutation profile of FBXO42 in HCC

In this study, we examined the types and frequency of genetic alterations in FBXO42 in HCC via utilizing HCC data from the TCGA–Firehose Legacy data set (379 samples) based on the cBioPortal platform. The alteration frequency of FBXO42 in HCC was 11%, encompassing various genetic alterations, such as missense mutation, truncating mutation, amplification, deep deletion, mRNA high and mRNA low (Fig. 2A, B). Furthermore, we also examined the relationship between FBXO42 gene mutation types and their corresponding mRNA expression levels. The findings revealed that in HCC samples, those with FBXO42 deep deletion exhibited significantly lower mRNA expression levels and those with FBXO42 amplification exhibited higher mRNA expression levels (Fig. 2C). The comprehensive mutational profiles revealed several mutations in the FBXO42 gene in HCC samples, including G301E, Q392Vfs*8, P540A, P571Afs*82 and S677I (Fig. 2D). Meanwhile, the analysis of FBXO42 gene expression and mutational profiles identified the top ten most frequently mutated genes: TP53, OBSCN, AXIN1, TSC2, SYCP2, ANK2, CHD9, IL6ST, CLTC and DNAH11 (Fig. 2E).Fig. 2Mutation landscape of FBXO42 in HCC. FBXO42 modulates the infiltration levels of diverse tumor-associated immune cells in HCC

More and more researches have shown that tumor development is modulated by the immune microenvironment [17–19]. Therefore, this study investigates the correlation between FBXO42 level and tumor-infiltrating immune cells in HCC. We investigated the correlation between FBXO42 gene expression and immune, stromal, and composite scores in HCC patients using the ESTIMATE algorithm. Our analysis revealed a negative correlation between FBXO42 level and the immune score (r = − 0.11, p = 0.04). However, there was no significant correlation between FBXO42 expression and stromal score (r = − 0.05, p = 0.35), and overall estimation score (r = − 0.09, p = 0.08) (Fig. 3A). By analyzing the TISIDB database, we discovered a significant negative correlation between the FBXO42 expression and the infiltration abundance of the majority of immune cells (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 2). Based on the TISIDB database, HCC samples were categorized into 6 distinct immune subtypes: C1 (wound-healing), C2 (IFN-γ-dominant), C3 (inflammatory), C4 (lymphodepleted), C5 (immunoquiescent), and C6 (TGF-β-dominant). This study revealed a significant correlation between FBXO42 expression and the immunophenotype of HCC (p = 2.81e−04) (Fig. 3C). In addition, FBXO42 expression exhibited a strong association with various molecular subtypes (p = 1.81e−6) (Fig. 3D). Immune cell infiltration analysis (EPIC) conducted using the SangerBox platform revealed that FBXO42 expression was positively correlated with CAFs infiltration, while it was negatively correlated with macrophage and natural killer (NK) cell infiltration in HCC (Fig. 3E). Ultimately, the influence of FBXO42 expression levels and varying degrees of immune cell infiltration on the prognosis of HCC patients was examined. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that HCC patients with elevated FBXO42 expression and increased infiltration of MDSCs, myeloid dendritic cells and macrophages exhibited a significantly reduced survival rate (Fig. 3F). FBXO42 modulates the tumor microenvironment in HCC

Considering the pivotal role of CD8 + T cells in the immune response to HCC, we performed a comprehensive analysis of markers associated with exhausted CD8 + T cells. Our findings revealed that FBXO42 expression exhibited a negative correlation with markers of immunosuppression: CTLA-4, PDCD1, TGFB1, BTLA and CD96 (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, the expression of FBXO42 was identified to be negatively correlated with several markers of immune activation, including CD27, CD276, ICOS, TNFSF15, and TMEM173 (Fig. 4B). As displayed in Fig. 4C, the expression of FBXO42 in HCC was negatively correlated with the multiple members of the immunoinhibitors, immunostimulators, chemokines and receptors. Ultimately, the TIMER 2.0 database was utilized to systematically investigate the associations between FBXO42 expression and the infiltration level of regulatory T cells (Tregs), MDSCs, and M1/M2 macrophages. This study revealed that FBXO42 exhibited significant positive correlations with Tregs, MDSCs, and M2 macrophages (Fig. 4D). FBXO42 is associated with cancer-associated fibroblasts and contributes to cancer stemness in HCC

As has been extensively documented in various reports, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and cancer stemness characteristics of tumor cells are pivotal elements that drive the progression of HCC [20, 21]. First, we examined the correlation between FBXO42 expression and the abundance of CAFs in HCC through TIMER2.0 database, and discovered that FBXO42 expression was positively associated with CAFs abundance (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, we found that FBXO42 levels were positively associated with the eight markers of CAFs (VIM, FAP, PDGFRB, POSTN, ELN, FLNA, COL1A1 and COL1A 2) (Fig. 5B). In addition, the analysis based on TCGA database displayed that the expression level of FBXO42 was significantly and positively associated with the aforementioned eight markers of CAF cells (Fig. 5C, D). Concurrently, FBXO42 exhibited a positive correlation with cancer stemness in HCC via SangerBox platform (Fig. 5E). In addition, FBXO42 levels were positively associated with the eight markers of cancer stemness (CD24, CD44, THY1, PROM1, EPCAM, NANOG, POU5F1 and SOX2) (Fig. 5F, G). FBXO42 facilitates the malignant progression of HCC

While our initial investigation into the effects of FBXO42 on HCC development utilized a series of bioinformatics analyses, further validation is required to elucidate how FBXO42 influences the biological behavior of HCC. First, HepG2 cells were transfected with LV-FBXO42 to enhance the expression level of FBXO42, while Hep3B cells were transfected with shFBXO42 to silence FBXO42 level. The transfection efficiency was subsequently validated using Western blot analysis (Fig. 6A). Accordingly, we discovered that the overexpression of FBXO42 significantly promoted the proliferative activity of HepG2 cells, as evidenced by CCK-8 assays (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, clone formation assays revealed that FBXO42 overexpression enhanced the ability of clone formation of HepG2 cells (Fig. 6D). In addition, EDU staining results uncovered a significant increase in the proliferative ability of HepG2 cells following FBXO42 overexpression (Fig. 6F). Meanwhile, FBXO42 significantly enhanced the migratory capacity of HepG2 cells (Fig. 6H). Conversely, the silencing of FBXO42 significantly inhibited the growth activity, clone formation capability, proliferative activity, and migratory capacity of Hep3B cells (Fig. 6C, E, G, and I). FBXO42 interacts with p57Kip2 and mediates its ubiquitination and degradation in HCC

As a substrate recognition subunit of the SCF type E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, FBXO42 had been demonstrated to facilitate TP53 ubiquitination and degradation [22]. To further elucidate the specific mechanisms by which FBXO42 promotes the malignant progression of HCC, we concentrated our efforts on the identification of proteins that interact with FBXO42 in HCC. As shown in Fig. 8A, a number of potential interacting proteins of FBXO42 have been predicted. The protein p57Kip2, encoded by the CDKN1C (cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 C), is a highly valid inhibitor of the G1 cyclin/Cdk complex and functions as a negative regulator of cellular proliferation [23]. Given that p57Kip2 serves as a well-known tumor suppressor for HCC growth [24, 25], we propose that p57Kip2 may be a potential target regulated by FBXO42. We set out to investigate whether FBXO42 interacts with p57Kip2. The interaction between FBXO42 and p57KiP2 was definitely validated by Co-IP assays in HepG2 cells (Fig. 8B). We then explored the association between FBXO42 and p57Kip2. Our data suggest that neither the overexpression nor the inhibition of FBXO42 has any significant effect on the mRNA levels of CDKN1C, which encodes p57Kip2 (Fig. 8C). Meanwhile, no significant correlation was discovered between the mRNA levels of FBXO42 and CDKN1C in HCC data from GEPIA (Fig. 8D). However, we observed that the protein level of p57Kip2 was reduced following the overexpression of FBXO42, whereas it increased upon FBXO42 knockdown (Fig. 8E). Therefore, we hypothesized that FBXO42 played a role in the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of p57Kip2. Our results demonstrated that treatment with MG132 significantly mitigated the reduction in p57Kip2 protein levels in HepG2 cells (Fig. 8F). Our findings revealed that overexpression of FBXO42 markedly reduced the half-life of p57Kip2 protein in HepG2 cells (Fig. 8G). The ubiquitination assay demonstrated that overexpression of FBXO42 in HepG2 cells significantly enhanced the ubiquitination of p57Kip2 (Fig. 8H). Discussion

The functions and significance of F-box protein family members in tumorigenesis of HCC have been extensively documented in the literatures. For instance, FBXO22 facilitated the K48-linked ubiquitination and degradation of 40S ribosomal protein S5 (RPS5) at Lys85. The reduction in RPS5 expression subsequently activated the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, leading to the elevated levels of HIF-1α and VEGF-A, which exacerbated HCC progression [26]. FBXO9, which is upregulated by zinc finger protein 143 (ZNF143), promotes tumor growth and metastasis through its interaction with FBXW7, leading to the ubiquitination of FBXW7 [27]. This study examined the functional role and regulatory mechanisms of FBXO42 in HCC. According to previous studies, FBXO42, as a component of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, played a critical role in the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of its target proteins [28, 29]. In glioblastoma studies, FBXO42 was found to promote chromosome aggregation and spindle attachment, prevent mitotic arrest and activation of the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC), and reduce cell viability and tumor maintenance [30]. FBXO42 acts as a novel positive regulator for a specific subset of p53 mutants and functions in cooperation with CCDC6 to modulate USP28-mediated stabilization of mutant p53. Depletion of either FBXO42 or CCDC6 results in reduced metastatic potential of p53 mutant pancreatic cancer cells, a phenotype comparable to the direct depletion of mutant p53 [31]. It was reported that FBXO42 exacerbated non-alcoholic fatty liver disease by enhancing the ubiquitination and degradation of acetyl-CoA acyltransferase 2 (ACAA2), thereby reducing its expression levels [32]. The demethylation activity of GASC1 ensures the transcriptional repression of FBXO42, a ubiquitin ligase that mediates the polyubiquitination and degradation of ROCK2. This results in the upregulation of ROCK2, thereby promoting HCC progression [33]. While FBXO42 has been implicated in the prior study on HCC, its specific role in the progression of HCC remains unreported. In this study, we observed a marked upregulation of FBXO42 expression levels in HCC tissues relative to normal liver tissues. Clinical correlation analysis uncovered that elevated FBXO42 expression was markedly associated with advanced tumor stage (T stage) and higher pathological grade. HCC patients with elevated FBXO42 expression levels demonstrated significantly poorer overall survival and progress free interval.

A mutation is a permanent alteration in the nucleotide sequence of a gene, encompassing base pair substitutions, insertions, or deletions. Gene mutations can facilitate tumor initiation and progression through diverse mechanisms [34], including the inactivation of tumor-inhibiting genes, activation of proto-oncogenes, and impairments in DNA repair pathways [35, 36]. Consequently, this study examined the frequency and specific types of FBXO42 gene mutations in HCC. The incidence of FBXO42 genetic alterations was found to be 11%. Analysis of FBXO42 gene expression and mutational profiling in HCC identified the top ten most frequently mutated genes: TP53, OBSCN, AXIN1, TSC2, SYCP2, ANK2, CHD9, IL6ST, CLTC, and DNAH11. As we all know, mutations in the TP53 gene led to the loss of its normal functionality, particularly its role in inhibiting tumor cell proliferation, thereby leading to uncontrolled expansion of these cells [37–39]. FBXO42 may facilitate the progression of HCC by inducing genetic alterations and activating the expression or signaling pathways of specific oncogenes in HCC cells.

In recent years, the role of the tumor immune microenvironment in tumor development and treatment has garnered significant attention [40]. In HCC, immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment is predominantly mediated by various types of immune cells, including macrophages, dendritic cells, T cells, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells. The functional role of these cells, promoting or inhibiting tumor progression, is determined by their phenotypic characteristics and the signaling cues they receive from the tumor and the surrounding microenvironment [41]. M2 macrophages typically exhibit both immunosuppressive and pro-angiogenic properties in HCC via secreting VEGF, IL-10 and TGF-β to promote tumor growth, angiogenesis and inhibit antitumor immune responses [42]. Meanwhile, the regulatory T cells (Tregs) accumulate within the HCC microenvironment and secrete immunosuppressive cytokines, such as TGF-β, which inhibit the activity of effector T cells and facilitate tumor immune escape [43]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is typically characterized by hypoxic and acidic conditions, wherein tumor cells predominantly depend on glycolysis and/or glutamine metabolism to sustain their metabolic demands [44]. However, antigen-activated naïve T cells require essential nutrients such as glucose, glutamine, arginine, and other amino acids to undergo differentiation into effector cytotoxic T cells and memory T cells. Tumors exhibiting elevated glycolytic activity demonstrate enhanced capacity to evade T-cell-mediated immune surveillance [45]. Tumor cells exploit arginine metabolism via the upregulation of inducible nitric oxide synthase, thereby facilitating tumor progression and angiogenesis. This metabolic alteration results in the depletion of arginine within the TME, and the consequent low arginine levels impair the survival and function of natural killer (NK) cells and T cells, ultimately suppressing their anti-tumor immune responses [46]. There are numerous factors that influence the immune microenvironment of HCC, offering a variety of new avenues for identifying novel targets in cancer immunotherapy. In this study, we found that the expression level of FBXO42 exhibited a negative correlation with both the ImmuneScore and the majority of immune cells infiltrating in HCC. Moreover, the survival analysis integrating gene expression and immune cell infiltration levels revealed that HCC patients with elevated FBXO42 expression and increased infiltration of MDSCs, myeloid dendritic cells, and macrophages tend to exhibit a poorer prognosis. Meanwhile, we investigated the association of FBXO42 with immune-related molecules, such as immunoinhibitors, immunostimulators, chemokines and receptors, the observed negative correlation implied that FBXO42 played a multifaceted role in HCC tumorigenesis. Analysis of the TIMER 2.0 database displayed a positive correlation between the expression of FBXO42 and the presence of immunosuppressive cell populations, including Tregs, MDSCs, and M2 macrophages. Our findings indicated that FBXO42 may play a role in establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment in HCC via recruiting of various immunosuppressive cells into the tumor microenvironment.

The role of CAFs and tumor stemness in the progression of HCC is becoming increasingly well-elucidated [47, 48]. CAFs could originate from a variety of cell types, including non-activated fibroblasts, bone-marrow mesenchymal stem cells, tumor cells, endothelial cells, adipocytes, and stellate cells. CAFs facilitated tumor cellular proliferation and invasion, as well as tumor angiogenesis, constitute the primary mechanisms that facilitate cancer progression [49, 50]. For example, extracellular vesicles derived from CAFs carrying CTGF can promote the proliferation and invasion of hepatocellular carcinoma cells through interaction with the Notch1 receptor and subsequent activation of the Notch1/Snail1 signaling pathway in recipient cells [51]. Conversely, intrinsic FMO2 in CAFs can reduce PJA1-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of GYS1, thereby enhancing p65 phosphorylation and CCL19 transcription in CAFs. Upregulated CCL19 signaling promotes tumor infiltration and M1-like polarization of macrophages in HCC, as well as facilitating tertiary lymphoid structure formation and the chemotactic activity of CD8 + T cells [52]. Recently, LncRNA NEAT1 had been identified in CAF-derived extracellular vesicles, and promotes the stemness induction of liver cancer cells by promoting liquid–liquid-phase separation of the transcription factor YAP [53]. By analyzing the TIMER 2.0 and TCGA data sets, we identified a positive correlation between FBXO42 level in HCC and both the abundance of CAFs infiltration and the expression levels of CAF markers. Meanwhile, tumor stemness refers to the phenomenon, wherein a small number of cells in a tumor have stem cell properties, which can not only self-replicate, but also differentiate into a variety of tumor cell types. They play a vital role in the occurrence, development, invasion and metastasis of tumors, and are the key factors leading to resistance to anticancer therapies [20]. It was recently reported that SMYD2 promoted BMP4 expression by maintaining mono-methylation of histone 3 lysine 4 (H3K4me1) and di-methylation of histone 3 lysine 36 (H3K36me2) at its promoter region. Meanwhile, knockdown or inhibition of BMP4 reduces the stemness of sorafenib-resistant cells, suppresses the activation of SMAD1/5/8 (R-SMADs), and decreases the expression of the inhibitor of ID3 gene [54]. Interestingly, PLOD1 positively regulates the NF-κB/IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway and metabolic reprogramming of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in the pituitary gland. Inhibition of the NF-κB/IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway and TCA cycle activity effectively suppresses PLOD1-induced proliferation and the acquisition of cancer stem cell-like properties in HCC cells [55]. In this study, the positive correlation between tumor stemness markers and FBXO42 expression in HCC was confirmed. Further investigations are warranted to elucidate the precise role of FBXO42 in CAFs and tumor stemness in HCC.

We subsequently embarked on an investigation into the role of FBXO42 in the HCC progression. Our data confirmed that FBXO42 enhanced the growth viability, proliferation capacity, and migratory potential of HCC cells. Furthermore, the inhibition of FBXO42 significantly suppressed the in vivo proliferation of HCC cells. Subsequently, our research demonstrated that FBXO42 and p57Kip2 exhibit an interaction in HCC cells. Ubiquitination is a post-translational modification process in which ubiquitin molecules are covalently attached to target proteins via the C-terminal carboxyl group of ubiquitin. This process is mediated by a sequential three-enzyme cascade comprising ubiquitin-activating enzyme E1, ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2, and ubiquitin ligase E3 [56]. Ubiquitination can regulate various cellular functions, including protein activity, subcellular localization, signal transduction, and proteasome-mediated degradation. Moreover, ubiquitination is a dynamic and reversible modification, allowing for precise regulation of cellular processes [57]. Since FBXO42 functions as a ubiquitin ligase and is found to facilitate the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of p57Kip2, leading to a reduction in its protein levels. Notably, this process did not impact the mRNA levels of CDKN1C. The protein encoded by the CDKN1C gene, known as p57Kip2 (also referred to as KIP2 or p57), functions as a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor. The protein p57Kip2 played a pivotal role in cell cycle regulation by specifically acting as a negative regulator of cell proliferation [58, 59]. Rescue experiments confirmed that the tumor-promoting effect of FBXO42 in HCC is dependent on the reduction of p57Kip2 protein levels, which results from ubiquitination and degradation.

Finally, we investigated whether FBXO42 was regulated by transcriptional regulation in HCC. As shown in Fig. 10A, CTCF and YY1 may be the upstream transcription initiation factor. In previous studies, CTCF and YY1 have been proven to be involved in the progression of HCC. For example, the transcriptional activation protein CTCF has been recognized as a key factor driving the overexpression of METTL21A, and this overexpression contributes to the progression of HCC through the regulation of methylation in the Bcl-2-associated athanogene-3 (BAG3) gene [60]. Meanwhile, it was demonstrated that CTCF directly interacts with the promoter region of USP27 and enhances its expression. Subsequently, USP27 stabilizes PFKFB3, a key glycolytic enzyme, via deubiquitination, leading to increased glycolytic activity and the promotion of tumor progression [61]. In addition, YY1 can activate the transcription of USP13, thereby upregulating the expression of WWP1 and exerting a carcinogenic effect in HCC [62]. Even so, the transcription factor YY1 can bind to the promoter region of LINC01608 and promote its transcription. The resulting transcript subsequently interacts with miR-875-5p, ultimately leading to the activation of the EGFR/ERK signaling pathway [63]. Through the analysis of TCGA data, we observed that high expression levels of YY1 were significantly correlated with poorer overall survival in HCC. Consequently, we elected to investigate whether a transcriptional regulatory relationship exists between YY1 and FBXO42. Our data uncovered that YY1 could upregulate the expression of FBXO42 in HCC. However, whether CTCF regulates the expression of FBXO42 in HCC at the transcriptional level remains to be further elucidated through in-depth investigation. This aspect was not explored further in the present study.

In summary, this study provided compelling evidence for the clinical relevance and functional significance of FBXO42 in HCC. This study demonstrated that FBXO42 was significantly upregulated in HCC tissues and was strongly associated with poor patient prognosis. Through both in vitro and in vivo experiments, we have further substantiated that YY1-mediated upregulation of FBXO42 promotes malignant progression in HCC via ubiquitinating p57Kip2. These findings suggest that FBXO42 may potentially serve as a promising therapeutic target for HCC treatment.
