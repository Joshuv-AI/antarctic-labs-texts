VEGFA gene variants are associated with breast cancer progression
Authors: Jessica Furriol, Elisabeth Wik, Sura Aziz, Cecilie Askeland, Gøril Knutsvik, Lars A Akslen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAngiogenesis is recognized as a hallmark of cancer, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a key regulator of the angiogenic process and is related to cancer progression. Anti‐VEGF therapy has been tried but with limited success and without useful stratification for angiogenesis markers. Further, the landscape of VEGF single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in breast cancer and their clinical relevance is not well studied, and their relation to tissue‐based angiogenesis markers has not been explored. Here, we studied a selection of VEGFA SNPs in nontumor lymph nodes from a population‐based breast cancer cohort (n = 544), and their relation to clinicopathologic variables, vascular tissue metrics, and breast cancer‐specific survival. Two of the SNP candidates (rs833068GA genotype and rs25648CC genotype) showed associations with angiogenesis tissue markers, and the VEGFA rs833068GA genotype was associated with breast cancer‐specific survival among ER‐negative cases. We also found trends of association between the rs699947CA genotype and large tumor diameter and ER‐negative tumors, and between the rs3025039CC genotype and large tumor diameter. Our findings indicate some associations between certain VEGF SNPs, in particular the rs833068GA genotype, and both vascular metrics and patient survival. These findings and their potential implications need to be validated by independent studies.

Introduction

Angiogenesis is recognized as a hallmark of cancer [1, 2], and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), a key regulator of this process, is involved in the development and progression of solid tumors [3]. In breast cancer, increased VEGF expression and tissue‐based vascular markers are related to the advanced stage of the disease and worse prognosis [4, 5]. Anti‐VEGF therapy has been tried but with limited success [6, 7], highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving tumor angiogenesis. One potential avenue for elucidating these mechanisms lies in the investigation of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the VEGFA gene, which may influence VEGF expression levels and angiogenic activity.

SNPs are the most frequent variation within the genome, accounting for more than 90% of the variability [8], and bioinformatics algorithms can assist in the prediction of functional roles of these genetic variations [9]. The human VEGFA gene, consisting of eight exons and seven introns, is located on chromosome 6p21.3 and is highly polymorphic. Currently, more than 1,000 common SNPs have been identified and reported in this gene, including upstream and downstream sequences (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/variation/view/).

In this study, we aimed to address the knowledge gaps by examining a selection of VEGFA SNPs in a well‐characterized population‐based breast cancer cohort. Through analyses linking genetic variants to clinicopathologic features, vascular tissue metrics, and cancer‐specific survival, we sought to uncover potential genetic predictors of angiogenesis and disease progression in breast cancer patients.

Materials and methods

The study was approved by the Western Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics, REC West (Ref #2014/1984), and written informed consent was waived. In accordance with national ethics guidelines and procedures for such retrospective studies, all participants were contacted and asked if they objected. Patient records and information were anonymised and de‐identified prior to analysis.

Patient series

The study initially included 544 patients with female breast cancer aged 50–69 years diagnosed during 1996–2003. All patients resided in Hordaland County (around 10% of the Norwegian population) at diagnosis, and the recruitment was part of the population‐based Norwegian Breast Cancer Screening Program. Patients with distant metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis (stage IV) were not included in the analyses.

The patients received treatment in a single institution according to standard national guidelines at the time. Follow‐up information was provided by the Norwegian Cause of Death Registry, and the last date of follow‐up was 30 June 2017. Outcome data included survival status, survival time, and cause of death. During the follow‐up period, 93 patients (17%) died from breast carcinoma, and 125 (23%) died from other causes. The median follow‐up of survivors was 18.0 years (range, 13.8–21.3 years). The 5‐year breast cancer‐specific mortality was 6.3% (34/544).

Clinicopathologic characteristics

As previously reported, information on age at diagnosis, largest tumor diameter, histologic type, histologic grade, lymph node status, an estrogen receptors (ER) and progesterone receptors (PR) was obtained from routine histopathology reports at the Department of Pathology, Haukeland University Hospital, Bergen, Norway, and Erb‐B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (HER2) status and tumor cell proliferation by Ki67 were included from a previous study by our group [10]. In brief, according to national guidelines during the period when patients had their primary diagnosis of breast cancer, ER and PR expression were considered positive when ≥10% of tumor nuclei stained positive. The established scoring system for the DAKO Hercep Test was used. HER2 silver in situ hybridisation (SISH) was performed on IHC 2+ cases (Ventana INFORM HER2 DNA probe staining). The 2+ cases were considered HER2 positive if the HER2/Chr17 ratio by SISH was equal to or greater than 2.0.

Histologic type and grade were evaluated by experienced breast pathologists, and assessed according to WHO criteria (histologic type) and the Nottingham criteria (histologic grade; [11]). Immunohistochemistry was applied for determining the molecular subtypes based on the St. Gallen 2013 definition [12]. Markers of microvessel proliferation, proliferative microvessel density (pMVD), and vascular proliferation index (VPI) were available from previous studies from our group and were included for comparison [10, 13].

DNA extraction

Of the 544 cases selected, 47 had too low tissue content for DNA extraction, or the FFPE block was missing. Thus, DNA extraction was performed on nontumor FFPE lymphoid tissues in 497 cases. For this, we selected lymph nodes with no morphologic sign of tumor cells. In lymph node‐positive patients, lymph nodes that appeared unaffected by tumor cells were selected. Of these, the DNA was degraded in 39 samples, making PCR unsuccessful, leaving 458 cases (84%) available for SNP analyses.

SNP selection

The human VEGFA gene is located on chromosome 6p21 between the positions 43770209 and 43786487. For SNP selection, we incorporated an additional 5 kb of upstream and 2 kb of downstream sequences to include the promoter and potential regulatory regions. In total, we found 7,268 SNPs, and from these, 1,479 were SNPs with known minor allele frequency (MAF) [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/variation/view (gene ID: 7422)]; (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/variation/view/) (supplementary material, Table S1). To narrow down the pool of SNPs for this study, we employed a systematic and targeted selection process based on established criteria: first, we considered previous literature to identify SNPs that have been implicated in relevant biological processes or disease associations to ensure that the selected SNPs show potential biological relevance. We then checked the genotype frequency of SNPs in the general European (EUR) population subsets from the 1,000 Genomes Project Phase3 from Ensembl (http://www.ensembl.org/Homo_sapiens/Variation) to prioritize SNPs that were both common and potentially relevant within the population of interest, thereby also enhancing the study's applicability and generalisability. Finally, using SNAP software (http://www.broadinstitute.org), we assessed the linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns among the identified SNPs. SNPs in high LD with each other tend to co‐segregate and may represent redundant information. Thus, by considering LD, we ensured the selection of a diverse set of SNPs that capture a broader spectrum of genetic variation within the region of interest. By integration of these selection criteria, we identified a subset of eight SNPs for screening and further analyses. These SNPs are likely to be biologically relevant, representative of common genetic variation in the population, and exhibit minimal redundancy due to LD.

All the eight candidate SNPs selected (rs3023035, rs3025010, rs3025039, rs699947, rs833070, rs833068, rs2010963 and rs25648) were in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE); MAF was higher than 5% in all cases. Information about the SNPs studied is provided in supplementary material, Table S2. Five of these variants are tag SNPs, meaning they are in genomic regions with high LD, and are part of haplotypes. These tag SNPs are rs3023035, rs3025010, rs833070, rs833068, and rs25648.

SNP genotyping

VEGFA SNPs were genotyped using TaqMan technology (Applied Biosystems, Wilmington, DE, USA). The PCR was carried out according to the manufacturer's instructions. After PCR, the genotype of each sample was automatically determined by measuring allele‐specific final fluorescence in the ABI Prism 7500HT Detection System, using the SDS 2.1 software for allele discrimination (Applied Biosystems).

Analysis of functional roles

All variants were investigated using HaploRegv4.1 (http://www.broadinstitute.org), RegulomeDB (https://regulomedb.org/), and GTExPortal (https://gtexportal.org). HaploReg [14] was used for exploring the impact of noncoding SNPs on clinical phenotypes and normal variation. HaploReg allows the visualization of the influence of the SNPs on chromatin structure, histone modifications, sequence conservation, transcription factor binding site changes, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) hits, and DNAse activity points. The RegulomeDB database [15] integrates regulatory information from ENCODE and other data sources and provides information based on computational and experimental data, and it was used to identify and compare potential regulatory variants. GTExPortal was used to analyze how genetic variation affects normal gene expression and how this relationship correlates with the development of disease and specifically to identify eQTL.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was assessed at the two‐sided 5% level. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Pearson's χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to compare continuous variables across groups. For genetic variation assessment within the population, HWE was calculated. SNPStats [16] was used for haplotype calculations and LD analysis.

For survival analyses, the endpoint was breast cancer‐specific survival, defined as the time in months from the date of diagnosis to the date of death of breast cancer. Univariate survival analysis (Kaplan–Meier method) was performed using the log‐rank test to compare differences in survival time between categories. Patients who died of other causes or who were alive at the last follow‐up were censored in the analyses.

VEGFA SNPs and associations with clinicopathologic variables

First, we examined how the VEGFA SNPs were associated with clinicopathologic variables like tumor diameter, histologic grade, lymph node status, and ER, PR, and HER2 status. When analyzing the whole cohort, our data showed trends of associations between the rs699947CA genotype and larger tumor diameter and ER‐negative status (p = 0.091 and 0.060, respectively), and a significant association with PR‐negative tumors (p = 0.03; supplementary material, Table S3). The rs2010963GC genotype was associated with PR‐negative tumors (p = 0.049), as the rs833070CT and rs25648CT genotypes also tended to be (both p = 0.09; supplementary material, Table S3). The rs3025039CC and rs699947CA genotypes were associated with larger tumor diameter, with a trend of significance (p = 0.063 and p = 0.09, respectively; supplementary material, Table S3). None of the other VEGFA SNPs, nor any of the haplotypes, were associated with clinicopathologic variables or the molecular subtypes when analyzing data from the whole cohort.

In subgroup analyses, for the ER‐positive subset, no statistically significant results were demonstrated when analyzing associations between the eight SNPs and the clinicopathologic variables tumor diameter, histologic grade, and lymph node status (supplementary material, Table S4). For the PR‐positive subset, the rs302539CT genotype was associated with a trend of significance to ER‐positive tumors (p = 0.10; supplementary material, Table S5). None of the other associations with clinicopathologic variables were statistically significant. For the Luminal A subset, no significant associations between the examined SNPs and clinicopathologic variables were found (supplementary material, Table S6). In the Luminal B/HER2 negative subset, the rs3025029CC and rs3023035CC genotypes were related to larger tumor diameter (≥20 mm), with a trend of significance (p = 0.05 and 0.099; supplementary material, Table S7). In the Luminal B/HER2 positive subset, the rs25648CC genotype was associated with a trend of significance to the smaller tumors (diameter < 20 mm; p = 0. 09), and the rs3025010CC genotype to lymph node metastases (p = 0.08; supplementary material, Table S8). In the triple‐negative subset, the rs3023035CT genotype showed a trend of association with lymph node metastases (p = 0.06; supplementary material, Table S9).

VEGFA SNPs and tissue vascular biomarkers

When analyzing the whole cohort, across subtypes, three of the SNP candidates showed some associations with vascular metrics. For the rs833068GA genotype, increased vascular proliferation (by pMVD and VPI) was observed (p = 0.02 for both, Figure 1A,B; supplementary material, Table S3). The rs25648CC genotype was associated with higher vascular proliferation by pMVD (p = 0.044), and with VPI (trend of significance, p = 0.10; supplementary material, Figure S1A,B and Table S3). A trend of association was also seen between the rs2010963GC genotype and pMVD and VPI (both p = 0.09; supplementary material, Figure S1C,D and Table S3). Similar trends were seen in the ER‐positive subset for the associations between the rs25648CC genotype and pMVD and VPI (p = 0.082 and p = 0.064, respectively; supplementary material, Figure S1E,F and Table S4). Further, in the ER‐positive subset, the rs833068GA genotype showed a trend to higher vascular proliferation metrics (pMVD and VPI, both p = 0.06; supplementary material, Figure S1G,H and Table S4).

Subgroup analyses within the molecular subtypes demonstrated higher pMVD and VPI in cases of the rs833086GA genotype in patients with tumors of the Luminal B/HER2 negative subtype (Figure 1C,D and supplementary material, Table S7). In the triple‐negative subset, the rs833070CT genotype was associated with pMVD (p = 0.03) and with a trend of significance with VPI (p = 0.09; supplementary material, Table S9). No significant associations were shown between the SNPs and vascular metrics in the Luminal A or Luminal B/HER2 positive subsets supplementary material, Tables S6 and S8). In the PR‐positive subset, the rs833068GA genotype was associated with higher pMVD and VPI (trend of significance, p = 0.09 and 0.1; supplementary material, Table S5).

VEGFA SNPs and patient survival

We then examined the associations between all SNPs and clinical outcomes (disease‐specific survival). When analyzing the complete cohort, the rs833068GA genotype was associated with reduced survival when compared with the rs833068GG genotype (Figure 2A and supplementary material, Table S3). The rs3025039CC genotype was associated with shorter disease‐specific survival when compared to the merged cases with the CT/TT genotypes (all cases; trend of significance, p = 0.056; supplementary material, Figure S2A). In the ER‐negative subset, the rs833068GA genotype was associated with reduced survival when compared with the GG genotype (p = 0.035; Figure 2B; see supplementary material, Figure S3 for all three genotypes included in the analysis). In the subset of patients with ER‐positive tumors, the rs25648CC genotype was associated with reduced survival when compared with merged cases of the CT/TT genotypes (trend of significance, p = 0.071; supplementary material, Figure S2B).

When investigating the associations between the SNPs and survival in patient subsets based on the tumor molecular subtypes, we demonstrated a trend of association between the rs833068GA genotype and reduced survival among the Luminal A cases (p = 0.08; Figure 2C), as was seen also for the rs25648CC genotype in this subset (p = 0.08; supplementary material, Table S6). In the Luminal B/HER2‐positive and ‐negative subsets, none of the SNPs were significantly associated with survival supplementary material, Tables S7 and S8). Also in the triple‐negative subset, the rs833068GA genotype tended to be associated with reduced survival (p = 0.1; Figure 2D). The findings from the present study provide no evidence that rs3025010, rs833070, and rs699947 VEGFA polymorphisms are associated with breast cancer‐specific survival either in all cases (supplementary material, Table S3), or in any of the subsets analyzed (supplementary material, Tables [Link], [Link]).

Functional analysis of the VEGFA SNPs

The GTExportal includes different species, underscoring their evolutionary importance. In terms of breast cancer relevance, rs833068 and rs833070 are situated within potential enhancer regions in breast variant human mammary epithelial cells (vHMEC), whereas rs699947 and rs3025010 reside in potential enhancer regions in primary breast myoepithelial cells. Furthermore, all SNPs except rs3025035 are located within DNAse I hypersensitivity sites in various cell lines, indicating their potential to influence protein binding. Notably, rs833070, rs2010963, rs25648, and rs833068 exhibit ‘hot spot’ in vHMEC, suggesting critical regulatory roles in mammary epithelial cells [17, 18].

Discussion

VEGF, a pivotal mediator of angiogenesis, plays a crucial role in the progression and metastasis of cancer [2, 17, 18, 19]. Notably, SNPs within the VEGFA gene have emerged as influential factors in cancer progression, exerting multifaceted effects on tumor development and therapeutic responses [20]. These genetic variations modulate VEGFA expression levels, angiogenesis processes, and the tumor microenvironment, influencing cancer progression and patient outcomes. Specifically, distinct SNPs in the VEGFA gene, such as rs699947 and rs2010963, have been associated with altered VEGF expression levels and increased susceptibility to breast, colorectal, prostate, and lung cancer [21, 22, 23, 24]. Also, in the Chinese Han population, the rs2010963 G allele has been associated with a lower risk of developing breast cancer [22].

Based on the established importance of angiogenesis in cancer progression [2, 19], we here asked whether selected VEGFA SNPs were related to tissue‐based vascular metrics, clinicopathologic phenotypes, and patient prognosis in breast cancer. Apart from the fact that such SNPs could potentially influence VEGFA expression and key processes like angiogenesis, such genomic variations could also interact with other proteins known to influence VEGFA expression and other cellular functions.

The presence of the SNP rs833068GA genotype was associated with both increased angiogenesis by vascular proliferation, as well as reduced patient survival in several subsets of breast cancer. This SNP has not been previously studied in breast cancer and has, to our knowledge, not been examined in relation to angiogenesis markers in any type of cancer. Only three previous studies have evaluated VEGFA rs833068 in relation to cancer. In a study of patients with gastric or biliary tract cancer who underwent anti‐angiogenesis therapy by sunitinib, an inhibitor of VEGFA receptor, rs833068GG genotype was associated with more aggressive disease and lack of treatment response pointing to a potential functional relevance [25]. In a study of ovarian cancer, the rs833068GA genotype was linked to worse survival in one of the cohorts studied [26]. Finally, rs833068 was examined as a risk factor for cervical cancer, although no association was found [27].

Regarding angiogenesis, there is no previous information on the relationship between VEGFA rs833068GA genotype and vascular metrics. Thus, our study of breast cancer is the first to demonstrate an association between the VEGFA rs833068GA genotype and increased angiogenesis as well as reduced patient survival. The value of this genetic marker as a breast cancer prognosticator, or whether it could even represent a predictive marker for therapy response, remains to be validated in independent cohorts. Unfortunately, we do not have data on VEGFA protein expression available for this cohort. From the literature, it is known that there is some uncertainty as to how well VEGFA protein expression (by IHC) reflects the biological activity of VEGFA [28]. Despite not demonstrating that any of these SNPs promote effects directly via VEGFA protein expression, we consider it important that we have demonstrated associations between the rs833068 genotypes and activated angiogenesis (by vascular proliferation). Thus, we demonstrate the SNP as a marker for the biological activity VEGFA is expected to exert in the tumors.

VEGFA rs833068 has been described to bind different proteins in various cells lines (ENCODE project) and is located within a binding motif for CTCF, RAD21, and SP1, proteins known to influence VEGFA expression as well as having many other cellular effects [29, 30, 31, 32]. RAD21 expression has been associated with poor prognosis and resistance to chemotherapy in high‐grade luminal, basal‐like, and HER2‐positive breast cancers [31]. Notably, VEGFA rs833068 is linked to cis‐eQTL of both VEGFA and MAD2L1BP, and in both cases, the expression is higher when the A allele is present [33, 34].

Lack of more extensive clinical information, such as recurrences, metastases, treatment schedules, and responses to therapy, is a limitation to this study.

In summary, our findings support the possibility that the VEGFA rs833068 SNP has potential importance in breast cancer and relates to angiogenesis and patient survival. These findings would need to be validated by independent cohorts.
