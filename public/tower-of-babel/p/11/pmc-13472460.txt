Analysis of stress distribution around conventional and basal dental implants in the posterior mandible: A finite element study
Authors: Gayathri N. Meikandan, D. Jayachandran, G. Jayaprakash, R. Saranyan, Priya Kesavan, V. M. Deepa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Dental implants have become a widely accepted solution for the replacement of missing teeth, offering functional and esthetic benefits. The long-term success of dental implants largely depends on the distribution of stresses in the surrounding bone, as excessive stress can lead to bone resorption and implant failure. Finite element analysis (FEA) is a powerful tool that allows for the simulation of mechanical behavior under functional loading, providing insights into stress patterns and implant performance in various anatomical and prosthetic configurations.Aim:The aim of the study was to analyze stress distribution around conventional and basal dental implants placed in the posterior region of the mandible using FEA.Materials and Methods:The finite element model simulated a partially edentulous posterior mandibular segment where the mandibular second premolar and first molar were missing. Two dental implants were placed in the posterior mandible to simulate clinical rehabilitation of this region. Three-dimensional models were developed using cone beam computed tomography scans, and FEA was performed with ANSYS Workbench 18.1. Two models were analyzed: Model 1: conventional implant (4.1 mm × 8 mm) in 10-mm residual bone height and Model 2: basal implant (3.5 mm × 16 mm) in 8-mm residual bone height. Stress distribution was assessed under masticatory loading conditions to evaluate implant stability and stress accumulation in surrounding bone structures.Results:The study was designed as a comparative FEA evaluating stress distribution between conventional and basal implant systems placed in the posterior mandible under identical loading conditions. Model 2 (basal implant) exhibited greater deflection (13 μm), which may compromise implant stability. Model 1 (conventional implant) demonstrated higher stress accumulation in the cortical bone (34.71 MPa).Conclusion:The present FEA demonstrates that although basal implants showed slightly lower crestal bone stress, the increased deformation observed may influence long-term biomechanical stability under functional loading conditions. Hence, Model 1 (conventional implant) is the preferred choice for clinical applications, offering better stability and longevity compared to Model 2.

INTRODUCTION

Dental implants serve as a well-established treatment for patients suffering from edentulism, significantly enhancing oral rehabilitation.[1] Their success is primarily influenced by the mechanical behavior of stress distribution within the surrounding bone.[2] Uneven stress distribution may cause complications such as bone resorption, implant loosening, or failure.[3] The posterior mandible presents distinct biomechanical challenges due to its high occlusal forces and variable bone densities.[4] Dental implants are exposed to multiple forces, including axial and lateral loads, which affect their stability and longevity.[5] Conventional implants, widely utilized in modern dentistry, are inserted into the alveolar bone and rely on osseointegration for stability.[6] Typically composed of titanium, these implants necessitate adequate bone volume for placement and require a healing period of 3–6 months before the final prosthesis placement.[7] They come in various shapes, such as cylindrical, screw-type, and blade-type, and offer advantages such as high success rates in patients with optimal bone density, long-term durability, and compatibility with multiple prosthetic solutions, including single crowns, bridges, and full-mouth restorations.[8] The effectiveness of traditional implants is based on osseointegration, which is defined as a direct structural and functional bond between the living bone and the surface of the implant.[9] This phenomenon is necessary for long-term therapeutic success and for implant stability.[10] The implant–bone interface is highly dynamic, involving complex interactions between biomaterial properties, biocompatibility, and mechanical forces.[11] Osseointegration consists of an initial mechanical interlocking of the implant with alveolar bone, followed by biological fixation through continuous bone remodeling and apposition at the implant surface.[12] Multiple factors, including implant surface properties, host bone quality, and mechanical loading, influence this process.[13] Basal implants, also known as cortical implants, differ from conventional implants in that they engage the highly dense cortical bone rather than the softer cancellous bone.[14] These implants are particularly beneficial for patients with severe bone atrophy where conventional implants are not viable. Unlike traditional implants, basal implants allow for immediate loading, require minimal bone volume, and do not depend on osseointegration. By avoiding the need for bone grafting procedures, they present a viable alternative for patients with compromised bone height. Both conventional and basal implants have specific clinical indications and limitations. While multiple studies have investigated various implant designs, there is limited comparative research focusing on stress distribution between conventional and basal implants in atrophic posterior mandibles. This study seeks to fill this gap by analyzing stress patterns using finite element analysis (FEA), thereby aiding clinicians in selecting the most suitable implant system for each clinical scenario. The purpose of this study was to evaluate and compare the stress distribution surrounding conventional and basal implants in the posterior mandible using FEA. The objectives are to construct three-dimensional (3D) models of the posterior mandible based on cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans, simulate masticatory forces, and analyze stress distribution around the implants.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The present FEA study was designed to simulate a partially edentulous posterior mandible in which the mandibular second premolar and first molar were missing and rehabilitated using a two-implant-supported fixed partial denture. A 3D computational approach was employed to evaluate stress distribution under functional loading conditions. Two 3D models were developed [Figures 1 and 2], based on CBCT scans of the posterior mandible, which were imported as DICOM files into Mimics software for segmentation and reconstruction. The models incorporated detailed representations of cortical and cancellous bone simulating D3 bone quality, with cortical bone thickness standardized at 2 mm and a minimum bone width of 6.8 mm maintained across both models. Two implant configurations were evaluated: Model 1 (control) consisted of a conventional root-form implant (4.1 mm × 8 mm) placed in 10 mm of residual bone height, whereas Model 2 comprised a basal implant (3.5 mm × 16 mm) positioned in 8 mm of residual bone height engaging the basal cortical bone. A fixed partial denture superstructure was designed over the implants to simulate clinical loading conditions. Loading conditions were applied based on the study by Meijer et al.,[15] wherein a vertical load of 35 N was applied along the long axis of the implant in an occlusogingival direction, a horizontal load of 10 N was applied in the labiolingual direction at 0°, and an oblique load of 70 N was applied at an angle of 120° to the occlusal plane. All materials, including cortical bone, cancellous bone, implants, and prosthetic components, were assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic, and their mechanical properties were derived from previously published studies [Tables 1 and 2].[16171819202122] The 3D models were then exported to ANSYS Workbench 18.1, where a convergence-tested finite element mesh using tetrahedral elements was generated, consisting of approximately 378,758–385,062 elements and 523,756–527,889 nodes. The inferior border of the mandible was constrained in all directions to simulate physiological boundary conditions. Stress analysis was performed to evaluate the distribution of maximum von Mises stress and principal stresses within cortical bone, cancellous bone, implant structures, and the prosthetic framework, and the results were interpreted using color-coded stress distribution maps for comparative assessment between the two models.

RESULTS

The maximum von Mises and principal stress values obtained for both models are summarized in Table 3, and the corresponding stress distribution patterns are illustrated in Figure 3. In both models, the highest stress concentrations were consistently observed in the crestal cortical bone region, particularly at the implant–bone interface, indicating this area as the primary load-bearing zone under functional loading conditions. In Model 1 (conventional implant), the peak principal stress in the cortical bone was 34.71 MPa, localized mainly at the implant–bone interface near the second premolar region, while a lower stress value of 16.55 MPa was observed on the outer bone surface in the lingual aspect of the first molar region, suggesting directional dissipation of stresses. The von Mises stress values in the implant, abutment, and screw remained well within their respective material yield limits, indicating satisfactory mechanical performance. However, the porcelain crown exhibited the highest stress concentration, reaching approximately 140 MPa, which exceeds the reported failure threshold of porcelain (45–60 MPa) and therefore warrants critical evaluation from a prosthetic standpoint. No relative displacement was observed at the implant–bone interface, confirming effective load transfer and simulated osseointegration. The total deformation in Model 1 was approximately 8.6 μm, primarily concentrated in the crown, whereas the bone deformation ranged from 3 to 4 μm, which is within acceptable physiological limits. In Model 2 (basal implant), the maximum principal stress in the cortical bone was 33.87 MPa, also concentrated at the implant–bone interface near the second premolar region, demonstrating a comparable stress distribution pattern to Model 1. However, a relatively higher stress value of 19.74 MPa was recorded on the outer surface of the bone in the buccal aspect of the first molar region, indicating a variation in stress transmission characteristics. The von Mises stress within the implant was 39.85 MPa, remaining well below the material’s yield strength, thereby suggesting structural safety. Similar to Model 1, the porcelain crown exhibited high stress levels of approximately 134 MPa, exceeding the material’s failure range (45–60 MPa), thus highlighting it as the most vulnerable component. However, Model 2 demonstrated comparatively greater deformation, with a maximum deflection of approximately 13 μm, predominantly occurring in the crown. The bone deformation ranged from 6 to 7 μm, which is higher than that observed in Model 1 and may indicate increased biomechanical strain on peri-implant bone. Overall, while both models showed similar stress concentration patterns at the crestal bone, Model 2 exhibited increased deformation despite slightly lower peak stress values, suggesting a potential trade-off between stress distribution and structural displacement under functional loading conditions.

DISCUSSION

The present study provides a comparative biomechanical evaluation of stress distribution in the posterior mandible using conventional and basal implant designs under simulated functional loading. In both models, stress concentrations were predominantly localized at the crestal cortical bone surrounding the implant neck, reaffirming this region as the critical zone for load transfer and a determinant of implant success. Model 1 demonstrated a higher peak principal stress (34.71 MPa) at the implant–bone interface in the premolar region, along with stress concentration on the lingual surface of the first molar (16.55 MPa). These findings are consistent with earlier reports by Meijer et al., 1993, and Geng et al., 2001, which emphasize the crestal bone as the primary site of stress accumulation,[2324] and with Hansson, 2003, who highlighted the influence of occlusal loading direction on cortical stress patterns.[25] Although Model 1 exhibited minimal deformation and absence of micromotion, indicating favorable primary stability, the relatively higher crestal stress may have implications for marginal bone loss under long-term functional loading. Similar observations were reported by Saini et al.[26] who, through 3D FEA of immediately placed and loaded conventional and customised 3D-printed dental implants, demonstrated that the highest stress concentrations occurred in the crestal cortical bone region adjacent to the implant neck, reinforcing the importance of peri-implant cortical bone in biomechanical load transfer. In contrast, Model 2 (basal implant) showed a slightly reduced peak principal stress (33.87 MPa) at the implant–bone interface, supporting the concept of improved stress distribution due to cortical bone engagement, as reported in previous studies.[27] However, the increased stress observed on the buccal surface of the first molar (19.74 MPa) suggests an altered load transmission pathway, possibly reflecting design-related differences. Importantly, Model 2 demonstrated greater overall deformation (13 μm) compared to Model 1, which may indicate a higher risk of biomechanical strain and peri-implant bone remodeling, in line with recent evidence linking micromovements to marginal bone loss by Chou et al., 2022. Recent findings by Monje et al., 2024, further support the role of cortical engagement in optimizing load distribution and reducing peri-implant bone loss,[28] which is partially corroborated by the present study, although the increased deformation seen with basal implants suggests a biomechanical trade-off that must be carefully considered in clinical decision-making. While basal implants offer a valuable alternative in atrophic ridges and may reduce the need for augmentation procedures, their use in high-load situations warrants caution. Both models maintained implant stresses within safe material limits; however, the consistently elevated stress in the porcelain crowns (134–140 MPa) far exceeded the reported fracture threshold (45–60 MPa), identifying the prosthetic component as a potential site of failure, as also supported by previous literature on ceramic restorations in posterior load-bearing regions.[29] Despite the strengths of FEA in providing detailed insights, the study is limited by assumptions of homogeneous bone properties, static loading conditions, and absence of soft tissue modeling. Therefore, future studies incorporating dynamic loading, variable bone densities, and long-term clinical validation are essential to enhance the clinical applicability of these findings.

CONCLUSION

The present FEA demonstrates that maximum stress concentration is localized at the cortical bone–implant interface, confirming its critical role in load transfer. Basal implants exhibited relatively improved stress distribution but were associated with increased deformation, which may compromise long-term biomechanical stability. The conventional implant model showed lower deformation and more predictable mechanical behavior, supporting its clinical reliability in D2 bone conditions. Significantly elevated stress values in porcelain crowns, exceeding fracture thresholds, indicate a potential risk of prosthetic failure under functional loading. Further research focusing on implant design optimization, advanced restorative materials, and long-term in vivo validation is essential to improve clinical outcomes.
