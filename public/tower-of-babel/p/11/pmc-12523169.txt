Determinants of mpox vaccination uptake among MSM during the 2022 outbreak: a single-centre retrospective study at Lyon University Hospital, France
Authors: Yanis Merad, Matthieu Godinot, Dulce Alfaiate, Agathe Becker, Florence Ader, Laurent Cotte, Anne Conrad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuring the 2022 mpox outbreak, prophylactic vaccination was offered to at risk individuals, including men who have sex with men (MSM) with multiple sexual partners. We aimed to evaluate mpox vaccine first dose uptake in MSM and its determinants.MethodsWe conducted a single-centre, retrospective study using data from MSM presenting at the STI and HIV clinics of Lyon University Hospital between 1 January 2022 and 28 February 2023. First dose uptake of mpox vaccination (modified vaccinia Ankara-Bavarian Nordic) was evaluated across three groups of MSM: people living with HIV (PWH), HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) users and non-PrEP users (NPU). Uptake rates were compared with Chi2 analysis and multivariable hazard ratios (HR) were calculated using a Cox model.ResultsA total of 9,256 MSM (PWH, n = 1,946; PrEP, n = 2,528; NPU, n = 4,782) were included. By February 2023, the first dose uptake rate was 49.6% (4,590/9,256). PrEP users were more likely to get vaccinated (72.2%) than PWH (32.7%, p < 0.0001) and NPU (44.5%, p < 0.0001). Half of PrEP users had been vaccinated by day 67 of the vaccination campaign. In multivariable analysis, an age ≤ 25 yo (HR, 0.35 [95% confidence interval (CI), 0.32–0.38]), as well as an age > 60 yo (HR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.72–0.95]) and HIV infection (HR, 0.46 [95% CI, 0.41–0.50]) were associated with a lower first dose uptake, as opposed to PrEP use (HR, 1.69 [95% CI, 1.59–1.81]) and chemsex (HR, 1.42 [95% CI, 1.30–1.54]).ConclusionsIn this large cohort of MSM, first dose uptake was high, especially in PrEP users. Among MSM at risk for mpox, PWH and younger individuals should be priority targets for vaccine promotion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24728-3.

Background

Between May 2022 and May 2023, almost 90,000 mpox infections were reported worldwide, including a large proportion in the Americas and Europe, where the disease was not historically endemic. During this large outbreak, the first case in France was reported in mid-May 2022. Since then, more than 5,000 cases have been reported in France [1]. This sexually transmitted infection (STI) caused by the Monkeypox virus (MPXV) predominantly circulated among men who have sex with men (MSM) with multiple sexual partners (> 95% of cases in France) [2, 3]. Prophylactic vaccination campaigns using the modified vaccinia virus Ankara-Bavarian Nordic vaccine (MVA-BN; Imvanex®, Jynneos®) debuted around July 2022 in several countries, including in France on 07/07/2022 [4]. They targeted individuals at high-risk of mpox, including highly exposed MSM, i.e. reporting more than one sexual partner.

Vaccine coverage studies found first dose uptake rates in at-risk individuals between 30 and 45% by the first trimester of 2023 [5–7]. However, major disparities in vaccine coverage have been highlighted, beyond inequal access to mpox vaccines [7, 8]. Individual factors associated with mpox vaccine uptake include sexual orientation and gender identity, age, education and income. Different studies suggested that the use of HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and a mpox risk-associated sex behavior were positively associated with vaccine uptake [9, 10]. Studies addressing the interaction between these factors are scarce and context-dependent: for instance, HIV infection has been shown to be either positively or negatively associated with mpox vaccine uptake across different settings [11, 12].

This study aims to describe the uptake of a first dose of prophylactic, pre-exposure mpox vaccine in a large cohort of MSM during the recent outbreak and the determinants of vaccination uptake.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective observational study at the University Hospital STI clinics (n = 2) and HIV clinic (n = 1) in Lyon, France, the largest regional mpox vaccination site in the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region. The target population comprised adult MSM (≥ 18 years-old [yo]) living around Lyon, France, at the time of the vaccination campaign. Individuals who visited these clinics for diverse reasons including HIV follow-up, PrEP uptake and follow-up, STI testing and/or treatment, etc. were considered for inclusion. In an effort to avoid overestimation of the uptake rate, we sought to capture MSM who presented to the clinics shortly before the start of the vaccination campaign (7/07/2022) but failed to present afterwards. Thus, all MSM presenting to the STI/HIV clinics as soon as January 2022 until February 2023 were screened for inclusion. MSM who received post-exposure mpox vaccination were excluded. Given the incubation time for mpox, MSM who declared mpox disease before 14 July 2022 were considered contaminated before the start of the vaccination campaign and were therefore excluded. Age, sex assigned-at-birth, sexual orientation, HIV status, PrEP use (defined as having ≥ 1 PrEP initiation or follow-up visit during study period), practice of chemsex (defined as self-reported use of psychoactive substances during sexual activity), mpox infection and vaccination data were retrospectively and anonymously retrieved from an electronic medical records database. All study participants gave informed consent for the use of their data. Statistics of weekly incident cases of mpox in the Auvergne-Rhône Alpes region during the study period were provided by the French national public health agency (Santé Publique France). This study was authorized by the local ethical review board (Scientific and Ethical Committee of Hospices Civils de Lyon) and registered at the French data protection authority (Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés, n°23-5176).

Patients were categorized into three mutually-exclusive sexual health profiles: people living with HIV (PWH), HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) users and other individuals (NPU, non-PrEP users). The main outcome was first mpox vaccine dose uptake across sexual health profiles at the date of data-pooling (28/02/2023). Secondary outcomes included the time to vaccination across sexual health profiles, a subgroup multivariable analysis of time to vaccination among PWH and a subgroup multivariable analysis of time to vaccination among participants reporting chemsex. These subgroup analyses were conducted to better discriminate the role of STI exposure (assessed through the proxy of chemsex) in the study population uptake rate. Univariable relative risks and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated and comparisons were performed with Chi-square test. Kaplan-Meier curves of the cumulative incidence rates of vaccination over time were plotted. Participants who developed mpox before vaccination were censored at the time of diagnosis confirmation. Unvaccinated and uninfected participants were censored at the date of data-pooling. The kinetics of vaccine uptake and reported cases of mpox in the region were plotted together to assess the coverage rate when incidence started to decrease. Univariable hazard ratios (HR) and their 95%CI were calculated in survival analysis and a multivariable Cox analysis including age and chemsex as covariates was performed. Age was handled as a categorical variable in multivariable analyses, due to non-linear correlation with the main outcome (Supplementary Figure S1). Analyses were based on two-sided p-values, with statistical significance defined by p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.0) and RStudio (v2023.06.0).

Results

From 9,319 adult MSM considered for inclusion, 9,256 individuals (comprising 1,946 PWH, 2,528 PrEP users and 4,782 NPU) were included in the primary analysis (Supplementary Figure S2). Median age in each group was 51 yo (interquartile range [IQR], 39–59), 34 yo (27–43) and 29 yo (23–40), respectively. Chemsex was reported by 13.6% of participants (425 PWH [21.8%], 685 PrEP users [27.1%] and 133 NPU [2.8%]). The total number of visits during the follow-up period was 13,850 and the median number of visits per participant was 3 (IQR 2–5). The majority (84%) of participants visited a clinic at least once after the start of the vaccination campaign.

By the end of follow-up, 4,590 out of 9,256 (49.6%) MSM had received at least one dose of MVA-BN. Compared to NPU (44.5%), first dose uptake was significantly higher among PrEP users (72.2%) and significantly lower among PWH (32.7%) (Table 1). Among MSM reporting chemsex, 790 out of 1,243 (63.6%) individuals received vaccination. Cumulative incidence analysis of first dose uptake over time (Fig. 1A) showed consistent results. Of note, 50% of PrEP users had been vaccinated by day 67 of the vaccination campaign.

In multivariable Cox analysis, an age ≤ 25 yo (HR 0.35), as well as an age > 60 yo (HR 0.83) and HIV infection (HR 0.46) were associated with a lower first dose uptake. Conversely, PrEP use (HR 1.69) and chemsex (HR 1.42) were associated with a higher first dose uptake rate (Table 2). Subgroup multivariable cumulative incidence analysis in PWH showed a significant association between first dose uptake and reported chemsex (Fig. 1B, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis in participants reporting chemsex showed a significantly higher cumulative incidence of vaccination in PrEP users compared to PWH and NPU (Fig. 1C, p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this large cohort of MSM presenting at the STI and HIV clinics from Lyon’s University Hospital during the 2022–2023 mpox vaccination campaign, the first dose uptake rate was nearly 50% across all groups. A markedly higher first dose uptake rate was found among PrEP users (72%), which is consistent with previous data [9]. This could be explained by two main hypotheses: first, PrEP users, who are more likely to have multiple sex partners, engage in group sex events or practice chemsex, are more exposed to mpox [2]. In addition, PrEP use reflects a higher baseline level of STI exposure awareness. PrEP users were also more likely to be targeted and reached by community-led initiatives to promote vaccination. For these reasons, PrEP users were likely to have a higher risk awareness for mpox, which has been suggested to be a key driver for first dose uptake [13]. Of note, a similar reasoning applies to individuals reporting chemsex (who are overrepresented among PrEP users) and could explain their higher vaccine uptake rate. Second, PrEP users have a closer follow-up and are more actively engaged in STI prevention strategies. In addition, the restricted accessibility of vaccines at the beginning of the campaign may have caused indirect competition between individuals to get vaccination appointments. In this context, it is very likely that the higher frequency of follow-up visits resulted in a quicker offer of mpox vaccination for PrEP users and increased the gap with other MSMs.

PWH had lower mpox vaccination uptake than other MSM groups. They have previously been described as either more or less acceptant of mpox vaccination [11, 12]. PWH represent a diverse group in terms of sexual behaviour patterns. Importantly, not all MSM with HIV have multiple sexual partners, which means they were not all strictly part of the vaccination target as defined by French health authorities. However, due to overlap with sexual exposure criteria and a higher incidence of mpox and severe mpox in this population group, this vaccination was often sought out by MSM with HIV and/or frequently offered to them by physicians [14]. We hypothesized that the lower overall first-dose uptake of PWH could be at least partially explained by a lower STI exposure level. To better discriminate the roles of HIV infection and STI exposure in vaccine uptake, we used chemsex as a proxy for sexual exposure in subgroup analyses. Consistently with our hypothesis, first dose uptake was positively correlated with chemsex among PWH. However, among individuals reporting chemsex, first dose uptake in PWH, although comparable to NPU, was still lower than in PrEP users. This finding suggests that a lower overall sexual exposure is not the only driver of the lower uptake rate in MSM with HIV. This observation could be explained by (i) a lower risk perception of mpox in PWH, (ii) a less frequent and/or less STI-focused follow-up, (iii) an underestimation of STI exposure levels by treating HIV physicians resulting in an insufficient vaccination offer to PWH.

Across all groups, a younger age (≤ 25 yo) was independently associated with a lower first dose uptake. A higher risk of STI has been described in younger MSM using PrEP [15]. This observation has been linked to a lower risk awareness regarding exposure to sexually-transmitted pathogens, which could in turn be responsible for the lower vaccination uptake we observed in younger MSM. However, we cannot rule out other barriers to vaccination in younger MSM, for instance a higher fear of sexual orientation disclosure and stigma, which was mentioned as a barrier to mpox vaccination uptake by other authors [16].

Finally, regarding the impact of the vaccination campaign on outbreak control, our data of the kinetics of vaccination uptake and the evolution of the epidemic in the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region suggest that pre-exposure vaccination was not the main driver of outbreak control, as the incidence of mpox began to decrease before a significant proportion of people were immunized by a first vaccine dose. A similar pattern was observed in the Netherlands, suggesting that vaccination only played a small role in the initial outbreak control, although it probably helped to prevent secondary outbreaks [6]. Contrastingly, an American modelling study estimated a 79% decrease in cases thanks to vaccination [17]. Vaccination might therefore have had a different impact on the outbreak control in different geographic areas, depending on the dynamics of the epidemic and other prevention measures implemented.

This is the largest cohort study to date reporting the mpox vaccine first dose uptake rate and its determinants among MSM. The high number of participants is representative of several risk profiles. In addition, free access to STI healthcare for all individuals in France (including people in situation of undocumented immigration) allowed us to minimize the confounding bias related to healthcare accessibility. However, there are some limitations to our conclusions. First, as explained above, PWH in our study had heterogeneous levels of STI exposure and were therefore not all targeted by the prophylactic mpox vaccination campaign. The retrospective design of our study precluded collection of data suggestive of risk-associated sexual behaviour, such as recent STI occurrence or number of sexual partners, which is why we were only able to use chemsex as a proxy for high-level STI exposure. Second, although the majority of mpox vaccinations in our region were performed at our centre, some participants might have received mpox vaccination elsewhere, which could account for an underestimation of first dose uptake. Third, the inclusion period started 6 months before the beginning of the vaccination campaign to capture a study population as close as possible to the target of the vaccination campaign, but it may nevertheless be biased, as it does not include individuals who never presented at the clinic.

Conclusion

Although nearly half of MSM followed-up in our centre received at least one mpox vaccine dose, we highlight important disparities in mpox vaccination uptake, with significantly lower vaccination rates in PWH and younger MSM. As mpox keeps circulating worldwide, among MSM at high-risk of mpox because of multiple sexual partners, PWH and younger individuals should be priority targets of vaccine offer by physicians and awareness campaigns aiming at promoting the uptake of mpox vaccination.
