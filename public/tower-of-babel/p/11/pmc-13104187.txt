Clenched fist syndrome: unveiling a motor manifestation of schizophrenia—a case report
Authors: Komal Samir, Javeria Zaheer, Arun Subash, Tushar Subash, Shoaib Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchizophrenia, a debilitating psychiatric disorder affecting ~1% of the global population, is characterized by positive, negative, cognitive, and neurological symptoms. Motor abnormalities are an under-recognized component and, if overlooked, may result in permanent deformities. Clenched fist syndrome is a rare clinical entity, with etiologies ranging from congenital and traumatic causes to psychiatric disorders, often in the absence of identifiable organic pathology.Case presentationWe report the case of a 24-year-old South Asian male from Karachi, Pakistan, diagnosed with schizophrenia, who presented with bilateral persistent clenched fists for 6 years, which significantly worsened following the death of his mother. Comprehensive physical, neurological, and radiological evaluations revealed no underlying organic cause, supporting a psychogenic etiology. Despite multidisciplinary treatment, including antipsychotic therapy, physiotherapy, casting, and electroconvulsive therapy, no improvement in hand posture was achieved, likely owing to the prolonged delay in diagnosis and intervention.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of early recognition and timely management of motor manifestations such as clenched fist syndrome in psychiatric disorders, which are frequently overshadowed by prominent psychotic symptoms. Delayed diagnosis may lead to irreversible musculoskeletal changes, emphasizing the need for clinician awareness and further research into the pathophysiology and optimal management of clenched fist syndrome.

Background

Schizophrenia is a complex and debilitating psychiatric condition, affecting around 1% of people worldwide and ranking among the top 10 global disability causes [1]. It is characterized by positive psychotic symptoms such as delusions and disorganized behavior, and negative symptoms such as reduced emotional expression and avolition, as well as cognitive and neurological impairments [2]. Motor abnormalities, although increasingly recognized, are still frequently underdiagnosed and may result in long-term disability if not addressed promptly [3].

Here we present a case of schizophrenia presenting with a rare motor phenomenon—bilateral clenched fist syndrome (CFS), also referred to as psychoflexed hands. This condition is characterized by sustained involuntary flexion of the hand without any apparent organic cause [4]. Although the association between CFS and psychiatric disorders has been reported, its occurrence in schizophrenia remains sparsely documented. Because CFS may mimic orthopedic, neurological, or medication-induced conditions, it is frequently misdiagnosed or attributed to behavioral noncompliance, particularly in patients with severe mental illness. This diagnostic ambiguity contributes to delayed intervention and poorer functional outcomes. By presenting this case, we hope to underscore the interplay between mental health disorders and motor abnormalities, emphasizing the importance of holistic assessment and management in psychiatric practice. We also highlight the importance of prompt diagnosis and intervention for successful treatment of CFS, as delayed diagnosis can lead to permanent muscle contractures and reduced response to physiotherapy.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old single South Asian male patient, resident of Korangi, Karachi, was brought to the outpatient department of Dr. Abdul Qadeer Khan Institute of Behavioral Sciences by his father. The patient presented with progressive social withdrawal, poverty of speech, poor self-care, self-talking behavior, generalized body weakness, and bilateral clenched fists, all persisting for approximately 6 years.

His decline in functionality was noted by his father, who observed that the patient spent most of his time alone, engaging minimally with others. In addition, the patient’s school reported his unresponsive behavior, and his self-care deteriorated to the extent that he required assistance with basic tasks such as changing clothes and hygiene maintenance.

There was no known family history of psychiatric, neurological, or musculoskeletal disorders. The patient had no significant past medical or surgical history and had not received any formal psychiatric or orthopedic treatment prior to presentation. According to the family, the symptoms were initially attributed to emotional distress and grief following the death of his mother 3.5 years prior, which contributed to a significant delay in seeking psychiatric care.

On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable. On mental status examination, the patient appeared poorly groomed, avoided eye contact, and exhibited marked psychomotor retardation. His behavior was withdrawn and minimally responsive. Speech was scant, low in volume, and non-spontaneous, with increased latency. Affect was restricted and incongruent to context. Thought form could not be fully assessed owing to limited verbal output; however, behavioral observation and collateral history suggested ongoing psychotic processes, including self-talking and social withdrawal. This self-talking behavior likely reflected ongoing auditory hallucinations or internal preoccupation with psychotic content. Such active psychosis may contribute to psychogenic motor manifestations through mechanisms of psychological regression or conversion, where unresolved internal conflict or distress manifests somatically. The temporal worsening of hand clenching following emotional stressors further supports this psychogenic interplay. Finally, insight and judgment were also markedly impaired.

The patient’s unusual hand posture was noticed during the history-taking stage, and upon further questioning, the family described that the patient had this clenched hand posture for 6 years now and overall, the symptoms worsened since the demise of his mother. After 3 weeks of inpatient care, owing to the familiarity with the new environment and a response to the ongoing treatments, he was no more reluctant to examinations. Examination revealed a wasted thenar appearance and bilateral clenched fists with restricted extension movements of phalanges sparing the thumb, but no fractures or any visible swelling could be observed (Figs. 2 and 3). To rule out any organic causes, the patient’s work-up was sent, including bilateral X-ray of the hands, all of which were unremarkable. For further evaluation, examination under anesthesia was done, which showed bilateral palmar contracture. Orthopedics, the plastic surgery team, and occupational therapists assessed the patient’s hand flexion issues, revealing no abnormalities on X-ray. Multiple challenges complicated diagnosis and management, including poor patient cooperation due to active psychosis, limited insight into illness, cultural stigma surrounding psychiatric disorders, and initial misattribution of symptoms to grief. These factors collectively resulted in a 6-year delay in diagnosis, contributing to irreversible musculoskeletal changes and reduced therapeutic response.

Several alternative diagnoses were considered. Catatonia was deemed unlikely owing to the absence of classic features such as waxy flexibility, echopraxia, echolalia, posturing involving other body parts, or autonomic instability. Drug-induced dystonia or rigidity was excluded as the patient had no prior exposure to antipsychotic medications before symptom onset. Contractures secondary to neglect alone were considered insufficient to explain the sustained involuntary flexion pattern, pain on attempted extension, and worsening during examination. Neurological causes were ruled out through normal imaging, neurological examination, and examination under anesthesia.

After addressing all the abovementioned diagnostic challenges and considering the various differentials, a diagnosis of schizophrenia with predominant negative symptoms was established. The conclusion of a psychogenic origin was based on the absence of structural, neurological, or pharmacological causes; normal radiological findings; preserved passive range of motion early in the course; and symptom exacerbation during psychological stress and examination attempts. While formal psychometric testing was not feasible owing to poor cooperation, the cumulative clinical evidence supported a psychogenic motor disorder. The abnormal posturing of the hands was considered a potential manifestation of regression, a reversion to immature pattern of behavior. The patient was started on muscle relaxants and structured physiotherapy. Initial antipsychotic treatment included clozapine, risperidone, and olanzapine; however, clozapine was discontinued owing to tachycardia. The regimen was subsequently modified to amisulpride, olanzapine, aripiprazole, and fortnightly flupentixol injections. Owing to a poor response to the ongoing treatment, electroconvulsive therapy was administered in a total of 12 sessions over 2 months, leading to significant improvement in psychotic symptoms and daily functioning. The patient was continued on maintenance pharmacotherapy and occupational therapy. Despite psychiatric improvement, there was no significant change in hand posture, likely owing to long-standing contractures and muscle atrophy.

Discussion

Clenched fist syndrome (CFS) is a rare motor condition characterized by involuntary flexion of one or both hands, often associated with pain upon attempted extension [5]. This motor manifestation can arise from multiple causes, including congenital factors, traumatic injuries, arthritic changes, and, notably, psychiatric illnesses [6, 7]. CFS is a rare presentation and it has been documented in literature only a few times, associated with various underlying conditions; Simmons et al. explained a few cases of CFS occurring in patients with conversion disorder, a condition where psychological stressors manifest as physical symptoms without any underlying organic cause [8]. Similarly, Nissen et al. reported a case of a patient with chronic schizophrenia exhibiting the characteristic hand posture of CFS following a hip fracture. This further strengthens the link between CFS and schizophrenia [9, 10]. Finally, Meiwendi et al., in their case report, explained the early recognition of CFS and prompt intervention in an elderly female with schizophrenia, which showed a positive response in correction of CFS deformity with continuous physiotherapy after casting and psychiatric symptoms with antipsychotics [11].

In this case, delayed presentation resulted in established contractures and thenar muscle atrophy, limiting therapeutic success despite comprehensive multidisciplinary care. Early physiotherapy in cases of functional hand posturing is essential to maintain joint mobility and muscle length. Prolonged sustained flexion can lead to adaptive shortening of flexor tendons, periarticular fibrosis, and eventual fixed contractures. Early intervention could potentially have prevented the development of irreversible palmar contractures and thenar muscle atrophy observed in this patient [12]. This highlights the importance of early recognition of motor symptoms in psychiatric patients and challenges the tendency to dismiss such findings as behavioral or secondary phenomena.

Conclusion

This case report highlights the need for heightened clinical vigilance for CFS in patients with psychiatric disorders presenting with unusual motor symptoms, as prompt recognition and appropriate management measures are crucial for optimizing patient outcomes. Multidisciplinary team efforts between psychiatric and occupational therapy specialties are essential for patients’ assessment and tailored treatment strategies in each patient with such complex presentation. Early involvement of hand surgeons and rehabilitation specialists is recommended when persistent contractures are first identified, as timely mechanical correction alongside psychiatric management may improve long-term functional outcomes. In conclusion, CFS represents a unique clinical entity with diverse etiologies, including psychiatric associations such as schizophrenia. It is also evident that a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms and optimal management approaches of CFS can and should be achieved which would ultimately enhance patient care and outcomes.
