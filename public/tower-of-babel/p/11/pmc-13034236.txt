Effect of targeted therapies on pulmonary function in rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Stéphane Hilliquin, Enrico Vismara, Virginia Berlengiero, Jérôme Avouac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInterstitial lung disease (ILD) is a serious extra-articular manifestation of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) associated with increased morbidity and mortality. The pulmonary safety of biologic Disease-Modifying Anti-Rheumatic Drugs (bDMARDs) and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) in RA-associated ILD (RA-ILD) remains uncertain. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the effect of bDMARDs and tsDMARDs on ILD progression in RA, focusing on pulmonary function test (PFT) parameters—forced vital capacity (FVC) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO)—and, when available, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) outcomes.MethodsPubMed and Cochrane databases were searched up to 2025 for controlled trials and observational studies, including patients with RA fulfilling the 1987 ACR or 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria. Studies reporting longitudinal changes in PFTs or HRCT after bDMARD or tsDMARD treatment were included. Meta-analyses were conducted using the inverse variance method (RevMan V.5.4.1) for pooled estimates of FVC and DLCO.Results18 observational studies including 958 patients (mean age 65 years; 57% female) were analysed. The most frequent ILD patterns were usual interstitial pneumonia (48%) and nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (40%). Data were available for rituximab (six studies), abatacept (4), JAK inhibitors (4), tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors (1) and tocilizumab (1). Pooled analysis showed no significant overall change in FVC (mean difference −0.85%, 95% CI −2.40 to 0.71; p=0.29) or DLCO (+0.79, 95% CI −0.30 to 1.88; p=0.16).ConclusionbDMARDs and tsDMARDs appear to stabilise pulmonary function in RA-ILD, without significant differences between agents. Further prospective studies integrating PFT and HRCT endpoints are needed to better define their pulmonary safety and long-term effects.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides the first integrated assessment of pulmonary outcomes across all bDMARD and tsDMARD classes in RA-ILD. Overall, targeted therapies were associated with stabilisation of lung function, with consistent trends toward preservation of FVC and DLCO observed with JAK inhibitors. Rituximab was associated with an improvement in DLCO among patients with more severe baseline pulmonary impairment.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease primarily characterised by synovial inflammation and progressive joint damage, with a global prevalence from 0.25% to 1%.1 It can occur at any age, but women are affected two to three times more frequently than men. Extra-articular involvement is common in RA. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is one of the most severe complications, affecting about 6% of patients with RA.2 RA-associated ILD (RA-ILD) is increasingly recognised as a leading cause of morbidity and premature mortality in patients with RA.13 Median survival in patients with RA-ILD has been reported to range between 2 and 14 years.4 Recent studies indicate that respiratory function parameters, particularly FVC and DLCO, tend to decline in the absence of treatment.5

Its pathogenesis remains only partially understood and involves a complex interplay between systemic autoimmunity, chronic inflammation and fibrotic remodelling of the lung. Established risk factors for RA-ILD include older age, male sex, smoking history, severe articular disease, high titres rheumatoid factor (RF) and antibodies to cyclic citrullinated peptides (ACPA).6 RA-ILD is heterogeneous, with usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) being the most frequent radiological pattern, associated with a poorer prognosis compared with non-UIP forms.7

Over the last two decades, biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) and targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (tsDMARDs) have transformed the therapeutic landscape of RA by targeting specific immune pathways to improve disease control and prevent structural joint damage.8 9 However, their efficacy in RA-associated ILD remains poorly characterised. No randomised clinical trials have specifically addressed this population, and available evidence is largely limited to observational studies, case series or post-hoc analyses, leaving significant uncertainty in guiding treatment decisions.6 Nevertheless, this aspect is increasingly acknowledged in recent guidelines, underlining the need for robust data to guide therapeutic strategies.10 Existing data suggest that bDMARDs may slow the progression of RA-ILD.11 More recently, new studies have evaluated the efficacy of JAK inhibitors (JAKi) in RA-ILD, suggesting potential benefit.12

This uncertainty poses a major clinical challenge, as the optimal management of RA-ILD remains undefined. Given the rarity of this complication and the limited evidence, conducting randomised clinical trials in this population is currently impractical.

Together, these gaps highlight the need for a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence on the efficacy of b/tsDMARDs in RA-ILD. The present meta-analysis therefore aims to synthesise available data on the efficacy of biologic and targeted synthetic DMARDs in patients with RA and interstitial lung involvement, providing a clearer understanding of their potential benefits and risks and supporting informed clinical decision-making in this high-risk population.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) to identify studies published until 2025. In addition, abstracts presented at the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) annual congresses from 2022 to 2024 were screened.

The search strategy included the following terms:

PubMed: (‘interstitial lung disease’ AND ‘Arthritis, Rheumatoid’(Mesh)) AND ‘Arthritis, Rheumatoid/therapy’(Mesh)

PubMed: (‘interstitial lung disease’ AND ‘Arthritis, Rheumatoid’(Mesh)) AND ‘Arthritis, Rheumatoid/therapy’(Mesh)

Cochrane CENTRAL: ‘rheumatoid arthritis and interstitial lung disease’

Cochrane CENTRAL: ‘rheumatoid arthritis and interstitial lung disease’

We limited the search to articles written in English and reporting on randomised controlled trials (RCTs) or observational studies involving adults (age>18 years). In addition, we manually searched the reference lists of all included studies to identify any additional eligible reports. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by three reviewers (VB, EV and SH), followed by full-text assessment for eligibility. Duplicate references were removed prior to screening.

The review protocol was registered in the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO; ID: 1178039).

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias in non-randomised studies was assessed using the ROBINS-E (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies - of Exposures) tool. RCTs were evaluated using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2.0) (online supplemental figure 1).

Statistical analysis

We conducted the meta-analysis in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Forced vital capacity (FVC), diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and 6-min walk test were analysed as continuous variables. For each outcome, pooled mean differences (MDs) and corresponding 95% CIs were calculated using the inverse-variance method within a random-effects model to account for potential heterogeneity across studies.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the χ² test and quantified with the I² statistic. A random-effects model was applied in the presence of substantial heterogeneity (I² >50%). Sensitivity analyses were performed, including analyses restricted to patients with baseline FVC <80%, studies with a median follow-up of 12 months and studies enrolling more than 20 patients.

Publication bias was assessed visually by inspection of funnel plot symmetry, generated using RevMan V.5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration) (online supplemental figure 2).

All analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) V.5.3 (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, Copenhagen). For outcomes that could not be meta-analysed— HRCT pattern and evolution—a narrative synthesis was provided.

Results

A total of 874 records were initially identified. After removal of duplicates, 803 records were screened by title and abstract, and 21 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Three studies were excluded due to insufficient data1416, leaving 18 studies121733 that met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. The 18 included studies comprised 984 patients with RA-ILD. The mean age was 64.9 years, 56.7% were women, and the mean RA disease duration was 115 months. The mean follow-up time after treatment initiation was 12 months (11/18 studies) and beyond 18 months (5/18 studies). Baseline data corresponded to the time of treatment initiation.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

All included studies were observational, most of them with a retrospective design. However, according to the ROBINS-E tool, all studies were judged to be at low risk of bias, reflecting minor concerns in design and reporting limitations (table 1 and online supplemental table 1). No randomised controlled trials specifically addressing the treatment of RA-ILD were identified.

Forced vital capacity

FVC data were available across the 18 studies included in the meta-analysis, comprising 984 patients at baseline and 973 at the end of follow-up (figure 2). When pooling all treatments, no significant difference in FVC change was observed (mean difference −0.79, 95% CI −2.18 to 0.6, p=0.27). A significant decline in FVC was noted among patients treated with abatacept (mean difference −1.80, 95% CI −3.56 to −0.05, p=0.04). Although rituximab appeared to exert a relevant effect, it did not achieve a statistically significant improvement (mean difference +2.98, 95% CI −1.66 to 7.56, p=0.21).

A substantial number of studies on JAK inhibitors indicated stable FVC values (mean difference +0.20, 95% CI −3.57 to 3.96, p=0.92). Other agents generally stabilised FVC, although the available data remain insufficient to draw firm conclusions regarding TNF inhibitors and tocilizumab.

Only two studies assessed FVC progression according to the chest CT ILD patterns in patients treated with rituximab.21 24 However, the available data remain inconsistent. In the small cohort reported by Duarte et al (n=3 UIP cases), no significant change in FVC was observed over time.21 Conversely, Narváez et al found a significant improvement in FVC among patients with a UIP pattern (73.1±16.4% vs 79.2±20.3% at 12 months, p=0.004; p<0.001 at 24 months), which was similarly observed in patients with non-UIP patterns.24

In an additional analysis of the abatacept cohort reported by Fernández-Díaz et al,22 Atienza-Mateo et al17 examined FVC progression according to the Chest CT ILD patterns. FVC values showed no significant change over 24 months in either UIP (82.1±21.4% vs 81.0±15.0%) or NSIP subgroups, supporting a stabilising effect of abatacept on pulmonary function irrespective of the underlying radiological pattern

DLCO

DLCO data were available from the 18 studies included in the meta-analysis, encompassing 984 patients at baseline and 973 at the end of follow-up (figure 3). When all treatments were pooled, no significant overall change in DLCO was observed (mean difference +1.81, 95% CI −0.44 to 4.05, p=0.12). Treatment-specific analyses showed that no agent significantly modified DLCO progression. Rituximab exhibited the most consistent trend toward improvement, with a mean increase of +3.46% (95% CI −0.70 to 7.61, p=0.10). Abatacept appeared to maintain stable DLCO values over time (mean difference +0.81%, 95% CI −1.86 to 3.46, p=0.55). JAKi similarly demonstrated stability (mean difference +2.13%, 95% CI −4.02 to 8.28, p=0.50), while data for TNF inhibitors and tocilizumab, although limited, were consistent with a comparable preservation of pulmonary function.

Regarding DLCO evolution according to Chest CT ILD patterns, data for rituximab are inconsistent. One study, conducted in a very small number of patients, did not report any significant change.21 Another study observed an increase in DLCO at 12 and 24 months, but only in patients with a UIP pattern.24 In the study evaluating abatacept, no significant change in DLCO was observed in patients with a UIP pattern. In contrast, patients with an NSIP pattern showed a significant increase in DLCO at 24 months.17

6-minutes walk test

The 6 min walk test (6MWT), reported longitudinally in three studies assessing rituximab or JAKi,19 showed a non-significant trend toward improvement (mean difference 23.67 m, 95% CI −6.64 to 54.98, p=0.13) (figure 4).

Results of a meta-analysis on a 6-min walk test. JAKi, Janus kinase inhibitors.

Overall, treatment effects on this parameter appeared limited, as no significant differences were observed before and after therapy. For rituximab, mean walking distance remained stable (393±90 m at baseline vs 414±86 m at follow-up, p>0,05). Similarly, in both studies evaluating JAKi, 6MWT values showed no significant change over time, suggesting preservation rather than improvement of exercise capacity.

HRCT

Assessment of ILD progression by HRCT or microCT was reported in several studies, but with substantial heterogeneity in both methodology and results. Some studies relied on qualitative radiological evaluation, whereas others applied semi-quantitative or automated quantitative scoring systems. The timing of follow-up assessments also varied widely, from 6 months to more than 36 months. Across studies, treatment effects were inconsistent. In most cases, pulmonary findings remained stable under therapy, as evidenced—when available—by quantitative or semi-quantitative analyses. The proportions of patients classified as improved, stable or worsened varied considerably between cohorts and treatment classes. Stability was the most common outcome, although reported improvement rates ranged from approximately 6% to 30%, depending on the scoring method and therapeutic agent evaluated. Rituximab and abatacept cohorts provided the largest datasets, yet inter-study comparisons remain challenging because of methodological variability (table 2). Overall, the marked heterogeneity in imaging endpoints and assessment techniques precludes firm conclusions regarding the impact of individual targeted therapies on HRCT-defined ILD progression.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results, taking into account baseline lung involvement severity (FVC<80% predicted), studies with more than 20 patients, and assessments at 12 months. Sensitivity analysis for DAS28 was not feasible, as values remained above 3.2 in all included studies.17 20 22 24 28 29

In patients classified as severe, with FVC assessed only at baseline, only studies evaluating rituximab were available.21 23 24 28 31 No significant change in FVC was observed in this subgroup, whereas a significant improvement in DLCO was noted (mean difference +5.85, 95% CI 0.86 to 10.83, p=0.02) (onlinesupplemental figures 3 4).

Further sensitivity analyses restricted to studies with more than 20 patients and a 12 month follow-up did not reveal any significant differences, including for treatments with rituximab, abatacept or JAK inhibitors. No significant changes were observed for either FVC or DLCO (onlinesupplemental figures 59).

Discussion

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the effect of bDMARDs and tsDMARDs on functional parameters in RA-ILD. The results of our meta-analysis indicate that these treatments are generally associated with the stabilisation of key pulmonary function parameters, namely FVC and DLCO. In addition, our systematic review of the literature revealed stability in other clinical or imaging outcomes, including HRCT findings and 6 min walk test performance. While rituximab appeared to be the most effective treatment across studies, this observation did not reach statistical significance. It is important to note that only rituximab was associated with a significant improvement in DLCO in patients with baseline FVC below 80%. In clinical practice, achieving stability in FVC and DLCO likely reflects a meaningful attenuation of fibrotic progression rather than complete reversibility, particularly in UIP-dominant RA-ILD.

We also performed an analysis of the 6MWT, an important parameter reflecting the functional impact of RA-ILD. Although the number of available studies is small, these results indicate that current therapies may help maintain functional status in RA-ILD without translating into measurable gains in walking performance. Larger and longer-term studies will be required to clarify whether specific agents can improve exercise tolerance beyond stabilisation.

RA-ILD is an increasingly recognised complication of rheumatoid arthritis, associated with excess mortality and substantial management challenges.2 34 In daily clinical practice, treatment decisions in this population are often made without strong supporting evidence, as randomised controlled trials (RCTs) specifically targeting RA-ILD remain unavailable. Nonetheless, recent international guidelines have begun to acknowledge the importance of RA-ILD and offer preliminary therapeutic recommendations.10 35 This highlights the need for evidence-based guidance in this complex population and underscores the relevance of synthesising available observational data to inform clinical decision-making. Our systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that all treatment classes show comparable effects on key pulmonary outcomes. Although rituximab demonstrated a trend toward greater efficacy, this observation did not reach statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Addressing the questions raised by this meta-analysis will require the conduct of randomised controlled trials. However, it will be essential to reduce heterogeneity among included patients, particularly with regard to RA disease duration, duration of lung involvement and ILD pattern. In our meta-analysis, specific data on early RA-associated ILD were rarely available, as most included cohorts comprised patients with long-standing and heterogeneous disease durations. Pathophysiological mechanisms are unlikely to be identical across different stages of disease evolution, which may partly explain heterogeneity in treatment responses, as previously suggested in other RA outcomes.36 Future RCTs should therefore include, at a minimum, patients with early-stage pulmonary involvement. This raises the important issue of screening for ILD in patients with RA, including those who are asymptomatic. To date, there are no recent consensus recommendations defining optimal screening strategies for RA-associated ILD. Nonetheless, recent prospective studies suggest that imaging-based approaches may allow the detection of subclinical ILD in RA,3739 supporting the rationale for earlier screening strategies in future trials. Screening approaches may reasonably be guided by established ILD risk factors, such as smoking status, male sex, late-onset RA and high ACPA titres.

Our findings align with previous data,11 while also offering several advances. Notably, this is the first meta-analysis to comprehensively include all bDMARD and tsDMARD classes, including TNFi and JAK inhibitors regardless of their selectivity. Although no therapeutic trial to date has directly compared JAK inhibitors to bDMARDs, our results suggest comparable efficacy across these treatment classes, in line with previous indirect comparisons.12 It should be noted that not all studies evaluating JAK inhibitors could be included in the meta-analysis. Notably, the study by Kalyoncu et al, which could not be included in the meta-analysis, reported a marked improvement in FVC with tofacitinib.40

However, our analysis did not assess the impact of conventional treatments, particularly methotrexate (or other csDMARDs) or glucocorticoids, on pulmonary function outcomes. Previous studies have suggested that methotrexate may influence the progression of RA-associated ILD.41 However, recent data indicate that methotrexate does not appear to worsen RA-ILD.42 Nevertheless, methotrexate is considered a cornerstone of RA management, and its use was broadly consistent across the included studies. Therefore, any potential confounding effect is likely to be limited.

This study was conducted using a robust and transparent methodology. The systematic review adhered to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines,43 and three independent reviewers screened the literature in a blinded fashion to reduce selection bias. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, and the risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-E tool,44 enabling us to include studies with an overall low risk of bias. The meta-analysis was then conducted using the Cochrane Review Manager software (RevMan, V.5.3), ensuring standardised and reproducible statistical procedures.

RA-ILD is a multifactorial condition influenced by age, smoking, high disease activity and the presence of ACPA and/or RF.45 46 ACPA may promote local pulmonary inflammation through the recognition of citrullinated proteins in the lung.47 48 Inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT), enriched in T cells, B cells and follicular dendritic cells, has also been described in RA-ILD.3 49 50 These ectopic lymphoid structures can act as germinal centres,5153 supporting local autoantibody production. This B cell-driven microenvironment may underlie the efficacy of B cell-depleting therapy such as rituximab.54

However, several limitations of this work must be acknowledged. First, most of the included studies were observational and retrospective in design, with only a limited number of prospective cohorts. This introduces an inherent risk of selection bias and residual confounding despite the inclusion of studies with low overall bias. One potential source of bias is the lack of consideration of concomitant treatments that may influence the measured outcomes, particularly csDMARDs and glucocorticoids. To validate these findings and strengthen the evidence base, RCTs dedicated to RA-ILD are urgently needed. Second, the follow-up period was relatively short in most studies—typically between 6 and 12 months. Given the slow and heterogeneous progression of ILD, such timeframes may not adequately capture long-term pulmonary changes. Extended follow-up will be essential to assess the durability of treatment effects and better guide long-term management strategies.

Third, an important limitation lies in the limited adjustment for potential confounding factors across studies. Variables such as smoking history, disease activity and autoantibody profiles were either inconsistently reported or not accounted for in analyses. These parameters are known to influence both ILD onset5558 and treatment response,59 60 and their exclusion limits the interpretability and generalisability of the findings. Future research should aim to systematically integrate these variables into study design and analysis.

Another important area for future investigation is the impact of treatment on RA-ILD subtypes. There are very limited data regarding treatment efficacy according to ILD pattern. In particular, evidence in patients with UIP is scarce, despite its recognised association with a poorer prognosis and reduced response to conventional therapies compared with NSIP. The most common radiological and histopathological patterns include usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), organising pneumonia (OP) and obliterative bronchiolitis.61 However, few studies have evaluated differential treatment responses by ILD pattern. In one study of abatacept, no significant difference in efficacy was observed between UIP and NSIP subtypes.17 It would be of interest to extend this analysis to other therapeutic agents to determine whether ILD pattern may influence treatment response and guide personalised therapy.

In addition, the evolution of HRCT abnormalities under treatment remains insufficiently studied. We observed substantial heterogeneity and a lack of standardised imaging criteria in radiographic parameters assessed, which limits the interpretation of treatment effects on structural lung changes. The future application of artificial intelligence (AI)—particularly deep learning approaches applied to HRCT—offers promising perspectives in this area. AI-based tools could enable objective, reproducible and sensitive assessments of ILD extent and progression over time.62 63 By improving standardisation and accuracy in radiographic evaluation, these technologies may contribute to the development of imaging-based endpoints and better therapeutic monitoring.

In conclusion, targeted therapies—including both bDMARDs and tsDMARDs—appear to preserve pulmonary function in most patients with RA-ILD. While rituximab and JAKi show the most consistent signals of benefit, no statistically significant superiority emerged across treatment classes. Stabilisation of FVC and DLCO should therefore be considered a meaningful therapeutic goal in this population. Future research should focus on standardised, quantitative endpoints, longer follow-up and molecular stratification to guide personalised management of RA-ILD.
