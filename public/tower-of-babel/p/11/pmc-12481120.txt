Embolic complications of cardiac myxomas: A case report of acute ischemia in a young woman
Authors: Jakub Jaczyński, Piotr Kukla, Wojciech Lichota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and importancePrimary cardiac tumors are rare with myxomas representing about 50 % of benign types, typically arising sporadically in adults aged 30-60 and predominantly in the left atrium. Symptoms result from obstruction, embolization or arrhythmias. Embolic complications, particularly in young patients without cardiovascular risk factors, should raise suspicion of an intracardiac tumor. Prompt recognition and management are critical to prevent serious outcomes.Case presentationA 24-year-old woman presented with sudden bilateral lower limb paralysis and ischemic pain. Clinical evaluation revealed absent pulses and sensory-motor deficits in both legs. CTA demonstrated thrombotic occlusion at the aortic bifurcation and major arteries of both lower limbs, as well as signs of renal and splenic ischemia. Emergency embolectomy was performed using a Fogarty catheter and histopathology of embolic material revealed a cardiac myxoma. On postoperative day 0, reperfusion syndrome developed in the right leg necessitating fasciotomy. Echocardiography identified a left atrial mass which was surgically excised. The patient recovered with residual right peroneal nerve palsy and was referred for rehabilitation.Clinical discussionIn young patients with embolic events and no prior cardiac disease, intracardiac tumors must be considered. Echocardiography is the diagnostic modality of choice, supported by CT/MRI when needed. Surgical resection is curative, but complications such as reperfusion syndrome can arise. Long-term follow-up with annual echocardiography is recommended due to recurrence risk.ConclusionThis case illustrates the importance of early diagnosis, multidisciplinary intervention and coordinated care in achieving favorable outcomes in patients with cardiac myxoma presenting with acute embolic events.Keywords: Acute limb ischemia, Myxoma, Intracardiac tumor, Case report

Introduction

Heart tumors are quite rare – they can be primary (benign and malignant; 75 % of primary cardiac tumors are benign, and 25 % are malignant [2]) or metastatic (about 30 times more frequent, usually malignant). Myxomas account for about 50 % of primary heart tumors in adults [1,9].

Primary heart tumors are mostly sporadic, with a hereditary background (most often Carney syndrome – PrKAR1A gene mutation) [8,10].

Myxomas occur in the 3rd to 6th decade of life, with a male-to-female ratio of 2:1, and 90 % are located in the left atrium (although they can be found in the ventricles, on the valves, or in the right atrium) [4,10].

About 12 % of primary heart tumors are found incidentally during the diagnostic work-up for other conditions [4,9].

The symptoms of heart tumors result from three main mechanisms: obstruction (mass effect), embolization, and arrhythmias. The most common symptoms are shortness of breath, chest pain, cough, syncope, hemoptysis, peripheral embolism, arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death.

Embolism due to heart tumors is most commonly associated with myxomas due to their gelatinous structure (fragility and hemorrhagic areas inside) [4].

Report

The 24-year-old patient was admitted to the Emergency Department due to sudden paralysis of both lower limbs and severe pain that appeared suddenly while commuting to work and has lasted for about 30 min. The patient reported a sudden loss of sensation and mobility of the lower limbs during the way to work.

The physical examination revealed pallor of the feet, absence of pulses in the femoral, popliteal, and foot arteries bilaterally, and sensory-motor paralysis of the toes bilaterally. The patient's medical history includes: Henoch-Schönlein purpura, vitamin D deficiency, liver hemangioma, vitamin B12 and folic acid deficiency; observation for arthritis – incomplete diagnostics (2023). The patient chronically uses levonorgestrel.

Hypovascular areas were visible in the poles of both kidneys, likely ischemic changes. In the spleen, hypovascular areas with heterogeneous enhancement were observed, mainly in the poles, peripherally - likely ischemic changes.

The patient was admitted to the Vascular Surgery Department due to acute ischemia of both lower limbs for interventional treatment. The patient underwent emergency surgery to remove an embolism in the aorta, iliac arteries, and lower limbs using the Fogarty catheter on 29.12.2023, approximately 60 min after admission (Fig. 2). Intraoperative material was sent for histopathological examination. Histopathological examination of the embolic material confirmed the diagnosis of cardiac myxoma, consistent with a benign neoplasm composed of stellate and myxoid cells embedded in a myxoid stroma. Due to signs of anemia, 1 unit of group-matched packed red blood cells was transfused without complications. The surgery was performed promptly, within an hour of the patient's arrival at the hospital. However, on postoperative day 0, right lower limb reperfusion syndrome was diagnosed (recurrent sensory-motor paralysis of the limb with preserved peripheral pulses, swelling of the right calf) – the patient was reoperated on 30.12.2024, with fasciotomy of the right anterior and medial calf compartments, resulting in resolution of symptoms and improvement in the local condition.

Intraoperative embolic material - cardiac myxoma.

Neurological consultation revealed right common peroneal nerve damage (both superficial and deep branches; no dorsiflexion of the right foot and right hallux; preserved plantar flexion with resistance; sensory deficit on the distal part of the right foot and in the area of the first and second metatarsal bones, waddling gait, tendency to varus deformity of the right foot). An echocardiogram was performed in the department (Fig. 3) following the embolectomy, during which an unusual mass suggestive of a cardiac origin was retrieved. Given the patient's young age and absence of cardiovascular risk factors, further investigation was warranted. The echocardiogram revealed a mass in the left atrium consistent with a myxoma.

An echocardiogram - unusual mass (cardiac myxoma).

The patient was initially treated with emergency surgery to remove emboli from the aorta, iliac arteries, and lower limbs using a Fogarty catheter on 29.12.2023, followed by a fasciotomy on 30.12.2023 due to reperfusion syndrome. After stabilization and further cardiac evaluation, the patient was consulted by the Heart Team and qualified for tumor resection in the Cardiac Surgery Department. Definitive surgery to remove the left atrial myxoma was performed on 09.02.2024. All surgical interventions-including vascular and cardiac procedures-were completed within a span of less than six weeks, reflecting timely and coordinated multidisciplinary care.

After hospitalization, the patient was referred for rehabilitation and follow-up in the cardiac surgery and vascular surgery outpatient clinics. Echocardiograms of relatives (parents, siblings) revealed no signs of a heart myxoma.

Discussion

The occurrence of an embolic event in a young person with sinus rhythm, without accompanying heart disease or infective endocarditis, should raise suspicion of an intracardiac tumor.

Echocardiography is the preferred diagnostic method, particularly optimal for small tumors (<1 cm) [4].

Differential diagnosis of heart tumors includes: intracardiac thrombus, vegetation in infective endocarditis, abscesses, primary and metastatic heart tumors, fat hypertrophy of the septum, cysts, and localized hypertrophy of the heart muscle [[1], [2], [3]].

Recurrence rate is 1–4 % - in the same or another chamber, they can be multiple; extra-cardiac recurrence of myxoma is associated with fragmentation.

MRI and CT (especially in cases of metastatic spread) are useful for differentiating between tumors and thrombus [3].

Annual echocardiography is recommended for all patients after resection due to the risk of recurrence [4].

Rapid diagnosis, revascularization, and cardiac surgery are crucial to prevent tumor progression and further embolic episodes. However, they could not prevent complications such as reperfusion syndrome [4,5,6,7]. In the presented case, comprehensive and rapid diagnostics and treatment were carried out, but they did not prevent the patient from experiencing complications. Nevertheless, the patient survived, and the neurological deficits were addressed with rehabilitation, leading to very good results once rehabilitation was completed. The patient underwent rehabilitation and returned to work with no significant limitations due to the treatment. This highlights the importance of time in treatment, especially when symptoms of acute ischemia are present.

This case presents several key strengths, most notably its illustration of a rare and life-threatening presentation of cardiac myxoma in a young woman without cardiovascular risk factors, highlighting the importance of considering intracardiac tumors in atypical embolic events. The diagnostic process, surgical interventions, and follow-up exemplify highly coordinated multidisciplinary care, involving emergency medicine, vascular and cardiac surgery, radiology, neurology, and rehabilitation services. Rapid CTA imaging, emergent embolectomy, histopathological confirmation, and subsequent echocardiography enabled timely identification and definitive treatment of the underlying myxoma. Despite the complexity of the case, the patient's positive outcome-marked by functional recovery and return to daily activities-reflects effective clinical decision-making and comprehensive care. However, limitations include the inherent constraint of generalizability from a single case report, and the absence of initial cardiac imaging prior to embolectomy, which might have expedited diagnosis. The development of reperfusion syndrome requiring fasciotomy also underscores the procedural risks, particularly when ischemia is extensive or prolonged. While the use of Fogarty catheter embolectomy is standard, its success in this case was facilitated by rapid intervention and may not be reproducible in settings with delayed presentation or limited resources. Furthermore, genetic testing for Carney complex was not performed, which may be considered in similar young patients. Overall, this case emphasizes the need for high clinical suspicion and rapid, collaborative management in cases of unexplained embolism, while also acknowledging the procedural and diagnostic challenges that may arise.
