Identification of a personalized eGFR threshold improves the prediction of kidney failure risk after transplantation
Authors: Symeon V. Savvopoulos, Irina Scheffner, Andreas Reppas, Wilfried Gwinner, Haralampos Hatzikirou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMany factors cause kidney transplant graft failure. To identify at-risk patients and tailor treatment, failure risks must be accurately predicted. We are trying to predict the temporal progression of graft function (as treated with estimated glomerular filtration rate) using only pre- and post-transplantation data to develop a model that could be used clinically to help patient management.MethodsWe develop a method that integrates a dynamic model of glomerular filtration rate with machine learning and explicitly accounts for parameter uncertainty. The algorithm is trained on a cohort of 892 kidney transplant recipients and validated in an independent cohort of 847 recipients. The model uses routinely collected pre-transplant variables together with early post-operative graft function as inputs.ResultsHere we show that the model predicts the temporal evolution of graft function and identifies a threshold in estimated glomerular filtration rate that enables classification of high-risk patients. Clinical events following kidney transplantation are predicted using only a limited number of inputs, achieving a graft outcome prediction accuracy of 0.88 ± 0.04 (F1-score: 0.81 ± 0.06) for the first postoperative year. An accuracy of 0.85 ± 0.05 (F1-score: 0.79 ± 0.07) is also achieved for clinical events in the second postoperative year, thereby strengthening the reliability of the prediction methodology over time.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that combining a dynamic filtration model with machine learning provides a robust basis for individualized prediction of graft outcomes after kidney transplantation and has the potential to be developed into a clinical decision-support tool for early identification of high-risk patients.

Introduction

The challenge in the management of patients with chronic organ disease is to prevent progressive organ deterioration and ultimate failure. Most chronic diseases are characterized by a dynamic process of injury and repair responses inherent to the specific disease present. This natural course may be modulated by a complex, time-variant interplay of disease-modifying factors, co-morbidity conditions, and potential clinical interventions. Rational appreciation of this complex interplay is the key for an individualized patient treatment in terms of diagnostic measures and therapeutic decisions. This includes recognition and weighing of relevant causative and non-causative risk factors, their potential interaction, and estimation of putative therapeutic effects with respect to the clinical disease course and the mid- and long-term outcomes1.

Kidney transplantation is a prime paradigm of a complex chronic disease with a dynamic course. For patients with end-stage kidney failure, kidney transplantation is the optimal choice of treatment as it restores kidney function significantly. The degree of kidney function is usually estimated by the glomerular filtration rate (GFR)2. In particular, the GFR is determined by the quality of the transplanted organ. After transplantation, various factors determine the further evolution of GFR, including peri- and post-operative complications and successful treatment of these complications. In an uneventful post-transplant course, kidney grafts can last several decades without relevant loss of GFR3. However, in many cases, repeated injuries lead to irreversible graft injury and progressive loss of GFR4,5. Injuries to the graft include acute and chronic T cell- and antibody-mediated rejection, drug toxicity, infections, recurrence of kidney disease, and effects from patients’ co-morbidities which may have a cumulative impact on the transplant at outcome. Appropriate therapeutic intervention may successfully treat these complications but often leads only to partial or no improvement. Hence, despite all previous diagnostic and therapeutic progress, the mean kidney graft survival is limited to ~15 years6,7. Thus, besides timely recognition of the injuries to the graft, the challenge is to have a precise understanding of the nature and extent of heterogenous and complex injury factors on the outcome, in this case, the graft function (GFR).

Common computational approaches for the assessment of chronic disease risk factors and outcomes are regression analysis and Cox proportional hazard analysis, which assign a certain effect value to the putative risk factors and numerically estimate the outcome of interest in a given study population8. In such approaches, the patient’s status is captured at a specific point in time by choosing particular risk factors, and in turn, an estimate for the future course is predicted. By nature, these approaches cannot truly integrate time-variant, sequential events of dynamic disease processes into the model, besides from condensing these multiple events into suitable factor variables. Also, the appropriate estimation of potential interactions between different factors that modulate the GFR is challenging. Armero et al. developed a model for predicting the GFR in children with chronic kidney disease. A Bayesian analysis estimated the joint model’s parameters, hyperparameters, and random effects9. The drawback of the proposed model is its struggle to accurately predict disease progression for children with serious or irregular profiles due to the complex and variable nature of chronic kidney disease, as evidenced by its deficiency in identifying such cases and its inability to fully explain the observed variability in progression patterns. Limitations of this model included inaccurate prediction of disease progression in children with severe graft impairment or with irregular functional changes and latent disease states, thus making identification of these cases virtually impossible despite application of advanced approaches like Markov Mixture Models.

Studies using large datasets and numerous analytical methodologies show that predictive modeling for kidney function has advanced in recent years. One important effort was Goldfarb-Rumyantzev et al.’s logistic regression and tree-based 3-year graft survival prediction system10. Their study examined pre-transplant characteristics from over 37,407 end-stage kidney disease patients receiving cadaveric kidney or kidney-pancreas transplantation. Both models, especially the tree-based approach, showed a linear correlation with graft survival, delivering a 76% positive predictive value. This study shows that pre-transplant variables can improve post-transplant predictions by building solid models for long-term kidney allograft survival prediction. Akl et al.11 and Rao et al.12 underline the potential of integrating machine learning and comprehensive risk indices to estimate graft survival and failure risk. These papers show how predictive analytics in kidney transplantation is improving, enabling more tailored and successful treatment approaches. Greco et al.13 employed data mining algorithms to develop predictive models for graft failure in kidney transplant patients, analyzing interactions between acute rejection (ARE), delayed graft function (DGF), chronic allograft changes (CAN) and body mass index (BMI). The study identified patients at higher risk of graft loss, particularly those with a BMI > 24.8 kg/m² and CAN, and proposed decision trees as an effective alternative to traditional statistical methods. All these studies were based on conventional clinical factors, without incorporating any dynamic modeling.

Recently, a sequence-to-sequence deep learning algorithm was developed to predict the patient-specific expected reference range of the GFR by using serial GFR measurements from the first 3 months post transplantation14. The algorithm was shown to be helpful identifying GFR deviations in the individual that indicated truly anomalous values and thus, need of potential further diagnostic work-up. Yet, a large number of patient’s GFR measurements was required for this assessment and the algorithm could not estimate the stability properties of this value, i.e. whether the GFR will change over time or stay invariant. Also, Raynaud et al. aimed to enhance risk stratification for kidney transplant recipients using a Bayesian approach. It continuously refined survival predictions based on clinical data but did not employ dynamic modeling15.

After reviewing existing modeling approaches, our objective was to develop a framework capable of capturing the early post-transplantation dynamics of GFR to enable long-term graft outcome prediction in kidney transplant recipients. A central feature of our model is the rigorous treatment of the damping factor, which governs the temporal oscillatory behavior of GFR and plays a pivotal role in predicting graft failure. Figure 1 illustrates the methodological workflow. The process begins with the integration of a new patient’s pre-transplant clinical data together with early post-transplant GFR recordings. These inputs allow the generation of a first-year GFR trajectory and enable partial personalized parameter estimation of a damped oscillator model. The uncertainty associated with parameters—particularly the damping factor that modulates oscillatory dynamics—is explicitly incorporated into this estimation step. The personalized model is then employed to simulate potential GFR trajectories over the first post-transplant year, spanning both functional and nonfunctional kidney state areas relative to a clinically defined threshold. By situating simulated trajectories within these regions, the framework provides an individualized, dynamic prediction of long-term graft function versus failure. This paper is structured as follows. Section “Methods” is the methods. Section “Results” presents the results, beginning with the rationale for our approach, followed by an exploration of key patient and clinical features used to estimate first-year graft function. We then discuss the identification of a critical GFR threshold for graft survival and provide individualized predictions of these thresholds. Finally, we demonstrate the algorithm’s predictive performance across patient groups and its robustness over time. The discussion is in Section “Discussion”. Applied to two temporally distinct single-center cohorts, the framework achieves robust discrimination between high- and low-risk patients at one and two years after transplantation (AUCs of ~0.85–0.90 and F1 scores around 0.8) and outperforms conventional statistical models and state-of-the-art machine-learning classifiers. We further show that the individualized thresholds cluster within a clinically plausible range (~30–50 mL/min/1.73 m²) that is consistent with observed long-term graft survival patterns. Methods

Clinical data

Data was taken from the Hannover Medical School, covering two distinct time periods. Specifically, the dataset of 1739 patients transplanted between the years 2000–2015 was splitted in half (Cohort 1-Training; transplanted 2000–2008, cohort 2-Validation; transplanted 2009–2015). This temporal division was applied to ensure that each cohort represents a distinct group of patients to preserve the integrity and chronological order of the data, which is crucial for understanding the temporal dynamics and potential changes in medical practices or patient demographics over time. By splitting the data in this manner, potential biases that could arise from random partitioning were mitigated, ensuring that each subset reflects different periods and possibly different clinical conditions and treatments. This approach provides a more robust validation of our model’s performance and helps identify any temporal trends or shifts in patient outcomes.

Data collection and analysis were performed with prior informed consent of the patients and with the approval of the Hannover Medical School Ethics review board (no 2765) that decides on all patient studies, according to national laws and based on the Declaration of Helsinki and regarding transplant patients, on the Declaration of Istanbul. Two different patient sets were used in this study. The training group N1 and the validation cohort N2 consisted of 892 and 847 patients, respectively. In the training group, 354 patients had no rejection, 260 patients had one rejection, and 278 patients had multiple rejections. In the validation cohort, 410 patients had no rejection, 256 patients had one rejection, and 181 patients had multiple rejections. Protocol biopsies were collected six weeks, 3, and 6 months after transplantation. Also, data collection was performed before and at transplantation. Data of additional biopsies for cause, performed upon unexplained graft deterioration (at any time), were also recorded. Biopsies were evaluated according to the Banff classification valid at the time of biopsy16. A detailed description of the pre- and early post-transplant variables of the cohorts, including the initial treatment with immunosuppressants, has been previously reported in the work of Scheffner et al.17.

Anti-thymocyte globulin was used in sensitized patients and for combined kidney/pancreas transplantations. The standard maintenance therapy consisted of a calcineurin inhibitor, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisolone. Until the end of 2004, patients with low immunological risk (i.e., first kidney transplant from deceased donors in patients without panel-reactive antibodies) received a dual maintenance therapy with a calcineurin inhibitor and prednisolone. Tacrolimus gradually replaced cyclosporine A over the years.

Acute T cell-mediated rejections (TCMR), including borderline cases were treated with steroid boli, with the exception of subclinical borderline cases in protocol biopsies, defined by an increase in serum creatinine over baseline by <20% at biopsy. In addition, mycophenolate mofetil was added in patients on a dual immunosuppressive maintenance therapy. Patients with TCMR occurring at 6 months or later or with vascular TCMR at any time point were switched from cyclosporine A to tacrolimus. Acute antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR) was treated with steroid boli, plasma exchange, rituximab, immune globulins, and a switch to or increase in tacrolimus when full histopathomorphological criteria of the rejection were present (with or without donor-specific antibodies), interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy was <25%, and baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) before the rejection was >25 ml/min. In all other cases, individual treatment decisions were made, mostly consisting of steroid boli, immune globulins, a switch to or increase in tacrolimus, or no treatment in cases with minor findings of glomerulitis and peritubular capillaritis. No standardized treatment was defined for BK polyomavirus nephropathy or other findings (e.g., acute tubular injury, tubulointerstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy, glomerulonephritis).

The glomerular filtration rate was calculated with the Cockcroft and Gault formula and expressed as mL/min/1.73 m2 body surface. The criteria for delayed graft function were <500 mL urine within the first 24 h post-transplantation and/or dialysis treatment necessary within the first week

Prediction of first-year graft function

Initially, only patients without rejection from the training group were used for feature selection and multilinear regression to predict the GFR at 365 d. The multilinear regression model included pre-transplantation biologically relevant data and modellable parameters, including 1) the age of the patient, 2) the sex of the patient, 3) the donor’s age, 4) the donor’s sex, 5) deceased donor/living donor graft, 6) cold ischemia time (hours), 7) presence or absence of delayed graft function, 8) HLA-mismatch at locus A, 9) HLA-mismatch at locus B, 10) HLA-mismatch at locus DR, 11) level of pre-formed antibodies at transplantation, 12) blood transfusions before transplantation, 13) the number of pregnancies before transplantation, 14) the number of previous transplantations, 15) the highest GFR achieved within six weeks after transplantation, and 16) the percentage of change between the highest GFR within six weeks and the GFR at 100 d. Since GFR measurement times were non-uniform i.e., GFR was sampled at various post-operative times, we have interpolated the GFR values for every 10 days for the first 100 days.

A sequential forward selection was performed using the Akaike criterion (AIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and the adjusted R squared for the best model selection18. The regression coefficients represented the importance of the underlying features. The derived multilinear model was assumed to be applicable to all other patient groups with one rejection and multiple rejections (Supplementary Section 1 of the Supplemental Methods including Supplementary Figs. 1–4 and Supplementary Tables 1, 2). Although the regression model may exhibit some limitations in patients experiencing rejection episodes, potentially due to the influence of various partially unknown factors, performing an uncertainty analysis on the predicted GFR values would be essential for accurately identifying the outcome of kidney transplantation. This approach would help account for the inherent variability and provide a more reliable assessment of transplantation success. Linear mixed-effects models were also used (Supplementary Tables 3, 4). Further details on the steps involved in the uncertainty analysis are provided in Section “Individualized graft failure risk assessment”.

Our selection is justified by the existence of two equilibria, i.e., the failed and the functional kidney state (Fig. 3A). These equilibria are typically separated by a GFR threshold θ which is mathematically characterized as a saddle-node (unstable). Finally, the functional steady-state is estimated as the predicted first-year GFR for each patient using a regression approach. The failed graft steady-state is assumed as GFR = 0 mL/min/1.73 m2. Next, our regression analysis, integrated with the ±20-unit uncertainty in critical GFR thresholds (θ), yielded a GFR prediction interval of ±40 units (from Eq. (11) and Supplementary Section 2 of Supplementary Material), consistently applicable across all patient categories. This interval was then used to determine the individualized minimum (GFRmin), and maximum (GFRmax) GFR predictions from the regression model, allowing for accurate calibration of the parameters α, b, and θ.

Validation process

In turn, the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was created by setting a threshold probability (pthr) range from 0 to 1, and comparing the actual values with the real kidney transplantation outcomes. Results

Approach rationale

Our approach aims to calculate and estimate the patient’s individualized risk of graft failure. For this, we develop a mathematical model to predict the individual temporal GFR dynamics. The components of the dynamic model are: (i) the oscillatory behavior of the GFR and (ii) the existence of two steady graft states. The former intends to model the fluctuations observed in GFR trajectories. The latter models the failed and the functional graft state, respectively. It is important to note that these states are assumed to be stable, which allows not only to make a temporal prediction but also to gain knowledge of the temporal persistence of the predicted state. This model involves a critical threshold GFR value (unstable) that separates the failed and functional graft states.

The challenge is the individualized parameter calibration of the model. In this regard, we employ the assumption that the first-year GFR value can be considered as a good proxy of the functional graft performance3, where the failed state can be assumed as null GFR. Using as a training set a cohort of patients, which represents the natural, transplant course not affected by rejection (patients without a rejection), we employ a linear regression analysis to estimate the first-year GFR based on pre-operative patient data and two postoperative GFR measurements, namely the highest GFR measurement within six weeks after the transplantation and the value of GFR at one hundred days. Using these data, we estimate the individual patient’s model parameters (details in Methods).

The most important model parameter is the GFR threshold value. GFR values above this threshold indicate a stable, functioning graft, whereas values below suggest a high risk of graft failure. Two cohorts were used in our analysis: a training cohort (N1) and a validation cohort (N2). The first cohort includes patients transplanted between 2000 and 2008, while the second cohort comprises patients transplanted between 2009 and 2015. We employed this temporal distinction to create the training and validation sets, ensuring that each cohort represents a distinct group of patients collected over different time intervals. This approach helps to mitigate sample bias and provides a more robust evaluation of the model’s performance by reflecting the temporal variations and potential changes in clinical practices over time. The model was calibrated exclusively using patients without rejections in the training cohort, as their measured GFR values were less influenced using immunosuppressant drugs, which are commonly administered in the treatment of rejections. This decision also helped us avoid introducing bias or data leakage into the analysis. Using uncertainty analysis techniques, we calculated the probability of graft failure in the two independent datasets, which included patients with no, one, or multiple rejections, using a small number of input variables. Our three-step strategy is outlined in Fig. 4. The first part involves multilinear regression to predict the GFR after one year (Machine Learning). Cross-validation was applied to the multilinear regression step to mitigate the risk of sample bias that may arise from a single partitioning of the dataset. This statistical safeguard ensured that the estimation of first-year GFR was robust and generalizable across different patient subsets. The subsequent stages of the workflow, however, focused on patient-specific dynamic model parameter estimation (mathematical modeling) and uncertainty analysis (Monte Carlo simulations). These stages inherently operate on an individualized level, where each patient’s post-transplant GFR data are used to calibrate a personalized model—rendering traditional cross-validation less applicable or meaningful in this context. Instead, variability and uncertainty were systematically addressed through Monte Carlo simulations, which better capture the distributional behavior of parameters and model outputs under uncertainty. First, we attempted to predict the GFR at 365 days post-transplantion in the training cohort. We used a linear regression analysis, which allowed not only to predict but also to identify the important determinant variables. The inputs for the regression analysis were limited to patients without any rejection within the first year (N = 354), as patients with rejection were assumed to have additional bias of eGFR (e.g., by severity and type of rejection, variable response to treatment of rejection), which can be hardly captured by a linear regression model. Also, we wanted to avoid any data leakage. In the feature selection, the inputs included patient age, sex, donor’s age, deceased donor vs. living donor, cold ischemia time, initial function vs. delayed function of the graft, HLA-mismatch, panel reactive antibodies, blood transfusions, number of pregnancies, number of previous transplantations, highest GFR within six weeks, and percentage change between the highest GFR within six weeks and the GFR at 100 days (Supplementary Section 1 in the Supplemental Methods). Our results show that the highest GFR within the first 6 weeks and the GFR speed along with the donor’s age are sufficient to predict the GFR at 365 days. In the early transplant period, donor characteristics, particularly donor age, is the predominant factor, as previously reported by our group3 and further corroborated by the Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) index. The R2 values of the multi-linear regression results were 0.66 for the non-rejection patients, 0.60 for patients with one rejection, and 0.46 for patients with multiple rejections. Details regarding the regression approach are included in Supplementary Section 1 of the Supplemental Methods including Supplementary Figs. 1–4 and Supplementary Tables 1, 2. To provide a conventional statistical baseline, we also fitted three linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) that include: (i) a 1-year GFR model using the 42-day GFR measurement only (Supplementary Eqs. (S.1)–(S.7)), (ii) a 1-year GFR model using the 42-day and 100-day measurements plus baseline covariates (Supplementary Eqs. (S.8)–(S.14)), and (iii) the same model with interaction terms (Supplementary Eqs. (S.15)–(S.21))23,24. All LMMs were trained on the non-rejection training cohort and evaluated via cross-validation and on the temporally distinct validation cohort (patients with none, one, or multiple rejections). Across all comparisons (e.g., RMSE/MAE for 1-year GFR and failure-risk discrimination), the LMMs did not yield better results than our patient-specific dynamic modeling workflow; performance was comparable or lower. The regression analysis results are shown in Supplementary Tables 3–5 of Supplementary Methods.

A GFR threshold range exists with respect to graft survival

Our GFR predictive model invoked the existence of a critical GFR threshold that is related to graft loss. To our knowledge, in the current literature the existence of a critical GFR threshold has not been investigated. Here, we employ a survival random forest analysis developed by Gwinner et al. to assess 15-year graft survival outcomes25. By leveraging an extensive dataset from a single center17, observed over up to 15 years and including several patients, the study developed a machine-learning model to predict clinically meaningful graft outcomes. The model integrated diverse clinical and laboratory variables from the first transplant year and identified key risk factors for premature graft failure. In Gwinner et al.’s analysis25, the model demonstrated robust predictive performance, with a 71% death-censored graft survival at 15 years in the training cohort, significant concordance indices in both training (0.79) and validation (0.74) cohorts, and strong calibration. The findings underscored the model’s ability to stratify patients into different risk levels for targeted monitoring and intervention strategies, emphasizing the prognostic importance of eGFR at one year, patient demographics, types of rejections, infections, and glomerulonephritis.

A safe range for failure critical threshold can be considered between 30 and 50 ml/min/1.73 m2 by comparing the likelihood of survival at 50% across all patient categories. Notably, our findings are consistent with those reported by Yoo et al.26, who applied various machine learning approaches to predict long-term graft survival in kidney transplant recipients. In their study, the 3-month post-transplant serum creatinine concentration emerged as the most influential predictor in their conventional decision tree (CART) model, with a data-driven cut-off value of 1.65 mg/dL. This threshold was identified through the CART algorithm, which selects optimal split points using criteria such as Gini impurity to maximize classification performance. In their decision tree, serum creatinine appeared at the first decision node, underscoring its critical role in stratifying patients according to 10-year graft outcomes. Patients with serum creatinine above this level had substantially higher failure rates. Clinically, this threshold corresponds approximately to an eGFR of 30–40 mL/min/1.73 m², closely matching the critical range identified in our survival model and reinforcing the biological relevance of this threshold in predicting graft failure risk.

Individualized prediction of critical GFR thresholds

The graft risk prediction is robust in time

We attempted to explore the robustness of the prediction over time. A basic property of our mathematical model is that the functional state is a steady state, i.e. it is robust in time. This implies that the prediction of a patient classified as low risk using the GFR at 365 days should theoretically be consistent if applied to a later GFR measurement. We used the GFR values at 730 days and recalculated our predictions. Figure 8A depicts the AUC for different annual predicted GFR values at two years in both the training and validation groups. The highest AUC was achieved when the maximum predicted GFR was applied across different thresholds, with a value of 0.85 ± 0.00 for the training group and 0.88 ± 0.03 for the validation group, respectively. Figure 8B shows the AUC for both groups for patients with and without rejection, which is on average 0.85 ± 0.05. Indeed, the prediction performance was similarly good compared with the one-year prediction, suggesting that the prediction of graft risk failure is rather robust. Discussion

This research introduces a framework for evaluating the graft function and risk of graft failure in kidney transplant patients using limited pre- and post-operative data. Our framework, spanning patients from two previous decades, is apparently independent of gradual changes in procedures and medications, as demonstrated by the validation results of the model. Utilizing a feature selection technique, we have identified the key measurements that have the most significant impact on GFR after one year. The donor’s age, the highest GFR within 6 weeks after transplantation, and the percentage change in GFR at 100 days were found to be the most critical clinical parameters.

When we trained the regression model using data only from patients without rejection, we obtained reasonably predictive results for patients with and without rejection. However, we went further to enhance our model by developing a dynamic model capable of capturing GFR fluctuations and corresponding long-term GFR behavior. A critical assumption in our approach is the existence of a GFR threshold value that distinguishes functioning grafts from failing ones. By utilizing survival functions from the N1 group, we were able to estimate this threshold.

It’s important to note that investigating the biological mechanisms underlying this threshold is beyond the scope of this paper. We used regression analysis to estimate model parameters, assuming it provides a robust estimate of individualized functional GFR (first-year value). Our proposed algorithm, trained on the N1 group without rejection, exhibited high AUC values for predicting graft risk at 365 and 730 days for all patients, both with and without rejection. This demonstrates the robustness of our method in distinguishing between low and high-risk patients regarding graft failure, even without providing exact GFR value predictions. Similar results were obtained when our algorithm was applied to the validation dataset (N2). Ultimately, we discovered that our method outperforms state-of-the-art classification methods, such as XGBoost and Random Forests, in predicting the fate of grafts. We also explored classical longitudinal approaches based on linear mixed-effects models. All models were trained on the N1 non-rejection cohort and evaluated with the same splits and on N2. Across endpoints, the LMMs did not improve prediction of first-year GFR (higher RMSE, lower R2) at 365 days relative to our proposed framework. This is consistent with the limited number of early post-transplant time points—where mixed models offer little advantage over simple regression—and with the fact that standard LMMs are essentially linear and therefore unable to capture the behavior of graft function that our dynamical-systems model was designed to represent. In our setting, increasing LMM complexity tended to add variance without commensurate gains in accuracy.

This framework is designed to accurately predict graft function, as reflected by GFR values, and the probability of transplant failure by leveraging minimal input—specifically, pre-transplant clinical data and only two early post-transplant GFR measurements. The use of joint modeling approaches, while common in similar contexts, was not suitable for our objectives27,28. First, the availability of only two GFR time points proximal to the transplantation event is insufficient for reliably parameterizing a joint model. Second, joint models are typically linear and thus inadequate for capturing the nonlinearities and dynamic complexities inherent in biological systems and disease progression. In contrast, our approach is grounded in dynamical systems theory rather than purely statistical modeling. While this study focuses on prediction, the structure of our framework enables future integration with advanced tools from control theory. This opens pathways for incorporating updated predictions as new GFR measurements become available (e.g., via Bayesian filtering), and for identifying optimal therapeutic strategies through the formulation and solution of optimal control problems29,30.

While our current modeling approach describes individually expected GFR trajectories based on cases without rejection, our model can be expanded and refined to incorporate additional factors, such as different rejection phenotypes or other clinical complications mentioned earlier. This extension may help modulate the individual GFR trajectory. Additionally, variations in anti-rejection therapies and their responses can be incorporated into our proposed model. However, due to sample size limitations, we were unable to precisely determine the impact of different therapies. We are currently developing a stochastic model that incorporates time-varying factors to simulate and predict the temporal evolution of graft function more accurately. The introduction of stochasticity/noise will necessitate the use of Monte-Carlo simulations31 and Kramer’s theory to determine the graft’s fate. In addition, improved predictions could be made by applying regression analysis to real-time measurements more frequently and by updating the model parameters32.

Our approach has several limitations. The data used for model development and validation were collected from a single center, primarily serving patients of Caucasian origin, with other ethnic groups being scarce (<1%). The immunosuppressive regimens administered across the cohort were also quite uniform. This homogeneity ensures consistency in treatment and patient responses but may reduce generalizability to other ethnic groups and clinical practices. Furthermore, our outcomes’ susceptibility to alternative interpolation methods should be examined in future research. Comparing results using different interpolation approaches could help to assess our findings’ robustness and locate the best missing data handling solutions. Linear interpolation, spline interpolation, and advanced machine learning methods may be used in this context. To reduce interpolation, boost clinical measurements should be frequent and consistent. Another way to improve the situation is by using Gaussian Processes that assess the uncertainty of the interpolated points. In addition, our modeling focused solely on rejection as the prime factor in graft injury and failure, omitting other significant factors like infections, drug toxicity, and relevant co-morbid conditions. Moreover, we did not further differentiate rejections in terms of severity, antibody- and T cell-mediated mechanisms, or responses to anti-rejection treatment. Also, GFR formulae based on serum creatinine inherently possesses errors and inaccuracies. Therefore, our approach may be more suitable for establishing an intra-individual relative range of variance in graft function rather than absolute GFR values. Finally, our methodology cannot foresee complications that independently occur in the long-term after transplantation. However, GFR from the first transplant year is highly predictive of the future course of graft function and graft survival33.

Our current mathematical model describes the temporal GFR evolution in a somewhat phenomenological manner. The Duffing oscillator employs a third-order polynomial forcing term to represent the expected bistability between stable and failing grafts. A more biologically relevant model could enhance interpretability by explicitly modeling the involved pathophysiological processes, thus enabling the investigation of novel intervention strategies. As an example, graft injury of different causes may result in progressive organ fibrosis as suggested by our in vitro/in silico model (Setten et al.34) which simulated the interplay of fibroblasts and macrophages under different oxygen stress and inflammatory conditions as function of a as a bistable state of injury and repair. This and the works of U. Alon in the topic35,36 could offer a solid starting point to develop a biologically consistent alternative to the Duffing oscillator forcing term.

Our study serves as an example of combining dynamic modeling and machine learning to address a biomedical problem37,38. This is an emerging research field, with two distinct approaches: (i) data-driven derivation of dynamic models and (ii) data-assisted model predictions. In the former category are methods like SiNDy39 and Koopman operator learning approaches40. In the latter, the most prominent method is physics-informed neural networks41, which require knowledge of reliable dynamic models. In cases of model structural uncertainty, methods such as BAM42 are recommended. Such analysis could be extended to additional cohorts that include patients from different institutions (undergoing different treatment protocols), individuals from diverse racial backgrounds, and those with severe comorbidities. Such expansion would allow us to further evaluate the generalizability of our findings and enhance the overall robustness of the analysis. Finally, when dealing with noisy continuous measurements, data assimilation methods, with the Kalman filter43 being the most prominent, combine model predictions and statistical inference. Our approach relates to the specific problem of functional evolution of kidney grafts but should also be applicable to other settings of chronic disease with deterioration of organ function.
