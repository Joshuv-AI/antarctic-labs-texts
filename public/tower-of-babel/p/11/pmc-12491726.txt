Factors associated with healthcare personnel uptake of updated COVID-19 vaccine doses in the United States
Authors: Eric Kontowicz, Kari K Harland, Amy Stubbs, David Beiser, Ian Plumb, Anusha Krishnadasan, David A Talan, Nicholas M Mohr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEvaluate factors associated with United States (US) healthcare personnel (HCP) uptake of an updated COVID-19 vaccine.MethodsWe analyzed data from 887 US HCP enrolled between September 25, 2023, and April 17, 2024, in a multisite case-control COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness study. Sociodemographic and attitudinal data were collected via standardized surveys, and vaccination status was verified using source documentation.ResultsOverall, 188 (21.2 %) HCP received the updated vaccine. HCP aged 50–64 years (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.02, 95 % CI: 1.11–3.67) versus ages 18–29, and HCP with undergraduate (aOR 3.75, 95 % CI: 1.97–7.12) or graduate degrees (aOR 7.73, 95 % CI: 4.10–14.55) versus high school/some college had higher odds of vaccination. Nurses and nurse assistants had lower odds (aOR 0.48, 95 % CI: 0.30–0.76) versus other job roles. Vaccinated HCP were more likely than unvaccinated HCP to cite concerns about personal infection (89.9 % vs. 44.6 %), infecting family/friends (96.3 % vs. 56.0 %), vaccine availability (63.3 % vs. 18.9 %), and peer-reviewed literature (56.4 % vs. 28.3 %) as important influences. These factors remained consistent with findings from a prior vaccine season.ConclusionsVaccine access, education, and peer-reviewed information remain drivers of HCP vaccine uptake. Strategies should address occupational disparities and emphasize transmission risks.Keywords: Health personnel, COVID-19 vaccines, Vaccination coverage, COVID-19, SARS-CoV-2

Introduction

Vaccination is effective at protecting against symptomatic (COVID-19), severe illness, and long-term effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection (Mohammed et al., 2023; Mohr et al., 2023a; Plumb et al., 2023a; Plumb et al., 2023b; Taquet et al., 2022). In September of 2023, the United States Food and Drug Administration approved a new COVID-19 monovalent vaccine targeting the Omicron subvariant XBB.1.5 (US Department of Health and Human Services, 2023). Despite evidence of efficacy (Link-Gelles, 2024) and safety (Gayed et al., 2024), vaccine hesitancy persists among the United States (US) general population and healthcare personnel (HCP), posing a significant challenge to public health efforts (Biswas et al., 2021; Haeder, 2023; Shook et al., 2023). Vaccination attitudes among HCP may drive COVID-19 vaccine decisions of the local community (Paterson et al., 2016; Razzaghi, 2020).

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimated that vaccination uptake of the 2023–2024 vaccine among US adults was 22.5 % (22.1–22.9); HCP vaccination is likely similar to these trends (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024). For HCP, COVID-19 vaccination helps protect the workforce and patients at elevated risk of severe illness (Ahmad and Osei, 2023; Waldman et al., 2022). Understanding factors that influence HCP vaccination is essential for improving vaccine coverage.

We previously reported that employer-based messaging and the vaccine's role in preventing SARS-CoV-2 transmission were influential in driving HCP vaccination decisions of the 2022–2023 bivalent COVID-19 vaccine (Mohr et al., 2023b). In this analysis we focus on uptake of the 2023–2024 COVID-19 monovalent vaccine, explore factors influencing HCP vaccination and compare these findings with our previous report to examine any changes in influential factors.

Methods

Participant population and study setting

This is a sub-study of the multisite case-control vaccine effectiveness study, Preventing Emerging Infections through Vaccine Effectiveness Testing Project II (PREVENT II). Our primary analysis includes US HCP enrolled between September 25, 2023 and April 17, 2024. For our secondary analysis we compared HCP responses during this period to HCP enrolled between October 3, 2022 and April 20, 2023 from our previous report (Mohr et al., 2023b). PREVENT II recruited HCP who were tested for SARS-CoV-2 during the study period, stratified as symptomatic SARS-CoV-2-positive cases or SARS-CoV-2-negative controls (with or without symptoms) based on diagnostic test results and self-reported symptoms. We collected data on vaccine decision-making using electronic surveys administered six weeks following enrollment. Changes to PREVENT II survey questions to reflect approval of new vaccine formulas were implemented on September 25, 2023 and impacted inclusion for this analysis. PREVENT II methods have been reported previously (Pilishvili et al., 2021).

Each participant completed a baseline electronic survey within 60 days following their SARS-CoV-2 testing date. Demographic and job-related information from this baseline survey were used in analysis. A follow-up survey was distributed to all eligible participants six weeks following their reported symptom start date (symptomatic cases and controls) or from their test date (asymptomatic controls). Participants completed this follow-up survey between September 27, 2023 and April 24, 2024. In the follow-up survey, data on which separate factors influenced HCP vaccine uptake and HCP reported vaccine receipt after September 1, 2023 were collected (Supplementary Table 1). One factor assessed for influencing HCP vaccination was employer special incentives from, which may have included gift cards, cafeteria discounts, or branded merchandise.

We included HCP that enrolled on or after September 25, 2023, completed their six-week follow-up survey, and had verified enrollment SARS-CoV-2 testing and vaccination records. HCP who did not self-report their vaccination status, had discordant self-reported and verified vaccine status, did not complete the factors influencing or impacting their vaccine decision questions, did not report their race and ethnicity, or did not report the highest level of education received, or HCP sex was not available were excluded (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Definitions

HCP were defined as having received an updated vaccine if self-reported and source document-verified with a receipt date on or after September 12, 2023 (Pfizer/Moderna) or October 14, 2023 (Novavax), and before completion of their six-week follow-up survey. Factors were defined as “important” to an HCPs vaccination decision if rated as “moderately influenced”, “strongly influenced”, “important”, “very important”, “moderately impacted”, or “significantly impacted” on the six-week follow-up survey (Supplementary Table 1). HCP demographics and job role were categorized based on self-report and job roles were defined as Physician/Advance Practice Provider (APP [e.g Nurse Practitioners, Physician Assistants, Clinical Nurse Specialists, etc.]), Nurse/Nurse Assistant, or other staff (e.g., therapists, social workers, administrative).

Ethics statement

This project was reviewed by the CDC and was conducted consistent with applicable federal law and CDC policy (U.S. Government, C.F.R. part 46.102(l)(2)). This research was deemed non-research public health assessment at 18 of the 19 sites local institutional review boards (IRBs). The remaining site's IRB considered this project as human subject research, and the local IRB approved the project. Written consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analysis

We used bivariate comparisons to assess the unadjusted odds between 2023 and 2024 HCP vaccine receipt and various factors: age category, sex, race and ethnicity, education level, and job role, using mixed-effect logistic regression. Bivariate models considered single covariates as a fixed effect and SARS-CoV-2 testing site and month of testing as random effects. Additionally, we performed comparisons of updated vaccine receipt versus non-receipt for each HCP characteristic using difference in proportions tests and 95 % confidence intervals estimated via Chi-squared tests using the Yates correction (Yates, 1984). HCP COVID-19 status at enrollment was assessed using proportion tests only, as including it in multivariable models could introduce collider bias given that controls were selected based on case counts. Significant differences were determined by non-overlapping 95 % confidence intervals.

A multivariable mixed-effects model was employed to analyze the odds of updated vaccine dose receipt and adjusted for age category, sex, race and ethnicity, education level, and job role of the HCP as fixed effects. This model treated SARS-CoV-2 testing site and month of testing as random effects. We assessed variable collinearity using variance inflation factor (Belsley et al., 2005).

A sensitivity analysis using COVID-19 negative HCP was preformed to evaluate for selection or collider bias. For this sensitivity analysis, we used bivariate and multivariate logistic regressions using the covariates previously described but did not include random effects due to data sparsity across site and month-of-testing clusters.

To evaluate factors that influenced HCPs' decisions regarding 2023–2024 vaccination, we compared responses categorized as important versus non-important factors using difference in proportion tests methodology described earlier (Yates, 1984).

To examine any changes in influential factors for HCP vaccine receipt we performed a secondary analysis comparing HCP included in this study to HCP included in our previous report (Mohr et al., 2023b). We compared the proportion of HCP from this report that received a 2023–2024 update vaccine dose to the proportion of HCP from our previous report that received a 2022–2023 bivalent dose, using the same difference in proportions methodology as described above. Analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

We included HCP enrolled between September 25, 2023 and April 17, 2024 and had verified enrollment testing and vaccination records (n = 987). The primary cause for HCP exclusion was discordant vaccination self-report and verified vaccines (n = 78, 7.9 %). A complete list of excluded HCP is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. We included 887 HCP for this analysis. A total of 494 HCP were COVID-19 positive, of which 83 (16.8 %) received a 2023–2024 vaccine dose. Among COVID-19 negative HCP (n = 393), 105 (26.7 %) received a 2023–2024 vaccine dose (Supplementary Fig. 1). Of the COVID-19 positive HCP, 33 (6.7 %) reported on their six-week follow-up survey that a known recent infection affected their ability to receive a vaccine. Differences in characteristics between HCP COVID-19 status are provided in Supplementary Table 2. The median time between enrollment SARS-CoV-2 testing and six-week follow-up survey completion was 42 days (interquartile range = 36 to 48 days). Among included HCP, 851 (95.9 %) received at least one vaccine dose prior to September 25, 2023 and 801 (90.3 %) completed their initial vaccination series between September 2020 and July 2022. Of the 188 HCP who received a 2023–2024 updated vaccine dose, 174 (92.6 %) had completed an initial vaccine series.

HCP characteristics associated with updated vaccine dose receipt

A greater proportion of HCP who chose to receive a 2023–2024 updated vaccine dose were COVID-19 negative at enrollment compared to those who chose not to receive the updated vaccine (55.9 % vaccine receipt vs. 41.2 % did not receive vaccine, 14.7 percentage point difference [PPD], 95 % confidence interval [95 %CI] 6.3 to 23.0). A higher proportion HCP who received an updated vaccine dose hold a graduate or doctoral degree (61.7 % vs. 29.8 %, 32.0 PPD, 95 %CI 23.9 to 40.0) and reported as a physician or APP (22.3 % vs. 11.4 %,10.9 PPD, 95 %CI 4.2 to 17.6), compared to HCP who did not receive a vaccine dose. HCP who did not receive an updated vaccine dose were more likely to identify as black, non-Hispanic (8.9 % vs. 3.2 %, −5.7 PPD, 95 % CI -9.3 to −2.1) compared to HCP with updated vaccine receipt. There was no difference in vaccination status based on age category or sex. Complete comparisons are provided in Table 1.

Our multivariable model found that compared to HCP who have some college or high school education, HCP who held an undergraduate or technical degree (aOR 3.75 [1.97–7.12]) and those who held a graduate or doctoral degree (aOR 7.73 [4.10–14.55]) had higher odds of receiving an updated vaccine dose. HCP between 50 and 64 years old (aOR 2.02 [1.11–3.67]) had higher odds of vaccine dose receipt compared to those 18 to 29 years old. Compared to HCP classified as other staff, nurses and nurse assistants had lower odds of vaccine receipt (aOR 0.48 [0.30–0.76]). We found no significant difference in vaccine receipt by sex, race, or ethnicity in the multivariable model. No significant collinearity was detected in our multivariable model. Complete bivariate and multivariable model results are provided in Table 1.

Our sensitivity analysis of HCP without COVID-19 yielded comparable results, with the exception that, HCP aged 40 to 49 years (aOR 2.58, 95 % CI [1.13–5.88]) had higher odds of vaccine receipt compared to those aged 18 to 29 years. Complete sensitivity analysis results are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Reported factors important for influencing HCP decision to receive an updated vaccine dose

A higher proportion of HCP who received an updated vaccine dose reported their decision was influenced by information from primary medical literature (56.4 % vs. 28.3 %, 28.1 PPD, 95 %CI 19.9 to 36.2), employers (53.2 % vs. 30.8 %, 22.4 PPD, 95 %CI 14.2 to 30.7), family and friends (36.2 % vs. 17.0 %, 19.2 PPD, 95 %CI 11.5 to 26.9), and government leaders (27.1 % vs. 12.2 %, 15.0 PPD, 95 %CI 7.8 to 22.1) compared to those who did not (Fig. 1). Few HCP, regardless of vaccination status, cited social media (5.6 %) as having an important influence over their vaccination decision.

Personal concerns of SARS-CoV-2 infection was an important consideration for HCP with updated vaccine receipt compared to those who did not (89.9 % vs. 44.6 %, 45.3 PPD, 95 %CI 39.3 to 51.3). Similarly, concerns about virus transmission to family members (96.3 % vs. 56.0 %, 40.3 PPD, 95 %CI 35.4 to 45.4) and in the workplace (92.6 % vs. 54.5 %, 38.1 PPD, 95 %CI 32.5 to 43.7) were important considerations for HCP who received an updated vaccine dose compared to those who did not. Vaccine effectiveness (92.6 % vs. 63.7.%, 28.9 PPD, 95 %CI 23.4 to 34.4), safety (83.5 % vs. 59.4 %, 29.5 PPD, 95 %CI 23.3 to 35.6), availability (63.3 % vs. 18.9 %, 44.4 PPD, 95 %CI 36.6 to 52.2), and cost (29.3 % vs. 7.9 %, 21.4 PPD, 95 %CI 14.3 to 28.5) were also important factors influencing the decision to receive an updated vaccine among HCP (Fig. 1, Table 2).

Comparing decision to 2022–2023 bivalent vaccination vs 2023–2024 monovalent vaccination

Compared with HCP who received the 2022–2023 bivalent vaccine (from our previous report (Mohr et al., 2023b)), those who received an updated 2023–2024 monovalent vaccine dose were more likely to report infection concerns (89.9 % vs. 84.0 %, 5.9 PPD, 95 %CI 0.1 to 11.7) and concern about transmission to a family member (96.3 % vs. 89.2 %, 7.2 PPD, 95 %CI 2.9 to 11.4) as important considerations in their decision (Table 3). Availability (63.3 % vs. 50.9 %, 12.5 PPD, 95 %CI 3.8 to 21.1) and vaccine cost (29.3 % vs. 19.8 %, 9.5 PPD, 95 %CI 1.7 to 17.3) were reported more frequently as important factors among HCP with 2023–2024 vaccine receipt compared to HCP with 2022–2023 vaccine receipt.

Discussion

Our results show HCP who were older and had more education were associated with increased odds of vaccine receipt and that primary medical literature and HCP employer-based messaging were influential sources of information for HCP who received a 2023–2024 updated vaccine dose. We found that vaccine effectiveness, safety, and HCP personal infection and transmission concerns impacted HCP's decision to receive a vaccine dose in 2023–2024. Risk of adverse side effects was an important factor influencing HCP who did not receive an updated vaccine dose. Our results highlight that HCP consider a variety of informational, practical, and interpersonal factors regarding their decision to receive a vaccine.

Previous studies have found socioeconomic disparities in vaccination uptake (Bruxvoort et al., 2025; Farah et al., 2023; Green-McKenzie et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2022). A recent study among U.S. adults found that individuals who were older, had higher socioeconomic status, or had comorbidities were more likely to receive a XBB COVID-19 vaccine (Bruxvoort et al., 2025). Among HCP it was previously reported that older, non-Hispanic Caucasians are more likely to receive updated vaccine doses (Farah et al., 2023). We did not find significant associations between vaccine receipt and race or oldest age group. However, we found that higher educational level among HCP was associated with increased uptake. Like our findings, previous studies have shown increased vaccine hesitancy among nurses at healthcare facilities (Baniak et al., 2021; Browne et al., 2022). Our work underscores the importance of focused engagement with individuals from diverse education backgrounds, job roles, and racial and ethnic groups to improve vaccine uptake.

Previous studies among U.S. adults found confidence in vaccine safety and concerns of infection was associated with increased uptake of the 2022–2023 COVID-19 booster vaccine (Lu et al., 2023; Razzaghi et al., 2022). We found similar results in our previous report among HCP who received a bivalent booster in 2022–2023 (Mohr et al., 2023b). Our current results show these considerations remained important to HCP 2023–2024 vaccine dose. However, despite concerns of individual infection or transmission to others overall vaccine coverage has reduced among both the general and HCP populations (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024).

As shown in a past review, convenient locations and short wait times to receive a vaccine can positively impact vaccine uptake (Truong et al., 2022). Another study found that a mobile immunization cart that came to HCP during their work-hours increased influenza vaccination uptake (Kung, 2014). In both our current and previous report (Mohr et al., 2023b) vaccine availability has remained an important factor in HCP vaccination decision making process. Further, maintaining low vaccine costs should be a goal of public health officials as they strive to increase HCP vaccine uptake. Multimodal educational interventions that include more than peer-reviewed literature aimed at enhancing understanding of vaccine safety, efficacy, and transmission reduction may improve vaccine uptake (Singh et al., 2022). Vaccine mandates have also been shown to increase vaccination rates (Bednarczyk et al., 2019; Green-McKenzie et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2014). Vaccination mandates changed or were completely removed from most of included sites (list of vaccine mandates and messaging in Supplementary Table 5), with every site no longer requiring a vaccination for 2023–2024. Prior to 2023, many of the sites required employees to be vaccinated (receipt of first two doses) but also sent out reminders and this likely impacted vaccine uptake. As public health officials and employers strive to increase vaccine uptake providing convenient locations, reduced wait times, low vaccine costs, mobile vaccination carts within facilities, and scheduling or appointment reminders could all help improve HCP vaccine uptake.

Public health organizations have increased their use of social media to reach public health goals. An evaluation of the We Can Do This COVID-19 public education campaign found that digital media ads were associated with increased vaccine uptake among their sampled population of US adults (Denison et al., 2023). Alternatively, we found that HCP seldom reported information from social media to influence their vaccination decision. This was also consistent with our previous report (Mohr et al., 2023b), suggesting that future HCP-tailored vaccine messaging would be better delivered through other platforms.

Our report has multiple limitations. First, this was a case-control study, and measuring exposure prevalence is difficult. We acknowledge the potential for collider bias, our findings suggest a low risk of this bias influencing our results as we did identify age being associated with cases, there was not a corresponding decrease of vaccine uptake by age. Further, we performed sensitivity analyses that only included COVID-19 negative HCP that produced similar results. Second, included HCP volunteered to participate in a vaccine effectiveness study and may not be fully representative of all HCP. Third, data on factors influencing vaccine decisions were collected retrospectively, and individuals may have had recall bias for their motivations or influencing factors for receiving the updated COVID-19 vaccine. Lastly, HCP enrolled into the parent study were likely to be experiencing symptoms, thus their opinions on vaccination may be influenced by a recent illness. Despite these limitations, we measured validated vaccine uptake rather than HCP vaccination intent. We also included HCP from 16 states with a wide range of patient contact and roles. These strengths in tandem with our findings closely aligning with previously observed trends gives us confidence in our results.

In conclusion, our findings highlight the persistence of key factors that influence HCP vaccination decisions. Notably, concerns of SARS-CoV-2 infection and transmission to family or friends are important in vaccination decisions. Vaccine availability was an important influential factor of HCP vaccine uptake. Future efforts to enhance HCP vaccination uptake would benefit from high vaccine availability and convenience; addressing vaccine uptake disparities for individuals from diverse educational backgrounds, job roles, or racial and ethnic populations; alleviating concerns of adverse vaccine side effects and implementing multiple educational strategies outside of peer-reviewed medical literature.
