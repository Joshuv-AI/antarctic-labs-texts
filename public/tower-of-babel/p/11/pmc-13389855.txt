Synergistic bimetallic Fe–Cu@hydrochar composite for efficient pharmaceutical removal via coupled adsorption and Fenton-like catalysis
Authors: Maria Aslam, Zia Ul Haq Khan, Mahmood M. S. Abdullah, Amjad Khan, Fida Ullah, Muhammad Idrees
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Azithromycin (AZM), a commonly prescribed macrolide antibiotic, is an environmental contaminant that necessitates efficient removal methods. In this research, the synthesis of a novel Fe–Cu/hydrochar nanocomposite (Fe–Cu/HC) and its utilization for the removal of AZM through adsorption and a modified Fenton reaction have been investigated. The structure of the composite was characterized using FTIR, XRD, SEM, EDX, TEM, particle size distribution, zeta potential analysis, electrochemical polarization, and UV-DRS spectroscopy, which confirmed the successful loading of the Fe–Cu compounds onto the hydrochar support. The experimental conditions were optimized with respect to the pH, catalyst amount, concentration, temperature, and hydrogen peroxide concentration. A removal efficiency of ∼99% was achieved for the Fe–Cu/HC composite under optimal conditions, and an efficiency of 82–85% was observed even after five cycles, suggesting that the composite is stable. A comparison of both calcined and non-calcined composites showed that the calcined composite outperformed the non-calcined counterpart. Kinetics experiments compared pseudo 1st order and pseudo 2nd order models, with the pseudo 2nd order model giving the best fit, whereas for the equilibrium adsorption experiments, the Langmuir and Freundlich models were examined, and the Langmuir model gave the best fit.

Introduction

Antibiotics are extensively used in treating bacterial infections in both animals and human beings through their biologically active antibacterial and antiparasitic properties.1 Antibiotics have been found in various environmental settings, including hospital and municipal effluents, surface and ground water, seawater, and others, at levels ranging from a few nanograms per liter to hundreds of micrograms per liter. This results in an increased health hazard in the food chain, despite being present at low concentrations. Antibiotics are therefore characterized as ubiquitous or “persistent substances” in aquatic environments worldwide because of their continuous entry through various types of pollution and their resistance to decay. The consumption rate of antibiotics has increased worldwide because of economic and population growth, with global antibiotic consumption estimated to range from 100 000 to 200 000 tons annually.2 Azithromycin (AZM) is an example of a broad-spectrum antibiotic, antimalarial, antiviral pharmacological drug of the macrolide class. Since its discovery, AZM has received approval from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). This drug has also undergone intensive study in relation to malaria.3,4 Despite its usefulness, AZM has been associated with increased risk of mortality in individuals with an elevated baseline risk, but not in younger and middle-aged patients. This medication also elevates the risk of cardiovascular deaths and heart arrhythmias.5 Thus, to maintain a healthy ecosystem, there is an urgent need to develop simple and economical techniques for the removal of AZM from aquatic ecosystems. In natural conditions, the degradation of antibiotics can occur due to photo-degradation, hydrolysis, and biodegradation; however, these processes are limited. Recently, some new technologies have been developed for the elimination of antibiotics using biological, chemical, or physicochemical processes. Some of those technologies are conventional biological technologies, adsorption technologies, membrane technologies, advanced oxidation processes (AOP), constructed wetlands (CW), microalgae technologies, and microbial electrochemical processes (MFC). Each technology has its own merits and demerits.6 For example, although adsorption is an eco-friendly process, it cannot remove all the pollutants.7,8 Furthermore, the use of Fenton's reagent is a successful method for the elimination of various toxic pollutants due to the non-toxic nature of the reagents, which does not result in the formation of residues.9 Combining various methods of water treatment would be beneficial to improving water treatment efficiency and reducing the cost and time involved. The mechanisms and advantages of a combined approach, particularly in the elimination of organic contaminants, have been demonstrated.10 Advanced oxidation processes and adsorption are two environmentally friendly methods for treating wastewater. The integration of adsorption and the Fenton process gives treatment efficiencies greater than the sum of the individual performances. Degradation removal efficiency is also higher for the combined process than that for each process separately.11,12 Bimetallic nanoparticle (BMNPs) nanocomposites, which involve the combination of two different metal elements on a nanoscale level through adsorption and/or catalysis, have been very important because of their enhanced surface area, economical synthesis, greater processability, and their ability to rapidly decontaminate pharmaceutical compounds in water. Metallic nanomaterials have shown improved properties over their metal counterparts, such as distinct physicochemical characteristics, synergistic interactions, and multiple mechanisms of action.13,14 The synergistic effect, which is a result of the complexity of the structure of BMNPs, is the major differentiating factor and the reason for the popularity of BMNPs in wastewater treatment. The combination of metals at a nanoscale imparts distinct catalytic, electronic, and surface properties to BMNPs, making them highly efficient for various applications, including wastewater treatment. BMNPs have been utilized for the elimination of anti-inflammatory drugs.15,16 The success of the process relies on the selection of the right adsorbent, as selecting the wrong adsorbent can result in low adsorption capacity, high cost, and a lack of scalability. Hydrochar has proved to be highly efficient as an adsorbent for the degradation of heavy metals from wastewater. Hydrochar is an environmentally friendly material that is produced by the hydrothermal and thermochemical degradation of biomass. The uniqueness of hydrochar over biochar is that it does not need a slow pyrolysis process.17 A moderate temperature of 200 to 400 °C and a high pressure of about 10 to 25 MPa in an aqueous environment during the hydrothermal liquefaction process make hydrocarbons or bio-crude oil, which is the main liquid product.18 Various approaches have been adopted for the photocatalytic degradation of pharmaceutical pollutants.19 Thus, the combination of an eco-friendly and bimetallically modified hydrochar adsorbent with efficient adsorption capacity can be used effectively for the highly sustainable purification of wastewater. A high-performance bimetallic nanocomposite can be added to the hydrochar to enhance the surface area and adsorption sites for the removal of pharmaceuticals.20 Magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) are one of the most significant types of nanoparticles due to their wide applications as adsorbents and have succeeded in removing between 80% and 99.9% of contaminants from polluted water.21 Recently, magnetic nanobiocomposites have gained significant recognition because of their high efficiency of removal.22 Due to its biodegradability, low cost and low toxicity, chitosan can be used for treating water through various techniques, such as adsorptive interactions, metal complexation, precipitation and ion exchange processes. Chitosan nanospheres prepared using bionanocomposites have successfully been used as an efficient approach for eliminating contaminants from aqueous systems.23 The loading of nanoparticles of zero-valent zinc (nZVZ) onto hydrochar produced from pistachio pulp and the stabilization of the nanoparticles with sodium citrate (SC) have been used as an effective approach for the adsorption of cadmium from aqueous solutions, achieving removal efficiencies of 73–92%.24 The use of AuNPs-hydrochar in the catalytic degradation of cephalexin (CPL) showed a nearly 99% degree of degradation under UV light using the optimum reaction conditions with a catalytic efficiency of 77% after five recycling runs.25 Activated carbon (AC), along with natural adsorbents such as wood and keratin, has been explored for the separation of organic pollutants. This has limitations at the laboratory scale, and its implications for large-scale use has yet to be fully explored. In contrast, there is a need for new knowledge on the interaction of nano-based adsorbents with pollutant moieties. The low toxicity of the adsorbents shows that nanomaterials are useful for the treatment of pollutants.26 Magnetic nanohybrid materials can be enhanced as remediation tools for more sustainable approaches to environmental remediation. Magnetic nanoparticles also show promising efficiency in the removal of contaminants due to their magnetic retrievability. Such advantages not only improve the usability of magnetic nanoparticles but also make them more sustainable.27 In this article, hydrochar was produced from waste almond shells through mild hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) conditions with the rational incorporation of Fe and Cu metals to form a bimetallic nanocomposite hydrochar intended for the adsorption-based removal of organic pollutants, followed by their oxidation-mediated degradation to ensure their complete elimination.28 A search of the retrospective literature confirms that no prior work has taken place on the synthesis of an Fe–Cu@HC composite, whilst the previously reported degradation of ampicillin via a combined adsorption-Fenton process supports the efficacy of such a system.29 This study aims to develop a Fe–Cu hydrochar composite using a chemical reduction technique by reducing Fe and Cu sources on hydrochar derived from almond shells. The synthesized composite will be applied to the adsorptive removal and Fenton-assisted oxidative degradation of azithromycin from aqueous media. The nanocomposite is analyzed using advanced techniques such as XRD, FTIR, PSA, SEM, TEM, and ZP. Further, the effects of various reaction parameters like pH value, the amount of catalyst, the concentration of azithromycin, and temperature on the synthesis of the nanocomposite were also studied. Furthermore, the effect of H2O2 and the cycling stability were also investigated. Based on an Fe–Cu-mediated Fenton reaction and reported degradation pathways of azithromycin, an oxidative degradation pathway was proposed.

Materials and chemicals

Reagents

The chemicals azithromycin, ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), copper(ii) nitrate trihydrate (Cu(NO3)2·3H2O), sodium borohydride (NaBH4), ethanol (C2H5OH), ascorbic acid, hydrochloric acid (HCl), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), EDTA, biochar, and distilled water (DW) were obtained from Merck, Pakistan.

Methodology

Synthesis of hydrochar (HC)

Almond plants are cultivated in tropical, subtropical and temperate regions. The almond shells biomass (15 mL) was obtained from local sources and thoroughly cleaned several times with DW to eliminate dust and surface impurities. It was then subjected to disintegration and drying processes. The drying of the biomass was carried out in an oven at 100 °C for 24 h, followed by crushing to get a particle size below 200 µm.30 4 g of NaOH was used to make a 100 mL solution. Then, 50 mL of a NaOH solution was mixed with the dried material, and it was filtered and placed in a Teflon-lined autoclave for 6 h at about 200 °C. After removal, the material was soaked in the remaining 50 mL of NaOH solution overnight before filtering again and drying in an oven for 6 h under mild temperature conditions at about 140 °C. Hydrochar was formed through this hydrothermal synthesis process and was ready to use for further processing (Fig. 1).

Preparation of the almond hydrochar.

Synthesis of the Fe–Cu@HC nanocomposite

The Fe–Cu hybrid composite was synthesized by preparing separate iron and copper precursor solutions followed by chemical reduction using NaBH4 as illustrated in Fig. 2. Initially, the iron precursor (FeCl3·6H2O) weighing 8.5 g was dissolved in 200 mL of DW to make the iron solution, while 7.6 g of Cu (NO3)2·3H2O was dissolved in 80 mL of DW to prepare the copper solution, resulting in an iron to copper molar ratio of about 1 : 1. Afterwards, the Cu solution was dropped into the Fe solution gradually while the mixture was stirred vigorously and allowed to stir for approximately 15–20 minutes to ensure complete homogenization. An aqueous NaBH4 solution was prepared by dissolving in 250 mL of chilled distilled water. This freshly prepared NaBH4 solution was introduced gradually in a dropwise manner to the solution containing the metal salts. This addition was carried out slowly to control the reaction and prevent excessive foaming. The Fe–Cu precursor solution was brought to pH 8.0 before reduction. Upon addition of NaBH4, the pH increased to approximately 10.5. Following the complete addition of NaBH4, the reaction mixture was stirred continuously for 1 h, during which a black precipitate characteristic of the Fe–Cu hybrid was observed. Following reduction, the reaction mixture was sonicated intermittently for 15–20 minutes to improve dispersion, while maintaining the pH of the system. The suspension was further stirred for an additional 30–60 minutes at 30–40 °C to ensure complete formation of the hybrid material. Finally, the obtained solid was subjected to centrifugation for 10–15 minutes at 6000 rpm. It was subsequently washed repeatedly with distilled water and ethanol (200 mL each). The washed product was subsequently dried in an oven at 60–80 °C. The composite was gently ground to obtain the hybrid powder for further characterization and application studies.31 To enhance the crystallinity and structural stability of the Fe–Cu nanoparticles, the dried composite was subjected to calcination at 450 °C for 2 h.

Preparation of the Fe–Cu@HC composite.

Materials characterization

The physicochemical properties of the synthesized Fe–Cu@HC were systematically characterized to evaluate its suitability for azithromycin degradation. The FTIR analysis was conducted over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 to identify the surface functional groups contributing to the adsorption and catalytic performance of the hydrochar. XRD studies were conducted using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) to determine the crystalline phases and verify the incorporation of Fe and Cu species into the hydrochar matrix (PANalytical X'Pert PRO, Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Scanning electron microscopy was utilized for analysis of the surface structure and morphological characteristics of the synthesized material, while the dispersion of the Fe and Cu particles on the hydrochar surface was investigated through TEM (JEM-1011) analysis. The elemental composition and successful incorporation of the metals were confirmed through EDX analysis (JSM-6380 LA, Japan). Particle size analysis was performed using a Nano Plus system based on a dynamic light scattering method. The removal efficiency of azithromycin was followed by using a UV-visible spectrophotometer based on the variable concentrations at the wavelength corresponding to the maximum absorbance (λmax). The catalytic performance of the Fe–Cu hydrochar in a heterogeneous Fenton process using hydrogen peroxide as the oxidant was used to study its oxidative degradation ability.

Batch adsorption experiment

Accordingly, an AZM solution was prepared in a stock concentration of 1 g L−1 by dissolving a precisely weighed quantity of AZM using ultrapure water obtained from a Milli-Q purification system (18.2 MΩ cm). Then, the solution was further diluted to working solutions in a concentration range of about 10 to 100 mg L−1 for sorption studies. In the batch adsorption experiments, 50 mg of the synthesized Fe–Cu@HC composite was added to 100 mL of AZM solution under optimized operating conditions. The methodical investigation on the optimum adsorption was conducted by varying key factors such as the initial AZM concentration, from 10–100 mg L−1, the pH of the solution, which ranged from 4 to 11, the contact time, from 0 to 120 minutes, and the temperature, from 25 to 40 °C. Ultrasonication was employed to improve the dispersion of the Fe–Cu@HC composite, along with facilitating mass transfer from the AZM molecules to the adsorbent surface. Results and discussion

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was used to assess the surface functional groups in both the HC and the synthesized nanocomposite. The FTIR spectra of the synthesized hydrochar and Fe–Cu@HC were obtained in a scan range of 4000–500 cm−1, as shown in Fig. 3. A broad absorption band observed in the 3300–3500 cm−1 region for both samples is attributed to the OH stretching vibration of surface (–OH) groups, indicating the presence of hydrogen-bonded –OH functionalities in hydrochar. The absorption peak centered at 2900–2930 cm−1 in the HC spectrum is because of carbon–hydrogen (C–H) stretching in the aliphatic structures originating from the biomass precursors. XRD analysis

The XRD pattern of the Fe–Cu@HC composite exhibits sharp, distinct peaks, indicating the formation of binary metal oxide entities over the hydrochar. Fig. 4a shows that the XRD spectrum for hydrochar (HC) displays a wide diffraction peak that indicates the presence of amorphous carbonaceous material. This is used as a reference for comparison with the Fe–Cu/HC composite. The diffractogram in Fig. 4b shows major peaks at 2θ values of 30.06, 31.62, 35.50, 43.15, 45.51, 57.05, and 62.56 degrees. These identifiable peaks represent a combination of Fe and Cu oxides present over the hydrochar. In particular, the peaks observed at 30.06, 35.50, 43.15, 57.05, and 62.56 are in good agreement with the typical peaks of Fe2O3/Fe3O4, as stated in JCPDS cards 33-0664 and 19-0629. Meanwhile, the diffraction peaks at 31.62° and 45.51° correspond to CuO and Cu2O, respectively, according to JCPDS cards 48-1548 and 05-0667. The remaining peaks are attributed to iron oxide phases (Fe2O3/Fe3O4), consistent with JCPDS cards 33-0664 and 19-0629. This confirms that the well-defined peaks signify that the Fe–Cu oxides on the surface of the hydrochar are semi-crystalline in nature. According to the results obtained through XRD analysis, the prepared bimetallic Fe–Cu nanoparticles consist of an iron phase and copper phase. Multi-point analysis indicates that the surface area value of the synthesized bimetallic iron and copper nanoparticles ranges between 44.02 m2 g−1 and 55.247 m2 g−1.35 In summary, the hydrothermal impregnation of Fe and Cu precursors on hydrochar generates bimetallic oxide crystals that are expected to accelerate the breakdown of pharmaceutical pollution through catalytic reaction. The mean crystallite size for the Fe–Cu/HC composite was calculated with the use of the Debye–Scherrer formula, as shown in eqn (7), using the FWHM, the β value of the particular diffraction peak, which is about 23.8 nm, indicating the nanocrystalline nature of the Fe–Cu oxide crystallites supported on hydrochar. EDX analysis

EDX was employed to identify the basic components of the combined materials. Fig. 6A depicts an EDX spectrum of pristine hydrochar (HC), where the strong peaks for carbon and oxygen dominate. It can be interpreted that HC is mainly composed of carbonaceous components with oxygen-containing functional groups, both of which were derived from biomass precursors. In addition, minor traces of inorganic elements that may have originated from residual ash content present in the raw biomass are detected. The synthesis of spherical nanoparticles is consistent with literature sources, where the same nanoparticles have been synthesized by a different method. Furthermore, Fig. 6B illustrates an EDX spectrum of the Fe–Cu@HC nanocomposite. Apart from the C and O peaks from the hydrochar, characteristic peaks for iron (Fe) and copper (Cu) are also distinctly observed, thus showing that the metal compounds have been successfully incorporated into the hydrochar surface. The Fe : Cu atomic ratio measured by EDX was different from that in the starting material. This is because of the sensitivity of EDX towards the surface, and therefore, the local elemental concentration and the efficiency of deposition of Fe and Cu on the hydrochar. Apart from the prominent peaks of C, O, Fe, and Cu, some traces of Na and Cl were also observed. The existence of these impurities could be due to the formation of some salts of the precursors. This fact has been considered. Thus, the EDX analysis is able to prove that Fe and Cu are successfully doped onto the surface of the hydrochar. The existence of some traces of Na and Cl elements and the surface ratio of Fe–Cu versus the precursor are considered as limitations of the EDX analysis.

The TEM image in Fig. 7A proves that the pure hydrochar is homogeneous and nonporous in the spherical cross-section, while the pyrolysis process causes porosity in the form of different pores. Nanoparticles with a well-defined crystal structure are convenient for calibration purposes because of their approximate nanoscale dimensions.41

Particle size analysis (PSA)

Particle size is a critical parameter that affects the physicochemical properties as well as the adsorption behavior of Fe–Cu/HC composite materials. Particle size determination was performed using the dynamic light scattering technique. The size and distribution of nanoparticles can be measured using several commercially available instruments. These instruments can either analyze or measure suspensions.42 The maximum value of the intensity distribution in Fig. 8a is found to be about 950 nm, with an approximate deviation of ±50 nm. In the intensity distribution, most of the dominant particles are large in size because of their strong light-scattering power. The average hydrodynamic size measured using the DLS method (approximately 900–1000 nm) is significantly higher compared to the size of the crystallites determined according to the Scherrer formula (23.8 nm). Such a discrepancy between both values is caused by the fact that while DLS determines the hydrodynamic size of particle aggregates in a suspension, the Scherrer formula determines the size of separate crystalline structures. Thus, the high value measured by the DLS method confirms the existence of aggregated crystallites. The volume distribution curve in Fig. 8b revealed a particle size of around 1000 nm, with a standard deviation of approximately ±80 nm. Thus, the contribution of submicron/micron-sized particles towards the total volume fraction appears to be considerable. The narrower distribution indicates a smaller effect from large aggregates in comparison to the intensity-based distribution. The particle size corresponding to the number distribution curve in Fig. 8c is 900 nm with a standard deviation of around ±60 nm. It should be understood that this number distribution is due to the presence of small-sized particles, as it is a number distribution and not a volume or mass distribution. Further confirmation of the polydispersity in the system can be seen in the graph of the autocorrelation function in Fig. 8d, in which a plot of ln(G2(T) − 1) vs. τ shows a gradually decaying curve. The small oscillations observed at higher values of the time lag indicate that there is a moderate degree of polydispersity. It is not an easy task to quantify changes in particle sizes and distributions in vivo because of several issues associated with the ability to detect low levels in complex matrices or contamination caused by staining methods.43 The results from the DLS study show that the Fe–Cu/HC nanoparticles aggregate into clusters in an aqueous environment, in agreement with observations made during SEM analysis. The information obtained from the DLS technique primarily represents the hydrodynamic size of the particles.

Zeta potential

The zeta potential (ZP) is a measure that gives the electrochemical equilibrium of particles in liquid solutions, for instance, nanoparticle (NP) colloidal suspensions, which have numerous uses in health care, drug manufacturing, chemical industries, mining, and wastewater treatment.44 The zeta potential value determined for Fe–Cu/HC from Fig. 9 is estimated to be approximately −22.5 mV with a standard deviation of ±3.5 mV. The existence of a negative charge on the surfaces of the particles means that the material is relatively stable owing to electrostatic repulsion between particles, which thereby minimizes agglomeration. The zeta potential value obtained falls in the range of −30 mV to −10 mV, indicating a moderately stable colloid. This shows that Fe–Cu/HC is moderately stable in aqueous solution and thus may be applied in adsorption studies.

Electrochemical polarization curve analysis

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is based on measuring the electrical response of an electrochemical system to a small alternating current (AC) potential or current applied over a wide range of frequencies. Thus, EIS is an ideal method for studying electrochemical systems in which the rates of the underlying electrochemical reactions vary by various orders of magnitude.45Fig. 10A denotes the polarization curve at 1000 Hz, which is even and symmetrical, with clearly defined anodic and cathodic regions. The electrochemical stability is confirmed by a moderate shift in potential relative to the current density. Fig. 10B shows the polarization curve at 2000 Hz in which the curves have a minor shift compared to Fig. 10A. The curve retains its shape but exhibits a wider response. This implies that there is a change in the electrochemical reaction of the substance, thus indicating improved interaction between the electrode and electrolyte. Fig. 10C shows the polarization curve at 3000 Hz, in which the curves are less smooth and scattered, possibly because the heterogeneous surface or greater active sites leads to greater fluctuation, which results in stronger electrochemical reactions. The polarization curves for Fe–Cu@HC at various conditions (1000, 2000, and 3000 Hz) follow the characteristics of the anodic and cathodic curves. The slow variation in shape and increase in fluctuations from 1000 to 3000 Hz suggest alterations in the surface nature and increased electrochemical reactions, respectively, which can be beneficial for obtaining better results in adsorption and Fenton-based processes.

Effect of pH

pH is an essential parameter that influences the effectiveness of the Fe–Cu/HC composite in removing pharmaceutical contaminants via adsorption and degradation. Degradation experiments were carried out at various pH values of 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11 with all other conditions remaining constant. Fig. 12a shows the effect of pH on the AZM removal process within the pH range of 3 to 11. As depicted in the graph, the efficiency of the degradation process is greatly dependent upon the pH value. The minimum values of the ratio of C/C0 were found at pH 3, which denotes that it is the point of maximum degradation efficiency, and conversely, the maximum values of the ratio of C/C0 were obtained at pH 11, which denotes that it is the point of minimum degradation efficiency. Efficiency decreases progressively with increasing pH (pH 3 > 5 > 7 > 9 > 11). An increase in pH made the degradation of azithromycin challenging, and acidic pH favored the degradation of azithromycin. With a decrease in pH resulting from the decreased availability of hydroxyl radicals, the process was also less efficient.48 The results indicated that acidic conditions increase the solubility of azithromycin, making it more susceptible to degradation. When the pH was 3, the azithromycin degradation efficiency reached ∼99% under optimum reaction conditions.49 Synergy between O2 activation and the catalytic decomposition of H2O2 exists. This finding is possibly attributed to the combined effect of Fe and Cu in enhancing the cycling reactions of Fe3+, Fe2+ and Cu2+, Cu+. Fe–Cu@HC dosage

The influence of catalyst dosage on the process efficiency was evaluated by altering the amount of Fe–Cu@HC from 10–50 mg L−1 while keeping all other factors constant, as shown in Fig. 12b. The result of varying the concentration of the nanocomposite from 10 to 50 mg L−1 increased the removal efficiency from 68 to 96.6%. Adsorption capacity per unit mass (qe) was found to decrease with enhanced adsorbent dosage due to the availability of excess adsorption sites. The adsorption capacity was observed to decrease due to the possibility of aggregation of the adsorbent particles by virtue of increasing the adsorbent concentration and hence reducing the surface area of the nanocomposites.52 The adsorbate dosage amount will cause an enhancement of the surface area and the availability of active adsorption sites, owing to the fact that there is an increase in the adsorbent dosage.53 Additionally, the clustering of catalyst particles on the nanoscale leads to a lower specific surface area, making the process less efficient.54

Azithromycin concentration effect

The impact of azithromycin concentration at the beginning of its removal by the Fe–Cu@HC composite was investigated by varying the pollutant concentration from 10 ppm to 50 ppm, keeping all other conditions constant. The percentage removal efficiency decreases progressively with increasing initial azithromycin concentration, as shown in Fig. 12c. This can be justified by considering the availability of adsorption sites on the surface of the composite material. At low concentrations, the quantity of available sites for adsorption would be greater compared to the azithromycin molecules existing within the solution, hence resulting in high effectiveness in adsorption and hence high removal percentage. However, at high concentrations, the drug molecules compete for the limited available adsorption sites, thereby decreasing the adsorption efficiency and, consequently, the removal percentage. Moreover, at high azithromycin concentrations, there would be limitations in mass transport and boundary layer resistance, which might hinder the migration of azithromycin molecules to the interior layers of the adsorbent material. Therefore, these findings indicate that as the concentration level of azithromycin increases, the adsorption capability of the Fe–Cu@HC composite material gradually decreases. When breakthrough occurs, the adsorbate will flow through without any treatment. However, an increase in the dose of the adsorbent will improve the performance, but at an increased cost of operation.55

Effect of temperature

The influence of temperature on the removal efficiency of azithromycin could be investigated by conducting adsorption experiments where only the temperature is changed from 25–45 °C, while all the other parameters remained constant, as shown in Fig. 12d. It was noted that the efficiency of degradation increased continuously with a rise in temperature, indicating that the process is endothermic. This can be attributed to a rise in adsorption of azithromycin due to an increase in temperature because of an increase in the mobility and diffusion rate of azithromycin, facilitating its penetration into the composite pores. The effect of increased temperature could promote the surface functional group activity of the adsorbent and drugs because of weakened interactions between the solute and solvent. On the contrary, a considerable rise in temperature above the optimized value would decrease the removal efficiency because of possible desorption or thermal disruption of surface-active sites. The findings indicate that temperature is an essential factor for determining adsorption dynamics. Therefore, optimization of temperature will be necessary to maximize the efficiency of the Fe–Cu@HC composite adsorption process. A one-dimensional numerical model that incorporates the kinetic adsorption of substances with gamma-distributed rate constants was constructed and calibrated to the experimental data. With a rise in temperature from 22/25 °C to 90 °C, the coefficient of adsorption dropped by 35, 40 and 60%.56

Effect of hydrogen peroxide

The effect of different H2O2 concentrations (300–900 ppm), corresponding to H2O2 volumes of 0.1–0.3 mL, was studied in order to establish the optimal dose of H2O2 necessary for the decomposition of azithromycin when the Fe–Cu@HC composite was used. As per the findings, the decomposition efficiency is directly proportional to an increment in the dose of H2O2, reaching the maximum level at a certain point. Such an increase could be explained by the release of very active ˙OH radicals as a result of catalysis involving iron and copper species contained in the composite. With low doses of H2O2, not enough reactive radicals are formed, so fewer azithromycin molecules could be oxidized. The removal efficiency reached approximately 27%, 35%, and 40% for 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mL, respectively, as demonstrated in Fig. 12e. Moreover, increasing the dosage beyond the optimum level resulted in a plateau in the removal efficiency because excess H2O2 scavenged ˙OH radicals, forming the less reactive radicals, thereby reducing the concentration of active radicals, as depicted in eqn (15) and (16).571516

In addition, higher doses of H2O2 might increase the competition between H2O2 and the pollutants for the active surface sites, thereby increasing the cost. Therefore, the amount of H2O2 is of great importance for the degradation process carried out by Fe–Cu@HC composite under various advanced oxidation processes (AOPs).

Comparison of the calcined and uncalcined composite with hydrochar

A comparative evaluation was conducted to study the performance variation between pristine hydrochar, Fe–Cu@HC without calcination, and Fe–Cu@HC after calcination under optimum conditions, [catalyst] = 50 mg L−1, [AZM] = 20 mg L−1, pH = 3, contact time = 120 min, temperature = 45 °C, and H2O2 volume = 0.3 mL. Pristine hydrochar exhibited a lower efficiency of removal (32%) compared to the calcined and uncalcined hydrochar, as illustrated in Fig. 12f. This poor performance indicates that hydrochar mainly acts through adsorption rather than catalysis. After adding Fe and Cu, the performance was significantly enhanced. In particular, the Fe/Cu@HC composite without calcination exhibited a very fast drop in pollutant concentration with respect to the time elapsed. It is seen that after 10 minutes, the C/C0 ratio had dropped to a value of about 0.57, while after 120 minutes, it became 0.30, which corresponded to 70% degradation. Calcination of Fe/Cu@HC gave rise to the maximum catalytic performance amongst all samples tested. This was marked by a sharp drop of C/C0 to about 0.40 during the initial 10 minutes, implying fast degradation kinetics. By 120 minutes, there was a reduction of the remaining concentration to about 0.24 or 76% degradation. The superior performance of the calcined composite could be due to positive influences brought about by calcination, such as the improvement of crystal structure, better dispersion of the Fe/Cu catalyst, creation of more surface-active sites, as well as improved interaction between the metal oxides and hydrochar support, all of which help to facilitate faster pollutant degradation. Several parameters influence the high efficiency of the process. First, the number of oxygen vacancies increases, working as electron donors in the region of the conduction band, thus increasing the quantity of separated charges. This means that there will be more active reactive centers for the reaction.58

Studying the stability and recyclability

To prove its potential utility for the treatment of wastewater, adsorption–degradation cycles were performed to investigate the stability and reusability of the Fe–Cu@HC hybrid material. After each cycle, the synthesized material was separated, washed with distilled water, and dried in an oven at 400 °C for 3 h for stability. After each cycle, the composite was separated, washed, and dried for reuse under identical operational conditions. Fig. 13a shows that with a rise in the number of cycles, the removal efficiency decreased gradually, although it still retained the majority of its original activity even after multiple reuses. Such a minor efficiency reduction can be explained by the partial loss of active metal species or partial blockage/structural change of the surface pores of the composite during repeated operations. However, in the overall performance, the stability suggests there were strong metal–support interactions and acceptable resistance to leaching and surface deterioration. Therefore, these results confirm that the Fe–Cu@HC composite shows satisfactory operational stability and recyclability, which are prerequisites for sustainable large-scale water treatment applications. Continuous recyclability without major loss in efficiency has been demonstrated, indicating economic feasibility and potential suitability for long-term pollutant removal processes.

Equilibrium adsorption model

Equilibrium adsorption was analyzed by employing the Langmuir model, as shown in Fig. 14a. The Langmuir adsorption theory is grounded on the hypothesis that the surface area of the adsorbate is homogeneous. From the work by Do (1998), the model becomes inappropriate in cases where there is non-homogeneous surface area. The Langmuir model depends solely on the pressure of the gas and applies at constant temperature.60 The Freundlich isotherm for adsorption processes is widely applied to determine the equilibrium relation between adsorbate molecules that are adsorbed per unit mass of carbon and the amount of adsorbate remaining in the solution. The Freundlich equation is valid only for deriving the relationship between the adsorption and pressure at low pressures but not for calculating the adsorption at high pressures,61 as presented in Fig. 14b. In conclusion, based on the analysis conducted using the information gathered, the Langmuir model provided the best fit when compared to the Freundlich model, because it has a higher correlation coefficient, thus indicating monolayer adsorption, as shown in Table 3.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the successful synthesis of a highly efficient and functionalized nanocomposite of a bimetallic Fe–Cu-supported hydrochar nanomaterial (Fe–Cu/HC) to effectively remove azithromycin by an integrated mechanism involving adsorption and a Fenton-like reaction. The performance of the Fe–Cu/HC nanomaterial was confirmed through various characterization techniques. From FTIR analysis, the FG (functional groups) on the Fe–Cu/HC nanomaterial (–OH, –COOH) surface were identified. XRD analysis showed the crystalline nature of the bimetallic Fe–Cu supported hydrochar. From SEM and TEM analyses, a highly porous hydrochar structure decorated with well-dispersed Fe–Cu nanoparticles was clearly observed, indicating the presence of a high number of reactive sites. Fe–Cu/HC proved to be an excellent sorbent with 99% removal of azithromycin achieved in its first cycle, with only a minor drop to 82–85% after five cycles. Adsorption was described as a second-order kinetic process, which indicates a major contribution from chemisorption, while adsorption at equilibrium was represented better by the Langmuir isotherm, suggesting homogeneous monolayer adsorption. This high efficiency can be explained by the interaction between adsorption and the Fenton oxidation reaction due to redox cycling of the Fe2+, Fe3+ and Cu+, Cu2+ ions, leading to activation of hydrogen peroxide to produce extremely active ˙OH radicals. In conclusion, the Fe–Cu@HC nanomaterial proves to be an affordable, eco-friendly, and highly efficient material for the elimination of pharmaceutical pollutants, exhibiting great potential in practical wastewater treatment applications.
