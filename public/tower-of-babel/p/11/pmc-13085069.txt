Disrupted Salivary Fungal Community in Patients With Oral Lichen Planus
Authors: Mengtong Zhang, Wengieng Xiong, Ruru Shao, Zhenyuan Wang, Zhengyu Shen, Qin Zhou, Guanhuan Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsOral lichen planus (OLP), a chronic inflammatory oral mucosal disorder with malignant potential, has been associated with oral microbial dysbiosis. While bacterial community alterations in OLP are well-documented, the fungal community architecture and ecological dynamics, particularly within salivary microbiota, remain poorly characterised.MethodsSaliva samples were collected from 30 participants and stratified into 3 cohorts: 10 healthy controls (HC), 10 reticular OLP (R-OLP) patients and 10 erosive OLP (E-OLP) patients. The fungal community profile was assessed using internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequencing, complemented by multi-dimensional analytical approaches, including diversity metrics, co-occurrence network construction and functional prediction.ResultsSignificant diversity disparities distinguished fungal communities across groups. Taxonomically, Pseudozyma and Simplicillium demonstrated reduced abundance in OLP patients versus HC, while Pyronema was exclusive to OLP cohorts. Fungal ecological networks in OLP exhibited increased density compared to HC. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis identified Pseudozyma as an HC-associated biomarker and Pyronema as an E-OLP discriminant, while predictive models highlighted Pseudozyma and Simplicillium as effective group stratifiers. FUNGuild profiling revealed predominant saprotrophic activity across all groups, with decreased pathotroph-symbiotroph guild representation in OLP patients.ConclusionsSalivary fungal communities in OLP exhibit structural and compositional alterations. Through integrated analyses of relative abundance, random forest modelling and LEfSe, this study indicated that 3 fungal taxa may be as potential biomarkers for distinguishing OLP from HC. Among these, Pseudozyma and Simplicillium showed characteristics suggesting a possible probiotic role, while Pyronema, with its detection limited to OLP and absence in HC, may be associated with the disease.Clinical relevanceThe fungal profile observed in this study, comprising the potentially beneficial taxa Pseudozyma and Simplicillium as well as the OLP-linked Pyronema, offers new directions for developing noninvasive diagnostics and microbial-targeted therapies for OLP.

Background

Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic oral mucosal disease affecting 0.5% to 2% of adults, with a higher prevalence in women.1,2 Clinically, it presents as reticular and erosive types, with the reticular form being the most common, characterised by asymptomatic whitish striae that are typically distributed bilaterally and symmetrically on the oral mucosa. The exact aetiology and pathogenesis of OLP remain unclear, which has impeded the development of etiological treatments.3,4 Recent advances in microbiome sequencing technologies have indicated that microbial dysbiosis may play a role in the development of OLP. Recently, a systematic review and meta-analysis revealed a significant association between OLP and oral fungal colonization, particularly involving Candida albicans and suggested the relevance of non-Albicans species and broader mycobiome dysbiosis. Although causality cannot be inferred from observational studies, fungal imbalance, especially in erosive OLP (E-OLP), may reflect or exacerbate mucosal disruption.5 Furthermore, research indicates that fungal infections can alter the oral microbiota and worsen symptoms, while microbial changes may also affect susceptibility to fungal infections.6, 7, 8, 9

In our previous study, we conducted a preliminary analysis of the oral mucosal fungal microbiota in OLP patients and found that compared to the control group, the fungal communities on the buccal mucosa of OLP patients exhibited reduced stability, with decreased abundance of 2 fungal genera (unclassified_Trichocomaceae and Pseudozyma).10 However, different sample types in oral microbiome studies can provide distinct information. Saliva, which nearly submerges all surfaces of oral hard and soft tissues, can capture free-floating fungi in the mouth and may also contain microbes shed from various oral surfaces, thus offering a more comprehensive overview of the oral fungal community.11 Previous research has demonstrated that the bacterial microbiota compositions in the buccal mucosa and saliva of healthy controls (HC) and OLP patients differ significantly and that the impact of different niches on microbial community composition may be greater than that of the host's health status.6 Accordingly, we elected to profile the salivary mycobiome for several reasons. Firstly, saliva integrates fungi from multiple oral niches. Secondly, its collection is noninvasive and easily repeatable. Furthermore, mucosal swabs often yield low fungal biomass and may miss planktonic or transiently adherent taxa that are more readily detectable in saliva.

To date, only 3 studies on the salivary microbiota in OLP have been published and the alterations in the oral microbiota in OLP remain to be fully elucidated.12, 13, 14 To investigate the potential association between salivary fungal alterations and OLP, we performed a comprehensive analysis of the salivary fungal profile in 20 OLP patients and 10 HC using internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) sequencing. Key fungal genera associated to OLP severity were identified using the reticulation/keratosis, erythema and ulceration (REU) scoring system and fungal-fungal correlations were further explored. Additionally, differential fungal genera were identified between groups. Our study highlights the significant role of salivary fungi in OLP, offering insights for future research and biomarker development.

Methods

Study participants

A total of 30 subjects participated in the study, divided into 3 groups: 10 HC, 10 patients with reticular OLP (R-OLP), 10 patients with E-OLP (see Additional file 1: Figure S1). This study was conducted at the Department of Oral Medicine at Shanghai Jiao Tong University's School of Stomatology and received approval from the ethics committee of the Ninth People’s Hospital, College of Stomatology, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (No. SH9H-(2016)123) and the written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. The present study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and prior to sample collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients diagnosed with OLP who visited the Department of Oral Medicine at Shanghai Jiao Tong University's School of Stomatology between September 2016 and February 2019 were included in the study. Diagnosis was made by 2 experienced oral medicine professionals and confirmed through histopathological examination. Participants were selected based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria.15 The inclusion criteria were established based on the credible set of modified World Health Organization (WHO) diagnostic criteria16 and 2 updated position papers.17,18 The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) presence of bilateral and symmetrical lesions; (b) presence of a lace-like network of slightly raised and gray-white lines (reticular type) and erosive-type lesions are only diagnosed with the presence of reticular lesions elsewhere; and (c) histopathologic characteristics of a subepithelial band-like infiltrate dominated by lymphocytes and liquefaction degeneration of basal cells. The exclusion criteria included: (a) severe systemic diseases; (b) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (c) smoking; (d) OLP treatment within the past 3 months; (e) bleeding on probing; (f) positive fungal cultures; (g) untreated dental caries or lesions; (h) salivary gland diseases; (i) use of prescription medications such as antibiotics or glucocorticoids within the past 3 months. Figure S1 in the additional file outlines the participant selection process and group allocation process (see Additional file 1: Figure S1).

Clinical assessment and sample collection

The REU scoring system described previously was employed to assess clinical manifestations by segmenting the oral cavity into ten distinct regions.19 At each site, the severity of lesions was evaluated based on the presence of reticular/hyperkeratotic/white popular (R) lesions, the extent of erosive/erythematous (E) lesions and the presence of ulcerative (U) lesions. The final weighted REU score was computed as a weighted sum of the reticular score, the erythematous score (weighted 1.5 times) and the ulcerative score (weighted 2.0 times) across all ten regions. Representative clinical and histopathological features are shown in Figure S2 (see Additional file 1: Figure S2).

Before sample collection, participants were instructed to avoid eating or drinking for at least 2 hours. Approximately 3 mL of whole unstimulated saliva (WUS) was collected from each participant in a sterile, DNA-free conical tube between 8:00 and 11:00 am, following a standardised protocol as previously described.20 The samples were transported to the laboratory on ice within 2 hours of collection and stored at −80 °C until further processing, in accordance with approved guidelines.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification and pyrosequencing analysis

Total genomic DNA was isolated from salivary samples using the E.Z.N.A. Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). To ensure efficient lysis of fungal cells, the protocol included a mechanical disruption step involving vortexing with sterile steel beads. Following amplification, the resulting amplicons were separated via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and further cleaned using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA). The quantity of the purified amplicons was determined using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Sequencing data analysis

The ITS region of fungal DNA was amplified using the primers ITS1 (TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG) and ITS4 (TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC), yielding an expected product size of 600 bp (excluding the primers). Library preparation and sequencing were performed by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co. Ltd. on an Illumina MiSeq system. To mitigate potential batch effects, all samples were processed in parallel throughout the DNA extraction, PCR amplification and library construction steps. Additionally, sample order was randomised during library pooling prior to the single sequencing run. Unique barcode sequences were incorporated into the primers for sample multiplexing. To differentiate samples in the sequencing pool, barcode sequence tags were incorporated into the universal primers. To ensure high-quality data for downstream analysis, the raw sequencing reads underwent quality control processing using Prinseq (v0.20.4), which involved the removal of adapter sequences and low-quality sequences. Chimeric sequences were identified and eliminated using UCHIME (v4.2.40), resulting in a set of filtered sequences. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at a 97% similarity threshold using Usearch (v5.2.236). The taxonomic classification of fungal sequences was performed using the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) Classifier with a bootstrap cutoff of 80%.

Biological and statistical analysis

The GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software (GraphPad Software LLC, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for analysing the clinical parameters. Qualified reads were dereplicated, sorted and clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTU) based on 97% sequence identity using Mothur 1.30.1. The RDP classifier 2.12 was utilised to assign taxonomy to representative OTU. Alpha diversity metrics, including Shannon and Simpson indexes, were calculated to assess community richness in both the healthy control and OLP groups using the 'vegan' package in R, as previously described.21 A nonparametric statistical test (Wilcoxon test) was performed to evaluate the differences in alpha diversity and taxonomy among different groups. Principal component analysis (PCA), nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) were conducted based on the species taxonomic units and sample abundance matrix to reflect the distance between samples through dimensionality reduction. The Unifrac algorithm was used to calculate the sample distance. Then, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe 1.1.0) was employed to identify the biomarkers that contributed most to the differences between the healthy group, the reticular OLP group and the erosive OLP group, with an LDA score > 3.5, P < .05. The interactions within the fungal community were evaluated using sparse correlations for compositional counts (SparCC 1.0.0),22 and the differences were tested using the Kruskal–Wallis rank test. Taxonomic units absent in at least 30% of the samples across groups were removed and the co-occurrence patterns with a SparCC correlation coefficient ≥ 0.5 and P < .05 were extracted and plotted. Gephi (v0.10.1) was used to visualize the co-occurrence network. In these networks, OTUs are represented by nodes and correlations are represented by links between them, with red indicating positive relationships and green indicating negative relationships. The topological properties of each node in the networks are represented by degree (the number of adjacent edges) and betweenness centrality (the number of shortest paths passing through a node). High mean degree and low betweenness centrality can be used together to infer keystone taxa with 85% accuracy in co-occurrence networks.23, 24, 25 Accordingly, the hub OTUs of each network were defined as the top 40% for degree and closeness centrality and the bottom 40% for betweenness centrality. To reflect the strength of synergistic or antagonistic relationships within the microbial community and the stability of the entire microbial community, negative and positive cohesion and connectedness were calculated as previously described.26 This approach is based on the cohesion framework proposed by Herren and was subsequently applied to compute the negative-to-positive cohesion ratio in a later study.26,27 Briefly, a null model (taxon shuffling, 200 iterations) was applied to correct the observed correlation matrix, removing spurious associations to produce a corrected matrix (Corrcorrected). For each taxon (i), its positive connectedness was defined as the mean of all positive corrected correlations with other taxa and its negative connectedness as the mean of all negative corrected correlations. For each sample (k), its positive cohesion (Ck+) and negative cohesion (Ck−) were calculated as the weighted sum of the relative abundances of all constituent taxa multiplied by their respective connectedness values (ai,k). From these results, 2 key stability ratios were derived: the negative-to-positive connectedness ratio and the negative-to-positive cohesion ratio. The correlations between the microbial communities of E-OLP and R-OLP at the genus level and the REU score were determined using Spearman correlation. Analysing ITS amplicons of OTUs present in at least 60% of samples using FunGuild can effectively identify, quantify fungal species in environmental samples and classify them taxonomically and ecologically. It calculates fungal abundance in trophic mode, guild, growth morphology from annotated databases and visualizes functional abundance differences among groups via heatmaps and bar charts. To find salivary biomarkers differentiating healthy individuals from R-OLP and E-OLP patients, we analysed OTUs with over 60% prevalence using a random forest classifier. Recursive feature elimination (RFE) with 5-fold cross-validation (CV) was applied to identify OTUs that remained stable across multiple CV folds. Model performance was evaluated via 5-fold nested CV, where the outer loop assessed performance and the inner loop performed feature selection and hyperparameter tuning. The random forest was configured with 500 trees and a minimum node size of 3. For each fold, accuracy, area under the curve (AUC) and the confusion matrix were calculated and misclassified samples were tallied. Feature importance was ranked according to Mean Decrease Accuracy and Mean Decrease Gini and the top 10 features were visualised. To further assess overfitting, the training set (out-of-bag accuracy, OOB) and CV accuracy were compared.

Using the Illumina MiSeq platform, a total of 975,722 raw paired-end reads targeting the ITS region were generated. After merging forward and reverse reads and applying quality filtering, 972,403 merged sequences with an average length of 242.90 bp were obtained across all 30 samples. These sequences were further processed using the UPARSE pipeline, which included quality trimming, dereplication, clustering and chimera removal. A total of 3667 OTUs were identified at a 97% identity threshold. Among these, 2,457 were singletons. Taxonomic classification using the RDP classifier confirmed that all 3667 OTUs were fungal in origin. The distribution of sequences across the 3 groups was as follows: 311,792 sequences for healthy controls, 325,503 for reticular OLP and 335,008 for erosive OLP. Among the fungal ITS OTUs, 2276 belonged to Ascomycota, 14 to Chytridiomycota, 627 to Basidiomycota, 67 to Zygomycota and 39 to Glomeromycota. The remaining OTUs were classified as unclassified fungi (643 OTUs).

Chao1 index measured community richness and Simpson index measured community diversity. The E-OLP group showed a significantly higher Chao1 index than the healthy subjects (P = .0445), with no significant change in the Simpson index. Also, HC group exhibited low sample dispersion. Healthy individuals displayed relatively low and stable fungal richness and diversity, with minimal outliers and intragroup differences. To reveal overall microbial structural differences among groups, we performed PCA, NMDS and PCoA analyses. The HC group was clustered, while the R-OLP and E-OLP groups were dispersed. NMDS (P = .009) and PCoA (P = .01) showed significant differences in fungal community structure among the 3 groups (HC, R-OLP, E-OLP). PCA analysis approached significance (P = .061), supporting the above conclusion (Figure 1C-E).

We analysed the fungal community composition of 3 groups at different taxonomic levels. At the phylum level, we observed distinct patterns for the 2 most prevalent phyla: Ascomycota (73.13% in healthy individuals, 71.45% in R-OLP and 75.88% in E-OLP) and Basidiomycota (22.60%, 21.28% and 16.73%, respectively; Figure 2A). Ascomycota showed higher abundance in the R-OLP and E-OLP groups, whereas Basidiomycota abundance was lower in the OLP groups compared to HC. However, none of these differences were statistically significant. At the family level, 190 families were detected, among which the top 13 families with the highest abundance are shown in the figure (Figure 2B). Ustilaginaceae was significantly lower in both R-OLP (P = .0006) and E-OLP (P = .0065) groups compared to HC. At the genus level, a total of 389 genera were detected, 151 of which were found in only 1 individual. We screened genera with a relative abundance of >0.1% and a prevalence of ≥60% in each group, as shown in Figure 2D-E. Figure 2E shows the genera that met the screening criteria in all 3 groups and are considered the core genera of the communities. Among these, compared to the HC, the relative abundance of Pseudozyma was significantly reduced in both the R-OLP and E-OLP groups (P < .01), and the relative abundance of unclassified_Saccharomycetales was lower in the R-OLP group (P < .05). Figure 2D presents the genera that met the screening criteria in 1 or 2 of the 3 groups. Compared to the HC, the relative abundance of Simplicillium was significantly reduced in both R-OLP and E-OLP (P < .05). Additionally, in R-OLP, the abundance of Pseudallescheria and unclassified_Lasiosphaeriaceae increased, while that of unclassified_Saccharomycetales decreased (P < .05). In E-OLP, the abundance of Penicillium, Pleurotus and Pyronema increased (P < .01, P < .05 and P < .05, respectively). Notably, Pyronema was absent in the healthy group samples but present at higher levels in the OLP groups, indicating its potential diagnostic significance.

Co-occurrence patterns between fungi and fungi in salivary samples of OLP patients

After filtering out OTUs with a prevalence of less than 30% in the 3 groups, SparCC analysis was performed, and significant microbial interaction networks were constructed. Topological properties (average degree and modularity coefficients) and the ratio of negative to positive cohesion were calculated. In the HC group, the fungal network consisted of 41 nodes and 244 edges (Figure 4A). The R-OLP network comprised 69 nodes and 847 edges (Figure 4C), while the E-OLP network included 106 nodes and 1289 edges (Figure 4B). Differences in nodes and OTU counts indicated sparser interactions in healthy controls than in OLP patients. Among the groups, the E-OLP network exhibited the highest average degree (14.849), showing the densest node connections, while HC had the lowest (6), indicating sparse connections. This may reflect a simpler structure and fewer microbial interactions in healthy individuals, whereas OLP states exhibited greater complexity, diversity and interactions. HC also showed lower positive correlations than R-OLP and E-OLP, suggesting significant microbial antagonism. The higher positive cohesion ratio in HC implies a greater balance of negative interactions, indicating that competition or inhibition may maintain community stability. In OLP, specific microbes may dominate through synergistic relationships, potentially linked to disease progression. Modularity assesses the extent to which a network can be divided into smaller parts, indicating ecological processes controlling community structure.28 Networks with modularity values above 0.4 are considered modular.29 The modularity coefficients of the 3 networks were 0.309, 0.358 and 0.348, respectively, suggesting a lack of distinct modular structure. Compared to HC, the absolute value of the negative-positive cohesion ratio was significantly lower in R-OLP and E-OLP patients (P < .0001 and P < .01). To identify keystone taxa, we examined degree, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality of keystone taxa in salivary microbial networks of healthy controls and OLP patients (Figure 4E). In the relevant study, OTU31_Thermomyces was identified as the keystone taxa of the HC group, while OTU159_Cryptococcus was recognised as the keystone taxa of the R-OLP group. Ecological guilds of sampled taxa

FUNGuild analysis used to annotate fungal functions was performed based on OTU relative abundance to compare functional categories and predict differences in microbial spectra among the 3 groups. 11 functional groups were identified and heatmaps were generated to visualize the enrichment changes.30 As shown in Figure 5A-B, the categories 'unassigned' and 'undefined saprotrophs' were the most diverse. Compared to the HC group, the Endophyte-Epiphyte-Fungal Parasite guild was significantly reduced in the R-OLP and E-OLP groups (P = .0029 and P = .0096, respectively). For trophic modes, fungi were classified into 6 types: Pathotroph-Saprotroph, Pathotroph-Saprotroph-Symbiotroph, Pathotroph-Symbiotroph, Saprotroph, Saprotroph-Symbiotroph and unassigned. As shown in Figure 5C-D, Saprotrophs were the predominant type across all groups, comprising 45.22% in HC, 32.51% in R-OLP and 34.89% in E-OLP. The HC group exhibited the highest abundance of Saprotrophs. In contrast, the abundances of Pathotroph-Saprotroph and Pathotroph-Saprotroph-Symbiotroph showed little variation among the groups. The E-OLP group demonstrated an increased abundance of Saprotroph-Symbiotroph guilds and the R-OLP group showed a rise in unassigned guilds, though these differences were not statistically significant. Identification of potential biomarkers based on salivary fungi

A random forest model was developed to investigate the ability of salivary fungi to distinguish healthy individuals from R-OLP and E-OLP patients. RFE coupled with 5-fold CV was employed to select features that remained stable across multiple validation folds. 14 OTUs features were retained, each appearing in at least 3 out of 5 folds, confirming their selection stability. A random forest classifier built on these 14 stable OTUs features was evaluated via 5-fold nested CV. The model yielded a mean accuracy of 53.3 ± 24.7 % and a mean AUC of 0.758 ± 0.205. The performance across the 5 folds was as follows: Fold 1, 50.0 %; Fold 2, 83.3 %; Fold 3, 66.7 %; Fold 4, 50.0 %; and Fold 5, 16.7 % (see Additional file 1: Figure S4B). In total, 14 of 30 samples were misclassified (46.7 %). The aggregated confusion matrix showed that the model performed best for HC, with an accuracy of 80.0 %, while the accuracy was lowest for E-OLP (50.0 %) and intermediate for R-OLP (60.0 %). Overfitting risk was evaluated by comparing the OOB error on the training set with the CV performance. The OOB accuracy (63.3 %) differed from the mean cross-validation accuracy (53.3 %) by 10.0 %, indicating a moderate level of overfitting risk (see Additional file 1: Figure S4A).

During feature selection, Mean Decrease Accuracy values were calculated to identify key features. The feature importance plot highlighted the top 10 OTUs that contributed most to the classification (Figure 6A). The top 3 features were OTU13 (Pseudozyma, MeanDecreaseAccuracy = 8.1), OTU2 (Simplicillium, 7.0) and OTU87 (Debaryomyces, 6.1). These differences indicate that disease subtypes may be associated with distinct microbial signatures.

Discussion

With the rise of microbiome sequencing, increasing research suggests that microbiota dysbiosis may play a significant role in OLP development.31 Although fungi account for only 0.004% of oral microbiota and related studies are limited, their compositional changes have been confirmed as biomarkers for monitoring the prevalence and prognosis of diseases like dental caries, oral cancer and periodontal disease, reflecting oral and systemic health.32 Fungal dysbiosis may influence salivary bacteria and reflect the direct impact of the host immune response, which is implicated in OLP pathogenesis.22

This study analysed the salivary mycobiota of OLP patients and HC, uncovering the diversity and composition characteristics of salivary fungi in OLP patients and preliminarily investigated the fungal genera related to OLP severity and their potential ecological and functional roles. The E-OLP group exhibited higher salivary fungal alpha-diversity than the other 2 groups. This contrasts with a prior study on oral mucosal samples, which found lower oral fungal alpha-diversity in E-OLP patients compared to both Nonerosive OLP (NE-OLP) patients and HC.33 Moreover, beta-diversity analysis revealed that the HC group was more concentrated, indicative of the stability of salivary microbiota in healthy individuals, while the R-OLP and E-OLP groups showed greater dispersion.

According to the relative abundance data at the genus level, Mycothermus is the most abundant, with no significant abundance difference between OLP patients and HC. However, previous research has indicated that Candida is the most abundant fungal genus in the saliva of both healthy individuals and OLP patients, with a significant increase in the relative abundance of Candida and Aspergillus in the OLP group compared to the HC group among the top 20 fungal genera.34 Candida is a commensal fungus in the mucosal microbiota of healthy individuals and also plays a crucial role in OLP.35 Additionally, Pseudozyma was found to be more prevalent in healthy individuals. Combined with LEfSe analysis, Pseudozyma was enriched in the HC group and may represent a candidate beneficial fungus, suggesting its potential as a microbial biomarker for health-associated oral mycobiota. Our prior research on oral mucosal mycobiota revealed a significantly lower level of Pseudozyma in the R-OLP group compared to the HC group.6 Pseudozyma are yeast-like fungi, some species of which can secrete extracellular metabolites and biosurfactants with considerable potential for biocontrol applications.36,37 The α-D-mannan contained in these yeast-like fungi is an important bio-response modulator that can exhibit various biological activities when administered to animals and humans.38 Studies have confirmed that α-D-mannan oligosaccharides can reduce the colonization of enteric pathogens such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella, or Campylobacter and improve gut health in different animal species.39 Collectively, these functional properties align with characteristics of beneficial microbes, indicating the potential candidacy of Pseudozyma as an oral probiotic. Its hypothesised mechanisms within the OLP pathological context may involve restoring fungal community balance and modulating the local immune-inflammatory microenvironment, thereby potentially intervening in disease progression. Of course, these proposed functions need direct experimental validation in the future. Notably, for the first time, our study detected a higher abundance of Pyronema in OLP patients, with no identification of this genus in healthy individuals. Furthermore, LEfSe analysis indicated that Pyronema is a biomarker species for the R-OLP group. These findings may suggest the potential pathogenicity of Pyronema in the pathogenesis of OLP. Pyronema is a well-known pyrophilous fungal genus and a dynamic member of microbial communities after fires. Current research on this genus primarily focuses on analysing microbial communities following wildfires; however, its medical relevance remains to be further investigated.40

It should be noted that the specific function of Pyronema in the oral environment is still unclear and current interpretations of its potential role remain speculative. The typical ecological habit of this genus is pyrophilous and adapted to high environmental temperatures, which differs significantly from its known postfire ecological role in terms of survival mechanisms, metabolic activity, and interactions with host tissues in the stable, warm and moist oral environment.41 Therefore, the association between Pyronema and OLP may not only reflect possible direct pathogenicity but could also indicate secondary colonization following oral microbial dysbiosis or result from interactions with other microorganisms.

Critically, our understanding of the oral mycobiome in OLP, including genera like Pyronema, is constrained by a significant technical challenge: the isolation and cultivation of the majority of oral fungi remain difficult. Current research heavily relies on cultivable models such as C. albicans, which limits the functional characterization of noncultivable or fastidious fungal taxa.42 Therefore, future efforts to develop novel culture methods for isolating elusive oral fungi will be a crucial research direction. Future studies should incorporate these improved techniques alongside functional experiments and longitudinal data to further clarify the true role of Pyronema and other fungi in oral health and disease.

In OLP, specific fungal enrichment and reduced fungal interactions are notable disease features. Analysis of saliva samples from OLP patients revealed distinct changes in the fungal community, primarily through the enrichment of fungal taxa associated with REU. Notably, Olpidiaster, Microdochium, Schizothecium, Phoma, Dipodascus, Pyronema and Cryptococcus were all negatively related to the REU score in the E-OLP group. Microdochium, a plant-pathogenic genus in Microdochiaceae (Xylariales), hasn't been reported to be associated with human infections.43 Phoma, an opportunistic pathogen in animals and humans, was confirmed in a renal - transplant patient's subcutaneous lesion in 1973.44 As the number of immunocompromised patients increases, Phoma-induced infections are on the rise.45 Pyronema was found to be enriched in the R-OLP group but showed a negative correlation with the severity of E-OLP, highlighting the complexity of microbiome research. While differences in fungi among groups reflect the overall disease state, trend-based associations may capture dynamic regulatory mechanisms during disease progression. A study revealed that the abundance of certain microbes correlated with dental caries progression. Specifically, C. albicans was more prevalent in late-stage lesions, whereas Cryptococcus neoformans and Candida species dominated in early lesions. This difference may be attributed to the varying effects of microbial strains and disease stages.46 Future studies are needed to clarify the roles of these genera via intervention studies like probiotic trials and antifungal therapies. For instance, probiotic targeting pathogenic fungi such as Phoma and C. albicans – which are often dormanii JS and Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG showed a 75% reduction in oral C. albicans abundance, highlighting the potential of probiotic interventions to modulate the oral mycobiota and justify further investigation into their mechanistic role in OLP management.47,48 In contrast, antifungal agents may offer a potential alternative or adjunct to existing treatment regimens for OLP. For instance, Lawsonia inermis, commonly known as henna, is a medicinal plant from the Lythraceae family recognised for its antifungal properties.49 A randomised double-blind clinical trial demonstrated that henna mouthwash can serve as an effective alternative to chlorhexidine mouthwash in the adjunctive treatment of symptomatic OLP, provided no side effects are reported.50 Metagenomic sequencing could assess their functional gene expression in OLP.

Co-occurrence network analyses using SparCC were conducted, revealing more complex fungal ecological networks in the OLP groups. This suggests disrupted microbial interactions, with the salivary fungal network in the HC group being relatively simple and sparsely connected, while OLP patients showed a more complex and densely connected network structure. Previous research has primarily focused on the interactions between fungi and bacteria in the oral microbiome. There is a lack of studies on the construction of pure fungal ecological networks. However, it has been indicated that in OLP patients, especially those with E-OLP, the connections between fungal and bacterial nodes are sparser, exhibiting lower connectivity and higher modularity.8 This seems to contradict our findings. Nonetheless, our analysis of fungi-only ecological networks revealed increased network complexity. This may reflect certain fungal genera gaining dominance through synergistic interactions. As a result, this could lead to an ecological imbalance in the salivary fungal community of OLP patients, thereby promoting disease progression. In addition, the ratio of negative to positive cohesion in the salivary fungal community network of OLP patients was significantly reduced, indicating lower network stability. The cohesion ratio, measured per sample, links directly to sample metadata. In contrast, the connectedness ratio, a taxon-level property, defines the overall competitive-cooperative architecture of the network. Microbial networks with a higher ratio of negative to positive cohesion tend to occur under lower environmental stress, suggesting that communities become less stable when stress levels increase.26 This pattern aligns with the recognised role of ecological competition in promoting microbiome stability. This further supports the notion that the oral micro-ecological system in OLP patients is in an unstable state. Such instability may allow certain pathogenic fungi to more easily dominate the community, thereby leading to disease development.

Furthermore, keystone taxa were identified in each group: Thermomyces in the HC group, Cryptococcus in the R-OLP group and Saccharomycopsis and Gautieria in the E-OLP group. Specifically, Cryptococcus in the E-OLP group was found to be negatively correlated with the REU score. Notably, Thermomyces, one of the identified keystone taxa, has also been reported to be associated with other gastrointestinal conditions, including changes in relative abundance in fungal esophagitis and gastric cancer contexts, which provides a broader microbial context for understanding its potential role in OLP pathogenesis.51,52 Cryptococcus, a globally significant invasive fungal pathogen, was ranked first in the highest-priority category of the World Health Organization's 2022 'Fungal Priority Pathogen List', suggesting its potential to pose a major threat to global public health, though the direct epidemiological quantification of this threat remains ongoing.53 Ghannoum et al. found Cryptococcus species in 20% of healthy, nonsmoking Ohio adults via a buffered saline oral rinse, DNA extraction and PCR analysis, providing circumstantial evidence that oral Cryptococcus could serve as an opportunistic pathogen, although causality remains to be established.54

In addition, the FUNGuild database was exploited to predict functional guilds and revealed that most fungal OTUs were saprotrophs, indicating saprotrophic fungi as the dominant nutritional type. These assignments are predictive only and lack experimental validation; thus, they should be interpreted as putative rather than confirmed ecological functions. The HC group was dominated by decomposer and pathogen-related guilds, while mutualistic guilds were enriched in the OLP groups, possibly due to ecological adaptations.55 Another FUNGuild analysis of human saliva revealed that fungal communities were primarily composed of pathotrophs, saprotrophs and symbiotrophs. Interestingly, significant differences in oral fungal community functions were found between Tibetans and Han Chinese, likely stemming from Tibetans' prolonged high-altitude residence.56 Although these interpretations are based solely on in-silico annotations, they reflect fungal heterogeneity across populations and suggest the hypothesis that microbes may develop adaptive mechanisms in different environments.

In this study, conducted under the constraint of a limited sample size (n = 30), a random forest classification model was developed based on OTUs and genus-level microbiome data. While the model achieved a mean accuracy of 53.3%, this result should be interpreted with consideration of the sample size limitations. Feature importance analysis identified several OTUs that played a critical role in classification. Notably, OTU13 (Pseudozyma) and OTU2 (Simplicillium) were consistently important across multiple comparisons, particularly in distinguishing HC from disease states, suggesting that salivary fungal features possess predictive value for OLP. These findings indicate that Pseudozyma and Simplicillium may be key fungal genera for OLP prediction. Pairwise comparisons provided meaningful biological insights. The higher discriminative accuracy between HC and R‑OLP (90.0%) indicates substantial differences in microbiome composition between these states, likely reflecting disease-associated microbial dysbiosis. The model ranked Pseudozyma and Simplicillium as the top features distinguishing HC from R-OLP, which aligns with their significantly reduced relative abundance in both OLP subtypes and implies they may act as potential probiotic (Figure 6A-B).

Beyond these, OTU99 (Pyronema) aided in discriminating HC from E-OLP, which further supports its discriminative value as a disease-associated biomarker as noted earlier (Figure 6D). In contrast, the lower accuracy in distinguishing the 2 disease subtypes (60.0% for E-OLP vs R-OLP) suggests that R-OLP and E‑OLP may share similar microbial features, or that the currently selected biomarkers are insufficient to fully differentiate them. This observation is consistent with the clinical perspective that these subtypes may represent different manifestations along a disease spectrum rather than entirely distinct entities.57 Additionally, OTU87 (Debaryomyces) was identified as an important feature in differentiating E‑OLP from R‑OLP. Intriguingly, studies in intestinal inflammation have demonstrated that Debaryomyces hansenii, a member of this genus, can colonize inflamed mucosal tissues and actively impair wound healing through specific immune pathways.58 Given that Debaryomyces is present in the oral mycobiota of OLP patients and that mucosal injury is a hallmark of OLP, it is plausible that this genus may similarly influence local epithelial repair and immune homeostasis in the oral cavity. Its precise role in OLP pathogenesis, however, requires further functional investigation.

This study constitutes an exploratory pilot investigation with a small sample size and the estimated model performance exhibited considerable volatility (standard deviation 24.7%). The single-centre design limits the external validity of the findings. Nevertheless, the key OTUs identified by the current model offer preliminary directions for developing microbiome-based auxiliary diagnostic tools for OLP. In particular, the high discriminative accuracy between HC and R-OLP suggests that microbiome-based features may contribute to early screening of the disease. Future studies should seek to improve classification accuracy by validating and refining the model in larger, multi-centre cohorts. Additionally, further research may explore the correlation between microbiome signatures and clinical parameters – such as disease activity and treatment response – as well as conduct functional investigations to elucidate the pathological mechanisms of key microbial taxa. In a random forest analysis of buccal mucosa samples, Talaromyces and Candida emerged as potential biomarkers, being significantly enriched in the NE-OLP and E-OLP groups, respectively.24 Furthermore, some unnamed genera, such as unclassified_Agaricostilbaceae and unclassified_Pleosporaceae, may play a crucial role in OLP.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations that should be considered. First, it is a single-centre study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. To evaluate the adequacy of our sample size, complementary analyses were conducted. Rarefaction curves approached a plateau, confirming that the sequencing depth per sample was sufficient. The species accumulation curve for the entire cohort showed a trend toward saturation, suggesting that the sampling effort captured a substantial portion of the prevalent fungal diversity (see Additional file 1: Figure S2). Notably, Analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) revealed no significant difference in the overall community structure between groups (R = 0.029, P = .324). This pattern implies that the fungal signatures associated with OLP are likely specific, rather than reflecting a broad structural reorganization. Collectively, these results indicate that the depth and resolution of our data support a focused investigation into differential taxa, which aligns with the primary aim of this study. Second, the human oral cavity harbours a vast diversity of fungi that can vary with age, differ between males and females and may be influenced by sex hormones, factors that our study may not have fully accounted for.59 Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of our study precludes a dynamic evaluation of OLP disease status. Thus far, research on OLP-related fungal taxa has been scarce and inconclusive. Disparities in race, diagnostic criteria and analytical methods might account for the inconsistent results.60 Future research should expand the sample size and employ a longitudinal design to further investigate the relationship between salivary fungal community changes and OLP progression.

Conclusions

In this study, oral salivary fungal characteristics were examined in R-OLP patients, E-OLP patients and HCs, with fungal community composition analysed across different taxonomic levels. Compared to the HC group, the relative abundance of Pseudozyma was significantly reduced in both OLP groups. Pyronema was absent in HC samples but enriched in OLP groups, suggesting potential discriminative value. These results were in line with the LEfSe analysis findings. The random forest model we established revealed that OTU13 (g__Pseudozyma) and OTU2 (g__Simplicillium) showed high scores in distinguishing healthy individuals from R-OLP and E-OLP patients. In our analysis, Pseudozyma, Simplicillium and Pyronema emerged as preliminary candidate biomarkers, warranting further investigation. The supporting findings are summarised in Figure S5 (see Additional file 1: Figure S5). Furthermore, our study showed reduced fungal community network stability in the OLP group compared to the healthy group, with R-OLP patients showing the greatest instability. It is worthy of note that the keystone taxa OTU159_Cryptococcus of R-OLP fungal network exhibited a significant negative correlation with REU scores, indicating a potential benefit for OLP. In summary, our study characterizes the salivary fungal community in OLP patients, providing insights for future research into the potential role of the oral mycobiome in the pathogenesis of OLP. Regarding clinical translation, oral microbiome dysbiosis has been implicated in OLP progression, including its potential malignant transformation.61 The fungal alterations identified here – such as the reduction of Pseudozyma and Simplicillium, the enrichment of Pyronema and the negative correlation of Cryptococcus with clinical severity – suggest their potential utility as non-invasive biomarkers for distinguishing OLP states and monitoring disease activity. These findings encourage further validation of these fungal signatures in larger, independent cohorts and motivate the exploration of targeted strategies, such as antifungal or probiotic interventions, based on the specific mycobiota profiles observed in OLP.
