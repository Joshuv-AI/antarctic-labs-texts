Dead bag syndrome—in the capsular bag subluxated intraocular lens centration and refixation: A new technique
Authors: Anchal Thakur, Muskaan Bansal, Divya Challa, Chintan Malhotra, Arun K Jain
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
We describe a technique of scleral IOL fixation and optic centration of in the capsular bag subluxated IOL in a patient of “dead bag syndrome.” A 29-year-old male diagnosed case of thalassemia presented with painless progressive diminution of vision in the left eye following uncomplicated sequential phacoemulsification in both eyes 15 years back. The right eye revealed completely centered IOL with significant amount of anterior capsular opacification (ACO), while the left eye revealed inferior subluxation of the IOL within capsular bag. The capsular bag was dilated, diaphanous with clear anterior and posterior capsule without any evidence of capsular fibrosis or opacification. Thus, a diagnosis of “dead bag syndrome” was made. The haptics were sutured to sclera (Hoffman’s pockets) using two loops of 9-0 polypropylene, passed anterior and posterior to IOL haptics within the capsular bag. Postoperatively, the patient had a vision of 20/40 with a centered intraocular lens.

Cataract surgery has become one of the most successful of all surgeries being performed worldwide. However, uneventful near-perfect state-of-the-art surgery may also have complications such as anterior capsular opacification (ACO), posterior capsular opacification (PCO), cystoid macular edema (CME), and IOL subluxations and dislocation.[1] IOL malpositions can be early or late. Although the incidence has been reported to range between 0.2 and 3%, most of them only represent the tip of the iceberg and the exact incidence is still not known.[2-5] Studies have reported the causes of early (within a few weeks) subluxations (more common) mostly being related to complicated cataract surgery such as loss of capsular or zonular integrity during surgery or asymmetric placement of the haptics leading to decentration of the IOL. Late dislocations can be due to various contributory factors such as inherent zonular weakness due to the presence of pseudo-exfoliation, previous vitreoretinal surgery, trauma, and myopia.[5] The time frame of presentation of late dislocation may range from 4.5 to 16 years.[2,5] Another rare entity commonly unnoticed and underreported is the dead bag syndrome as a cause of late dislocation of the IOL. We describe management of such a case with unilateral subluxation of the IOL, which presented 15 years after an uncomplicated phacoemulsification with in the bag IOL implantation.

Technique

A 29-year-old male, known case of thalassemia major underwent sequential uncomplicated bilateral cataract surgery 15 years back by single surgeon (A.K.J). He presented to our lens clinic services with left eye diminution of vision, which was painless and progressive. On examination, the visual acuity was 20/20 in the right eye and 20/125 in the left eye. Anterior segment slit-lamp biomicroscopic examination of the right eye revealed completely centered IOL with significant amount of anterior capsular fibrosis extending from 12 to 5.30 clock hours [Fig. 1a]. Left eye revealed inferior subluxation IOL with in the capsular bag with a slightly smaller rhexis [Fig. 1b]. The capsular bag was dilated, diaphanous with clear anterior and posterior capsule without any evidence of capsular fibrosis or opacification. The intraocular pressures and the dilated fundus examination of both the eyes were unremarkable. The patient was undertaken for surgery under peribulbar anesthesia. Two side port entries were made, and cohesive viscoelastic was injected. The rhexis was slightly enlarged with the micro-rhexis forceps. The capsular bag integrity was assessed. It was found that the capsular bag was dilated with thin diaphanous, fragile capsule with some folds. Two opposite scleral Hoffman’s pockets were made at 1 and 7 clock position, 2 mm form the limbus. 9-0 polypropylene suture was passed above and below the haptic with docking of the long needle into the lumen of 26 G needle. Thus, the haptic was fixed within the bag to the sclera at both the ends after titrating the sutures for centration of the IOL [Video 1]. The postoperative vision was 20/40 with +0.5DS -1.5 DC x 165 with a perfectly centered IOL at 3 months postsurgery [Fig. 1c].

(b) Left eye revealed inferior subluxation of the IOL with in the capsular bag. (c) Slit-lamp picture of the left eye showing a centered IOL after surgery

Discussion

The term dead bag was 1st coined by Samuel Masket who defined this entity as a cause of late dislocation of the IOL in a capsule, which has remained always clear after surgery.[6] The exact etiopathogenesis is still unknown, but it is hypothesized that presence of lens epithelial cells is crucial for maintaining the capsular bag integrity.[7] Culp in their study of 10 suspected cases of dead bag syndrome have shown that the capsules in such cases histopathologically show some signs of thinning, degradation, and splitting.[8] In their study, they have postulated that the capsules lacking lens epithelial cells (LECs) exhibit some capsular delamination in areas of zonular attachments that leads to loss of zonular fibers. LECs contributes to the health and structural integrity of the lens capsule either through their direct interaction with the capsule or via the production of protective factors.[7,9]

This case supports this hypothesis since we found a perfectly centered IOL in the eye, which had anterior capsular fibrosis and a significantly decentered IOL in the other eye of the same patient with a clear but a diaphanous floppy bag. The patient was operated by the same surgeon (A.K.J) at a similar time period with the same standard uneventful phacoemulsification with a single piece IOL in the bag. The patient had no history of trauma, nor did he had any signs of exfoliation or pseudo-exfoliation at the time of surgery.

Another common cause of late dislocation of the IOL is pseudo-exfoliation syndrome.[10] The known incidence is around 5-20%, although this disease is quite underreported.[11] In a series of 40 patients with late IOL dislocation, more than 50% patients had capsular tension ring (CTR) or histopathological appearance of pseudo-exfoliative material on the capsule. All such cases had the presence of (LECs) or Soemmering’s ring formation.[12] Fibrous metaplasia of anterior LECs, with capsular contraction and rarely capsular phimosis, may also be observed unlike that in a patient with a dead bag. True exfoliation syndrome is another clinical entity consisting of the presence of thin delamination of the capsule visible on slit-lamp examination although zonular instability is not frequently present. Patients with true exfoliation typically present in their seventies to eighties and have a significant history of exposure to high heat or infrared radiation.[13] Histopathologically, lens capsule splitting, delamination, or schisis is the main feature, but varying amounts of LECs are found to be present in true exfoliation specimens.[14-18] This is in contrast to the dead bag syndrome where there is complete absence of lens epithelial cells on histopathology.

Management of such cases depends on the degree of luxation, whereas mild cases need to be just observed and patients with significant IOL subluxation may require removal of the IOL-bag complex with secondary IOL in the later stages.[19,20] The middle path is IOL repositioning that can be a management strategy in patients with visually significant in the bag subluxation. The advantage is that this procedure does not require a large limbal incision, thereby decreasing the endothelial damage as well as surgical astigmatism. Various techniques have been described for repositioning and suturing a completely dislocated IOL to the iris, sulcus, or pars plana using a posterior or the anterior route. Oshika[21] in the year 1997 described a simple technique for fixating the subluxated bag. In his technique, the IOL haptics were refixed to the sclera using a loop of suture. In this procedure, one suture was passed below the haptic and the other was passed in front of haptic, but anterior to the rhexis margin fixing the subluxated IOL with the bag to the sclera. Another technique was the fixation of the IOL to the iris with modified McCannel suturing.[22,23] None of the studies have described fixing a dislocated IOL in the bag using the anterior route.

We describe a simple technique to achieve IOL centration in a clear, diaphanous (dead bag) but subluxated bag. Both the haptics were fixated using a loop of 9-0 polypropylene using a Hoffman’s pocket within the bag. The IOL haptic was fixed after titrating the centration although a similar technique was described by Oshika[21] in 1999 using a loop of suture to fix the haptics to the sclera. In their technique, the suture was passed through the sulcus: one arm of loop posterior to IOL haptic within the capsular bag and second arm anterior to IOL haptic but outside the capsular bag anterior to capsule-rhexis margin. This can lead to ovalization or irregularity of rhexis margin and may expose the haptics outside the capsular bag. In our technique, both arms of the suture were passed anterior and posterior to IOL haptic within the capsular bag and piercing the capsular bag at equator only and then out through the ciliary sulcus into the Hoffman’s pocket, thus keeping the haptics of the IOL within the bag. This prevents the possibility of haptics coming out of bag and distortion of capsule-rhexis margin also. Moreover, since it was a single piece square haptic design IOL, sulcus fixation was not an advisable option as externalisation of square haptics out of bag could lead to the possibility of pigment dispersion and secondary glaucoma.[24] The only drawback of our case was that a histopathological proof of a “dead bag” could not be established since the bag was not explanted but instead utilized to successfully reposition the subluxated IOL. Thus, this technique was first of its kind to successful reposition the subluxated IOL in the capsular bag in a patient of “dead bag syndrome.” Whether thalassemia major has any role to play in the pathogenesis of this entity, we are unable to comment about. Anemia and hypoxia in these patients may have a role to play in survival of residual lens epithelial cells in crystalline lens equatorial region. This is a subject for further studies.

To conclude, this case highlights and further supports the hypothesis that the some lens epithelial cells are essential for the capsular integrity. The pathophysiology itself leads us to an improvisation in surgical technique in managing such tough scenarios.
