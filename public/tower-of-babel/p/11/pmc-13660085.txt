Knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to the use of artificial intelligence in nursing education and practice: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abdulaziz Mofdy Almarwani, Mona Ibrahim Hebeshy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into nursing education and practice has demonstrated significant potential to enhance efficiency, reduce errors, and optimize healthcare delivery. However, the successful implementation of AI requires careful consideration of the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of nursing professionals and students.AimTo examine the association between knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding AI use in nursing education and clinical practice.MethodSystematic review and meta-analysis. Following PRISMA guidelines, a comprehensive search was conducted across three major databases (SCOPUS, Web of Science, and PubMed) for studies published between January 2019 and April 2025.ResultsTen qualified cross-sectional studies were identified from an initial pool of 283 records. A random-effects meta-analysis revealed a moderate positive correlation between knowledge and attitudes {Pearson’s correlation (r) = 0.43, 95% confidence interval (CI) = [0.31, 0.53]}, a moderate attitude–practice relationship {Pearson’s correlation (r) = 0.46, 95% CI = [0.32, 0.59]}, and a weak knowledge–practice association {Pearson’s correlation (r) = 0.26, 95% CI = [0.13, 0.38]}. Significant heterogeneity was observed in the analysis [between-study variance (τ²) = 0.03–0.05; inconsistency (I²) = 89.88%–95.02%; Cochran’s Q (Q) = 16.44–157.00, p < 0.001), thereby suggesting that contextual factors such as institutional policies and prior AI exposure may influence these relationships.ConclusionsThese findings partially aligned with the KAP theoretical framework, thus highlighting the significant association between AI knowledge, attitude, and clinical implementation. We recommend integrated AI education that emphasizes attitude formation, targeted training to address implementation barriers, and organizational support systems for AI integration. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to establish causal relationships and examine the contextual factors influencing AI use across diverse healthcare settings.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Knowledge, Attitude, Practice, Nursing, Education, Practice, KAP

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has significantly transformed various industries—including healthcare, education, and business—that play a crucial role in social and economic development [1]. In healthcare, AI enhances but does not replace the work of healthcare professionals, such as doctors, nurses, and midwives [2]. It has been shown that AI integration reduces healthcare costs while improving the efficiency of healthcare services [3]. In nursing, AI applications in administrative tasks, patient data management, and clinical decision-making can enhance efficiency and productivity [4]. Previous literature has reported a high rate of nursing students who have adopted AI in their nursing education [41%-69%] [5–7]. Given AI’s growing influence in healthcare, it is essential that nurses not only possess knowledge of AI applications but also develop a positive attitude toward its adoption and use in clinical practice.

The interplay among knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) can enable a better understanding of how well-equipped nursing students and professionals are to adopt and integrate AI into clinical and educational settings [8]. Previous literature has highlighted a gap between positive attitudes and practical engagement and suggested a need for improved AI training and accessibility in medical education [9]. The underlying interrelationship among the aspects of knowledge, attitude, and practice is often explained through the KAP survey. This survey evaluates a specific population to determine what is known, believed, and done in relation to a particular topic [10, 11]. The KAP survey is widely utilized for behavioral intervention in health-seeking behavior research [10–12]. Moreover, there is a correlation among the components of KAP and health issues faced by patients [13].

As AI continues to evolve, traditional nursing care is becoming more precise, innovative, and personalized [14]. To keep pace with these advancements, nursing students should also be equipped with the necessary skills, knowledge, positive attitude, and perception toward AI [15, 16]. Despite AI’s increasing relevance in education and healthcare, only a few nursing and medical schools have incorporated AI courses into their nursing curricula [17]. This gap highlights the need for further research on nursing students and practicing nurses’ KAP related to AI utilization. Understanding these factors can inform curriculum development and policy decisions that support AI integration in nursing education and practice.

Several studies have conducted meta-analyses to assess the application of AI in nursing [18, 19]. Recently, Amiri et al. [20] conducted a meta-analysis that assessed and confirmed a high level of knowledge and better attitude toward AI among medical, dental, and nursing students. Further, Baigi et al. [21] conducted a meta-analysis to assess healthcare students’ skills, attitudes, and knowledge toward AI. However, these meta-analyses did not take into account the practice toward AI among nursing students or nurses. Considering that the association among KAP is evident in healthcare [13], this study suggests the potential relationship among KAP in the context of AI; hence, this meta-analysis seeks to assess this relationship in nursing education and practice.

Methods

Search Strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in April 2025 using the electronic databases Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed (MEDLINE), and CINAHL for the period January 1, 2019, to April 29, 2025. This time period was selected due to the continuous evolution and integration of AI into nursing education and healthcare systems [4, 22]. The search was limited to peer-reviewed journal articles published in English. To ensure methodological rigor, the search strategy was developed in collaboration with a research librarian and was based on the population, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO) framework (Table 1). The search terms included combinations of keywords such as “artificial intelligence” OR “AI”; “nursing” OR “healthcare”; “knowledge” OR “attitude” OR “practice” (see Table 2 for detailed search terms).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (i) original studies that examined knowledge, attitude, or practice related to AI in nursing care; (ii) nursing education studies, (iii) cross-sectional studies that employed quantitative or mixed-method approaches, (iv) studies published in English; (v) studies published in a peer-reviewed journal; and (vi) studies published between January 2019 and April 2025. Articles related to nursing education were considered essential to nursing practice, as they provide the required knowledge and skills for delivering quality patient care.

Studies were excluded if they (i) had employed a qualitative or descriptive research design, (ii) were unpublished studies (e.g., preprints, dissertations); (iii) were not related to nursing and had involved dentistry, medical, pharmacy, or other allied health students or practitioners; (iv) discussed outcomes or topics other than AI readiness; (v) were review studies, case reports, conference papers, newspaper articles, magazine articles or commentaries; and (vi) could not be subjected to full review due to the nonavailability of their full text. Although the remaining exclusion criteria is related to the PICO framework, the current meta-analysis also excluded qualitative studies due to the nonavailability of statistically measurable estimates (i.e., effect size) in the relationships among study variables. For example, a qualitative study titled “Postgraduate nursing students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding artificial intelligence: a qualitative study” by Zhang et al. [23] was excluded because it only discussed the insights into nursing students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices and did not evaluate the relationship among these three study variables, which was the main purpose of the study.

Study selection

The study selection process followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [24]. Search results from electronic databases were imported into the EndNote v21 reference management software for organization. The selection process comprised four sequential phases. First, duplicate records were removed through both automated and manual verification. Second, two independent reviewers screened the remaining studies by title and abstract to assess their relevance to the research objectives. Third, potentially eligible studies underwent full-text review against the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, articles that met al.l criteria were selected for data extraction. A few articles that surveyed nursing students or nurses under the healthcare umbrella were also manually included in the meta-analysis. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer to reach a consensus. This rigorous process ensured the inclusion of only the most methodologically sound and relevant studies for analysis.

Data extraction

The data was extracted and documented on a standardized form to include the following information: Bibliographic information (first author’s last name, year of publication), study characteristics (geographical location, study design), population parameters (sample size, gender distribution), variables studied, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) or Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient (rs). Extraction was independently performed by two researchers, with any discrepancies resolved through A re-examination of source materials and team discussion to ensure accuracy.

Quality assessment

The articles that were finally included were appraised for their methodological quality using the National Institutes of Health Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies (NIH-QAT) [25]. This validated instrument assessed 14 critical domains, including clarity of research objectives, adequate description of sample size, appropriate calculation of sample size with sufficient support and justification, appropriateness of study timeframe, and adequacy of statistical analysis. The quality assessment was conducted by two independent authors, and the results were then combined and reconciled in case of any discrepancy.

Statistical analysis

All data analysis was conducted using STATA v17.0 software. To create a single measure of effect size for each study, the study extracted Pearson’s correlation or Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient to calculate the effect size of association. Pearson’s correlation coefficient represents the direction and magnitude of association between continuous variables, while Spearman’s rho correlation test represents the direction and magnitude of monotonic relationship (direction and strength) between two continuous or ordinal (rank-ordered) variables or when the continuous variable has a non-normal distribution or is distorted by outliers [26]. Nevertheless, both coefficients range from ‒1 to + 1, thereby indicating the strength and direction of the relationship [27]: the sign (‒) or (+) indicates direction, either negative or positive, while the closer the coefficient is to 1, the greater the strength of the correlation [27]. Considering this difference, the effect size scores for Pearson’s correlation and Spearman’s rho correlation were separately calculated. Both Pearson’s and Spearman’s rho correlation coefficients were transformed into Fisher’s Z using a table for conversion into the magnitude of effect for the meta-analysis as per the guideline provided by Gilpin [28]. Gilpin’s table for conversion accounts for the differences between Pearson’s correlation and Spearman’s rho correlation. However, Gilpin’s table for conversion presented the transformation of Fisher’s Z, Pearson’s correlation, and Spearman’s rho correlation coefficients into intervals, which may lead to the nonavailability of the exact score of Pearson’s correlation coefficients. The use of Fisher’s Z transformation is recommended when averaging correlation coefficients, particularly in cases with a small sample size [29]. To obtain a more exact effect size in meta-analysis, Fisher’s Z transformation for Pearson’s correlation was computed using Eq. 1 [30, 31]. The standard error (SE) for Fisher’s Z was calculated using Eq. 2. The final effect size was calculated as the pooled r and 95% confidence interval (CI) [30].

Further, various diagnostic tests were conducted to assess accuracy, including heterogeneity and publication bias [32]. First, the heterogeneity of the r values among studies was assessed by computing the Q-statistic (derived from the chi-square test) and the inconsistency index (I²) [30, 32]. If the chi-square was significant at the 5% level (p < 0.05) or I² > 50%, the heterogeneity was identified [33]. High heterogeneity can be resolved with a random-effects meta-analysis, which allows for heterogeneity by assuming that the underlying effects follow a normal distribution [34, 35]. The potential sources of high heterogeneity are further assessed with a subgroup analysis of the target population and study area. In addition, a leave-out-one sensitivity analysis was conducted to test the credibility and robustness of the pooling results [36]. Furthermore, the publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Ten studies were included in the review [22, 37–45). Figure 1 presents the selection using the PRISMA flow diagram, highlighting the steps taken in each main stage, including identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion [24]. All studies included in the review were cross-sectional studies, with 4,707 participants. The characteristics of the 10 studies included in the meta-analysis are presented in Table 3. Nursing students were targeted as a sample in 40% of studies (n = 4), while 50% of studies (n = 5) targeted nursing professionals as a sample. Only one study (10%) targeted both nursing students and professionals in the sample. Furthermore, 80% of the studies (n = 8) applied Pearson’s correlation analysis to assess the association between knowledge, attitudes, and practice, while only 2% of the studies (n = 2) applied Spearman’s rho correlation analysis. Lastly, 50% of the studies (n = 5) were conducted in Middle East countries, while the remaining 50% of studies (n = 5) were conducted in countries other than the Middle East region.

Quality appraisal

The quality appraisal of all included articles was conducted using the NIH-QAT for observational cohort and cross-sectional studies. All included articles had quality rating scores of 50% or more on the NIH-QAT assessment scale.

Publication bias

Rothstein et al. [46] advocated that publication bias in the meta-analysis results should be thoroughly assessed since, in most cases, significant results are highly likely to be published as nonsignificant results. Moreover, published studies were also included in the meta-analysis, which further intensified the risk of publication bias. To assess publication bias, the study conducted Egger’s regression test [47] for funnel plot asymmetry for each set of effect size. The results revealed that the correlation between knowledge in and attitude toward AI (z = 1.79, p = 0.074), between knowledge in and practice toward AI (z = − 0.49, p = 0.928), and between attitude toward and practice of AI (z = − 0.37, p = 0.715). Hence, there was no issue of publication bias for this set of effect size.

Association between knowledge of and attitudes toward artificial intelligence

All 10 studies were included to assess the association between knowledge of and attitude toward AI among nursing students and professionals (Fig. 2). A higher level of knowledge of AI was consistently associated with a more favorable attitude toward AI. The random effects mean correlation between knowledge and attitude toward AI was 0.43, with a 95% prediction interval ranging from ‒0.265 to 0.704. When studies were collectively assessed in the meta-analysis, high heterogeneity was identified (τ2 = 0.05, I2 = 95.02%, QT = 157.00, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). This justified the application of the random-effects model [34, 35]. The studies were further assessed using a subgroup analysis of the target population and study area.

Forest plot – correlation between knowledge and attitudes towards AI

Association between AI knowledge and practices

Six studies were included to assess the association between AI knowledge and practices (Fig. 3). Studies consistently reported a positive association between AI knowledge and practices, as the random-effects mean correlation between the variables was 0.26, with a 95% prediction interval from ‒0.125 to 1.011. However, the meta-analysis revealed high heterogeneity within studies (τ2 = 0.03, I2 = 89.88%, QT = 62.87, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3), which justified the application of the random-effects model [34, 35] and was further assessed through a subgroup analysis of the target population and study area. It must be noted that two studies revealed an insignificant association between AI knowledge and practices in nursing education and practice [22, 45) and need to be further assessed to determine their significance or nonsignificance in the context of utilizing AI in nursing education and practice.

Forest plot – correlation between knowledge and practices towards AI

Association between attitude toward AI and related practices

Six studies were included to assess the association between attitudes toward AI and related practices (Fig. 4). All studies reported a positive association between the two, as the random-effects mean correlation between the variables was 0.46, with a 95% prediction interval from ‒0.204 to 1.130. However, the meta-analysis revealed high heterogeneity within studies (τ2 = 0.05, I2 = 93.92%, QT = 16.44, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4), which justified the application of the random-effects model and was further assessed through a subgroup analysis of the target population and study area.

Forest plot – correlation between attitudes and practices towards AI

In summary, the effect size for the correlation between AI knowledge and practices showed a weak association, while the effect size for the correlation between knowledge of and attitude toward AI as well as the correlation between the attitude toward AI and AI practices revealed a moderate correlation.

In terms of population type, the analysis excluded one study that examined both nursing professionals and students as the target population to obtain a clear picture of underlying differences. The association between knowledge of and attitudes toward AI among nursing professionals and students was almost the same, as the test of group differences did not yield a significant result (Qresidual = 0.58, p = 0.450). Similarly, the association between knowledge and practices for both nursing professionals and students was rather weak, thereby resulting in nonsignificant differences across groups (Qresidual = 0.11, p = 0.740). However, the association between knowledge and practices for nursing professionals and students was weak (pooled r = 0.300, p < 0.01) and strong (pooled r = 0.610, p < 0.001) respectively, thereby resulting in significant differences across groups (Qresidual = 7.58, p < 0.001).

In terms of region, the association between knowledge and attitudes in the Middle East was higher (pooled r = 0.507, p < 0.001) than the association in region other than Middle East (pooled r = 0.447, p < 0.001) (Qresidual = 27.04, p = < 0.001). Furthermore, the association between knowledge and practices in Middle East and region other than Middle East was moderate (pooled r = 0.507, p < 0.001) and rather weak (pooled r = 0.145, p < 0.001) (Qresidual = 11.33, p = < 0.001). However, the association between knowledge and practices in the Middle East and region other than Middle East was equally significant (Middle East: pooled r = 0.579, p < 0.001; region other than Middle East: pooled r = 0.364, p < 0.001) (Qresidual = 3.66, p = 0.056).

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study conducted a meta-analysis to examine the relationship among the knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to AI in nursing. The findings indicate statistically significant associations among these three variables. Specifically, a weak but positive relationship was found between knowledge and practice in AI (r = 0.22), while a moderate positive correlation was observed between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.42) as well as between attitude and practice (r = 0.43). These results suggest that as knowledge of AI increases, attitudes toward AI become more positive, and AI-related practices improve. Similar findings were reported by Amiri et al. [20], who reported a significant association between attitudes and knowledge toward AI, as well as by Hassankhani et al. [48], who reported the extensive impact of AI on radiology and KAP among medical students, radiology trainees, and radiologists. These results emphasize the interrelationship among KAP in shaping AI adoption in nursing. This interrelationship can bring behavioral change in the use of AI among nurses and nursing students [37] that would subsequently induce them to show the intention or actual AI adoption behavior in their daily work-related activities [39].

Further, the findings of this study were somewhat consistent with the KAP framework [13]. The study findings indicated that having more knowledge of AI among nurses and nursing students tended to reveal a positive attitude toward AI and engagement in effective AI practices. Furthermore, nurses and nursing students who have a positive attitude toward AI utilization can effectively adopt AI technologies in nursing practice. Collectively, KAP in nursing education and practice contribute to behavioral interventions that are much needed for the successful implementation of AI in the field of nursing. Nevertheless, the study findings were not consistent with the KAP theory [12, 49], which explains the effect of knowledge on attitudes and practices as well as the effect of attitudes on practices.

Despite the overall positive associations found in this meta-analysis, two studies reported a nonsignificant association between knowledge and practice [22, 45]. Labrague et al. [22] found that perception toward AI utilization in nursing practice significantly affects attitudes toward AI, which collectively enhances the intention to adopt AI technology. Similarly, Song et al. [45] identified that acquiring knowledge alone may not necessarily translate into active practice, thereby reinforcing the idea that attitude plays a crucial mediating role between knowledge and practice. However, since the studies included in the meta-analysis did not assess the mediating role of “attitude” in the relationship between knowledge and practices, the current meta-analysis did not extensively study or include any such analysis or findings. Hence, following Bettinghaus [49], the notion of positive behavioral change presented in the KAP theory was not justified in the current study.

Further, the subgroup analysis of population type indicated a significant difference in the association between attitude and practices. Similarly, the subgroup analysis of region indicated a significant difference in the relationship between knowledge and attitudes as well as between knowledge and practices. These significant differences highlighted the potential sources of high heterogeneity in the meta-analysis [50]. For example, the association between attitudes and practices was stronger for nursing students compared to nursing professionals, which signifies the role of nursing education. However, Cuijpers et al. [50] also argued that high heterogeneity is possible in a small number of studies that identify high variability in the correlation between studies included in the meta-analysis. Hence, more studies that analyze the association between knowledge, attitude, and practices as specified by the KAP framework must be conducted to identify the specific reasons underlying such variability.

The study findings revealed how the interrelationship among AI knowledge, attitude, and practices can foster the preparedness and willingness to embrace the use of AI as a valuable tool in nursing practice [45]. This meta-analysis emphasizes the importance of these three factors in motivating nursing educators, students, and practitioners to integrate AI into their professional roles. By strengthening AI knowledge, fostering positive attitudes toward AI, and encouraging active AI practices, nursing professionals could perceive AI as a useful, efficient, and user-friendly tool in their daily work. This, in turn, would facilitate greater engagement with AI technologies and support a culture of technological adaptation in nursing education and practice. Overall, the current systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that acquiring new knowledge empowered AI users in nursing education and practice to consistently and proactively adopt AI in nursing education and practice., they can learn new techniques to adopt AI in their daily work activities and later exhibit positive behavior and active engagement toward AI [51].

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review has several strengths: First, it comprehensively identified all potential articles related to the topic. Second, it adopted a robust methodology approach, incorporating meta-analysis to compute combined effect sizes, which strengthened the reliability of the findings. Third, it combined several studies that individually assessed the association among the three variables studied based on the KAP framework. This contributes to providing a detailed explanation of how the three variables interact and enable the provision of solutions to health issues.

However, this study has several limitations. First, only studies written in the English language were included. There may be other relevant studies written in languages other than English that were not included here due to the language barrier. Second, this study found limited literature that assessed the association among knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to AI in nursing education. Most of the studies included here only assess the knowledge, attitudes, or practices related to AI in the field of nursing. Third, the small number of studies that examine these associations contributed to high heterogeneity, thus requiring caution in interpreting the results. Fourth, although the search was conducted for the last five years, most of the studies included in the review were published between 2024 and 2025—that is, that last two years—thus indicating a potential publication bias that may overestimate effect sizes. Fifth, the search was limited to cross-sectional research that assesses the association among knowledge, attitude, and practice related to AI. Lastly, the search was limited to nursing students only and excluded studies involving dentists, medical, pharmacy, or other allied health students or practitioners from the meta-analysis.

Suggestions for future research

The findings and limitations of this study suggest several avenues for future research. First, given the established interrelationship among knowledge, attitude, and practice, future studies should examine specific AI applications—such as clinical decision support systems and simulation-based learning—to determine which applications demonstrate the strongest associations among these variables in nursing education and clinical practice. Second, the present systematic review only focused on cross-sectional studies; however, PRISMA assessment revealed several qualitative, descriptive, and experimental studies that were excluded. Future research should incorporate diverse research methodologies to provide a more comprehensive overview of the literature assessing the prevalence and relationship among knowledge, attitude, and practices related to AI. Moreover, the KAP theory suggests that knowledge influences attitudes, which in turn influences the AI practices of nursing students and professionals. Considering the limited literature that explains cause-and-effect relationship and serial mediation, future studies should assess the causal effect of knowledge on attitudes and practices as well as the causal effect of attitudes on practices and the serial mediating effect of attitudes in the relationship between knowledge and practices. Third, while this study focused on nursing students and professionals, future studies can conduct a meta-analysis across various healthcare professionals, including doctors, physicians, midwives, and even and educators. Expanding the scope to interdisciplinary healthcare education and practice would enhance the generalizability of findings and provide a more holistic perspective on AI integration. Fourth, this review was restricted to knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to AI. However, skills, perception, readiness, and intention toward AI in nursing education and practice were not considered due to the limited accessibility of literature. Future research should examine these additional variables to develop a more nuanced understanding of AI adoption in nursing. Fifth, the meta-analysis found two studies that assess the knowledge and practices related to AI that revealed insignificant results. More studies should be conducted to assess and confirm the association between knowledge and practices related to AI in nursing education and practice. Sixth, the geographic distribution of studies in this review was predominantly concentrated in the Middle East, thus limiting the applicability of findings to other regions. To enhance the generalizability of research outcomes, future studies should include diverse geographical contexts, incorporating data from North America, Europe, Asia, and Africa to provide a more comprehensive global perspective on AI adoption in nursing education and practice. Seventh, future studies should consider adopting longitudinal or case-control approaches to compare the knowledge, attitudes, and practices surrounding the use of AI in nursing education and practice with traditional methods. Lastly, more qualitative studies could be conducted to evaluate the acceptance and readiness of the target participants, thereby enabling a comparison of perspectives across different colleges, professional backgrounds, and age groups.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis identified a moderate and positive association between the knowledge of and attitude towards AI and attitude toward and practices related to AI, and a weak and positive association between knowledge of and practices related to AI. These findings are partially aligned with the KAP framework being an effective tool to address the adoption of AI in nursing education and practice.
