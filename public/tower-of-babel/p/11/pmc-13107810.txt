Evaluation of [18F]MK-6240 binding to tau protein in postmortem human brains of Down syndrome and Alzheimer’s disease and assessment of off-target (non-tau) binding
Authors: Fariha Karim, Agnes P. Biju, Christopher Liang, Camryn J. Santos, Maharishi Rajarethenam, Jogeshwar Mukherjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Down Syndrome (DS) are characterized by the aggregation of tau tangles. As a novel tau PET tracer in AD, [18F]MK-6240 has the potential in Down syndrome-associated Alzheimer’s disease (DSAD) to elucidate pathophysiology and advance diagnostic strategies. Autoradiography of frontal cortex and temporal cortex postmortem brain slices of DSAD (n = 5), AD (n = 5), and cognitively normal (CN) (n = 5) cases indicated similarly high [18F]MK-6240 binding in DSAD and AD cases. [18F]MK-6240 binding in CN was substantially less than DSAD and AD. Anti-tau immunostains confirmed total tau presence that aligned with the localization of [18F]MK-6240 binding. DSAD and AD cases exhibited higher gray matter (GM)/white matter ratios of 2.8 and 2.5 respectively. For drug effects on [18F]MK-6240 binding, self-displacement by MK-6240 (10 µM) reduced binding by 88% among DSAD cases and 85% among AD cases while IPPI (10 µM) displaced [18F]MK-6240 by 81% and 74% in DSAD and AD cases respectively. KuFal194 (10 µM), a specific phosphokinase inhibitor, minimally displaced [18F]MK-6240 binding. Harmine competed with [18F]MK-6240 with an IC50 value of 290 nM and 92 nM for DSAD and AD cases, respectively. High meninges off-target (non-tau) binding of [18F]MK-6240 was observed in a CN case, comparable to the GM in DSAD and AD. MK-6240 (10 µM) blocked 44% and T807 (10 µM) blocked 30% of meninges binding. Incubation of meninges in the presence of 0.2% polyethyleneimine reduced 70% of [18F]MK-6240 binding. The tau radioligand, [125I]IPPI, an analog of [18F]MK-6240, exhibited minimal binding to CN meninges compared to the DSAD and AD cases. Our findings suggest [18F]MK-6240 to be selective tau imaging agent in DSAD and AD, harmine to uniquely compete with [18F]MK-6240 binding in DSAD and provide preliminary insights to the off-target nonspecific binding of [18F]MK-6240 in postmortem meninges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02267-1.

Introduction

Adults with Down syndrome (DS) are predisposed to develop Alzheimer’s disease (AD) starting with its pathology of fibrillar amyloid beta (Aβ) plaque and neurofibrillary tangles [57, 65, 81]. The pathological correlation between DS and AD may be affected by the triplication of chromosome 21 in DS, causing an abundance of Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles. The deposition of tau closely parallels the severity of cognitive decline in AD more than Aβ plaque, emphasizing tau mechanisms to track disease progression [9, 55]. The downregulation of protein phosphatase 2A, a major tau regulator, results in abnormal tau hyperphosphorylation to disrupt protein homeostasis via oxidative damage and energy metabolic dysfunction [11, 39, 63]. Neurofibrillary tangles arise from the abnormal hyperphosphorylation of normal tau, leading to neurofibrillary degeneration that contributes to cognitive impairment in AD [26, 78]. Dual specificity tyrosine-phosphorylation regulated kinase 1A (DYRK1A) promotes tau phosphorylation and is overexpressed in DS, further contributing to neurofibrillary degeneration that may develop into AD pathogenesis [41, 68]. Tau accumulation is spatially similar in DS and AD throughout the brain originating from the entorhinal region to gradually spread into the limbic regions and eventually the isocortical regions [7, 8, 64, 75, 79, 83]. The frontal cortex (FCX) is suggested as an early region for AD symptoms to develop in DS, possibly underlying executive dysfunction and behavioral changes that align with the rate of cognitive deterioration [1, 22, 36].

Positron emission tomography (PET) studies using tau imaging agents provide visual opportunities to improve upon diagnostic strategies in multiple diseases including AD [27]. [18F]Flortaucipir (also known as T807 or AV-1451) was among the first tau PET tracers to show promise in evaluations of clinical AD with its strong affinity to tau aggregates [14, 46, 82]. In addition to AD, [18F]flortaucipir is being used in DS to show positivity for neurofibrillary tau tangles alongside Aβ plaque and p-tau217 accumulation in earlier ages compared to AD [80, 84]. However, issues such as selectivity of binding and low tau affinity in non-AD dementias of [18F]flortaucipir have been reported [44, 45, 47, 70]. Second generation tau PET tracers, such as [18F]MK-6240, sought to improve upon the capabilities of [18F]flortaucipir and has been validated throughout multiple in vivo and in vitro studies [2, 3, 17, 25, 59, 60, 77]. The reported advantages of [18F]MK-6240 compared to [18F]flortaucipir include higher affinity to tau aggregates, more sensitive detection of tau in early AD progression, and greater dynamic range in standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) estimates [5, 25]. Although [18F]MK-6240 improves upon [18F]flortaucipir, [18F]MK-6240 also exhibits off-target binding to the meninges in a number of PET studies [2, 21, 43]. Significant binding of [18F]MK-6240 was demonstrated in genetic frontotemporal dementia but not in other non-AD tauopathies such as progressive supranuclear palsy and Pick’s disease [2, 37]. More thorough investigations of [18F]MK-6240 and its binding characteristics in various conditions are necessary, especially when information about [18F]MK-6240 and other tau PET tracers in Down syndrome-associated Alzheimer’s disease (DSAD) is currently limited.

To continue the efforts to improve upon existing tau radiotracers, we have developed several azaindole derivatives, such as IPPI [54], INFT [40], and ISAS [71], to use on postmortem AD brain slices to uncover their capabilities. [125I]IPPI ([125I]3-(1H-pyrrolo[2,3-c]pyridine-1-yl)isoquinoline) effectively displayed high selectivity for tau in not only AD cases but also in DSAD cases, showing promise as a radioligand analog of [18F]MK-6240 for in vitro studies [6, 31]. The success of [18F]MK-6240 in AD cases implies potential as a tau imaging agent in DSAD [30]. Since the FCX is an early area in DS for AD symptoms and tau accumulation initially spreads to limbic regions including the temporal cortex (TCX), it is of interest to study the FCX and TCX for similarities in [18F]MK-6240 binding [36, 64, 65]. This comparative autoradiography study using [18F]MK-6240 in DSAD, AD, and cognitively normal (CN) cases can augment understanding of tau accumulation and its pathophysiological attributes in human FCX and TCX. The selective binding characteristics of [18F]MK-6240 was also further confirmed using drug effects. Efforts to understand and mitigate off-target binding of [18F]MK-6240 to meninges were also carried out.

Materials and methods

Human tissue samples

All postmortem human brain studies were approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee of University of California, Irvine. Human postmortem brain tissue samples of DSAD, AD, and CN cases (male and female), each consisting of FCX and TCX, were obtained from UCI Memory Impairment and Neurological Disorders (MIND) institute for in vitro experiments (Table 1). All DSAD and AD cases are positive for plaque (Table 1). Brain slices, 10 µm thick, were obtained from frozen tissue block using a Leica 1850 cryotome cooled to -20 °C and collected on Fisher glass slides. Brain slices from the tissue block of each case are within 200 microns from one another. All slides were then stored at -80 °C. Frozen cerebral meninges glass-mounted tissue slices (each slide containing two 10 µm thick slices) from adult normal subject, NBP2-77524, batch A610385 were purchased from Novus Biologicals, Colorado, USA. Immunohistochemistry

Brain slices of all cases and meninges were immunostained for tau by UCI Pathology core services. For total Tau, DAKO polyclonal antibody which detects all 6 six isoforms of tau from the microtubule-associated protein tau gene, was used at a dilution 1:3000, A0024 (Agilent, CA, USA) using reported protocols [19]. Slices of meninges and 08-42 DSAD FCX were similarly stained with H&E to compare with each other. Immunostained slides were scanned using the Ventana Roche slide scanner and the images generated were used for analysis using QuPath (version 0.4.4) [4]. Several annotations for tau and negative annotations with no tau were made on the immunohistochemistry (IHC) images of the brain slices for each case. After training on the manual annotations, a tau pixel classifier was run on the entire brain slice to provide a tau pixelwise image of each brain section as previously reported [53].

[18F]MK-6240 autoradiography

Brain sections and meninges were treated with approximately 10 µCi/mL [18F]MK-6240 (407 kBq/mL) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4. The chambers were incubated at 25 °C for 1 h and then underwent multiple washes of 100% PBS for 1 min, 70% ethanol in PBS for 2 min, 30% ethanol in PBS for 1 min, and lastly 100% PBS for 1 min [2]. The brain sections were air dried and then transferred into a film cassette with a phosphor screen film. After 24 h, the films were taken out of the cassette and read on the Phosphor Autoradiographic Imaging System/Cyclone Storage Phosphor System (Packard Instruments Co). The acquisition and analysis program OptiQuant (Packard Instruments Co, version 5.0) was used to draw regions of interest (ROIs) on the autoradiographic images and the extent of binding was measured in digital light unit per unit mm2 (DLU/mm2). Background activity levels were subtracted from all images. Higher DLU/mm2 from autoradiography indicated higher [18F]MK-6240 binding. Half-maximal inhibitor concentration (IC50) of harmine on [18F]MK-6240 binding was determined by using adjacent brain slices of FCX and TCX DSAD and AD cases with different concentrations (10–9 to 10–5 M) of harmine (Adooq Bioscience, Irvine, CA). Unlabeled MK-6240 (1ClickChemistry, Inc, Tinton Falls, New Jersey, USA) at 10 µM was designated for nonspecific binding to determine specific binding of [18F]MK-6240. Nonspecific binding was subtracted from the total binding to calculate specific binding at different concentrations of harmine. Plotting specific binding against harmine concentrations using GraphPad Prism 10 provided IC50 values for the different cases.

To investigate drug effects (tau and DYRK1A) on [18F]MK-6240 binding, unlabeled MK-6240, IPPI (3-(1H-pyrrolo[2,3-c]pyridine-1-yl)isoquinoline) and KuFal194 (10-iodo-11H-indolo[3,2-c]quinoline-6-carboxylic acid) (AABlocks LLC, San Diego, CA, USA) were used with [18F]MK-6240 binding in brain slices of FCX and TCX DSAD cases. The incubation of [18F]MK-6240 in the total binding and competition experiments with KuFal194 (10 µM) included 10 µM of 1,3-bis(4-cyanophenyl)urea (BCU) (1ClickChemistry, Inc, Tinton Falls, New Jersey, USA) as previously reported [58]. Inclusion of BCU has been shown to assist in membrane permeability of carboxylic acid containing drugs such as KuFal194 [69]. Incubation with BCU did not affect total binding of [18F]MK-6240. Incubation and washing procedures were the same as the other experiments in this study.

To investigate [18F]MK-6240 binding to meninges, total binding of [18F]MK-6240 to meninges slices was carried out. Additionally, [18F]MK-6240 binding in the presence of unlabeled MK-6240 (10 µM) for self-displacement and T807 (10 µM) (AABlocks LLC, San Diego, CA, USA) were carried out separately. Potential effects of reducing nonspecific binding by inclusion of 0.2% polyethylenimine (PEI, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in PBS incubation buffer was also investigated.

[125I]IPPI autoradiography

[125I]IPPI (0.17 µCi/mL) was also evaluated for binding to meninges along with other AD and DSAD FCX and TCX cases under normal PBS conditions and with PBS with 0.2% PEI. Incubation and washing procedures were the same as the other experiments in this study. Brain tissue treated with [125I]IPPI was exposed to a phosphor screen film for 2 weeks before reading the film.

Statistical analysis

The DLU/mm2 values from OptiQuant were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPrism Software, CA, USA) and Microsoft Excel 16. Student’s t-test were performed with p values < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. Error bars are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test confirmed normality of distribution among all groups. Pearson’s correlation and linear regression was used for the parametric comparison between [18F]MK-6240 and [125I]IPPI DLU/mm2 values [6] within DSAD and AD cases were determined in GraphPad Prism 10. Post hoc power analysis (Prism, version 10) with specified means and alpha = 0.05 indicated > 90% power for n = 5 DSAD and > 60% power for n = 5 AD cases when compared to CN cases in both FCX and TCX. Since [18F]MK-6240 has already been established in AD, greater power was prioritized in DSAD.

Results

[18F]MK-6240 binding in DSAD, AD, and CN cases

DSAD cases

Binding of [18F]MK-6240 was evaluated in all DSAD cases (Fig. 1). Figure 1A–D showed brain slices of representative case 12–36 DSAD FCX while Fig. 1E–H showed brain slices of case 12–36 DSAD TCX. Within brain slices of all cases, the presence of tau was confirmed by the anti-tau immunostainings (Fig. 1B, F). The tau pixel classifier confirmed the abundance of tau mostly within the gray matter (GM) regions (Fig. 1C, G). The areas on the slice where there was anti-tau detected and [18F]MK-6240 binding on autoradiographic images aligned with each other. There was substantially more [18F]MK-6240 binding in GM than white matter (WM) among both TCX and FCX regions (Fig. 1I J). The extent of GM binding of [18F]MK-6240 varied across the subjects in both the brain regions with the FCX showing higher average GM compared to TCX (Fig. 1J). The average GM/WM ratio in TCX was 2.5 while FCX had a GM/WM ratio of 3.1 in DSAD.

AD cases

Binding of [18F]MK-6240 in all AD cases were evaluated (Fig. 2). Figure 2A–D displayed brain slices of representative case 06–36 AD FCX while Fig. 2E–H displayed 06–36 AD TCX. Anti-tau immunostains (Fig. 2B, F) of the five AD cases revealed the presence of tau, confirmed by the tau pixel classifier (Fig. 2C, G). There was general alignment between the anti-tau IHC and the [18F]MK-6240 binding on AD TCX autoradiographic images. The [18F]MK-6240 binding in TCX and FCX cases were greater in GM compared to WM (Fig. 2I J). Variation in the GM binding of [18F]MK-6240 across the subjects was observed in both the brain regions with averaged [18F]MK-6240 binding similar in FCX and TCX (Fig. 2J). The average GM/WM ratio in TCX was 2.4 while FCX had a GM/WM ratio of 2.5 in AD.

CN cases

Figure 3 depicts [18F]MK-6240 binding in all CN cases. Figure 3A–D showed brain slices of representative case 01–25 CN FCX while Fig. 3E–H showed case 01–25 CN TCX. Anti-tau immunostains (Fig. 3B, F) of the five CN cases revealed little tau, confirmed by the tau pixel classifier (Fig. 3C, G). The locations of detected anti-tau and the [18F]MK-6240 binding on AD TCX autoradiographic image aligned, however, 03–07 had the highest [18F]MK-6240 binding amongst all CN cases. The CN cases 03–07 and 17–14 were assigned the highest tangle stage (Table 1) among all CN cases which may be attributed to higher [18F]MK-6240 binding (Fig. 3I). The average GM/WM ratio in TCX was 1.9 while FCX had a GM/WM ratio of 2.1 in CN. This higher ratio may be due to the two outlier CN cases. Regardless, all [18F]MK-6240 binding values in CN cases were noticeably lower than AD and DSAD. There were no significant differences between GM and WM in FCX and TCX (Fig. 3J).

Comparison of DSAD, AD and CN cases

Figure 4 summarizes the comparisons between DSAD, AD, and CN GM and WM in TCX and FCX [18F]MK-6240 binding. As expected, among both TCX (Fig. 4A) and FCX (Fig. 4B) regions, DSAD GM was significantly greater than CN GM. The average WM in both the DSAD and AD cases of TCX (Fig 4A) and FCX (Fig 4B) was higher than the CN cases as well. It is unclear if this higher WM is due to spillover effects of GM or there may be traces of tau within these regions [48, 62]. The GM/WM ratios for each case (DSAD and AD) resulted in no significant differences between TCX and FCX. In consideration of both FCX and TCX regions, the average GM/WM ratios were 2.8, 2.5, and 2.0 for DSAD, AD, and CN cases respectively. The higher average ratio in CN subjects was driven by the two outliers. Although within the CN, the GM/WM was higher than expected, the GM ratio of AD/CN and DSAD/CN were 2.9 and 3.5 respectively, confirming the diagnostic ability of [18F]MK-6240 for tau.

In vitro binding competition of [18F]MK-6240 by harmine

Selective in vitro [18F]MK-6240 binding patterns were investigated during drug competition studies using harmine which has been shown to bind to MAO-A and DYRK1A [58] (Fig. 5). Per case, there were three adjacent FCX and TCX brain slices within each of the five harmine concentrations. Specific binding was obtained by subtracting nonspecific binding ([18F]MK-6240 plus unlabeled MK-6240, Figs. 5B & 6B) from the total binding of [18F]MK-6240 (Figs. 5A & 6A). Similarly, specific binding of different [18F]MK-6240 + harmine concentrations were calculated (Figs. 5C, D & 6C, D). There was greater displacement of [18F]MK-6240 as harmine concentrations increased. The competitive effect of harmine was consistent within both TCX and FCX regions and across different DSAD (Fig. 5E) and AD cases (Fig. 6E). In DSAD cases, harmine had an IC50=290 ± 218 nM (08-42 FCX IC50=535 nM, 07-31 FCX IC50=222 nM, and 12-36 TCX IC50=115 nM) (Fig. 5E). In AD cases, harmine had an IC50=92 ± 15 nM (06-36 FCX IC50=81 nM and 06-36 TCX IC50=102 nM) (Fig. 6E). This displacement of [18F]MK-6240 by harmine in AD cases is in agreement with previously reported findings in AD where a single concentration of harmine was used [3].

In vitro competition of [18F]MK-6240

To further understand how [18F]MK-6240 binds in DSAD cases compared to AD cases, various drugs were used to observe their effects on [18F]MK-6240 binding in both TCX and FCX (Fig. 7). When compared to total [18F]MK-6240 binding (Fig. 7A, E), unlabeled MK-6240 and IPPI significantly inhibited [18F]MK-6240 binding which was expected and consistent with tau binding (Fig. 7I). Both DSAD and AD exhibited similar displacement in all drug groups. MK-6240 displaced [18F]MK-6240 by 88% among DSAD cases and 85% among AD cases. IPPI displaced [18F]MK-6240 by 81% and 74% in DSAD and AD cases respectively. KuFal194 minimally displaced [18F]MK-6240 by 34% in DSAD cases and 20% in AD cases but was not found to be significant (Fig. 7I).

In vitro meninges binding of [18F]MK-6240

PET studies using [18F]MK-6240 in AD subjects have reported off-target binding to the meninges [43, 49]. Understanding the [18F]MK-6240 binding characteristics in the meninges is necessary in order to minimize this off-target binding which will assist in the quantification of [18F]MK-6240 PET studies. All human meninges postmortem slices for this study were adjacent to one another and from the same CN case. Adjacent meninges slices were immunostained with H&E (Fig. 8A) and anti-tau (Fig. 8B). To quantify [18F]MK-6240 binding in the meninges, slices of CN meninges underwent various autoradiography studies. Substantial off-target binding of [18F]MK-6240 was confirmed throughout the meninges (Fig. 8C). Compared to total binding, there was self-blocking with 10 µM MK-6240 (44% decrease; Fig. 8D). Similarly, the tau agent T807 was not able to block this off-target binding of [18F]MK-6240 (30% decrease; Fig. 8E). Because of the weak blocking effect by tau drugs, buffer conditions of incubation of [18F]MK-6240 was changed by including PEI. There was a greater reduction of [18F]MK-6240 (70% decrease, Fig. 8F, G).

In vitro meninges binding of [125I]IPPI

The [18F]MK-6240 analog [125I]IPPI was also studied for in vitro binding to meninges and compare with DSAD and AD cases. [125I]IPPI comparatively exhibited little binding in the meninges under the same conditions as [18F]MK-6240 (Fig. 10). Discussion

Several novel radiotracers have been successfully developed to detect tau pathology in human AD, revolutionizing clinical diagnostic strategies. With the genetic predisposition of people with DS developing AD diagnosis, expanding upon this success in DSAD can provide more information about the features and progression of the disease. In this study, [18F]MK-6240 binding to tau was validated to be highly selective in DSAD within postmortem human brain slices of FCX and TCX GM, similar to AD. In both DSAD and AD cases, [18F]MK-6240 was significantly displaced by harmine, unlabeled MK-6240, and IPPI but not KuFal194. The meninges of a CN case were confirmed to have [18F]MK-6240 binding that was significantly self-displaced by 44% despite the minimal tau detected throughout the meninges slice. T807 also significantly displaced [18F]MK-6240 binding in meninges but the inclusion of PEI resulted in the greatest displacement in meninges, even more than PEI in DSAD and AD. [125I]IPPI binding to meninges was minimal.

As expected with the anti-tau distribution in IHC, binding of [18F]MK-6240 corresponded to tau in DSAD cases in FCX and TCX GM. One of the few in vitro studies of MK-6240 in DSAD confirmed [3H]MK-6240 binding to tau deposits in human hippocampus and medial frontal gyrus [36]. With both [3H]MK-6240 and [18F]MK-6240 binding to tau in human DSAD brains, this provides valuable insight into the capabilities of MK-6240 beyond AD. When comparing FCX and TCX regions, there were minimal differences in the quantification of [18F]MK-6240 binding to tau. Previous literature suggests slight preferential binding to TCX as the expected pattern of tau accumulation develops early in the temporal network [7, 76]. Since all the DSAD in this study were Braak stage VI, there was no clear distinction between FCX and TCX tau accumulation that may differ in ascending Braak stages. In DSAD stages III-VI, tau pathology appears more prevalent in the FCX [36]. Regardless, these results validate the potential in using [18F]MK-6240 as a reliable tau imaging agent in both DSAD FCX and TCX.

[18F]MK-6240 binding was inhibited by increasing concentrations of harmine with each case and exhibited similar IC50s. This is in agreement to our previous observations on the effects of harmine on [125I]IPPI [31]. Harmine binds to multiple forms of phosphorylated tau including total tau which is abundant in all DSAD and AD cases [23, 42, 72]. However, harmine may not be the most informational agent for tau since it binds to not only phosphorylated tau but also to DYRK1A and MAO-A, therefore weakening its binding specificity [23, 42, 72]. DYRK1A inhibition has previously been shown to improve cognitive function in DS but the link between DYRK1A and tau is only established in postmortem human tissue [20, 41]. DYRK1A inhibitors are a potential therapeutic strategy to prevent tau hyperphosphorylation so determining compounds that inhibit DYRK1A can attest to this strategy [15, 16, 42, 58]. However, in our study, the DYRK1A agent KuFal194 did not substantially inhibit [18F]MK-6240 binding among DSAD and AD cases. Similarly, the MAO-A inhibitor clorgyline did not inhibit [18F]MK-6240 binding [3], so harmine by inference seems to uniquely compete with tau agents at a tau-related binding site. Thus, [18F]MK-6240 binding appears selective to tau and not to MAO-A sites or to DYRK1A sites in the brain slices. Additional verification of this harmine effect on [18F]MK-6240 is required. Similar findings of harmine were reported for [125I]IPPI which is an analog of MK-6240 [31].

To accurately assess tau accumulation, tracking and minimizing off-target binding is ideal. Ongoing PET studies in human subjects have identified off-target binding to the meninges and have made efforts to address the issue [43, 49, 74]. In a recent PET study of 42 subjects, a head-to-head comparison of [18F]MK-6240 and [18F]AV-1451 showed significantly higher meningeal uptake in [18F]MK-6240 [21]. In a larger PET imaging study of 433 early AD subjects, 35% of the subjects exhibited moderate to high extra-cerebral off-target meningeal binding of [18F]MK-6240 [49]. Correction factors were developed to correct for the spill-in effects into the cortical regions of this off-target binding. PET imaging data of about 500 older adults without significant cognitive impairment used imaging processing methods including partial volume correction for quantifying the off-target signal from [18F]MK-6240 [29]. Meningeal binding was unimodally distributed in relation to adjacent target regions, contaminating SUVRs of target regions [29]. This finding aligned with another [18F]MK-6240 study that found meningeal uptake to significantly impact measuring cortical differences in SUVR of 40 healthy controls (average SUVR=1.29) and 10 amnestic mild cognitive impairment (average SUVR=1.12) [51]. Kinetics of longitudinal changes in the meningeal binding of [18F]MK-6240 in 24 cognitively normal and 8 cognitively impaired subjects have been reported [24]. About 50% of the subjects exhibited irreversible meningeal binding with little longitudinal changes over a period of 12 months, emphasizing the need for correction factors and explanations for meningeal binding [24]. Magnitude and location of [18F]MK-6240 varied in different subjects and inter-subject in CN extracerebral regions including the meninges, requiring partial volume correction techniques [43, 74]. Other factors may influence differential [18F]MK-6240 meningeal binding including sex differences and APOE4 carriage [50, 73]. Correction methods for the off-target binding in skull and meninges are currently being used for obtaining quantitative measures of tau PET radiotracers [56].

In an effort to understand this meningeal binding of [18F]MK-6240, for an otherwise very selective PET imaging agent, we carried out a preliminary study using postmortem meninges. Our postmortem meninges tissue evaluation revealed extensive [18F]MK-6240 binding which was off-target since there was an absence of tau confirmed by immunostains. A majority of this binding in the meninges was therefore not displaceable by tau agents, MK-6240 and T807. The partial blocking by T807 could be explained by sharing regions for off-target binding with [18F]MK-6240 primarily to the meninges and substantia nigra [5, 44]. Similar to T807, [18F]MK-6240 potentially has off-target binding to cells containing neuromelanin and melanin regardless of tau presence [2, 44]. Meninges contain melanocytes which have high affinity for amines and are distinctly distributed per individual [28, 35]. The cationic polymer PEI inhibited [18F]MK-6240 binding the most. Nonspecific binding has been minimized by using up to 1% PEI with carbon-11 and fluorine-18 labeled radioligands binding to cloned receptors and human AD brain tissue [10, 12, 13, 34, 67]. Electrostatic interactions of PEI with negatively charged cell surfaces may affect specific binding between ligands and receptors including in the cerebral meninges [32, 38]. Such an interaction at the tau binding site is the likely cause for the small decrease in binding of both [18F]MK-6240 and [125I]IPPI in the presence of PEI on FCX and TCX sections of DSAD and AD subjects. The greatest effect of PEI was on the CN meninges. The dural lymphatic system importantly facilitates the clearance of extracellular tau so its dysfunction results in tau deposition and therefore promoting neurodegeneration [61, 66]. Meningeal lymphatic vessels are crucial for fluid drainage but exhibit a decrease in function with increasing age [18, 33]. The CN case used for meninges was 66, an age that is at risk of dysfunction of the lymphatic system. However, there was little to no tau detected via anti-tau staining on an adjacent slice.

To further ascertain this meninges binding, we used [125I]IPPI, which is an analog for [18F]MK-6240. There was little [125I]IPPI binding in meninges while [18F]MK-6240 bound to meninges in a comparable amount to DSAD FCX (Fig. 11C, D, H). The primary structural difference between the two radiotracers is the amine group on [18F]MK-6240 which is not present on [125I]IPPI (Fig. 11A, E). Otherwise, there are no geometric differences between the molecular structures of the two radiotracers as visualized in the energy-minimized 3D structures (Fig. 11B, F). Both radiotracers bound to DSAD and AD FCX and TCX regions similarly while there was a major difference in meninges binding (Fig. 11I) The unique features of [18F]MK-6240 off-target binding including the variability in meningeal binding among different subjects as discussed above must continue to be investigated in order to maximize quantitative accuracy in [18F]MK-6240 binding throughout the brain.

Despite the valuable findings of this study, there are also limitations. There is a small number of DSAD, AD, and CN cases categorized in the same Braak stage, offering minimal diversity in tau abundance and disease severity. Some inter-case variability across brain slices was present but did not affect the acquisition of [18F]MK-6240 binding. Cases were not age matched between different groups, so CN cases were the oldest while DSAD and AD cases were more similar in age. The meningeal findings are preliminary, and a larger study is necessary. More cases of meninges, including AD and DSAD, are required in order to further confirm results from this study. Nevertheless, this study still establishes the use of [18F]MK-6240 as a radioligand to elucidate tau accumulation in DSAD while future studies will aim to improve upon the listed limitations.

In conclusion, the autoradiography binding features of [18F]MK-6240 hold promise in its application in FCX and TCX regions in DSAD brains similar to AD tauopathy. Current AD strategies can be applied to DSAD as an initial basis and then adapted to best approach unique features of DSAD. Postmortem meningeal findings of [18F]MK-6240 are preliminary and a larger study with meninges is warranted.
