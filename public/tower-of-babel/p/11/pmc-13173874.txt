Preharvest silicon nutrition mitigates vibration-induced senescence and extends vase life in cut carnation
Authors: Atefeh Safaei Far, Sadegh Mousavi-Fard, Abdolhossein Rezaei Nejad, Feizollah Shahbazi, Bahram Hosseinzadeh Samani, Dimitrios Fanourakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transportation-related mechanical vibration is a major but often overlooked factor reducing the postharvest quality of cut flowers. This study investigated whether preharvest foliar applications of silicon (Si)- and calcium (Ca)-based compounds can mitigate vibration-induced deterioration in cut carnation. Plants were sprayed with potassium silicate, sodium silicate, calcium silicate, nano-silicon, calcium chloride, or calcium nitrate at 100 and 200 mg L⁻¹, and harvested flowers were exposed to simulated road vibration (10 Hz, 10 min). Vase life, relative fresh weight, stem mechanical properties, water relations, oxidative stress markers, pigment content, antioxidant enzyme activities, and leaf nutrient concentrations were evaluated. Simulated vibration significantly reduced vase life, relative water content, pigment pools, and stem mechanical strength, while increasing malondialdehyde accumulation and electrolyte leakage. Foliar application of Si-based compounds markedly alleviated these effects. Under non-stress conditions, potassium silicate at 200 mg L⁻¹ produced the greatest improvements in vase life (by 2.5 days), stem rigidity, antioxidant capacity, and pigment retention. Under vibration stress, nano-silicon at 100 mg L⁻¹ and potassium silicate at 100–200 mg L⁻¹ extended vase life by 2.0–2.8 days, while restoring water balance, limiting oxidative damage, and enhancing phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, peroxidase, and catalase activities. Multivariate analyses (PCA and TOPSIS) consistently ranked potassium silicate and nano-silicon as the most effective treatments. These findings demonstrate that Si nutrition primes coordinated physiological, biochemical, and structural defense mechanisms that enhance the resilience of cut carnations to vibration-induced stress. Preharvest foliar application of potassium silicate or nano-silicon (100 mg L⁻¹) represents a practical and sustainable strategy to improve postharvest quality and extend vase life following transportation.

Introduction

In the horticultural commodity trade production and retail sites are commonly separated by national international and often intercontinental distances inevitably exposing products to prolonged transportation [14, 41]. During transport horticultural commodities are subjected to continuous and irregular mechanical vibrations that are largely overlooked in postharvest management strategies [51]. Such vibration-induced stress has been associated with reductions in vase life [3]. Importantly vibration-related damage is often not externally visible during transport but manifests during subsequent distribution and retail stages where poor postharvest longevity becomes evident [29]. Cultivation regimes that reduce vulnerability to vibration-induced stress are therefore highly desirable.

Mechanical vibration imposes repetitive dynamic loading on stems which may compromise their physical integrity [53]. Stem bending resistance and elastic modulus are critical quality attributes in cut flowers as they determine the ability of the stem to remain upright and maintain the floral display [40]. Reduced stem stiffness increases susceptibility to neck bending vascular collapse and xylem dysfunction all of which impair water transport and accelerate wilting [31]. Therefore mechanical properties such as stem diameter bending resistance and modulus of elasticity are not merely structural traits but functional determinants of postharvest performance and vase life [39]. However, the effects of transport-induced vibration on these mechanical properties remain largely unexplored.

Beyond structural impacts vibration stress triggers physiological and biochemical responses that influence postharvest performance. Wilting is the principal event terminating vase life in cut flowers and is fundamentally the outcome of impaired water relations [1, 13]. Maintenance of a favourable water balance depends on both sustained water uptake through a functional xylem network and restriction of transpirational water loss. Mechanical vibration has been shown to disturb water relations thereby directly contributing to vibration-induced reductions in vase life [29]. Vibration stress is also closely linked with oxidative imbalance [29]. Mechanical perturbation accelerates the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) resulting in lipid peroxidation increased electrolyte leakage and progressive membrane destabilization [2]. Consequently the antioxidant system becomes a key determinant of postharvest longevity as enhanced enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant capacity mitigates oxidative damage and preserves cellular functionality [5]. In parallel chlorophyll degradation is a sensitive indicator of physiological deterioration and oxidative injury [37, 50]. Retention of chlorophyll and carotenoids reflects preservation of chloroplast integrity and metabolic homeostasis both of which are prerequisites for sustaining cellular repair mechanisms during vase life [22]. Earlier findings indicate that vibration-stimulated oxidative stress partly underlies the observed reduction in vase life [29].

Among mineral nutrients Ca and Si play central roles in reinforcing plant structural and physiological resilience [52]. Ca stabilizes membranes and cell walls through pectate cross-linking and contributes to the maintenance of tissue cohesion and selective permeability [44]. Si although not essential is increasingly recognized as a multifunctional element that enhances tolerance to abiotic stresses by strengthening cell walls improving water relations and stimulating antioxidant defenses [36, 38]. Recent studies in ornamental crops have demonstrated that Si and Ca applications can reduce stem bending maintain relative water content (RWC) preserve pigment levels and extend vase life [18, 23]. Mineral fortification with Ca or Si strengthens cell walls and cuticular barriers improving firmness and stress resistance yet their effects are species- and formulation-dependent [12]. Whether such fortification can alleviate vibration-induced stress, and which chemical forms and application rates are most effective, remains unknown.

Although transport-induced mechanical vibration has been recognized as an important but underestimated postharvest stress factor in cut flowers existing studies have mainly focused on describing its detrimental effects on vase life and water relations [29], without identifying effective preharvest mitigation strategies. In parallel, Si and Ca nutrition have been widely reported to improve postharvest quality and stress tolerance in ornamental crops, yet their role in conferring resilience specifically against vibration-induced mechanical stress remains largely unexplored. In particular, there is a lack of integrated studies combining biomechanical measurements with physiological and biochemical assessments to explain how nutrient-based preharvest treatments may protect stem structural integrity, membrane stability, and antioxidant capacity under vibration stress. Therefore, targeted preharvest mineral nutrition represents a promising but largely untested strategy for mitigating vibration-induced damage in cut flowers. Carnation (Dianthus caryophyllus L.) was selected as a model species, as it is among the most commercially important cut flowers worldwide and is particularly sensitive to postharvest physiological deterioration during transportation.

The objectives of this study were to (i) quantify the effects of preharvest foliar applications of different Si- and Ca-based compounds on stem mechanical properties, water relations, oxidative status, and postharvest longevity of cut carnation flowers under simulated vibration stress (SVS), and (ii) identify the most effective nutrient sources and application rates for mitigating vibration-induced damage. We hypothesized that Si-based treatments would be more effective than Ca-based treatments in enhancing stem mechanical strength and stress tolerance under vibration, through improved membrane stability, water relations, antioxidant capacity, and pigment retention, ultimately leading to extended vase life.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

The experiment was conducted in the research greenhouse of the Faculty of Agriculture, Lorestan University, covered with polycarbonate panels (Khorramabad, Iran; 33°29′ N, 48°21′ E; 1147 m a.s.l.). The warm season was selected to ensure stable plant growth, rapid biomass accumulation, and uniform flowering, thereby allowing the imposed foliar treatments to exert their full physiological effects before harvest. During this period, environmental conditions were regulated using a shading system (providing up to 30% light reduction, depending on solar radiation) and a pad–and–fan cooling unit. Mean daytime air temperature ranged from 20 to 25 °C, nighttime temperature from 10 to 13 °C, relative air humidity from 50 to 60%, and photosynthetic photon flux density from 400 to 500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

Rooted cuttings of carnation (Dianthus caryophyllus L. cv. ‘White Liberty’) were obtained from a commercial nursery in Mahalat County, and were approximately 4–6 weeks old at the time of transplanting. This cultivar was selected because of its wide commercial use and documented sensitivity to stem bending and postharvest deterioration, making it a suitable model for assessing vibration-induced stress and mitigation strategies. Each 4‑L plastic pot contained a single carnation plant grown in a substrate consisting of two parts fully decomposed manure (produced at Lorestan University), one part agricultural soil (loamy soil collected from the experimental farm of Lorestan University; pH 7.2, organic matter 1.8%), and one part sand (washed river sand with particle size 0.5–2.0 mm) (v/v/v). After establishment, all plants were pinched at the fourth node to synchronize lateral shoot development and ensure uniform stem length and flowering time across treatments, thereby minimizing morphological variability unrelated to the applied treatments.

During the experimental period, plants were fertigated automatically, maintaining substrate moisture close to field capacity, using a standard nutrient solution for carnation cultivation (EC 1.8–2.0 dS m⁻¹; pH 6.0), supplied via a drip irrigation system every 2–3 days depending on climatic conditions. Drainage was monitored periodically to avoid salt accumulation in the substrate. No growth regulators were applied at any stage of cultivation. Pest and disease incidence was monitored regularly through visual inspection, and no pesticide or fungicide applications were required during the experiment. All plants were managed uniformly according to standard commercial carnation cultivation practices.

One month after pinching foliar treatments were initiated using potassium silicate (K₂SiO₃; 20% SiO₂ 10% K₂O) sodium silicate (Na₂SiO₃; 27% SiO₂ 9% Na₂O) calcium silicate (CaSiO₃; 18% SiO₂ 25% CaO) nano-silicon (SiO₂ nanoparticles; >99% purity particle size 20–40 nm) calcium chloride (CaCl₂·2 H₂O; 27% Ca) and calcium nitrate (Ca(NO₃)₂·4 H₂O; 19% Ca 15.5% N) each applied at 100 and 200 mg L⁻¹ in addition to a water-sprayed control. For the aqueous solutions used in this study mg L⁻¹ is equivalent to ppm (1 ppm ≈ 1 mg L⁻¹) and mg L⁻¹ is used consistently throughout the manuscript. All products were obtained from Zist Azma Biotech Co. (Mashhad Iran) and handled according to manufacturer specifications. These chemical forms were selected to represent the major commercially available Si and Ca sources with contrasting solubility ionic composition and bioavailability while the selected concentration range was based on earlier reports demonstrating physiological efficacy without phytotoxicity in ornamental crops [8, 33]. Nano-silicon was included because its reduced particle size markedly enhances leaf surface adhesion, penetration through the cuticle, and cellular availability of Si, thereby enabling rapid physiological responses at comparatively low concentrations. Foliar sprays were applied weekly from 30 to 120 days after planting, and every two weeks thereafter until harvest, resulting in a total of 19 applications over the 7-month cultivation period. Foliar applications were performed using a hand-held pressure sprayer (2 l capacity; nozzle type: cone spray, 0.8 mm orifice; local commercial brand, Iran). Spraying was conducted at a constant walking speed of approximately 0.5 m s⁻¹, delivering a spray volume of approximately 25 mL per plant, until full leaf wetting without runoff. Applications were carried out in the early morning to minimize evaporation and ensure uniform leaf coverage. Foliar application was chosen over root-zone supply to ensure rapid uptake, uniform distribution, and minimal interference from substrate heterogeneity, while also reflecting common commercial practices in floriculture.

This application regime was selected to ensure consistent nutrient deposition and uptake throughout canopy development and stem formation, and to allow stable treatment differentiation at harvest. Because foliar-applied Si and Ca can be gradually diluted by new growth and partially removed during leaf expansion, repeated applications are commonly used in research to maintain a sustained physiological “nutritional status” rather than a transient pulse. While this schedule is more intensive than typical commercial practice, it provides a robust proof-of-concept for vibration-stress mitigation and allows evaluation of the maximum potential of Si- and Ca-based treatments under controlled conditions.

The experiment was arranged as a factorial completely randomized design. Each individual pot containing a single plant was considered one experimental unit, and pots were randomly assigned to foliar treatments. All foliar applications were performed at the pot level, and each pot received the same treatment throughout the experimental period. To minimize positional effects, pots were randomly distributed within the greenhouse. To minimize edge effects, border plants were excluded from all measurements and analyses.

A total of 260 plants were used for measurements in the study. To minimize edge effects, additional border plants were included around the experimental units. Based on the greenhouse layout, approximately 200 border plants were grown, resulting in a total of ~ 460 plants. Each foliar treatment was applied to 10 biological replicates, with one plant per pot representing a single replicate. Only the 260 core plants were sampled for analysis, while the border plants were excluded from all measurements.

At the appearance of flower buds stems were harvested at the brush stage which represents the commercially optimal harvest stage for carnation. To impose mechanical stress harvested flowers were exposed to simulated road vibration at 10 Hz for 10 min using a vibration simulator following the protocol described by [29]. This vibration regime was selected because low-frequency mechanical excitation (including the 8–12 Hz range) is frequently reported as a dominant component of road transport vibration spectra for horticultural products and often coincides with structural resonance frequencies that exacerbate mechanical stress. Although actual transport durations are much longer than 10 min, short-term exposure at this frequency range is commonly used in laboratory studies as an accelerated stress test to reproduce cumulative mechanical effects under controlled conditions.

Vase life and relative fresh weight

Vase life was defined as the period during which cut flowers maintained acceptable commercial and aesthetic quality. The end of vase life was determined when at least one of the following criteria was observed: bending of the flower neck loss of more than two petals visible wilting or yellowing drying or abscission of more than 50% of the leaves [15, 49]. All termination criteria were weighted equally, and vase life was recorded as the first occurrence of any one of these endpoints.

Prior to placement in holding solutions, the initial fresh weight of each flower stem (FW0) and the weight of the containers with solutions were recorded using a precision balance (± 0.01 g). Throughout the vase-life period flower and solution weights were recorded at 48-h intervals. Leaf antioxidant enzyme activity

Catalase activity

CAT activity was determined according to [19]. Fresh leaf tissue (0.3 g) was ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 1.5 mL of potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) containing 1 mM EDTA and 2% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP). The homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used for enzyme assays. The reaction mixture consisted of 400 µL potassium phosphate buffer (12.5 mM, pH 7.0), 300 µL H₂O₂ solution (prepared by diluting 20 µL H₂O₂ in 20 mL distilled water), and 50 µL enzyme extract. The decrease in absorbance at 240 nm was recorded every 10 s for 2 min at 25 °C using a spectrophotometer. CAT activity was expressed as µmol H₂O₂ decomposed min⁻¹ g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW).

Peroxidase activity

POD activity was assayed according to [30]. Fresh leaf tissue (0.3 g) was homogenized in 1.5 mL potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was used as the enzyme extract. The reaction mixture contained 1.2 mL potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 50 µL H₂O₂, 50 µL guaiacol, and 50 µL enzyme extract. The increase in absorbance at 475 nm was recorded every 10 s for 2 min at 25 °C. POD activity was expressed as µmol H₂O₂ decomposed min⁻¹ g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW).

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase activity

PAL activity was determined according to [45]. Fresh leaf tissue (0.1 g) was ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 1 mL of 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was used as the enzyme extract. The reaction mixture contained 1000 µL of 50 mM Tris buffer (pH 8.8), 1200 µL of 1 mM phenylalanine, and 200 µL of enzyme extract and was incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by adding 500 µL of 4 N HCl, followed by extraction with 2 mL toluene and vortexing for 10 s. The absorbance of the upper phase was measured at 290 nm. PAL activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of trans-cinnamic acid (ε = 2000 l mmol⁻¹ cm⁻¹) and expressed as µmol min⁻¹ g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW).

Malondialdehyde content

Lipid peroxidation was quantified as malondialdehyde (MDA) according to [43]. Fresh leaf tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized in 5 mL of 20% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) containing 0.5% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid (TBA). The homogenate was incubated at 100 °C for 15 min, rapidly cooled in an ice bath, and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 15 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 450, 532, and 600 nm. MDA concentration was calculated using the standard TBA–MDA equations and expressed as µmol g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW).

Leaf nutrient analysis

Leaf calcium and potassium content

Dried leaf samples (0.1 g) collected from the third node were digested in a mixed acid solution consisting of 2 mL concentrated HCl (37%) and 3 mL concentrated HNO₃ (65%). Samples were pre-digested for 24 h at room temperature and subsequently heated in a water bath at 80–90 °C for 30–45 min until complete dissolution. After cooling, the digests were filtered through Whatman No. 42 filter paper and diluted to a final volume of 50 mL with deionized water. Ca and K concentrations were determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; SPECTRO ARCOS, Germany) and expressed as mg kg⁻¹ dry weight (DW).

Leaf silicon content

Dried leaf samples (0.1 g) were digested following[16]. Each sample was placed in a 50 mL polypropylene tube and treated with 0.3 mL of concentrated NaOH (18.5 M). Samples were heated on a laboratory hot plate until complete dissolution, then cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, 2 mL H₂O₂ was added and the mixture was heated at 200 °C for 15 min. After cooling, the digests were filtered, and 9 mL deionized water was added to each 1 mL of filtrate to obtain a 1:10 dilution. Si concentration was determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; SPECTRO ARCOS, Germany). Values were corrected for dilution and expressed as mg kg⁻¹ dry weight (DW).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SAS (version 9.4) and Minitab (version 21). The number of biological replicates varied according to the type of measurement. Vase life, relative fresh weight, leaf and petal water relations, ion leakage, pigment content, and antioxidant enzyme activities were evaluated using ten biological replicates per treatment (n = 10). Stem mechanical properties and nutrient analyses were performed using four biological replicates per treatment (n = 4), due to the destructive and instrument-intensive nature of these analyses. In all cases, each biological replicate represented an independent plant (one stem per pot). The experiment was arranged in a factorial completely randomized design, and data were subjected to two-way ANOVA to evaluate the effects of foliar treatment, concentration, and their interaction. Following a significant two-way ANOVA, mean separation was performed using Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) test at p ≤ 0.05. Fisher’s LSD was selected because comparisons were planned a priori among a predefined set of treatments and were conducted only when the overall F-test was significant, consistent with common practice in factorial plant physiology experiments with well-defined treatment structures. Prior to mean separation, data were checked for normality and homogeneity of variances, and no major violations of ANOVA assumptions were detected. Where relevant, ANOVA main effects and interactions are reported, and mean separation was applied only within significant effects.

Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted in the R environment (version 4.3.1) using RStudio (version 2023.06.1). Multi-criteria decision analysis was performed using the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) to rank the 26 foliar treatments based on 18 quantitative traits and to identify the optimal Ca and Si treatments under SVS conditions.

Under non-stress conditions, foliar application of Si and Ca compounds significantly increased vase life compared with the untreated control. The greatest enhancement was recorded with potassium silicate at 200 mg L⁻¹ (K200), which extended vase life to nearly 15 d.

When flowers were subjected to SVS, foliar sprays markedly alleviated the detrimental effects of vibration. The most effective treatment was nano-silicon at 100 mg L⁻¹ (B100), restoring vase life to approximately 13–14 d. In contrast, sodium silicate treatments were consistently less effective, demonstrating that both the chemical form and concentration of the applied nutrient strongly determined the mitigation of SVS-induced senescence.

Discussion

Transportation-related mechanical vibration is a major but frequently underestimated cause of postharvest deterioration in cut flowers [29]. In the present study, SVS significantly shortened vase life (Fig. 2), impaired water relations (Table 2), weakened stem mechanical properties (Fig. 3a–c), promoted oxidative damage (Table 2), and altered nutrient homeostasis (Fig. 5a–c) in carnation flowers. These findings confirm earlier observations that vibration accelerates senescence by disrupting membrane integrity inducing oxidative stress and compromising xylem functionality [3, 29].

Silicon-based treatments alleviate vibration-induced senescence

Among all foliar treatments tested, potassium silicate and nano-silicon consistently provided the greatest protection against vibration stress. Under non-stress conditions, potassium silicate at 200 mg L⁻¹ resulted in the highest vase life (Fig. 2), RFW (Table 1), pigment retention and antioxidant enzyme activities (Table 3), and stem mechanical strength (Fig. 3a–c), whereas under vibration stress nano-silicon at 100 mg L⁻¹ and potassium silicate at 100–200 mg L⁻¹ were the most effective. This clear concentration-dependent response demonstrates that the physiological efficacy of Si is strongly governed by both its chemical form and application rate, likely reflecting differences in foliar penetration efficiency, translocation, and cellular bioavailability.

Si is deposited in plant tissues as amorphous silica (SiO₂·nH₂O) phytoliths particularly in epidermal cells xylem vessels and sclerenchyma fibers where it forms a silica–cellulose framework that reinforces cell walls and increases tissue stiffness [25, 25]. Such deposition enhances resistance to bending forces and repetitive dynamic loading, as imposed by transportation-related vibration. In the present study, the strong positive correlations between stem diameter, bending force, elastic modulus, and vase life (Fig. 4) confirm that Si-induced structural reinforcement is a critical determinant of postharvest longevity under mechanical stress.

Maintenance of water relations and membrane stability

SVS markedly reduced RWC and increased electrolyte leakage and MDA accumulation in untreated control flowers (Table 2) indicating extensive membrane destabilization and lipid peroxidation. These changes are typical symptoms of vibration-induced oxidative stress reflecting loss of membrane selectivity disruption of cellular compartmentalization and impaired water transport capacity [17, 42].

Although several physiological traits were measured in leaves, these parameters are mechanistically linked to floral longevity because leaves remain the primary source of water, carbohydrates, and systemic stress signaling supporting cut flower function after harvest. Leaf water status directly influences whole-cut-flower hydraulic conductance and transpiration-driven xylem flow, thereby affecting water supply to floral organs. Similarly, leaf antioxidant capacity and membrane stability reflect systemic oxidative status and vascular integrity, which regulate carbohydrate remobilization and senescence progression. Therefore, improvements in leaf hydration, membrane stability, and antioxidant defense are expected to translate into enhanced stem functionality and prolonged cut flower vase life.

In contrast, potassium silicate and nano-silicon treatments substantially reduced MDA accumulation and ion leakage while maintaining significantly higher leaf and petal RWC (Table 2). These responses demonstrate that Si enhanced membrane stability and preserved cellular hydration both of which are essential for sustaining turgor pressure xylem functionality and metabolic activity during vase life [13, 52]. Si deposition at the cuticle and cell wall level likely reduced transpirational water loss and strengthened apoplastic barriers, thereby limiting stress-induced dehydration.

Calcium chloride also improved water relations and partially reduced membrane leakage (Table 2), consistent with the role of Ca in stabilizing phospholipid bilayers and cross-linking homogalacturonan in cell walls. However, its protective effects were weaker and less consistent than those of Si. The comparatively lower effectiveness of Ca under vibration stress may be related to its limited mobility and mode of action within plant tissues. Ca is largely immobile in the phloem and its protective role is primarily restricted to membrane stabilization and pectate cross-linking in existing cell walls, without substantially enhancing structural reinforcement or stress-induced metabolic reprogramming. In contrast, Si is actively deposited in epidermal and vascular tissues and contributes directly to mechanical strengthening, cell wall thickening, and stimulation of antioxidant and phenylpropanoid pathways. As a result, Ca alone may be insufficient to counteract the combined mechanical and oxidative components of vibration stress, whereas Si provides both biochemical and biomechanical protection.

Activation of antioxidant and phenylpropanoid pathways

SVS induced substantial oxidative pressure, as evidenced by the modulation of CAT, POD, and PAL activities (Table 3). Si treatments, particularly potassium silicate and nano-silicon, significantly enhanced the activity of all three enzymes under both non-stress and vibration stress conditions, indicating a priming effect of Si on the plant antioxidant machinery.

It should be noted that the very large relative increases in PAL activity (Table 3) likely reflect at least in part a stress-induced overcompensation response rather than a simple linear improvement in physiological status. Such strong induction of PAL is characteristic of defense-related metabolic reprogramming where phenylpropanoid biosynthesis is rapidly upregulated to reinforce cell walls and enhance stress tolerance [28]. Therefore, high PAL activity should be interpreted as an indicator of active stress adaptation and structural reinforcement, rather than as a direct proxy of improved metabolic performance per se.

The stimulation of CAT and POD activities [10, 11, 21] reflects a more efficient detoxification of hydrogen peroxide and other ROS thereby preventing excessive lipid peroxidation and preserving membrane functionality [9, 27, 35, 47]. This enzymatic protection is consistent with the reduced MDA accumulation and electrolyte leakage observed in Si-treated plants (Table 2) and underscores the central role of ROS scavenging in maintaining postharvest quality.

The pronounced induction of PAL activity under vibration stress in Si-treated flowers further suggests activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway which is responsible for the biosynthesis of lignin and a wide range of phenolic compounds [20]. Increased PAL activity is closely linked with secondary cell wall thickening and enhanced sclerenchyma development [26] processes that directly improve stem rigidity and resistance to bending (Fig. 3a–c). This coordinated activation of antioxidant and structural biosynthetic pathways provides a mechanistic explanation for how Si nutrition translates into superior stem mechanical strength and extended vase life under mechanical stress.

Preservation of pigment pools and metabolic functionality

SVS caused a pronounced decline in chlorophyll and carotenoid contents in untreated control flowers (Table 3) indicating chloroplast disintegration and impairment of primary metabolic processes. Such pigment loss is a typical hallmark of oxidative injury and accelerated senescence [2], as disruption of thylakoid membranes compromises both light harvesting and photoprotective capacity.

In contrast, potassium silicate at 200 mg L⁻¹ under non-stress conditions and nano-silicon at 100 mg L⁻¹ under vibration stress were the most effective treatments in preserving pigment pools (Table 3). Maintenance of chlorophyll reflects sustained chloroplast integrity and metabolic homeostasis while elevated carotenoid concentrations provide additional photoprotection by quenching singlet oxygen and scavenging ROS [17]. Through this dual role, carotenoids limit oxidative damage to photosynthetic membranes, thereby delaying senescence and sustaining cellular repair processes during vase life.

Nutrient homeostasis under vibration stress

Leaf nutrient analysis showed that SVS reduced Si, Ca, and K concentrations in untreated control flowers (Fig. 5a–c) indicating that mechanical stress disrupts nutrient retention and internal ionic balance. Such nutrient depletion is likely linked with impaired membrane selectivity reduced phloem transport efficiency and enhanced ion leakage associated with oxidative damage [34].

Foliar application of potassium silicate markedly increased both Si and K accumulation, while calcium chloride enhanced leaf Ca concentration (Fig. 5a–c) confirming the effective uptake of these elements through the leaf surface [46]. These changes are physiologically meaningful as Si and K act synergistically to improve osmotic adjustment tissue rigidity and hydraulic conductance whereas Ca contributes to membrane stabilization and cell wall cohesion through pectate cross-linking [21, 38].

In contrast sodium silicate treatments reduced leaf Si concentration and frequently exacerbated stress symptoms probably due to sodium-induced ionic imbalance and limited Si bioavailability [34]. Therefore, careful selection of Si source and concentration is essential for maintaining nutrient homeostasis and enhancing postharvest resilience under vibration stress.

Multivariate integration of treatment effects

The multivariate analyses provided strong integrative validation of the univariate results. Both PCA and TOPSIS consistently identified Si-based treatments as the most effective in enhancing physiological performance and postharvest quality. Treatments based on potassium silicate and nano-silicon clustered on the positive side of PC1 in the PCA biplot (Fig. 6) and achieved the highest relative closeness values in TOPSIS ranking (Fig. 7), with potassium silicate at 200 mg L⁻¹ emerging as the overall optimal treatment.

The congruence between PCA clustering and TOPSIS ranking demonstrates that improvements in vase life, water relations, stem mechanical strength, pigment retention, antioxidant enzyme activity, and nutrient accumulation are not independent responses but part of a coordinated physiological syndrome induced by Si nutrition (Fig. 8).

Taken together, the present findings provide direct validation of the working hypothesis stated in the Introduction. We hypothesized that Si-based treatments would be more effective than Ca-based treatments in enhancing stem mechanical strength and stress tolerance under vibration through coordinated improvements in membrane stability, water relations, antioxidant capacity, and pigment retention, ultimately extending vase life. The results clearly support this hypothesis. Across all physiological, biochemical, and mechanical parameters, potassium silicate and nano-silicon consistently outperformed Ca-based treatments under both non-stress and vibration stress conditions. Improvements in stem bending resistance and elastic modulus were strongly correlated with extended vase life, while reductions in MDA accumulation and ion leakage, together with enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity and pigment retention, indicate that Si-induced protection operates through integrated structural and metabolic reinforcement. Although Ca contributed to membrane stabilization, it did not confer the same degree of biomechanical strengthening or coordinated antioxidant activation. Thus, the hypothesis is supported, with the refinement that Si-mediated mechanical reinforcement, coupled with enhanced antioxidant capacity, represents the dominant mechanism underpinning improved postharvest resilience under vibration stress.

Practical implications and limitations

From a commercial perspective, foliar application of potassium silicate at 100–200 mg L⁻¹ or nano-silicon at 100 mg L⁻¹ represents a cost-effective and environmentally friendly strategy to improve the postharvest performance of cut carnations. These treatments enhanced stem mechanical strength (Fig. 3a–c), maintained tissue hydration and membrane integrity (Table 2), strengthened antioxidant defenses and pigment retention (Table 3), and ultimately extended vase life during transportation (Fig. 2).

Importantly, these benefits were achieved through preharvest management, requiring no modification of postharvest handling protocols or holding solutions, making the approach readily transferable to commercial production systems. Incorporation of Si-based foliar sprays into standard cultivation practices could therefore reduce economic losses associated with transport-related mechanical damage, improve product uniformity, and enhance consumer satisfaction.

Although a formal economic analysis was beyond the scope of this study, the proposed preharvest foliar treatments are based on low-cost commercial inputs and require no additional infrastructure or operational changes. Even modest improvements in postharvest performance may have substantial economic implications at the commercial scale. For example, an increase of only 10% in vase life following transportation could translate into significant savings for growers and retailers by reducing product rejection and enhancing overall product quality. Importantly, these benefits would apply across a wide range of commercial scenarios, including both short supply chains with limited transport exposure and long-distance supply chains characterized by extended vibration and handling. Overall, such improvements would directly enhance profit margins while simultaneously reducing the environmental footprint of floriculture supply chains.

Beyond its practical relevance, this study provides novel integrative insight by explicitly linking transportation-related vibration stress with quantitative stem biomechanical traits and preharvest mineral nutrition. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to combine mechanical loading, stem structural properties, water relations, oxidative metabolism, and nutrient status within a single experimental framework in cut flowers. This integrative perspective advances current understanding of postharvest stress physiology and establishes a mechanistic basis for developing preharvest strategies to improve transport resilience.

Although potassium silicate at 200 mg L⁻¹ was the most effective treatment in the present study, it remains uncertain whether further increases in application rate would provide additional benefits or induce negative effects. Higher concentrations (e.g., 250–300 mg L⁻¹) may potentially enhance structural reinforcement and antioxidant capacity. However, excessive Si or accompanying cations (particularly K⁺ or Na⁺) could also lead to nutrient imbalance, osmotic stress, or reduced foliar uptake efficiency. Previous studies indicate that supra-optimal Si doses do not necessarily result in proportional physiological gains and may even suppress growth or mineral homeostasis under certain conditions. Therefore, future studies should systematically evaluate higher Si concentrations to identify optimal thresholds between beneficial and potentially detrimental effects under both stress and non-stress conditions.

Despite the clear physiological and practical relevance of the present findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. The experiment was conducted under controlled greenhouse conditions and focused on a single cultivar, which may limit direct extrapolation to other genotypes or production systems. In addition, only one vibration frequency and duration were tested, whereas commercial transport conditions involve more complex and variable mechanical spectra. Higher Si concentrations and combined nutrient strategies were not evaluated, and a formal economic analysis was beyond the scope of this study. Future research should therefore examine a broader range of cultivars, greenhouse environments, vibration regimes, and nutrient doses, as well as include multi-season greenhouse trials and quantitative cost–benefit assessments to fully validate the applicability of these strategies at the commercial scale.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that preharvest foliar application of silicon, particularly potassium silicate and nano-silicon, significantly enhances the tolerance of cut carnation flowers to transportation-related vibration stress. In agreement with our initial hypothesis, Si-based treatments were more effective than Ca-based treatments in reinforcing stem mechanical strength, maintaining water relations and membrane stability, stimulating antioxidant and phenylpropanoid pathways, preserving pigment pools, and ultimately extending vase life. The strong correlations between stem biomechanical traits and postharvest longevity confirm that structural reinforcement represents a key mechanism underlying vibration tolerance. Together, these findings validate the proposed hypothesis and establish silicon nutrition as a robust and mechanistically grounded preharvest strategy to improve transport resilience and postharvest quality in cut flowers.
