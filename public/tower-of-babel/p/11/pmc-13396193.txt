Inference of spatial chromatin accessibility via integration of spatial transcriptomics and single-cell multi-omics data
Authors: Ishita Debnath, Zhana Duren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntegrating spatial transcriptomics, which maps gene expression location within tissues, with single-cell multi-omics data, profiling gene expression and chromatin accessibility (or other epigenomic data) for the same cell, offers powerful insights into gene regulation. However, commercially available kits for simultaneous spatial multi-omics profiling are currently unavailable, hindering widespread data generation. Here, we present ISON (Integrated Spatial Omics Network), a unified computational method for integrative spatial multi-omics analysis from single cell multiome data and spatial transcriptomics data. ISON accurately predicts chromatin accessibility profiles for spatial spots and reconstructs spatially resolved gene regulatory networks, demonstrating scalability in both time and memory. Importantly, ISON’s chromatin accessibility prediction captures patterns consistent with cis- and trans- regulatory information and enables estimation of transcription factor (TF) activity at the spot level, distinguishing between TFs even within the same family, which is unique and is not present in approaches relying solely on chromatin accessibility data. The application of ISON to Alzheimer’s disease data reveals disease- and age-specific spatially variable gene regulatory modules, highlighting its potential to uncover spatially organized mechanisms driving complex biological processes.Subject terms: Gene regulatory networks, Cellular signalling networks, Computational models, Epigenetics

Introduction

Cells interact to carry out essential biological functions and adapt to their environment. This process relies on a coordinated flow of information, both within and between cells, via two main signaling mechanisms: intercellular and intracellular. Intercellular signaling involves communication between cells, often through ligand-receptor interactions. For example, a ligand from one cell binds to a receptor on another, influencing its behavior. In contrast, intracellular signaling occurs within a single cell, with information flowing from DNA to RNA to proteins. These proteins then regulate a wide range of cellular processes by binding to DNA, RNA, or other proteins. These molecular interactions form complex regulatory networks that use elements like transcription factors (TFs) and cis-regulatory elements to precisely control cellular function and gene expression1,2.

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) techniques enable researchers to understand cell-cell interactions within their tissue environments by mapping gene expression directly onto intact tissue sections while preserving the spatial context of cells. This spatial resolution is crucial for maintaining information about cellular heterogeneity and tissue organization, which are key to understanding complex cell-cell communication (CCC)3,4. Many computational tools have been developed to infer CCC5–9, with the majority relying on predefined ligand-receptor pairs. CellAgentChat10 is an agent-based framework for inferring both long-range and short-range CCC. CellContrast9 is a notable method that infers CCC without depending on these predefined pairs. These methods allow researchers to identify true spatial neighbors and the signals they exchange, providing a deeper understanding of intercellular signaling.

Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) capture the flow of information in the central dogma by describing complex relationships among TFs, the accessible regions of the genome, and target genes11–13. GRNs also identify regulatory modules that organize the expression of functionally related genes in response to internal and external signals14. Many methods have been developed to infer GRNs based solely on gene expression data, but they often have low accuracy due to the lack of information on cis-regulatory elements where TFs bind15–17. Methods have been developed to infer cis-regulatory network from ATAC-seq, histone modification data, and/or 3D chromatin contact data18,19. Single-cell multiome (sc-multiome) technologies simultaneously profile scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq within the same cell. Methods have been developed for such GRN inference from sc-multiome data20–24. These tools, including LINGER from our group, estimate TF activity from single-cell multiomics data, infer regulatory relations among TFs, target genes (TGs), and cis-regulatory elements. Importantly, GRN inference from sc-multiome data can provide significantly higher accuracy than methods using only gene expression data20.

Although advances have been made in gaining insight into intracellular and intercellular regulatory networks separately, there is a lack of integrative frameworks that combine both intracellular and intercellular communication dynamics within the same spatial resolution. Current computational methods for studying spatial cellular organization and heterogeneity, such as DestVI25, iSORT26, and STEM27, primarily integrate scRNA-seq with ST, leaving broader multi-omics regulatory interactions underexplored. This highlights the need for analytical approaches capable of incorporating multi-omics data within spatial contexts.

Recent advances in spatial multiome technologies28,29 show great potential for capturing spatially resolved cis-regulation30. However, the lack of commercially available platforms that simultaneously capture spatial gene expression and epigenomic features poses practical challenges for most laboratories. A feasible solution is the computational integration of independently generated ST and single-cell multiome datasets, both of which can be readily produced using commercially available 10x Genomics kits.

Here, we introduce ISON (Integrated Spatial Omics Network) for integrative spatial multi-omics analysis and spatially resolved gene regulatory network inference. ISON uses representation learning to perform this integration by jointly decomposing sc-multiome and ST data into lower-dimensional embeddings which captures shared and modality-specific patterns. These latent embeddings are then used to predict chromatin accessibility information onto spatial locations, effectively inferring spatial chromatin organization from transcriptomic data. Thus, by using sc-multiome and ST data, the inferred spatial ATAC signals are more robust and interpretable compared to direct measurements, which often suffer from sparse coverage and technical artifacts. We show that the spatial chromatin accessibility predicted by ISON is enriched for experimentally detected cis- and trans- regulatory signals. Furthermore, we demonstrate how spatial chromatin accessibility profiles inferred by ISON can be used to reconstruct GRNs that reveal disease-associated processes. Importantly, ISON provides a practical and scalable alternative in settings where generating spatial multiome data remains technically demanding, costly, and inaccessible to many laboratories, thereby broadening access to spatial regulatory analysis.

Results

ISON: integrated spatial omics network from spatial transcriptomics and single cell multiomics data

We introduce ISON, a unified and robust framework for integrative spatial multi-omic analysis and spatially resolved gene regulatory network inference from ST and sc-multiome data. ISON operates in two primary steps: first, it predicts the spatial epigenome for each spatial spot, and subsequently, it performs gene regulatory analysis using both spatial gene expression and the predicted spatial epigenome profiles.

To achieve the prediction of spatial epigenome, ISON learns a low-dimensional cell/spot embedding that effectively corrects for technical variations between ST and single-cell gene expression data and achieves cross-modality alignment between the RNA and ATAC profiles within single-cell multiome data. This integration enables the reconstruction of the spatial arrangement of epigenome within tissues where only transcriptomic data is spatially resolved. Utilizing the predicted epigenomic and ST data, we reconstruct spatially resolved gene regulatory networks using LINGER20, encompassing both cis- regulation and trans- regulation. The proposed model can be effectively applied to case-control study designs, contrasting diseased and healthy subjects (Fig. 1). This analytical approach enables the comprehensive identification of gene regulatory modules associated with disease and the elucidation of their distinct spatial distributions within specific tissue regions.

Integration of single-cell multiome with spatial transcriptome using ISON.

a ISON takes spatial transcriptome and paired single-cell multiome data as input. It captures a shared low-dimensional representation of the spatial gene expression and scRNA-seq and captures chromatin accessibility pattern of scATAC-seq into a lower dimensional embedding. The patterns of the two modalities are then mapped and aligned at a single-cell level. Then, spatial chromatin accessibility data is obtained by transferring from single-cell data to spatial transcriptome using shared representations. b Spatial chromatin accessibility obtained from ISON preserves cis- and trans- regulatory signal. Spatial chromatin accessibility can be used to obtain spatially resolved gene-regulatory networks, which can help in identifying disease-associated gene regulatory modules.

To learn the cell representation and spatial epigenome prediction, we implement this approach in two distinct models: one based on non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) with Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence (joint KL-NMF), and another using a joint variational autoencoders (VAE)31 framework with contrastive loss for self-supervised alignment (contrastive joint VAE). For KL-NMF model, we model both gene expression and chromatin accessibility for all data modalities, namely, ST, scRNA-seq, and scATAC-seq, following a Poisson distribution. The expected value for each gene or chromatin accessibility peak is defined as a weighted sum of K distinct latent regulatory programs. These programs act as a shared low-dimensional representation of cellular states. Importantly, the regulatory effects of these programs on genes are assumed to be consistent across single-cell and spatial gene expression datasets. Furthermore, for sc-multiome data, the cellular contributions of these programs are shared between the gene expression and chromatin accessibility measurements from the same cell. Thus, by sharing these matrices, it can align the gene expression patterns between different techniques and learns a consistent mapping between regulatory programs and multi-modal profiles at the single-cell level. We also incorporated a Laplacian regularization to penalize abrupt changes in program activity between neighboring spots, encouraging smooth transitions and maintaining spatial continuity in the inferred regulatory landscape. To account for technical variation between ST and sc-multiome data, ISON incorporates modality-specific correction vectors into its matrix factorization framework. These vectors enable the model to selectively apply modality-specific corrections during reconstruction, effectively mitigating batch effects while preserving biological signals (See “Methods” for details).

To incorporate non-linearity, we also developed a contrastive joint VAE model, which jointly models gene expression and chromatin accessibility, moving beyond the linear assumptions of NMF. The first VAE encodes gene expression data from both ST and sc-multiome assays, performing dimensionality reduction. The second VAE encodes chromatin accessibility data from the multiome in a similar manner. To align these modalities, we apply self-supervised contrastive learning, mapping the RNA and ATAC embeddings from the multiome into a shared latent space. Each RNA-ATAC pair from the same single-cell serves as a positive pair, while RNA-ATAC pairs from different cells are treated as negative pairs. For prediction, the encoder from the first VAE generates latent embeddings from the spatial transcriptomic input. These embeddings are then passed through the trained decoder of the second VAE to reconstruct the corresponding chromatin accessibility profile (See Methods for details).

Both models can predict spatial chromatin accessibility: the RNA data from both modalities are first processed by an encoder, using either NMF or VAE, to yield spot representations, which are then fed into the ATAC’s decoder, and then alignment is performed for the final prediction.

ISON accurately predicts spatial chromatin accessibility

To benchmark the accuracy of spatial chromatin accessibility prediction, we evaluated the performance of ISON and other methods using 10X Genomics sc-multiome data from Alzheimer’s and Wild type mouse brains and spatial RNA+ATAC data from the mouse brain section at postnatal day 21/22 (P21/22)28, which was obtained using spatial-ATAC-RNA-seq assays. Although these competing methods used are not originally designed for the integration of multi-modal spatial omics technologies, they were used for benchmarking due to the current lack of such computational tools. Considering the lack of spatial multiome and sc-multiome data from the same tissue context, we first evaluate it only using sc-multiome data. We performed this analysis to assess the contribution of spatial information and to evaluate model performance when spatial information is excluded. We split the 10X Genomics Wild Type mouse brain sc-multiome data32 into two groups: one subset was treated as spatial RNA-ATAC data but without spatial location information, and the second subset was retained as sc-multiome data. We used this setup to evaluate whether KL-NMF method can predict ATAC profiles for the spatial data using the other three modalities. For our baseline methods, spatial chromatin accessibility was predicted by averaging the accessibility profiles of the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) single cells, where neighbors were selected based on gene expression canonical correlation analysis (CCA); we refer to this approach as CCA+KNN33,34. The additional baseline method is Multi-Omics Factor Analysis (MOFA)35, in which gene expression and chromatin accessibility are jointly embedded using sc-multiome data to learn shared cell-level weights matrix and modality specific loading factors. These learned gene-expression loadings are then applied to spatial transcriptomics data to estimate spot-level weight matrix, which is subsequently used with ATAC loadings to infer spatial chromatin accessibility. To assess the spatial ATAC prediction, we calculated Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between predicted and observed values across spots for each peak and then used the mean PCC and mean RMSE across peaks to compare our method with others (referred as peak-wise PCC and peak-wise RMSE). KL-NMF achieved lower peak-wise RMSE score and higher peak-wise PCC than CCA+KNN and MOFA, suggesting improved agreement with observed accessibility patterns in this non-spatial controlled setting (Fig. S1). Moreover, the experiment required no spatial information and thus supports the reliability of the new method’s accessibility prediction module and confirm that its performance is not only dependent on spatial context but also on effective multi-modal integration.

We then compared the performance of ISON on the P21 and P22 spatial multiome mouse brain datasets. These datasets simultaneously profile gene expression and chromatin accessibility at the same spatial spots, capturing both modalities at matched spatial resolution. Since we did not have the sc-multiome data, we split the spots in the spatial RNA+ATAC data into two groups and assumed that one group of the split is treated as sc-multiome and the other as spatial RNA-ATAC multiome to validate our method. Subsequently, the sc-multiome data from the first group and the spatial-RNA data from the second are used to predict the spatial ATAC data from second group. We compared ISON with Tangram36, RCTD37, SPAGE38, GIMVI39 and CCA+KNN. For gene imputation techniques SPAGE and GIMVI, we treated individual chromatin accessibility peaks as ‘missing genes’ to predict spatial chromatin accessibility. For spot deconvolution technique RCTD, we first estimated cell type proportions at each ST data spot using scRNA-seq as a reference. These proportions were then multiplied by the average chromatin accessibility peak values for each cell type to predict spatial chromatin accessibility.

We used peak-wise PCC and RMSE to assess spatial ATAC prediction to compare our model’s performance with others. On the P21 dataset, KL-NMF achieved a mean PCC of 0.23 and RMSE of 1.19, compared to 0.09 and 1.22 for contrastive joint VAE (Fig. 2a, b). On the P22 dataset, KL-NMF reached a mean PCC of 0.15 and RMSE of 0.83, versus 0.07 and 0.84 for contrastive joint VAE (Fig. S2). Because the KL-NMF-based model consistently outperforms the contrastive joint VAE, we adopt it as the default representation in ISON (hereafter “ISON”) and refer to the contrastive joint VAE simply as “VAE”. When compared with other methods, ISON also shows higher PCC and lower RMSE (Fig. 2a–c). We also evaluated the performance at the spot level by computing the Pearson correlation coefficient for each spot between predicted and observed accessibility values across peaks. Under this spot-wise metric, ISON outperformed all other methods, achieving average PCC of 0.56 and 0.64 for the P21 and P22 data, respectively (Fig. S3). Although ISON outperforms other methods, the peak-wise PCCs on raw data remain modest (0.23 for P21 and 0.15 for P22) due to severe dropout and sparsity in spatial multiome measurements; to mitigate this, we imputed the observed ATAC and evaluated predictions against the imputed profiles, finding that ISON achieved mean peak-wise PCCs of 0.69 and 0.63 for P21 and P22 with MAGIC-based imputation40 and consistently outperformed all other methods across different imputation strategies (Figs. S4, S5, S6, S7)41–43. ISON achieved the lowest average peak-wise RMSE among all methods. Compared with other approaches, these differences were statistically significant, except for RCTD, where the RMSE was slightly higher but not statistically significant (Fig. S2b). Notably, ISON showed consistently higher and statistically significant PCC values compared to all other methods, indicating superior preservation of cross-spot accessibility structure. RMSE measures absolute deviations and is more influenced by peaks with very large counts, whereas PCC evaluates agreement in relative variation across spots and effectively weights peaks more equally. Additionally, we computed residuals for the P21 and P22 datasets to assess potential bias and found that they were predominantly centered around zero in both datasets, indicating overall accurate predictions with only a small number of outliers (Fig. S8a, b). This suggests that ISON was able to capture the actual spatial-ATAC seq distribution as well as performed well in terms of prediction.

Since the available spatial multiome data is limited and highly sparse, we constructed a simulation study following the scDesign344 tutorial to benchmark performance. We compared ISON with SPAGE, which has the closest prediction performance among existing methods. Multiple spot resolutions were tested by varying the number of cells per spot, capturing realistic differences in spot composition and resolution. Across all simulations, ISON consistently achieves higher overall PCC values computed on vectorized (flattened) predicted and observed spatial ATAC matrices (Fig. S9a), as well as lower RMSE compared to SPAGE (Fig. S9b), indicating improved agreement between predicted and true spatial ATAC profiles. Across different numbers of cells per spot, the improvement in overall PCC achieved by ISON (0.55–0.70) relative to SPAGE (0.44–0.54) ranges from approximately 20 to 30%. ISON also achieved higher peak-wise PCC values across all simulation conditions. Although the gain in peak-level PCC was modest when the number of cells per spot was 4–5, these improvements were statistically significant for all tested spot sizes (Fig. S9c). We further examined the distribution of observed and predicted accessibility values conditional on observed ATAC counts (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, or ≥5) in the simulated data (Fig. S10). Predicted accessibility values increase as observed counts increase, indicating that ISON captures the overall trend of the empirical signal across varying simulation settings. Although predicted values tend to underestimate the magnitude of observed accessibility, the consistent monotonic relationship demonstrates meaningful predictive performance. These results demonstrate the robustness of ISON in reconstructing spatial epigenomic landscapes under realistic multi-cell-type, variable-resolution spot mixtures.

Imaging-based spatial transcriptomics technologies typically capture a relatively small set of genes per spot, raising the question of whether models like ISON remain effective under such low-gene-coverage conditions. We therefore assessed ISON’s applicability to imaging-based ST settings by randomly selecting subsets of 100–1000 genes from the P22 spatial RNA data, resulting in an average of 18–180 expressed genes per spot. Even with only 100 selected genes with 18 genes expressed per spot, ISON maintained ATAC prediction average peak-wise PCC values above 0.4 (using MAGIC-imputed spatial ATAC as ground truth), indicating reliable prediction accuracy under low-gene-coverage settings (Fig. S11). Together, these simulations demonstrate ISON’s robustness and generalizability across variable spot resolutions and reduced gene coverage.

To assess the robustness of ISON and test its generalizability across datasets, we treated the P21 spatial-multiome data as sc-multiome data by disregarding it’s spatial coordinates and used it, along with P22 spatial RNA-seq data, to predict spatial ATAC-seq data for P22. Residual analysis comparing cross-dataset predictions with observed spatial ATAC data showed that most residuals were concentrated near zero, indicating minimal systematic bias and supporting the robustness of ISON across datasets (Fig. S8c). We then imputed the observed P22 spatial ATAC data using MAGIC for downstream evaluation. Peak-wise performance metrics yielded a mean PCC of 0.65 and mean RMSE of 0.18, while spot-wise evaluation resulted in a mean PCC of 0.97 and mean RMSE of 0.49. We observed that total counts per spot (Fig. S12a) or per peak (Fig. S12b) influenced both RMSE and PCC: features with higher total counts tended to exhibit larger RMSE but also higher PCC, whereas low-count features showed lower RMSE and lower PCC. Peaks with extreme total counts still demonstrate a consistent positive relationship between predicted and observed values (Fig. S12c), indicating that ISON captures overall accessibility trends despite increased absolute error at higher count magnitudes. In selecting representative best- and worst-performing examples, we excluded about 6% of peaks with extreme total counts (defined as <1000 or >5000) to focus on additional patterns beyond those illustrated above. Among the remaining peaks, most exhibited PCC values between 0.35 and 0.75 and RMSE values between 0 and 0.6 (Fig. S13a). Best- and worst-performing representative peaks (see Peaks and Genes Assignment to Modules for more details) were selected from different programs across performance categories (Figs. 2d and S14, S15). High-performing peaks showed clear positive agreement between predicted and observed values, whereas low-PCC peaks (approximately 0.4), often characterized by very small total counts, exhibited weaker and more diffuse patterns. Similarly, best- and worst-performing representative spots were selected for evaluation from the full set of spots (Fig. S16a, b). Consistent with this, exclusion of outlier peaks resulted in a modest improvement in RMSE and a slight decrease in PCC, while preserving the overall trends (Fig. S17). Overall, more than 93% of spots exhibited PCC >0.9 (Fig. S13b), and even high-RMSE examples, typically associated with larger total counts, retained high PCC, indicating strong overall agreement with only localized deviations (Fig. S16c). Thus, these visualizations provide a detailed characterization of ISON’s behavior and demonstrate consistent alignment between predicted and observed accessibility patterns across varying signal patterns. To further assess whether ISON captures biologically relevant spatial patterns, we analyzed peak-wise PCC in relation to spatial variability and observed higher predictive accuracy for peaks with greater spatial variability (Fig. 2e). Predicted gene expression patterns were also denoised and exhibited spatial structures closely resembling the observed data, with enhanced spatial clarity (Fig. S18). RMSE, PCC, total counts and Moran’s I values for predicted and observed peaks from the P22 dataset are provided in Supplementary Data 1, and RMSE, PCC and total counts for the spots are provided in Supplementary Data 2. Overall, these analyses demonstrate that ISON achieves accurate and robust predictions with minimal systematic bias, strong agreement between predicted and observed values, and reliable performance across datasets and signal intensities, supporting its utility for downstream spatial regulatory analyses.

ISON effectively captures the gene regulatory landscape

To assess whether ISON can infer signals consistent with biologically meaningful cis-regulatory relations, we computed correlation of gene expression and peak accessibility across spatial spots. A high correlation between accessibility of peaks and expression of nearby genes suggests that these peaks may function as cis-regulatory elements. We calculated correlations between gene expression and predicted peak accessibility after stratifying peak-gene pairs into groups by genomic distance in the P21 (Fig. 3a) and P22 (Fig. S19) spatial multiome datasets. Peak-gene pairs that were closer in the genome showed stronger correlations, with correlation strength decreasing as distance increased, consistent with a distance-dependent regulatory relationship (Fig. 3a, right). The chromatin accessibility predicted by our model closely follows this expected biological gradient, whereas the observed accessibility is less consistent with this pattern, indicating higher noise levels in the experimental data (Fig. 3a, left). We further evaluated whether these peak-gene pairs correspond to annotated biological links using cis-eQTL links45 and Hi-C contacts46. For eQTL-based validation, ISON-predicted ATAC achieves an AUPR that is 6% higher than random, whereas experimental ATAC performs similarly to random. For Hi-C-based validation, ISON-predicted ATAC achieves an AUPR 15% above random, while experimental ATAC achieves only 10% above random (Fig. 3b). Absolute AUPR values were modest overall, consistent with the fact that reference datasets such as Hi-C contacts and cis-eQTL links provide incomplete and indirect proxies of regulatory interactions and that correlation-based inference is inherently constrained by indirect coupling and measurement noise. Therefore, comparative performance provides a more informative assessment than absolute scores alone, and under this framework, correlations between observed gene expression and ISON-predicted chromatin accessibility show stronger enrichment for independently supported peak-gene interactions than those derived from observed spatial multiome data. Together, these results indicate that ISON provides a denoised and more interpretable representation of regulatory patterns, effectively capturing biologically supported regulatory links that may be partially obscured by noise and sparsity in experimentally measured spatial multiome data.

To evaluate whether trans-regulatory patterns are captured in ISON’s chromatin accessibility predictions, we analyzed 884 motifs from the cisBP mouse motif database47 and computed motif deviation scores using ChromVAR48 for both real and predicted P22 spatial ATAC datasets. These deviation scores provide a bias-corrected measure of how far the per-cell accessibility of a motif deviates from its expected accessibility based on the average across all cells49. To focus on the most informative regulators, we first filtered for high-variability motifs with standard deviation SD > 2.5 for observed ATAC data corresponding approximately to the top 10% highly variable motifs, yielding a subset of 93 motifs for downstream evaluation, reasoning that only a fraction of TFs are active in a given cellular context and many motifs represent multiple TFs, making this subset most likely to capture biologically relevant regulators. We calculated the PCC between motif activities derived from observed and predicted ATAC (Fig. S20). For all 93 motifs, predicted and observed activities were significantly correlated after multiple-testing correction (Fig. 3c). The average PCC was 0.47 (Q1 = 0.43, Q3 = 0.52), with a minimum of 0.16 and no negative correlations. Figure 3d illustrates the spatial pattern of the Mesp2 motif from actual and predicted ATAC signals, which has a PCC of 0.64 and a standard deviation of 3.34. As the threshold increases, such as top 25% variable motifs, around 90% (198 out of 221 motifs) were statistically significantly positively correlated after Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate correction, but around 10% are non-significant and have near-zero or negative correlations. This reduces the overall strength and consistency of the predictive performance (Fig. S21). Supplementary Data 3 includes detailed information for all 884 motifs. These results demonstrate that ISON faithfully captures patterns with biologically meaningful trans-regulatory patterns across spatial regions, supporting its robustness in reconstructing chromatin accessibility landscapes.

ISON also demonstrates a strong ability to retain spatial variability in chromatin accessibility. To evaluate this, we compared the spatial autocorrelation of predicted and observed chromatin accessibility using Moran’s I statistic, as shown in Fig. 3e. The analysis revealed a positive correlation of 0.44 between Moran’s I values of ISON’s predictions and the observed data. This indicates that peaks with high spatial variability in the observed data are accurately predicted to be spatially variable by the model. Overall, ISON produces chromatin accessibility predictions that are enriched for cis- and trans-regulatory patterns and exhibit biologically meaningful spatial variability in regulatory signals.

To biologically interpret the latent programs learned by ISON, we examined how each factor corresponds to spatially organized cell populations and their associated genes and regulatory elements using spot-level chromatin accessibility inferred from ISON trained on the P21 spatial multiome dataset (treated as sc-multiome data without spatial coordinates) together with P22 spatial RNA-seq data. These programs represent genes and peaks enriched in specific spatial regions (see Peaks and Genes Assignment to Modules for more details). Module-level activity at each spot was converted to spot-wise proportions for both the gene expression-based and chromatin accessibility-based programs. Structure plots (Fig. S22a) show that most spatial spots represent mixtures of multiple regulatory programs. To quantify agreement between gene expression-based and chromatin accessibility-based programs, we computed the correlation between their corresponding module proportion profiles for each spot. The distribution of these spot-wise correlations (Fig. S22b) shows strong cross-modality agreement (mean ≈ 0.87, median ≈ 0.91), indicating that the inferred regulatory programs are consistently represented across modalities. We further performed downstream analyses to assess whether peaks and genes assigned to the same program exhibit cis-regulatory relationships within a 20 kb genomic window. Fisher’s exact tests revealed statistically significant peak-gene associations for six programs (Fig. S23a). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant Gene Ontology (GO) terms for most programs with enriched peak-gene associations (Fig. S23b). Overall, these results support the biological relevance of modules exhibiting significant cis-regulatory coupling.

ISON is a scalable, multi-sample model that effectively handles batch effects

To assess ISON’s scalability, we evaluated the memory consumption and runtime performance of ISON in comparison with other methods. To test it, we used 12 samples from 10X Visium ST and sc-multiome data and generated synthetic datasets with increasing sample sizes by duplicating samples (see “Methods” for more details). ISON outperformed other methods in terms of computational speed, as can be seen in Fig. 4b. We benchmarked ISON on both GPU and CPU environments to evaluate the impact of hardware acceleration. For datasets with 900 thousand cells/spots (300,000 per modality), processing takes 55 min on GPU, which is only about 11% of SPAGE’s, 4% of GIMVI’s, and 17% of RCTD. We were unable to benchmark Tangram on the larger simulated datasets than 100 thousand cells (33.3 thousand for each modality), as it exceeded our allocated computational time. The CPU implementation takes 85 minutes for 900,000 cells, which is slower than the GPU implementation but still significantly faster than other methods, highlighting the advantage of using parallel computation for large-scale datasets. As shown in Fig. 4a, ISON consistently used less memory than most other methods, particularly as the number of spots increased. Its memory usage was only slightly higher than RCTD. For a dataset of 900,000 cells, ISON’s memory usage was 278 GB, which is only about 17% of SPAGE’s and 39% of GIMVI’s. With its efficient computational time and low memory usage, ISON demonstrates excellent scalability, allowing it to be used effectively on large sample datasets.

Building on the analysis of its scalability, we now assess whether ISON’s predictive accuracy can be improved by integrating data from multiple samples. To examine the effects of biological variation and sample size on performance, we split genes into training and testing sets, with the latter simulating open chromatin peaks. These splits were done using these three training frameworks for matched evaluation: (i) Single-sample training using one sample (N spots, referred as Single), (ii) a Multi-sample, size-matched regimen built by randomly drawing N spots across all 12 samples (thus increasing biological diversity while holding size constant, referred as Multi_matched), and (iii) a Multi-sample, full regimen using all available spots from all samples (referred as Multi_full). ISON was run under each framework, and performance was assessed based on the hold-out gene expression prediction (Fig. 4c). Multi_full outperformed Single, which in turn outperformed Multi_matched, indicating that larger training sets improves ISON’s predictive accuracy. This finding highlights the importance of larger, more comprehensive training sets for improving model generalization and robustness.

In summary, we demonstrate that ISON is both computationally efficient and scalable for large spatial-ATAC prediction tasks, consistently outperforming existing methods in memory usage and runtime. Moreover, ISON’s performance improves with increasing dataset size, with predictive accuracy rising substantially when trained on multiple samples, demonstrating that it scales effectively to larger datasets. These findings highlight ISON’s ability to generalize across varied biological conditions while maintaining computational efficiency, making it a robust tool for large-scale, multimodal spatial epigenomics prediction.

Spatial gene regulatory network analysis sheds light on Alzheimer’s disease

We next applied ISON to 10X Genomics Visium and sc-multiome datasets from a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease to evaluate its utility in case-control analyses and in resolving spatially organized regulatory programs. The dataset, consisting of 12 samples, includes 10X sc-multiome and Visium spatial transcriptomic profiles from the brain tissue of both wild-type and transgenic TgCRND8 Alzemer’s disease mice. The samples were collected across three time points, with two replicates for each condition. To explore spatial regulatory patterns, we compared predicted spatial ATAC-seq with raw and denoised ST data. Spatially variable peaks identified from the predicted ATAC are closely matched with denoised gene expression (Fig. S24b, c). Moreover, both the denoised expression and predicted accessibility displayed much cleaner and more coherent spatial organization than the raw transcriptomic data (Fig. S24a).

We inferred GRN using LINGER by taking the ST and predicted spatial ATAC as inputs. In addition to the GRNs, LINGER also outputs the TF activity as a weighted sum of expression of its target genes. We observed that the spatial patterns of TFs identified Klf2, Klf4, Klf7, Klf11 (Fig. 5a) differed from the chromVAR motif activity, which aggregates the entire Klf family and cannot distinguish individual members (Fig. 5b). The spots that are active in chromVAR motif activity (Fig. 5b) are also active in at least one of the four corresponding individual TF spatial map in (Fig. 5a), demonstrating that the individual TF activities represent a higher-resolution characterization that remains fully aligned with the broader motif accessibility patterns. ChromVAR identifies motif accessibility patterns aggregated at the level of TF families, which is useful for capturing shared regulatory activity. However, by resolving spatial patterns for individual TFs, our approach provides finer granularity and allows us to distinguish whether all members of a TF family are active in the same regions or whether certain members show region-specific or cell-type-specific activity. In Fig. 5a, the spatial maps illustrate these distinct patterns, which cannot be directly assessed from chromVAR’s aggregated family-level scores. This detail enables us to classify spatially distinct regulatory programs and to explore potential interactions among TFs that would otherwise be masked when analyzing the entire family. Individual TF activity estimation provides higher-resolution regulatory insights by distinguishing the spatial specific activity of specific TFs that would not have been clear in motif analyses.

We aim to demonstrate how ISON can support case-control analyses by spatially resolved gene regulatory networks. To achieve this, we applied K-means clustering to the TF-target gene network from LINGER, identifying 12 distinct modules of co-regulated genes. To assess whether these modules were enriched in differentially expressed genes, we performed GSEA. The analysis utilized the log fold change between transgenic and wild-type conditions as the scoring metric to evaluate if genes within a module were significantly enriched at either end of the ranked gene list. This yielded normalized enrichment scores (NES) and associated p-values (Condition effect in Fig. 5d). Beyond this primary comparison, we also conducted GSEA to analyze differential expression between two age groups (young vs. old) within the transgenic and wild-type cohorts separately (Time-Wild and Time-Transgenic effect in Fig. 5d). Finally, we calculated a difference score to evaluate the interaction effect between condition and age (Interaction effect in Fig. 5d). Five modules (0, 2, 5, 6, and 8) were found to be consistently enriched across multiple cell types in all types of comparisons between conditions, between ages, and for the condition-age interaction. Among these, module 2 stood out as being enriched in every cell type examined across all comparisons. Similarly, module 0 showed selective enrichment in L5/6 NP and L6 IT Car3 neurons, module 5 was enriched in both L6b and L5/6 neurons, and module 6 displayed enrichment in L6b neurons as well as Immune/Vascular cells, all consistently across the same set of comparisons. However, module 8 exhibited broad enrichment across nearly all cell types in all comparisons except for L2/3 IT, L5/6 NP, and L6 IT Car3 neurons.

Among these, the module 5-L5/6 NP cell-type pair was particularly notable for its strong statistical significance and high NES score of 1.67 for interaction effect (Fig. S25), which means these group of genes are highly expressed in transgenic mice and their expression is expected to increase with aging in these mice. Figure 5e highlights an interaction effect for module 5 within the L5/6 NP cell type, where expression levels in transgenic old samples are distinctly higher compared to those in wild-type old, transgenic early, and wild-type early samples. The average expression values of the genes in module 5 - L5/6 NP pair for transgenic-type old, wild-type old, transgenic-type early, and wild-type early samples were 0.23, 0.17, 0.13, and 0.21, respectively. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the genes within significant modules are enriched in neuronal function relevant terms (Figs. 5c and S26). The module 5 genes are enriched in Amyloid Precursor Protein Metabolic Process, which is particularly relevant given that the TgCRND8 mouse model expresses a double mutant form of the human amyloid precursor protein (APP) gene50. This mutation leads to increased production of amyloid-β peptides, a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease pathology, and supports the biological relevance of our findings by linking gene expression changes to known disease mechanisms in this model51–53. Reproducibility was assessed by selecting one replicate from each age-type group and analyzing these samples independently. Module 5 exhibited a consistent interaction effect within L5/6 NP cells across both groups, matching the results from the full dataset (Fig. S27), demonstrating the robustness of the biological signal.

In summary, ISON enables high-resolution mapping of spatial regulatory activity, revealing region-specific transcription factor dynamics and gene modules associated with Alzheimer’s pathology. These results highlight its utility in uncovering biologically meaningful patterns in complex case-control datasets.

Discussion

In this paper, we have introduced a computational framework, ISON, for spatially resolved gene regulatory network analysis using sc-multiome and ST data as inputs. ISON learns a cell/spot representation and uses these shared embeddings to infer spatial chromatin accessibility patterns. Through benchmarking on real-world datasets, ISON consistently outperformed existing approaches. It can also enhance spatial gene expression data by denoising and improving signal resolution using the integrated multi-omic structure. We demonstrated that ISON is computationally efficient and performs well across varying sample sizes, maintaining robust performance without the burden of increased runtime or memory usage. ISON is a powerful tool for uncovering the spatial organization of regulatory programs, as evidenced by its ability to capture patterns consistent with cis- and trans-regulatory programs and to enable spatially resolved inference of gene regulatory relationships among transcription factors, cis-regulatory elements, and target genes54,55. Furthermore, through our application of ISON, we demonstrated that the derived gene regulatory modules effectively capture disease-associated patterns and reveal spatial dynamics of Alzheimer’s disease while also providing a framework that can be extended to other case-control analyses.

More broadly, these considerations highlight a practical limitation in the field. The lack of widely accessible commercial kits for paired spatial multiome profiling remains a challenge, and ISON addresses this gap by integrating high-dimensional single-cell data with lower-resolution spatial data. To our knowledge, ISON is the first method to infer spatially resolved, RNA-informed chromatin accessibility proxies from sc-multiome and spatial transcriptomics data. By revealing the spatial organization of gene regulatory programs, ISON provides insights into intercellular communication and coordinated gene regulation within the tissue microenvironment. Looking forward, investigating how spatially resolved gene regulatory networks and cell-cell communication mutually influence one another to drive adaptation to environmental changes represents a promising direction for future research.

A limitation of this study is that the model has been trained on low resolution spatial transcriptomics technologies (e.g., 10x Visium). The model’s performance has not been evaluated on higher-resolution or single-cell spatial methods (e.g., MERFISH, Slide-seqV2) due to the lack of paired single-cell multi-omics and high-resolution spatial transcriptomics data for the same biological context. Evaluating the model with these higher-resolution datasets is a promising direction for future research.

Methods

In this section, we discuss two distinct approaches for predicting spatial chromatin accessibility by integrating single-cell multiome data with spatial transcriptomics: (1) a joint non-negative matrix factorization framework, and (2) a variational autoencoder-based model.

Non-negative matrix factorization with Kullback-Leibler divergence

Our joint KL-NMF algorithm processes single-cell multiome data, including scRNA-seq (E) and scATAC-seq (O), alongside spatial transcriptomic data (Es). The primary goal is to decompose these datasets into low-dimensional representations, represented as W and H, to extract meaningful features while preserving spatial and biological context.

Factor effects

We implemented a joint Kullback-Leibler (KL) non-negative matrix factorization to perform dimension reduction of our spatial transcriptomic and single-cell multiome data. Single-cell multiome data consists of scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data, which are denoted by EG×N1 and OP×N1 respectively, where G is denoted for total number of genes, P for total number of peaks and N1 is total number of cells. The spatial transcriptomic data is denoted as EsG×N2, where G is denoted for total number of genes and N2 is total number of spots. The inputs for ISON are scATAC-seq, scRNA-seq, and spatial transcriptomic data. The input matrices are decomposed into W and H matrices. Thus,

The matrices O, E, and Es are each factorized into two non-negative matrices, W and H, with the decompositions constrained to K components. Specifically, the matrix O is factorized as O ≈ W1H1, where W1∈RP×K and H1∈RK×N1. The matrix E is factorized as E ≈ W2H1, where W2∈RG×K and shares the same H1 matrix as O. Finally, the matrix Es is decomposed as Es ≈ W2H2, where H2∈RK×N2 and W2 is shared with E. Thus, W2 is common to both E and Es, while H1 is shared between O and E.

We use KL divergence as we assume that the gene expression and chromatin accessibility follows the Poisson distribution. The Poisson distribution provides a widely used likelihood model for count-based data in this context. KL divergence compares the probability distributions and ensures that the probability distribution of the original matrices and the multiplication of low-rank factorizations are similar by minimizing the loss functions between them. This helps in capturing the structural features of the observed matrices while also maintaining the interpretability.

The key motivation is that the three input data matrices can have very different scales (e.g., different matrix sizes, sparsity levels, and entry magnitudes), and since KL divergence is summed over all entries, the raw KL terms may differ by orders of magnitude. If fixed μ1 and μ2 were used, their appropriate magnitudes would become highly dataset-specific: depending on the scale and sparsity of the input matrices, well-balanced values could vary from extremely small (e.g., 10e-08) to extremely large (e.g., 10e+08). This makes parameter tuning difficult and unstable across datasets, whereas our normalized formulation keeps λ1 in a more interpretable range (typically around 1) while μ1 and μ2 are automatically adjusted to achieve balanced contributions among loss terms.

Specifically, the naive loss terms L1, L2, and L3 are computed from the input matrices E, Es and O, respectively, under the assumption that the corresponding factorization outputs are very small and close to zero. This provides a common reference point that estimates how large each KL term would be before learning meaningful factors. We then use these baseline values to form ratios (e.g., L1/L2 and L1/L3), which act as scale-correction factors and stabilize the overall objective.

Platform effects

To eliminate batch effects in scRNA-seq E, scATAC-seq O, and ST Es matrices, we incorporate a one-hot encoded feature vector (F) and a vector containing the averages of the features (V), with each column representing the corresponding dataset. For E and Es matrices, we merge Vgenes∈RG×2 vector, which consists of the averages of the genes. For O matrix, we add Vpeaks∈RP×2 vector, which consists of the averages of the peaks. We incorporate a one-hot encoded vector F, each element is set to 1 if it corresponds to the respective category. For Es matrix, we use Fspots∈R2×N2 and for E and O matrices, we used Fcells∈R2×N1. We then merge these F vectors with the H matrices and combine the V vectors with the W matrices. While computing the Os matrix we remove the platform effects from the matrix computation.

Spatial information

To integrate the spatial information in our algorithm, we generate a Laplacian matrix L from a graph matrix we compute using the Es matrix, which we add for the computation of H2. We utilize the x and y coordinates extracted from spatial transcriptomic data to create a graph matrix. Euclidean distance is computed between each point, and depending on the spatial proximity of each point, a point is classified as a corner (3 neighbors), edge (5 neighbors), or interior point (8 neighbors), and is allowed to connect to a maximum of 3, 5, or 8 closest neighbors, respectively. Each point is linked to its nearest neighbors, with the Euclidean distance between them calculated. This matrix is subsequently transformed into a binary adjacency matrix. From this adjacency matrix, we derive the Laplacian matrix L, which in turn is used for computing H2.

Joint variational autoencoders with contrastive loss

We also designed a VAE31 framework that incorporates two VAEs: one to model batch effects and the other to capture chromatin accessibility patterns. The VAE responsible for controlling batch effects effectively accounts for platform-specific differences between the two techniques. To align the latent embeddings across modalities, we use the Information Noise-Contrastive Estimation (InfoNCE) loss56, which is a self-supervised contrastive loss function. In this section, we describe the architecture of the model and the loss functions used.

Architecture

We used joint variational autoencoders to perform dimension reduction and learn the distributions of the latent representations from the input single-cell multiome data and spatial gene expression profiles.

In our first step, we aim to remove the batch effect from both the spatial and scRNA-seq data. The E and Es matrices are used as input for the first variational autoencoder. This variational autoencoder perform dimension reduction and optimizes the distribution of both the inputs to resemble a standard Gaussian distribution. The single-cell chromatin accessibility matrix O acts as the input for the second variational autoencoder, where the latent space is obtained and is mapped using semi-supervised contrastive learning. Then, in the next step, we map the latent representations closer to each other in the shared space of both O and E matrices, we form RNA-ATAC pairs between the two matrices to compute contrastive loss.

Data

Advanced technologies have been developed to capture spatially resolved genome-wide epigenome and transcriptome, and we used this dataset for validating our method28. Spatial ATAC+RNA data was obtained from the brains of mice at postnatal day 21/22 (P21/22) with a spatial resolution of size 20 μm for each pixel. The dataset includes both ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data. The P21 sample has 2.5k spots, and the P22 samples have 10k spots.

We also used 10X Genomics scMultiome32 + ST57 dataset. This publicly accessible dataset combined Chromium Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression and Visium Spatial Gene Expression technologies to study and evaluate open chromatin regions and gene expression profiles in brains of TgCRND8 transgenic Alzheimer’s disease-like mice and Wild-type mice at 2.5, 5.7, 13.2, and 17.9 months of age. Twelve brains were collected at different time points: six from wild-type mice and six from transgenic mice. The late-stage data collection for wild-type mice was done at 13.2 months, and for transgenic mice was done at 17.9 months. There are 33459 cells in the Chromium dataset and 36801 spots in the Visium dataset. One hemisphere of the brain was used for single-cell RNA+ATAC assay, and the other hemisphere was used for ST.

For all data, we filtered the genes and peaks in ST and sc-multiome datasets by selecting those in the top 80th percentile based on expression levels.

Validation and evaluation

We used the spatial RNA-ATAC-seq dataset to validate our method. The spatial gene expression and chromatin accessibility profiles were obtained from the mouse brains at postnatal days 21 and 22, and in this study, known as P21 and P22 datasets. For benchmarking of our method, we performed a train-test split of the P21 and P22 samples separately. Both the datasets are split based on the spots. In this context, the training sets act as the single-cell data, while the test sets act as the spatial data. Thus, the E and O matrices are represented by the training set of spatial RNA and ATAC, respectively, and the test sets of spatial RNA and ATAC act as the Es and Os matrices.

We also performed further analyses using the P21 and P22 spatial ATAC-RNA data. We assigned the P21 RNA and ATAC data as the E and O matrices to represent gene expression and chromatin accessibility for individual spots. The P22 spatial RNA data was the Es matrix providing spatially resolved gene expression information. We reconstructed the spatial ATAC-seq data Os for the P22 sample by using the relationships inferred from the joint decomposition of O, E, and Es matrices.

Simulation frameworks for evaluating prediction performance

To rigorously evaluate ISON’s predictive performance in a controlled setting, we designed a simulation framework for spot-resolution spatial multiome data using the scDesign3 framework44. We selected matched sc-multiome and Visium spatial transcriptomics samples from the 10x Genomics Alzheimer’s dataset (same age and condition). The sc-multiome data were clustered and manually annotated using marker genes, and the Visium spots were deconvoluted with CARD58 to estimate cell-type proportions per spot. We refined the simulation and preprocessing pipeline to reduce technical noise. Specifically, we applied additional quality control filtering to remove lowly informative genes and potential high-count outlier cells. Genes were retained if they were detected in at least 3% of cells, and cells with unusually high library sizes (total counts >20,000) were removed, as these likely reflect doublets or technical artifacts. This additional filtering step improved the stability of downstream simulations and model training. The simulated spatial multiome data were generated by combining the original sc-multiome sample with its replicate and randomly splitting cells into two groups. One group was used to generate simulated spot-level data by aggregating 5 × n cells per spot and averaging the counts, yielding an average of n cells per spot (with n ranging from 4 to 9, constrained by the smallest cell-type size of 46 cells). The second group was used as input to ISON and SPAGE, along with the simulated spatial RNA data. Predicted spatial ATAC profiles were then compared to the true profiles using PCC as the performance metric. Performance was evaluated across all values of n, providing multiple simulation conditions rather than a single instance. Regarding model settings, we used λ1 = 3 and a batch size of 1024 for ISON in the simulation experiments. The larger batch size was chosen because the simulated data was substantially less sparse than real-world spatial multiome data; in this setting, larger batches reduce variance in parameter updates and improve stability during factor learning. Similarly, we set a higher number of factors (K = 32), as the simulated dataset benefits from increased model capacity to capture its richer and denser structure.

For the gene-subsampling experiment, we randomly selected 100, 200, 500, or 1000 genes from the P22 spatial RNA dataset, and the average number of expressed genes per spot was 18.05, 38.09, 93.98, and 179.83, respectively. The P21 spatial RNA-ATAC dataset was used as the reference sc-multiome input. ISON’s predicted spatial ATAC profiles were compared with MAGIC-imputed ATAC signals using PCC to assess performance under reduced gene coverage.

Hyperparameter tuning

We used the P21 and P22 spatial ATAC + RNA datasets described in Validation and Evaluation to determine optimal hyperparameters for ISON. The model has three tunable parameters: λ1, λ2, and K. PCC was used as the evaluation metric to select the best configuration. A systematic grid search was performed to explore the parameter space and identify stable settings that yield high peak-wise PCC values. Across datasets, the method was robust, with default parameters λ1 = 15, λ2 = 0.001, and K = 8 performing consistently well.

We also conducted a grid search on a single batch (transgenic 17.9-month sample) from the Alzheimer’s dataset, where optimal performance was achieved at K = 64 while keeping other parameters the same. The model was trained with a batch size of 512 for 50 iterations. Empirically, λ1 = 15 provided a stable and balanced contribution across data modalities, and the method was relatively insensitive to variations in λ2. Increasing K allows the model to capture more granular cell types, whereas smaller K is appropriate when fewer cell types are expected. These parameters can be adjusted by users to suit their specific datasets. Results of the hyperparameter tuning are provided in Fig. S28.

To determine the optimal λ1, we adopted a biology-informed tuning strategy and evaluated it using scDesign3-based simulated data with nine cells per spot. This implementation leverages the enrichment of cis-regulatory interactions at short genomic distances. Using the learned embeddings W1 (peaks) and W2 (genes), we defined peak-gene pairs within 10 kb as proximal pairs and pairs separated by >1 Mb or located on different chromosomes as distal pairs. We computed a contrastive separation score as the difference between the mean cosine similarity of proximal and distal pairs. Genomic proximity was used as a weak supervisory signal reflecting probabilistic enrichment, rather than assuming all nearby pairs are regulatory. To ensure numerical stability and biologically plausible predictions, the model includes an internal safeguard that does not report results when the predicted average spatial ATAC exceeds 100× the observed average single-cell ATAC, as such cases typically indicate invalid parameter settings. In simulation study, larger separation scores—computed without ground truth—consistently corresponded to higher predictive PCC when ground truth was available (Fig. S29), indicating that maximizing this separation provides an effective and objective criterion for λ1 selection.

Spatial variability

To compute the spatial variability, we used Moran’s I statistic. We first created a graph from spatial coordinates and then computed neighborhood enrichment. For P21 and P22 data, we used the ‘RNA_clusters’ label information to calculate the neighborhood enrichment. Then, we calculated Moran’s I for each peak or gene. Moran’s I values range from −1 to 1, and a positive Moran’s I indicates positive spatial autocorrelation, which means that the spots close to each other have similar patterns whereas a negative Moran’s I indicates negative spatial autocorrelation. If Moran’s I value is close to zero, then it indicates absence of spatial pattern. For these computations, we used Squidpy59 package version 1.6.5.

Simulation design for scalability testing

We used the 10X Single-Cell Multiome and Visium datasets to simulate datasets of varying sample sizes in order to evaluate the scalability of ISON and other methods. Simulated samples were generated by randomly selecting samples from each dataset with replacement and downsampling their counts, restricting the total at a maximum of 1000 counts per cell. Each new sample was assigned a modified barcode by appending a “1” to the original. For the Visium data, spatial coordinates were duplicated based on the selected spots from the original dataset, and the corresponding cluster labels were similarly duplicated to match the resampled spots and cells.

Peaks and genes assignment to modules

For peaks and genes assignment, we normalized the W1 and W2 matrices and selected the top features to assign them to modules. Specifically, we first scaled the columns (modules) to the same magnitude by dividing each column by its sum. Then, we converted the row (features) -wise values into proportions such that they sum to one for each feature. For each module, we selected the top 800 peaks from this normalized W1 and the top 200 genes from normalized W2.

Gene regulatory network inference

We utilized chromVAR48 to infer TF binding motifs from the spatial chromatin accessibility data generated by our model, providing insights into regulatory elements associated with chromatin dynamics. We also used LINGER20 to derive trans-regulatory relationships between TFs and their TGs, and to construct a TF-TG regulatory network from Visium spatial transcriptomic and the generated epigenomic data. We also obtained TF activity information from LINGER.

Cell type annotation and pseudo-bulking

To obtain cell types, we initially performed log-transformation and PCA to obtain low dimensional representations of the spatial transcriptomic data. We used Harmony60 to remove batch effects between different samples and obtain robust cell clustering using the Leiden61 algorithm. The cell clusters were manually annotated based on the expression of known marker genes known in human cortical tissue62. For each cluster, we evaluated marker gene expression profiles and assigned a cell type label according to the most highly expressed gene markers. The cluster labels obtained were reasonable and corresponded well with known brain regions (Fig. S30). Then, to reduce noise and facilitate comparative analyses, we performed pseudo-bulking by aggregating gene expression counts across spatial spots, grouped by batch and cell type.

For each annotated cell type, we computed average expression values for four biological states: transgenic_old, transgenic_young, wild_old, and wild_young. Regulatory module identification and enrichment analysis

To identify co-regulated gene modules, we applied K-means clustering to the normalized TF-TG interaction matrix, resulting in 12 distinct regulatory modules (Fig. S31). For each module, we extracted gene sets and performed GSEA63 using the GSEAPY64 Python package. We computed NES for each logFC contrast and assessed significance using family-wise error rate (FWER)-adjusted p-values, considering a threshold of p − value < 0.05.

Modules with high positive NES values were interpreted as being enriched for upregulated genes, while those with highly negative NES values were enriched for downregulated genes. To evaluate the biological relevance of each module, we compared the module gene sets to the “GO Biological Process 2025” gene sets. Functional annotation and combined score were used to identify overrepresented biological processes within each module, providing context for the underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Comparison with baseline methods

We evaluated the performance of our method with Tangram36, RCTD37, GIMVI39, SPAGE38, and CCA+KNN33,34. We apply Seurat-based CCA to generate low-dimensional embeddings of gene expression data and construct a similarity matrix integrating single-cell and spatial gene expression information. Using this similarity matrix, we implement KNN with 15 neighbors to predict spatial chromatin accessibility based on the spatial gene expression data. For Tangram, we used the default parameters recommended in their tutorial. For RCTD, we enabled doublet_mode and set max_cores to 8. To evaluate RCTD’s scalability, we set n_max_cells to 1,000,000 to accommodate larger sample sizes. We use the RCTD function to generate a normalized weight matrix representing cell type proportions for each spatial spot and then use these weights with scATAC-seq data to reconstruct spatially resolved ATAC-seq profiles. For GIMVI, we used default parameters with 100 training epochs. During scalability assessments of GIMVI, we applied early stopping at 25 epochs. For SPAGE, we followed the default tutorial settings.

Imputation methods

To benchmark ISON against existing approaches, we applied MAGIC40, DeepImpute41, CL-Impute43, and SCALE42 to the raw spatial ATAC data, generating imputed spatial ATAC profiles for comparison. Imputation methods were used to denoise the raw data, providing a fair comparison with ISON-predicted ATAC. For all methods, we employed the default settings recommended in their respective tutorials.

Implementation details

ISON was implemented using PyTorch version 1.11, with Scanpy65 employed for handling the AnnData format. ISON is compatible with both CPU and GPU execution. For GPU runs, we used the NVIDIA A100 with CUDA Toolkit 11.4.3. The maximum allowed runtime for each computation was 72 hours, with a memory allocation limit of 256 GB RAM. On our computing server, when a partition is not shared by other users, the job scheduler may temporarily permit processes to exceed their requested memory allocation. As a result, the observed peak memory usage can slightly surpass the 256 GB that was requested. This behavior did not pose any issues for memory monitoring.
