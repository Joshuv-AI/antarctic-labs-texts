FungiNetDB: a uniform (pre-)processing platform for fungal expression data
Authors: Sascha Schäuble, Daniela Albrecht-Eckardt, Gianni Panagiotou, Thomas Dandekar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFungi, and particularly fungal pathogens, are having an increasing impact on human health and economy. At the same time, modern high-throughput technologies offer insights deep into the molecular level and thus mechanisms, giving scientists new opportunities to identify fungal biomarkers and essential components for survival and virulence. This wealth of data, however, is most often only analyzed in the context of a specific scientific question, while many more projects may benefit from a multifaceted view on e.g. fungal gene expression under various conditions. The prime challenge is the limited access to readily pre-processed data and circumventing technological biases introduced by different sequencing platforms and software tools across different projects. We here present FungiNetDB, a web platform comprising 139 fungal pathogenicity datasets and statistical analysis of more than 2000 different pairwise gene expression comparisons. FungiNetDB thus resembles a most comprehensive fungal transcriptomics resource, which can be explored without any programming knowledge, allows highly customizable filtering and cross-project comparisons and download of all offered data tables and visualizations.Keywords: database, warehouse, gene expression, fungal pathogens

Introduction

Human fungal pathogens are on the rise and pose a growing threat to global health, particularly in immunocompromised populations, where infections often lead to high morbidity and mortality. Their clinical importance has been recognized by the WHO and the presentation of a priority list of fungal pathogens in 2022 (Fisher and Denning, 2023, WHO, 2023). Compared to bacterial or viral pathogens, less is known about fungi, leaving major gaps in our understanding of their pathogenicity mechanisms. The upscaled generation of large-scale OMICs data allows us to address these gaps and investigate fungal biology during infection at unmet depth, e.g. by transcriptomic profiling to explore gene expression impact on how fungal pathogens invade, adapt, and survive within the human host.

To maximize the usage of such large data resources, effective means for exploration and visualization have to be utilized. Desktop tools such as the Integrative Genomics Viewer IGV (Thorvaldsdóttir et al. 2013) provide secure means to investigate individual data sets, but are limited towards cross-comparing multiple data sets. Publicly available data portals, on the other hand, represent a proven and powerful approach to present and comprehensively explore or analyze vast amounts of data. Especially cancer data were centralized by, e.g. the GDC (Genomic Data Commons) or ICGC (International Cancer Genomics Consortium) platforms (Grossman et al. 2016, Zhang et al. 2019). In addition, the need for uniformly re-processing data to avoid biases introduced by different software tools dedicated to similar data pre-processing tasks was recognized and addressed, e.g. on the Xena platform, which re-analyzed cancer data from TCGA, TARGET (Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments) and GTEx (Genotype–Tissue Expression) (Goldman et al. 2020). Fungal data platforms, however, are less available. FungiDB (https://fungidb.org), the Fungal & Oomycete Informatics Resources platform, allows to analyze fungal data across various data types and, among others, offers to host transcriptomic evidences (Basenko et al. 2018a). These data include statistical significance information for many, albeit not for all contained datasets based on the tool DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014). FungiDB content is accessible after registration, since it was switched to a subscription model in early 2025 (Basenko et al. 2018a). Dedicated fungal domain sources for Candida (CGD, Candida Genome Database, www.candidagenome.org) and Aspergillus species (AspGD, Aspergillus Genome Database, www.aspgd.org, not accessible at the time of this publication), including Candida albicans and Aspergillus fumigatus, provide genomic sequence and functional information data (Arnaud et al. 2012, Lew-Smith et al. 2025). A freely accessible web resource focusing on uniformly processed fungal expression data, ready for easy exploration, cross-comparison, visualization and download, however, is lacking.

We here present FungiNetDB, a publicly accessible web platform which, at the time of writing, includes 139 datasets with n = 2107 pairwise gene expression comparisons of fungal pathogenicity datasets of the FungiNet consortium (https://www.funginet.de/) and external sources. We host data from 23 different fungal and host species, primarily from the fungal pathogens C. albicans, A. fumigatus, and Lichtheimia corymbifera (Mucorales spp.), which are members of the highest two categories in the WHO fungal pathogen priority list from 2022. Data pre-processing and analysis was done using the R-based pipeline GEO2RNAseq (Seelbinder et al. 2019) for all data. Next to the essential quality control, read-trimming and mapping steps, this pipeline specifically also includes software-based removal of any remaining ribosomal RNAs using sortMeRNA (Kopylova et al. 2012) and statistical comparisons using multiple tools by default to ensure a high reliability of our results. All analysis results are available for download as table or standard visualizations. FungiNetDB therefore offers a valuable addition to the current landscape of fungal data repositories focused on providing pre-analyzed gene expression data and means for cross-comparison.

Analyzing RNA-seq data involves multiple pre-processing steps, each requiring careful choice of methodology. Quality filtering and trimming ensure that sequencing errors and technical artifacts do not compromise downstream analyses. Read mapping tools align sequences to fungal reference genomes, which themselves may vary in completeness and annotation quality across species. Finally, statistical frameworks are applied to quantify differential expression and to highlight genes central to infection processes, such as those involved in stress responses, virulence factor production, and immune evasion. Multiple software packages exist for each of these steps, yet differences in implementation can result in non-comparable outcomes across studies.

Methods

Backend

The integral database for FungiNetDB is realized with PostgreSQL (v16) hosted on server infrastructure at the Leibniz-HKI, Jena, Germany, to ensure its continuous availability. FungiNetDB consists of 148 tables, including core view tables to handle user rights for private and public datasets, or data tables holding metadata of the conducted experiments. All pre-processed data results, including comparison-specific statistics and fold-changes, are organized in project-specific files on the backend server. Further tables were introduced to flexibly store evolving lists of supported organisms or updated technologies over time, while other tables manage a growing list of, for example, media or compounds tested anew, but hitherto not included in the database for any prior experiments.

Templates

To ensure compatibility with the FAIR principles (making data findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable), we created a comprehensive metadata template, which enables proper pre-processing and statistical analysis of any given RNA sequencing data. The spreadsheet-based template comprises detailed IDF (Investigation Description Format) and SDRF (Sample and Data Relationship Format) sheets, as well as sheets with example data for easy adaptation. Users can download the templates from https://funginetdb.hki-jena.de/FungiNetDB/downloads.php and run their own pre-processing with GEO2RNAseq to cross-compare with FungiNetDB-available datasets. For registered users, FungiNetDB itself contains online metadata forms to effectively incorporate further datasets into the backends’ PostgreSQL-database.

Pre-processing

Pre-processing of raw sequencing reads (single or paired end) including quality control and gene abundance estimation is done with the GEO2RNAseq pipeline (Seelbinder et al. 2019) based on the R programming language. In brief, quality analysis is done using FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) before and after trimming. Read-quality trimming is achieved using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014). Prior to reference mapping, reads are rRNA-filtered by default using SortMeRNA with a single rRNA database combining all rRNA databases shipped with SortMeRNA (Kopylova et al. 2012). Reads are then mapped against available reference genomes commonly available at NCBI datasets (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/) or specific domains, such as CGD for Candida spp. using HiSat2 (Kim et al. 2019). Gene abundance estimation is realized using featureCounts in either single- or paired-end mode with default parameters (Liao et al. 2014). MultiQC (Ewels et al. 2016) is used to summarize and assess the output of FastQC, Trimmomatic, HiSat, and featureCounts. The resulting count data is then normalized using median-of-ratios (MRN) (Anders and Huber, 2010), TPM (Transcripts Per Kilobase Million), and RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase Million). Both TPM and RPKM are provided to ensure backward comparability, but have to be considered with care for cross-experiment comparison (Zhao et al. 2020). Differential gene expression is determined using several tools. In contrast to, e.g. FungiDB, where differential gene expression is determined using DESeq2, in FungiNetDB pairwise tests for predefined comparisons are performed using a consensus voting of four tools by default including DESeq (Anders and Huber, 2010), DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014), limma voom (Ritchie et al. 2015), and edgeR (Robinson et al. 2009) to report multiple test corrected P-values.

Frontend

User access to the database is organized via a web portal set up through an apache2 web server. It is constructed using a combination of html and css, Javascript, jQuery UI (v1.13, with an underlying jQuery v3.6.0), Datatables (v1.11.3) with several extensions and PHP (v8.3.3). Access for public and registered users to manage data entries is available at https://funginetdb.hki-jena.de/FungiNetDB/. All frontend construction files are containerized using docker to ensure its robustness and accessibility with a stable software environment encapsulated from the hosting operating system. Actual data are retrieved via a dedicated port access to the backend database location.

Results

FungiNetDB provides easy web access for currently 139 experimental datasets, stored in the FungiNetDB backend database, comprising data from 23 different fungal species and host systems (Fig. 1). The public access via the “Access FungiNetDB” button at the frontpage https://funginetdb.hki-jena.de/ allows for exploring all publicly available datasets within the database. Additional logins are provided for more than 200 FungiNet members, who have contributed to datasets available at FungiNetDB, and ensure proper user right, data and privacy management. Registered users can upload new data using customized online forms that interactively change their status per module depending on completeness of mandatory and optional data (Supplementary Fig. S1).

FungiNetDB expression platform: FungiNetDB incorporates gene expression data uniformly pre-processed with the GEO2RNAseq pipeline. All data can be explored and downloaded from the web frontend or further filtered and visualized online.

Upon accessing FungiNetDB the user interface is divided into two parts, Gene Search and Experiment Search. While Gene Search allows exploration of specific sets of genes, Experiment Search offers an overview across all data hosted at FungiNetDB. The Experiment Search offers a powerful search box, which aids to filter the included datasets for all displayed columns. Which columns to show is fully customizable and includes the investigation title, organism, GEO accession number, or involved FungiNet members and projects. Upon following links for individual projects, additional information, for instance, metadata in the “Source” tab of each dataset, can be selected after clicking the “Column visibility” button, which is available in each dataset tab. Precise pipeline calls and statistical details are available in the “Data Processing” tab, while further methodological details can be sought following the direct publication links given in the “Related Publication” tab. Individual or up to three datasets at the same time can be further explored by either displaying analysis tables or utilizing different visual summaries after following the “Show statistics” link from the menu (Fig. 2). The downloadable table view (Fig. 2A) summarizes available fold change and statistical data. It is fully customizable to show the columns of interest, such as mean values, fold changes or statistical test results and can be filtered for desired genes. A first visual inspection of all comparisons of interest is feasible using box plot statistics (Fig. 2B), which can be further fine-tuned regarding considered P-value and fold change cut-offs. The difference or similarities across up to three conditions is best visualized using a venn diagram overview, which can also be customized to include, e.g. only up- or downregulated genes (Fig. 2C). Finally, individual gene fold changes across selected conditions can also be displayed as bar plots. The displayed genes can be specified and all fold changes across the selected comparisons can be downloaded. All visual inspections are readily downloadable as vectorized svg or rasterized png files.

Data analysis and download capabilities: Upon loading the statistics visualization for project ID 126 (Investigation title: “Analysis of Transcript Abundance in white, intermediate, and opaque cell states from WT and EFG1 heterozygotes in Candida albicans”), several options exist for data exploration. A) table view of a single condition comparison. B) Boxplot statistics for all selected criteria and comparisons. C) Venn diagram for up to three selected comparisons. D) Bar plot and efficient table comparison of log2(fold change) for chosen comparisons. Genes to display can be customized by including or excluding gene IDs in the respective form object.

The Gene Search section in the top view of FungiNetDB is a powerful tool to investigate selected genes of interest across all available datasets. Different name synonyms are resolved using specific synonym tables within the PostgreSQL database. The retrieved information on the expression of the requested genes across all available experiments is displayed as a table and includes log2(fold change) and statistical information for all statistical tests that have been computed (Fig. 3A). These data are readily downloadable for further analysis. For instance, a scientist might be interested in cross-comparing the expression of the gene ECE1. ECE1 encodes for Candidalysin in C. albicans, the first discovered cytolytic peptide toxin in a human fungal pathogen, which is essential for mucosal infection and imposes negative effects on bacterial gut species and their metabolic output (Liang et al. 2024, Moyes et al. 2016). A search in FungiNetDB reveals 585 entries with statistical evaluation for ECE1 expression in C. albicans and in known homologues (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Data S1). Across 70 projects, 50% of the comparisons (n = 292) reveal negative (n = 116) or positive (n = 176) differential expression (Fig. 3B). These insights highlight a repeated molecular change and potential impact of this gene across various investigations, but also the need for exploring the direction of change in the context of the conducted comparisons. For instance, the project with ID 53 and investigation title “Transcription profiling during nitrogen sensing and uptake in Candida albicans.” shows a pronounced upregulation of ECE1 upon arginine feeding (Supplementary Data S1) (Ramachandra et al. 2014). Of note, the associated publication dates two years before the published characterization of Candidalysin, supporting the importance of reinvestigating prior datasets to reach a comprehensive view. In contrast, with the exception of a TUP1 mutant strain, iron starvation experiments yielded no significance (Experiment ID 43), while ECE1 was mostly downregulated upon addition of weak organic acids to MRS medium (Experiment ID 27, Supplementary Data S1) (Cottier et al. 2015).

Cross-data analysis of selected genes. A) Initial table summary view, when exploring available data for ECE1 cross-comparison. B) ECE1 and C) pksP volcano and stacked bar plot summary statistics for prominent genes involved in fungal infection with C. albicans or A. fumigatus, respectively. All displayed experiment IDs refer to separate projects, which commonly involve multiple tested conditions (e.g. different mutant or time data, Supplementary Data S1).

Similarly to ECE1, a search for the polyketide synthase gene pksP of A. fumigatus (Langfelder et al. 1998) reveals a range of expression changes [n(up-regulation) = 38, n(downregulation) = 40, n(no significant change) = 75, Fig. 3C]. While expression of pksP in A. fumigatus cultivated in human blood did not show significant differences (experiment ID 31, Supplementary Data S1) (Irmer et al. 2015), expression of pksP is mostly upregulated upon endogenously produced and secreted fungal oxylipins (experiment ID 143) (Niu et al. 2020), and primarily downregulated upon knock out of the Negative cofactor two A and B genes NctA and NctB (experiment ID150) (Furukawa et al. 2020).

Although these two examples provide only a glimpse into the data hosted by FungiNetDB, they demonstrate the versatility—and ease of use—of the web portal towards investigation of fungal genes of interest across multiple datasets to reveal differential expression in diverse contexts.

Discussion

It is widely acknowledged that proper data sharing is key to advancing life science research. Databases such as FungiDB, and CGD provide valuable genomic information and tools for fungal organisms (Basenko et al. 2018b, Lew-Smith et al. 2025). While FungiDB includes 194 RNA-seq datasets on a diverse range of fungal species, the current number of 139 gene expression datasets hosted at FungiNetDB comprise primarily data on WHO fungal priority pathogens (WHO, 2023). These include, but are not limited to 33 A. fumigatus (n = 15 at FungiDB) and 64 C. albicans (n = 18 at FungiDB) uniformly analyzed datasets, respectively. Also, FungiNetDB includes readily comparable data across different datasets, which has been proven valuable in other contexts, particularly in, e.g. the cancer research area (Goldman et al. 2020). FungiNetDB offers highly customizable data tables of uniformly pre-processed expression data focused on the fungal domain and particularly human fungal pathogens. It provides easy accessibility, comprehensive metadata information and is designed to be usable by non-programming experts. Post-processing of large-scaled data is also feasible by computationally skilled users based on downloadable data tables and metadata hosted at FungiNetDB. FungiNetDB provides a freely accessible web portal-based access to fungal expression data particularly focused on WHO fungal priority pathogens generated by the FungiNet consortium and beyond, given external data sources. The scope of FungiNetDB is limited to comprehensive metadata and bulk transcriptome expression data. The metadata includes detailed information of applied pipeline parameters for data pre-processing and raw data links to public databases such as Gene Expression Omnibus (Barrett et al. 2013). Over the last decade, next to bulk transcriptomics also single RNA sequencing and meta-transcriptomic as well as -genomic datasets are increasingly generated. Although FungiNetDB currently hosts primarily bulk transcriptome data, it may also hold further omics data analysis results and thereby increase the value beyond cross-comparing transcriptomics to also include multi-omics fungal data across different projects. Besides these potential expansions, FungiNetDB will continue to grow also in the number of included datasets. Given newly appearing and pre-processed expression datasets in the fungal domain, the platform will offer steadily growing contextual insights into mode of actions of fungi beyond individual experiments. In conclusion, FungiNetDB offers a valuable resource to evaluate expression data in single and also across various conditions and to ultimately aid uncovering novel mechanism that help tackling current and future challenges of fungal pathogenicity.
