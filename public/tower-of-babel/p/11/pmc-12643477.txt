IL-13 protects epithelial cells from SARS-CoV-2 infection by inhibiting ACE2-mediated virus binding and cell entry
Authors: Vadim Pivniouk, Oksana Pivniouk, Jennifer L Uhrlaub, Seongmin Hahn, Allyson Molzahn, Hiroki Kimura, Mari Numata, Hong Wei Chu, Julie G Ledford, Monica Kraft, Janko Ž Nikolich, Donata Vercelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) preferentially infects airway epithelial cells. This infection is mediated by the binding of SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) protein to ACE2 expressed on target cells. Patients with allergic (type-2) asthma have been reported to be less susceptible to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), but these effects are controversial and mechanistically unclear. We previously showed lower expression of ACE2 mRNA in airway epithelial cells from type-2 asthmatics compared to healthy donors. Moreover, we and others demonstrated that the type-2 cytokine interleukin 13 (IL-13) suppresses ACE2 expression and SARS-CoV-2 infection in human epithelial cells. To better understand the relationship between type-2 inflammation, ACE2 expression, and SARS-CoV-2 infection, we investigated the effects of IL-13 on critical steps of epithelial cell infection by SARS-CoV-2: S protein-mediated binding to ACE2 on epithelial cells, and ACE2-mediated SARS-CoV-2 entry into these cells. Recombinant IL-13 significantly inhibited both these processes. This inhibition appeared to be mediated by IL-13-induced suppression of ACE2 transcription because IL-13 failed to affect S protein-mediated viral entry into cells that express ACE2 under the control of an IL-13 unresponsive heterologous promoter. We propose that IL-13 protects epithelial cells from SARS-CoV-2 infection largely by inhibiting ACE2 expression and ACE2-mediated downstream events that allow the virus to access its cellular targets.Keywords: COVID-19, type-2 inflammation, SARS-CoV-2, ACE2, epithelial cells

Introduction

The global pandemic of a new respiratory illness, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), was caused by the novel coronavirus, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2).1,2 This virus uses angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) as its main receptor3 and the cellular protease TMPRSS2 for Spike (S) protein priming.3 SARS-CoV-2 preferentially targets the airways and infects human airway epithelial cells2 such as lung type II pneumocytes and nasal goblet cells that express ACE2 and TMPRSS2.4 Because these genes are also expressed by gut4 and kidney5 epithelial cells, these organs are susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection.6–8

The relation between COVID-19 and asthma remains controversial. Some studies suggested that type-2 asthmatics are less susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection [reviewed in Pathinayake et al.9]. Recently we showed that patients with asthma, high levels of T2 cytokine in serum, and a higher frequency of Th2 cells had less severe COVID with reduced viral loads.10 In contrast, other study linked type-2 immunity to poor COVID-19 outcomes.11 We showed that ACE2 expression was significantly decreased in airway epithelial cells from patients with type-2 asthma and allergic rhinitis compared to healthy controls.12 Furthermore, we consistently found a significant negative correlation between ACE2 and IL13 expression.12 Another group showed that IL-13 stimulation inhibits SARS-CoV-2 infection of human bronchial epithelial cells cultured at the air-liquid interface (ALI).13 More recently, SARS-CoV-2 replication was found to be decreased and further inhibitable by IL-13 in airway epithelial cells from children with allergic asthma but not in airway epithelial cells from healthy, non-sensitized children.14 Interestingly, in this setting SARS-CoV-2 replication was strongly associated with epithelial differentiation and cilia formation.14 However, as previously noted,15 no study to date has conclusively found that the lower ACE2 expression due to T2 inflammation is a mechanism of protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection. Our study was designed specifically to address this question and dissect the effects of IL-13 on critical steps required for SARS-CoV-2 epithelial cell infection, such as viral attachment and cell entry.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and primary airway epithelial cells

Vero cells (CCL-81) and Vero E6 cells (CRL-1586) derived from the African green monkey (Chlorocebus sabaeus) kidney, human lung Calu-3 cells (HTB55™) and HEK293T/17 cells (CRL-11268™) were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, Virginia, USA). HEK293T/ACE2 cells stably transduced with human ACE2 were purchased from GeneCopoeia (SL221, Rockville, Maryland, USA). All cell lines were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) supplemented with FBS (10%, Sigma, St Louis, Missouri, USA), GlutaMax (2 mM), penicillin (50 units/ml) and streptomycin (50 µg/ml, all from Gibco).

Four donors (3 males, 1 female, 22 to 54 yr of age) of human primary airway cells were recruited from the population of Tucson, Arizona, and provided informed consent before undergoing any procedure. The study protocol was approved by the University of Arizona Institutional Review Board. Study participants had no evidence of airway obstruction and no history of pulmonary disease. All donors were atopic, as determined by clinical history, allergen skin testing, and blood eosinophil levels. Participants underwent bronchoscopy with endobronchial-protected brushing, as previously described.16 To obtain bronchial epithelial cells, brushing of the proximal airways was performed using a separate protected cytologic brush for each pass, for a total of ten passes. Participants were discharged after their forced expiratory volume in 1 second reached 90% of their pre-bronchoscopy, post-albuterol value.

Freshly isolated airway bronchial epithelial cells from endobronchial brushing were cultured with PneumaCultTM-EX Plus Medium (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). Once confluent, cells were trypsinized and seeded onto collagen-coated polyester 12-mm-diameter TranswellTM insert membranes (Corning, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) at 4 × 104 cells/well. To allow for differentiation, cells were cultured under ALI conditions for 3 wk with PneumaCultTM -Air-Liquid Interface Medium (StemCell Technologies). Differentiation status of the airway epithelial cells was assessed after 3 wk of ALI culture by microscopic examination. Specifically, we confirmed (1) the formation of a well-developed pseudostratified epithelial layer with sufficient thickness and (2) the presence of motile cilia on ciliated cells, as observed by their coordinated beating under light microscopy. These features are indicative of proper mucociliary differentiation under ALI conditions.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and RT-qPCR

RNA from cells was extracted with the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Germantown, Maryland, USA). The cDNA was synthesized on total RNA (500 ng) using the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen). RT-qPCR was carried out either with the QuantiTect SYBR Green PCR kit (Qiagen) on an ABI 7900 Applied Biosystems thermocycler (ThermoFisher Scientific) or on a CFX96 Touch system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). All genes were amplified using commercially available primers (QuantiTect Primer Assays, Qiagen) or TaqMan probes (Applied Biosystems). Each sample was run in triplicate. The change in gene expression relative to phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) or peptidylprolyl isomerase A (PPIA) and calculated using the 2-ΔΔct method.17

SARS-CoV-2 infection

SARS-CoV-2, isolate USA-WA1/2020 (deposited by Dr Natalie J. Thornburg at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) was obtained from the World Reference Center for Emerging Viruses and Arboviruses (WRCEVA). SARS-CoV-2 stocks were generated from the received vial as a single passage on mycoplasma-negative Vero cells (ATCC CCL-81).

Calu-3 cells were infected with SARS-CoV-2 as previously described.18 Briefly, cells grown in 96-well plate were treated with PBS or IL-13 (10 or 50 ng/ml). After 72 h, the medium was removed, and SARS-CoV-2 (50 or 500 plaque forming units [pfu]) was added to all wells for 2 h. Cells were then cultured for 48 h in fresh medium containing the original IL-13 concentrations. Virus was released by freezing plates at −80 °C. To determine viral titers, supernatants from each row of an infected Calu-3 plate were titrated by 10-fold serial dilutions on 96-well indicator Vero plates, and plaque numbers were determined by quantitative plaque assay as previously described.19 Results were expressed as average numbers of pfu/well detected in 8 replicates derived from the original Calu-3 plate.

Functional S1 protein binding assay

The S1 binding assay was described previously.18 Briefly, epithelial cells grown to confluency were treated with IL-13 (2 or 10 ng/ml) or PBS at 37 °C. After 72 h cells were harvested, incubated with recombinant His-tagged S1 protein (5.625 µg/ml: Sino Biological US, Wayne, Pennsylvania, USA) followed by an anti-His tag-PE antibody (Cat no. 362603, Biolegend, San Diego, California, USA) and analyzed by flow cytometry. Cell debris were excluded by gating based on forward and side scatter. Use of a commercial dye (Zombie UV, Biolegend) demonstrated that cell viability was consistently >80%. Normal rat serum (2%) was used to block nonspecific staining. At least 20,000 events were acquired on an LSR II or a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software (Version 10.10.0, Becton Dickinson, 2023). S1 binding was expressed as the percentage of S1-binding cells in each sample relative to the average of PBS-treated samples.

Flow cytometry evaluation of ACE2 expression

Surface expression of ACE2 was determined as previously described.18 Briefly, Vero E6 and Calu-3 cells were grown in 12-well plates until confluent and treated with IL-13 (1 or 10 ng/ml) or PBS for 72 h. Cells were harvested and stained with either mouse anti-human ACE2-PE (Sino Biological, clone 36, for Vero E6 cells), or mouse anti-human ACE2-AF647 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN: clone 535919, for Calu-3 cells). Nonspecific staining was blocked with normal mouse serum (5%). In brief, 20,000 to 30,000 events were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa or FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and ACE2 expression was analyzed after gating on live cells using FlowJo software (Version 10.6.0, Becton Dickinson, 2019).

Plasmids

The lentiviral packaging plasmid, psPAX2, and the VSV-G envelope expressing plasmid, pMD2.G, (Addgene plasmid no. 12260; http://n2t.net/addgene : 12260; RRID: Addgene 12260, and Addgene plasmid no. 12259; http://n2t.net/addgene : 12259; RRID: Addgene_12259) were gifts from Didier Trono (École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne, Switzerland). The lentiviral reporter plasmid pCIB-GFP expressing GFP was kindly provided by Dr Michael Johnson (University of Arizona). The SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein expressing plasmid HDM-SARS2-Spike-delta21 (Addgene plasmid no. 155130; http://n2t.net/addgene : 155130; RRID: Addgene_155130)20 was a gift from Jesse Bloom (University of Washington, Seattle, WA).

Production of pseudotyped lentiviral particles

Pseudotyped lentiviral particles were prepared as previously described.18 Briefly, HEK 293 T/17 cells were transfected with the packaging plasmid psPAX2 (15 µg) plus envelope plasmids (either HDM-SARS2-Spike-delta21 or pMD2.G: 15 µg) and a human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) reporter plasmid expressing GFP (pCIB-GFP: 15 µg) using Lipofectamine 3,000 (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 48 h later, culture supernatants containing pseudotyped lentiviruses were collected and stored at −80 °C. The functional titer of VSV-G-pseudotyped lentivirus [2.34 × 106 transducing units (TU)/ml] was determined by flow cytometry quantification of GFP+ Vero E6 cells as described.21,22 The titer of SARS-CoV-2-S protein-pseudotyped virus (1 × 104 TU/ml) was estimated by side-by-side comparison with serial dilutions of VSV-G-pseudotyped lentivirus in Vero E6 cells.

Cell transduction with pseudotyped viral particles

Epithelial cells were transduced with pseudotyped lentivirus as described previously.18 Briefly, cells were treated with IL-13 (10 ng/ml) or PBS. After 48 h, IL-13-containing medium was replaced with polybrene (6 µg/ml)-supplemented medium containing either SARS-CoV-2 S protein- (2 × 103 TU) or VSV-G protein- (2.34 × 104 TU) pseudotyped lentivirus. Culture plates were centrifuged at 2,000×g for 1 hour at room temperature and cultured overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Transduction efficiency was assessed 72 hours post-transduction by quantifying GFP fluorescence. An Axio Vert microscope equipped with an Axiocam 305 camera (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, White Plains, New York, USA) was used to capture at least 20 (SARS-CoV-2-pseudotyped virus) or 10 (VSV-G-pseudotyped virus) frames for each well under 10× magnification, and numbers of fluorescent pixels were counted using Slide Analyzer.18

Statistical analysis

Statistical differences between treatment groups were assessed by an unpaired, 2-tailed t test or a Wilcoxon 2-sample test after assessing the normality of sample distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1) and Microsoft Excel.

Results

IL-13 inhibits ACE2 expression and SARS-CoV-2 infection in epithelial cells

Our initial experiments were designed to validate our cellular models for studying the effects of IL-13 on SARS-CoV-2 infection. To this end, we first examined the impact of IL-13 on ACE2 expression in Vero E6 green monkey kidney epithelial cells, which are widely used for SARS-CoV-2 infection studies;3;23,24 These cells were incubated with or without rIL-13 (10 ng/ml) and ACE2 mRNA levels were assessed by RT-qPCR after 24, 48, or 72 h. ACE2 transcription was strongly downregulated by IL-13 at all time points (Fig. 1A). A low IL-13 concentration (2 ng/ml) was sufficient to inhibit ACE2 expression (Fig. S1). A 24 h incubation with IL-13 (10 ng/ml) also inhibited ACE2 transcription in human lung-derived Calu-3 epithelial cells; these effects were further validated in primary human nasal and bronchial epithelial cells from non-asthmatic subjects (Fig. 1B–D). Reduced ACE2 transcription led to reduced cell surface protein expression. Flow cytometry analysis with anti-ACE2 antibodies demonstrated robust ACE2 surface expression by unstimulated kidney Vero E6 and lung Calu-3 epithelial cells (Fig. 2A and not shown), which was significantly inhibited following a 72-hour incubation with rIL-13 (10 or 2 ng/ml: Fig. 2A–C).

We then sought to confirm that IL-13 can prevent infection of epithelial cells with live SARS-CoV-2 virus. Calu-3 lung cells were pretreated with PBS or IL-13 (10 or 50 ng/ml) for 72 h and then infected with live SARS-CoV-2 (50 or 500 pfu) for 2 h. Infection was evaluated using a plaque assay.18 IL-13 (10 ng/ml) inhibited Calu-3 cell infection with a high dose of virus (500 pfu) by 57% (P = 0.02: Fig. 3A) and reduced infection with a lower SARS-CoV-2 dose (50 pfu) by 99% (P = 0.0002: Fig. 3B). Incubation with a higher IL-13 concentration (50 ng/ml) resulted in an even stronger blockade of Calu-3 infection by both SARS-CoV-2 doses (Fig. 3A and B).

IL-13 inhibits epithelial cell infection by SARS-CoV-2. Calu-3 cells treated with PBS or IL-13 (10 or 50 ng/ml) for 72 h were infected with SARS-CoV-2 [WA/2020, ∼500 (A) or 50 (B) pfu] for 2 h. Cells were then cultured for 48 h and viral titer determined by dispensing 10-fold serial dilutions of Calu-3 culture supernatants on 96-well indicator Vero plates. The number of pfu/well of Calu-3 plates was calculated by dividing the number of plaques on indicator Vero plates by the dilution factor. Shown are pfu detected in SARS-CoV-2-infected replicate wells pretreated with PBS or IL-13. Data represent the mean ± SEM from 1 representative experiment (n = 12 per group) out of 2. A 2-tailed t test was used for statistical analysis after assessing the normality of sample distribution.

In combination, these results confirmed previous reports12–14 and showed that IL-13 inhibits ACE2 expression and SARS-CoV-2 infection in epithelial cells, thereby validating our cellular models as effective tools for further mechanistic analyses.

IL-13 inhibits ACE2-dependent binding of SARS-CoV-2 S protein to epithelial cells

We next tested the effects of IL-13 on key ACE2-dependent events required for epithelial cell infection by SARS-CoV-2. Virions bind their target cells using the S1 fragment of the S protein.25,26 To investigate the effects of IL-13 on this early stage of infection, we used flow cytometry to test His-tagged S1 protein binding to Vero E6 and Calu-3 epithelial cells18 cultured with or without IL-13. About 20% of Vero E6 and Calu-3 cells cultured without IL-13 bound S1 protein (Fig. 4A). A 72-h incubation with IL-13 (2 or 10 ng/ml) strongly (up to 80%) and dose-dependently decreased S1 protein binding to Vero E6 cells (Fig. 4B). At odds with a previous suggestion,13 this inhibition was unlikely to depend on mucus production because Muc5B and Muc5AC expression was virtually unaffected in IL-13-stimulated Vero E6 cells (Fig. S2). Untreated Calu-3 cells also bound S1 protein (up to 17.5%, Fig. 4A), and this binding was also strongly inhibited by IL-13 (10 ng/ml) (Fig. 4C).

IL-13 inhibits S1 protein-mediated attachment to epithelial cells. (A) Representative flow cytometry plots showing SARS-CoV-2 S1 protein binding to Vero E6 and Calu-3 epithelial cells. Cells were treated with PBS or IL-13 (10 ng/ml) for 72 h and then incubated with or without recombinant His-tagged S1 protein, followed by an anti-His-PE antibody. S1 binding was assessed by flow cytometry. Effects of IL-13 on S1 protein binding to Vero E6 (2 or 10 ng/ml) (B) or Calu-3 (10 ng/ml) (C) cells. Data are shown as percentages of S1 protein-binding cells in IL-13- vs. PBS-treated cultures. Results represent the mean ± SEM from one representative experiment out of three. Data points represent replicate cultures (n = 4 per treatment group). An unpaired 2-tailed t test was used for statistical analysis.

IL-13 inhibits ACE2-dependent entry of SARS-CoV-2 S protein-pseudotyped lentiviral particles into epithelial cells

S protein binding to ACE2 on the epithelial membrane promotes SARS-CoV-2 entry into target cells. To assess the effect of IL-13 on the entry process, we relied on replication-deficient, SARS-CoV-2 S protein-pseudotyped lentiviral particles carrying a GFP reporter gene. Vero E6 cells treated with or without IL-13 (10 ng/ml) for 48 h were transduced with the pseudotyped lentivirus; viral transduction efficiency was quantified by GFP fluorescence. S protein-pseudotyped lentiviral particles readily transduced untreated Vero E6 cells (Fig. 5A), but the number of GFP-positive cells drastically decreased in IL-13-treated cultures (Fig. 5B). Pixel-level quantification of GFP fluorescence demonstrated that the IL-13-induced inhibition of S protein-mediated viral transduction efficiency was highly significant (P = 0.0007, Fig. 5C). Importantly, the effects of IL-13 were ACE2- and SARS-CoV-2-specific because IL-13 treatment did not affect transduction of Vero E6 cells with lentiviral particles pseudotyped with a VSV-G protein that rely on the low-density lipoprotein receptor for cell entry (Fig. 5D–F).

Results represent the mean ± SEM of 10 random frames from 1 out of 2 representative experiments. A Wilcoxon 2-sample test was used for statistical analysis.

To further explore the mechanisms underlying these findings, we also tested the effect of IL-13 on the entry of SARS-CoV-2 S protein-pseudotyped lentiviral particles into HEK293T/ACE2 cells that express recombinant human ACE2 under the control of a heterologous CMV promoter. IL-13 stimulation did not inhibit ACE2 surface protein expression on these cells (Fig. 6A–C). Consistent with this finding, cell transduction by SARS-CoV-2 S protein-pseudotyped lentivirus was comparable in the presence and absence of IL-13 (Fig. 6D–F). Transduction of HEK293T/ACE2 cells with VSV-G-pseudotyped lentivirus was also unaffected by IL-13 (Fig. 6G–I). Taken together, these results indicate that IL-13 blocks SARS-CoV-2 cell entry into Vero E6 cells by inhibiting ACE2 expression, and this inhibition is mediated through endogenous ACE2 promoter.

Discussion

Several studies have reported reduced risk and/or severity of SARS-CoV-2 infection in patients with allergy and/or allergic asthma.27–31 We and others12,32 previously found that airway epithelial cells from subjects with allergy and asthma express decreased levels of the SARS-CoV-2 receptor, ACE2 compared to healthy controls. Furthermore, it has been shown that IL-13, a prototypic type-2 cytokine, inhibits ACE2 transcription;12,32,33 and SARS-CoV-2 infection in human bronchial epithelial cells.13 However, it is unclear to what extent the effects of IL-13 on individual steps of SARS-CoV-2 infection depend on the ability of this cytokine to inhibit ACE2 expression.

To address this gap in knowledge, we tested how IL-13 impacts ACE2-dependent S protein-mediated cell attachment and entry using epithelial cells derived from organs (lung, kidney) naturally targeted by this virus.6–8 Our data show that as a result of IL-13-induced downregulation of ACE2 transcription, SARS-CoV-2 binding to epithelial cells, SARS-CoV-2 entry into these cells, and infection of these cells with live SARS-CoV-2 are all strongly suppressed.

Several molecular mechanisms may underlie these effects of IL-13. Binding sites for STAT transcription factors, the pre-eminent molecular mediators of IL-13 signaling, have been identified in the ACE2 promoter and shown to bind STAT3 in human airway epithelial cells.34 STAT3, in turn, is activated during IL-13-dependent inflammation in asthma.35 Therefore, in combination with other tissue-derived cues, IL-13 might have direct, STAT(3)-mediated regulatory effects on ACE2 expression in airway epithelium.

Viral infections and interferons have been shown to induce the transcription of deltaACE2 (dACE2), an alternative truncated form of ACE2.36 While we did not specifically investigate the effects of IL-13 on the expression of this truncated ACE2 transcript, two lines of evidence indicate that IL-13 targets full-length rather than truncated ACE2 transcripts in our model. Indeed, the effects of IL-13 on the surface expression of ACE2 in general, and S1 protein binding in particular, were unlikely to be mediated by dACE2 because this truncated transcript lacks the SARS-CoV-2 binding domain and is poorly, if at all, expressed at the protein level. Moreover, IL-13 strongly inhibited ACE2 transcription in Vero cells that do not express dACE2.36

Mucus hypersecretion leading to inhibition of viral replication has also been invoked as a mechanism potentially explaining the protective effects of type-2 immunity in COVID-19.13,37 While we cannot generally exclude that IL-13-induced mucus hypersecretion may affect SARS-CoV-2 susceptibility, our data in Vero E6 cells, which do not upregulate Muc5B and Muc5AC expression in response to IL-13, suggest that inhibition of ACE2 transcription by this type-2 cytokine may be sufficient to decrease susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection in patients with type-2 allergic inflammation.

Our work has several limitations. Most of our studies were conducted in vitro and thus cannot show how strong and long-lasting the effects of IL-13 on ACE2 expression should be to protect against COVID-19 in vivo. Furthermore, besides reducing ACE2 expression, allergic inflammation in vivo is likely to affect SARS-CoV-2 susceptibility and severity in ways our model cannot detect. Finally, our live SARS-CoV-2 infection studies did not include primary epithelial cells and therefore could not explore the impact of IL-13 on epithelial differentiation. At the molecular level, the transcriptional regulators involved in IL-13-dependent ACE2 downregulation also remain to be identified. This issue is especially intriguing because our previous studies18 showed that ACE2 transcription in, and SARS-CoV-2 infection of, airway epithelial cells are inhibited by a bacterial lysate, OM-85, that strongly inhibits type-2 inflammation and IL-13 expression in vivo acting through innate immune signaling.38 Thus, ACE2 transcription and the events downstream of this process appear to be regulated by distinct but functionally convergent mechanisms. An additional note of caution comes from a recent study of airway epithelial cells that revealed complex relationships among SARS-CoV-2 replication, ACE2 expression levels, IL-13 responsiveness, and functional pathways of ciliated epithelium differentiation in the setting of allergic asthma. In this study, lower SARS-CoV-2 transcript levels following in vitro infection were primarily associated with a downregulation of ciliated epithelial differentiation programs.14 While these lingering questions are important and worth pursuing, our results indicate for the first time that IL-13 protects epithelial cells from SARS-CoV-2 binding, entry and infection by targeting the endogenous ACE2 promoter and inhibiting ACE2 transcription.
