An acquired case of plaque-like CD34-positive dermal fibroma
Authors: Colleen Glennon, Marla Rodriguez Vazquez, Christian Cerda-Smith, Christopher Stamey, Maria Angelica Selim, Amber Fresco, Daniela Kroshinsky
Published: 2026

Introduction

A plaque-like cluster of differentiation 34 (CD34)-positive dermal fibroma (CD34+DF), also known as a medallion-like dermal dendrocyte hamartoma, is a rare, benign spindle cell neoplasm that can be congenital or acquired.1 A broad differential diagnosis is warranted, as these tumors can mimic several other lesions, such as dermatofibromas, dermatomyofibromas, diffuse neurofibromas, congenital nevi, fibroblastic connective tissue nevi, anetoderma, or aplasia cutis.1,2 Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP), a rare soft tissue sarcoma with locally aggressive potential, is a particularly important alternative condition to consider.3 This has led to increased interest in characterizing CD34+DF, as accurate and early identification is imperative to determine most appropriate management.

In 2004, Rodríguez-Jurado et al initially described CD34+DF as an asymptomatic, well-circumscribed, slightly atrophic and superficially wrinkled, round, oval or triangular, and brown to erythematous congenital lesion occurring in isolation on the neck or upper trunk in 3 girls (ages 7, 10, 11).4 Specimens obtained from the 3 patients confirmed dermal spindle cell proliferations positive for CD34 and factor XIIIa. The authors initially referred to the described lesions as medallion-like dermal dendrocyte hamartoma, but further study has revealed that medallion-like dermal dendrocyte hamartoma lack dermal dendrocytic lineage and are not hamartomas, prompting more recent literature to use the term, CD34+DF.5

Since the initial 2004 case series, several clinically and morphologically unique presentations with similar histopathology have been described. CD34+DF is now thought to represent a heterogenous group, which includes both acquired (occurring in adulthood) and factor XIIIa-negative variants.2 For example, some patients have reported associated symptoms or presented with lesions on the extremities without clinical epidermal change.2 This heterogenous presentation complicates differentiation from DFSP. While CD34+DF tends to follow a benign clinical course warranting conservative management, DFSP requires surgical intervention, underscoring the importance of accurate diagnosis.

In this report, we aim to further enhance understanding of this rare neoplasm by presenting the clinical, histopathologic, and immunohistochemical features of an acquired case.

Case Report

A healthy 32-year-old female presented with a 4 mm × 5 mm, triangular, thin, and indurated flesh-colored papule of 2 y duration to the right anterior shoulder (Fig 1). The patient described the initial lesion as raised, erythematous, and pruritic, but reported that it became asymptomatic and flatter over time. There was no associated tenderness, bleeding, or prior history of trauma to the affected area. A previous trial of class I topical corticosteroids was not helpful. The patient’s dermatologic history included mild acne and benign nevi, with no reported past skin cancer. A 3.0 mm punch biopsy was performed, and the specimen was sent for histopathologic examination.

Lesion at initial presentation. 4 mm × 5 mm, triangular, thin, and indurated flesh-colored papule on right anterior shoulder.

Histologic evaluation of the lesion showed a band-like proliferation of spindle cells within the reticular dermis without extension to the subcutis. Cytologically, the fusiform dermal cells display small nuclei, with small nucleoli and no mitotic activity (Figs 2, A and B). Immunohistochemical studies were subsequently performed, which revealed lesional cells positive for CD34 and negative for smooth muscle actin and factor XIIIa (Fig 3). Verhoeff-Van Gieson staining showed retention of elastic fibers within the stroma. Overall, the lesion showed features of a CD34+DF, which is histologically indistinguishable from a medallion-like dermal dendrocyte hamartoma.

A, Histologic examination of the lesion reveals reticular dermal proliferation of spindle cells with papillary dermis spared (×10). B, Spindle cells have small nuclei without hyperchromasia or pleomorphism (×20).

The vast majority of spindle cells are decorated with CD34 (×20).

Monitoring versus excision were presented as reasonable treatment options, after which the patient chose to proceed with removal. Following an elliptical excision with 3 mm margins, the specimen was again sent for histopathologic analysis, which was consistent with the pre-surgical diagnosis of CD34+DF but noted that the lesion extended to the surgical margins. Given the positive margins, additional treatment options were discussed. This included monitoring, as CD34+DF is a benign lesion, versus Mohs surgery for complete removal versus checking the specimen for collagen type I alpha 1 combined with platelet-derived growth factor beta (COL1A1-PDGFB) gene fusion to rule out dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, the most important differential diagnosis. After discussion, the patient opted to proceed with Mohs, which prevented additional testing.

Discussion

This case provides an additional example of an acquired, factor XIIIa-negative variant of CD34+DF. Although unlikely, it is impossible to retrospectively rule out DFSP, as molecular testing was not pursued. Clinically, DFSP presents similarly to our patient, with an asymptomatic skin-colored plaque. However, DFSP slowly enlarges over time and eventually becomes nodular, which often results in delayed diagnosis.6 Our patient’s initial report of the lesion included symptoms (pruritus) and she denied associated growth over time, further supporting diagnosis being most consistent with benign CD34+DF.

Histopathologically, both CD34+DF and DFSP are characterized by CD34-positive spindle cell proliferations that may extend into the subcutaneous fat.2 In contrast to DFSP, the proliferation of CD34-positive spindle cells in CD34+DF is band-like and mixed with mast cells, has rare mitotic activity, and spares adnexal structures, vessels and nerves.2,7 Immunohistochemistry is only helpful in cases of CD34+DF that are factor XIIIa-positive, as DFSP is factor XIIIa-negative. Molecular techniques are therefore indicated in borderline cases to identify or exclude features of DFSP. This includes fluorescent in situ hybridization assays to detect the t(17;22) (q22;q13) translocation or reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction to identify the COL1A1-PDGFB fusion gene that results from the above translocation.1,8 This genetic mutation has been demonstrated in 90% of DFSP tumors.6

In conclusion, we report a case of acquired CD34+DF that was treated with Mohs surgery using shared decision making with our patient. However, testing for the above fusion gene or translocation may have provided the patient with additional confidence that the lesion was benign, potentially preventing further surgical intervention.
