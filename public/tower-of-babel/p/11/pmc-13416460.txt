The partner gene modulates response to asciminib in ABL1-rearranged Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia
Authors: Laura N Eadie, Daniel P McDougal, Elias Lagonik, Caitlin E Schutz, Elyse C Page, Tace S Conlin, Jacqueline Rehn, John B Bruning, Andrew S Moore, David T Yeung, Timothy P Hughes, Deborah L White
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•NUP214::ABL1 acute lymphoblastic leukemia models demonstrate asciminib sensitivity.•Models lacking complete ABL1 SH3 domain (ABL1 exon 2 deleted) retain sensitivity.•In silico modeling predicts NUP214 exon 32 mimics the missing ABL1 exon 2.•A chimeric SH3 domain is predicted, maintaining asciminib sensitivity.Keywords: STAMP inhibitors, Acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Precision medicine, Pre-clinical studies, Structural modeling

Introduction

Survival rates for children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) now approach 90% in developed countries, however, children with high-risk disease, neonates, adolescents and young adults and older adults still have poor outcomes and increased risk of relapse [1]. For those patients who do relapse, long term survival rates are dismal, particularly in the T-cell ALL (T-ALL) setting [2]. A large subset of B-cell ALL (B-ALL) patients have a gene expression signature similar to that of BCR::ABL1+ leukemia, harboring gene fusions involving kinase and cytokine receptor genes [3]. This BCR::ABL1-like subtype was formally recognized as a high-risk disease entity in the 2022 World Health Organisation’s classification of acute leukemias [4].

Up to 20% of BCR::ABL1-like ALL encompasses ABL-class fusions which frequently involve fusion of the ABL1 gene to various partners including NUP214, ETV6, RANBP2, RCSD1, SFPQ, ZMIZ1, SNX2 and others (collectively ∼30%) [5,6]. The NUP214::ABL1 fusion is reported in ∼3% of T-ALL cases [7,8]. Patients with ABL-rearranged (ABLr) disease experience very poor early response, event free and overall survival [9]. The majority of ABL1 gene fusions result from either an ABL1 exon 2 (ETV6, RANBP2, ZMIZ1) or exon 4 (RCSD1, SFPQ, SNX2) breakpoint. However, the NUP214::ABL1 fusion has been reported with breakpoints in ABL1 exon 2 [8,10], exon 3 [6,8,11] and more rarely, in exons 1 and 4 [8]. The NUP214::ABL1 gene fusion is a leukemic driver resulting in aggressive disease with poor outcomes in both the B- and T-ALL settings [8,9,12].

ATP-competitive tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as dasatinib, used as front-line treatment in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), are effective against BCR::ABL1-like fusions involving ABL1 or ABL2 in laboratory models [6,[13], [14], [15]]. Recent studies suggest concomitant TKIs during frontline [[16], [17], [18]] and consolidation chemotherapy [9,[16], [17], [18]] elicit better early responses potentially leading to superior long-term outcomes. Indeed, clinical trials evaluating chemotherapy and TKI combination protocols in ALL patients (NCT06124157, ISRCTN #64515327) demonstrated improved long-term outcomes compared with patients not receiving TKI [18,19]. Inclusion of TKIs in treatment regimens for ALL patients with the BCR::ABL1 fusion has reduced the need for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in a proportion of this cohort and the associated high treatment related mortality [[20], [21], [22]]. However, there are cases where development of ATP-pocket point mutations, that prevent effective TKI binding, has led to TKI-resistance following an initial successful response [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Additionally, dasatinib and ponatinib are associated with significant side effects and off target interactions and new treatment strategies are required to overcome TKI failure and avoid potential long-term effects.

Asciminib is a first-in-class specifically targeting the ABL myristoyl pocket (STAMP) compound developed to overcome resistance to ATP-competitive TKIs [27]. Native ABL is auto-regulated by a myristate moiety that binds within the C-lobe of the ABL kinase domain [28,29]. However, fusion of ABL1 to BCR or other 5′ partner genes truncates the myristate preventing autoinhibition and resulting in the constitutively active kinase and downstream signaling observed in ABLr leukemia patients [29]. Asciminib mimics the myristate and once bound, the ABL SH3 and SH2 domains bind to and stabilize the kinase domain, locking ABL in the inactive conformation. Asciminib is now a front-line therapy for newly diagnosed adults with CML and those previously treated with TKIs [30,31]. Early results from a recently concluded trial assessing TKI-asciminib combination therapy in BCR::ABL1+ ALL patients (NCT02081378) demonstrate durable molecular responses [32]. Importantly, asciminib has demonstrated superior efficacy in de novo CML patients compared with TKIs and has an excellent safety and tolerability profile [31].

Initial in vitro studies examining potential mechanisms of asciminib resistance reported the importance of the ABCG2 efflux transporter and myristoyl pocket point mutations [33,34]. More recent investigations evaluated the allosteric action of asciminib and the role of the ABL SH3 and SH2 domains for asciminib activity in in vitro models of CML [35,36]. We have reported the efficacy of asciminib in ABLr ALL cell lines and highlighted that asciminib efficacy was dependent on inclusion of a critical region of ABL SH3 domain [37,38]. Others subsequently demonstrated that the exon 2-encoded part of the SH3 domain is essential for asciminib efficacy against BCR::ABL1 in the CML setting [35,36].

In this current study, we evaluate asciminib efficacy in three pre-clinical patient derived xenograft (PDX) models of ABLr B- and T-ALL reporting sensitivity of NUP214::ABL1 disease. We also use cell lines engineered to express different regions of the ABL1 SH3 and SH2 domains to investigate the key region required for asciminib efficacy using NUP214::ABL1 as the model gene fusion. Results demonstrate varying asciminib sensitivity in cell death assays following deletion of specific regions of the SH3 domain. We then use AlphaFold3 [39] modeling to predict structural outcomes following deletion of different regions of the SH3/SH2 domains. Our findings indicate the ABL1 fusion partner plays an important role in modulating response to asciminib and better characterize which ABLr ALL patients may benefit from the addition of asciminib to treatment regimens.

Methods

ALL patient samples

We studied leukemia samples from biobanked material from three cases with NUP214::ABL1 disease (B-ALL diagnosis, n = 2 (e32a3, e34a3); T-ALL relapse, n = 1 (e31a2). Patient details are provided in Table S1 and Fig. S1. Transcriptomic sequencing data are deposited at the European Genome Phenome archive, accession EGAD50000001395. Patients provided written informed consent for sample collection and storage for future ethically approved research, including the studies presented here. All research was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Development of ALL patient derived xenografts (PDX) and in vivo drug treatments

Six to twelve week old NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME) were treated subcutaneously with 0.1 mg enrofloxacin in 0.9% sodium chloride per 10 g bodyweight prior to sub-lethal gamma irradiation at 200 cGy. Spleen- or bone marrow (BM)-derived patient blasts isolated from primary xenografts (1.5-1.8×106 cells) were injected into the tail vein of secondary recipients. Leukemic engraftment and disease progression was monitored by weekly blood sampling and flow cytometric analysis of human (h) CD45+ cells and hCD19+ (B-ALL PDXs) or hCD7+ (T-ALL PDX). Treatment commenced once hCD45+ levels reached a median of 5%. Mice were randomized into treatment groups (n = 5) as indicated: A) vehicle control via oral gavage (for the T-ALL PDX: citric acid; for the B-ALL PDXs: PEG300, Solutol HS15, NaOH, HCl, sodium acetate solution) B) 20 mg/kg/day once daily dasatinib via oral gavage (T-ALL PDX only) C) 30 mg/kg once daily asciminib via oral gavage. Treatment was for 28 days and mice were maintained on 0.3 mg/mL enrofloxacin supplemented water for the duration of the experiment. Animals were monitored daily and were humanely killed when displaying either clinical signs of leukemia (eg: weight loss, reduced movement, ruffled fur) or when blood levels of hCD45+ cells were >50%. At experimental end, cardiac bleed and complete blood count (CBC) where possible were performed, and spleen, liver, thymus (where evident) and BM were harvested. Flow cytometric immunophenotyping was performed on peripheral blood and/or BM and peripheral organs, and formalin fixed organ sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or hCD45 by the University of Adelaide Medical School Histology Service and scanned by the SAHMRI Light Microscopy and Laser Capture Core Facility. Slides were visualized with CaseViewer software (v2.2, 3DHISTECHTM Ltd). All experiments were performed on protocols approved by the SAHMRI Animal Ethics Committee.

Site directed mutagenesis of NUP214::ABL1

The NUP214::ABL1 fusion gene was amplified from the cDNA of B-ALL Patient e32a3 with a NUP214::ABL1 (e32a3) fusion and subcloned into a Gateway pDONR-221 vector (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) using the Gateway BP Clonase II enzyme mix (Invitrogen) as described previously [37]. We designed four separate retroviral constructs, each with regions of ∼25 amino acids in ABL1 exon 3 deleted by site directed mutagenesis, and designated these constructs Δ1-4. We used the HAC cloning method described previously [38] to create the NUP214::ABL1 e31a3 and e32a2 isoforms. Full length NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 served as control. The rationale for deleting these regions is provided in Supplementary Note 1.

Structural modeling and molecular docking

Structures were predicted for full length NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 and each Δ1-4 isoform, as well as wildtype ABL1 using AlphaFold3 [39]. To model the binding modes of asciminib and nilotinib, both compounds were docked to each structure in ICM-Pro (Molsoft L.L.C, San Diego, CA) and rigorously energy minimized; each complex was then refined. The models in the main text were selected based on similarity of each domain to the crystal structure of ABL1 (5MO4) and clear residue interactions. An alignment of each of the five models output from AlphaFold3 (coloured by pLDDT score) are provided in Figs S2-3. Structural analysis and visualization was performed in PyMOL (Schrodinger, INC) with detailed interpretation of results provided in Supplementary Note 2. Primary amino acid sequences of ABL1 and NUP214 were obtained from UniProt under the accession numbers PP00519 (ABL1) and P35658 (NUP214).

Methods for additional experiments are available as Supplementary.

Results

Asciminib treatment significantly increases survival outcomes and reduces leukemic blast infiltration in PDX models of ABLr ALL

Following our discovery of asciminib activity against NUP214::ABL1 ALL in vitro [37], we investigated asciminib efficacy in PDX models of disease. We established three PDX models from patients with different NUP214::ABL1 fusions (Fig. S1; Table S1). Mice were divided into two cohorts: mice in cohort 1 were humanely euthanized immediately following completion of asciminib treatment and those in cohort 2 upon clinical signs of illness or when PB levels of hCD45+ reached 50%. Cohort 2 was termed post-treatment cessation (PTC) and was assessed for alterations in survival and peripheral organ infiltration.

Vehicle control mice developed an aggressive leukemia while asciminib treatment significantly improved survival in all models (asciminib vs vehicle: B-ALL Patient e32a3=103 vs 76 d, p = 0.0067; B-ALL Patient e34a3=110 vs 74 d, p = 0.0067; T-ALL Patient e31a2=84 vs 46 d, p = 0.0100. Fig. 1A; Fig. S4A-B). As previously reported [5,6], dasatinib-treated mice also demonstrated improved survival compared with control and the survival duration was superior to that of asciminib-treated mice (dasatinib vs asciminib : 93 vs 84 d, p = 0.0101; Fig. S4B). Spleen and liver weights of NUP214::ABL1 B-ALL e32a3 mice culled while receiving asciminib were significantly reduced compared with vehicle-treated mice (243 vs 585 mg, p = 0.0109 and 1089 vs 1666, p = 0.0357, respectively; Fig. 1B-C), although this may also be attributable to organ harvest occurring at an earlier timepoint. Analogous observations were made for the other two PDX models (Fig. S4C-F).

Importantly, flow cytometric analyses and histology confirmed decreased disease burden in the bone marrow and peripheral organs of mice culled while receiving asciminib compared with vehicle-treated controls (Figs S5-8, Supplementary Note 3). Additionally, asciminib treatment reduced levels of circulating leukemic blasts in all PDX models compared with vehicle treated cohorts (Supplementary Note 3). Taken together, these results demonstrate the potential of asciminib as a therapy for NUP214::ABL1 ALL patients.

Deletion of specific regions of ABL1 exon 3 confers varied response to asciminib in cells expressing NUP214::ABL1

Previous studies report the importance of the ABL1 exon 2 encoded portion of the SH3 domain for protein folding and asciminib efficacy against BCR::ABL1 [35,36]. However, we now demonstrate in vivo efficacy of asciminib against NUP214::ABL1 disease lacking ABL1 exon 2 (both B-ALL patients harbor ABL1 exon 3 breakpoints). To investigate this further, Ba/F3 cells expressing NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 full length and Δ1-4 isoforms (Fig. 2; Supplementary Note 1) were assessed for ability to grow in the absence of IL-3. All cell lines were readily transformed to cytokine independence and retained kinase activity (Fig. 3A). We then investigated kinase inhibition in the presence of asciminib versus dasatinib, and efficacy of both inhibitors in viability assays. As expected, complete inhibition of STAT5 activity was observed in the presence of dasatinib (86-93% decrease depending on the Δisoform). Asciminib also inhibited kinase activity in cells expressing all four Δisoforms, but notably, the decrease in STAT5 activity for NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 Δ1 cells (49%) was significantly less than that observed for the other isoforms (Δ2=59%, p = 0.0721; Δ3=66%, p = 0.0342; Δ4=72%, p = 0.0017; Fig. 3B). Additionally, differing viabilities were observed upon treatment with asciminib. Ba/F3 cells expressing full length NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 (LD50=158 nM) and NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 Δ2-4 (LD50=57, 29, 51 nM, respectively) demonstrated asciminib sensitivity within the clinically achievable range [30]. Interestingly, NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 Δ1 cells were resistant to asciminib: LD50=18.3 μM, p < 0.0001 compared with full length NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 cells (Fig. 3D). Dasatinib-mediated cell death was within the clinically achievable range [40] for all cell lines (LD50≤4 nM) with no significant difference observed for any Δisoform compared with full length NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 (p = 0.4, Fig. 3E). Importantly, Sanger sequencing of the entire NUP214::ABL1 fusion gene confirmed the absence of additional ABL1 mutations in all cell lines (data not shown).

Structural modeling of NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 Δ1-4 isoform quaternary architecture

We next investigated the potential effect of the NUP214 exon 32 fusion on ABL1 structure using modeling to derive a hypothesis for our in vitro findings. We generated structural models with AlphaFold3 for wildtype ABL1, NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 full length and Δ1-4 isoforms. The predicted structures showed conservation of the kinase domain fold, and both asciminib and nilotinib were successfully modeled into their respective binding sites (Fig. 4A,C, Supplementary Figs S2-3). Modeling supported binding of nilotinib and asciminib to NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 full length, as well as the Δ1-4 isoforms, indicating alterations to the SH2 and SH3 domains are unlikely to significantly affect the structure of the inactive kinase domain

Our in vitro data demonstrate NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 cells remain sensitive to asciminib, which would require a functional SH3 domain to maintain allosteric inhibition [35,36,38]. Interestingly, the structural modeling indicates NUP214 exon 32 may complete the RT-loop structure, which could rescue SH3 domain function and preserve interactions with the kinase domain (Fig. 4B, Fig. S9). Indeed, NUP214 exon 32 harbors sequence similarity with ABL1, which could compensate for the loss of ABL1 exon 2 and maintain interactions with the SH2-kinase linker and N-lobe during protein folding. However, BCR::ABL1 e13a3 and e14a3 isoforms, which are asciminib resistant due to perturbation of the SH3 domain fold [35,36], show a comparative absence of sequence homology (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, when we compared the predicted folding of Δ1-4 isoforms when present in wildtype ABL1 versus NUP214::ABL1, we found that Δ2-4 isoforms retained the SH3 fold when ABL1 exon 2 was present (as is the case in wildtype ABL1; Fig. S10). However, in the absence of ABL1 exon 2 (as is the case in the NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 fusion studied here), only Δ3 and Δ4 isoforms maintained SH3 folding (Fig. 4C). This distinction highlights the importance of ABL1 exon 2 in maintaining protein folding and the compensatory role of the 5′ fusion partner (NUP214 exon 32).

To evaluate the importance of the chimeric SH3 domain (incomplete ABL1 SH3 domain compensated by NUP214 exon 32) for asciminib sensitivity, we generated Ba/F3 cell lines expressing NUP214::ABL1 with exon 32 deleted (NUP214::ABL1 e31a3). We also generated Ba/F3 NUP214::ABL1 e32a2 cells to directly compare wildtype versus chimeric SH3 domains. Both cell lines were transformed to cytokine independence and retained kinase activity as measured by pSTAT5 levels (Fig. 3A). As observed with the Δisoform cell lines, kinase activity was inhibitable with both asciminib and dasatinib, although asciminib was less effective at reducing pSTAT5 levels in NUP214::ABL1 e31a3 cells compared with NUP214::ABL1 e32a2 cells (49% versus 99% decrease, respectively; Fig. 3C). However, when we investigated cell viability following drug treatment, we found NUP214::ABL1 e31a3 cells were completely resistant to asciminib (LD50=20 μM) while retaining sensitivity to dasatinib (LD50=0.7 nM). As expected, NUP214::ABL1 e32a2 cells were sensitive to both asciminib (LD50=5 nM) and dasatinib (LD50=0.5 nM; Fig. 3D-E). Combined, these data provide evidence for our hypothesis that NUP214 exon 32 is required for asciminib sensitivity in the NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 setting.

SH3 domain-mediated stabilization of the αC-helix is essential for asciminib efficacy

Having established the importance of a functional SH3 domain (either wildtype or chimeric) for asciminib sensitivity, we investigated potential structural hypotheses for the differences in asciminib-mediated cell death between Δisoform cell lines. In wildtype ABL1, the SH3 domain interacts with the SH2-kinase linker and the kinase domain N-lobe, which are important for maintaining the auto-inhibited inactive conformation [28,29]. In the asciminib-resistant NUP214::ABL1 Δ1 isoform, a section of the SH3 domain is deleted which abolishes a salt bridge between residues p.E117 (SH3 domain) and p.K313 (C-terminal end of the αC-helix; Fig. 4D). SH3-mediated stabilization of the αC-helix is essential for auto-regulation and allosteric asciminib-dependent inhibition of kinase activity [41]. The p.K313E mutation confers asciminib resistance that is independent of effective asciminib binding [27]. Maintenance of this salt bridge is predicted in NUP214::ABL1 Δ3-4 isoforms by proper folding of the SH3 domain, but not in the NUP214::ABL1 Δ2 isoform. This should render NUP214::ABL1 Δ2 cells resistant to asciminib, however, this is not the case (LD50=60 nM; Fig. 3D). Instead, in the Δ2 isoform, the SH3 β-sheet rests anti-parallel to the SH2 β-sheet and the SH2-kinase linker (Fig. 4C-D). We hypothesize that this conformation likely maintains stabilization of the αC-helix through stable docking against the SH2-kinase linker. Taken together, our findings show complete deletion of the SH3 domain is not required for asciminib resistance, but rather only a small section that is essential for asciminib-mediated inhibition.

Discussion

The STAMP compound asciminib can overcome resistance in BCR::ABL1+ patients previously treated with TKIs [30] or can be used in combination with TKIs to restore treatment efficacy [27,42]. We have reported varying efficacy of asciminib against different ABL1 and ABL2 fusions identified in ABLr ALL [37]. Here, we explored potential reasons for the varied efficacy using in vitro and in silico structural modeling. We also established three pre-clinical patient derived xenografts and confirmed in vivo asciminib efficacy against NUP214::ABL1 disease in both T- and B-ALL settings.

Dasatinib is the current adjunct TKI therapy for BCR::ABL1+ ALL and ABLr ALL [18,21]. In this study, asciminib-treated mice experienced similar survival to mice treated with dasatinib, although complete disease eradication was not observed in either cohort. This was not unexpected, given that, unlike CML, neither drug would be used as a monotherapy in the high-risk ALL setting. Additionally, the treatment window was relatively short. In all three PDX models, asciminib decreased splenomegaly, eradicated ALL blasts from the BM and liver, and significantly extended survival following treatment cessation. Our data provide a rationale for expansion of future asciminib clinical trials to include patients with the NUP214::ABL1 fusion.

There are 21 ABL1 gene fusions reported in ALL [8,43], 19 have documented ABL1 breakpoints in exon 2 (n = 12), exon 3 (n = 2) or exon 4 (n = 8), plus 6 additional fusions where ABL1 is the 5′ gene resulting in kinase domain truncation. We discovered that ABL1 fusions with exon 4 breakpoints are resistant to asciminib [37], but there is conjecture surrounding asciminib efficacy against ABL1 fusions with exon 3 breakpoints [[35], [36], [37]]. Thus, we generated four different NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 isoforms with large deletions within exon 3 to determine critical regions of the ABL1 SH3 and SH2 domains necessary for asciminib sensitivity (see Supplementary Note 1). Using Ba/F3 models, we demonstrated each isoform retained the ability to induce cytokine independent growth and constitutive kinase signalling activation (increased pSTAT5 levels). All isoforms also demonstrated reduced pSTAT5 levels in the presence of asciminib, although the reduction was not as significant as that observed for dasatinib. This is likely due to the fact that dasatinib is an ABL/SRC inhibitor preventing both ABL and SRC kinase-mediated STAT5 activation. Conversely, asciminib specifically targets the ABL kinase only. Thus, in the presence of dasatinib, STAT5 activation is inhibited at multiple nodes resulting in the superior inhibition observed. The observation that asciminib sensitivity was retained by an ABL1 fusion with an a3 breakpoint is intriguing and in contrast to recent studies in the CML setting [35,36]. These previous studies examined asciminib efficacy against isoforms of BCR::ABL1 with exon 3 breakpoints (e13a3, e14a3) reporting constitutive STAT5 activation that was not reduced following asciminib treatment. In these studies, authors postulated ABL1 exon 2 residues are essential for proper protein folding with subsequent impacts on asciminib mode-of-action when BCR is the fusion partner. Conversely, our results demonstrate that asciminib can bind to NUP214::ABL1 fusions in the absence of the exon 2 residues and mediate kinase inhibition. However, asciminib is unable to induce apoptosis in cells lacking the initial part of ABL1 exon 3 encoding a key region of the SH3 domain (NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 Δ1 cells). The SH3-SH2-kinase domain interface is critical for auto-inhibition of ABL1 [28,29,44]. Our results suggest the Δ1 isoform negatively impacts binding of the remaining SH3 domain to the SH2-kinase linker region (Fig. 4D), which destabilizes the inactive conformation, preventing asciminib-mediated cell death. Indeed, comparing all Δisoform cells, the smallest percentage decrease in pSTAT5 was observed in the NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 Δ1 cells (Fig. 3B). Conversely, we hypothesize the other Δisoforms, which delete regions of the SH2 domain, allow better binding of the SH3 domain to the SH2-kinase linker facilitating adoption of a more stable inactive conformation. This is supported by increased asciminib sensitivity in Δ2-4 cells compared with full length NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 cells and better inhibition of kinase signalling.

Structural comparison of the NUP214::ABL1 e32a3 model used in our in vitro experiments to wildtype ABL1 indicates predicted homology of the SH3 domain fold. Sequence homology of this region was not observed in asciminib-resistant BCR::ABL1 isoforms lacking ABL1 exon 2 and provides a putative basis for the discrepancy between our study and others investigating BCR::ABL1 fusions [35,36]. Additionally, a recent study demonstrated ETV6::ABL1 cells harboring a complete SH3 domain (e4a2 breakpoint) were resistant to asciminib [45]. Thus, determination of asciminib sensitivity in ALL is not as simple as evaluating the inclusion/exclusion of ABL1 exon 2. Instead, the fusion partner appears to play a key role in asciminib sensitivity which has significant clinical impact.

Our modeling predicted the SH3 domain was not preserved for the Δ2 isoform (Fig. 4C), highlighting a region of ABL1 exon 3 that is likely important for mediating asciminib sensitivity (see Supplementary Note 2). The structural models of asciminib-sensitive NUP214::ABL1 Δ3-4 isoforms predicted interaction between the partial SH3 domain (p.E117) and the C-terminus of the kinase domain αC-helix (p.K313) via a salt bridge, while the Δ2 isoform allosterically anchors the αC-helix via the SH2-kinase linker. Allosteric modulation of the αC-helix has previously been demonstrated as critical for activation and regulation of kinases, including ABL1 [46,47]. Previous mutation of the residues identified here (abolishing salt bridge formation) resulted in constitutive activation of the ABL1 kinase due to disruption of SH3-mediated stabilization of the αC-helix [41] and asciminib resistance [27,48]. While AlphaFold predictions can be unreliable for proteins that undergo conformational changes [49,50], the prediction of salt bridge abolition in the Δ1 isoform supports our experimental observations of asciminib resistance.

Asciminib sensitivity is retained in Δ2-4 cells despite deletions within the SH2 domain. This contrasts with observations in the BCR::ABL1 setting where resistance occurs upon total deletion of the SH2 domain [36]. Our structural models predict that the modified SH2 domain retains proximity to the myristoyl binding pocket and maintains stabilizing interactions with the SH2-kinase linker, potentially through salt bridge formation (observed for Δ3-4 isoforms), de novo interactions with NUP214 exon 32 (observed for Δ2 isoform) or other molecular interactions occurring between the SH3 domain, SH2-kinase linker and the N-lobe. Our findings reveal a potential adaptability in allosteric regulation of ABL1 kinase activity by asciminib which may have important considerations for allosteric resistance mechanisms as well as future therapeutic targets and drug design.

Conclusion

The frontline use of TKIs in patients with ABL class ALL can lead to deeper responses and improved long term survival [[16], [17], [18]]. Early results assessing asciminib efficacy in patients with BCR::ABL1+ ALL are promising. Given asciminib's superior safety profile and minimal off-target effects compared with dasatinib and ponatinib, there is significant clinical interest to extend asciminib therapy to other relevant B-ALL and T-ALL subtypes. Our pre-clinical models establish asciminib as an attractive, low-toxicity option for TKI-refractory, -resistant or -intolerant patients with NUP214::ABL1 disease. Our structural models predict the breakpoint exon of NUP214 (e32) mimics the folding of the missing ABL1 region to preserve SH3 domain folding and function – this is a previously unreported phenomenon and emphasizes the potential impact of fusion partners on asciminib response. We also identify a key region of the ABL1 SH3 domain critical for asciminib activity against NUP214::ABL1. These data contribute to our understanding of the mode of action of asciminib and strongly support clinical trials evaluating asciminib as an adjunct therapy for NUP214::ABL1 patients.
