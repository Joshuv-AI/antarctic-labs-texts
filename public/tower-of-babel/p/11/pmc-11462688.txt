ABLE-SCORE, a simplified risk score for major adverse cardiovascular outcomes in left ventricular hypertrabeculation: a multicenter longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Limin Liu, Rui Zeng, Ligang Ding, Simin Cai, Aiyue Chen, Yuchen Dong, Likun Zhou, Mengtong Xu, Yuanwei Xu, Le Li, Zhenhao Zhang, Zhao Hu, Zhuxin Zhang, Yulong Xiong, Zhicheng Hu, Yongqing Li, Minjie Lu, Lingmin Wu, Lihui Zheng, Xiaohan Fan, Tan Li, Yan Yao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background Left ventricular hypertrabeculation (LVHT) is a heterogeneous entity with life-threatening complications and variable prognosis. However, there are limited prediction models available to identify individuals at high risk of adverse outcomes, and the current risk score in LVHT is comparatively complex for clinical practice. This study aimed to develop and validate a simplified risk score to predict major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in LVHT.MethodsThis multicenter longitudinal cohort study consecutively enrolled morphologically diagnosed LVHT patients between January 2009 and December 2020 at Fuwai Hospital (derivation cohort, n = 300; internal validation cohort, n = 129), and between January 2014 and December 2022 at two national-level medical centers (external validation cohort, n = 95). The derivation/internal validation cohorts and the external validation cohort were followed annually until December 2022 and December 2023, respectively. MACE was defined as a composite of all-cause mortality, heart transplantation/left ventricular assist device implantation, cardiac resynchronization therapy, malignant ventricular arrhythmia, and thromboembolism. A simplified risk score, the ABLE-SCORE, was developed based on independent risk factors in the multivariable Cox regression predictive model for MACE, and underwent both internal and external validations to confirm its discrimination, calibration, and clinical applicability.ResultsA total of 524 LVHT patients (43.5 ± 16.6 years, 65.8% male) were included in the study. The ABLE-SCORE was established using four easily accessible clinical variables: age at diagnosis, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide levels, left atrium enlargement, and left ventricular ejection fraction ≤ 40% measured by echocardiography. The risk score showed excellent performance in discrimination, with Harrell’s C-index of 0.821 [95% confidence interval (CI), 0.772–0.869], 0.786 (95%CI, 0.703–0.869), and 0.750 (95%CI, 0.644–0.856) in the derivation, internal validation, and external validation cohort, respectively. Calibration plots of the three datasets suggested accurate agreement between the predicted and observed 5-year risk of MACE in LVHT. According to decision curve analysis, the ABLE-SCORE displayed greater net benefits than the existing risk score for LVHT, indicating its strength in clinical applicability.ConclusionsA simplified and efficient risk score for MACE was developed and validated using a large LVHT cohort, making it a reliable and convenient tool for the risk stratification and clinical management of patients with LVHT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-024-03666-8.

Background

Left ventricular hypertrabeculation (LVHT) is a poorly understood phenotypic trait of the heart with heterogeneous complications and variable prognosis. It is characterized by excessively prominent trabeculations and deep intertrabecular recesses in the left ventricle, forming a particular bi-layered (thin compacted and thick hypertrabeculated) appearance of the myocardium [1]. Affected individuals may experience diverse clinical outcomes, varying from no symptoms to life-threatening events such as decompensated heart failure (HF), malignant ventricular arrhythmia, systemic thromboembolism, and sudden cardiac death (SCD) [2, 3]. At present, no gold-standard diagnosis has been established for this cardiac abnormality [1, 4, 5]. The current approach to diagnose LVHT mainly relies on morphologic findings by assessing the ratio of hypertrabeculated to compacted myocardium using transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) [6–8] and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) [9–11], without taking into consideration the LV function or relevant clinical parameters. With advancements in imaging modalities, the detection rates of LVHT have shown a significant increase in recent years. Among these cases, some acquired or even transient forms of LVHT due to physiological adaptations to hemodynamic overload have been observed in healthy populations, like athletes and pregnant women [12–16]. As a result, it is crucial for clinicians to differentiate patients at high risk of adverse outcomes from those with benign forms of LVHT.

Although being recognized as a clinical entity for over 30 years, the prognosis of LVHT differs greatly in the reported literature, with estimated mortality rates ranging from 2 to 35% in earlier studies [17–21]. Given the remarkable heterogeneity and variable prognosis, it is essential to early identify high-risk LVHT patients who might experience a poor prognosis through effective risk stratification. Currently, a very limited number of risk prediction models have been established for estimating major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in LVHT [22, 23]. The existing models are comparatively complex for clinical practice, as they depend on the results of genetic testing and CMR scans, which could hinder their widespread adoption, particularly in regions with constrained healthcare resources. Moreover, the current risk prediction models have not taken into account of any basic laboratory parameters or clinically accessible biomarkers that might be associated with the outcome. Thus, there is a significant demand for the construction of more simplified and broadly applicable models to assess the risk of LVHT patients. Therefore, we investigated the long-term follow-up data of morphologically diagnosed LVHT cohorts from three national-level cardiovascular centers in the northeast, southwest and north China, and sought to (i) develop and validate a risk score for individualized MACE prediction using easily accessible clinical variables; and (ii) compare the clinical benefits of our simplified risk score with the pre-existing risk score and provide guidance for clinical surveillance and management in this particular population.

Methods

Study design and population

In this multicenter, observational, retrospective, longitudinal cohort study, consecutive patients who met the imaging diagnostic criteria for LVHT (Jenni criteria via TTE [7] and/or Petersen criteria via CMR [9], with CMR prevailing over TTE in case of discrepancy) between January 1, 2009, and December 31, 2020, at Fuwai Hospital, and between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2022, at the First Hospital of China Medical University and West China Hospital Sichuan University were enrolled. Referring to the previously published high-quality study on model construction, the LVHT cohort at Fuwai hospital was randomly split into the model derivation dataset (n = 300) and internal validation dataset (n = 129) at the ratio of 7:3 [24]. Cohorts from the other two medical centers were used for external validation (n = 95). At baseline, all patients underwent comprehensive medical history and cardiovascular evaluations, including circulating biomarkers, 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), 24-h Holter monitoring, TTE, and CMR. Time of inclusion was the date of LVHT diagnosis. Participants were followed annually by telephone contacts and/or outpatient visits. The final follow-up took place in December 31, 2022, and December 31, 2023, at Fuwai Hospital and the other two medical centers, respectively. Individuals with missing data of essential laboratory parameters or unavailable information on the occurrence and date of clinical outcomes were excluded from the analysis. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles, followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines [25], and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital (Ethics approval number: 2022–1837). Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of MACE in LVHT patients. MACE was defined as a combination of the following clinical endpoints: (1) all-cause mortality; (2) heart transplantation or left ventricular assist device implantation; (3) cardiac resynchronization therapy; (4) malignant arrhythmia: aborted SCD, ventricular fibrillation, sustained ventricular tachycardia, or appropriate implantable cardioverter-defibrillator therapy; and (5) thromboembolism: embolic stroke, transient ischemic attack, embolic myocardial infarction, or peripheral artery embolism.

Predictors

Based on clinical experience and contemporary literature addressing the risk stratification of MACE among LVHT patients, potential predictors were selected if they demonstrated either clinical relevance or statistical significance to the primary outcome. Candidate variables contained patient demographics, cardiovascular risk factors, family history, clinical manifestations, medical treatment, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-pro BNP) levels, ECG abnormalities, size of cardiac chambers, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), cardiac thrombosis, as well as location and extent of hypertrabeculation. Analyses were conducted according to concrete medical records and auxiliary examinations performed closest to the date of LVHT diagnosis. Body surface area (BSA) was calculated by Stevenson BSA formula [BSA (mm2) = 0.0061 × height (cm) + 0.0128 × weight (kg) − 0.1529)]. Cardiovascular risk factors were defined as a composite of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and smoking. A positive family history was determined by the existence of members in the family who fulfilled the morphologic diagnostic criteria for LVHT. Plasma NT-pro BNP, measured using electrochemiluminescent immunoassay, were categorized into 3 groups (< 300 pg/mL, 300–1000 pg/mL, and > 1000 pg/mL) according to the diagnostic and prognostic cut-off values in HF patients [26–28]. An abnormal ECG was distinguished by the lack of normal sinus rhythm, wide QRS complexes, or irregularities in repolarization [29]. According to recommendations for cardiac chamber quantification by TTE [30], left atrium (LA) enlargement was diagnosed if the left atrial anteroposterior dimension was over 3.8 cm in females or over 4.0 cm in males; left ventricle enlargement was diagnosed when the left ventricular end-diastolic diameter exceeded 5.2 cm in females or 5.8 cm in males. The LVEF measured via TTE was divided into two groups (LVEF > 40 and LVEF ≤ 40) for analysis, following the diagnostic threshold for HF with reduced EF [26]. Cardiac thrombosis was defined as a thrombus detected at any place within the cardiac cavity by TTE and/or CMR. The location and number of LV segments affected by hypertrabeculation were evaluated using the standardized 16-segment quantitation of the left ventricle through TTE [31].

Model development and validation

For model development, the effect of variables on the primary outcome was assessed by univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses. Candidate variables included in the multivariable Cox regression predictive model were selected based on clinical plausibility and statistical relevance (P-value < 0.1 on univariable analysis) to the occurrence of MACE in LVHT. The final model was fitted using backward selection according to Akaike’s information criterion. For model validation, discrimination and calibration ability were evaluated using Harrell’s C-index and Hosmer–Lemeshow test.

Risk score

A risk scoring system (the ABLE-SCORE) was established based on regression coefficients of the risk factors in the final multivariable Cox regression predictive model according to procedures by Wilson et al. [32]. Detailed construction process of the ABLE-SCORE is presented in Additional file 1: Table S1. S0 (t) is the baseline survival probability at time t. Σβixi and Σβixˉi were calculated by summing up the products of the regression coefficients of each predictor (βi), the individual values (xi), and the mean values (xˉi), respectively.

The total score for each patient was calculated by adding together the points corresponding to all predictors. Patients in the derivation cohort were stratified into three groups according to tertiles of the total risk score, and the discrepancy in cumulative incidence of MACE among different groups was evaluated by Kaplan–Meier curves.

Performance comparison among different models

We compared the performance of different risk prediction models for MACE in LVHT by adding new variables to the previously established risk model [22]. Harrell’s C-index, absolute integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and net reclassification index (NRI) were calculated to assess the effectiveness of the old and new models. The IDI or NRI > 0 suggested enhanced predictive performance compared to previous models.

Clinical utility

Decision curve analyses (DCA) were conducted in the derivation, internal validation, and external validation cohorts to evaluate the practical benefits of our risk prediction model in clinical practice. In addition, we further compared the clinical applicability of our new model with the previously published risk prediction model in LVHT [22]. The clinical benefit was assessed by the “net benefit”. A value of 0 indicated no benefit, while higher values indicated greater benefit.

All analyses were conducted using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R (R foundation). Statistical significance was defined by a two-tailed P-value < 0.05.

Results

A total of 545 consecutive patients fulfilling the imaging diagnostic criteria for LVHT were screened for eligibility, of whom 524 individuals [345 (65.8%) men; mean age of diagnosis 43.5 ± 16.6 years] were included, with 300 patients [196 (65.3%) men; mean age of diagnosis 42.5 ± 15.9 years] in the derivation cohort, 129 patients [85 (65.9%) men; mean age of diagnosis 43.7 ± 19.1 years] in the internal validation cohort, and 95 patients [64 (67.4%) men; mean age of diagnosis 46.2 ± 15.1 years] in the external validation cohort (Fig. 1). During a median follow-up of 4.81 years [interquartile range (IQR): 3.03–6.94 years], 97 (32.3%) and 38 (29.5%) LVHT patients in the derivation and internal validation cohorts at Fuwai Hospital experienced at least one endpoint of MACE, respectively. Model development

Table 2 illustrates the baseline characteristics of the derivation cohort according to the occurrence of MACE. Table 3 summarizes the development of the Cox regression predictive model for MACE in LVHT. As shown in these tables, 17 out of 60 candidate variables were chosen for the multivariable Cox regression model due to their statistical significance (age, dyspnea, chest pain, edema, New York Heart Association heart functional class III/IV, treatment of diuretics or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, NT-pro BNP, LA enlargement, left ventricle enlargement, LVEF ≤ 40%, and cardiac thrombosis) and clinical relevance (gender, cardiovascular risk factors, family history of LVHT, ECG abnormality, and number of hypertrabeculated segments) to MACE [22, 33]. On multivariable analysis, age at diagnosis, NT-pro BNP levels, LA enlargement, and LVEF ≤ 40% by TTE were demonstrated to be independently associated with MACE in LVHT. Model validation

The predictive performance of the multivariable Cox regression model in three LVHT datasets is shown in Additional file 2: Fig. S1. Our model presented excellent discrimination capability, with Harrell’s C-index of 0.818 (95%CI: 0.767–0.864), 0.795 (95%CI 0.715–0.875), and 0.753 (95%CI 0.648–0.858) in the derivation, internal validation, and external validation cohort, respectively. Calibration plots of the three datasets suggested accurate agreement between the predicted and observed 5-year risk of MACE. DCA indicated high net benefits across the three datasets for thresholds between 10 and 35%, indicating its effectiveness in clinical utility.

Risk score

The multivariable Cox regression predictive model was converted into a simplified risk scoring system, the ABLE-SCORE (A: age at diagnosis; B: NT-pro BNP; L: LA enlargement; E: LVEF ≤ 40%), with the total score ranging from 0 to 26 points (Fig. 2A). The probability of developing MACE according to score punctuation at 2, 5, and 10 years is illustrated in Fig. 2B. LVHT patients in the derivation cohort were stratified into three groups according to tertiles of the total risk score. Compared with low-risk patients (0–8 points), those at intermediate risk (9–17 points) and high risk (18–26 points) displayed significantly higher cumulative incidence of MACE (Fig. 2C). According to Harrell’s C-index, calibration plots, and DCA of the three LVHT datasets, the ABLE-SCORE showed excellent discrimination and calibration capability, and presented satisfactory net benefits in clinical applicability (Fig. 3). Performance comparison among different models

We compared the predictive performance of different risk scores for MACE in LVHT by incorporating additional variables to the pre-existing risk model [22]. The ABLE-SCORE showed strong discrimination ability in comparison with other risk scores according to Harrell’s C-index (Table 4). Both IDI and NRI revealed that adding NT-pro BNP, LA enlargement, or a combination of the two variables to the previously published risk score for MACE [22] significantly enhanced its original performance. Clinical utility

To evaluate the effectiveness of our simplified risk score in a clinical setting, DCA was performed to compare the clinical benefit of the ABLE-SCORE with the previously published MACE prediction score in LVHT [22]. As depicted in Fig. 2D and Fig. 2E, the ABLE-SCORE was associated with greater net benefit than the pre-existing MACE prediction score [22] for potential thresholds ranging from 10 to 35%, indicating its strength in predicting MACE for LVHT patients in clinical practice.

Discussion

In this multicenter longitudinal cohort study, a simplified risk prediction model for MACE in LVHT was developed using four independent risk factors: age at diagnosis, NT-pro BNP levels, LA enlargement, and LVEF ≤ 40%. The model demonstrated excellent discrimination and calibration ability by both internal and external validations, and was further converted into a risk score, the ABLE-SCORE, allowing clinicians to identify high-risk patients and provide targeted management. Derived from easily accessible laboratory and echocardiographic variables, the ABLE-SCORE showed efficient predictive performance and satisfactory net benefits compared with the current risk score for MACE in LVHT [22], indicating its reliability and convenience in clinical application.

LVHT is a heterogeneous entity with relatively poor prognosis. A recent large scale meta-analysis has suggested that the cardiovascular and all-cause mortality rates of LVHT are approximately 25- and fivefold higher than the general population [34]. However, current data regarding the prognosis and risk stratification of LVHT remains scarce. A risk prediction model for MACE has been previously reported, and it was composed of six variables: age, gender, cardiovascular risk factors, family aggregation by genetic testing, ECG abnormality, and LVEF on CMR [22]. The model has contributed important insights into the association between clinical/genetic information and MACE among LVHT individuals, but the implementation of genetic testing and CMR scans as regular procedures for risk assessment has precluded its widespread clinical use, especially in some developing countries and regions with limited healthcare resources. As a result, there is a crucial need to establish more simplified and widely applicable risk models for LVHT patients using easily accessible variables. Moreover, since the previous study also aimed to construct a LVHT safety algorithm to screen patients free from events during follow-up, some minor clinical events such as non-sustained ventricular tachycardia and HF hospitalization have been included in its endpoints of MACE [22]. In this current study, the main purpose of our risk prediction model was to identify true high-risk LVHT patients with possibilities of experiencing life-threatening adverse events. Therefore, our primary outcome predominantly encompassed severe clinical conditions.

Enrolled by consistent imaging diagnostic criteria, the baseline characteristics of patients in our study were generally similar to the previously reported LVHT cohorts, including age, cardiac function, size of the left ventricle, and LVEF [19–21, 35–39]. Over a median follow-up of nearly 5 years, MACE occurred in approximately 30% of the entire population, with decompensated HF and malignant ventricular arrhythmias being the leading cause of mortality. On multivariable Cox regression analysis, age at diagnosis, NT-pro BNP levels, LA enlargement, and LVEF ≤ 40% by TTE were demonstrated to be independent risk factors for MACE in LVHT.

Previous investigations have shown a potential connection between higher NT-pro BNP levels and increased risk of death or heart transplantation in individuals with LVHT [40, 41]. To the best of our knowledge, NT-pro BNP, a biomarker reflecting the extent of cardiac dysfunction, was included for the first time in the risk prediction model for MACE in LVHT as a candidate variable. In our cohort, only 9 (5.8%) patients experienced MACE in the group with NT-pro BNP levels < 300 pg/mL. In contrast, the incidence of MACE elevated to 3.1 times and 8.6 times higher in groups with NT-pro BNP levels of 300–1000 pg/mL and > 1000 pg/mL, indicating a strong prognostic relevance between elevated NT-pro BNP and MACE among LVHT patients. LA enlargement was also retained as an independent risk factor in our multivariable prediction model. LVHT patients with dilated LA presented a higher prevalence of HF, atrial fibrillation, and cardiac thrombosis, consequently resulting in a greater number of thromboembolic events and increased mortality rates. According to performance comparison among different risk models, both IDI and NRI revealed that incorporating NT-pro BNP, LA enlargement, or a combination of the two variables into the existing MACE prediction model [22] could significantly enhance its original predictive performance. Consistent with prior studies [20, 22, 34, 35, 37, 40, 42–44], our data further highlighted the importance of impaired LV systolic function in the prognosis of LVHT, and LVEF ≤ 40% on TTE at baseline was demonstrated to be an independent predictor for MACE in patients with LVHT. Although previous research has indicated a link between the extent of myocardial hypertrabeculation and global LV systolic dysfunction in LVHT [33], the multivariable analysis of our study revealed that the number of hypertrabeculated segments did not significantly influence the outcomes of MACE among patients with LVHT. In addition, prior investigations have also suggested the potential prognostic value of late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) on CMR for adverse clinical outcomes in LVHT patients [37, 45]. However, evaluation based on individuals with LGE data in our center revealed that incorporating LGE into the multivariable Cox regression model did not change the four previously identified risk factors for MACE in the ABLE-SCORE (Additional file 1: Table S3).

Based on our simplified risk prediction model, a risk scoring system for MACE in LVHT was established. The ABLE-SCORE displayed good discrimination and calibration capability, which enabled accurate risk stratification for up to 5 years of follow-up, with significant differences in the cumulative incidence of MACE among low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups of LVHT patients. DCA indicated that the ABLE-SCORE possessed greater net benefits than the previously reported risk score [22], suggesting its strength in determining high-risk patients and facilitating decision-making in a clinical setting. To enable the widely use of this new risk score, we designed an online calculator to assist both clinicians and patients in the risk assessment of LVHT (www.able-score.com), and this internet-based calculator could be easily integrated into hospital information systems or intelligent diagnosis and treatment platforms in the future. The ABLE-SCORE might be a promising tool for the MACE prediction in LVHT, allowing for more individualized, precise patient surveillance and management, and ultimately, a better outcome.

Several potential limitations of our study warrant discussion. First, owing to the retrospective nature of the study, potential bias caused by incomplete follow-up could not be excluded. Encouragingly, our study yielded relatively low rates of missing data and follow-up failures. All participants have received in-depth clinical and imaging assessment according to consistent standards. Second, similar to prior LVHT studies [22, 44], not all participants underwent CMR scans. Given an absence of CMR data in 23.1% of the entire population, LGE was not included in the multivariable analysis of our risk scoring system. Nevertheless, statistical assessment based on LVHT patients who underwent CMR at our center revealed that LGE did not emerge as an independent predictor for MACE, although it had good predictive value in univariable analysis. Third, because our study was retrospective, data regarding the dynamic alterations in patients’ laboratory tests and imaging evaluations were lacking. In addition, detailed information on time, dosage, and course of medical treatment was not available, so the corresponding prognostic inferences could not be analyzed. Forth, since an effective risk prediction model should be derived from precise and thorough clinical data, consecutive subjects enrolled in our study were predominantly hospitalized patients with detailed records, which might potentially introduce some selection bias due to limited outpatient data. However, literature review suggested that our cohort shared similar baseline characteristics with previously reported LVHT cohorts in major clinical parameters such as age, gender, cardiac function, and LVEF [19–21, 35–39]. Fifth, there is currently a lack of research concerning the specific NT-pro BNP cutoff values and their prognostic relevance for LVHT patients. In our risk score, the NT-pro BNP categorization criteria have been based solely on studies conducted with heart failure patients in general, and our grouping method is somewhat exploratory in nature. Sixth, although we have referenced the previously published authoritative LVHT risk scores [22, 23] to define the endpoints of MACE, the components of MACE chosen for our study might not cover all relevant aspects. Seventh, our risk prediction model has not taken into account of genetic testing. On the one hand, it is hard to regularly perform genetic testing for patients given the current economic and healthcare realities in China. On the other hand, the value of genetic testing for the diagnosis and prognosis of LVHT still remains controversial. Several genetic variants within the sarcomere, cytoskeleton, Z-disc, and nuclear envelope have been reported to be tentatively associated with the LVHT phenotype, but most identified mutations have strong overlaps with other cardiomyopathies or inherited conditions, bringing into question their relevance in the pathogenesis of LVHT [12, 38, 42, 46–48]. In our study, we included the family history of LVHT as a candidate variable in the risk model, yet it was eliminated after conducting the multivariable Cox regression analysis.

Conclusions

Using large multicenter LVHT cohorts in China, a simplified risk score consisting of four easily accessible clinical predictors was developed to estimate the individualized risk for MACE. The ABLE-SCORE demonstrated good discrimination and calibration ability by both internal and external validations, and presented greater net benefits in clinical applicability comparing with the existing risk score [22], making it a reliable and convenient tool for conducting risk stratification and supporting clinical decisions in patients with LVHT.
