Entamoeba histolytica Gal/GalNAc lectin intermediate subunit as a potential driver of inflammation and epithelial damage in intestinal amebiasis
Authors: Hongze Zhang, Shaokun Pan, Meng Feng, Dai Dong, Yanqing Zhao, Ruixue Zhou, Wenjie Li, Xunjia Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEntamoeba histolytica is a common enteric protozoan that sometimes transitions from a symbiont to a pathogen, causing intestinal amebiasis and extraintestinal abscesses in the host. The early-to-intermediate stages of intestinal amebiasis are characterized by an intense inflammatory response. Here, using the cecal infection model of C3H/HeNCrl mice, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing of cecal tissues and identified the prominent pro-inflammatory function of host macrophages in intestinal amebiasis. On the amebic cell surface, Igl is the intermediate subunit of galactose- and N-acetyl-D-galactosamine-inhibitable lectins, which contributes to parasite adherence. We proposed a potential mechanism whereby E. histolytica Igl diffuses along with amebic extracellular vesicles and contacts host macrophages, presumably binding to the TLR4 co-receptor MD2 through its C3 region and initiating the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway in host cells. Moreover, by inducing macrophage inflammation and cytokine production, E. histolytica appears to indirectly compromise intestinal epithelium integrity through the Igl protein. To our knowledge, this is the first report showing a host single-cell atlas in the field of amebiasis research. In response to the parasite invasion during intestinal amebiasis, our study yields additional clues elucidating the inflammatory formation and epithelial damage of gut mucosal immune system, and may contribute to the identification of potential drug targets for these amebas.Subject terms: Antimicrobial responses, Parasite host response

Introduction

Intestinal protozoan inhabitants play an important role in shaping the gut bacterial microbiota and maintaining host-microbe equilibrium in healthy individuals1,2. Protozoa of the genus Entamoeba colonize animals widely, and at least seven Entamoeba species are endobiotic in the human gut3. Among them, the only one that can become virulent and cause serious damage to the host is Entamoeba histolytica, the causative agent of various amebiases such as amebic colitis and liver abscesses4. As a common intestinal protozoan prevalent in the tropics and subtropics, E. histolytica is capable of utilizing host resources and interacting with the microbiota to meet its nutritional and protective needs5. However, though 90% of E. histolytica infections are asymptomatic, at least 500 million people are infected worldwide, and tens of millions develop amebiasis symptoms, mostly in countries with lower socioeconomic conditions3,6. Invasive amebiasis causes approximately 100,000 deaths annually, ranking as the second leading cause of death among protozoan parasitosis6,7.

Amebiasis usually begins with the disruption of intestinal homeostasis, followed by severe tissue destruction and inflammation, eventually leading to massive intestinal ulceration8. In some cases, the parasite enters the bloodstream and causes more fatal extraintestinal abscesses9,10. Although it is unclear how E. histolytica transitions from symbiont to pathogen, human biopsy specimens and experimental animal infections have shown that the early-to-intermediate stages of host intestinal amebiasis are characterized by an intense inflammatory response11,12. Most often, the invading amebic trophozoites are cleared after this acute inflammatory response, but in a small percentage of individuals, the parasite escapes elimination and intestinal lesions continue to develop12. At that stage, inflammation in the gut is instead attenuated, which may be related to the development of a non-protective adaptive immune response12,13.

As an indispensable prerequisite for parasite colonization and invasion, E. histolytica adherence is mediated by a group of galactose (Gal)- and N-acetyl-D-galactosamine (GalNAc)-inhibitable lectins, which are composed of a transmembrane heavy subunit (Hgl), a disulfide bond-linked glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored light subunit (Lgl), and a non-covalently associated GPI-anchored intermediate subunit (Igl)4,14. Upon attachment, amebas induce cell death and tissue invasion through a series of cytotoxic mechanisms, including apoptosis, phagocytosis, and trogocytosis9,15. In this process, amoebapores, cysteine proteases, amebic acid vesicle components, and intracellular calcium signaling in trophozoites all play a role16–19. Interestingly, in the initial stage of intestinal amebiasis, though the intestinal epithelial barrier remains intact and no parasite contact with the epithelial cells is observed, moderate inflammation and infiltration of innate immune cells are already present in the subepithelial lamina propria13. This suggests that before E. histolytica trophozoites adhere to and kill host cells, some virulence molecules released by the parasite can transit through the epithelium and reach immune cells, acting as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) to initiate intestinal inflammation12. The transepithelial movement of these virulence molecules is contributed to by amebic synthesis and secretion of prostaglandin E2, which disrupts the tight junctions of intestinal epithelial cells by binding to E-prostanoid-4 receptors12,20. After tight junction disruption, the transfer of E. histolytica PAMPs to the basolateral surface of intestinal epithelium can be detected21. Moreover, if a virulence molecule is present in amebic extracellular vesicles (EVs), it can be endocytosed by intestinal epithelial cells, transported to the basolateral side of epithelial cells through multivesicular bodies, and then released into the lamina propria22,23.

E. histolytica primarily uses humans as its natural host24. Thus, owing to the lack of suitable animal models to simulate the entire cycle of human disease, the study of amebiasis remains complicated and challenging25. At present, mice are still the most commonly used animals for laboratory studies of E. histolytica infection, among which some inbred mouse strains are susceptible to intestinal amebiasis, such as C3H/HeN, C3H/HeJ, and CBA/J24,26,27. In recent years, single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has become a powerful tool, revolutionizing our understanding of complex biological systems in various infectious diseases at the individual cell level28. This technology allows not only screening for cell types that play an important role in the infection process, but also identifying their interaction patterns with other cells29,30. However, to date, no host single-cell atlas of clinical samples or animal models has been developed in the field of amebiasis research. In this study, to explore the gene expression characteristics of different host cell types in the acute phase of intestinal amebiasis, E. histolytica trophozoites were inoculated into the cecum of C3H/HeNCrl mice, and fresh cecal tissues were collected for single-cell RNA-seq. After determining the important cell types, the underlying mechanisms of their gene expression changes and cell-cell interactions were preliminarily explored.

Results

Intense inflammatory response in the acute phase of intestinal amebiasis

First, to determine the clinical characteristics of the acute phase of intestinal amebiasis, a mouse model was established by inoculating E. histolytica trophozoites into the cecum of 7-week-old male C3H/HeNCrl mice. Murine body weight, leukocyte proportion, and inflammatory infiltration of the cecal tissue were regularly detected (Fig. 1a). After surgery, the infected mice lost weight compared to control-treated mice (Fig. 1b). At 6 and 8 d post-inoculation, murine routine blood tests showed not only increased proportions of neutrophils, monocytes, and eosinophils, but also a decreased proportion of lymphocytes, suggesting the presence of inflammation caused by bacterial or parasitic infection (Fig. 1c). As the three inflammatory cytokines that have attracted much attention in studies of amebiasis, infiltration of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6 were also detected in the cecal tissue of ameba-infected mice (Fig. 1d).

Owing to the obvious pathological changes in mice in the Infection 6-day and Infection 8-day groups, single-cell RNA-seq was further performed on their cecal tissues. The experiment consisted of three groups with three mice in each group. Initially, data quality control was conducted to filter out low-quality information after sequencing (Fig. S1a). Upon combining all the data, single cells from infected and control-treated samples were clustered and visualized in Fig. S1b, and the proportion of cell clusters in each group was shown in Fig. S1c. Using cell-specific marker genes (Fig. S1d), 68,058 single cells from the ceca of nine different mice were annotated into 15 cell types (Fig. 1e). Since the more abundant lymph nodes in the cecum and the dominant position of B and T cells in these lymph nodes, the single-cell atlas in our study exhibited higher proportions of the two cell types (Fig. S1e)31. Setting |log2FC| value > 0.585 and adjusted p value < 0.05 as the criteria, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each cell type in Infection 6-day and Infection 8-day groups were identified (Fig. 1f). Detailed gene expression changes of each cell type are shown in Table S1.

Among all cell types, mononuclear phagocytes (MNPs) and epithelial cells showed the greatest differences in terms of DEG numbers and fold changes (Fig. 1f). Thus, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of various Gene Ontology (GO) terms was performed for these two cell types. In the cecum of ameba-infected mice, MNPs exhibited significant positive enrichment of pro-inflammatory GO terms, which were highly correlated with innate immunity and cytokines (Fig. 1g). Epithelial cells showed not only significant negative enrichment of GO terms associated with aerobic respiration and tight junctions, but also significant positive enrichment of immune-related GO terms, suggesting inflammatory injuries of the intestinal epithelium under E. histolytica infection (Fig. 1h).

Important role of mononuclear phagocytes in intestinal amebiasis

To further identify the cell types that play an important role in intestinal amebiasis, GSEA of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was performed on individual cells, and the Amebiasis pathway (mmu05146) was selected for comparison. For the enrichment level of the Amebiasis pathway, every single cell in each group was scored separately and plotted in a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization (Fig. 2a). As shown by the dashed circles, after E. histolytica infection, the most significant enrichment occurred in cecal MNPs. The AUCell scores of the Amebiasis pathway gene set for all cell types are exhibited in Fig. 2b. In the Infection 6-day and Infection 8-day groups, the scaled scores of MNPs improved the most. Correspondingly, the MNP proportion increased the most after parasitic inoculation (Fig. 2c). These results suggested a critical function of host MNPs in intestinal amebiasis.

Cecal MNPs were further subclustered to conduct in-depth research on this important cell type. First, subclustering of all single cells in MNPs was visualized in Fig. S3a, and the proportion of cell clusters in each group was shown in Fig. S3b. Using cell-specific marker genes (Fig. S3c), the single cells were annotated into 4 cell types (Fig. S4d). As shown in Fig. S3e, after intracecal inoculation of E. histolytica trophozoites, the proportion of monocyte-derived DCs significantly decreased, while the proportions of anti-inflammatory macrophages and pro-inflammatory macrophages increased in these MNPs. Setting |log2FC| value > 0.585 and |percentage difference | > 0.01 as the criteria, differentially expressed genes in murine pro-inflammatory macrophages in Infection 6-day and Infection 8-day groups were identified (Fig. S3f). The GO terms indicated a significant increase in the activity of cytokines and chemokines, which is consistent with the manifestation of intense inflammatory responses.

As mentioned earlier, in KEGG enrichment analysis of MNPs, the Amebiasis pathway was the most upregulated both 6 and 8 d post-inoculation, suggesting that intestinal inflammation in mice was more likely to be induced directly by E. histolytica trophozoites than indirectly by the imbalanced gut microbiota caused by parasite colonization. From the perspective of a human cell line, the pro-inflammatory effect of E. histolytica trophozoites on phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)-differentiated U937 monocytes was demonstrated (Fig. 2h). As an important molecule on the amebic cell surface contributing to parasite adherence, native Igl protein could also act as a PAMP to initiate inflammatory signals in human U937 cells (Fig. 2i).

E. histolytica Igl may induce an inflammatory response in macrophages through its C3 segment

The C3 region of E. histolytica Igl not only has the most conserved propensity among various Igls of Entamoeba species, but also plays a potential role in the carbohydrate recognition of parasite adherence, making it more likely to participate in host immune recognition as an important PAMP segment. Using lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and IL-4 as controls, eukaryote-expressed E. histolytica Igl-C3 could activate host macrophages and differentiate them into a pro-inflammatory phenotype, but changes in their cytokine transcription levels were smaller than those of LPS stimulation (Fig. 3a). In addition to TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, expressions of IL-10, IL-23, and TGF-β1, which are often mentioned in studies of amebiasis, were also examined. To detect cytokine production and release, western blotting of U937 cells and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) of cell culture supernatants were performed, respectively (Fig. 3b, c). After Igl-C3 stimulation, cytokine expression of PMA-differentiated human THP-1 cells and mouse RAW264.7 cells was further examined to confirm this pro-inflammatory effect (Fig. S4a, b).

Igl-C3 indirectly compromises intestinal epithelium integrity by inducing host inflammation

Single-cell RNA-seq of mouse cecal tissues revealed that, besides MNPs, epithelial cells were another cell type with significant gene expression changes (Fig. 1f). The upregulation of DEGs was most significant in MNPs, while the downregulation of DEGs was most significant in epithelial cells. To explore whether DEG changes in these two cell types were correlated, the interactions between different cell types in the cecum were analyzed using the CellChat database. In the Ctrl group, no cellular communication from MNPs to epithelial cells was detected, but such interactions emerged 6 and 8 d after parasitic inoculation (Fig. 4a). With the persistence of E. histolytica infection, the interaction number from MNPs to epithelial cells continued to increase (Fig. 4b). At different time points after amebic infection, detailed changes in the cell–cell interactions between MNPs and epithelial cells were revealed in Fig. 4c, being associated with immune regulation, host defense, and transduction of inflammatory signals.

In addition to intense inflammation, another characteristic of intestinal amebiasis is severe destruction of the gut tissue. In our study, the GSEA plots exhibited significant negative enrichment of the GO Tight Junction term (GO:0070160) in the cecal epithelial cells of ameba-infected mice (Fig. 4d). The Cdh, Cldn, and Tjp gene families play important roles in maintaining the tight junctions of intestinal epithelium, and their gene expressions were also mostly decreased after E. histolytica trophozoite infection (Fig. 4e)3,32–34. For each cell type in the single-cell RNA-seq data, incoming signaling patterns of cellular communication in different groups were shown in Fig. S7a, and outgoing signaling patterns of cellular communication in different groups were shown in Fig. S7b. In terms of the CDH- and CLDN-related pathways, the intensity of signal sending and receiving in epithelial cells was significantly upregulated, suggesting ongoing changes in tight junctions and barrier functions. Based on their importance in studies relevant to intestinal tight junctions, the Cdh, Cldn, and Tjp gene family members, Cdh1, Cldn3, and Tjp1, respectively, were selected for further detection. The decreased expression of E-cadherin (Cdh1), Claudin-3 (Cldn3), and ZO-1 (Tjp1) in mouse cecal tissues was examined by immunohistochemistry to verify the single-cell RNA-seq results (Fig. S8a–c).

Subsequently, to determine whether E. histolytica Igl-C3 contributes to this injury of intestinal epithelium, a three-dimensional triple culture system was established for partially mimicking the structure of epithelial cell monolayer and lamina propria macrophages (Fig. 4f). The epithelial cell monolayer consists of Caco-2 and HT29-MTX-E12 cells, whose common ratios for simulating the intestinal epithelium are 3:1 and 9:1. In the present study, after comparing mucus production, a 3:1 ratio was used for follow-up experiments (Fig. 4g). Upon Igl-C3 treatment for 24 h, transepithelial electronic resistance (TEER) of the epithelial cell monolayer decreased slightly, whereas in the presence of macrophages, Igl-C3 incubation resulted in a much more significant TEER reduction (Fig. 4h). Similarly, in the presence of macrophages, Igl-C3 treatment significantly increased the permeability of epithelial cell monolayers to FITC-Dextran (Fig. 4i). In response to macrophage inflammation induced by Igl-C3, expressions of tight junction-related CDH1, CLDN3, and TJP1 genes were all reduced in epithelial Caco-2 and HT29-MTX-E12 cells (Fig. 4j).

Given that previous studies have demonstrated that inflammatory cytokines can impair intestinal epithelium integrity, here, anti-TNF-α, anti-IL-1β, and anti-IL-6 neutralizing antibodies were further added to the coculture environment for in-depth analysis. After different treatments, TEER of the epithelial cell monolayers was shown in Fig. 4k, while their permeability to FITC–Dextran was exhibited in Fig. 4l. Finally, TNF-α and IL-1β were confirmed to cause more severe damage to the intestinal epithelium, while IL-6 had a minor impact on the integrity of epithelial structure.

Macrophages appear to recognize Igl-C3 via TLR4 and initiate the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway

Our results suggested that Igl-C3 could act as a PAMP to induce an inflammatory response in macrophages and indirectly compromise intestinal epithelium integrity through inflammatory cytokines released by macrophages. Since the pathogenic effect of Igl-C3 in intestinal amebiasis was mainly reflected in its interaction with macrophages, the underlying mechanism of such interaction was subsequently explored. In the transcriptomic sequencing of Igl-C3-treated U937 cells, the interaction network between the top 20 KEGG pathways with the most significant upregulation indicated that changes in the Amebiasis pathway might be related to toll-like receptor (TLR) signals (Fig. 5a, b). As the two TLRs being reported to identify E. histolytica PAMPs, the responses of TLR2 and TLR4 to Igl-C3 were detected by preincubation with the corresponding inhibitors (Fig. 5c). Effects of the two inhibitors on human U937 cell viability were shown in Fig.e S9a. Macrophages were demonstrated to recognize the C3 segment of E. histolytica Igl via TLR4.

After the recognition of Igl-C3 by TLR4, inflammatory signals within host macrophages were found to be initiated by the classical TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway through western blotting and laser confocal microscopy (Fig. 5d, e). Upon 24 h of Igl-C3 treatment, the expression of MyD88 in macrophages increased, and the expression of phospho-NF-κB was upregulated relative to total NF-κB, exhibiting a tendency to enter the nucleus. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) treatment was further performed to silence the MYD88 gene in macrophages for verification (Fig. 5f). After siRNA treatment, the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway was inhibited accordingly (Fig. 5g, h), and expressions of pro-inflammatory cytokines were also significantly reduced (Fig. 5i).

In the single-cell RNA-seq data, comparing the Infection 6-day group and Ctrl group MNPs, a GSEA plot of the KEGG Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (mmu04620) and its core-enriched genes was displayed in Fig. S10a. Similarly, comparing the Infection 8-day group and Ctrl group MNPs, that GSEA plot and core-enriched genes were displayed in Fig. S10b. At different time points upon amebic infection, the gene sets of the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway all showed a significant positive enrichment, and expressions of Tlr4, Myd88, and Nfkbia genes increased, suggesting that the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway might be active and crucial to the pro-inflammatory process of macrophages during intestinal amebiasis.

Potential binding of Igl-C3 to the TLR4 co-receptor MD2 on the surface of macrophages to activate inflammatory signals

To explore how TLR4 recognized Igl-C3 on the surface of host macrophages, inhibitor and siRNA treatments were first performed on human U937 cells targeting the TLR4 co-receptor MD2. As an indispensable accessory protein of TLR4, MD2 can bind to it and recruit the adaptor protein MyD88, producing a large number of pro-inflammatory cytokines. The effect of the MD2 inhibitor on human U937 cell viability was shown in Fig. S9b. After preincubation with the inhibitor, the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway of the macrophages was tentatively inhibited, and the transcription and translation levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were decreased (Fig. 6a, b). The release of pro-inflammatory cytokines into the cell culture supernatant was also significantly reduced (Fig. 6c). Targeting MD2 (LY96), the siRNA treatment on human U937 cells was shown in Fig. 6d. After silencing the LY96 gene, evidence suggested that expressions of pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages decreased significantly (Fig. 6e).

Then, the role of the MD2 protein in the overall pro-inflammatory effect during E. histolytica infection was preliminarily investigated through in vitro experiments. After preincubation with the MD2 inhibitor, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the parasite-treated macrophages significantly decreased (Fig. 6f). Meanwhile, the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines into the cell culture supernatant was reduced (Fig. 6g).

In the ELISA experiments, both eukaryotic Igl-C3 and prokaryotic Igl-C3 exhibited potential binding to human MD2 protein, but the strength was lower than that of LPS (Fig. 6h). Subsequently, Biacore analysis was performed to detect the protein affinity of eukaryotic Igl-C3/human MD2. As shown in Fig. 6i, Igl-C3 could directly bind to MD2 with strong affinity. Protein affinity between native E. histolytica Igl and human MD2 was also demonstrated, indicating the presence of such stimulation in the clinical infection state (Fig. 6j). Finally, the spatial sites of eukaryotic Igl-C3 and LPS binding to MD2 were compared through competitive experiments (Fig. 6k). When loaded in the order of LPS first and then Igl-C3, response unit of the latter binding curve significantly increased, indicating that the spatial sites where Igl-C3 and LPS bind to human MD2 were not similar or overlapping, and LPS/MD2 binding might enhance the affinity of MD2 to Igl-C3. The affinities of MD2 to Igl-C3 before and after LPS/MD2 binding were further compared through Biacore analysis (Fig. 6l). After binding to LPS, the association and dissociation rates of Igl-C3/human MD2 both decreased.

E. histolytica Igl diffuses along with amebic EVs to stimulate host macrophages

At the end of the present study, EV-related experiments were conducted to explore how E. histolytica Igl contacts host macrophages to induce pro-inflammatory signals in intestinal amebiasis. The presence of Igl in the amebic EVs was first confirmed by western blotting (Fig. 7a). Subsequently, colonic organoids were applied to investigate the pro-inflammatory and epithelium-damaging effects of these EVs (Fig. S11a). Here, our colonic organoids were derived from a human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) line, ChiPSC7, and their availability was confirmed using bright-field examination and immunofluorescent staining (Fig. S11b, c). After amebic EV treatment, transcriptions of TNF and IL1B increased, while expressions of tight junction-related CDH1, CLDN3, and TJP1 genes were all reduced in the organoids (Fig. 7b). Due to its low expression level, the transcriptional signal of IL6 was undetermined in the quantitative real-time RT-PCR assay. In the culture supernatant of EV-treated colonic organoids, releases of pro-inflammatory TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were all significantly increased (Fig. 7c). Permeability of EV-treated colonic organoids to FITC-Dextran was also increased (Fig. 7d).

To investigate whether the pro-inflammatory effect of amebic EVs is related to the Igl protein on their membrane surface, these EVs were tentatively used to stimulate differentiated U937 cells in vitro, and anti-Igl monoclonal antibody (mAb) EH3077 was added simultaneously for neutralization. In the presence of EH3077, evidence suggested that the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway of EV-treated macrophages was partially inhibited, and the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines decreased (Fig. 7e, f). The release of pro-inflammatory cytokines into the cell culture supernatant was also partially reduced (Fig. 7g).

Then, after coculturing E. histolytica trophozoites with host macrophages, quantitative proteomics was applied to compare changes in protein composition and function between the macrophage coculture group and control culture group amebic EVs. As shown in Fig. 7h, a total of 1693 different proteins were annotated, and 1072 of which were shared between the two groups. The top 20 most abundant proteins in the control culture group amebic EVs were exhibited in Fig. S12a, and the top 20 most abundant proteins in the macrophage coculture group amebic EVs were exhibited in Fig. S12b. Within each group, the fractions of total protein abundance across independent biological replicates were generally consistent (Fig. 7i). Meanwhile, high correlations existed among samples in each group (Fig. 7j).

After coculture with host macrophages, 74 proteins were upregulated, and 45 proteins were downregulated in amebic EVs (Fig. 7k). To identify the variations in protein function, changes of terms under Molecular Function in the GO enrichment analysis were compared between the macrophage coculture group and control culture group amebic EVs (Fig. 7l, m). Upon coculture, the molecular function of proteins in amebic EVs showed a more obvious upregulation trend, mostly related to binding and catalysis. Comparing the macrophage coculture group and control culture group amebic EVs, the interaction network of upregulated differential proteins was exhibited in Fig. 7n, and that of downregulated differential proteins was exhibited in Fig. S12c. In the two subtypes of E. histolytica Igl, the content of Igl2 in amebic EVs was significantly increased, which was potentially associated with heat shock protein (HSP) 70, molybdenum cofactor sulfurase, and signal peptidase complex catalytic subunit. Except for Igl2, the content of Lgl in amebic EVs also increased significantly after coculture with host macrophages (Fig. 7o). Finally, upon incubation with macrophages, expressions of both Igl1 and Igl2 in E. histolytica trophozoites were upregulated, consistent with the content change trend of these proteins in amebic EVs (Fig. 7p).

Discussion

The gut mucosal immune system consists of epithelial cells, lamina propria, and lymph nodes, constituting a protective barrier for intestinal tract integrity and making indispensable contributions in resisting pathogen infections35. Host innate immune defenses in this system, represented by neutrophils and macrophages, always play an important role at the early-to-intermediate stages of E. histolytica infection24. Recruited by inflammatory cytokines released by epithelial cells and several immune cells, neutrophils migrate from the circulation to infection sites, where they show multiple antiamoebic functions such as degranulation, phagocytosis, and formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)12,36,37. However, the role of neutrophils is double-edged, as they are also induced to die by E. histolytica trophozoites, in turn promoting inflammation and tissue damage through the release of their lytic products38,39. Similarly, macrophages perform complicated and two-sided functions during amebiasis. Host macrophages recognize several PAMPs of the ameba via TLR2 and TLR4, then produce various inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-640–42. Nitric oxide (NO) is an anti-amebic agent that inhibits essential enzymes involved in E. histolytica metabolism43. On the one hand, activated macrophages can mediate the amoebicidal effect of interferon-gamma, producing large amounts of NO from L-arginine through NO synthase24. On the other hand, the abundant inflammatory cytokines released by macrophages not only contribute to the recruitment of innate immune effectors and the initiation of adaptive immune responses, but also aggravate the damage of intestinal tissue44. During the formation of an amebic liver abscess, monocytes have already been demonstrated to cause severe liver damage by releasing microbicidal factors45. In this study, based on single-cell RNA-seq results, we focused on the significant pro-inflammatory effects of E. histolytica and its virulence molecule, Igl, on macrophages. Notably, host adaptive immunity appears to lack protection against E. histolytica infection, as cellular immune responses are suppressed during the acute phase of intestinal amebiasis, whereas humoral immunity has little influence on disease progression, outcome, or recurrence46,47.

In the present study, acting as a PAMP, the pro-inflammatory effects of E. histolytica Igl on host macrophages through its C3 region were preliminarily investigated. The single-cell RNA-seq data from ameba-infected C3H/HeNCrl mice also suggested the activation of inflammatory signaling pathways related to TLR4. It is worth noting that among the inbred mouse strains susceptible to intestinal amebiasis, the Tlr4 gene is expressed normally in C3H/HeN mice, but functionally deficient due to a mutation in C3H/HeJ mice48. After the TLR4 mutation, these C3H/HeJ mice developed tolerance to LPS, but become more susceptible to various Gram-negative bacteria49,50. As for E. histolytica, the infection rate in C3H/HeJ mice was only slightly higher than that in C3H/HeN mice, indicating that the parasite actually relies on multiple virulence molecules working together26. Moreover, through Biacore competitive binding analysis, we found that LPS/MD2 binding might enhance the affinity of MD2 to Igl-C3, providing new clues for understanding how parasite-bacteria interaction promotes the disease progression.

On the amebic cell surface, Igl plays an important role as the intermediate subunit of Gal/GalNAc lectin during parasite adherence51. PAMPs are mostly conserved molecules on the surface of pathogens, among which adhesion-related proteins have been extensively studied in multiple pathogens due to their highly conserved propensities and spatial structures that are easier to contact host cells52,53. Adhesion proteins FadA (binding E-cadherin and VE-cadherin) and Fap2 (binding Gal/GalNAc) exist on the cell surface of Fusobacterium nucleatum, not only helping the bacteria attach to intestinal epithelium, but also inducing the host to produce inflammatory cytokines and recruit immune cells54–56. On the cell surface of Escherichia coli, the adhesion protein FimH (binding mannose) can be recognized by TLR4 of host cells, and then activate their TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway to express various inflammatory cytokines57. Recognized by TLR7 of host cells as a PAMP, the hemagglutinin protein (binding sialic acid) of the influenza virus can activate inflammatory signals and trigger an antiviral innate immune response58,59. Likewise, on the surface of E. histolytica, Hgl (binding Gal/GalNAc) can stimulate intestinal epithelial cells via TLR2 and TLR4, leading to the activation of intracellular NF-κB signaling and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines40,60. However, though it is similar in function and distribution to Hgl, the binding of E. histolytica Igl to host cell receptors has not been reported8. Except for these Gal/GalNAc lectin component proteins, lipophosphopeptidogylcan is another important molecule on the amebic cell surface, which can be recognized by TLR2 and TLR4 of macrophages and activate inflammatory signals24,41. In the amebic cytoplasm, peroxiredoxin and E. histolytica DNA are also effective PAMPs to a certain extent42,61. Various amebic PAMPs, including Igl, might contribute greatly to the inflammatory formation of intestinal amebiasis12.

Given that amebic PAMPs could induce inflammatory responses in macrophages, it is meaningful to further clarify whether these pathogen-derived molecules could modulate the intestinal barrier and associated immune cell functions. Under the mucous layer, epithelial cells and innate immune cells form a second layer of protection through complex interactions, jointly participating in the immune surveillance of the host intestine35,62. In the gut mucosal immune system, epithelial cells not only directly resist pathogens as a physical barrier, but also send signals to nearby immune cells by producing different cytokines35. Here, oppositely, through single-cell RNA-seq and validation using a three-dimensional triple culture system, E. histolytica Igl-C3 appears to indirectly disrupt intestinal epithelial integrity by inducing macrophage inflammation. In other inflammatory diseases of the gut, this important role of macrophages and their interactions with intestinal epithelial cells has already been elucidated63. As an example, in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), activated macrophages typically infiltrate the intestine in large numbers62,64. In both Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, released by the macrophages are identified as key factors causing intestinal epithelial damage63,65–67. For epithelial cells, pro-inflammatory changes in macrophages can promote their expression of claudin-2, a pore-forming tight junction molecule, and trigger the internalization and degradation of barrier-sealing molecules such as E-cadherin and claudin-4, ultimately leading to intestinal barrier leakage62,63. Interestingly, intestinal amebiasis and IBD not only share similar clinical manifestations, but also have genetic links in susceptibility and sometimes co-occur in a single patient8,68,69. In our single-cell RNA-seq and bulk transcriptomic sequencing data, KEGG enrichment analysis of both murine MNPs in the ameba-infected animal ceca and human macrophages after E. histolytica Igl-C3 stimulation revealed significant positive enrichment of the Inflammatory bowel disease pathway (Figs. 5a and S2a). These similar mechanisms of intestinal inflammation formation and epithelial damage might partly explain such a curious relationship between the two diseases. Except for IBD, aflatoxin B1 in food can exert non-carcinogenic toxicities, over-recruiting monocytes and macrophages to induce severe colitis in BALB/c mice70. Subsequently, activated macrophages secrete abundant inflammatory cytokines that damage the colonic epithelium. Moreover, during Clostridioides difficile infection, the bacteria can induce host macrophages to release inflammatory cytokines through their production of toxins A and B, inhibiting the repair of intestinal epithelial damage and resulting in severe diarrhea71.

Communication between parasites and the host immune system involves EVs derived from both pathogens and host cells, which transmit information from one to the other in ways such as proteins and RNA72. During E. histolytica infection, more than 200 amebic proteins were detected in the extracellular environment, being involved in parasite colonization, cyst formation, and evasion of the host immune system73. However, many of these proteins do not contain secretory signaling sequences, indicating their departure from amebic trophozoites via non-classical secretory pathways such as the secretion of EVs74. E. histolytica EVs are approximately 80–400 nm in diameter and have morphological features typical of EVs from other systems23,75,76. Through omics detection of the RNA and proteins within them, amebic EVs were found to contribute greatly to parasite communication, development, and pathogenicity74–76. Here, these EVs were further linked to the inflammation and disruption of tight junctions in iPSC-derived colonic organoids.

E. histolytica EVs act as efficient delivery vehicles for amebic PAMPs to stimulate host immune cells and trigger inflammation, while the pro-inflammatory ability of these EVs is closely related to the virulence of different amebic strains76,77. As the two innate immune cell types playing important roles in amebiasis, interactions of amebic EVs with neutrophils and macrophages have already been preliminarily studied23,76,77. Both murine bone marrow-derived monocytes and human THP-1 macrophages can internalize and be activated by amebic EVs, upregulating multiple inflammatory signaling pathways and increasing pro-inflammatory cytokine expressions76,77. During hepatic amebiasis, parasite EVs might drive the monocyte-mediated immunopathology, which induces a stronger pro-inflammatory effect in male-derived monocytes than in female-derived ones76. Likewise, in a recent study, after coculture of E. histolytica trophozoites with host neutrophils, amebic EVs were collected, and their protein composition and biological function were examined23. Probably by carrying and transporting reactive oxygen species, these EVs can exert an immunomodulatory effect on activated neutrophils, downregulating their respiratory burst and NET production23. As a contrast, in our study, the parasite was cocultured with host macrophages, and quantitative proteomic sequencing was then performed on the amebic EVs. Impressively, whether cocultured with host macrophages or neutrophils, E. histolytica EVs exhibited not only upregulated binding and catalysis functions in GO enrichment analysis, but also increased the relative abundance of Gal/GalNAc lectin component proteins (Fig. 7f, i)23. Besides, the most abundant proteins in these studies were very similar, and some of which were potentially associated with Gal/GalNAc lectins. For example, abundant aldehyde-alcohol dehydrogenase and myosin heavy chain proteins in EVs can, together with Gal/GalNAc lectins, form the uroid region during capping, contributing to the removal of antibodies and other opsonizing molecules from the parasite surface23,78,79. Combined with such EV omics results of parasite coculture with macrophages or neutrophils, Gal/GalNAc lectin was suggested to play a more complex role in the host innate immune defense, serving as a potential PAMP and signaling promoter23. Within the first five minutes of trophozoite incubation with host cells, the transfer of Gal/GalNAc lectins to target cells has been previously reported, which could also occur through EVs80. Moreover, our study found that the increased Igl2 level in amebic EVs has a potential functional association with HSP 70. Interestingly, the content of HSP 70 correlates with E. histolytica virulence, and the relationship between these two proteins deserves further exploration81,82.

However, this study still has some limitations. To date, the colon is the most common site for E. histolytica infection, while the relatively mature intracecal inoculation model is basically used in animal experiments, leading to insufficient simulation of intestinal amebiasis12,24. To mimic amebic colitis, a mouse model was used by ligating the animal colon at its proximal end with a length of approximately 2 cm and inoculating E. histolytica trophozoites into the colonic loop83. This colonic loop model is currently applied to short-term infections at 3 h, but improvements may extend its duration of infection and better simulate intestinal amebiasis. Moreover, owing to the ability of accumulating polyploid cells in their proliferative phase, we failed to achieve gene silencing of wild-type Igl and alternative expression of C3-deficient Igl in E. histolytica trophozoites8. In this study, no Igl-edited parasites were used to verify the effect of Igl-C3 on host macrophages. Finally, our study did not employ in vivo experiments to confirm the pro-inflammatory and epithelium-damaging functions of E. histolytica Igl. Though attempts have been made to use human intestinal epithelial cell monolayers and organoids for a certain degree of simulation, the drawback remains. Especially for the epithelial cell monolayer, there are also issues of structural simplification and inherent biases of tumor-derived cells. In the future, mouse models will still be necessary for further verification.

Overall, using an intracecal inoculation infection model, the present study identified the gene expression characteristics of different host cell types during the acute phase of intestinal amebiasis. To our knowledge, this is the first report showing a host single-cell atlas in the field of amebiasis research. Further, based on our observations, we proposed a potential mechanism whereby E. histolytica Igl diffuses along with amebic EVs and contacts host macrophages, presumably binding to the TLR4 co-receptor MD2 through its C3 region and initiating the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway in host cells (Fig. 8). By inducing macrophage inflammation and cytokine production, E. histolytica seems capable of indirectly compromising the integrity of intestinal epithelium through Igl protein. In response to the parasite invasion during intestinal amebiasis, our results yield additional clues elucidating the inflammatory formation and epithelial damage of the gut mucosal immune system, and may contribute to the identification of potential drug targets for these amebas.

Methods

Amebic strains and cell cultures

Trophozoites of E. histolytica isolates, HM-1:IMSS and SAW755CR, were grown axenically at 36.5 °C, in YIMDHA-S medium containing 10% and 15% (v/v) heat-inactivated adult bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), respectively.

Human pro-monocytic myeloid leukemia cell lines, U937 and THP-1, were grown in RPMI-1640 medium (#10-040-CV; Corning, Manassas, VA, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (HyClone Laboratories, Logan, UT, USA). Human intestinal epithelial cells, Caco-2, were grown in Eagle’s minimum essential medium (MEM; #10-010-CV; Corning, Manassas, VA, USA) containing 20% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, 1% (v/v) MEM non-essential amino acids (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), 1% (v/v) 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), and 1% (v/v) 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco). Human HT29-MTX-E12 and mouse RAW264.7 cells were grown in DMEM medium (#10-013-CV; Corning, Manassas, VA, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, 1% (v/v) MEM non-essential amino acids, 1% (v/v) 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1% (v/v) 1 mM sodium pyruvate. The above cells were grown at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells were cultured in OPM-293 CD05 medium (81075-001; Opmbiosciences, Shanghai, China) under conditions of 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 120 rpm shaking.

After seeding, human U937 and THP-1 monocytes were pretreated with 100 ng/mL PMA (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h to differentiate them into macrophage-like phenotypes. For the construction of the intestinal epithelial model, human Caco-2 and HT29-MTX-E12 cells were seeded on the apical side of Falcon Transparent Inserts (0.4 μm pore size; 353095; Corning, Tewksbury, MA, USA) in ratios of 3:1 or 9:184,85. DMEM medium was used across all coculture experiments.

Animal model for intestinal amebiasis

Seven-week-old male C3H/HeNCrl mice were purchased from the Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Company and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. Mice were housed in ventilated cages with a 12 h light-dark cycle, at approximately 22 °C temperature with 55 ± 10% humidity, and had ad libitum access to food and water. For a more stable induction of intestinal amebiasis, the more virulent SAW755CR strain was used instead of the general HM-1:IMSS strain during intracecal inoculation of E. histolytica trophozoites, whose toxicity was maintained by regular passage through the hamster liver (Songlian Experimental Animal Factory, Shanghai, China) as previously mentioned86. Mice in experimental groups were inoculated directly with 100 µL of 1 × 106 axenic trophozoites into the cecum (n ≥ 3), while mice in control groups received the same volume of YIMDHA-S medium without trophozoites (n ≥ 3). Six-week-old male hamsters were employed here, without any specific strain classification.

Amebiasis severity was evaluated by monitoring animal body weight changes, routine blood tests, and immunohistochemical staining of cecal tissues. At 4, 6, and 8 d post-inoculation, the mice were euthanized, blood samples were collected into 1 mL EDTA K2 tubes, and cecal tissues were harvested for 4% paraformaldehyde fixation. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, routine blood tests were performed using a ProCyte Dx Hematology Analyzer (IDEXX Laboratories, ME, USA), and the proportion of various leukocytes was calculated by ProCyte Dx software version 00-35. Meanwhile, after fixation in paraformaldehyde and paraffin embedding, cecal tissue sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemical staining. Expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, E-cadherin, Claudin-3, and ZO-1 in the cecum were detected3,8,24,32,87,88.

In strict accordance with the guidelines of the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals (1988.11.1), all animal experiments were conducted with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University (permit no. 20160225-097). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use, and maximum efforts were made to minimize animal suffering. The experiment endpoint was defined as the euthanasia of the mice and the collection of blood samples.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 and GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1. Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For a two-group comparison, if the data follow a normal distribution, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. The nonparametric Wilcoxon rank sum test was applied to data that didn’t follow a normal distribution.

The sample size for each experimental dataset was determined based on our prior experience, which can be represented by the scatter points in the corresponding figure. No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample size, and measurements were taken from distinct samples. All biological replicates were performed at least three times with consistent results. Data are expressed as mean with SD. Differences of p value < 0.05 were considered significant.

Raw transcriptomic sequencing datasets of human U937 cells are available through NCBI accession number PRJNA1297930. Raw quantitative proteomics datasets of amebic EVs have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the iProX partner repository with identifier PXD080021 (https://www.iprox.cn/).
