Change of guards: ELAVL proteins switch miRNA export responsibility to regulate differentiation of PC12 cells
Authors: Sritama Ray, Suvendra N Bhattacharyya, Kamalika Mukherjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn the rat pheochromocytoma cell line PC12, which resembles sympathetic neurons, miRNA activity decreases during differentiation, and inactivation of the let-7a miRNP is essential for differentiation. We sought to examine how let-7a activity is affected during differentiation. Extracellular vesicle-mediated miRNA export is a common strategy used by mammalian cells to regulate miRNA activity. HuR, an ELAVL protein that influences miRNA stability and activity by exporting Ago2-unbound miRNAs via extracellular vesicles, decreases in differentiating PC12 cells, whereas another ELAVL protein, HuD, increases. We found that HuD expression increases to takes up HuR’s role in miRNA export regulation, thereby aiding differentiation by modulating specific miRNAs, such as let-7a and miR-125b. HuD binds these miRNAs, reducing their activity and promoting their export, thereby supporting PC12 differentiation. This switch in miRNA export responsibility from HuR to HuD may be both necessary and sufficient for neuronal differentiation.KEYWORDS: miRNA; HuR; HuD; ELAVL proteins; PC12 cells; neuronal differentiation, miRNA export

Introduction

Post-transcriptional gene regulation is essential, and during differentiation, neuronal cells alter gene expression, necessitating precise control of protein production, which is crucial for forming neural networks and synapses [1]. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) regulate mRNA stability, translation, splicing, and degradation, allowing neurons to respond to signals and ensuring proper differentiation [2–4].

HuR and HuD are key RBPs regulating neuronal differentiation [5,6]. They belong to the Hu family and have multiple RNA recognition motifs (RRMs) that bind to AU-rich elements (AREs) in the 3′ UTRs of target mRNAs [6], thereby modulating mRNA stability, localization, and translation. HuR is a ubiquitously expressed, nuclear protein that regulates RNA splicing and post-transcriptional events. During stress, HuR translocates to the cytoplasm, stabilizing and enhancing translation of mRNAs involved in growth, proliferation, and stress responses [7]. It also relieves repression of target mRNAs by displacing repressive miRNAs from the 3′ UTR during starvation [8], thereby releasing stored mRNAs from RNA processing bodies (P-bodies), sites known for RNA storage and degradation, and recruiting them to polysomes [9]. Additionally, HuR mediates exosome- or extracellular vesicle (EV)-mediated export of specific miRNAs, such as miR-122, during nutrient deprivation, thereby adjusting cellular miRNA levels and activity in hepatic cells [10].

HuD is more neuron-specific, expressed in differentiated neurons. It stabilizes and enhances mRNA translation related to neuronal development and synaptic function [11]. Unlike HuR, which promotes growth, HuD is linked to inhibiting proliferation and promoting cell differentiation [12]. Despite structural similarities, HuR and HuD are considered to have distinct but opposing roles, vital for neuronal function [5].

This study demonstrates that HuD levels increase while HuR levels decrease during nerve growth factor-induced differentiation of PC12 cells, a model that mimics the development of sympathetic neurons [13]. HuD expression suppresses HuR expression and facilitates PC12 cell differentiation. Interestingly, in cells undergoing differentiation, we found that HuD regulates microRNAs such as let-7a and miR-125b, which are inhibitors of neuronal differentiation, by sequestering and exporting them from the cells, thereby decreasing their intracellular concentrations and promoting PC12 differentiation. These data suggest a transition in miRNA regulation during PC12 cell differentiation, in which HuD replaces HuR as the key regulator of miRNA export, a process essential for lowering activity and abundance of let-7a, a process required for PC12 differentiation.

Results

Reciprocal expression of HuD and HuR during NGF-induced differentiation of PC12 cells

While following the expression of ELAVL proteins during NGF-induced differentiation of PC12 cells, we observed an increase in HuD expression, along with an elevation in Neurofilament-L protein – a well-established marker of neuronal differentiation in PC12 cells [14] — and a decrease in HuR expression. Western blot analysis revealed a time-dependent increase in HuD protein, with significant increases observed at 24, 48, and 72 hours of differentiation (Figure 1(A-C) ; Figure S1A). Interestingly, although HuD protein levels continued to increase, the mRNA encoding HuD did not rise beyond 48 hours (Figure 1(D)). Conversely, HuR exhibited a consistent decline at both the protein and mRNA levels during differentiation (Figure 1B and D). High HuD levels in differentiated PC12 cells were cytosolic. At the same time, residual HuR remained predominantly nuclear in differentiated PC12 cells (Figure 1(E)). Withdrawal of NGF from differentiated PC12 cells, leading to dedifferentiation, resulted in decreased levels of Neurofilamanet-L and HuD proteins, along with an increase in HuR expression (Figures 1(F-G)), indicating an inverse expression pattern of HuR and HuD during differentiation and dedifferentiation. The mRNA levels of HuR and HuD also exhibited patterns similar to those of their respective proteins during NGF withdrawal for differentiated PC12 cells (72 h of NGF differentiation before NGF withdrawal for 48 and 72 h) (Figure 1(H)). These findings suggest existence of a tightly regulated inverse expression pattern of HuR and HuD that may act as a molecular switch controlling neuronal differentiation and dedifferentiation.

HuD promotes, and HuR restricts neuronal differentiation

HuR is a growth-promoting protein [15], while HuD is considered to be involved in differentiation processes [16]. Thus, reciprocal regulation of both proteins must be ensured during neuronal differentiation. We used a FLAG-HA-tagged rat HuD protein (FH-HuD) to express it in PC12 cells. We examined HuD’s role in NGF-induced differentiation by assessing the expression of key differentiation markers and neurite length in FH-HuD-expressing cells, in the absence of additional NGF. FH-HuD expression significantly increased GAP43 and Neurofilament-M mRNA levels and Neurofilament-L protein expression (Figure 2A-D), suggesting enhanced neuronal differentiation. It is accompanied by a twofold increase in endogenous HuD levels (Fig. S2). Additionally, a notable increase in neurite length relative to controls was observed in FH-HuD-expressing cells (Figure 2(C)), indicating that HuD promotes neuronal differentiation. In contrast, HA-HuR expression reduced GAP43 and Neurofilament-M mRNA levels, accompanied by decreased Neurofilament-L protein expression even in the presence of NGF (Figure 2E and G). Neurite length was also significantly reduced (Figure 2(F)), suggesting that HuR, unlike HuD, suppresses neuronal differentiation.

Further analysis showed that HuD and HuR reciprocally regulate each other during neuronal differentiation. FH-HuD expression decreased HuR levels in PC12 cells (Figure 2H-I), while their mRNA levels stayed the same (Figure 2J). However, knocking down HuD with siRNA partly increased HuR mRNA levels (Figure 2(K)). This suggests that HuD may exert negative feedback on protein stability and translation efficiency, rather than altering transcriptional control. This is consistent with our recent report suggesting that HuR mRNA is entrapped in P-bodies, whereas HuD mRNA escapes in differentiated PC12 cells [17]. Considering P-bodies are translationally silent sites for storage and degradation of mRNAs [9], this mechanism could explain the reciprocal regulation of these two proteins’ expression in differentiated PC12 cells. Interestingly, this antagonistic interaction was not seen in non-neuronal HeLa and HEK293 cells. HuD expression does not significantly alter HuR protein or mRNA levels in HEK293 and HeLa cells (Fig. S3A-B), suggesting that a cell-type-specific regulatory mechanism exists between HuR and HuD.

HuD causes neuronal differentiation by lowering miRNA activity by ‘sponging’ and exporting repressive miRNAs from differentiating neuronal cells

Previous studies have shown that neuronal differentiation in PC12 cells is accompanied by a decline in let-7a activity, which is both necessary and sufficient for the upregulation of the target gene KRAS, a key regulator of differentiation-related signalling pathways [18,19]. In the NGF-withdrawn condition, let-7a activity lowering is crucial for protecting against neuronal cell death [19]. Interestingly, an in situ mRNA localization study using Renilla luciferase reporter mRNA-specific probes [20] detected P-body-localized let-7a-repressed mRNAs that are released from P-bodies during differentiation, supporting a reversal of let-7a repressive activity and consistent with previous data on let-7a inactivation during differentiation (Figure 3(A)).

Consistent with these findings, we observed a significant reduction in let-7a activity upon FH-HuD expression. Luciferase reporter assays using constructs expressing Renilla luciferase mRNA containing three bulged let-7a binding sites revealed decreased reporter mRNA repression in FH-HuD-expressing cells, indicating diminished let-7a activity as it also happens with NGF-induced differentiation of PC12 cells (Figure 3(B, C)). This coincided with a marked increase in KRAS mRNA -a target of let-7a (Figure 3(B, D)), supporting the idea that HuD suppresses let-7a function to upregulate differentiation-promoting genes.

To assess if this effect also applied to other inhibitory miRNAs, we examined miR-125b levels, another crucial regulator of neuronal differentiation. miR-125b indirectly suppresses neurofilament L, whose expression increases in differentiated PC12 cells, indicating potential impairment of miR-125b function [21]. Notably, expression of FH-HuD led to a similar downregulation of both let-7a and miR-125b miRNAs (Figure 3E). Interestingly, a comparable effect was observed in non-neuronal HEK293 cells, where FH-HuD expression led to a significant reduction in both the expression and activity of let-7a (Figure 3F). Collectively, these findings suggest that HuD can modulate miRNA levels independent of cell types.

Lowering of miRNA activity and levels can be achieved via extracellular export of miRNAs [10,22]. We isolated extracellular vesicles (EVs) from control and differentiated PC12 cells following the established protocol [23]used for isolation of EVs from hepatic and macrophage cell culture supernatant and found no significant change in EV size but a moderate decrease in EV number with PC12 cells differentiation (Figure 4(A)). However, the miRNA content in EVs derived from NGF-differentiated cells(+NGF) increased compared to non-differentiated cells (−NGF) while the Flotilin-1, a EV-marker protein, levels in EVs isolated did not get altered (Figure 4(A–C)) . How is miRNA export achieved in differentiating cells? HuR is known to bind to candidate miRNAs by sponging them from Ago2 and facilitating their extracellular export via EVs, thereby promoting loss of miRNA activity, as noted in hepatic and macrophage cells [10,24]. But HuR, a regulator for let-7a miRNA [24,25], was found to be reduced in differentiated PC12 cells (Figure 1(B, C)). Interestingly, we observed HuD’s association with let-7a and miR-125b in cells expressing FH-HuD by analysis of RNA isolated from immunoprecipitated FH-HuD from differentiated PC12 cells (Figure 4(D-E)). In FH-HuD-expressing PC12 cells, we also observed an increase in miRNA export via EVs. Extracellular vesicles or EVs were isolated from cells transfected with pCI-neo (control) or FH-HuD expression plasmid, and confirmed for EV-marker protein Flotillin-1 (Figure 4(F)). qRT-PCR analysis revealed a significant increase in the level of let-7a and miR-125b in EVs following FH-HuD expression (Figure 4(F)). These results suggest that HuD promotes the extracellular export of let-7a and miR-125b, contributing to their decreased levels and repressive activities observed in FH-HuD-expressing PC12 cells. To confirm that the decrease in cellular miRNA levels was contributed by EV-mediated export, we treated PC12 cells with GW4869, an inhibitor of exosome/EV production. In both control and FH-HuD-expressing cells, GW4869 treatment resulted in a significant accumulation of let-7a and miR-125b within the cells (Figure 4(G)), indicating that their extracellular export depends on extracellular vesicle or exosome biogenesis, which is affected by GW4869, a chemical compound known for its ability to inhibit neutral sphingomyelinase (N-SMase) activity, an enzyme essential for Exosome biogenesis [26]. As FH-HuD-driven miRNA reduction was rescued by GW4869, these data suggest that HuD-mediated miRNA control operates through an EV-mediated export pathway that can be inhibited by GW4869. Thus, HuD, by binding specific miRNAs, helps their export via EVs, promoting PC12 cell differentiation. HuD facilitates the removal of let-7a from the cytoplasm, thereby relieving repression of targets such as KRAS. Thus, the miRNA export function of ELAVL proteins switches with neuronal differentiation, and ELAVL4 HuD plays a critical role in controlling miRNA export, obviating the need for canonical ELAVL1 HuR as a miRNA export regulator in differentiated PC12 cells.

Discussion

During neuronal differentiation, cells transit from proliferative progenitors to fully developed, polarized neurons with specialized structures, such as dendrites and axons [27]. This process involves changes in gene regulation mediated by RNA-binding proteins and miRNAs. These proteins control mRNA stability and translation by recruiting RNA-degrading or stabilizing factors. ELAVL1, or HuR, regulates mRNA stability, translation, and responds to stress, also affecting miRNA activity in stressed hepatic cells. Neuronal homologs HuB, C, D likely have similar roles in mRNA regulation, but their specific functions and effects on miRNAs in neurons remain unclear.

Although HuR and HuD share structural features, they have opposing roles in neuronal differentiation. HuR, ubiquitously expressed, promotes cell growth, proliferation, and stress responses by stabilizing target mRNAs and preventing miRNA-mediated repression [7]. Under stress, HuR relocates from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, where it modulates translation and exports specific miRNAs via exosomes or EVs in non-neuronal cells [9,10,28]. Conversely, HuD, primarily expressed in neurons, increases during differentiation, supporting neuronal maturation by stabilizing mRNAs involved in synaptogenesis and neural identity [11]. HuD increased while HuR decreased during PC12 cell differentiation, indicating a reciprocal switch that ensures their coordinated regulation during neuronal differentiation.

HuD promotes differentiation by reducing inhibitory miRNAs, such as let-7a and miR-125b, through exosome or EV export, thereby lifting repression. During PC12 differentiation, RNA export shifts with lower HuR and higher HuD. Unlike HuR, HuD is primarily cytoplasmic and facilitates miRNA export upon expression (Figure 5). The spectrum of miRNAs regulated by HuD remains elusive, and how HuD binds them for packaging into EVs remains unknown. For HuR, ubiquitination targets miRNAs for endosomal sorting, but the role of HuD’s post-translational modifications is unclear for its miRNA bindings and export function in differentiating neuronal cells. It’s also unknown if HuD interacts with Ago-miRNPs to displace miRNAs. Investigating HuD-miRNA binding defects and their impact on neuronal differentiation, as well as the roles of ELAVL2 and ELAVL3, remains important.

HuD-driven miRNA export in differentiated PC12 cells regulates miRNA activity, promoting differentiation. NGF-induced differentiation or ectopic HuD expression in non-differentiated PC12 cells increases miRNA export. By binding to miRNAs, HuD promotes their export via extracellular vesicles, a process influenced by export regulatory proteins like HuD. In differentiating PC12 cells, HuD represses HuR, another miRNA export regulator, thereby controlling miRNA activity and potentially regulating gene expression during cell differentiation.

Export of miRNA is anticipated to happen in neurons, but no mechanism or controller proteins for miRNA export have been reported in controlling neuronal miRNA export. The recent development of in vitro miRNA loading into endosomes has been established, and this assay may be useful for uncovering several interesting aspects of HuD-driven miRNA export in future studies involving recombinant HuD protein and exploring HuD-driven regulation of miRNA export in the context of neurodegenerative diseases [23]. Recent reports suggest HuD’s possible role in augmenting Alzheimer’s Disease, where conditional knockdown of HuD relieves symptoms of AD in a mouse model [29]. HuD may influence neurodegeneration and inflammation by regulating the export of specific miRNAs.

PC12 cells, which mimic sympathetic neurons, serve as an excellent model for studying neuronal differentiation. However, they have limitations, including the absence of precise excitatory and synaptic behaviours typical of neurons, despite exhibiting neurite growth and related phenomena characteristic of neurogenesis from precursor cells in the mammalian brain [30,31]. Therefore, they are considered a valuable ex vivo model for investigating neurodegeneration and screening for factors that might prevent the harmful effects of neurodegeneration-inducing agents, such as amyloid proteins [30]. Given HuD’s role in PC12 differentiation and its neuron-specific expression, it is likely that HuD has similar miRNA regulatory functions in neurons that need to be tested in a more physiological context. The specificity of miRNA interactions, along with possible export regulatory partners such as STX5 or RalA [23,32], endosomal maturation, and HuD-mediated miRNA loading control, all warrant further investigation. It is also crucial to determine whether HuD associates reversibly with miRNAs prior to their loading into endosomes. High-throughput RNA sequencing of HuD-associated RNAs and EV-associated miRNAs is essential for identifying candidate miRNAs, non-coding RNAs, and mRNAs regulated by HuD in neurons, thereby elucidating the possible physiological significance of these regulations. In pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours, lower HuD expression is associated with worse prognosis, higher tumour grade, and increased angioinvasion. It is also connected to cell dysfunction in type 2 diabetes as its expression gets altered in β–cells [33,34]. Exploring HuD’s miRNA regulatory role in pancreatic cancer cells could provide further insights and future therapeutic leads.

The connection between HuD expression and HuR downregulation is a noteworthy aspect discussed in this manuscript. Understanding how HuD and HuR reciprocally regulate each other during the differentiation and non-differentiation of PC12 cells is vital and warrants detailed study. Using reporter mRNAs with the 3’ UTR of each gene could clarify whether the stability of HuR or HuD mRNAs – closely aligned with protein levels – is influenced by the changes observed. Additionally, identifying cis-regulatory elements and trans-acting miRNAs or proteins that drive reciprocal expression of HuR and HuD is crucial. This could illuminate the mechanisms underlying the differential regulation of HuD and HuR mRNAs. Our recent work indicates that HuR and HuD mRNAs are reciprocally targeted to RNA processing bodies (P-bodies or PBs) during PC12 cell differentiation, suggesting that PB targeting may reciprocally regulate HuR and HuD mRNAs and may account for lower HuR levels in differentiating PC12 cells [17]. The observed relocalization of repressed let-7a reporter mRNAs from PBs detected by in situ hybridization supports the idea that increased P-body localization of HuR mRNA may account for downregulation of its expression (Figure 3A). Thus, PB-escape of HuD mRNA and PB-localization of HuR mRNA could explain their opposing expression patterns, likely controlled by RNA-binding proteins and miRNAs regulating PB-entry of HuR mRNAs in differentiating PC12 cells.

Experimental procedures and reagents

Cell culture

PC12 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% heat-inactivated horse serum, 5% FBS, and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. HeLa and HEK293 cells were grown in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. PC12 cells were differentiated with 100 ng/mL NGF in medium containing DMEM, 0.75% horse serum, and 0.25% FBS for 72 hours, then dedifferentiated with anti-NGF antibody (1:100, Sigma).

Cell transfection

All cell types were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) per the manufacturer’s instructions. For immunoprecipitation, 2 µg of plasmid DNA was transfected into cells in 60 mm dishes. For Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and RNA-FISH, 500 ng of plasmid was used in 12-well plates. Luciferase assays involved co-transfecting 20 ng of RL reporter and 200 ng of Firefly plasmids per well in 24-well plates. Transfection lasted 6 hours, then media was replaced. After 24 hours, cells were passaged to larger surfaces. For siRNA transfection, RNAi Max reagent was used, and a 20 nM siRNA mix (Dharmacon, ON-TARGETplus SmartPool Rat Elavl4 siRNA; L-108537-01–0005) was transfected for 72 h in a 12-well cell culture format before the cells were used for RNA and protein quantification [17].

Western blot, Immunofluorescence and Immunoprecipitation

Samples mixed with 5X loading buffer and heated at 95°C for 10 minutes. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 3% BSA in 1X TBST for 1 hour, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After TBST washes, membranes were treated with HRP-secondary antibodies [1: 8000] for 1–1.5 hours at room temperature, washed again, and detected with chemiluminescent substrates. Imaging was done using UVP BioImager 600 and VisionWorks software (version 6.80).

Fior immunofluorescence, cells on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 minutes, followed by three washes. Permeabilization and blocking used PBS with 10% goat serum, 20% BSA, and 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 minutes. Coverslips incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies in PBS with 20% BSA. After three PBS washes, secondary antibodies with Alexa Fluor were added and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. Excess was washed away with PBS. Samples were mounted with Vectashield containing DAPI.

For Immunoprecipitation, Cells were lysed in buffer with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 0.5% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1× PMSF, and RNase inhibitor, then incubated 30 min at 4°C. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 3,000 xg for 15 min at 4°C and incubated overnight with antibody-bound protein G agarose beads (Invitrogen). The next day, beads were washed three times with IP buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, RNase inhibitor). Beads were divided for RNA and protein extraction, then analysed by qRT-PCR and Western blot, respectively.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol or TRIzol LS (Invitrogen). mRNA and miRNA levels were quantified via two-step qRT-PCR. For mRNA, 200 ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed using the Eurogentec Reverse Transcriptase Core Kit and amplified with MESA GREEN qPCR Master Mix. For miRNA, 200 ng RNA was analysed with TaqMan miRNA-specific primers (Invitrogen), using equal amount of FH-HuD-associated and exosomal miRNAs. Data analysis used the comparative Ct method, with GAPDH and U6 snRNA as internal controls. Western blots validated the presence of FH-HuD in IP and EV samples. qPCR was performed on Bio-Rad CFX96™ and Applied Biosystems 7500. Reverse transcription: 25°C for 10 min, 48°C for 30 min, 95°C for 5 min. qPCR: initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, then 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 sec and 60°C for 1 min.

Luciferase assay

miRNA repression was assessed via dual-luciferase reporter assay. Cells were lysed with passive lysis buffer (Promega) and analysed with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System on a VICTOR X3 plate reader (PerkinElmer). Renilla luciferase values were normalized to firefly luciferase to determine repression levels.

EV isolation and characterization

EVs were isolated using a modified protocol based on a previous study [36]. Cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% EV-depleted horse serum, 5% EV-depleted FBS, and 1% Pen-Strep. EVs were removed from FBS by ultracentrifugation at 100,000xg for 5 hours at 4°C. Conditioned media was centrifuged at 2,000 xg for 15 min and 10,000xg for 30 min, then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The supernatant was layered onto a 30% sucrose gradient and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 xg for 90 min (SW41Ti, Beckman). The EV layer was collected, washed with PBS, and pelleted again at 100,000 xg for 90 min. The pellet was resuspended in PBS. For characterization, EVs were diluted 1:10 in PBS and analysed with a Nanosight NS-300 for particle count and size distribution.

In situ hybridization

Cy3 Labelling of Oligos was performed following published procedures [17]. The oligos were obtained from Eurogentec, having sequences as follows: RL 1: 5’aT*cacaaagatgatT*ttctttggaaggtT*ca; RL 2: 5’aT*tagctggaggcagcgT*taccatgcagaaa; and RL 3: 5’aT*agtccagcacgtT*catttgcttgcagT*ga.for RNA FISH, Cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. After three 5-min washes with PBS, the coverslips were permeabilized with 70% alcohol overnight at 4°C. Cells were rehydrated with 25% Formamide and 2X SSC at room temperature for 10 min, then incubated with hybridization solution containing 10% Dextran Sulphate, 2 mM Vanadyl Ribonucleoside Complex, 0.02% RNase-free BSA, 40 μg Salmon Sperm DNA, 2X SSC, 25% Formamide, 30 ng probe at 37°C overnight in a humid chamber. After two washes with 2X SSC containing 20% Formamide for 15 min each at 37°C, coverslips were mounted with Vectashield with DAPI.

Image capture and post-capture image processing

All confocal imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope, and image processing and analysis were carried out using Imaris 7 software, developed by Bitplane AG. The neurite length of differentiated PC12 cells was measured in micrometres (µm) using Zen software.

Statistical analysis

All graphs and statistical analyses were conducted using Prism software (v5.00 and v8.00; GraphPad, San Diego, CA). Nonparametric two-tailed unpaired and paired t-tests were used for statistical evaluation. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant, whereas P values greater than 0.05 were deemed not necessary. Error bars represent the means ± standard deviation (SD).
