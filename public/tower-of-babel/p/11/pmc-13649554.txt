An SP110–SP100 axis is a critical regulator of promyelocytic leukaemia body dynamics and mitotic fidelity
Authors: Eric J. Aird, Julius Rabl, Tabea Knuesel, Kevin Groen, Samah W. Awwad, Boris Korablev, Lynn Scherpe, Waleed Al-Herz, Robin Hupfer, Mike Recher, Stephen P. Jackson, Benjamin G. Hale, Jacob E. Corn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stimulation of the innate immune system by foreign RNA elicits a potent interferon response and can trigger cell death. The mechanisms by which cells balance a robust response with cell-intrinsic lethality are still being uncovered. Here, using genome-wide CRISPR–Cas9 genetic screens with triphosphorylated RNA stimulation, we discover that promyelocytic leukaemia (PML) nuclear body-localized speckled protein 110 (SP110) is a potent inhibitor of type 1 interferon-driven cell death. Death suppression by SP110 counteracts a toxic activity of SP100, a major constituent of PML bodies. Loss of SP110 leads to mitotic retention of SP100 and PML bodies, which associate with and perturb segregating chromosomes, leading to micronucleus formation, DNA damage and genotoxic cell death. A combination of cryo-electron microscopy, AlphaFold modelling and cellular biochemistry reveals that SP110 dissolves toxic SP100 oligomers via necessary and sufficient direct interactions between their caspase activation and recruitment domains. These data reveal the critical roles of SP100 and SP110 in governing the disassembly of PML bodies during mitosis, as well as the repercussions if this process is misregulated.

Main

The innate immune system guards against foreign pathogens and regulates cancer growth1,2. Both foreign and host molecules, including nucleic acids3, can stimulate an innate response and elicit the interferon signalling cascade leading to the upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs). ISGs perform downstream functions including direct antipathogen activity1, stimulating the production of inflammatory cytokines4 and remodelling liquid–liquid phase-separated condensates to reinforce signalling cascades5–7. An estimated 10% of the human genome consists of ISGs, many of which are poorly characterized8,9.

Cell death is one intrinsic response to innate immune signalling, and hyperactivation or alterations to ISGs can disrupt the balance between sufficient innate immunity signalling and toxicity10–12. For example, insufficient amounts of cytosolic stress granule condensate proteins G3BP1/2 result in excessive inflammation and cell death in response to cytosolic DNA13. Precise mechanisms that balance the cellular response to innate immune stimulation with cell death are poorly described, especially in the context of large intracellular structures such as biomolecular condensates.

5′ triphosphorylated RNA (ppp-RNA) is a potent activator of ISG expression through recognition by diverse factors14–16, and ppp-RNA-induced interferon signalling leads to moderate amounts of intrinsic cell death17,18. Functional genomics has investigated genes that shape cellular fitness in response to various pathogens19,20. These studies yielded potential insights into interferon responses but were also confounded by factors such as viral entry and replication. A better understanding of the mechanisms that balance an appropriate cellular response to a molecularly defined innate immune stimulus with cell death could lead to novel biochemical insights and approaches to treating disease.

Using multiple genome-wide CRISPR inhibition (CRISPRi) screens with ppp-RNA as a stimulus, we discover that promyelocytic leukaemia (PML) body-localized speckled protein 110 (SP110) is a potent negative regulator of interferon signalling and downstream cell toxicity. This is mediated by an inhibitory direct interaction with PML body-localized SP100, whose interferon-induced upregulation is sufficient to trigger genotoxicity and cell death. Surprisingly, we find that balanced upregulation of SP110 in the face of interferon-induced SP100 expression is necessary for the normal mitotic dissolution of PML bodies. Failure to balance the SP110–SP100 axis leads to the accumulation of mitotically retained PML body/SP100 on segregating chromosomes, resulting in chromosome mis-segregation, micronucleus formation and lethal DNA damage. A cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of SP100 reveals that it forms homomeric oligomers, which are necessary and sufficient for cell death, via its caspase activation and recruitment domain (CARD). AlphaFold modelling and cellular biochemistry highlight the atomic details by which the necessary and sufficient CARD of SP110 disassembles the SP100 oligomers, enabling PML body dissolution. The balance of SP100 and SP110 expression is relevant during interferon signalling but also regulates appropriate PML body dissolution in non-immune contexts. Our findings together reveal a regulatory partnership between SP110 and SP100 that maintains cell health by appropriately remodelling biomolecular condensates.

Results

SP110 loss confers hypersensitivity to interferon stimulation

Our laboratory and others have reported that electroporation of triphosphorylated guide RNAs used in CRISPR genome editing elicits a remarkably strong interferon response14,15. Using a triphosphorylated guide RNA as a molecularly defined model for potent ppp-RNA stimulation, we sought to identify factors that regulate the balance between ISG signalling and cell death. We used a genome-wide CRISPRi screen to individually knock down (KD) the expression of each human gene and assess the impact on cellular fitness upon ppp-RNA treatment (Fig. 1a). A combination of statistical metrics (Methods) identified hundreds of genes that were significantly enriched or depleted in the ppp-RNA-treated condition relative to mock-treated cells (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1 and 2). Among these were several genes known to be essential for type 1 interferon signalling such as IFNAR1/2, STAT2 and TYK2. Guide RNAs targeting these genes were enriched in the ppp-RNA-treated cells, presumably due to an inability to propagate an interferon response leading to a selective fitness advantage. RNA sensors of the DExD-box helicase (DDX) family such as RIGI (DDX58) and DDX5622 were also enriched in treated cells, with the lower effect sizes reflecting their known redundancy in recognizing ppp-RNA. Conversely, negative regulators of interferon signalling such as USP18 and ISG15 were significantly depleted in the treated condition. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) reinforced that the screen accurately identified factors involved in innate immune signalling (Extended Data Fig. 1a).

Among the top depleted genes was SP110, one of four human speckled proteins (SPs) localized to phase-separated nuclear PML bodies that are generally linked to modulating the response to pathogen infections and regulating chromatin structure23–25. We individually validated the top depleted genes from the screen using a growth competition assay (Fig. 1c) and confirmed that SP110 KD rendered cells extraordinarily hypersensitive to ppp-RNA treatment, to a similar extent as known negative regulators of the interferon response such as USP18 (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1b–d). We also generated isogenic SP110-knockout (KO) clones and verified that these phenocopy CRISPRi KD (Extended Data Fig. 1e). Innate immune hypersensitivity of SP110-KO cells was rescued through complementation of full-length SP110 via lentiviral overexpression (Fig. 1e), confirming the observed lethality is specific to loss of SP110.

We tested whether SP110 is directly involved in single-strand RNA sensing but instead found that bypassing single-strand RNA recognition with poly(I:C), poly(dA:dT) or recombinant interferon β (IFNβ) addition also induced cell death in SP110-KO cells (Fig. 1f). We established that IFNβ-mediated cell death is independent of TP53 status and relevant in multiple cell types, as RPE1 SP110−/−; TP53+/+ cells and Jurkat SP110−/− cells were hypersensitized to death caused by interferon stimulation (Fig. 1g and Extended Data Fig. 1f–h). In addition, primary T lymphoblasts isolated from SP110-deficient patients (SP110 c.642delC) with veno-occlusive disease with immunodeficiency (VODI)26 exhibited augmented interferon-stimulated cell death relative to a healthy donor control (Fig. 1h and Extended Data Fig. 1i). Thus, SP110 is a potent negative regulator of interferon-induced cell death that acts downstream of ppp-RNA sensing. The strength of this effect and the unclear mechanistic basis led us to further investigate how SP110 protects against cell death.

SP110 loss drives SP100 interferon susceptibility

To discover which protein(s) or pathway(s) promote interferon-stimulated toxicity upon SP110 loss, we performed a synthetic viability CRISPRi screen in RPE1 SP110−/−;TP53−/− cells (Extended Data Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1 and 2). As in TP53−/− RPE1 cells (indicated throughout as WT), STAT2 and TYK2 KD desensitized SP110-KO cells to ppp-RNA treatment, consistent with an SP110-independent effect (Fig. 2a). However, we found that KD of SP100 was strongly protective against ppp-RNA treatment in SP110-KO cells without conferring a growth advantage to WT cells (Fig. 2a). SP100 is a major constituent of SP110-containing PML bodies and, like SP110, is induced by interferon signalling23. We also noticed that KD of PML partially rescued the IFNβ hypersensitivity of SP110-KO cells (Fig. 2a). Competition assays with individual KDs validated that positive-scoring genes (IFNAR2 and SP100) rescued toxicity of stimulated SP110-KO cells to varying degrees and negative-scoring genes (USP18) exacerbated toxicity (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 2b). We further confirmed that SP100 loss rescues SP110-induced interferon-mediated cell death through the generation of SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 1e). We then performed rescue experiments in this dual-KO background. Reintroduction of SP100 was sufficient to recapitulate interferon-induced cell death (Fig. 2d). Surprisingly, we also found that overexpression of SP100 was sufficient to induce cell death in the absence of IFNβ stimulation. Even in SP110-proficient cells, overexpression of SP100 induced lethality (Extended Data Fig. 2d). The magnitude of cell death in WT cells was lower than in SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells, implying that the balance between SP100 and SP110 expression is linked to the degree of toxicity. We explored this by titrating IFNβ in SP110-KO cells and observed dose-dependent toxicity (Extended Data Fig. 2e). We furthermore found that the basal expression of SP110 and SP100 are highly correlated (r = 0.69) across 1,474 cell lines in the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE)27 (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g). Indeed, the SP100 expression shows a stronger correlation with SP110 across all CCLE lines than with any other transcript (Extended Data Fig. 2h). SP110 and SP100 are broadly expressed across cell and tissue types in contrast to another SP, SP140, whose expression is largely lymphoid restricted (Extended Data Fig. 2i). Because SP110 and SP100 are both co-induced by interferon stimulation, our data suggest that SP110 levels are regulated to prevent cell death resulting from the toxic activity of SP100.

SP100 toxicity is not dependent on transcriptional or viral restriction functions

SP110 and SP100 are both frequently associated with chromatin function23, so we assessed the genomic localization of endogenous SP100 in WT and SP110-KO cells. Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) confirmed that SP100 is predominately localized to promoters in WT cells as previously characterized28 (Extended Data Fig. 3a–e). However, in SP110-KO cells, SP100 no longer showed specific enrichment at promoters and instead displayed a diffuse ChIP-seq signal (Extended Data Fig. 3a,c,e,f). SP100 still preserved overall chromatin association in SP110-KO cells as in WT (Extended Data Fig. 3g). These data demonstrated that SP100’s precise chromatin positioning depends upon SP110, but its DNA binding capacity is independent of SP110.

Due to its promoter localization, we initially hypothesized that SP100-induced cell death might be caused by a dysregulated interferon response. However, using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to assess global transcriptomic changes in stimulated WT, SP110-KO and SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells, we observed few significant changes in expression (56 genes with Padj < 0.05) between the single- and double-knockout cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Differentially expressed genes in WT versus SP110-KO and WT versus SP110;SP100 dual-KO were frequently common between the conditions (Extended Data Fig. 4b,c). Transcriptome-wide, there was no interferon signature present among differentially expressed genes in either SP110-KO or SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells when compared with WT cells (Extended Data Fig. 4d–f). All three cell types mounted an equal interferon-induced transcriptional response as measured by upregulation of common ISGs (Extended Data Fig. 4g), including RIGI, whose promoter is bound by SP100 in WT cells (Extended Data Fig. 3e). The interferon hypersensitive SP110-KO cells showed no evidence of ISG hyperactivation, and the interferon-resistant SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells showed no evidence of ISG dampening. Combined with the chromatin binding data (Extended Data Fig. 3), these results suggest that the transcriptional functions of SP100 do not underlie its toxic effects in RPE1 cells.

SP100 has described roles as a viral restriction factor29,30, and we sought to elucidate whether viral infection plays a role in the observed SP110/SP100 toxicity. We infected A549 cells (WT, SP110-KO, SP100-KO and SP110;SP100 dual-KO) with three different viruses: H5N1 influenza A virus, herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) and vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) (Extended Data Fig. 5a). While we observed increased cell death in infected SP110-KO cells with all three viruses, this was not rescued in SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells (Extended Data Fig. 5b–d). We also quantified viral replication and observed minimal differences with H5N1 or HSV-1 replication regardless of SP110 or SP100 expression status (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). VSV replication was reduced in the absence of SP110, but this was not dependent on SP100 (Extended Data Fig. 5d). In summary, these data indicated that SP100-dependent cellular toxicity is independent of transcriptional regulation and viral replication restriction in our studied cellular contexts, suggesting unexplored cellular functions of SP100 and SP110.

Ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM) signalling, activated during double-strand break formation31, was upregulated in IFNβ-treated SP110-KO cells using phosphorylated KAP1 as a marker (Fig. 3d). As with cell death, SP100 overexpression was sufficient to induce ATM signalling even in the absence of IFNβ (Extended Data Fig. 6c). ATM signalling was not upregulated in the SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells, demonstrating that DNA damage is SP100 dependent (Fig. 3d). By contrast, we observed no ATR signalling (via CHK1 phosphorylation), which is a marker of replication stress31 (Fig. 3e), nor did SP110-KO lead to an increase in R-loops formed during transcription–replication conflicts and replication stress (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 6d,e).

Loss of cyclin B1 and activation of p53 are linked with excessive DNA damage, cell cycle arrest and the induction of cell death32,33. We detected an absence of cyclin B1 in interferon-stimulated SP110-KO cells (Fig. 3d) and when overexpressing SP100 (Extended Data Fig. 6c). We measured p53 activation using CDKN1A (p21) expression as a marker and again found upregulation only in SP110-KO cells stimulated with IFNβ (Extended Data Fig. 6f). No p21 upregulation was measured in WT or SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells, consistent with the observed DNA damage phenotype.

Micronuclei can be cause or consequence of DNA damage. We found that preventing cycling of SP110-KO cells rescued the formation of both micronuclei and 53BP1 foci to WT levels (Fig. 3g,h). The observed genotoxic DNA damage is therefore dependent on mitosis, suggesting a link to chromosome segregation. The SP100-induced micronuclei contained only acentric chromosome fragments, ruling out mitotic spindle failure or kinetochore damage as the cause (Extended Data Fig. 6g). We therefore examined the dynamics of SP100 and PML bodies during mitotic chromosome segregation.

In WT and SP110-KO cells, SP100 and PML colocalized in interphase cells but were mostly diffuse during mitosis (Fig. 3i). This is consistent with the known mitotic disassembly of PML bodies34. We verified that SP100 is not relocalized or degraded during mitosis (Extended Data Fig. 6h). Remarkably, treatment of SP110-KO cells with IFNβ led to mitotic retention of SP100 and PML foci, although they do not exclusively colocalize (Fig. 3i). We also observed that SP100 preferentially bound to abnormally lagging chromosomes, forming prominent anaphase bridges (Fig. 3j). SP100 was retained on nearly 100% of mitotic SP110-KO cells stimulated with IFNβ (Fig. 3k). We also found IFNβ-induced micronuclei formation and mitotic retention of SP100 and PML in SP110-KO A549 lung carcinoma (Extended Data Fig. 7a–c) and U2OS osteosarcoma cells (Extended Data Fig. 7d–g), indicating that this phenotype is conserved across multiple cell types.

We observed strong colocalization of 53BP1 and PML foci in interphase IFNβ-treated SP110-KO cells (rmedian = 0.68), further indicating that DNA damage following dysfunctional chromosome separation is coupled to the retention of PML bodies (Extended Data Fig. 8a–c). Etoposide-treated cells exhibited less overlap between 53BP1 and PML bodies than IFNβ-treated SP110-KO cells (rmedian = 0.53; Extended Data Fig. 8d–f), showing that PML colocalization is not a general property of DNA damage. This suggested to us that retention of SP100-containing PML bodies was causative of chromosome lag, followed by micronucleus formation and chromosome shattering. Genetic or chemical perturbation with As2O3 of PML in SP110-KO cells abrogated IFNβ-induced SP100 retention (Extended Data Fig. 8g). Moreover, KD of PML or As2O3 treatment was sufficient to rescue IFNβ-induced cell death in SP110-KO cells (Extended Data Fig. 8h–j). Hence, the opposing activities of SP110 and SP100 are upstream and causative of dysregulated PML body dynamics, which is the eventual cause of cell toxicity. In summary, we found that loss of SP110 led to the inappropriate mitotic retention of SP100-localized PML bodies on segregating DNA, which caused chromosome mis-segregation, genotoxicity and cell death.

We quantified both the number and size of PML bodies with and without interferon stimulation in interphase cells. While PML body number and size were relatively consistent in unstimulated cells, we observed striking differences in IFNβ-treated cells (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 9a). PML body dynamics are regulated by SUMOylation35,36, and SP100 possesses both a SUMO-interacting motif (SIM) and a SUMO acceptor residue (K297)37. We asked how these molecular features regulate SP100-induced genotoxicity. In complementation experiments in SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells, overexpression of SP100 ΔSIM failed to induce lethality and displayed diffuse nuclear localization (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 9b). By contrast, SUMO-deficient SP100-K297R elicited increased toxicity relative to WT or mutation of the neighbouring SP100-K298R (Fig. 4b). We also noted that SP110 appears as a top hit in a genome-wide CRISPR screen for increased sensitivity to a pan-SUMO inhibitor (TAK-981), although this was not mechanistically characterized38. These results suggested that un-SUMOylated SP100 drives toxicity in SP110-KO cells.

We validated that broadly inhibiting SUMOylation with TAK-981 leads to cell death specifically in SP110-KO cells relative to WT cells, even in the absence of interferon stimulation (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 9c). TAK-981 did not induce a robust type 1 interferon response or SP100 upregulation in RPE1 cells (Extended Data Fig. 9d). Notably, TAK-981 toxicity was significantly abrogated in SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells (Fig. 4c). Moreover, perturbation of PML bodies with As2O3 in SP110-KO cells even mitigated the increased toxicity caused by combined treatment with IFNβ and TAK-981. TAK-981 treatment was also sufficient to induce mitotic retention of SP100 on lagging chromosomes in SP110-KO cells but not WT cells, and this was further exacerbated by IFNβ treatment (Fig. 4d). Taken together, these data demonstrated that SP100 SUMOylation is required for dissolution of SP100-containing PML bodies during mitosis, and that SP110 is critical to prevent the accumulation of enlarged PML bodies and toxic, un-SUMOylated SP100 that results in chromosome segregation errors.

The SP110 CARD prevents SP100 CARD-induced lethality

All four speckled proteins contain C-terminal chromatin reader domains—Sp100, AIRE-1, NucP41/75, DEAF-1 (SAND), plant homeobox domain (PHD) and bromodomain (BRD)—that enhance their transcriptional regulatory potential, as well as an N-terminal CARD multimerization domain, for which no specific function has been attributed23. We generated domain deletion SP110 cDNAs for stable lentiviral re-expression in SP110-KO cells and tested their ability to rescue IFNβ-induced cell death (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 10a). In line with the lack of a transcriptional phenotype (Extended Data Fig. 4), the PHD, BRD and SAND domains were dispensable. Instead, SP110’s CARD was necessary to rescue cell toxicity (Fig. 5b). Moreover, expression of the SP110 CARD domain alone was sufficient to rescue cell death to a level comparable with full-length SP110. The SP CARDs have high sequence homology23, but we found that only the SP110 CARD could prevent interferon-induced toxicity (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 10b). Using confocal imaging, we found that the SP110 CARD is also required for nuclear focus formation (Extended Data Fig. 10c). We applied the same structure–function analysis to SP100 in SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells, testing the ability of SP100 constructs to induce cell death (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 10d). Like SP110, we found that PHD, BRD and SAND domains of SP100 were dispensable for cell death, but the CARD was necessary (Fig. 5e). Strikingly, the SP100 CARD alone was sufficient to induce cell death, an inverse of protection with the SP110 CARD alone. Like the SP110 CARD, SP100 localization to nuclear foci required its CARD (Extended Data Fig. 10e). We substituted other SP CARDs into SP100 and found they did not induce the same degree of toxicity as SP100’s CARD, except for SP140L, which has an 85% sequence homology due to a partial gene duplication of the SP100 CARD in primates39 (Fig. 5f and Extended Data Fig. 10f). In summary, SP100 toxicity relies solely on its CARD domain and is independent of its chromatin-binding domains.

CARDs are often involved in protein–protein interactions to form homo- and hetero-CARD–CARD oligomers40. We screened for interactors of SP110’s CARD using immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP–MS) with stably expressed full-length SP110, SP110 ΔCARD and SP110 CARD alone in SP110-KO cells. Only three proteins were enriched with full-length SP110: BAG2, the SENP7 deSUMOylase and SP100 (Fig. 5g). Strikingly, SP100 was enriched with full-length SP110 and CARD only, but not ΔCARD, suggesting a heteromeric CARD–CARD interaction. No other CARD-containing proteins, including other SPs or caspases, were enriched in any condition. We used co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) and western blotting to confirm that SP110 and SP100 interact with each other, and that this interaction strongly depends on the CARD domain of each protein (Fig. 5h,i). Overall, our data reveal that the respective protective and cell killing activities of SP110 and SP100 are mediated by their CARD domains, that these proteins bind one another through a CARD–CARD interaction and that loss of this interaction renders cells hypersensitive to interferon signalling.

SP110 modulates oligomerization of SP100 CARD

CARD domains can form heteromeric interactions with other CARDs and homo-oligomeric assemblies with themselves41–43. To explore the oligomeric state of the SP100 and SP110 CARDs, we recombinantly expressed and purified each CARD. Using negative staining and imaging by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), we observed that the SP100 CARD forms multimicrometre-long homo-oligomers in vitro (Fig. 6a). The SP100 CARD homo-oligomers potentially represented the toxic species that led to PML body enlargement and mitotic retention. Therefore, we used cryo-EM to solve a 3.5-Å structure and obtain atomic insights into its mechanism (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1). The SP100 CARD oligomer is composed of two antiparallel strands that form a double helix (Fig. 6b). The oligomer is 65 Å wide with a 10 Å central cavity and a twist length of 120 Å. Each SP100 CARD, consisting of six alpha helices, has two interaction interfaces in cis on the same strand and one interaction interface in trans to the opposite strand (Fig. 5c). We stably expressed SP100 with alanine point mutations at each binding interface in SP110;SP100 dual-KO cells. Each single interface mutation was sufficient to completely prevent lethality to a similar extent as complete SP100 KO, whereas a mutation outside of the interface still induced cell death (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 3h,i). Therefore, the SP100 homo-oligomeric CARD interactions contribute to cellular toxicity and may constitute the molecular basis of the SP100- and PML-containing structures retained on lagging anaphase chromosomes.

We observed no oligomer formation with recombinantly expressed SP110 CARD (Supplementary Fig. 3j). However, when testing the in vitro interaction between SP100 and SP110 CARDs, we found that SP110 had a striking ability to disassemble SP100 filaments. We mixed SP100 and SP110 at various ratios and imaged with TEM. As little as 1:10 SP110 CARD was sufficient to break SP100 oligomers, and a 1:1 ratio led to a complete loss of visible assemblies (Fig. 6e). We used Alphafold345 to model an SP110–SP100 CARD heterodimer, obtaining a strong interface predicted template modelling (ipTM) score of 0.85 (Fig. 6f). This modelled heterodimer is predicted to block SP100 homo-oligomer formation, which would be consistent with SP110’s ability to support mitotic dissolution of the SP100-containing PML bodies (Fig. 6g). We tested this hypothesis by creating point mutations in full-length SP110. We found that four of five SP110 point mutations at the putative interface with SP100 (interface #1) were sufficient to cause IFNβ-induced cell death to a similar extent as complete SP110 KO (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 3k). Taken together, these data reveal the molecular details of an SP100/SP110 CARD–CARD switch that regulates the dissolution of PML bodies during mitosis to support normal chromosome segregation (Fig. 6i).

Discussion

In this study, we discovered that SP110 controls SP100 oligomerization, which in turn regulates the properties and mitotic dissolution of nuclear PML bodies. When SP110 is deficient, any condition that results in excess un-SUMOylated SP100 leads to inappropriate focal accumulation of PML bodies on mitotic DNA that restricts chromosome segregation, causing genotoxicity and cell death (Fig. 6j). The SP100–SP110 axis is molecularly achieved by newly described functions for the SP100 and SP110 CARD domains that mediate the assembly and disassembly of SP100 oligomers.

Human SPs share similar domain architecture yet perform distinct cell-type-specific functions23. Here, we establish pleiotropic functions of SP110 and SP100 that are independent of their reported roles in transcription and viral defence23,24,30. Although our study was initiated in the context of an interferon response, the roles of SP100 and SP110 are not confined to innate immunity. Both proteins are widely expressed across human cell types46, suggesting that their regulation of PML body homeostasis is not a specialized immune function but rather a fundamental process of cellular maintenance. Moreover, control of the SP110–SP100 axis is not restricted to transcriptional upregulation as SUMOylation inhibition induced mitotic SP100 retention (Fig. 4d). Differential SP110 phosphorylation was also observed in response to interferon signalling47, implying that other post-translational modifications may impact the SP110–SP100 axis. By contrast, human SP140 expression is largely restricted to immune cells, where it maintains heterochromatin status by repressing topoisomerases and governing macrophage cell fate48,49. This functional divergence positions the SP110/SP100 partnership as distinct from the more specialized, lineage-specific roles of SP140.

SP proteins, including SP100 and SP110, are best known for their roles in chromatin transactions23. However, we find that the activity of SP110 and SP100 in cell death strictly involves the CARDs of each protein to regulate PML body homeostasis. SP100’s cell killing capacity is achieved through the formation of un-SUMOylated CARD-mediated oligomers that prevent mitotic dissolution. Other proteins form CARD-dependent oligomers, but these are distinct in both structure and function16,42,43 (Supplementary Fig. 3). AIRE, a close relative of SPs, forms chromatinized homomultimers to facilitate transcriptional activity in T cells via its PHD50,51. This contrasts with SP100’s CARD homo-oligomerization that functions independent of its chromatin reading domains. MAVS forms CARD oligomers that amplify the RIG-I signalling cascade within ZCCHC3 phase-separated bodies7,42. Substituting the MAVS CARD with the CARD from ASC prevents recruitment into ZCCHC3 condensates7, which parallels our observation that SP CARDs are not interchangeable in regulating SP100-induced toxicity. Biologically, SP110’s CARD prevents SP100 over-oligomerization in trans, thereby enabling mitotic PML body dissolution and preventing chromosome mis-segregation. Purposefully perturbing SP100 CARD oligomerization could therefore present intriguing therapeutic options for patients with SP110-deficient VODI.

Nuclear PML bodies are dramatically disassembled during mitosis and coalesce into distinct structures (mitotic accumulations of PML protein (MAPPs)) that are kept separate from condensed chromosomes34,35,52,53. However, the molecular basis for PML body disassembly and the potential consequences of inappropriate PML retention were previously unknown. We find that SP110 is required for dissolution of PML bodies during mitosis by regulating SP100 oligomerization. Failure to disassemble SP100 oligomers leads to chromosomal PML body retention and a pathological gain of function that compromises genome stability. Biomolecular phase-separated bodies perform myriad critical cellular functions, but emerging data indicate that they present distinct challenges to the cell and must be actively surveilled to ensure cell health and prevent disease54–58. For example, TDP-43 is solubilized by PML-mediated SUMOylation to protect against aggregation that manifests as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis56. G3BP1 controls the assembly of phase-separated cytosolic stress granules, whose misregulation has been linked to neurodegenerative disorders13,57,58. Our work reveals the SP100–SP110 axis as a critical rheostat for phase-separated PML body homeostasis, demonstrating that disruption of its mitotic disassembly manifests as a potent source of genomic instability. Future research will be essential to establish whether the regulated disassembly of biomolecular phase-separated bodies is a general requirement to maintain cell health, the failure of which could underpin a wide variety of human pathologies.

Methods

Cloning, nucleotide and protein sequences

All nucleic acid sequences can be found in Supplementary Table 2. DNA was purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies and Microsynth AG. SP110 and SP100 full-length and domain deletion constructs were amplified from cDNA generated from RNA isolated from RPE1 cells. Domain deletion constructs were assembled using site-directed mutagenesis. Other cloning procedures were performed using either Gibson assembly or Golden Gate assembly.

Cell culture

hTERT-RPE1 (ATCC), HEK-293T (ATCC), U2OS (ATCC), A549 (generously provided by Dr Tim Clausen), MDCK (ATCC) and Vero-CCL81 (ATCC) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco), supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 µg ml−1 penicillin–streptomycin. Jurkat (ATCC) cells were cultured in RPMI medium (Gibco), 10% FBS and 100 µg ml−1 penicillin–streptomycin. Phytohemagglutinin (PHA)-stimulated T lymphoblasts were cultured in RPMI medium (Gibco), 0.1% IL-2, 10% FBS and 100 µg ml−1 penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and tested negative (MycoAlert; Lonza). Cell lines were short tandem repeat profiled (Microsynth AG). Cell lines used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 3. All clonal cell lines were genotyped by Illumina sequencing and phenotyped for absence of protein production by western blot.

Chemical treatment conditions

Recombinant human IFNβ (Abcam ab71475) was added at 10 ng ml−1 and incubated for 1–3 days. Poly(I:C) (HMV; Invivogen) was added at 500 ng ml−1 and incubated 72 h. Poly(dA:dT) (Invivogen) was added at 25 ng ml−1 and incubated for 72 h. Ruxolitinib (MedChemExpress) was added for 72 h at 20 µM. To induce DNA damage, cells were treated with etoposide (25 µM) for 24 h as a positive control. As2O3 (10 µM) was used to disrupt PML bodies. To modulate the cell cycle, 1 µM palbociclib (G1 arrest; MedChemExpress) or 6 µM RO-3306 (G2/M arrest; Sigma-Aldrich) were used for 48 h. TAK-981 (75 nM; MedChemExpress) was used as a pan-SUMOylation inhibitor for 48 h. TAK-243 (500 nM; MedChemExpress) was used as a ubiquitination inhibitor for 48 h.

Lentivirus production

Per reaction, 1 µg of genome vector, 800 ng of packaging plasmid (Addgene #8455; courtesy of Bob Weinberg), 200 ng of envelope plasmid (Addgene #8454; courtesy of Bob Weinberg) and 6 µl of polyethyleneimine were mixed in 200 µl Opti-MEM and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Each mixture was then added dropwise to a well in a six-well plate containing 5 × 105 plated HEK-293T cells. After incubating for 2–3 days, the supernatant was collected, passed through a 0.2-µm filter and used immediately or frozen at −80 °C. Cells were transduced in the presence of 8 µg ml−1 polybrene.

In vitro transcription

ppp-RNA were produced in vitro using previously published methods14. In brief, overlapping oligomers containing a T7 promoter, the desired protospacer and single guide RNA (sgRNA) scaffold were amplified using Phusion polymerase (New England Biolabs). The unpurified DNA product was then subjected to in vitro transcription using the NEB HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs), incubating at 37 °C for 16 h. The following day, RNA was treated with DNase I, purified using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), quantified by Nanodrop and stored at −80 °C.

Electroporation

Electroporations were conducted in 96-well format (1–2 × 105 cells per well) or with 100-µl cuvettes (5 × 106 cells per cuvette) using Lonza 4D electroporation kits. The kit/program for each cell type was as follows: HEK-293T (SF kit/DG-130), Jurkat (SE kit/CL-120) and RPE1 (P3 kit/EA-104). After electroporation, 80 µl of prewarmed media was added to each cuvette, and cells were allowed to sit at room temperature for 10 min before transferring to 2 ml medium in a six-well plate. For electroporation of Cas9–sgRNA to generate KOs, 100 pmol of Cas9 was complexed to 100 pmol of sgRNA and incubated for 10 min at room temperature, then added to the cells/electroporation buffer mixture. For transfection of ppp-RNA, 50 pmol of in-vitro-transcribed RNA per 1 × 105 cells was added to the cells/electroporation buffer mixture.

CRISPRi screen

RPE1 TP53−/− WT or SP110−/− cells stably expressing dCas9–blue fluorescent protein (BFP)–KRAB were transduced with a lentiviral CRISPRi sgRNA library59 containing ~104,000 elements (5 sgRNAs per gene + non-targeting controls) at a target multiplicity of infection of 0.3 in the presence of 8 µg ml−1 polybrene. The target sgRNA coverage was 1,000×. The lentivirus genome contained an untethered mCherry reporter gene to monitor transduction efficiency. Two days post-transduction, cells were split into two replicates and treated with 10 µg ml−1 puromycin for 7 days. Cells were passaged and fresh puromycin was added every 2 days. Nine days post-transduction, cells were electroporated with either in-vitro-transcribed ppp-RNA or a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer control. After roughly ten doublings (~14 days), cell pellets were collected and flash frozen. Genomic DNA was purified using the Puregene kit (Gentra), and the concentration was measured using a Nanodrop. The sgRNA cassette was amplified with NEBNext Ultra II Q5 HiFi polymerase (New England Biolabs) with indexed primers using the following steps with 5 µg of gDNA input per 50-µl reaction: 1 × 98 °C for 30 s; 22× of 98 °C for 10 s, then 65 °C for 75 s; 1 × 65 °C for 5 min. All common reactions were pooled and purified in a double solid phase reversible immobilization (SPRI) bead purification (SeraMag; Cytiva). In the first round, 0.65× SPRI beads were added, and the supernatant was collected. In the second round, 1× SPRI beads were used, and the amplicon was eluted in 20 µl H2O. The purified amplicons were analysed on a TapeStation DNA flow cell (Agilent) for the expected amplicon size of 270 bp. Indexed amplicons were sequenced on a NextSeq2000 P3 flow cell (Illumina) with 50-bp single end reads with a target of 50 million reads per sample. Reads were demultiplexed and analysed by both MAGeCK60 and DrugZ61, first at the sgRNA level and then at the gene level, to identify enriched and depleted genes. Raw sgRNA counts are provided in Supplementary Data 1, and the gene-level drugZ output is listed in Supplementary Data 2. GSEA (v4.3.3) was performed separately on all enriched and depleted genes, with reported gene lists having a nominative P value <0.05.

Competition assay

Two populations of cells (that is, WT versus SP110−/− transduced with a fluorescent marker protein) were equally mixed on day −1 in a six-well plate. On day 0, cells were analysed by flow cytometry and treated with the indicated conditions. Cell populations were passaged and subsequently assessed by flow cytometry for 7 days post-treatment.

Flow cytometry

Fluorescently labelled cells (via lentiviral transduction) were analysed using an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher), software v3.2.1. Data were analysed in FlowJo (v10.9). Live cells were gated on side scatter area (SSC-A) versus forward scatter area (FSC-A), followed by single cells on forward scatter height (FSC-H) versus FSC-A. Fluorophore presence was assessed by gating on SSC-A versus a fluorescent protein (XFP) (BFP, green fluorescent protein (GFP) or mCherry).

Clone genotyping

Genomic DNA was isolated using QuickExtract solution (Lucigen). Reactions were incubated for 10 min at 65 °C, followed by 5 min at 98 °C and cooling to 4 °C. Primers containing Illumina adapter sequences were designed to amplify a ~200-bp region surrounding the Cas9 target sites in SP110 and SP100. Genomic DNA was amplified in two rounds using NEBNext Ultra II Q5 HiFi polymerase (New England Biolabs). In PCR 1, the above designed primers were used to amplify genomic DNA in 22 cycles. One microlitre from PCR 1 was used as input for PCR 2, where Illumina indexes were added in ten cycles. Reactions were purified using 0.8× SPRI beads (SeraMag; Cytiva). Concentrations were measured on a Qubit and analysed on a TapeStation DNA flow cell (Agilent). Samples were sequenced on either a MiSeq or NextSeq2000 with 2 × 150 paired-end reads. The target read count per sample was 100,000 reads. Reads were demultiplexed and analysed with CRISPResso262 (v2.0.20b) using default settings.

Western blot

Cell lysates were collected by pelleting, washing and resuspending cells in 1× RIPA buffer supplemented with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher). Lysates were incubated on ice for 15 min and pelleted at 15,000g for 15 min, and the soluble fraction was collected. Protein concentration was determined using Bradford assay. Twenty micrograms of input was electrophoresed on a 4–20% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel. A wet transfer to a 0.8-µm nitrocellulose membrane was performed at 4 °C. The membrane was blocked in Tris-buffered saline + Tween 20 (TBS-T) + 5% milk for 45 min, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C in TBS-T + 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) containing primary antibody. The antibodies and concentrations used are found in Supplementary Table 4. The next day, membranes were washed in TBS-T and then incubated in TBS-T + 5% milk + 1:10,000 secondary antibody (LI-COR 800CW and/or 680RD). Membranes were again washed in TBS-T. A final wash in PBS was performed before imaging on a LI-COR Odyssey using the 800-nm and 700-nm channels.

Reverse-transcription quantitative PCR

Cellular RNA was isolated using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen) with on-column DNase digestion. RNA concentration was measured by Nanodrop, and 125 ng was used as input for reverse transcription (iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit; Bio-Rad). Quantitative PCR was then performed with a 1:10 dilution of cDNA using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) on a QuantStudio 6 (Thermo Fisher) per manufacturer’s protocol. Data were then analysed via the ddCt method using ACTB as the endogenous control.

T-lymphoblast viability assay

Patient- or healthy-control-derived peripheral blood mononuclear cells were expanded in 96-well plates (Axonlab) with initial stimulation using PHA (5 µg ml−1, Sigma-Aldrich) and human IL-2 (200 units ml−1, Novartis). Fresh IL-2 was added to the culture every 5–7 days. Frozen T cell blasts were used for the indicated experiments after thawing. Cells from patients with VODI, both harbouring the SP110 c.642delC mutation26, or healthy donor control cells, were treated with a mock buffer control or 25 ng ml−1 IFNβ for 72 h. Six days post-stimulation, the relative cell viability was measured by quantifying ATP abundance (CeltTiter-Glo; Promega). The patient cohort and associated immune cell characterization have been approved by the Ethical Commission of Northwestern and Central Switzerland (EKNZ-2015-187).

ChIP-seq

A total of 10 × 106 RPE1 WT or SP110−/− cells were incubated with 10 ng ml−1 IFNβ for 6 h. Cells were detached, washed, fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room temperature and quenched with 50 mM glycine. Cell pellets were washed and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Pellets were lysed in three sequential lysis buffers to remove cytoplasm and obtain isolated nuclei according to a previously published protocol63. Nuclei were sonicated with a Covaris S220 to obtain a desired fragment length of 300 bp. Lysates were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with 5 µg of prebound anti-SP100 or rabbit IgG isotype control protein A dynabeads (Thermo Fisher). The next morning, beads were washed in 6× RIPA buffer, followed by 1× TBS, and eluted in 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 1 mM EDTA, 1% SDS by incubating at 65 °C overnight to reserve crosslinks. The following day, the dynabeads were collected on a magnetic stand, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was then incubated with RNase A and proteinase K (Qiagen). DNA was purified (MinElute kit, Qiagen) and analysed on a TapeStation (Agilent). The NEBNextII kit (New England Biolabs) was used to prepare the sequencing library, and the indexed library was sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) with 150-bp paired-end reads with a target of 25 million reads per sample. Reads were demultiplexed, aligned with Bowtie264 and peaks called with MACS365. Graphics were generated using deepTools66 and ChIPseeker67 (v1.32.1). Data from the comparison with Dong et al. (2022) were retrieved from SRA SRR1706288328.

Cells were inoculated with H5N1–GFP, VSV–GFP or HSV-1–GFP for 1 h at a multiplicity of infection of 10, washed once with PBS, and the medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 100 U ml−1 penicillin–streptomycin, 0.3% BSA, 0.1% FBS, 0.2 M HEPES and 1 µg ml−1 TPCK trypsin for H5N1–GFP, or with DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and 100 U ml−1 penicillin–streptomycin for VSV–GFP and HSV-1–GFP. GFP expression (green calibrated units × µm2/image) and cell confluence were monitored using the Incucyte S3 live-cell analysis system (Sartorius).

Immunofluorescence

Stably expressing H2B–GFP RPE1 WT and SP110−/− cells were generated using lentivirus generated from Addgene plasmid #91788 (courtesy of Beverly Torok-Storb). Cells were plated on eight-well glass slides (Ibidi) and incubated in ± 10 ng IFNβ for 3 days. Cells were fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature, washed in PBS and permeabilized in PBS + 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Following another PBS wash, cells were blocked in PBS + 2% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody (Supplementary Table 4) was added in PBS + 1% BSA and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, cells were washed and incubated in PBS + 1% BSA with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were washed, air dried and mounted in ProLong Diamond Mountant (Thermo Fisher). Slides were imaged on a Nikon Spinning Disk SoRa (ETHZ ScopeM) with a 100× oil-immersion objective. Images were analysed using ImageJ73 (v2.14.0). For 53BP1 foci and PML body quantification, images had a constant threshold applied, and then the ‘analyze particles’ function was used. Micronuclei were counted manually in the H2B channel. PML and 53BP1 Pearson object correlation was calculated using the JACoP plugin74.

R-loop detection assay

Detection of RNA:DNA hybrids (R-loops) was performed using the RNaseH1D210N–GFP reporter assay as previously described75. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were purchased as SMARTpools from Horizon (siSP100: L-015307-00-0005; siSP110: L-011875-00-0005). Twenty-four hours after siRNA transfection, cells were plated in 24-well plates, and expression of GFP–RNaseH1D210N was induced by adding doxycycline (1 ng ml−1). After an additional 24 h, cells were treated with either dimethyl sulfoxide or 25 ng ml−1 IFNβ. CHK1 inhibitor (LY2603618) treatment was used as a positive control for the induction of R-loops. Cells were pre-extracted with CSK buffer (10 mM HEPES–NaOH pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 300 mM sucrose and 0.5% Triton X-100) for 5 min on ice before fixation in 4% PFA, and DNA was counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 µg ml−1). Image acquisition was performed on an Opera Phenix spinning disc confocal microscope (PerkinElmer) at 40× magnification. GFP nuclear intensity was analysed with Harmony image analysis.

Subcellular fractionation

Pelleted and PBS-washed RPE1 cells were resuspended in cytoplasmic lysis buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 60 mM KCl, 300 mM sucrose, 0.25% Triton X-100 (v/v) and 1 mM EDTA) and incubated on ice for 10 min. Lysate was pelleted at 400g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected as the soluble (So) fraction. The remaining nuclear pellet was washed with nuclear wash buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 10 mM KCl, 300 mM sucrose and 1 mM EDTA) and pelleted at 400g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was aspirated, and the pellet was resuspended in nuclear lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 140 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40 (v/v) and 1 mM EDTA) and vortexed for 30 s. The resuspended nuclear fraction was incubated on ice for 20 min and then pelleted at 18,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting soluble nuclear fraction (Nu) was collected. The chromatin-containing pellet was resuspended in nuclear lysis buffer, sonicated for five duty cycles, 30 s on, 30 s off using a water bath sonicator, and pelleted at 18,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. The solubilized supernatant (Ch) was then collected. All fractions were stored at −20 °C.

IP–MS

RPE1 SP110−/− cells were stably transduced with FLAG-tagged SP110, SP110 ΔCARD or SP110 CARD and selected with puromycin for 7 days. The input for immunoprecipitation was collected from 5 × 106 cells stimulated for 24 h with 10 ng ml−1 IFNβ. All lysis steps were performed on ice. Cells were pelleted, washed and resuspended in cytoplasm lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 320 mM sucrose, 0.5% Igepal, 0.1 mM EDTA and protease inhibitor cocktail) for 15 min. The nuclei were gently pelleted and resuspended in nuclear lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, 1% Igepal, 10% glycerol, 1 U ml−1 benzonase, 1 mM MgCl2 and protease inhibitor cocktail). The nuclei were lysed by passing through a douncer 30 times. The insoluble fraction was pelleted, and the supernatant was used as input for immunoprecipitation. Anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma) were incubated with supernatant overnight at 4 °C, rotating. Beads were washed in 3× lysis buffer followed by 1× TBS, and then bait protein was eluted by incubating in TBS containing 150 ng µl−1 FLAG peptide for 10 min, shaking. Samples were submitted to the mass spectrometry facility at the Functional Genomics Center Zurich. Data were analysed using Limma76 using spectral counts as the input. The outputs are given in Supplementary Data 4.

Co-IP

A total of 2 × 105 HEK-293T cells per condition were transiently transfected with 1,000 ng of each plasmid in the presence of polyethyleneimine. Forty-eight hours later, cells were collected and lysed directly in nuclear lysis buffer (above). Lysates were clarified and used as input for immunoprecipitation with anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads. Protein was eluted with 150 ng µl−1 FLAG peptide. The input and elution fractions were analysed by western blot.

Recombinant protein production

Human SP110 and SP100 CARD domain cDNA sequences were cloned into pET28b backbone with an N-terminal His6 tag, maltose-binding protein (MBP) and TEV cleavage site (Supplementary Table 5). Each construct was transformed into BL21 Rosetta 2 (Merck) and grown in LB medium at 30 °C. At an optical density (OD)600 of 0.6, the cells were transferred to 18 °C for 30 min, then isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM to induce expression. Cells were incubated for 16 h at 18 °C, then pelleted at 4,000g for 30 min. The cell pellet was lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 8, 200 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM imidazole and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) and sonicated on ice for 8 min; 10 s on, 10 s off, 20% amplitude. The lysate was pelleted at 20,000g for 30 min, and the soluble fraction was collected. The soluble fraction was supplemented up to 15 mM imidazole and 500 mM NaCl and added to Ni-NTA agarose beads equilibrated in wash buffer (lysis buffer + 20 mM imidazole). The bead–lysate mixture was incubated for 1 h at 4 °C, rotating. Target protein was eluted in elution buffer (lysis buffer + 300 mM imidazole) and dialysed overnight at 4 °C in dialysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl and 1 mM dithiothreitol) in the presence of TEV protease to cleave off the His6–MBP tag. Dialysed protein was then subjected to a second Ni-NTA purification to remove the His6–TEV and His6–MBP. The flow-through fraction was collected and again dialysed overnight in dialysis buffer. The next day, the purified protein was concentrated in a 3-kDa molecular weight cut-off concentrator. Protein purity was assessed by sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and size-exclusion chromatography, and 1 mg ml−1 aliquots were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

TEM

Quantifoil R2/2 grids coated with continuous 2-nm carbon foil were glow discharged for 45 s at 25 mA. Five microlitres of protein at 0.5 mg ml−1 were applied to the grid for 30 s, washed twice with water, stained for 30 s with 2% uranyl acetate and dried under an incandescent lamp. Grids were imaged on a FEI Morgagni microscope at 100 kV at 32,000× magnification. For the SP110/SP100 mixing experiment, purified aliquots of each protein were thawed and mixed at the indicated ratios for 30 min before mounting on grids.

Cryo-EM sample preparation

Quantifoil R2/2 holey carbon copper grids (Quantifoil) were cleaned by overnight incubation in ethyl acetate and coated with 1-nm continuous carbon foil by floatation. The grids were subjected to glow discharge (15 mA for 15 s) in a Pelco easiGlow glow discharge cleaning system (Ted Pella). The climate chamber of a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher) was equilibrated to 4 °C at 95% humidity. Four microlitres of SP100 (crosslinked with 0.5% glutaraldehyde and containing 0.05% NP40) were applied to the grid, incubated for 30 s, blotted for 1 s and subsequently vitrified by plunging into an ethane–propane mixture (1:2) maintained at liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K).

Cryo-EM data collection and image processing

A total of 10,995 movies were collected in counting mode on a Titan Krios cryo-electron microscope (Thermo Fisher) equipped with a K2 direct electron detector (Gatan) at a nominal magnification of 165,000×, equalling a pixel size of 0.84 Å at the specimen level. The energy filter slit width was set to 20 eV. Micrographs were exposed for 7 s and fractionated into 40 frames, with a dose rate of 8 e− px−1 s−1 and an electron dose of 64 e− Å−2. Data were collected at defocus set between −1 µm and −3 µm. Image processing was performed in cryoSPARC77. Movies were drift corrected, averaged and dose weighted with patch motion correction. Contrast transfer function (CTF) fitting was performed with Patch CTF. Filament Tracer was used for particle picking, using 70 Å filament diameter, 0.25× repeat distance and 140 Å minimum length, resulting in 3,201,771 particles. The 2,884,159 particles that remained after particle picking inspection and particle extraction (360 pixels box size binned to 96 pixels) were two-dimensionally classified into 200 classes, resulting in 362,138 particles, which were subsequently extracted without binning (box size 360 pixels). Three subsequent two-dimensional classifications resulted in 296,140 particles that were refined to a resolution of 3.5 Å with Helix Refine, using an initial model that had been generated from a subset of 74,239 particles using Helix Refine and initial helical parameters that had been determined from the initial model using Symmetry Search. The refined helical parameters of the final reconstruction were 46.4° helical twist and 19.8 Å helical rise.

Cryo-EM model building and refinement

An initial model of the SP100 card domain was generated with AlphaFold2 structure prediction78 and placed into the symmetrized, sharpened map of the SP100 filament using the rigid body fitting function ‘Fit in Map’ of UCSF Chimera79. The atomic model was rebuilt in Coot80. The model was refined with application of non-crystallographic symmetry using Phenix real-space refinement81.

Statistics and reproducibility

All bar graphs demonstrate the mean ± standard deviation (s.d.), except where noted. All biological replicates were performed at independent timepoints. Data with statistical tests performed are indicated in the respective figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses except in cases of experimental failure. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. All graphs were produced in Prism (v10.1.1; GraphPad), and figures were assembled using Affinity Designer (v1.10.8; Serif).
