Joint developmental trajectories of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior in Chinese adolescents: The roles of contextual, personality, and cognitive factors
Authors: Qingling Zhao, Xiaojie Zhou, Nengzhi Jiang, Wen Zhang, Lin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Internalizing and externalizing problems have been documented to frequently co-occur, yet their joint developmental trajectories and the influences of contextual and individual factors remain poorly understood, particularly among adolescents in resource-limited regions of China. This three-wave longitudinal study investigated independent and joint developmental patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior, with attention to the predictive roles of contextual factors (childhood emotional abuse and neglect), individual personality characteristics (rejection sensitivity), and cognitive factors (coping styles and meaning in life). Surveys were administrated to 1542 high school students (45.1% boys) at three time points with six-month intervals. Parallel process latent growth mixture models identified four distinct joint trajectories of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior: dual-high-growth group, dual-moderate-growth group, healthy group, and dual-high-decline group. Childhood emotional abuse, emotional neglect, rejection sensitivity, and negative coping were significant risk factors for comorbid symptom trajectories, while positive coping and meaning in life emerged as protective factors. These findings have important implications for understanding the complexity of comorbid internalizing and externalizing behaviors during the high school years in underdeveloped regions of China.

Introduction

Internalizing and externalizing problems, including anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior, have consistently demonstrated high prevalence among adolescents, particularly for Chinese high school students due to the substantial academic stressors inherent in their educational system1–3. Both problems have been documented to co-occur and are affected by multiple factors, including contextual, personality-related, and cognitive factors4,5. Yet, there is a dearth of literature investigating the heterogeneous joint developmental patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior among Chinese high school students, let alone the effects of multiple factors. Accordingly, this three-wave longitudinal study aimed to examine the comorbidity between anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior, and identify key predictive factors underlying their joint development.

The Chinese cultural context is particularly useful when examining the comorbidity of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior among high school students. Rooted in Confucian values, Chinese society places great emphasis on academic achievement, just as encapsulated in the Chinese proverb “shu zhong zi you huang jin wu, shu zhong zi you yan ru yu” (i.e., “Golden mansions reside in books, jade-like beauty awaits through scholarship”). Within this culture, the National College Entrance Examination (Gaokao) — a high-stakes competitive examination that largely determines university admission and future career opportunities — imposes considerable psychological pressure on high school students. Such strain has been linked to the development of both internalizing and externalizing problems, including anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior6. However, mental health education and counseling services in China remain relatively underdeveloped, especially in resource-limited regions, where systemic deficiencies in mental health literacy and persistent stigmatization of psychological issues coexist7. In this way, many adolescents encounter significant barriers in both identifying and mitigating internalizing and externalizing problems. Given this context, it is imperative to investigate the joint developmental patterns of internalizing and externalizing problems in this population and identify modifiable influencing factors.

Anxious/depressive symptoms refer to co-occurring emotional states characterized by persistent feelings of excessive nervousness, sadness, or despair, often accompanied by physical tension, sleep disturbances8, which are highly prevalent among adolescents worldwide9. Approximately 25% to 50% of youth with depressive symptoms also exhibit anxiety disorders, and 10% to 15% of those with anxiety disorders report comorbid depressive symptoms10. Notably, the development of anxious/depressive symptoms during adolescence is not uniform; substantial heterogeneity and individual differences have been observed across developmental trajectories11. To our knowledge, only one study has explicitly examined heterogeneous developmental trajectories of anxious/depressive symptoms. Using a sample of 1,354 American adolescents aged 10–18 years, Dugré and colleagues identified four distinct trajectory classes: low group (42.2%), moderate group (42.8%); increasing group (11.9%) and high group (3.1%)4. However, the generalizability of this finding to high school students residing in underdeveloped regions of China remains empirically unestablished. Therefore, the first aim of the present study was to examine heterogeneous developmental patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms among high school adolescents residing in underdeveloped regions of China.

Aggressive behavior, conceptualized as intentionally harmful conduct towards others12, is a common form of externalizing psychopathology during adolescence13. Current neurodevelopmental models posit that asynchronous maturation within neural circuitry underlying behavioral regulation — particularly the imbalance between prefrontal cortical control systems and limbic-striatal reactivity — may compromise adolescents’ capacity to inhibit aggressive impulses14. This maturational gap may partially explain the individual differences observed in both baseline aggression levels and developmental trajectories. Existing trajectory studies have revealed divergent patterns of aggression development across subgroups. For example, Dugré and colleagues identified bimodal distributions (low-aggression vs. high-aggression groups) in a sample of 1,354 American adolescents aged 10–184. In addition, Xie and colleagues studying 2,267 American students from grades 4 through 12, distinguished increasing and decreasing trajectories alongside low and high groups15. Moreover, Yang and colleagues (1,214 American adolescents aged 11.5–15)16and Isen and colleagues (twin samples followed from ages 9–10 to 16 − 8)17identified a three-class model (non or low-aggressive, moderate, and high-aggression trajectories).

Crucially, existing findings on aggressive behavior primarily derive from Western cultural contexts, where behavioral norms and socialization practices differ markedly from those in collectivistic contexts such as China18. Furthermore, cultural neuroscience research suggests that emotion regulation processes are shaped by culturally specific values and practices19. We therefore hypothesize that aggression trajectories among Chinese adolescents would demonstrate lower rates of high-aggression classifications compared with Western samples and distinct developmental patterns shaped by Confucian value internalization processes. Accordingly, the second aim of the present study was to examine heterogeneous developmental patterns of aggressive behavior.

Prior research indicates that anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior in adolescents exhibit similar independent developmental trajectories: approximately half of adolescents maintain low levels of both over the long term; nearly half experience moderate (or fluctuating) levels; and a small minority exhibit high levels4. Notably, these shared trajectory patterns suggest that these two types of problems may also develop jointly. Variable-centered studies have also demonstrated the co-occurrence of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior11. Furthermore, according to the developmental cascade theory20, the various levels, domains, and systems within a developmental system engage in extensive interactions, which accumulate and propagate over time, thereby exerting a profound influence on individual development pathways. More specifically, changes in one domain (e.g., early anxious/depressive symptoms) can trigger cascading effects across other domains—for example, social withdrawal due to anxiety may lead to peer rejection, which in turn exacerbates aggressive behavior—highlighting how internalizing and externalizing problems can mutually influence each other’s development20. Such evidence reinforces the likelihood of comorbidity between anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior. Therefore, the third aim of this study was to examine whether anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior co-develop in high school adolescents.

According to the p-factor theory, one underlying general psychopathological factor (i.e., the p-factor) captures an individual’s propensity to develop both internalizing and externalizing problems21. The p-factor is conceptualized as a latent trait encompassing dispositional negative emotionality, impulsive responsivity to emotions, thought dysfunction, and diminished cognitive efficiency, framed within a broader model of general functional impairment22. Furthermore, empirical studies have demonstrated that the p-factor exhibits robust associations with multifactorial determinants spanning contextual adversities, temperamental traits, and biological substrates23–26. Therefore, building on this theoretical and empirical foundation, the present study aimed to further investigate how multidimensional determinants—encompassing contextual (e.g., childhood emotional abuse and neglect), individual personality-related (rejection sensitivity), and cognitive (coping styles and meaning in life) factors—collectively predict the co-development of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior in adolescents.

From a contextual perspective, childhood emotional abuse and neglect represent critical predictors of both internalizing and externalizing problems in adolescence. Childhood emotional abuse involves harmful caregiver behaviors such as verbal attacks, emotional manipulation, intentional humiliation, or belittlement, which induce the child’s intense negative emotions, including fear, guilt, and shame27,28. In contrast, emotional neglect refers to a caregiver’s failure to meet the child’s basic emotional needs, such as being emotionally unresponsive to the child’s distress, ignoring social and affective needs, or imposing expectations that exceed the child’s developmental capacity or safety27,28. According to the latent vulnerability theory, childhood maltreatment increases susceptibility to various psychiatric disorders, which frequently present with comorbid conditions29. Empirical studies further confirm that early adverse experiences could predict the co-development of internalizing and externalizing problems30. However, the distinct effects of specific types of childhood trauma (e.g., emotional abuse versus emotional neglect) on this co-development remain inadequately understood31. Therefore, this study aimed to separately examine the predictive roles of childhood emotional abuse and emotional neglect in shaping joint developmental courses of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior.

Personality traits, such as rejection sensitivity—a disposition defined by the tendency to anxiously anticipate, readily perceive, and intensely react to interpersonal rejection—may serve as significant predictors of the co-development of internalizing and externalizing problems32. The rejection sensitivity model proposes that early experiences of rejection foster a heightened vigilance to potential social exclusion, which can subsequently elicit strong negative emotional reactions and maladaptive behavioral responses such as social withdrawal or aggression33. Empirical studies have further demonstrated that individuals with high rejection sensitivity are more likely to exhibit elevated levels of depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior34,35. However, within the Chinese context, to our knowledge, no research to date has specifically investigated the predictive role of rejection sensitivity in relation to joint developmental trajectories of internalizing and externalizing problems. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the predictive effect of rejection sensitivity on the joint development of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior among Chinese adolescents.

Cognitive factors, such as coping styles and meaning in life, may play a crucial role in the joint development of internalizing and externalizing problems. Coping styles refer to the cognitive and behavioral efforts individuals employ to manage situations perceived as exceeding their adaptive resources, with the goal of reducing negative impacts and facilitating adjustment to environmental demands36. Empirical evidence indicates that adolescents who exhibit comorbid internalizing and externalizing problems are particularly characterized by maladaptive or unproductive coping strategies37, suggesting that ineffective coping may contribute to the co-development and maintenance of these symptoms.

Furthermore, meaning in life—defined as the extent to which individuals perceive their lives as coherent, purposeful, and significant—has been shown to buffer against various forms of psychopathology38,39. The hierarchical meaning-making model posits that a sense of meaning can promote resilience and facilitate adaptive emotional regulation, thereby potentially mitigating the severity or progression of both anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior40. Moreover, empirical research also demonstrated that meaning in life serves as a significant negative predictor of both internalizing and externalizing problems among adolescents41. However, the role of meaning in life in shaping the joint trajectories of these problems remains underexplored, particularly in non-Western cultural contexts. Therefore, this study also aimed to examine the predictive contributions of coping styles and meaning in life to the co-development of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior.

This three-wave longitudinal study aimed to elucidate the short-term joint developmental patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior among high school students residing in underdeveloped regions of China, with attention to the predictive roles of contextual factors (childhood emotional abuse and neglect), personality factors (rejection sensitivity), and cognitive factors (coping styles and meaning in life). Specifically, the study had three primary objectives: (1) to examine heterogeneous developmental patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms, and aggressive behavior, (2) to establish their joint developmental patterns, and (3) to investigate whether childhood emotional abuse, emotional neglect, rejection sensitivity, coping styles, and meaning in life predict membership in these joint trajectories. Guided by the developmental cascade theory20, the latent vulnerability theory29, the p-factor theory21, the rejection sensitivity model33, and the hierarchical meaning-making model40, along with existing empirical evidence, we proposed the following hypotheses: (1) anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior would follow three or more distinct heterogeneous developmental courses; (2) anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior would exhibit co-occurring developmental patterns; (3) childhood emotional abuse, emotional neglect, rejection sensitivity, coping styles, and meaning in life would significantly predict membership in these joint trajectories.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Participants were freshmen and sophomores recruited from a high school located in Zaozhuang, a small eastern Chinese city in Shandong Province with a permanent resident population of approximately 3.80 million in 2024, showing a gradual decline in recent years. The city’s annual per capita disposable income is around $5,000, and it ranks the lowest among all prefecture-level cities in the province in terms of Gross Domestic Product (GDP).

Students completed questionnaires at three time points, spaced six months apart. The initial measurement was conducted in October 2023 (T1), followed by subsequent measurements in March 2024 (T2) and October 2024 (T3). Prior to each data collection, informed consent was obtained from both the adolescent participants and their parents. The adolescents were informed of the voluntary nature of their participation and assured of the confidentiality of the study before each assessment. All data collection procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the first author’s university.

A total of 1,934 students participated at T1, 1,977 at T2, and 1,090 at T3. We included only those participants who took part in at least two waves of data collection, resulting in a final sample of 1,542 (demographic characteristics summarized in Table 1).

Measures

Anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior (T1, T2 and T3)

On a 3-point scale ranging from 0 (completely inconsistent) to 2 (completely consistent), adolescents rated the 13-item anxious/depressive symptoms subscale and the 17-item aggressive behavior subscale of Youth Self-Report (YSR)42. Sample items include: “I often cry” (anxious/depressive symptoms) and “I often argue” (aggressive behavior). Responses across each subscale were averaged, with higher scores indicating higher levels of anxious/depressive symptoms or aggressive behavior. The YSR has demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese adolescent populations, which were suitable for evaluating adolescents’ emotional and behavioral problems43. In this study, internal consistencies were high for both anxious/depressive symptoms (T1: α = 0.92, T2: α = 0.93, T3: α = 0.94) and aggressive behavior (T1: α = 0.91, T2: α = 0.93, T3: α = 0.93).

Childhood emotional abuse and neglect (T1)

Adolescents’ experiences of childhood emotional abuse and neglect were assessed using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ)27,44,45, which has been documented to be valid in Chinese adolescents46. The questionnaire contains 28 items, including 25 clinical items and 3 validity items (used for evaluation purposes and not scored). Among the clinical items, 5 items tap emotional abuse (e.g., “When I was growing up, someone in my family called me names like ‘stupid’, ‘lazy’, or ‘ugly’ ”), and 5 items tap emotional neglect (e.g., “Someone in my family made me feel important or special”). Each item was responded to on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Higher mean scores on the subscales indicate more severe experiences of emotional abuse or neglect. The CTQ has been established as a reliable instrument in previous research47. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α of T1 emotional abuse and emotional neglect were 0.75 and 0.82, respectively.

Rejection sensitivity (T1)

Rejection sensitivity was assessed using the Rejection Sensitivity Questionnaire (RSQ)48, which comprises 18 items (e.g., “I can easily accept criticism from others”). Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). A mean score of all items was calculated, with higher scores indicating higher levels of rejection sensitivity. The RSQ has been documented to be applicable to Chinese adolescents49. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for rejection sensitivity at T1 was 0.78.

Coping styles (T1)

Adolescents’ coping styles were assessed using the Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ)50. The questionnaire consists of 20 items rated on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (never) to 4 (always). It includes two subscales: positive coping (e.g., “Relieving oneself through work, study, or other activities”) and negative coping (e.g., “Trying to forget the whole event”). Mean scores were calculated for each subscale, with higher scores indicating a greater use of the corresponding coping style. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α of positive coping and negative coping at T1 were 0.92 and 0.82, respectively.

Meaning in life (T1)

The Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ)51was used to assess adolescents’ sense of meaning in life. The scale consists of 9 items (e.g., “I am searching for meaning in my life”), rated on a Likert 7-point scale ranging from 1 (Definitely not true) to 7 (Definitely true). A mean score was calculated, with higher scores reflecting a stronger sense of meaning in life. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for meaning in life at T1 was 0.87.

Demographic variables (T1)

Demographic characteristics were reported by participants at T1, including grade, gender, sibling status, registered permanent residence, and paternal/maternal education attainment. Grade was dummy-coded as 0 = Grade 10, 1 = Grade 11; gender was dummy-coded as 0 = girls, 1 = boys, sibling status was dummy-coded as 0 = only child, 1 = having one or more siblings, and registered permanent residence was dummy-coded as 0 = agricultural registered permanent residence, 1 = non-agricultural registered permanent residence. For parental education attainment, one item was used to assess education level for fathers and mothers, separately, with five response categories ranging from 1 (primary school or below) to 5 (postgraduate or above). Higher scores on this item indicate higher levels of education attainment.

Data analyses

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0 and Mplus 8.11. First, common method bias, descriptive statistics and correlation analysis were performed. Second, latent growth curve models (LGCMs) were specified to examine the independent development patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior separately; Next, parallel-process latent growth curve modeling was applied to evaluate the joint development of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior and to identify the best-fitting model. Finally, multivariate logistic regression was used to explore the predictive effect of context and individual factors.

Results

Common method biases

Prior to the main analyses, Harman’s single-factor test was employed to examine the common method biases. The results indicated that the first factor accounted for 22.8% of the total variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. Thus, there was no significant problem with common method bias in the data of this study.

Descriptive statistics

Means, standard deviations and correlation matrix for all variables are presented in Table 2. Anxious/depressive symptoms were positively associated with aggressive behavior both within and across time points. Moreover, anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior measured at each wave were significantly correlated with nearly all main study variables, with the exception of the correlation between T3 anxious/depressive symptoms and T1 emotional neglect, and that between T3 aggressive behavior and T1 negative coping. Among demographic characteristics, only gender was significantly connected with the outcome variable anxious/depressive symptoms, across three time points.

Developmental patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior

Following established guidelines for identifying heterogeneous developmental trajectories52, several fit indices were used to determine the optimal number of latent classes for both anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior. These included Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and the sample-size-adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC), with lower values indicating better model fit. Additionally, entropy values closer to 1 reflect clearer classification. Significant p-values (< 0.05) for Lo–Mendell–Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMR-LRT) and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) support the superiority of a k-class model over a k–1-class model. Each class was also required to comprise at least 3% of the sample.

Based on these criteria, a four-class model was identified as best representing the trajectories of anxious/depressive symptoms (see Table 3). The first class (8% of the sample), labeled high-stable group, began with a high initial level significantly above the mean (IAD = 2.33, p < .01) and showed a non-significant slight increase over time (SAD = 0.06, p = .80). The second class (29% of the sample), termed moderate-growth group, started slightly above average (IAD = 1.72, p < .01) and increased significantly (SAD = 0.10, p < .01). The third class (53% of the sample), low-stable group, exhibited a low initial level (IAD = 1.24, p < .01) and remained stable over time (SAD = -0.03, p = .80). The final class (9% of the sample), high-decline group, began above average (IAD = 2.01, p < .01) and decreased significantly, falling below average by the third time point (SAD = -0.39, p < .01). In order to further investigate the predictive effects of contextual factors (childhood emotional abuse and neglect), individual personality characteristics (rejection sensitivity), and cognitive factors (coping styles and meaning in life) on the joint trajectory memberships, we conducted a multivariate logistic regression analysis, with the aforementioned factors as predictors, demographic characteristics (including grade, gender, sibling status, registered permanent residence, and paternal/maternal education attainment) as covariates, and joint trajectory groups as dependent variable. Contextual factors

Higher emotional abuse significantly increased the likelihood of belonging to dual-high-growth group compared with the other three groups. Additionally, it increased the odds of belonging to dual-moderate-growth group or dual-high-decline group relative to healthy group, and further elevated the probability of belonging to dual-high-decline group relative to dual-moderate-growth group.

Higher emotional neglect increased the probability of membership in dual-high-decline group relative to healthy group or dual-moderate-growth group. That is, emotional abuse acted as a pervasive risk factor, raising the likelihood of falling into a range of non-healthy trajectories from moderate to severe forms. Emotional neglect, by contrast, exerted a targeted risk impact, specifically amplifying the risk of belonging to dual-high-decline group, and its impact on this path aligns with that of emotional abuse.

Individual personality factors

Higher rejection sensitivity was associated with greater odds of belonging to dual-high-growth group compared with the other three groups. Additionally, it elevated the odds of belonging to either dual-moderate-growth group or dual-high-decline group relative to healthy group. In other words, rejection sensitivity serves as a key determinant of developmental trajectories: it raises the possibility of individuals entering non-healthy developmental paths, and creates obvious risk differences between the healthy trajectory and different types of suboptimal trajectories.

Individual cognitive factors

Higher positive coping increased the odds of belonging to healthy group relative to the other three groups. Conversely, higher negative coping decreased the odds of belonging to healthy group compared with the other three groups, while also elevating the odds of belonging to dual-high-growth group relative to dual-moderate-growth group. Additionally, higher meaning in life decreased the probability of belonging to dual-high-growth group compared with dual-moderate-growth group or dual-high-decline group. It suggests that positive coping serves as a core protective factor that sustains healthy developmental trajectories, while negative coping heightens the risk of deviating from healthy development and differentiates the likelihood of specific suboptimal trajectories. Meaning in life, by contrast, acts as a regulatory factor that curbs the risk of the most intense type of co-occurring developmental shift.

Discussion

This three-wave longitudinal study offers novel insights into the comorbidity of internalizing and externalizing problems among Chinese high school adolescents in underdeveloped regions, with attention to the predictive roles of contextual, personality, and cognitive factors. The results identified four short-term joint developmental patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior. Furthermore, childhood emotional abuse, rejection sensitivity, and negative coping served as pervasive core risk factors, while emotional neglect acted as a targeted risk factor, heightening the risk of following adverse joint trajectories. In contrast, positive coping and meaning in life emerged as a core protective factor and a specific regulatory protective factor, respectively, reducing the likelihood of these adverse joint trajectories. These findings enhance our understanding of the development of internalizing and externalizing problems in Chinese high school students residing in underdeveloped regions and provide an empirical foundation for designing prevention and intervention programs aimed at addressing these comorbid issues in underserved areas.

This study identified four heterogeneous developmental patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms: high-stable group (8%), moderate-growth group (29%), low-stable group (53%), and high-decline group (9%). The number of trajectories is consistent with previous Western research4; however, the proportion of adolescents in low-stable group was significantly larger than that reported in Western adolescent samples4. From the perspective of social desirability bias, this cross-cultural discrepancy may stem from culturally shaped differences in emotional expression norms that influence adolescents’ self-reports. In traditional Chinese collectivist culture, emotional restraint and interpersonal harmony are valued as maturity markers53, while open expression of negative emotions, such as anxious/depressive symptoms, is often stigmatized as fragility or “burdening others”7. Under such normative pressure, it is plausible that Chinese adolescents may potentially engage in defensive self-presentation—downplaying or concealing negative emotions on scales—which could lead to artificially lower scores and overrepresentation in the low-stable group. In contrast, Western individualistic cultures prioritize emotional authenticity and self-disclosure54, with weaker stigma around negative emotions. It is plausible that Western youth face less pressure to suppress distress, resulting in more accurate symptom reports and a lower proportion of the low-stable group. This serves as a tentative explanation for the observed cross-cultural difference, given the lack of direct empirical evidence for this specific mechanism in the current study.

Three short-term distinct developmental courses of aggressive behavior were identified: high-decline group (7%), low-stable group (82%), and moderate-growth group (11%). The proportion of adolescents in low-stable group was significantly higher than that reported in Western studies15–17. This divergence may be influenced by the emphasis within Chinese society on harmonious interpersonal relationships, where aggressive behavior is generally viewed as disruptive to social order55. As a result, adolescents may experience heightened social pressure from families, schools, and broader societal institutions to suppress outward expressions of aggression. While Western societies also uphold social norms, their stronger emphasis on individual rights and freedoms may result in greater tolerance toward certain forms of aggressive behavior56. Thus, Chinese adolescents may generally exhibit lower levels of aggressive behavior compared to their Western counterparts.

Four joint developmental patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior among adolescents were identified: dual-high-growth group (9%), dual-moderate-growth group (28%), healthy group (55%), dual-high-decline group (8%). The findings provide empirical support for the developmental cascade theory20by documenting the co-occurrence and heterogeneous developmental patterns of these two types of problems. Notably, this study identified a distinct dual-high-decline group (8%), which contrasts with prior Western studies that report comorbid symptoms tend to persist or escalate4. The presence of this decline group underscores the importance of identifying protective factors and mechanisms that may facilitate the remission of comorbid symptomatology, particularly within the unique cultural context of China.

The findings indicate that childhood emotional abuse and neglect, rejection sensitivity, coping styles and meaning in life could predict the short-term joint developmental patterns of adolescent anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior. More specifically, higher childhood emotional abuse elevated the risk of all non-healthy trajectories, including dual-high-growth, dual-moderate-growth, and dual-high-decline groups, relative to healthy group or less severe non-healthy trajectories; by contrast, higher childhood emotional neglect only increased the likelihood of belonging to dual-high-decline group relative to healthy group and dual-moderate-growth group. Additionally, higher rejection sensitivity and negative coping also elevated non-healthy trajectory risk: higher rejection sensitivity increased the odds of being in dual-high-growth group (relative to the other three groups) and in dual-moderate-growth or dual-high-decline groups (relative to healthy group); higher negative coping reduced the probability of remaining in healthy group relative to the other three groups, while also increasing the likelihood of being in dual-high-growth group (relative to dual-moderate-growth group). In contrast, higher positive coping significantly increased the probability of belonging to healthy group relative to all three non-healthy trajectories, while a strong sense of meaning in life reduced the likelihood of being in dual-high-growth group (relative to dual-moderate-growth and dual-high-decline groups), rather than conferring protection across all non-healthy trajectories.

These findings align with several theoretical frameworks21,29,33,40and extend existing empirical evidence37. First, consistent with the p-factor theory21, which posits a general latent factor underlying diverse psychopathologies, childhood emotional abuse emerged as a pervasive risk factor—impacting all non-healthy trajectories—reflecting its role in shaping a broad vulnerability to co-occurring internalizing and externalizing problems. Second, the targeted risk effect of emotional neglect (specific to dual-high-decline) aligns with the latent vulnerability theory29: emotional neglect may foster context-specific vulnerabilities rather than a global risk, explaining its limited trajectory-specific impact27,28. Third, the pervasive risk of rejection sensitivity resonates with the rejection sensitivity model33, which argues that chronic expectations of social rejection shape hypervigilant threat appraisals—predisposing adolescents to a range of maladaptive trajectories by amplifying both emotional distress and aggressive responses to perceived rejection34,35. Fourth, the differential protective effects of meaning in life (specific to dual-high-growth) support the hierarchical meaning-making model40, which emphasizes that meaning in life operates as a higher-order regulatory resource: it prioritizes buffering against the most severe psychopathological trajectories rather than exerting uniform protection38,40. Fifth, negative coping’s role as a core risk factor (undermining healthy functioning across all non-healthy trajectories) aligns with empirical work linking negative coping to exacerbated comorbid symptoms36,37, while positive coping’s universal protective effect underscores its role in disrupting maladaptive cognitive-behavioral cycles.

Collectively, these findings suggest that interventions should target pervasive risk factors (e.g., trauma-informed care for emotional abuse survivors to mitigate broad vulnerability21) and context-specific risks (e.g., tailored emotion-regulation training for those exposed to emotional neglect, addressing dual-high-decline vulnerability29). Cognitive restructuring to challenge negative social expectations33and skill-building for positive coping36can reduce non-healthy trajectory risk. Additionally, fostering meaning in life—via values clarification or purpose-focused interventions40—may be particularly impactful for adolescents at risk of the severe dual-high-growth trajectory. These strategies collectively promote healthy development by addressing both broad and specific pathways to co-occurring internalizing and externalizing problems.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although we employed a three-wave longitudinal research design on high school students in Chinese underdeveloped regions, the limited number of measurement waves may constrain our ability to capture nuanced long-term developmental trajectories of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior57. Future work should include more measurement points to better elaborate these developmental trajectories. Second, although we adopted a longitudinal research design with half-year intervals, it remains unclear whether this span is optimal for detecting developmental changes in the constructs under study. Future studies could adopt alternative designs, such as shorter-interval designs (e.g., experience sampling or diary methods;58) and longer-term follow-ups, to enhance temporal precision. Third, although the sample included 1,542 Chinese high school students in the underdeveloped regions, it was recruited from only one school in a single Chinese city, whose relative homogeneity may limit the generalizability of the findings. Replication in more diverse cultural and socioeconomic contexts is needed to evaluate the external validity of the identified trajectories and predictors. Fourth, the models tested in the present study were based solely on self-report measures, which introduces the potential for shared method variance, as well as under-reporting or over-reporting biases regarding sensitive experiences, such as childhood emotional abuse and neglect. Future research could adopt multi-informant assessment methods—such as peer-nominated aggression and parent-reported childhood abuse and neglect—to complement self-reported data and enhance the validity of findings. Finally, although this study incorporated predictors from contextual, personality, and cognitive domains, other influential factors—such as academic pressure59, major life events60, peer relationships61and community-level influences62—were not examined. Future studies should explore a wider range of potential determinants to develop a more comprehensive model of comorbid internalizing and externalizing problems.

Strengths and implications

Despite these limitations, our study offers several relevant contributions to the literature on adolescent internalizing and externalizing problems, with important implications for the development of interventions targeting comorbid cases. First, this three-wave longitudinal study adopted a dynamic perspective to identify heterogeneous developmental patterns of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior in adolescents, revealing distinct patterns that enhance our understanding of symptom progression over time. Second, this is the first study, to our knowledge, to examine the joint development of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior among high school adolescents in the underdeveloped regions within the cultural context of China. Third, our research extended the current understanding of factors shaping the joint developmental courses of internalizing and externalizing problems by uncovering the distinct predictive roles of key contextual, personality, and cognitive factors. Overall, these findings highlight that comorbid anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior do not follow an inevitable trajectory of deterioration; instead, they exhibit heterogeneous developmental patterns, including potential decline trajectory. Specifically, supportive parenting (as the antithesis of childhood emotional abuse and neglect), positive personality attributes (contrasting with maladaptive traits like rejection sensitivity), and adaptive cognitive styles (e.g., positive coping and a robust sense of meaning in life) serve as key protective factors in mitigating the onset, persistence and escalation of symptom severity among adolescents with co-occurring internalizing and externalizing problems. These insights offer a robust empirical foundation for developing targeted prevention strategies and early intervention programs.

Conclusions

This is the first study, to our knowledge, that illuminated the complex joint developmental trajectories of anxious/depressive symptoms and aggressive behavior among high school adolescents in underdeveloped regions of China. Using a three-wave longitudinal design, we identified four distinct short-term co-developmental patterns, underscoring that symptom progression is neither uniform nor inevitably negative. Importantly, the presence of a dual-high-decline group challenges deterministic views and offers clinical optimism. Contextual, personality, and cognitive factors significantly predicted trajectory membership. Childhood emotional abuse, rejection sensitivity, and negative coping served as pervasive core risk factors, and emotional neglect acted as a targeted risk factor, increasing vulnerability to adverse developmental trajectories, whereas positive coping and meaning in life emerged as a core protective factor and a specific regulatory protective factor, respectively, decreasing this vulnerability. These findings align with the developmental cascade theory and the p-factor theory, highlighting the interplay between environmental risks and individual differences in shaping mental health outcomes. The results advocate for targeted interventions that address early emotional abuse and neglect, reduce rejection sensitivity, foster adaptive coping skills, and enhance meaning in life. Future studies should employ more frequent assessments and diverse samples to improve generalizability and explore additional influencing mechanisms.
