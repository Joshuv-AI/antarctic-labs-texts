Health-Related Quality of Life, Challenges, and Experiences of Patients With Congenital Heart Disease After Cardiac Catheterization: A Mixed-Methods Study
Authors: Fatima Ali, Laila Akbar Ladak, Asra Usmani, Mahnoor Javaid, Taha Anwar, Babar Sultan Hasan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Pakistan, where health care resources for congenital heart disease (CHD) are expanding, this study aimed to assess the health-related quality of life (HRQoL), challenges, and experiences of patients with CHD after cardiac catheterization. Using a mixed-methods approach, quantitative arm compared the HRQoL of patients with CHD with that of age-matched siblings identified predictors of low HRQoL scores, and qualitative arm explored patient’s perceptions of quality of life, the challenges, and their experiences.MethodsIn this prospective, cross-sectional study conducted at The Aga Khan University Hospital, quantitative data were collected via pediatric quality-of-life questionnaires from 120 case–control pairs, while qualitative insights were gathered through semistructured interviews. Data analysis used descriptive stats, paired t tests, and content analysis for HRQoL themes.ResultsA total of 320 interviews were analyzed, including 120 patients with CHD, 120 age-matched siblings, and 80 parents. Quantitative data revealed high scores with no significant HRQoL differences between patients with CHD and controls. Minor differences were observed in physical and emotional domains, and higher-risk patients showed few concerns in cardiac-related HRQoL. Predictors such as age at diagnosis and Procedural Risk in Congenital Cardiac Catheterization (PREDIC3T) category were associated with low HRQoL scores. Qualitative thematic analysis highlighted the importance of physical and mental well-being, the impact of financial stability, and the need for empathetic communication from health care providers.ConclusionsThis study found high HRQoL scores in patients with CHD after cardiac catheterization, with significant associations of low scores to late age at diagnosis and PREDIC3T categories. Despite these positive outcomes, challenges like access to care and national support highlight the need for systemic improvements.

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) affects approximately 1% of live births globally, with >90% of cases in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).1,2 Since 1990, CHD prevalence has increased by 18.7%, largely due to advances in surgery and catheter-based interventions that have improved survival.3,4 Congenital cardiac catheterization interventions, ranging from simple to complex, may be used alone or alongside surgeries, such as patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) stenting before single-ventricle palliation, or pulmonary artery stenting after tetralogy of Fallot repair.5

While widely practiced in both high-income countries and LMICs,5,6 most published data on congenital cardiac interventions come from high-income countries and primarily address procedural aspects such as risk prediction,7,8 adverse events,9 radiation exposure,10 performance,11 and global quality improvement initiatives.5,6,12 As a result, data on patient-centered outcomes, such as health-related quality of life (HRQoL),13,14 remain limited. The HRQoL reflects a patient’s perceived physical, emotional, and social well-being and is a critical measure of patient-reported outcomes. However, challenges experienced during treatment, quality of life perceptions, and lived experiences remain underexplored particularly in LMICs, where poverty and low literacy can exacerbate these effects.15

This study aimed to assess the HRQoL of postcardiac catheterization patients compared with that of the age-matched siblings. Using a mixed-methods approach, it integrated quantitative and qualitative data to explore patient experiences, identify predictors of HRQoL, and analyze subgroup trends over 2 decades.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This prospective mixed-methods study at The Aga Khan University Hospital combined quantitative HRQoL assessment with qualitative insights from patients and parents to explore CHD experiences. Using concurrent triangulation, it emphasized participant engagement and comprehensive analysis, drawing on over 20 years of expertise in managing 150 to 200 congenital cardiac cases annually. Because of the COVID-19 pandemic, data collection was adapted to telephone interviews, as outlined in the published study protocol after the institutional review board approval (IRB 2020-3456-8839).15 Informed consent/assent was obtained from all patients and their parents.

Sample size

Quantitative data were collected based on 8 predictors,15 an effect size of 0.15, 80% power, and 0.05 significance. This resulted in a target sample size of approximately 108 patients, which we exceeded by 10% (n = 120) to allow for better subgroup analysis and potential data loss. Cases were postcatheterization patients (2000-2021), and controls were age-matched healthy siblings in the same family (whether immediate siblings, cousins, or distant relatives [±2 years]), and for qualitative analysis, 5 to 7 participants per age group (13-18, 19-25, and 25+ years) and parents of younger children (1-4 and 5-12 years) were interviewed to ensure thematic saturation and demographic balance.

Study population and recruitment

This study included patients with CHD (aged ≥2 years) who underwent 1 of the 5 common catheterization procedures—PDA and atrial septal defect (ASD) device closures, balloon pulmonary valvuloplasty, balloon aortic valvuloplasty (BAV), or coarctation of aorta (CoA) stenting at least 6 months before.6,12 Exclusions included diagnostic-only cases, other interventions, chromosomal or major noncardiac conditions, and postdiagnosis surgeries. Data elements and study metrics

The primary outcome of the study was HRQoL among postcardiac catheterization patients; however, challenges and experiences were also explored.

Demographic characteristics and clinical variables were collected for quantitative and qualitative analyses. Demographic data included age, sex, socioeconomic status (income, education, and residence), occupation, and family structure (nuclear or extended). Clinical data included CHD type, age at diagnosis, procedure type, procedure risk category (Procedural Risk in Congenital Cardiac Catheterization [PREDIC3T]).16 (Supplemental Table S1), reinterventions, adverse events categorized by 2 levels of severity17 (refer for definitions to Supplemental Table S2), and residual lesions.6,12 The efficacy of the procedures was classified as ideal, adequate, or inadequate based on established criteria6,12 (Supplemental Table S3).

Tools for HRQoL assessment

Quantitative data were collected using PedsQL, its Urdu version including both generic and cardiac modules, with both self-reported and parent–proxy versions. Scores (0-4) were reverse-scored and scaled to 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better HRQoL. Qualitative data were gathered via semistructured interviews by the primary investigator, using a guide developed in collaboration with cardiologists and HRQoL experts.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 22). Descriptive statistics summarized continuous variables using means and SDs and categorical variables using frequencies and percentages. Paired t tests were used to compare HRQoL between cases and controls. P values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant without adjustment for multiplicity. Cohen d (0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large) assessed HRQoL differences between cases and controls. Subanalyses compared patient and parent ratings (under 18) and HRQoL across 2 decades. Qualitative data underwent content analysis by 2 reviewers (L.A.L., F.A.), ensuring validity and cultural relevance.18,19

Quality control/rigor

To ensure data reliability, the research assistant was trained by principal investigators and completed a dry run. The first 30% of quantitative interviews were reviewed within 24to 48 hours. Qualitative interviews were conducted by the principal investigator. Findings followed the COREQ checklist for transparent reporting.18

Results

A total of 320 interviews were quantitatively analyzed, including 120 patients with CHD after cardiac catheterization, 120 age-matched siblings, and 80 parents. The qualitative analysis achieved thematic saturation after approximately 30 interviews with 16 parents of patients aged ≤12 years and 14 patients aged >12 years (Figure 1).

Cases and controls had similar baseline characteristics. Mean age was 15.65 ± 10.23 years, with 54.55% were men, 79.15% students, and 89.15% single, and 71.65% lived in nuclear families. Quantitative assessment of HRQoL

HRQoL assessment

General HRQoL

No statistically or clinically significant difference was found between patients with CHD and siblings (mean, 99.26 vs 99.68; Cohen d = −0.02). Small effect sizes were noted in physical and emotional well-being (d = −0.36 and −0.22, respectively).

Cardiac HRQoL

High scores were observed in the treatment, cognitive function, and heart domains (99.90, 99.56, and 98.75, respectively). Low-risk vs high-risk comparisons showed small effect sizes for treatment and cognitive issues (d = −0.31 and −0.34, respectively), with no statistical significance. Predictors analysis

A higher-risk PREDIC3T category (groups 3-5) was associated with lower HRQoL in both crude and adjusted models. Late diagnosis was also linked to lower HRQoL in the adjusted model. In contrast, the current age showed a positive association, with HRQoL improving as age increased. Discussion

This is the first study from Pakistan, an LMIC, to assess HRQoL and the challenges and experiences of patients with CHD following cardiac catheterization. Using a mixed-methods approach, we found that most patients reported high HRQoL scores, suggesting positive outcomes overall. However, certain factors like a late diagnosis and higher-risk procedures (based on PREDIC3T risk classification) were linked to lower HRQoL, while older age at the time of assessment was associated with better scores.

Beyond the numbers, sociocultural support, especially financial help and compassionate communication from health care providers, significantly influenced patient satisfaction. That said, challenges such as limited access to care, poor coordination between hospitals, and low national awareness of CHD were commonly reported and negatively affected overall quality of care.

Using the PedsQL Cardiac Module for a more detailed analysis, HRQoL scores were high across all domains. When comparing high-risk procedures16 (eg, balloon pulmonary valvotomy in neonates, coarctation stenting, and BAV at any age) with low-risk interventions (eg, PDA or ASD device closures), the differences in HRQoL scores were small. However, children in the high-risk group showed slightly greater challenges in the treatment-related and cognitive domains. This suggests the need for closer follow-up, especially of developmental and learning milestones. Families should be encouraged to raise any concerns about school performance early as timely referrals to developmental specialists may help.

These findings also highlight the need to study how social determinants such as income, distance to hospitals, and poverty interact with medical risk to influence HRQoL. Our results align with previous studies reporting improvements in HRQoL following catheterization. For example, children with ASD, PDA, or VSD showed increased HRQoL scores within 1 and 6 months postprocedurally, with similar trends observed in a Chinese cohort.13,14,19 In our study, patients were assessed at least 6 months after the procedure, and average HRQoL scores were even higher (mean, 97.47 ± 14.16). Predictor analysis revealed a positive association between current age and HRQoL, suggesting that quality of life tends to improve as patients grow older. This trend may reflect not only the long-term benefits of successful catheter-based interventions but also greater psychological adaptation and coping mechanisms that develop with age, a phenomenon often described as “response shift.” In contrast, patients who experienced a delayed diagnosis reported lower QoL, particularly in psychosocial domains. This discrepancy may be influenced by the perception that an earlier diagnosis was missed, leading families to feel that timely care was not provided. Such perceptions can negatively impact emotional well-being and trust in the health care system, ultimately lowering reported quality of life. These findings underscore the importance of early recognition, prompt diagnosis, and effective communication in shaping long-term patient and parent-reported outcomes.

However, our study did not include patients with failed or highly complex procedures, such as neonatal interventions, so results may not fully reflect the broader CHD population. Larger, multicenter studies are needed to better understand HRQoL in those groups.

When comparing catheterization with surgical treatment, our patients reported higher satisfaction. This contrasts with findings from Ladak et al,20 where surgical patients had lower HRQoL, especially in domains related to physical appearance due to scarring. The less-invasive nature of catheter-based procedures likely contributed to better outcomes in our study. Both studies noted that lesion complexity was linked to lower HRQoL. However, our higher scores may reflect successful outcomes and the exclusion of complex or failed cases.

Other studies, such as that by Mellion et al,21 found no difference in HRQoL between surgically treated patients with CHD and healthy controls. Differences in age range, sample size, and case complexity may explain these discrepancies, emphasizing that HRQoL depends on various personal and environmental factors, as outlined in the Wilson and Cleary model that describes both medical and nonmedical factors affecting HRQoL.22

In our study, cultural expectations and sex significantly influenced patient concerns. Many female patients and their parents being worried about the future marriage prospects and pregnancy and due to the associated social stigma, health issues were often hidden from potential in-laws or extended family (Central Illustration). Religious faith and support from extended family helped many families cope with challenges. Although some received financial aid from hospitals and community networks, many still faced cost burdens and treatment delays, especially in the public health care sector.

About 20% of participants were adults. While no subgroup analysis was done, most reported symptom relief, improved daily functioning, and hope for a healthy future. Common concerns included restenosis, device complications, activity restrictions, and the risk of passing CHD onto children. Empathetic communication and reassurance from doctors helped alleviate much of their anxiety.

In Pakistan, where over 22% live below the poverty line and the literacy rate is 58%, CHD awareness remains low. Many families learn of the diagnosis late, often leading to shock or denial. Limited access to cardiac centers, especially in rural areas, worsens these problems. Improving outcomes requires a multistep approach: training primary care providers for early CHD recognition and timely referral, enhancing communication between hospitals, creating a national CHD program and registry, and issuing government-funded health cards to reduce patient expenses.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, being a single-center study conducted at a referral-based private hospital, the findings may lack generalizability, as participants may not fully represent the broader Pakistani population. For instance, only 5% of participants were uneducated, potentially underrepresenting a key demographic characteristic. Multicenter studies are needed to validate these results.

Second, we did not assess preprocedural HRQoL due to funding constraints. Although qualitative interviews helped address this gap, findings may still be affected by recall bias. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced participant responses. To mitigate this, research assistant was trained in interviewing techniques, and participant privacy was ensured.

Third, controls were age-matched siblings (±2 years) from the same family. While this ensured environmental similarity, it may have introduced psychosocial bias, as siblings of chronically ill children may also experience reduced HRQoL. However, in our cohort, the procedures generally had ideal or adequate efficacy with limited residual morbidity, which may have reduced the caregiving burden.

Finally, although age was included as a continuous variable in regression models, stratified age group analysis was not performed due to limited statistical power. Future studies with larger samples may better explore age-specific trends in HRQoL.

Conclusion

The study found positive HRQoL outcomes in patients with CHD after cardiac catheterization, with high overall scores. However, delay in diagnosis and high-risk PREDIC3T categories were significant predictors of lower outcomes. Despite favorable results, challenges like limited access to care and insufficient national support highlight the need for systemic improvements to enhance patient outcomes and quality of life.
