Action observation therapy in pediatric cerebral palsy: an fNIRS-based investigation of cortical responses and balance—a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Melike Özipek, Mevhibe Saricaoglu, Lütfü Hanoğlu, Fatma Mutluay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral palsy (CP) is a group of permanent neurodevelopmental disorders affecting movement and posture, often accompanied by sensory, perceptual, and cognitive impairments. Action Observation Therapy (AOT), based on mirror neuron activation, has emerged as a promising approach for motor learning and rehabilitation. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of AOT on brain activation and motor-behavioral outcomes in children with CP.MethodsParticipants aged 7–15 years who met the inclusion criteria were randomly assigned to either the AOT Group (n = 12) or the Control Group (n = 12). The AOT Group received balance training combined with action observation videos, whereas the Control Group received conventional balance training with non-motor videos and written instructions. Both groups participated in the same exercise program for 30 min per day, five days per week, for three weeks, with progressively increasing difficulty. Pre- and post-treatment assessments included the Pediatric Balance Scale as the primary outcome measure, along with secondary outcomes of functional mobility (Timed Up and Go Test), trunk control (Trunk Impairment Scale), and gross motor function (Gross Motor Function Measure-88 sections D and E). Cortical hemodynamic responses during balance tasks on hard and soft surfaces were assessed using functional Near Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) as the mechanistic outcome measure.ResultsBoth groups showed significant improvements in balance performance, TUG scores, trunk control, and gross motor function after treatment (p < 0.05), with no significant between-group differences (p > 0.05). Exercise training and ground type significantly influenced cerebral oxygenation, with variations in oxyhemoglobin concentrations across brain regions depending on time, group, and video observation interactions (p < 0.05). Oxyhemoglobin levels decreased in the AOT Group, whereas the Control Group showed increases or surface-dependent fluctuations (p < 0.05).ConclusionsBoth AOT and conventional motor learning-based balance training improved balance and motor function in children with CP. However, AOT did not provide additional functional benefits. Exercise training and ground type significantly influenced cerebral oxygenation. Decreased oxyhemoglobin levels in the AOT Group and variable responses in the Control Group suggest modulation of cortical activation by sensorimotor context.Trial registration ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05995197).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12984-026-02097-1.

Introduction

Cerebral Palsy (CP) is a group of permanent neurodevelopmental disorders that affect movement and posture, often accompanied by sensory, perceptual, and cognitive impairments [1, 2]. One of the most common motor problems in children with CP is impaired balance. This impairment arises from various neuromuscular and structural deficiencies, including poor selective motor control, the persistence of primitive reflexes, inadequate postural control, and limited joint range of motion—particularly in the knees and ankles [3]. These deficits contribute to increased postural sway, misalignment of the hips and ankles, a narrowed base of support, and overall balance dysfunction [3, 4].

Balance is essential for functional movements [5]. In children with CP, impairments in postural control mechanisms result in significantly reduced balance responses compared to typically developing peers, leading to functional limitations in daily life activities [4]. Moreover, individuals with CP exhibit delays and inadequacies in both compensatory postural adjustments—driven by sensory feedback—and anticipatory adjustments, which are regulated by the central nervous system. Studies have shown that these mechanisms are particularly impaired in children with hemiplegic and diplegic CP [4]. Given this impairment, balance training has been shown to improve postural control and balance performance in children with CP. Consequently, improvements in balance have been reported to enhance functional skills in children with CP [6].

Action Observation Therapy (AOT) is based on the activation of mirror neuron networks located in motor-related cortical regions, including the parietal and frontal areas [7, 8]. This observation-based activation supports motor learning through observation by activating the same neural structures involved in the execution of movement [9–11]. Compared to verbal instructions alone, observation of motor actions facilitates motor learning. Therapy involves observing, imitating, and repeating the movements. This process improves motor planning and supports neuroplasticity, ultimately enhancing motor performance [11, 12]. Studies show that balance tasks are performed more quickly when repeated after observation [12–15]. While AOT is used in the management of pain and motor impairments in individuals with orthopedic disorders, stroke, Parkinson’s disease, and other neurological disorders, its use in CP rehabilitation is less frequent [14–16]. Previous studies investigating the effects of AOT on balance and gross motor function in children with CP have reported mixed findings. While some studies demonstrated improvements in balance and motor performance outcomes, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have concluded that the current evidence remains limited and inconclusive, particularly regarding standing balance and gross motor function outcomes [7, 17].

Functional Near Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a non-invasive, portable neuroimaging method that allows real-time monitoring of cortical activation by measuring changes in oxy- and deoxyhemoglobin concentrations [11–13, 18]. Its suitability for use, particularly in children with CP, is due to its ability to allow measurement during movement, its low cost, and its safety. Its tolerance for movement, low cost, and safety make it particularly suitable for use in children with CP [19]. fNIRS has been widely used to assess brain activity during motor tasks such as standing, walking, and balance exercises [11, 20, 21]. Studies have shown that fNIRS can detect altered activation patterns in children with CP compared to healthy peers, especially in sensorimotor and prefrontal regions. Moreover, it has been effectively used to evaluate cortical responses to action observation, highlighting its potential in assessing the neurophysiological effects of AOT-based interventions [22, 23].

Previous research has predominantly concentrated on the functional recovery and rehabilitation of upper extremities in children with CP through the use of AOT [24–28]. In contrast, studies addressing balance and gross motor functions remain limited in number [5].

The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of balance training combined with AOT on balance performance and cortical activation, compared with balance training alone, in children with spastic CP, using fNIRS.

Materials and methods

Participants and sample size

The sample size was determined a priori using G-Power software (version 3.1) for the ‘primary outcome’ balance improvement measured by ‘Pediatric Balance Scale (PBS)’ [29]. This was based on a previous dual-task training study in children with cerebral palsy reporting significant PBS improvements (large effect size) in a similar population [30]. Assuming an effect size of f = 0.70, a statistical power of 0.90, and an alpha level of 0.05, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 12 participants per group (total n = 24) would be required to detect a significant between-group difference. The patient population was children aged 7–15, diagnosed with CP, of both genders, who met the inclusion criteria and volunteered to participate in the study. Inclusion criteria were: diagnosis of spastic CP, Gross Motor Function Classification System levels I–II, ability to cooperate with evaluations, absence of visual or hearing impairments, and motor impairment as the primary affected domain according to the health board report. Exclusion criteria included botulinum toxin injection or orthopedic surgery within the previous six months and diagnosis of dyskinetic, ataxic, or hypotonic CP. Eligible participants were randomly assigned to either the AOT Group or the Control Group using a computer-generated randomization sequence prepared by an independent researcher who was not involved in the assessments or interventions. Group allocation was concealed until assignment. Participants were enrolled and assigned by a researcher not involved in outcome assessment. Outcome assessments and fNIRS data analyses were performed by a blinded researcher who was unaware of group allocation.

Study design

The study was designed according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by the Istanbul Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Ethical Report No: E-10840098-772.02-3957), registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with the number NCT05995197. The relevant rules and regulations carried out all methods, and written informed consent was obtained from the patient and their relatives.

The experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with our previously published study protocol [31]. The study has four stages. The first stage was the selection of participants and their inclusion in the study; the second stage was the pre-rehabilitation evaluation; the third stage was the rehabilitation process; and finally, the fourth stage was repeating the evaluations to show the post-rehabilitation effect.

This study was a single-blinded randomized controlled trial. Before exercise training, all patients’ history and demographic information were taken, and pre-and post-treatment evaluation measurements were made by a physiotherapist who is competent in pediatric evaluation. Participants were recruited between July and October 2025. All interventions and assessments were conducted in a dedicated physiotherapy and neurorehabilitation laboratory designed for clinical evaluations and fNIRS measurements, providing a standardized, quiet, and well-equipped environment. Outcome measurements

Primary outcome

Pediatric balance scale

Pediatric Balance Scale (PBS) is used to assess the functional balance skills of school-aged children. The scale consists of 14 items, each scored between 0 (lowest function) and 4 (highest function). The highest possible score is 56. A higher score indicates good balance function [32].

Secondary outcomes

Timed up and go test

Timed Up and Go Test (TUG) assesses functional mobility and static and dynamic balance. Participants are positioned in a seated chair without armrests, with hips, knees, and ankles flexed at 90°. They then stand up, walk 3 m, return, and sit back down in the chair. The timer is started with a “go” command given by the therapist and stopped when the hips touch the chair. The process is repeated three times, and the performance is recorded by the therapist in seconds. A shorter time indicates good balance performance [33].

The trunk impairment scale

The Trunk Impairment Scale (TIS) measures static sitting balance, dynamic sitting balance, and trunk coordination. It consists of three sub-sections: static, dynamic, and coordination. The highest possible scores for static, dynamic, and coordination are 7, 10, and 6, respectively. Therefore, the total TIS score ranges from 0 to 23. A high score indicates good static and dynamic trunk balance and trunk coordination [34].

The gross motor function measure-88

The Gross Motor Function Measure (GMFM-88) consists of 5 main sections and 88 items. The supine-prone and rolling position section contains 17 items. The sitting section contains 20 items, the crawling-kneeling section contains 14 items, the standing section contains 13 items, and the walking-running-stair climbing section contains 24 items. Each activity is scored between 0 and 3 according to the child’s level of performance: 0 points for those who cannot initiate the activity, 1 point for those who can initiate it independently, 2 points for those who can partially complete it, and 3 points for those who can complete it independently. A percentage performance calculation is made using the scores obtained from all sections [35].

Mechanistic outcome

fNIRS recordings

fNIRS is a new method for measuring local cerebral blood flow. It uses optical scattering and absorption of near-infrared wavelengths of light (700–950 nm) to provide information about brain activity in the measured region [36]. fNIRS recordings were obtained from the fNIRS device (Medical Technologies LLC, Berlin, Germany) using the NIRStar Acquisition (NIRx Medizintechnik GmbH, Germany) program. Recordings from 20 channels of 8 sources, 8 detectors, and 34 optodes were taken. Optodes were positioned on the premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and primary motor cortex to scan motor areas by the EEG 10–20 system (Fig. 2). This configuration was selected to specifically capture cortical activation related to balance control and motor execution during standing tasks. Optode placement targeting motor-related cortical areas was based on previous fNIRS literature demonstrating activation of the primary motor cortex, premotor cortex, and supplementary motor area during motor execution and postural control tasks [37, 38].

The distance between optodes was adjusted to a maximum of 3.4 cm. The sampling rate was 3.47 Hz. The regions corresponding to the fNIRS channel locations are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

During fNIRS recordings, participants watched two videos demonstrating balance tasks: standing on hard and soft ground. Each video featured a person maintaining a normal, upright posture without performing any movements, allowing participants to understand the tasks clearly.

An essential point in evaluating the balance function was the ground used. The ground used in the balance test varied depending on the purpose and the person’s balance level, and the hard and soft ground were most often preferred. Hard ground provided a more stable surface and made it easier for the person to maintain balance. Soft ground, on the other hand, was a more variable and unstable surface, thus making it difficult for the person to maintain balance. Regarding proprioception, muscle activation, and coordination, soft ground appears to have more sensitive properties for balance assessment.

fNIRS recordings were then performed with a three-minute break between each experiment. During fNIRS recordings, experiments were conducted with patients standing 1.25 m away from a 1.80 m×1.20 m screen. fNIRS recordings were started with a 20-second rest period. The paradigm included standing balance tasks on hard and soft ground, repeated six times for each condition. Each mission was 20 s in length. Patients watched a video for 20 s, stood on the floor for 20 s, and then rested for 20 s. The tasks were repeated first with the standing task process on hard ground and then with the same process on soft ground. Two separate videos were prepared for two groups. During fNIRS recordings, participants in the AOT group watched action observation videos demonstrating the balance tasks performed on both hard and soft surfaces, in which a peer maintained an upright standing posture under each condition (Fig. 2). The videos were designed to match the experimental protocol, including standing balance on stable (hard) and unstable (soft) surfaces. In the control group, participants watched a documentary film without motor-related content. The observed tasks corresponded directly to the experimental conditions used during fNIRS measurements. Pre-intervention fNIRS recordings were repeated using the same protocol following the rehabilitation period.

Exercise training

The physiotherapist explained the intervention procedures to both groups. During the sessions, children with CP sat comfortably approximately 1 m from a computer screen and were instructed to remain still during video observation. Each session consisted of a 3-minute observation phase followed by a 3-minute exercise performance phase.

In the AOT group, participants watched a 3-minute action observation video showing a same-age peer performing the target motor tasks in a step-by-step sequence (Fig. 3). A standardized “observe-then-perform” protocol was applied, in which participants first observed the complete movement sequence and then immediately reproduced the same movements under the supervision of the physiotherapist.

In the control group, participants received the same exercise instructions and performed the same exercises. However, instead of action observation videos, they watched a 3-minute documentary video without motor content before performing the exercises.

AOT was selected because previous studies demonstrated its effectiveness in supporting motor learning and improving motor function in children with CP through activation of the mirror neuron system [9, 10]. The intervention protocol used in this study was developed based on previously reported effective AOT applications. Abdelhaleem et al. reported that AOT protocols in children with CP vary between 2 and 12 weeks in duration, with frequencies ranging from 1 to 5 sessions per week and session durations between 15 and 60 min [7]. Accordingly, a home-based program consisting of 15 sessions over 3 weeks, conducted 5 days per week, was designed to optimize feasibility and family adherence.

The observation and execution durations were adapted from the protocols described by Molinaro et al. and Buccino et al. [10, 39]. To ensure standardization, each video included 3 min of observation followed by 3 min of task execution. The videos were prepared using age-appropriate peer models, and the tasks were organized progressively from simple to complex to enhance attention, motivation, and task engagement. Furthermore, the intervention was designed as a home-based, family-centered program to support family participation and facilitate integration into the child’s natural environment. All sessions were completed as planned, indicating that the selected protocol was feasible and well accepted.

Progression: The applied physiotherapy program was structured progressively over three weeks to target postural control and balance. In the first week, exercises focused on static balance, including standing on both feet or one foot with eyes open or closed on varying surfaces (Fig. 3A). The second week introduced more challenging postures, such as tandem stance and weight-shifting exercises, as well as initiating stepping movements (Fig. 3B). In the third week, dynamic balance tasks were emphasized, including multi-directional stepping and ball-kicking exercises performed on unstable surfaces (Fig. 3C).

Analysis of fNIRS data

fNIRS data were preprocessed using Homer 3, an open-source, Matlab-based software package. The steps followed aligned with the latest recommendations for fNIRS data processing. In the fNIRS analysis, noisy channels with very low optical density (coefficient of variation > 10%) were excluded using the enPruneChannels function. The remaining raw optical density data were then converted to changes in optical density (OD) using the hmrIntensity2OD function. Motion artifacts were corrected using a Wavelet transform. The hmrR_MotionCorrectWavelet function (iqr = 1.50) and the hmrR_MotionArtifactByChannel function (tMotion = 0.5, tMask = 1.0, STDEVthresh = 20.0, AMPthresh = 5.0) were applied to correct and detect motion artifacts, respectively. Trials with movement artifacts occurring 2 s before the presentation of the stimuli and 20 s after the presentation of the stimuli were excluded from the analysis. The signal was filtered with a high-pass cut-off frequency of 0.01 Hz (hmrBandpassFilt) to account for low-frequency aberrations and further filtered with a low-pass cut-off frequency of 0.5 Hz using a third-order Butterworth filter to remove the effects of self-generated physiological components. The preprocessed optical density data of both wavelengths were then converted into oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (Hb) concentration changes using the modified Beer–Lambert equation (ppf = 1.0 ± 1.0). The age- and wavelength-adjusted differential path length factor (DPF) required for this equation was calculated according to ref. [40]. To calculate the hemodynamic response function (HRF), it was deconvolved using a General Linear Model (GLM) based on ordinary least squares regression over a time range of -5 to 20 s. The hmrR_GLM function (trange = -2.0 to 14.0, glmSolveMethod = 1, idxBasis = 1, paramsBasis = 1.0 1.0, rhoSD_ssThresh = 40.0, flagNuisanceRMethod = 2, driftOrder = 3, c_vector = 0) was used. To minimize the non-brain component from superficial tissue, the average signal from the channels with the shortest source-detector distances was used in the setup (rhoSD_ssThresh = 40, flagNuisanceRMethod = 2). The HRF was modeled using a sequential set of Gaussian HRF basis functions with a standard deviation of 0.5 s and means separated by 0.5 s. A third-order polynomial was included to account for baseline drift. The HRFs recovered for each channel (HbO and Hb) were baseline-corrected by subtracting the average HRF value between − 5 and 0 s from each data point. Finally, hemodynamic response functions (HbO and Hb) were determined for each channel from 2 s before the onset of the stimulus to 15 s after the onset of the stimulus. However, only the period from 0 to 15 s after stimulus onset was included in the statistical analysis, and the average HbO value during this period was used.

No significant differences were observed in the number of retained trials after preprocessing between groups.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Science) version 25.0 was used for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics of the data were presented as mean and standard deviation values. The normal distribution of variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test. Differences between the two groups were examined using the Independent Samples t-Test for continuous variables (e.g., age, GMFM level, duration of physical therapy) and the Chi-Square Test for categorical variables (e.g., gender, type of delivery, type of CP).

Differences between groups were evaluated with the Independent Samples t-Test. To observe the treatment effect within groups, comparisons of pre- and post-treatment values were conducted using the Paired Samples t-Test. In addition, following the standard analytical approach for pre–post intervention studies, between-group comparisons were performed using the Change Scores (Post − Pre), which were analyzed with an Independent Samples t-test.

Differences between groups in fNIRS channels were examined using the Independent Samples t-Test. To observe the treatment effect within groups, pre- and post-treatment comparisons of fNIRS channels were conducted using the Paired Samples t-Test.

For the analysis of fNIRS data, the data from the AOT and control groups were compared using ANOVA. In the ANOVA analysis for each channel, the AOT and control groups represented the between-group factors, while condition (standing on hard ground, standing on soft ground, and resting) and time (pre and post) served as within-group factors.

Significance for all tests was determined based on Greenhouse-Geisser corrected p-values, and a significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

fNIRS analysis results

Overall, AOT resulted in a reduction of HbO responses in motor-related cortical areas, whereas the control group showed increased activation, suggesting more efficient neural processing following the intervention.

Between-group differences

At baseline, limited between-group differences were observed. During the hard ground protocol, HbO concentrations were higher in the AOT group in the left somatosensory association area (Channel 2) and right somatosensory association area (Channel 12), whereas the control group showed higher activation in Channels 7 and 16 (p < 0.05). No significant differences were found under soft ground or resting conditions.

Following the intervention, the control group demonstrated higher HbO concentrations than the AOT group in several motor-related regions, particularly during resting and standing conditions across both ground types (Channels 1, 4, 5, and 16; p < 0.05). No significant between-group differences were observed during action observation tasks (Fig. 4; Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effectiveness of balance training combined with AOT compared to conventional balance training in children with spastic CP. The findings revealed that exercise and surface variability significantly influenced HbO concentration in the premotor, supplementary motor, somatosensory, and primary motor cortex regions. Notably, a reduction in HbO concentration was observed in the Premotor/Supplementary Motor Area and Somatosensory Association Area after exercise in the AOT Group, whereas the Control Group exhibited either increased levels or surface-dependent fluctuations. Both groups demonstrated significant post-intervention improvements in balance and gross motor function; however, there was no significant difference in effectiveness between the AOT and conventional physiotherapy approaches. These results underscore the role of environmental factors-such as surface type and task variability-in modulating motor and sensory cortical responses and suggest that the careful selection of training activities and settings is essential in designing effective rehabilitation programs.

To ensure group comparability, the demographic and clinical characteristics of participants were analyzed prior to the intervention. The two groups were similar in terms of age, gender, CP type and functional level, use of medications and assistive devices, history of orthopedic surgery, and physiotherapy frequency. This homogeneity minimized confounding variables and strengthened the internal validity of the study, enabling a clearer interpretation of intervention effects.

Previous studies revealed no standard protocol regarding the method, duration, and frequency of AOT application [7, 10, 25, 39]. Our study was conducted in accordance with the literature. Children and families had a very high level of compliance with the treatment program. All planned sessions were completed without any interruptions. Conducting the sessions in a home environment ensured successful treatment and active participation from the children.

Both groups showed significant improvements in the primary outcome, including functional balance and mobility, as reflected by PBS and TUG scores, with no between-group differences. These findings further support the effectiveness of task-specific balance training, regardless of AOT inclusion. Improvements in trunk control, although not directly targeted, may be attributed to increased postural demands and enhanced core activation during dynamic balance tasks [41–43].

Previous studies have primarily investigated AOT in relation to upper extremity rehabilitation in children with CP, while evidence on its effects on gross motor function remains limited [10, 26, 44–46]. Some studies have reported within-group improvements in gross motor performance following AOT, but between-group differences were often inconsistent or only evident in specific GMFM subdomains [5, 24, 47, 48]. For instance, improvements were frequently noted in domains such as standing, walking, and stair climbing (GMFM-D, GMFM-E), yet not consistently superior to conventional therapies.

Consistent with these findings, our study revealed significant improvements in the secondary outcome, GMFM-D and GMFM-E scores, in both the AOT and Control Groups, but without a statistically significant difference between them. These results suggest that the structured balance training administered to both groups contributed substantially to functional gains, potentially overshadowing the specific contribution of AOT. The short duration of the intervention and the relatively high baseline functional level of participants may have further limited the observable impact of AOT on gross motor outcomes.

When discussing the absence of intergroup differences in GMFM-88 scores, the relatively short intervention duration (3 weeks; 15 sessions; approximately 7.5 h of application in total) should be considered. According to the contemporary motor learning and neurorehabilitation literature, clinically significant changes in gross motor function in children with cerebral palsy generally require significantly higher intervention doses. Systematic reviews and syntheses of evidence have consistently emphasized that effective neurorehabilitation interventions are characterized by high-intensity, task-specific training and cumulative application doses typically of 30–40 h or more [49, 50]. Similarly, studies investigating intensive training paradigms such as restraint-based movement therapy and ambidexterity training have reported functional gains following significantly higher training volumes [51, 52].

In this context, the lack of significant changes in GMFM-88 in the present study may be attributed to an insufficient intervention dose rather than a lack of intervention efficacy. This interpretation is further supported by evidence suggesting that early neurophysiological changes can precede measurable improvements in standardized clinical outcome measures, often requiring longer intervention durations to capture functional reorganization [38]. Therefore, although the observed neurophysiological and balance-related changes indicate early adaptations, longer and more intensive training protocols are likely necessary to generate measurable changes in gross motor function.

This study used fNIRS to assess cortical activation during AOT videos and balance tasks on varying surfaces, based on changes in hemoglobin concentration. fNIRS was chosen for its ability to monitor real-time neural activity and oxygenation in motor cortical regions during movement [53]. Previous research has shown that motor learning induces neuroplastic changes in both cortical and subcortical areas [54]. In children with CP, studies have reported reduced interhemispheric connectivity but increased motor cortex activity during lower limb movements [20], and greater activation in motor and prefrontal areas following robot-assisted gait training [21] as well as during various lower limb tasks compared to typically developing peers [55]. Our results align with these findings and emphasize the role of multiple cortical regions in postural control. Before treatment, tasks on firm ground activated right-hemisphere regions, including the somatosensory association area, primary motor cortex, premotor/supplementary motor area, and Broca’s area. On soft ground, left somatosensory and Broca’s areas showed higher HbO levels, suggesting increased cortical demand for postural adaptation on unstable surfaces. Similar hemodynamic responses were observed during both AOT and neutral videos, indicating motor-related areas may be activated during general visual processing, possibly due to attentional engagement. Group comparisons revealed broader pre-treatment activation in the AOT Group, suggesting greater cortical readiness for motor observation. Post-treatment, both groups showed increased HbO levels during balance tasks, particularly on soft surfaces. The AOT Group exhibited marked increases in the right premotor/supplementary motor area, while the Control Group showed activation across somatosensory and motor-related regions. Additionally, the AOT Group maintained higher activation during AOT, whereas the Control Group showed greater post-treatment responses to surface-related challenges. These results indicate distinct cortical adaptation patterns shaped by intervention type and task demands.

Clinically, both the AOT and Control Groups demonstrated significant improvements in balance and gross motor function following the intervention. Although no statistically significant differences were found between the groups, fNIRS data revealed distinct cortical activation patterns that reflected the specific mechanisms engaged by each approach. Post-treatment increases in HbO in both groups support the neurophysiological efficacy of motor learning-based rehabilitation. In the AOT Group, broader and more prominent HbO increases were observed in regions such as the supplementary motor area, premotor cortex, somatosensory cortex, and Broca’s area, particularly during AOT tasks. This pattern suggests enhanced engagement of motor planning and observation-related networks. These findings align with the underlying principles of AOT, which relies on the activation of the mirror neuron system—notably within the premotor and inferior frontal cortices—to simulate and reinforce motor planning and execution. In children with CP, where motor execution is often disrupted due to damage in corticospinal tracts and basal ganglia, AOT may activate these regions through observation, providing an alternative pathway to stimulate motor-related networks and facilitate cortical reorganization.

Furthermore, balance tasks performed on soft or unstable surfaces led to notable bilateral HbO increases in areas such as the premotor cortex, primary motor cortex, somatosensory cortex, and Broca’s area. These activations suggest that maintaining posture under challenging sensory conditions requires greater cortical involvement due to reduced somatosensory feedback and increased reliance on visual and vestibular inputs.

Overall, the observed cortical responses are consistent with the expected functional roles of the involved brain regions. Increased activation in the supplementary motor area reflects enhanced motor planning and the encoding of postural strategies, while the primary motor cortex is associated with voluntary motor output and muscle control. The premotor cortex, a key component of the mirror neuron system, plays a critical role in motor observation and preparatory activation. Lastly, the somatosensory cortex contributes to sensorimotor integration and the processing of proprioceptive feedback, which are particularly crucial during balance tasks performed under challenging sensory conditions.

In contrast to the AOT Group’s anticipatory and planning-based activation patterns, the Control Group demonstrated increased HbO levels primarily in response to task difficulty and environmental demands (e.g., surface instability). Increased cortical oxygenation during challenging motor tasks is generally interpreted as reflecting greater attentional, sensorimotor, and executive processing demands. Conversely, reduced cortical activation following motor learning has been associated with improved neural efficiency and task automatization, whereby motor performance can be maintained with lower cortical resource utilization. This interpretation is consistent with the neural efficiency hypothesis, which proposes that practice and skill acquisition may lead to more economical recruitment of cortical networks during task execution [56–58]. From a motor learning perspective, early stages of skill acquisition are typically characterized by widespread cortical recruitment. In contrast, continued practice may result in more selective and efficient activation patterns as motor control becomes increasingly refined and predictive. Previous neuroimaging and fNIRS studies in motor learning and rehabilitation have similarly reported reductions in cortical oxygenation or activation following training, particularly when performance improvements are accompanied by enhanced movement efficiency and reduced cognitive demand [38, 59, 60]. Therefore, the post-intervention HbO decrease observed in the AOT Group may reflect a transition toward more efficient sensorimotor processing and anticipatory motor control rather than reduced engagement per se. In contrast, the increased HbO response observed in the Control Group may indicate continued reliance on compensatory or effort-dependent cortical recruitment during task execution. Nevertheless, given the complexity of interpreting hemodynamic responses in pediatric neurorehabilitation, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in longitudinal studies combining behavioral and multimodal neurophysiological measures.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is well established that both cortical and subcortical regions play critical roles in motor skill learning and contribute to changes in functional neural networks. In particular, the cerebellum has been shown to be essential for maintaining balance and for the transfer of learned motor skills to different tasks. However, due to technical limitations, cerebellar and subcortical activity could not be assessed in the present study. The use of fNIRS allowed measurement of brain activity only in cortical regions.

Second, the relatively small sample size and the inability to include participants with homogeneous cerebral palsy subtypes and functional levels may limit the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution and need to be confirmed by future studies with larger and more homogeneous samples. A major limitation of this study is the short intervention duration of only 3 weeks. This period may represent an insufficient dose to produce measurable changes in clinical scales assessing gross motor function in children with CP. In particular, the absence of between-group differences in GMFM outcomes may be attributable to the limited intervention duration rather than the ineffectiveness of either intervention approach, as improvements in gross motor functional capacity often require longer treatment periods to become detectable. Another limitation of the study is that the Control Group watched a 3-minute documentary video without motor actions prior to the exercise session. Although this procedure helped standardize screen exposure across participants, it may not have adequately controlled for attentional engagement or cognitive load. Documentary content can vary in its level of interest and cognitive demand and may therefore elicit different degrees of attentional allocation compared to other types of visually presented stimuli.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that both AOT and traditional motor learning-based interventions can induce functional improvements accompanied by measurable cortical changes. However, AOT may offer additional benefits by engaging cognitive-motor circuits involved in observation and planning, potentially enhancing anticipatory control and promoting more efficient neuroplasticity. The use of fNIRS provides a valuable window into these neural processes, reinforcing the role of targeted, mechanism-based therapies in pediatric neurorehabilitation for children with CP.
