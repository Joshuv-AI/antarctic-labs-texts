Time-dependent metabolic effects of portion-control plate interventions in adults with chronic diseases: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shiyu Zhang, Yanjing Chen, Caihua Ye, Chengying Yu, Chu Wang, Xinjun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDietary modification is central to chronic disease management, but conventional education-based approaches often require sustained self-regulation, which is challenging for populations with limited health literacy. Portion-control plates are an environmentally friendly tool that uses visual segmentation to guide portion selection. However, quantitative evidence on their metabolic benefits and whether these effects persist remains limited.AimThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the metabolic effects of portion-control plate interventions in adults with chronic diseases and to examine whether these effects vary across follow-up durations.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane library, CKNI, VIP, Wanfang were searched from January 1999 to July 2025. Data were pooled to calculate mean differences and 95% confidence intervals using a random or fixed effect model. To assess temporal dynamics, analyses were stratified by follow-up duration (≤ 3 months, 3–6 months, and 6–12 months).ResultsFourteen RCTs involving 1,762 participants were included. Body weight showed early reduction at 3 months (MD = -3.68 kg, 95% CI -7.78 to -0.42 ), attenuating by 6 months (MD = -1.77 kg, 95% CI − 4.46 to 0.92). Hemoglobin A1c remained relatively stable across time points (-1.08%, -0.72%, and -1.10% at 3, 6, and 12 months; overall MD = -0.82%). Fasting glucose improved progressively from 6 to 12 months (P = 0.02 for subgroup difference). Low-density lipoprotein reductions were sustained at 6 and 12 months, while diastolic blood pressure improved only at 12 months (MD = -2.72 mmHg). Evidence certainty was highest for 12-month HbA1c and lipid outcomes.ConclusionPortion-control plate interventions produce modest metabolic improvements with distinct time-dependent patterns, including early weight reduction, sustained glycaemic and lipid benefits, and delayed vascular effects. As a low-burden environmental strategy that can be easily delivered by public health practitioners and primary care providers, visually guided portion control may represent a scalable dietary intervention for chronic disease management.Trial registrationThe review was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD420251061435).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03380-9.

Background

Chronic diseases are the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 74% of all deaths globally [1, 2]. The increasing prevalence of these conditions, together with their associated disability burden, has placed substantial pressure on health systems and healthcare expenditures [3]. Among the modifiable determinants of chronic disease, unhealthy dietary behaviours are widely recognised as a major contributor to disease development and progression [4]. Poor dietary patterns, including excessive portion sizes, imbalanced nutrient intake and unhealthy food choices, disrupt energy regulation and increase the risk of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes, obesity and cardiovascular disease [5]. Consequently, improving dietary behaviour remains a cornerstone of chronic disease prevention and management.

Despite the central role of diet in chronic disease management, achieving sustained dietary behaviour change remains challenging. Conventional dietary interventions, such as educational programmes, printed materials and digital health tools, primarily rely on conscious self-regulation and sustained cognitive engagement [6, 7]. However, the effectiveness of these approaches is often limited by factors such as health literacy, motivation, and long-term adherence, particularly among individuals living with chronic diseases [8]. These challenges have prompted growing interest in environmental and behavioural strategies that can facilitate healthier choices while reducing cognitive burden.

One such strategy involves the use of portion-control plates (PCP), which translate dietary recommendations into visually guided eating cues. These plates typically incorporate segmented designs and visual indicators (e.g., colour-coded zones or proportional divisions) to guide portion size and food composition during meals [9]. By providing immediate visual references at the point of consumption, portion-control plates simplify portion estimation and may support healthier dietary choices in routine eating contexts.

From a behavioural science perspective, portion-control plates can be conceptualised as a form of visual choice architecture. By embedding behavioural cues within the eating environment, these tools may influence dietary behaviour through both automatic cue-driven responses and conscious dietary regulation, consistent with dual-process models of decision-making [10, 11]. Emerging studies suggest that such visually guided interventions may improve dietary adherence, enhance food selection, and lead to modest improvements in metabolic outcomes among individuals with chronic diseases [9, 12–14]. However, the existing evidence base remains heterogeneous, with variations in study design, sample size, and intervention duration contributing to inconsistent findings.

Importantly, the temporal sustainability of these metabolic effects remains unclear. Existing trials report outcomes across diverse follow-up periods, ranging from acute post-meal assessments to evaluations at 3, 6, or 12 months; yet no synthesis has systematically examined whether intervention effects persist, attenuate, or accumulate over time. Understanding these temporal dynamics has important clinical implications. If metabolic benefits diminish rapidly, short-term or intermittent implementation may be most appropriate; conversely, if benefits accumulate or stabilise, sustained use may be necessary to achieve optimal outcomes. The lack of evidence on the ideal length of portion-control plate interventions for particular metabolic goals, such as body weight, glycaemic control, lipid profiles and cardiovascular indicators, restricts the capacity to develop effective and targeted dietary strategies. These questions are particularly relevant for adults living with chronic diseases, especially older individuals who may experience declining cognitive capacity, limited health literacy, and reduced motivation for sustained behaviour change [15–17]. In such contexts, low-effort and visually guided dietary tools may offer a practical and scalable approach to supporting healthier eating behaviours.

Although several randomised controlled trials have evaluated portion-control plate–based dietary interventions among adults with chronic diseases, their reported effects on metabolic outcomes remain inconsistent [14, 18–19]. Previous meta-analyses have examined portion-control tools in general populations [20], weight management among individuals with obesity [21], or the broader effects of tableware on eating behaviour [22]. However, none have specifically focused on adults with chronic diseases or investigated how metabolic responses to portion-control plate interventions evolve over time.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the metabolic effects of portion-control plate interventions in adults with chronic diseases and to examine their temporal patterns across different follow-up durations. By synthesising evidence from randomised controlled trials and conducting time-stratified analyses, this study seeks to clarify the sustainability of intervention effects and inform the design of more effective and scalable dietary strategies for chronic disease management. Besides, clarifying these temporal patterns may help public health practitioners and primary care providers tailor intervention duration and reinforcement strategies to individual patient needs, supporting more efficient and targeted implementation in community settings.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO on 28 May 2025(registration number: CRD420251061435) and reported in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [23]. After registration, the analysis plan was refined to include time-stratified subgroup analyses by follow-up duration based on observed heterogeneity in temporal patterns across outcomes.

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design (PICOS) framework, as presented in Table 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria based on the PICOS framework

Eligibility criteria were defined according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design (PICOS) framework. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials (RCT) involving adults (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with chronic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, obesity, or cardiovascular disease. Interventions consisted of PCP–based dietary interventions, either implemented as a standalone tool or combined with structured dietary education, while comparator groups used standard tableware or received conventional dietary education without portion-control plates. Studies were required to report at least one relevant outcome related to metabolic indicators (e.g., weight, BMI, WC, HbA1c, FPG, lipid profiles, or blood pressure) or dietary behaviors (e.g., energy intake, portion size, food selection, or eating patterns). Studies were excluded if they involved healthy participants as control groups, employed non-randomized or quasi-experimental designs, reported insufficient, missing, or irretrievable outcome data, or lacked accessible full text after attempts to contact corresponding authors.

Search strategy

The literature search used terms and relevant medical subject headings (MeSH) related to portion-control plates, tableware, dietary behaviors, and metabolic outcomes. More details were provided in Table A.1. Seven electronic databases were searched for eligible studies from July 1999 to 15 July 2025: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP. The reference lists of included studies and previously published reviews were searched manually to identify additional publications. Searches were restricted to English and Chinese publications.

Study selection and data extraction

Study selection followed PRISMA 2020 recommendations (Items 8–9). Records were imported into NoteExpress 21 (Beijing Aegean Software, 2022) for deduplication. Two reviewers (SZ and CY) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment for final eligibility. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (XJ). Reasons for exclusion were documented in the PRISMA flow diagram.

A standardized data extraction form was developed and pilot-tested prior to use. Two reviewers (SZ and YC) independently extracted data on study characteristics, participant demographics, intervention components, comparator details, follow-up duration, and reported outcomes. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (CY). When outcome data were incomplete or unclear, corresponding authors were contacted to obtain missing information.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) across five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each domain was rated as low risk, some concerns, or high risk in accordance with RoB 2 guidance. Two reviewers (SZ and CY) independently performed the assessments, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (XJ).

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4. Continuous outcomes were pooled as mean differences (MD) or standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), depending on the consistency of measurement scales across studies. When means or standard deviations were not directly reported, values were estimated using established statistical conversion methods [24]. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the χ² test (P < 0.10 indicating statistical significance) and quantified with the I² statistic, with approximate thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Given the expected clinical and methodological variability among included studies, a random-effects model was applied as the primary analytical approach. Pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed according to follow-up duration (≤ 3 months, 3–6 months, 6–12 months) to explore temporal patterns in intervention effects. As fewer than ten studies were available for most outcomes, meta-regression was not performed to avoid overfitting and spurious findings. All prespecified effect modifiers were therefore explored using categorical subgroup analyses, and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates.

Publication bias and certainty of evidence

Potential publication bias was assessed using funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s regression test, with a significance level of P < 0.10 used to indicate possible bias [25]. Egger’s test was conducted in Stata version 17.0. The certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)pro GDT tool, with downgrading based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [26].

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The database search identified 7,290 records, including 4,813 English-language and 2,477 Chinese-language publications. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 40 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 14 randomised controlled trials (RCT) met the inclusion criteria. A list of articles excluded at full-text and the reasons for exclusion can be found in Supplementary Table B1. The study selection process is summarised in Fig. 1. The data extraction table is included in the supplementary Table B2.

Four main types of PCP-based dietary interventions were identified: proportion-guided plates using fixed-area visual segmentation [19, 29–31, 34, 37]; functionally enhanced plates incorporating oil- and salt-filtering features [14, 36]; precision portion-control plates with graduations or customised volume capacities [18, 19, 26, 35]; and portion-sized plates engraved with food-exchange references [32, 33].

All interventions were accompanied by dietary education delivered by registered dietitians, diabetes educators, or trained nurses in sessions lasting 15–60 min. Across studies, intervention designs were commonly grounded in social cognitive theory and cognitive behavioural frameworks. By providing visual segmentation and portion cues, these tools restructure eating environments to support self-monitoring, reduce cognitive burden, and reinforce healthier eating patterns. Several trials further incorporated nudge strategies, motivational interviewing, goal setting, or multidisciplinary support to enhance adherence.

Risk of bias assessment

Seven studies were rated as low risk of bias, six as having some concerns, and one as high risk. The most frequent concern involved selective outcome reporting, often due to absent or unregistered analysis plans, followed by bias in the randomisation process. Only one study was judged to have a high risk of missing outcome data, while most trials demonstrated a low risk of outcome measurement and intervention adherence. A detailed risk-of-bias summary is provided in Supplementary Table C.1.

Anthropometric outcomes

Across eligible trials, PCP–based dietary interventions were associated with short-term reductions in body weight, whereas effects on BMI and WC were smaller and not statistically significant.

Seven trials (n = 523) reported weight outcomes at 3 [26, 28, 29] and 6 months [18, 28, 29, 34]. At 3 months, the intervention produced a modest reduction in body weight (MD = -3.68 kg, 95% CI -7.78 to -0.42; I² = 0%), with the largest effect observed in the 3-month subgroup. At 6 months, the pooled effect diminished and was no longer statistically significant (MD = -1.77 kg, 95% CI − 4.46 to 0.92). The overall pooled effect was MD = -2.35 kg (95% CI -4.60 to -0.10; P < 0.001). The time-dependent changes in body weight are presented in Fig. 2. Five studies [14, 18, 29, 31, 36] (n = 561) assessed BMI at six months and showed no statistically significant change (MD = -1.17 kg/m², 95% CI -2.63 to 0.28), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 82%). Similarly, three RCT [26, 29, 37] (n = 172) examining WC reported no significant pooled effect (MD = -2.20 cm, 95% CI -6.69 to 2.29), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 54%). Variability in baseline adiposity, intervention adherence, and plate design may partly explain these findings. Forest plots for these analyses are provided in Supplementary Figures D1–D3.

Glycaemic outcomes

PCP-based dietary interventions demonstrated consistent improvements in glycaemic indicators, with stronger effects observed in longer follow-up periods.

Nine RCTs (n = 1,568) evaluated Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) at 3 months [26, 32], 6 months [14, 18, 19, 31, 34–36], and 12 months [19, 35]. The PCP intervention was associated with a significant reduction in HbA1c at 3 months (MD = -1.08%, 95% CI -1.84 to -0.32; P = 0.005), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 61%). At 6 months, HbA1c remained significantly reduced (MD = -0.72%, 95% CI -1.23 to -0.22), although substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 85%). At 12 months, a larger and more stable reduction in HbA1c was identified (MD = -1.10%, 95% CI -1.57 to -0.63), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 61%). The overall pooled effect across follow-up durations was MD = -0.82% (95% CI -1.19 to -0.45; P < 0.001). Between-subgroup heterogeneity was low, indicating a consistent direction of effect over time; however, estimates at 3 and 12 months were each based on only two trials, limiting precision.

Six RCTs (n = 914) reported FPG outcomes at 6 months [14, 35, 36] and 12 months [19, 33, 35]. At 6 months, FPG was significantly reduced (MD = -0.61 mmol/L, 95% CI -1.04 to -0.17; P = 0.006), with no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%). At 12 months, a greater and more precise reduction was observed (MD = -1.45 mmol/L, 95% CI -1.69 to -1.21; P < 0.00001), also with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). The overall pooled effect was MD = -1.04 mmol/L (95% CI -1.47 to -0.61; P < 0.00001), although moderate overall heterogeneity was present (I² = 59%), potentially reflecting differences in baseline glycaemic control and medication adjustment strategies across studies. The time-dependent changes in HbA1c and FPG following the intervention are presented in Fig. 3. Three RCT [19, 30, 35] (n = 609) assessed 2-hour postprandial blood glucose (2-h PPG) at 12 months and demonstrated a statistically modest reduction (MD = − 1.76 mmol/L, 95% CI − 2.85 to − 0.68), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 65%). Forest plots for these analyses are presented in Supplementary Figures D4–D6.

Lipid outcomes

Overall, PCP–based dietary interventions produced favourable effects on total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, whereas effects on high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and triglycerides (TG) were minimal or non-significant.

Five RCTs (n = 818) evaluated TC at 6 months [14, 18, 36] and 12 months [19, 33]. At 6 months, TC was significantly reduced (MD = -0.22 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.36 to -0.08; P = 0.003), with low heterogeneity (I² = 13%). At 12 months, the reduction was smaller and did not reach statistical significance (MD = -0.17 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.37 to 0.03; P = 0.10), with no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%). The overall pooled effect demonstrated a modest reduction in TC (MD = -0.20 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.32 to -0.09; P = 0.0006), with no significant heterogeneity (I² = 0%). These findings suggest that the intervention exerted a cholesterol-lowering effect in the short- to medium-term (≤ 6 months). However, inferences regarding attenuation of this effect at 12 months remain limited, as the long-term subgroup analysis was based on only two trials.

Five RCTs (n = 890) assessed triglycerides. No significant reduction was observed at 6 months [14, 18, 36] (MD = -0.15 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.43 to 0.14; P = 0.31), while a small increase was found at 12 months [19, 33] (MD = 0.17 mmol/L, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.32; P = 0.02). The overall effect was not significant (MD = 0.01 mmol/L, 95% CI − 0.26 to 0.27; P = 0.96). Five RCT (n = 951) assessed LDL at 6 months [14, 18] and 12 months [19, 33, 35]. At 6 months, LDL was significantly reduced (MD = -0.21 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.41 to -0.01; P = 0.04), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). At 12 months, a smaller but more precise reduction was observed (MD = -0.11 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.17 to -0.06; P < 0.0001), also with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). The overall pooled effect indicated a modest reduction in LDL (MD = -0.12 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.17 to -0.07; P < 0.0001). Tests for subgroup differences revealed no statistically significant difference between the 6-month and 12-month follow-up estimates, although the 6-month estimate was based on only two trials, limiting precision.

In contrast, four RCTs [14, 18, 19, 33] (n = 787) found no significant effect on HDL cholesterol (MD = 0.01 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.05 to 0.07; I² = 26%), and five RCT [14, 18, 19, 33, 36] (n = 890) reported no significant reduction in TG (MD = 0.01 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.26 to 0.27), with considerable heterogeneity (I² = 96%). Blood pressure outcomes

Effects on blood pressure were modest, with modest reductions observed only for diastolic blood pressure (DBP) at longer follow-up (12 months), whereas systolic blood pressure (SBP) showed no consistent benefit across timepoints.

Four RCTs (n = 691) assessed SBP at 6 months [18, 36] and 12 months [19, 35]. At 6 months, two RCT showed no significant difference between intervention and control groups (MD = -0.50 mmHg, 95% CI -12.13 to 11.13; P = 0.93), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 86%). At 12 months, two RCT similarly demonstrated no statistically significant reduction in SBP (MD = -3.18 mmHg, 95% CI -9.12 to 2.76; P = 0.29). The overall pooled estimate across all follow-up timepoints remained non-significant (MD = − 1.76 mmHg, 95% CI − 8.08 to 4.56), with considerable heterogeneity (I² = 71%). Similarly, four RCTs (n = 691) evaluated DBP. At 6 months [18, 36], no significant effect was observed (MD = -0.52 mmHg, 95% CI -7.66 to 6.63; P = 0.89), with high heterogeneity (I² = 87%). At 12 months [19, 35], a modest but statistically significant reduction in DBP was identified (MD = -2.72 mmHg, 95% CI -5.27 to 0.18; P = 0.04), accompanied by low heterogeneity (I² = 19%). However, the overall pooled effect across all timepoints did not reach statistical significance (MD = -1.70 mmHg, 95% CI -4.69 to 1.30; P = 0.27), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 68%). The observed heterogeneity may be partly attributable to differences in baseline blood pressure levels, intervention adherence, and concomitant antihypertensive medication use across studies. Dietary behaviour outcomes

Only three studies reported dietary behaviour outcomes. One trial observed a significant reduction in daily energy intake (-291 kcal/day), carbohydrate intake (-45 g/day), and fat intake (-13 g/day), with minimal change in protein intake [14]. Another study reported a plate adherence rate of 63.2% for twice-daily use [29]. A third trial found only modest and non-significant improvements in appetite, dietary restraint, emotional eating, and food cravings, suggesting that behavioural effects may be limited or difficult to sustain over time [26].

Safety, sensitivity analyses, publication bias, and certainty of evidence

Only one study actively monitored adverse events, reporting a single case of mild hypoglycaemia that resolved without hospitalisation [18]. No serious adverse events were reported in the remaining trials.

Sensitivity analyses indicated that pooled effects for HbA1c, FPG, TC, and LDL cholesterol remained stable when individual studies were sequentially excluded. In contrast, the effects on body weight, BMI, WC, 2 h-PPG, TG, DBP, and SBP became non-significant after removing specific trials, suggesting sensitivity to individual studies. HDL cholesterol results remained non-significant across all scenarios. Detailed results of sensitivity analyses for all outcomes are presented in Supplementary Tables D1–D12.

Egger’s regression suggested borderline publication bias for weight, BMI, and fasting plasma glucose but not for other outcomes. Funnel plots showed acceptable symmetry (Supplementary Figures E1–E12).

Using the GRADE framework, high-certainty evidence was observed for LDL cholesterol and triglycerides (12-month). Moderate-certainty evidence was found for HbA1c (12-month) and diastolic blood pressure (12-month). Low- or very-low-certainty evidence was assigned to body weight, HbA1c (6-month), fasting plasma glucose, total cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, BMI, and WC. Overall, apart from certain lipid outcomes, the evidence for most endpoints, particularly those related to weight, short-term glycaemic control, and blood pressure, remains limited, highlighting the need for larger and longer-term RCT (Supplementary Tables F1 and F2).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides, to our knowledge, the first quantitative synthesis examining the temporal dynamics of metabolic responses to portion-control plate interventions in adults with chronic diseases. The findings demonstrate outcome-specific heterogeneity in time-dependent effects. Glycaemic parameters remained relatively stable across follow-up periods, body weight showed early responsiveness followed by attenuation, lipid outcomes demonstrated moderate persistence, and vascular benefits emerged only after prolonged exposure. These findings challenge the assumption that metabolic outcomes respond uniformly to dietary interventions and suggest that intervention duration should be aligned with specific clinical targets.

The early but attenuating effect on body weight observed in this study is consistent with behavioural theories such as the dual-process model [38]. Visual portion cues embedded in plate design may initially trigger automatic cue-driven regulation of portion size, leading to rapid reductions in caloric intake. However, without reinforcement through reflective behavioural strategies or sustained self-regulation, adherence may decline over time as the novelty of the intervention diminishes [39, 40]. This interpretation is consistent with the modest adherence rate (~ 63%) reported at six months in one trial [29], suggesting that environmental nudges alone may be insufficient for sustained weight management without complementary behavioural reinforcement. Similar attenuation patterns have not been explicitly highlighted in previous obesity-focused meta-analyses [20].

Despite reductions in body weight, no significant changes were observed in BMI or WC. This discrepancy may reflect differences in body composition dynamics and measurement sensitivity. For example, visceral adiposity may respond differently to dietary modification compared with subcutaneous fat depots [41–43]. Although WC reductions were not statistically significant, even modest improvements in central adiposity may have clinically meaningful implications for cardiometabolic risk in populations with chronic diseases [44]. The limited number of studies reporting WC and body composition outcomes restricts further interpretation, highlighting the need for more detailed phenotypic assessments in future trials.

For glycaemic outcomes, the relatively stable reductions in HbA1c across follow-up periods suggest that sustained plate use may gradually facilitate improvements in dietary structure and meal composition. Portion-control plates provide visual guidance that can simplify portion regulation and promote more balanced macronutrient distribution during meals. Over time, these repeated dietary adjustments may contribute to improved glycaemic stability. The progressive improvement in fasting glucose observed between six and twelve months (-0.61 to -1.45 mmol/L; P = 0.02 for subgroup difference) further supports this possibility. Interestingly, HbA1c demonstrated a non-linear trajectory, with an initial reduction at three months (-1.08%), slight attenuation at six months (-0.72%), and renewed improvement at twelve months (-1.10%). One potential explanation is the gradual internalisation of portion-control habits. Alternatively, improved dietary patterns may interact with pharmacological treatment or medication adherence, although overlapping confidence intervals preclude definitive conclusions.

For vascular outcomes, improvements in diastolic blood pressure were observed only after twelve months, suggesting delayed cardiovascular responses to dietary modification. Physiological evidence suggests that structural vascular adaptations, such as improved endothelial function and reduced arterial stiffness, usually require long-term exposure to favourable metabolic conditions and modest weight loss. This temporal lag highlights that cardiovascular benefits may emerge more slowly than metabolic improvements. Similarly, the modest but persistent reductions in LDL cholesterol at six and twelve months (-0.21 and -0.11 mmol/L) suggest that portion-control plates may influence lipid metabolism primarily through reduced caloric intake and improved dietary balance rather than targeted modification of dietary fat composition.

These findings have practical implications for implementing plate-based interventions. A phased strategy may therefore be beneficial. In the initial phase (0–3 months), public health practitioners and primary care providers can demonstrate plate use and set realistic weight loss goals. During the maintenance phase (3–6 months), regular follow-up (telephone or outpatient) helps reinforce adherence and prevent weight rebound. Periodic booster strategies may further sustain adherence beyond the typical habit formation period of approximately 66 days [45]. In contrast, the relatively stable glycaemic improvements suggest that portion-control plates may serve as a sustainable adjunct for metabolic control among individuals with diabetes or metabolic disorders. For cardiovascular risk reduction, longer intervention durations (≥ 12 months) may be necessary to achieve measurable vascular benefits.

The low cognitive burden and scalability of plate-based interventions make them particularly suitable for populations with limited health literacy, including older adults living with chronic diseases. Unlike digital health interventions that require technological literacy or sustained engagement with electronic platforms, portion-control plates operate through environmental cues embedded directly within everyday eating contexts. This simplicity could make it easier to integrate the programme into routine clinical care and community-based health programmes, particularly in settings where resources are limited.

Finally, the divergence observed between weight and glycaemic trajectories, where weight effects attenuated while glycaemic improvements persisted, suggests that plate based interventions may influence metabolic outcomes through time differentiated behavioural pathways. Early weight changes may primarily reflect reductions in caloric intake driven by visual portion cues, whereas longer-term glycaemic improvements may arise from cumulative improvements in dietary structure and meal composition. However, limited dietary behaviour data restrict direct evaluation of these mechanisms. Future trials should therefore incorporate detailed dietary assessments and adherence monitoring to clarify how visually guided dietary tools influence long-term metabolic outcomes. Primary care providers can integrate portion-control plates into routine dietary counselling as a simple, low-burden tool. Based on these stage-specific findings, they can guide patients to: prioritize early weight loss in the first 3 months, reinforce continued use for glycaemic and lipid benefits at 6 months, and motivate persistence for blood pressure improvements beyond 12 months. As most trials delivered the intervention through trained health professionals, this approach fits seamlessly into existing community and primary care workflows and offers a scalable strategy for chronic disease management.

Strengths and limitations

This review addresses an important evidence gap by providing the first comprehensive synthesis of PCP–based dietary interventions specifically in chronic disease populations, with explicit stratification by follow-up duration. By integrating evidence from randomised controlled trials and examining time-dependent patterns of metabolic outcomes, this study offers new insights into how visually guided dietary interventions may influence metabolic health over time. The theory-informed interpretation of findings further provides practice-relevant implications for primary healthcare and dietary management strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, most included trials had follow-up periods of ≤ 12 months, limiting the ability to assess long-term behavioural sustainability and metabolic durability. Second, sample sizes for several outcomes (e.g., BMI, systolic blood pressure, and 2-h PPG) were relatively small, making pooled estimates more sensitive to individual studies. Third, heterogeneity in plate design, intervention intensity, and implementation context may have contributed to variability in observed effects. Finally, as this review synthesised aggregate-level data, the mechanisms underlying the observed temporal patterns could not be directly examined. For example, it remains unclear whether the sustained glycaemic effects reflect true habit internalisation or interactions with pharmacological treatment. Future studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, embedded mediator analyses, and more standardised outcome reporting are needed to clarify the optimal duration and mechanisms of PCP-based dietary interventions. Fourth, the included studies enrolled populations with diverse chronic conditions (e.g., type 2 diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular disease), introducing clinical heterogeneity that may limit generalizability. Stratified analyses by disease type were not feasible owing to the limited number of eligible studies.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis demonstrates that portion-control plate interventions produce modest metabolic benefits in adults with chronic diseases, with effects varying across outcome domains and intervention duration. Weight reduction appears strongest in the early phase but may attenuate over time, whereas glycaemic and lipid parameters show relatively stable improvements with sustained plate use. In contrast, vascular benefits may require longer intervention periods to become detectable. These time-dependent patterns suggest that different metabolic outcomes may respond to plate-based interventions through distinct behavioural and physiological pathways. Consequently, optimal implementation strategies may need to be tailored to specific clinical targets. For example, weight management may benefit from stronger early adherence support, whereas glycaemic control may rely on sustained plate use as a simple dietary adjunct. Cardiovascular risk reduction may require longer intervention duration and continued engagement. The findings of this study may complement existing treatment regimens rather than replace established medical management. Integrating these findings into standard clinical practice may provide a more balanced and clinically appropriate approach to patient care. Future research should confirm these temporal patterns using larger trials with extended follow-up and improved adherence monitoring, and should explore strategies to optimise long-term implementation of portion-control plate interventions in chronic disease management.
