Antibiotic-driven and microbiota-targeted therapy for advanced management of pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Yue Xing, Chunli Liu, Chenting Zhang, Dansha Zhou, Biao Hu, Qian Jiang, Jiyuan Chen, Ziying Lin, Ting Wang, Han Yan, Aofeng Liu, Wenju Lu, Xiaosong Ben, Kai Yang, Jason X-J Yuan, Weifeng Zhan, Jian Wang

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary hypertension (PH), particularly secondary to hypoxic lung diseases like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), lacks effective targeted therapies. Emerging evidence suggests that microbiota imbalances contribute to PH progression, raising the possibility of microbiome-targeted interventions. This study explores the role of antibiotics in modulating microbiota and ameliorating PH.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) database to assess changes in mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) after antibiotic treatment. Subsequently, clinical data of 220 PH patients (including group 1, 3, and 4 PH) from single clinical center were analyzed, with 16S rRNA sequencing performed on pharyngeal and fecal samples to evaluate microbiota composition. Lactobacillus and Anaerostipes correlated negatively with PH severity, suggesting a protective role.ConclusionAntibiotic-driven microbiota modulation may alleviate PH progression by targeting dysbiosis and reducing inflammation. Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a progressive and life-threatening condition characterized by elevated pulmonary arterial pressure and vascular remodeling, ultimately leading to right heart failure and premature death. Among its various classifications, PH secondary to hypoxic lung diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD-PH, Group 3 PH), presents unique clinical challenges [1]. Unlike Group 1 PH (pulmonary arterial hypertension, PAH), which has benefited from targeted therapies like endothelin receptor antagonists and phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, Group 3 PH lacks effective disease-modifying treatments [2]. Current management primarily focuses on optimizing underlying lung disease and providing supportive care, highlighting an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies.

Studies indicated that patients with various forms of PH demonstrate unique hemodynamic patterns in blood circulation and diverse degrees of immunological dysfunctions [3]. The group 3 PH often have lower right ventricular pressures compared to those with group 1 PH. This difference in blood flow may be due to different pathogenic mechanisms and, potentially, different treatment approaches [3].

Emerging evidence underscores the pivotal role of chronic inflammation and microbiota dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of PH. In COPD-PH, persistent hypoxia and systemic inflammation contribute to pulmonary vascular dysfunction. Notably, dysbiosis in the gut and respiratory microbiomes is linked to aberrant immune responses and vascular remodeling [4, 5]. The gut-lung axis, a bidirectional communication network linking the intestinal and pulmonary microbiomes, has been implicated in modulating systemic inflammation and immune responses [6]. Disruptions in this axis, driven by genetic or environmental factors (e.g., antibiotics, diet), may exacerbate PH progression by promoting perivascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [7]. Airway colonization by pro-inflammatory bacteria like Streptococcus has been shown to induce PH in animal models, an effect reversible with antibiotic treatment [8]. Similarly, PH patients exhibit distinct gut microbiome profiles enriched in pathogenic taxa and depleted in beneficial species such as Lactobacillus, which produce anti-inflammatory short-chain fatty acids [5].

Antibiotics, traditionally used to treat infections, may offer unexpected benefits in PH by reshaping microbial communities. Long-term, low-dose macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) are recommended for COPD to reduce exacerbations [9], and their anti-inflammatory properties could extend to mitigating PH progression. Retrospective studies suggest that antibiotic use correlates with reduced pulmonary artery pressure in PH patients [3], but the mechanisms remain unclear.

This study investigates the therapeutic potential of antibiotics in PH through a multi-faceted approach. We first analyzed retrospective data from the MIMIC-IV database to evaluate changes in pulmonary hemodynamics post-antibiotic treatment. Next, we characterized microbiota composition in PH patients using 16 S rRNA sequencing of pharyngeal and fecal samples, comparing microbiome changes before and after antibiotic administration. Finally, we employed a hypoxia-induced PH rat model to test the effects of antibiotic cocktails on hemodynamics, vascular remodeling, and microbiome ecology. Our findings aim to elucidate whether antibiotic-driven microbiota modulation might serve as a safe and effective strategy for PH management, particularly in hypoxic lung disease-associated cases.

Materials and methods

Study population from MIMIC database

A retrospective cohort analysis was conducted using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) (version 2.0) [10], a publicly accessible database contains a comprehensive and high-granularity dataset of well-defined patients and it is maintained collaboratively by Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

Recruitment of participants

Patients with PH were recruited from the Department of Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University. Healthy controls, matched for age and sex, were enrolled from the Physical Examination Center. The diagnosis of PH was confirmed by right-heart catheterization, with a mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg.

Collection of feces and pharyngeal swabs samples from human

Nasopharyngeal swabs were collected by inserting the sterilized cotton swabs towards the pharynx, and rotating it at least 3 times over the posterior pharyngeal wall for 5 seconds, and avoiding it get in touch with the tongue, buccal mucosa, or saliva. Then the head swab was immediately cut off and put into a sterilized cryopreservation tube. The specimens were stored at −80 °C for further processing within 2 weeks.

Fecal samples were obtained from the individuals who had not received any antibiotic treatment for at least two weeks before sample collection. In the seven days before sample collection, subjects did not take any food containing probiotics such as yogurt. Each sample was either frozen immediately at − 80 °C or briefly stored in personal − 20 °C freezers before transport to the laboratory within 24 h. DNA was extracted as previously described [4].

Establish of hypoxia-induced rat model

Adult male Sprague Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old, 180–200 g) were used for the experiments and development of hypoxia-induced PH model exposing to 10% O2 for 4 weeks. Rats were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China), and housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities of the State Key Laboratory of Respiratory Disease at Guangzhou Medical University. Four to five rats per cage were kept, and the rats were free access to autoclaved standard commercial rodent chow and clean water at room temperature (22–25 °C) in a 12:12 h light-to-dark cycle. All procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou Medical University (Guangzhou, China).

Antibiotics-treatment of rats

The rats were gavaged with antibiotic cocktail, which was dissolved in sterile water containing ampicillin (110 mg/kg, Meilunbio, Shanghai, China, Cat. #69-52-3), vancomycin (55 mg/kg, Meilunbio, Cat. #1404-93-9), metronidazole (110 mg/kg, Meilunbio, Cat. #443-48-1) and neomycin (110 mg/kg, Meilunbio, Cat. #1405-10-3).

In vivo hemodynamic measurements

Right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) was measured using the same method as we described previously using a Millar catheter (Millar Instruments, Houston, US) [4]. All rats were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (30 mg/kg) and placed on a heating pad. Hemodynamic measurements were performed under normoxic conditions at room temperature. After locating the right jugular vein, a polyvinyl catheter was inserted into the RV to measure RVSP.

Histological assessments

Following hemodynamic measurements, rat lung, heart, and small intestine were collected. Lung and small intestinal tissues were isolated from each experimental group, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and prepared for histological analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on lung and small intestinal tissue sections to assess pulmonary artery muscularization and intestinal villus length, respectively. CaseViewer Software (version 2.3) was utilized for quantitative analysis of vascular remodeling, which was calculated by the ratio of wall area to total vascular area.

Calculation of fulton index

The right ventricular hypertrophy was assessed using the Fulton index, calculated by the ratio of the dry weight of the RV free wall to the combined dry weight of the left ventricle (LV) and septum (S) [RV/(LV + S)].

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

Pharyngeal swabs and feces were collected in a sterilized cryopreservation tube, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Microbial DNA was extracted using a fecal DNA isolation kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen, Redwood City, CA, USA), as previously described [4]. The final DNA concentration was determined by NanoDrop 2000 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, NC), and the purification quality of DNA was evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified with the primer pairs 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) by a thermocycler polymerase chain reaction system. 16S rRNA sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) according to the standard protocols by Majorbio BioPharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The taxonomy of each 16S rRNA gene sequence was analysed by RDP Classifier algorithm (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/) against the Silva (SSU123) 16S rRNA database. To compare the variation in richness and diversity, the Ace, Sobs, Shannon and Simpson indexes were used to measure the α-diversity. The β-diversity was estimated by computing the Bray-Curtis and visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA).

Statistical analysis

The optimal risk score for the mortality cut-off value of white blood cells was identified using the X-tile plots. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis explored the significant difference in patients’ survival, with the log-rank test. Spearman correlation analysis was performed on the relationship between leukocyte and pulmonary hemodynamic parameters. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS statistical software (version 26.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and R software (version 4.0.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

All the data were firstly evaluated following normality testing with Shapiro-Wilk test. For data do not follow a normal distribution, comparisons among groups were assessed using Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc analysis. Comparison between two groups were assessed by Wilcoxon Rank-sum test and the data were presented as median with interquartile range. When data follow a normal distribution, one-way ANOVA was performed followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post hoc test only if F achieved P < 0.05 and there was no significant variance inhomogeneity, the data were presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed with a minimum of group size (n) of five in each group.

Results

Baseline characteristics of PH patients from the MIMIC database

The study included 419 eligible patients admitted PH with antibiotics usage from the MIMIC-IV database (Fig. 1A). The general characteristics were displayed in Table 1. The mean patient age was 72.0 (62.0, 79.0) years, with 268 males (64%) and 151 females (36%). Among all patients, 50 patients (11.9%) were diagnosed with COPD, and Cefazolin was the most commonly prescribed antibiotic, used in 399 patients (95.2%). Azithromycin was used in 8 patients (1.9%) and Ceftazidime in 12 patients (2.9%). Mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) was significantly associated with antibiotics usages compared by pre- and post-antibiotics on the same patient (Fig. 1B).

Clinical profiling of PH patients from a single-center Asian cohort

Given the underrepresentation of Asian populations in the MIMIC database, most of which are white race and other, we included Asian patients from a single clinical center. A total of 220 eligible individuals were recruited by identifying PH patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Ethical No. 202004). Patients were stratified by PH classification: Group 1 (n = 43, 5 antibiotic-exposed), Group 3 (n = 151, 110 antibiotic-exposed), and Group 4 (n = 26, 4 antibiotic-exposed) (Table 2). This study recorded clinical data of the PH patients and at the same time collected pharyngeal and feces samples of 110 patients utilizing 16 S rRNA sequencing to explore lung and gut microbiome composition (Fig. 2A). Those patients who received anti-infection treatment have been taking antibiotics for more than one week. Initial analyses showed that group 3 PH patients had relatively mild pulmonary hypertension (Fig. 2B). Additionally, antibiotic-treated patients displayed significantly reduced mPAP compared to antibiotic-naive individuals (Fig. 2C). We than further evaluated prognostic biomarkers in group 3 PH. Hypoxia associated PH patients with a neutrophil ratio exceeding 88% exhibited lower survival rates and an increased risk of mortality (Fig. 2D and E). Assessed clinical correlations demonstrating that WBC levels were moderately associated with pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), mean right ventricular pressure (mRVP), and mPAP, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.24 to 0.66 (Fig. 2F). These findings suggest that prolonged antibiotic use in Group 3 PH may attenuate PH progression relative to chronic or hypoxic lung diseases.

Antibiotic-induced modulation of the respiratory-gut microbiome axis in PH

Comparative microbiome analysis revealed significant dysbiosis in PH patients versus healthy controls. In pharyngeal samples, Streptococcus and Prevotella dominated the microbial community, consistent with their known roles in respiratory tract colonization (Fig. 3A). Microbiota diversity was reduced in patients who had taken antibiotics, with β-diversity analysis and species richness showing significant differences between antibiotic-treated and antibiotic-naive patients (Fig. 3B and C). Notably, antibiotic-treated patients exhibited reduced abundance of pathogenic genera (e.g., Haemophilus) but enrichment of commensals like Veillonella (Fig. 3D).

The relative abundance of gut microbiome taxa at the genus level is outlined (Fig. 3E), species richness of PH patients was declined compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 3G). Similar to the pharyngeal microbiota, the use of antibiotics modulated the gut microbiome composition and reduced the diversity of gut microbiota (Fig. 3F and G). Fecal microbiota showed marked depletion of beneficial taxa such as Blautia and Akkermansia in PH, alongside increased Enterococcus associated with systemic inflammation, yet also up-regulated beneficial taxa such as Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium (Fig. 3H) The pharyngeal-gut axis was further supported by shared taxa (e.g., Streptococcus) across both sites.

Antibiotic interventions attenuate hypoxia-induced PH in a preclinical model

To investigate the causal role of commensal microbiota in PH, we established a hypoxic PH rat model, which represent group 3 PH to a large degree, with two antibiotic intervention strategies: preventative (Abx-Prev) and therapeutic (Abx-Treat) administration (Fig. 4A). The Abx-Prev group (antibiotics administered prior to hypoxia exposure) exhibited improved pulmonary hemodynamics compared to untreated hypoxic controls. Antibiotics improved pulmonary hemodynamics, including reductions in right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) (Fig. 4B) and right ventricular hypertrophy (Fulton index) (Fig. 4C), alongside reduced pulmonary vascular remodeling (Fig. 4D). Compared to therapeutic antibiotics, prophylactic antibiotics more effectively reversed vascular medial thickening than therapeutic intervention (Fig. 4E), aligning with our human data showing antibiotic-associated suppression of pro-inflammatory pulmonary vascular remodeling. Antibiotics also had protective effects on the gut, preserving intestinal epithelial cells (Fig. 4F) and villi length (Fig. 4G). The Abx-Treat group (antibiotics initiated after PH onset) showed intermediate RVSP reduction, suggesting microbiota modulation may mitigate but not fully reverse vascular pathology. Delayed intervention correlated with persistent muscularization of distal arterioles.

Antibiotic treatment markedly altered the gut microbiome with significantly different bacterial community observed by PCoA (Fig. 5B). Discussion

In this study, we performed a retrospective cohort analysis utilizing the publicly accessible MIMIC IV database to assess mean pulmonary artery pressure before and after treatment with the antibiotic cefazolin, revealing a significant reduction in pulmonary artery pressure. To further investigate the relationship between antibiotics and mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP), we examined the effects of oral antibiotics on the airway and gut microecosystem in patients with hypoxic lung disease-related pulmonary hypertension, employing 16 S rRNA sequencing and a hypoxia-induced rat model.

Based on the clinical data from PH patients, we found that patients with group 3 PH had lower mPAP compared to other types of PH. Those who received antibiotics had lower mPAP compared to those who did not receive antibiotics. PH patients with neutrophil ratios greater than 88% had lower survival rates and a higher risk of mortality. We suggested that the low PAP in hypoxia-PH patients was possibly due to the long-term antibiotic use. As previously we found, airway exposure of Streptococcus, a pro-inflammatory genus, was sufficient to induce PH by potentially inducing perivascular inflammation, which could be significantly rescued by the use of antibiotics [8]. This observation suggests that antibiotics targeting specific components of microbiota can be used as a potential targeted approach for PH. The presence of microbiome species in both the lung and gut showed a strong correlation with reduced diversity within individuals due to antibiotic use. Additionally, β-diversity analysis revealed notable distinctions between patients who had taken antibiotics and those who had not.

To confirm whether antibiotics work, we tested how well antibiotics treated hypoxia-induced PH in rats and monitored changes in the gut microbiome. Administering antibiotics either preventatively or therapeutically to rats with hypoxia-induced PH resulted in a notable improvement in the pulmonary hemodynamics. Antibiotics had a rescue effect on lung and gut pathology. This is similar to the findings of Becattini [11]. Lung vascular wall thickness exhibited less thickening and gut villi length was preserved in both two antibiotics treated groups. The gut microbiome was strongly disrupted by antibiotics showing fewer bacterial species except Escherichia-Shigella, Lactobacillus and Parasutterella. Particularly, Lactobacillus and Anaerostipes, acknowledged as probiotic for the gut [12] and producers of short-chain fatty acids and enriched in the healthy control rats, were highly negatively correlated with RVSP and Fulton index.

The microbiome is considered one of the environmental factors involved in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases [13, 14]. Although there is currently no direct evidence proving the interaction between the gut and lung microbiota, which is also acknowledged as the “gut-lung axis [15],” some surveys have demonstrated that the coincidence of gut and lung dysbiosis exists in chronic lung diseases such as asthma, pulmonary fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and PH [4–6, 16, 17], this may be due to the use of antibiotics controlling the imbalanced microbial community in the lungs, thereby affecting the reduction of pulmonary artery pressure.

Given that PH is currently more inclined to be considered a systemic disease, numerous emerging comprehensive targeted treatment approaches are surfacing. Current advances in microbiota-based therapies primarily involve fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) [18, 19], with numerous ongoing clinical trials [20, 21]. However, the feasibility of this approach is limited by the engraftment efficiency of beneficial bacteria after transplantation [22]. In some cases, FMT is associated with adverse events, such as infection and gastrointestinal symptoms [23, 24], which may limit its widespread application. Considering the limitations of FMT, antibiotic-driven microbiota modulation may provide a safe approach to treat PH. We speculate that antibiotics can be used to modulate the microbiota and accelerated lung functional recovery in PH patients. The type of antibiotics used for the treatment of PH, along with the dose, time and duration of the treatment, needs further study to optimize or maximize the antibiotics-induced microbiota modulation. Additionally, the development of novel antibiotics with a selective spectrum of activity may help to minimize the impact on beneficial bacteria while targeting harmful species.

In summary, this study indicated that the usage of antibiotics is highly associated with the development of PH probably through communication with the microecosystem. Antibiotics pre-treatment protected hypoxia-induced rats against lung vascular and gut pathophysiologic injury. Since antibiotics offer enormous potential for microbiome-targeted modulation to mitigate the progression of noninfectious diseases [25], the antibiotic treatment of lung vascular diseases, such as hypoxic PH, may provide a novel and low-risk therapeutic strategy. We anticipate that future extensive and longitudinal studies will investigate whether the use of common antibiotics may increase the likelihood of lung microbial colonization and reduce the risk of long-term local lung inflammation and microbial dysbiosis.

Conclusion

In summary, our analysis demonstrates a significant association between antibiotic treatment and reduced mPAP in patients with PH, particularly those with hypoxia-induced PH. Antibiotics may help protect against pulmonary vascular remodeling and improve clinical outcomes, while also modulating gut and lung microbiota to mitigate inflammation. Our findings suggest that antibiotic-driven microbiome modulation could be a safer alternative for therapeutic intervention in PH. This research highlights the complex relationship between antibiotics, microbiota, and pulmonary hemodynamics, indicating a need for further studies to explore the long-term effects of antibiotics on lung health. Ultimately, this approach may lead to novel strategies for managing lung vascular diseases like hypoxic PH.
