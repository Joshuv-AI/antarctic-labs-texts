Intracerebroventricular human mesenchymal stem cells induce MMP9-driven transient inflammation in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Su Hyeon Myeong, Na Kyung Lee, Na-Hee Lee, Soo Jin Choi, Hyo Jin Son, Jong Wook Chang, Hee Jin Kim, Duk L. Na
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are often considered hypoimmunogenic. However, a transient fever observed after intracerebroventricular (ICV) administration in a clinical trial suggests an acute host response. This study examines the mechanisms underlying this reaction, with a focus on MSC migration and the role of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9).MethodsWe analyzed cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients treated with saline (n = 3) or human MSCs (hMSCs) (n = 6) using an exploratory protease array, followed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The function of MMP9 was examined further through in-vitro migration and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation assays in MMP9-silenced hMSCs (siMMP9-hMSCs). In-vivo, siMMP9-hMSCs were delivered ICV into 5xFAD mice to evaluate cell distribution and immune responses.ResultsCSF protease profiling of AD patients revealed that MSC administration increased MMP9 levels. MMP9 knockdown reduced hMSC migration and attenuated LPS induced cytokine increase in the conditioned media (TNF-α and IL-1β) or in the hMSC lysates (IL-1β, IL-6, and CRP) in-vitro. In 5xFAD mice, siMMP9-hMSCs exhibited altered migration and inflammation signatures, characterized by restricted periventricular distribution accompanied by increased CD45 leukocyte accumulation and caspase-3 activity. Naïve hMSCs, on the other hand, dispersed more broadly.ConclusionsMMP9 promotes the migration of hMSCs and influences the initial interactions between the host and the graft after ICV delivery. Loss of MMP9 activity limits dispersion and is associated with increased local immune activation. This highlights the importance of MMP9-dependent processes in the early post-transplantation phase. These findings may inform strategies to optimize the safety of central nervous system-directed stem cell therapies.Trial registration number: ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02054208.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-04958-x.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia. It is characterized pathologically by the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ), neurofibrillary tangles, and progressive neuronal loss [1–3]. Despite the recent FDA approvals of lecanemab and donanemab, the clinical benefits are modest, and concerns about amyloid-related imaging abnormalities persist [4–6]. Because AD has a multifactorial pathogenesis, targeting a single pathway may be insufficient for modifying the disease. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), with their broad immunomodulatory and paracrine effects, represent a potential complementary strategy.

MSCs have been used as potent sources for cell-based therapies for several reasons. They can be isolated relatively easily from various tissues, including umbilical cord blood, Wharton’s jelly, placenta, bone marrow, and adipose tissue [7, 8], and can home to injured or inflamed brain regions, where local signals guide their migration and engraftment [9, 10]. Through the secretion of growth factors, chemokines, and cytokines, MSCs exert therapeutic effects in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD [11, 12], by reducing Aβ deposition [13], attenuating tau pathology [14], inhibiting neuronal apoptosis [15], and suppressing neuroinflammation [16]. In addition, MSCs exhibit immunomodulatory effects by shifting microglia toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and modulating astrocyte activity [17, 18].

Determining the optimal delivery route is a major challenge for MSC-based therapies in patients with AD. Previous studies have examined intravenous, intranasal, intra-arterial, intrathecal, intraparenchymal, and intracerebroventricular (ICV) routes [19–24]. Intranasal delivery provides a noninvasive option in AD mouse models but it lacks trials in human [25, 26]. While direct intraparenchymal injection yields the most efficient CNS delivery, it requires invasive approaches [23]. In clinical settings, ICV administration offers advantages, particularly when used with an Ommaya reservoir, as it is less invasive than repeated parenchymal injections. However, whether administered MSCs can penetrate the ventricular wall in humans remains unknown. It also enables the widespread distribution of cells via cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), which is particularly relevant for diffuse AD pathology.

In a previous Phase I/IIa clinical trial in AD (NCT02054208), transient fever occurred within 6–12 h, resolving within approximately 24 h, in all nine Phase I patients and 21 of 24 Phase IIa patients following ICV administration of human MSCs (hMSCs), whereas no fever was observed in 12 saline-treated controls [14]. This trial demonstrated that hMSCs significantly reduced levels of amyloid-beta and tau in cerebrospinal fluid. However, it did not result in clinical cognitive improvement and frequently triggered inflammatory side effects. However, an open-label extension study showed that administering dexamethasone alongside hMSCs effectively mitigated these immune-related adverse events and reduced inflammatory markers [27]. This response was unexpected because MSCs are generally considered hypoimmunogenic due to their lack of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II antigens and costimulatory molecules, such as CD40, CD80, and CD86 [28, 29]. However, prior studies have shown that allogeneic MSCs can trigger host immune activation, including the infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells, B cells, and natural killer T (NKT) cells [28]. These findings emphasize that local immune responses can occur even after the administration of cells with low immunogenicity.

Based on these observations, we hypothesized that transient fever reflects a short-lived inflammatory response after ICV administration. Specifically, we speculated that interactions between injected hMSCs and barrier structures, such as the ependymal wall or choroid plexus, could initiate localized immune activation. To investigate this possibility, we analyzed CSF samples collected before and after hMSC administration in a clinical trial (NCT02054208) using a human protease array. In parallel, we developed an in-vitro AD mouse-derived ependymal co-culture model to evaluate protease expression and activity. Through the integration of clinical CSF protease profiling and mechanistic in-vitro assays, our study aimed to characterize the transient inflammatory and proteolytic changes that accompany ICV hMSC delivery in AD. This combined clinical-experimental approach sheds new light on the transient inflammatory and proteolytic mechanisms that may occur following ICV administration of hMSCs in AD.

Methods

CSF collection from patients with AD before and after hMSC injection

The details of the clinical trial have been previously described [14, 27]. Patients were randomly assigned to receive either hMSCs or saline. CSF samples were collected within 30 min before administration and 20 h after administration saline or hMSCs into the lateral ventricle via the Ommaya reservoir from patients with mild to moderate AD who had undergone a Phase IIa randomized clinical trial (NCT02054208) at the Samsung Medical Center, Seoul, Korea. Patients were enrolled based on key criteria including a diagnosis of probable AD according to the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association workgroup (NIA-AA) [30], age between 50 and 85 years, and MMSE score between 18 and 26. Amyloid PET positivity was required to confirm AD pathology. Exclusion criteria included severe white matter hyperintensities on brain MRI, major psychiatric disorder or systemic conditions that could affect cognition. In this study, CSF was collected from three of 12 saline-treated patients (none developed fever) and six of 24 hMSC-treated patients, of whom 21 experienced fever. Detailed patient information is provided in our previous publication [31]. The saline-treated patients received 2 mL normal saline, whereas the hMSC-treated patients received 3.0 × 10⁷ cells/2 mL hMSCs. The clinical trial design was approved by the Korea Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Samsung Medical Center. All the participants and their caregivers provided written informed consent.

Human protease array using CSF samples of patients with AD

CSF was collected from AD patients before and after administration of saline (n = 3) or hMSCs (n = 6). Samples were obtained in polypropylene tubes, transported immediately on ice, subjected to centrifugation, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C until use. A human protease array (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) containing 34 proteases spotted in duplicate was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The detection of signals was facilitated by chemiluminescence, and their quantification was conducted using ImageJ (NIH, USA). The background correction process entailed the subtraction of the mean signal intensity of the array’s negative control spots, as recommended by the manufacturer. For each protease, the difference between post-treatment and pre-treatment signal intensity (after – before) was calculated for individual patients. These Δ values were then employed for the purpose of conducting group comparisons between patients treated with saline and those treated with hMSC. The CSF protease array was performed as an exploratory analysis. Given the relatively small number of participants and the large number of analytes, statistical tests were not corrected for multiple comparisons.

Quantification of human matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) in CSF by ELISA

To validate the changes in MMP9 levels identified by the human protease array, CSF MMP9 concentrations were independently quantified using a human MMP9 ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. CSF samples collected at baseline (pre-treatment) and 20 h post-administration of either saline (n = 3) or hMSCs (n = 6) were analyzed in duplicate. Concentrations were determined using standard curves generated for each assay. To maintain consistency with the protease array analysis, the change in MMP9 levels for each participant was expressed as ΔMMP9 (post-treatment – pre-treatment). These results are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

hMSC preparation

The hMSCs used in the clinical trial were clinical-grade Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP) products and were manufactured by MEDIPOST, Inc. (Biomedical Research Institute Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea) in accordance with GMP guidelines. For the experimental studies, research-grade umbilical cord blood-derived MSCs (UCB-MSCs) were used for the cell-culture experiments, and separate UCB-MSC lots were used for the animal experiments. Regardless of intended use, all MSCs were expanded using the same or equivalent reagents and quality-control standards suitable for their application. Frozen vials of characterized human MSCs were obtained from MEDIPOST, Inc. The cells were cultured in 1 minimal essential medium alpha (MEM-α; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biowest, Riverside, MO, USA), and an antibiotic reagent, gentamicin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Passage 5 cells were cultured in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. Once the hMSCs reached 80–90% confluence, they were harvested and prepared for transplantation.

Matrix metalloproteinase-9 siRNA transfection and LPS treatment

After seeding the hMSCs into six-well plates, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free medium once the cells reached 60–70% confluency. The experimental groups were then transfected with a mixture of Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Cat. No. 13778-075; Invitrogen, USA) and 50 nM of MMP9 small interfering RNA (siRNA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. An equivalent volume was applied to each well. Scrambled siRNA (SN-1012, Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) was used as the negative control. For the in-vitro cytokine assay, three independent wells were used, with 4 × 104 hMSCs per well seeded in a 12-well plate containing 1 mL of media. hMSCs were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; final concentration 50 ng/mL; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) one hour after siRNA transfection. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 24 h to assess the role of MMP9 in the inflammatory response. All conditioned media were harvested for subsequent analyses. The LPS stimulation step was only performed for in-vitro experiments conducted for the pro-inflammatory cytokine ELISA; no LPS treatment was included in any other in-vitro assays or in in-vivo studies involving siMMP9-hMSC transplantation. Scrambled siRNA was used as a negative control for both knockdown verification (RT-qPCR) and migration assays. However, it was not included in the LPS-stimulated cytokine ELISA experiments due to limited cell availability.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction

RNA was extracted from hMSCs treated with MMP9 siRNA using the TRIzol reagent (Ambion-Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesised with 1 µg RNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (Bionner, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). RT-qPCR analyses were performed in hMSC and siMMP9 groups using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The primers were as follows: MMP9 (Forward, 5′-TTC CCC TTC ACT TTC CTG GGT A-3′; Reverse, 5′-CGC CAC GAG GAA CAA ACT GTA T-3′), CXCR4 (Forward, 5′-GAG TCG ATG CTG ATC CCA AT-3′; Reverse, 5′-AAG GCT ATC AGA AGC GCA AG-3′), GAPDH (Forward, 5′-AGG GCT GCT TTT AAC TCT GGT-3′; Reverse, 5′-CCC CAC TTG ATT TTG GAG GGA-3′). The PCR results were quantified using the comparative cycle threshold (ΔΔCt) method.

Analysis of migration abilities by wound healing technique

hMSCs were seeded onto a six-well plate and incubated with MMP9 small interfering RNA (siRNA) (Bioneer, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) for 24 h. After 24 h, the cells were treated with 5 µg/mL mitomycin C (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for two hrs. A 200-µL plastic micropipette tip was used to create a vertical scratch in the middle of the well. Three independent wells were cultured for each experimental group (hMSC, scrambled siRNA, and siMMP9), and three fields per well were measured to provide replicate data for analysis. Images were acquired at various time points and quantified using ImageJ software developed by the National Institute of Health, USA.

Experimental animals

All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, Approval number: 20170223001, Date: March 15, 2017) of the Samsung Biomedical Research Institute (SBRI) at the Samsung Medical Center. The SBRI is an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC International)-accredited facility that abides by the Institute of Laboratory Animal Resources (ILAR) guide. 5xFAD mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). All experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. Deep anesthesia (2% isoflurane) was utilized during all invasive procedures (hMSC injection and cardiac perfusion) to strictly minimize pain and distress. No unexpected adverse events, including but not limited to surgical complications, infection, or mortality, were observed during the course of the study. The mice were observed daily for indications of distress, such as weight loss exceeding 20% or a hunched posture. During the course of the study, no adverse events were observed, and no animals reached humane endpoints that would necessitate early euthanasia.

The 5xFAD transgenic AD mouse model expressed five AD-linked mutations (K670N/M671L, I716V, and V717I mutations in APP, and the M146L and L286V mutations in PSEN1; RRID: MMRRC_034848-JAX). These mice begin accumulating Aβ42 at 1.5 months of age, and neuronal loss becomes apparent by 6 months. Male and female mice aged 6 and 9 months were randomly allocated to experimental groups. Specifically, 11 mice (5 males and 6 females) were used for ependyma whole mounting. For immunohistochemistry (IHC) experiments 3 days post-injection, six mice were used: hMSCs were injected into three mice (two males and one female), and siMMP9-hMSCs were injected into three mice (one male and two females). Nine mice aged 9–10 months were analyzed for protein analysis following siMMP9-hMSC injection at 3, 9, and 24 h post-injection (3 h: 3 mice, 1 male and 2 females; 9 h: 3 mice, 2 males and 1 female; 24 h: 3 mice, 1 male and 2 females). The sham and vehicle groups included a total of 18 mice, aged 7–8 months, with eight males and 10 females across all time points. The mice were housed under the following conditions: temperature (23 ± 3 °C), humidity (50 ± 20%), air ventilation (18–27 changes/hour), and a 12-hour light–dark cycle.

In-vitro penetration simulation by co-culturing AD mouse ependyma and hMSCs

hMSCs (4.5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded on a transwell insert in a 12-well plate. The ependyma of the left and right hemispheres of 5xFAD mice were harvested, and one ependymal tissue sample was placed in each well of a 12-well plate. The hMSCs were co-cultured with the isolated ependyma for 24 h at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2. After 24 h, co-cultured media were harvested and concentrated at 4000 rpm and 4°C for 60 min. hMSCs in the upper insert were harvested using trypsin and lysed with lysis buffer for zymography. Media and MSCs (equal amount of total protein 5 µg) were separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel with gelatin. After separation, the gel was treated with a renaturing solution for 30 min at room temperature (RT) incubated with the developing buffer at 37 °C overnight in a shaking incubator and stained using Coomassie. After incubation with destaining solution, MMP9 enzymatic activity was measured using the ImageJ software and quantified.

ICV cannulation and hMSC injection in an AD mouse model

The standard brain cannulation information guidelines provided by the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were followed. A cannula system consisting of a guide, dummy, an internal cannula, and a screw (P Technologies, Roanoke, VA, USA) was used. Mice were anaesthetised with 2% isoflurane (Hana Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) during the surgical procedure and placed on a rodent universal stereotactic frame (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, USA). The cannula was implanted into the right lateral ventricle (coordinates from bregma: A/P: −0.4 mm, M/L: +1.0 mm, and D/V: −2.3 mm) and mounted with dental cement. A dummy cannula was used to prevent CSF leakage and infection. One week after cannulation (2 × 105 cells/7 µL/head), hMSCs were injected through the cannula using 25 µL Hamilton syringe (Hamilton Company, Reno, Nevada, USA). The sham group underwent cannulation without injection. The MEM-α group received an ICV injection of MEM-α medium without cells. The hMSC and siMMP9-hMSC groups received an ICV injection of their respective cells, suspended in MEM-α medium, via the same cannulation procedure. At 3, 9, and 24 h after hMSC injection, mice were euthanized via cardiac perfusion with PBS under deep anesthesia (2% isoflurane; Hana Pharmaceuticals), and whole brains were harvested for ELISA analysis. A separate set of mice was euthanized 72 h after injection to perform immunohistochemical staining of STEM121, CD45, and caspase-3. The penetration distance and area of the injected cell aggregates within the whole brain (cross-sectional area) were also quantified.

Quantification of pro-inflammatory cytokines in AD mouse brain tissue

The mouse brain tissues were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. The samples were ground into a powder in liquid nitrogen and lysed in a 1:1 mixture of urea buffer (7 M urea, 2.8 M thiourea, 4% CHAPS, 130 mM dithiothreitol, and 40 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.8) and RIPA buffer (25 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.6; 150 mM NaCl; 1% sodium deoxycholate; 1% Triton X-100; and 0.5% SDS). The lysates were then centrifuged at 13,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Total protein concentration was determined using a Bradford assay (Bio-Rad), and 100 ng of total protein was used for subsequent analyses. The cytokine concentrations were quantified using mouse-specific ELISA kits from R&D Systems: Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), Interleukin-6 IL-6, and C-reactive protein (CRP), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The analyte concentrations were calculated from the standard curves generated with each assay.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis of AD mouse brain tissue

The harvested mouse brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Biosesang, Republic of Korea) for at least 24 h and then embedded in paraffin blocks. Then, 4-µm-thick coronal plane paraffin serial section slides were prepared using a microtome (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The slides were de-paraffinized and a citrate buffer solution (Dako, Brüsseler Straße, Germany) was used for antigen retrieval. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-STEM121 (Cellartis, Japan; RRID: AB_2801314), anti-rat CD45 (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA; RRID: AB_312966), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; RRID: AB_2341188). For whole-mount analysis, vimentin antibody was used (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA; RRID: AB_793997). Following incubation with primary antibodies, the slides were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT (Cy3-conjugated goat anti-mouse and 488 conjugated goat anti-rabbit; Jackson Immuno Research, Europe Ltd., Newmarket, UK). After phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washing, the slides were mounted using Gel/mount™ (Biomeda Corp., Foster City, USA). Fluorescence microscopy images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss AG). To ensure consistent anatomical representation, quantification was performed using three consecutive immunostained sections from each brain.

Whole-brain, hematoxylin- and eosin-stained (H&E) coronal sections were scanned at high resolution (Scanscope AT; Aperio Technologies, Vista, CA, USA) and analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH). For each mouse (n = 3 per group), the center of the transplanted cell aggregate was identified, and the distance from three consistent anatomical landmarks on the ipsilateral lateral ventricular wall to the center point was measured. The average of these three distances (mm) was used as the representative penetration distance for each mouse. To evaluate aggregate size, we manually outlined the perimeter of the transplanted cell mass using the freehand selection tool. Then, we normalized the enclosed area (mm2) to the total cross-sectional area (mm2) of the brain in the same section. Finally, we expressed the result as a percentage of the brain area occupied by the aggregate.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using Prism v9.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data are presented as mean ± SD, and statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05. An unpaired t-test (Welch’s correction) was used to compare the results. The effects of LPS stimulation and siRNA treatment, as well as their interaction, were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at *p < 0.05. Due to the small sample size, the standardized effect size (Hedges’ g) [32] was also calculated using group means and standard deviations to evaluate the magnitude of the differences between the hMSC and siMMP9-hMSC groups for the histological results.

Results

Comparison of CSF protease changes between saline- and hMSC-treated patients

Human protease array analysis was performed using CSF collected before and after saline or hMSC administration in AD patients (Fig. 1). For each analyte, the change in signal intensity (after–before) was calculated and compared between groups using unpaired Welch’s t-tests. The majority of proteases exhibited negligible variations between the saline and hMSC conditions. However, the analysis revealed that MMP-9 (*p < 0.05) and cathepsin B (**p < 0.01) exhibited higher Δ values in the hMSC group, although variability was observed among individuals. Given that the array was utilized as an exploratory screening approach, the findings were interpreted as preliminary and not confirmatory. These results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, as statistical tests were not corrected for multiple comparisons. The observed pattern was used to select MMP-9 for subsequent ELISA-based quantification. Consistent with the protease array results, the change in CSF MMP-9 levels (Δ MMP-9) was higher in hMSC group compared with that in saline group (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Co-culturing 5xFAD ependymal tissue with hMSCs increases MMP9 enzymatic activity

Immunostaining for vimentin confirmed the identity of the ependymal tissue isolated from 5xFAD mice (see Supplementary Fig. 2). The ependymal tissue and hMSCs were co-cultured for 24 h using a transwell insert system (Fig. 2A). Gelatin zymography revealed a significant increase in MMP9 enzymatic activity in the co-culture compared to hMSCs cultured alone. Co-culture with 5xFAD ependyma resulted in significantly elevated MMP9 activity in the conditioned medium (CM) (***p < 0.001). A similar increase was observed in hMSC lysates (**p < 0.01) (Fig. 2B–C). These results suggest that exposure to 5xFAD-derived ependymal tissue enhances MMP9 activity in the secreted and intracellular compartments of hMSCs.

MMP9 knockdown in hMSCs reduced migration and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion

MMP9 mRNA expression was quantified at 24 and 72 h after siRNA transfection. At 24 h, siMMP9-hMSCs exhibited significantly lower MMP9 expression compared with untreated hMSCs (****p < 0.0001) and scrambled siRNA controls (**p < 0.01), confirming effective knockdown. At this point in time, the expression of MMP9 in siMMP9-hMSCs was reduced to approximately 18% of the level in hMSC controls (82% knockdown). No significant difference was observed between hMSCs and the scrambled siRNA controls (p = 0.3869). At 72 h, MMP9 expression in siMMP9-hMSCs had recovered to approximately 48% of the level in hMSC control (52% knockdown). The differences versus hMSCs (p = 0.0804) or scrambled controls (p = 0.0707) were no longer significant (Fig. 3A). These findings show that siRNA-mediated suppression of MMP9 was most effective at 24 h and diminished by 72 h, supporting the use of the early post-transfection period for subsequent experiments.

Cell migration was then assessed using a wound-healing assay. At baseline (0 h), corresponding to the initial wound area, no difference was observed among the three groups (p = 0.657). In comparison with hMSCs and scrambled siRNA controls, siMMP9-hMSCs exhibited reduced migration at 18 h (***p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B and C). These findings suggest that reduced MMP9 expression is associated with impaired wound closure in-vitro.

Following LPS stimulation (50 ng/mL), cytokine levels were quantified in the conditioned media and corresponding hMSC lysates (Fig. 4). LPS stimulation significantly increased TNF-α and IL-1β levels in the conditioned media of control hMSCs (*p < 0.05; Fig. 4A–B). In siMMP9-treated hMSCs, only TNF-α in the conditioned media was significantly increased after LPS treatment, although the increment was attenuated compared with control hMSCs (*p < 0.05; Fig. 4A). IL-1β levels in siMMP9 MSCs were not significantly increased by LPS stimulation (Fig. 4B). In contrast, IL-6 and CRP levels in the conditioned media did not show significant changes in either group (Fig. 4C–D). Regarding intracellular cytokines, LPS stimulation significantly increased IL-1β, IL-6, and CRP levels in control hMSCs lysates compared with their unstimulated cells (*p < 0.05; Fig. 4E–H). However, in siMMP9-treated hMSCs, no significant increases in TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and CRP were observed after LPS treatment. These results indicate that MMP9 knockdown hMSCs exhibit attenuated proinflammatory cytokine responses both in the conditioned media and intracellularly.

siMMP9-hMSCs exhibit restricted distribution and increased local immune activation three days after ICV administration

To evaluate the effect of MMP9 knockdown on hMSC engraftment, we examined cell distribution, immune cell accumulation, and apoptosis 72 h after the ICV administration of hMSCs or siMMP9-hMSCs in 5xFAD mice. As shown in Fig. 6A, hMSCs dispersed more broadly into the surrounding parenchyma, whereas siMMP9-hMSCs remained largely localized to the periventricular region. Quantitative analysis showed that siMMP9-hMSCs formed smaller graft aggregates compared with hMSCs. Three adjacent coronal sections per mouse were evaluated for quantitative analysis. STEM121 immunoreactivity was lower in the siMMP9-hMSC group; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.314; Hedges’ g = 0.57). CD45 immunostaining revealed greater leukocyte accumulation in the siMMP9-hMSC group (p = 0.011; Hedges’ g = − 4.38). Caspase-3 staining was also higher around siMMP9-hMSC grafts (p = 0.001; Hedges’ g = − 12.74) (Fig. 6C). Penetration distance did not differ significantly between the groups (hMSC = 0.958 ± 0.162 mm; siMMP9-hMSC = 0.601 ± 0.308 mm; p = 0.174).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the influence of ICV administration of hMSCs on early host responses in AD, with a focus on MMP9-mediated effects at the ventricular interface. CSF protease profiling of AD patients revealed that hMSC administration increased MMP9 levels. Ependymal co-culture experiments suggested that hMSCs are a major source of MMP9 secretion. Functionally, MMP9 knockdown reduced the migratory capacity of hMSCs and attenuated their LPS-induced inflammatory cytokine production in-vitro. In 5xFAD mice, MMP9-silenced hMSCs exhibited limited distribution within the parenchyma, restricted migration, and promoted greater local immune cell accumulation and apoptotic activity. These results suggest that MMP9 may contribute to hMSC migration and modulates early inflammatory interactions following ICV administration.

Increased MMP9 levels have been observed in clinical and experimental contexts. Analysis of CSF from AD patients using a protease array showed an increase in MMP9 following hMSC administration. MMP9 may contribute to the cells’ ability to penetrate the ependymal layer and migrate toward the brain parenchyma. In-vitro co-culture experiments using 5xFAD mouse ependymal tissue revealed that MMP9 activity was readily detectable in the hMSC-derived conditioned media and lysates. These findings are consistent with an hMSC-associated contribution to CSF MMP9 activity. However, the limited sample size and absence of direct in-vivo assessment of MMP9 in patients preclude firm mechanistic conclusions. Furthermore, our results do not rule out the possibility that endogenous ventricular or parenchymal cells may also upregulate MMP9 in response to hMSC administration. Therefore, the increase in CSF MMP9 following hMSC delivery may reflect a combination of hMSC-derived secretion and host inflammatory signaling triggered by interaction with the ventricular wall or early graft-host responses. It is also possible that host tissue contributes to the increase in MMP9, as inflammatory signals such as TNF-α can induce MMP9 expression in brain-resident cells [33]. However, the CSF protease array was conducted on a small cohort of patients (n = 6 hMSC-treated and n = 3 saline-treated) and analyzed without correction for multiple comparisons, consistent with its exploratory nature. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as generating hypotheses rather than as definitive evidence that MMP9 is the primary mediator of hMSC-associated effects. Validation in larger, more powerful cohorts is necessary to establish the clinical relevance of these CSF MMP9 fluctuations.

Silencing MMP9 using siRNA markedly reduced the migration of hMSCs in both in-vitro and in-vivo settings. This reduction in mobility suggests that MMP9 facilitates hMSC movement, possibly by modifying extracellular matrix components and promoting cell-tissue interactions. Reduced MMP9 activity was also associated with stronger local immune cell accumulation and signs of cellular stress. Although siMMP9-hMSCs exhibited reduced cytokine production in-vitro, their restricted localization near the ventricular surface in-vivo likely exposed them to a more immunoreactive environment, leading to increased leukocyte accumulation. This is likely because restricted migration keeps transplanted cells in closer proximity to inflammatory stimuli in the periventricular region. The experimental data directly supports the reduced migration of siMMP9-hMSCs and the altered distribution of immune cells. However, the implications for longer-term host-graft interactions remain interpretive. Additionally, whole-brain qRT-PCR analysis revealed lower CXCR4 mRNA levels in mice that received siMMP9-hMSCs (Supplementary Fig. 4). While this measurement reflects total brain expression rather than CXCR4 expression specifically within transplanted cells, this trend suggests that MMP9 knockdown may influence chemokine-related signaling pathways that modulate local tissue responses to grafted cells [34].

The spatial distribution difference between hMSCs and siMMP9-hMSCs affects how the host immune system interacts with the graft. Cells that remain near the ventricular surface are more likely to encounter immune effector cells. In contrast, cells that disperse more widely may experience a different pattern of immune exposure. These observations suggest that the extent of migration shapes the inflammatory microenvironment surrounding transplanted cells. However, the study did not directly assess graft survival. We acknowledge that siRNA-mediated MMP9 suppression is transient, with maximal knockdown occurring at 24 h and partial recovery occurring at 72 h. This is consistent with previous findings [35]. To align the in-vivo experiment with this timeframe, cells were injected during the period of maximal suppression and analyzed within the window in which suppression remained effective. Direct quantification of MMP9 expression in-vivo was not feasible due to the limited number of transplanted human cells. However, the experimental design ensured that histological assessment occurred during the expected period of MMP9 suppression. From a translational perspective, transient increases in MMP9 can have beneficial or undesirable effects depending on whether enhanced migration outweighs local inflammatory responses. Therefore, adjunctive strategies, such as transient MMP modulation or anti-inflammatory support, may warrant investigation. Incorporating CSF monitoring of MMP9 or cytokines in future clinical trials could help evaluate these short-term responses and guide dosing or timing of administration. Additionally, the siRNA approach only partially and transiently suppressed MMP9 rather than completely eliminating it, which limits the ability to definitively interpret MMP9’s function. Future studies employing complementary strategies, such as CRISPR-mediated knockout or pharmacological MMP9 inhibition, would provide more robust mechanistic validation.

It is important to note that this study has several limitations. First, species-specific differences between humans and mice complicate the interpretation of immune and molecular interactions across models. Although CSF can be collected from mice, the obtainable volume is extremely limited and insufficient for the multiplexed cytokine and protease assays used in this study. Consequently, cytokine profiling in animal experiments was performed using whole-brain lysates, which may not accurately reflect compartment-specific immune responses within the central nervous system (CNS). This approach may dilute localized or transient signals at the ventricular interface and lacks the resolution to distinguish inflammatory responses arising specifically from the ependymal or periventricular regions, where transplanted cells initially reside.

Second, although scrambled siRNA controls were included in the in-vitro qPCR and wound healing assays, they were not included in-vitro LPS stimulation experiments or in-vivo studies due to practical limitations. As illustrated in Fig. 3, hMSCs treated with scrambled siRNA exhibited similar behavior to untreated hMSCs with regard to MMP9 expression and migratory behavior. These observations suggest that including this control would not substantially alter the interpretation of cellular behavior but would require a considerable increase in animal use.

Third, the mouse-equivalent hMSC dose used in clinical trials did not elicit an inflammatory response in preliminary tests, necessitating the use of a higher dose to better model human outcomes. For this reason, the in-vivo comparison focused on hMSCs versus siMMP9-hMSCs. The absence of scrambled siRNA transplantation control was based on our prior in-vitro equivalence testing and the need to minimize the number of animal groups. While this adjustment allowed us to investigate relevant mechanistic pathways, using a higher dose than the clinical dose may limit the direct translatability of the findings. In addition, potential sex-related differences, the biological discrepancies between human AD and the 5xFAD mouse model, and the relatively higher cell dose used in mice should also be considered when interpreting the findings. These factors may modestly influence early host–graft interactions but are unlikely to alter the overall conclusions of the study.

Fourth, due to the limited availability of human ependymal tissue, we used a co-culture model based on 5xFAD mouse ependyma. Although this model successfully provided proof of concept, it may not fully recapitulate the cellular and molecular features of the human ventricular system. Additionally, the present study did not evaluate the reciprocal responses of ependymal cells following exposure to hMSCs, a topic that warrants further investigation. Given the absence of MMP9 measurement in the ependymal lysates, it remains uncertain whether the observed increase is exclusively attributable to hMSCs or if the surrounding tissue also plays a contributing role. Potential contributors include generic mouse proteins or injury-associated signals from tissue isolation. Future studies will incorporate wild-type (WT) controls, non-ependymal tissue, tissue-only conditioned media, and pathway interventions (e.g., cytokine neutralization) to assess specificity. Overall, the data demonstrate that MMP9 promotes the migration of hMSCs and influences the initial tissue responses following ventricular transplantation. Reduced MMP9 activity limits cell dispersal and increases local immune cell accumulation. This indicates that protease activity contributes to the interaction between transplanted cells and the ventricular environment.

Conclusions

This study sheds new light on the molecular and cellular responses triggered by the ICV administration of hMSCs. The increased MMP9 activity was detected in the CSF of patients and in co-culture experiments. This suggests that administered hMSCs could contribute to the local protease activity near the ventricular lining. However, definitive conclusions regarding the cellular source and mechanism are limited by the small number of patient samples and the lack of direct in-vivo MMP9 imaging. While our findings support a role for MMP9 in facilitating hMSC migration and shaping early host–graft interactions, these conclusions are based on initial distribution and inflammatory responses. We did not obtain direct evidence of ependymal penetration or MMP9-mediated barrier remodeling. Reduced MMP9 activity in hMSCs resulted in restricted dispersion and increased local immune cell accumulation. This highlights the importance of MMP9-dependent processes in the early post-transplantation phase. Together, these results underscore the need for continued investigation into transient inflammatory and molecular changes following ICV hMSC delivery, as well as their implications for optimizing the safety and therapeutic potential of stem cell–based interventions within the CNS.
