Pathology-Anchored Transcranial Sonography: A Cascaded Super-Resolution Deep Learning System for Early-Stage Parkinson’s Disease Grading
Authors: Yishen Zhao, Weiguo Cui, Shuang Liang, Yu Gu, Xue Bai, Lu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDelayed diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease (PD) due to undetectable early pathological changes remains a major clinical challenge limiting effective treatment. This study presents a novel diagnostic approach that integrates non-invasive imaging techniques with deep learning, facilitating accurate early diagnosis and staging of PD. A rat model of PD induced by 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) was established. Neuronal damage was quantitatively assessed through histological examination, while transcranial sonography (TCS) was employed to capture and analyze brain region images. This approach enabled the establishment of a quantitative relationship between TCS-derived imaging features and the extent of pathological injury. A deep learning framework based on TCS images was developed, integrating cascaded super-resolution reconstruction techniques (Wide Activation Super-Resolution Network (WDSR) with traditional interpolation methods) to enhance TCS image quality (PSNR = 30.67, SSIM = 0.94). Furthermore, the ResNet18 model was incorporated for disease staging of PD with 89% diagnostic accuracy. This advancement holds promise for enhancing early intervention and precision medicine strategies in PD management.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic progressive neurodegenerative disorder prevalent worldwide, characterized by core clinical manifestations including resting tremor, rigidity (increased muscle tone), bradykinesia, and postural instability1. Currently, PD diagnosis primarily relies on the assessment and analysis of clinical symptoms and neurological signs. However, owing to the inherently subjective nature of this methodology, there is ample opportunity for enhancement in the diagnostic accuracy rates, particularly in the early stages2. Research findings have demonstrated that by the time behavioral symptoms manifest in patients with PD, 60–80% of dopaminergic neurons within the substantia nigra-striatal pathway of the brain have already undergone degeneration or been lost. Neuropathological and imaging studies show that functional damage to this region can occur as early as five to ten years before clinical diagnosis1,3. These findings reveal a significant temporal and spatial separation between the clinical symptoms and pathological progression of PD, as well as the existence of an early diagnostic window. Therefore, the development of methods that can rapidly and accurately diagnose PD at an early-stage has become an urgent need in clinical practice, which is of critical importance for achieving early intervention and improving patient prognosis.

However, early diagnosis of PD poses significant challenges. The main diagnostic methods currently used in clinical practice include clinical symptom observation, physical examination, neuroimaging4, blood testing, biomarker testing, and genetic testing. It should be noted that traditional diagnostic methods based on clinical symptom observation have significant limitations, as characteristic motor symptoms often only become fully apparent in the middle to late stages of the disease, while clinical manifestations in the early stages are both mild and atypical3. Imaging tests5 such as positron emission tomography (PET) and single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), can show reduced dopamine transporter uptake and decreased dopamine synthesis in the patient’s brain, which is valuable for early diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and monitoring of the condition. However, these methods face issues such as high diagnostic costs6, long imaging times, and radiation exposure7 associated with nuclear medicine. In recent years, diagnostic technologies based on genetic and blood-based biomarkers have made some progress8,9. However, due to the complexity of operational procedures, insufficient standardization and sensitivity, and high testing costs, their application remains in the research and development verification stage. Further large-scale population studies are needed to assess their reliability.

Over the past decade, transcranial sonography (TCS) has gained widespread attention in the diagnosis and assessment of neurodegenerative movement disorders due to its non-invasive, cost-effective, and real-time imaging advantages10,11. It has shown great potential, particularly in the early diagnosis of PD, and is expected to provide new solutions for clinical practice3. TCS can detect certain characteristic abnormalities in the brains of PD patients, such as neuronal loss12, structural abnormalities in the cerebral cortex and deep nuclei, and iron ion deposits13–15 in the brain. These imaging findings not only provide objective evidence for early screening, staging assessment, and treatment decisions but also serve as important biomarkers for dynamic monitoring of disease progression. However, in the early stages of PD, subtle degenerative changes in brain tissue often do not cause significant behavioral or imaging changes, making early diagnosis challenging. Although previous studies have explored the potential of substantia nigra hyperechogenicity (SNH) signals in TCS for early diagnosis, their effectiveness as early diagnostic markers has not been fully verified, especially in animal models16–18.

In addition, the inherent limitations of TCS in terms of image resolution and signal-to-noise ratio, as well as the time-consuming, subjective, and difficult-to-quantify nature of traditional manual interpretation methods, restrict its application in early diagnosis. These issues have prompted deep learning-based automated diagnosis and disease prediction for PD to become a hot topic of research19,20. However, most existing studies focus only on automated detection of PD21–25 (Parkinson’s or non-Parkinson’s).

This study takes a two-step approach to address the challenge of early PD diagnosis. First, using a 6-OHDA-induced rat model, we quantify the correlation between TCS imaging features and nigral neuronal loss through Nissl staining analysis. Second, leveraging this imaging-pathology correlation, we develop a cascaded super-resolution deep learning system that enables automated staging of early-stage PD (Fig. 1).

Results

Temporal evolution of TCS echoes mirrors progressive neuronal loss in a rat model of PD

By comparing TCS images and neuronal Nissl staining pathology at different stages of PD progression in rats, we revealed the temporal association between neuronal damage in the substantia nigra region and changes in ultrasound imaging signals in a rat model of PD. Compared to the control group (Fig. 2A), at 7 days of disease progression (class 1), the damaged side of the substantia nigra in rats already exhibited partial blurring of neuronal cell contours and vacuolization changes (Fig. 2G), with a loss rate of Nissl bodies reaching 10.7% ± 2.5% (Fig. 2H), while no significant echo enhancement was observed in the TCS images at this stage (Fig. 2B). At 14 days post-injury (class 2), the injured side exhibited a marked decrease in neuronal numbers, disrupted cellular structures, vacuolization in some cells, nuclear condensation in some neurons, and dissolution and disappearance of Nissl bodies (Fig. 2G), with a Nissl body loss rate of 34.8% ± 0.8% (Fig. 2H). At this stage, TCS revealed prominent large-area strong echoes in the injected SN region and surrounding areas (Fig. 2C). At 21 days post-injury (Class 3), the loss of Nissl bodies in substantia nigra neurons further worsened (Fig. 2G), with a Nissl body loss rate of 51.7% ± 4.3% (Fig. 2H). TCS images showed well-defined, highly echogenic lesions with strong signal intensity (Fig. 2D). We digitized the echo imaging by measuring the average gray values of the SNH region and SN (Fig. 2E, F). The histogram shows that the average gray value of the SN region gradually increases with the progression of PD, which is consistent with the trend of neuronal Nissl body loss. Concurrently, a Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between the gray values of the substantia nigra region in TCS images and the loss rate of Nissl bodies in pathological images. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between the loss rate of Nissl bodies in the substantia nigra and the gray values in the SN region of TCS images (r = 0.953, 95% CI = 0.896–0.980, P < 0.001). These data indicate a strong positive correlation between TCS imaging progression and neuronal loss in the substantia nigra region.

The association between TCS imaging features and neuronal damage indicates that when neuronal loss exceeds a certain threshold, structural changes in the substantia nigra are sufficient to trigger a significant ultrasound signal response. Especially during the critical period when neuronal loss reaches 30–50%, TCS demonstrates significant diagnostic value. However, TCS has certain limitations in capturing the extremely early stages of neuronal degeneration in PD (approximately 10% neuronal loss), as neurons have begun to undergo pathological changes, but no obvious imaging abnormalities are visible to the naked eye. At this stage, image processing algorithms must be combined to enhance the detection capability of early-stage lesions (approximately 10% loss).

Cascade network (Bicubic + WDSR) significantly improves TCS image quality

This study proposes the use of a cascaded super-resolution network for super-resolution reconstruction of TCS images. The method first employs bicubic interpolation to perform an initial reconstruction of the dataset at a 2× magnification, followed by the WDSR algorithm to perform deep learning-based reconstruction of the newly reconstructed image at a 4× magnification. This approach combines the advantages of traditional methods in preserving details with the precise reconstruction capabilities of deep learning, achieving a significant improvement in PSNR = 30.67 and SSIM = 0.94 (Fig. 3A), effectively addressing the resolution limitations of TCS images in the early diagnosis of PD. When using the traditional bicubic interpolation method alone for image reconstruction, the best results are achieved at 2× magnification (PSNR ≈ 27, SSIM ≈ 0.8), but performance declines sharply as magnification increases (8x, PSNR ≈ 23, SSIM ≈ 0.35) (Fig. 3B). When using the WDSR method alone for image reconstruction, the best performance is achieved at 4× magnification (PSNR ≈ 23, SSIM ≈ 0.55) (Fig. 3C). The performance of reconstructing TCS images using either the bicubic interpolation method or the WDSR method alone is significantly lower than that achieved by the cascaded super-resolution network. The enlarged section in Fig. 3A shows that after processing with the cascaded method, noise in the black regions is significantly reduced, and the contours in high-signal areas are rendered more clearly.

Deep learning-based early diagnosis and staging of PD progression using TCS

As shown in Table 1 and Fig. 4, the deep learning system proposed in this study, which combines cascaded resolution reconstruction and ResNet18, demonstrates superior classification performance in PD diagnosis grading using TCS images, with an average classification accuracy of 0.895, a macro-averaging F1 score of 0.874, and an acc of 0.89. Compared to directly using the ResNet18 network on raw TCS images for PD diagnosis and grading, there has been a significant improvement. Especially in the Class 0 and Class 1 datasets of the PD progression, the accuracy rates improved from 0.746 to 0.894 and from 0.902 to 0.975, respectively. The classification results demonstrate that the system possesses strong discriminative capabilities in distinguishing TCS images from different stages of PD progression in rats.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully established a quantitative relationship between TCS imaging features and pathological damage in a 6-hydroxydopamine-induced PD rat model. It should be noted that the craniectomy performed in rats was necessitated by the thickness of the rodent skull, which precludes transcranial ultrasound imaging. In human clinical practice, TCS is routinely and non-invasively performed through natural acoustic windows such as the temporal bone. Therefore, the animal model used here serves purely as a controlled system to investigate the pathological correlates of TCS signals, and does not imply any invasive component in clinical application. We improved TCS image quality using a cascade super-resolution reconstruction technique (Bicubic + WDSR) and combined it with a deep learning model to achieve early diagnosis and disease staging of PD. In summary, this system can accurately estimate the extent of neuronal damage, providing a non-invasive, rapid tool for clinical disease assessment. The results indicate that this technology can achieve precise screening for PD without relying on expensive equipment or complex procedures, providing a feasible solution for PD screening in resource-limited areas and offering data support for long-term patient management and efficacy assessment.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been the subject of extensive investigation in both clinical and preclinical studies for Parkinson’s disease (PD), offering valuable insights into structural, functional, and molecular changes in the brain. For instance, conventional structural MRI is capable of detecting atrophy in regions such as the substantia nigra and basal ganglia. Advanced variants, including functional MRI (fMRI), assess alterations in connectivity. Neuromelanin-sensitive MRI (NMS-MRI) is another advanced imaging technique that can visualize dopaminergic neuron loss with high specificity. These techniques are of significant importance for the differential diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease and for understanding its pathophysiology3,6. However, despite their extensive utilization, these MRI methods (including computed tomography (CT) technologies) frequently exhibit inadequate sensitivity and specificity for the early detection of PD. These methods often encounter challenges such as substantial expenses, costly equipment, and protracted imaging processes, rendering them ill-suited for routine clinical applications in PD diagnosis and long-term monitoring7. TCS, as a non-invasive, radiation-free, low-cost, easy-to-operate, and repeatable technique, can reflect biological changes related to neuronal density, iron deposition, and glial cell activation, demonstrating specific advantages and potential in the field of PD. Due to limitations in device resolution and imaging mechanisms, TCS images are unable to capture subtle pathological features, particularly in the early stages of PD, which significantly constrains the diagnostic accuracy of deep learning models. Previous studies25 have used a deep learning model called Multiple Empirical Kernel Mapping Based Broad Learning System (MEKM-BLS) based on TCS images to achieve automatic PD detection, but the model only achieved 78.4% accuracy and could not perform PD disease staging. In this study, a cascaded super-resolution network was used for super-resolution reconstruction of TCS images. Its two-stage strategy effectively integrates multi-scale features, enhancing the ability to restore image texture and edge details, thereby providing a more stable and accurate image input foundation for the subsequent construction of a PD progression staging model. Based on stable image reconstruction results, this study further constructed a deep learning model for staging PD progression based on the ResNet18 network, achieving a diagnostic accuracy of 0.89. This model enables early diagnosis and disease staging of PD, significantly improving sensitivity to subtle imaging features in early PD, and demonstrates the application potential of TCS images in the dynamic assessment of PD progression.

Despite the encouraging results, this study has some limitations that need to be addressed in future research. The model still shows a relatively low recall rate (0.677) for Class 1 (7 days post-surgery, ~10% neuronal loss), which is mainly due to the subtle pathological changes in the extremely early stage of PD. The slight loss of dopaminergic neurons has not yet caused obvious structural changes in the substantia nigra, leading to weak and non-specific TCS imaging signals, which are difficult to capture even after super-resolution reconstruction. For this problem, future research can integrate multi-modal data, such as blood biomarkers and electrophysiological signals, to enrich the feature information of early PD, thereby improving the diagnostic accuracy of the extremely early stage. Due to the uneven sample size across different disease stages in the self-built TCS dataset, the generalization ability of image processing methods also faces certain challenges. Additionally, the key high-echo features observed in TCS images are not unique to Parkinson’s disease; other neurodegenerative diseases such as multiple system atrophy26 (MSA) and progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) may also exhibit similar imaging characteristics27. Therefore, TCS images should be combined with clinical phenotypes and multimodal imaging for multidimensional differential diagnosis.

Methods

Construction of animal models

All animal experiments in this study were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of Capital Medical University (Approval No. AEEI-2023-125). This study used 36 adult male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats with an average weight of 150-200 g, purchased from the Capital Medical University Laboratory Animal Center Platform. Animals were housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled, pathogen-free environment under a 12-hour light/dark cycle and had free access to water and food. All animals underwent a one-week adaptation period prior to the experiment.

SD rats were randomly assigned to the following groups: the sham surgery group and the 6-OHDA modeling group. The 6-OHDA modeling group was further divided into three subgroups based on the time post-modeling surgery for experimental purposes. The specific grouping details are as follows: sham surgery group (n = 8), 7 days post-surgery group (n = 8), 14 days post-surgery group (n = 8), and 21 days post-surgery group (n = 12). Anesthetize animals with isoflurane (maintain a concentration of 1.5% and an oxygen flow rate of 2–3 L/min) and place them in a stereotaxic apparatus. Make an incision along the midline of the head and drill a 2 mm diameter hole. Inject only on the right side at the following location: −4.30 AP, +1.5 ML, +7.6 DV (anterior-posterior (AP), medial-lateral (ML), dorsal-ventral (DV)). This stereotaxic coordinate corresponds to the medial forebrain bundle (MFB) region, the key injection site for inducing dopaminergic neuronal damage in the substantia nigra with a high modeling success rate. In the 6-OHDA modeling group, 1.6 μl of 6-OHDA (0.32 μl/1.6 μl, dissolved in 0.2% ascorbic acid solution, protected from heat and light exposure, stored at 4 °C) was injected into the target site at a rate of 0.8 μl per minute using a 10 μl Hamilton syringe (Sigma, USA). After the injection is completed, keep the syringe in place for 5 min, then slowly remove it. Disinfect the wound with iodine solution, then close the incision. In the sham surgery group, inject 1.6 μl of 0.2% ascorbic acid saline solution into the target site. Once the rats have fully recovered from anesthesia, transfer them to the animal room for rearing.

Transcranial ultrasound image acquisition

TCS image acquisition was performed at corresponding times after the molding experiment according to the groups. Rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane, and the skull was exposed through surgery. The location was quickly identified, and part of the skull was removed, leaving the dura mater intact. After surgery, TCS imaging was performed on surviving rats. A Vevo1100 ultrasound machine was used with an MS250 high-frequency probe, and B-mode was selected as the default imaging mode. The device parameters were adjusted to: center frequency 21 MHz, imaging depth 21.00 mm, scanning width 23.04 mm, and dynamic range controlled at 60 dB. The image settings and gain were kept the same for all animals. The midbrain substantia nigra region of rats was scanned and probed, and the collected image files were exported and saved (Fig. 1A). In our study, the contours of SNH were carefully traced and their areas calculated. Images in which SNH was not detected were recorded as 0 mm2. For the mean gray values of SN, we randomly took three circular areas that were not larger than the size of SN, measured their mean gray values, and then took the average.

The TCS images collected from 36 rats were cropped using the same ROI to retain the ultrasound image area, ultimately forming an ROI image dataset with a size of 581 × 447. All data were labeled according to the progression time into four categories (Table 4): control group (class 0), 7-day group (class 1), 14-day group (class 2), and 21-day group (class 3).

Cascaded super-resolution deep learning system

Figure 1B illustrates the workflow of the system. We have developed a deep learning system based on cascaded super-resolution reconstruction for the diagnosis and staging of early-stage PD. The system consists of two parts: the first part uses a cascaded super-resolution reconstruction method to reconstruct TCS images; the second part uses a ResNet18 network for PD diagnosis and staging. Figure 2 shows the network architecture of the system.

The first part of the system uses a two-stage cascaded architecture based on bicubic interpolation28 and a wide activation super-resolution network29 (WDSR) for super-resolution reconstruction of TCS. Super-resolution reconstruction is divided into two stages: the first stage uses 2× bicubic interpolation to quickly smooth the image and suppress random noise; the second stage uses the WDSR network to restore high-frequency pathological details, extracting details through multi-layer wide activation residual blocks and enhancing the ability to extract microscopic features such as local echo heterogeneity in ultrasound images (Fig. 5A).

Network architecture of the system.

A Bicubic with WDSR for TCS image super resolution. B ResNet18 for PD grading.

The second part of the system uses the ResNet18 model for PD disease progression diagnosis and grading (Fig. 5B). ResNet18 was selected as the classification backbone due to the optimal trade-off between classification performance and computational efficiency in the cascaded super-resolution system. Compared with deeper networks such as ResNet50/101, ResNet18 has a lighter network structure, lower computational cost, and faster inference speed, which is suitable for cascading with the WDSR super-resolution module to form an end-to-end efficient diagnosis system; meanwhile, ResNet18 retains the classic residual learning mechanism, which can effectively extract the key imaging features of TCS images for PD staging and ensure sufficient classification performance. The images enhanced by super-resolution were divided into 70% for the training set and 30% for the test set. The training set was trained using five-fold cross-validation, which was further automatically randomly divided into five mutually exclusive subsets, with a 4:1 ratio for training and validation iterations. Before training, the images are first resized to 300 × 300 pixels, then subjected to random spatial augmentation strategies such as random rotation, random cropping, translation, and mirror flipping. To enhance the model’s robustness against interference, Gaussian noise and random occlusion are introduced. In our study, the implementation was based on Python 3.7, using PyTorch 1.10.2 and CUDA 11.3 on an NVIDIA RTX 3080 Laptop GPU (16GB).

Evaluation metrics

In the super-resolution reconstruction task, we use the commonly used peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index measure (SSIM) metrics to evaluate the quality changes in TCS images before and after super-resolution reconstruction. The original high-resolution TCS images (581 × 447 pixels) were used as the ground truth reference; the low-resolution input images were obtained by downsampling the original high-resolution images, and the super-resolution reconstructed output was quantitatively compared with the original ground truth to calculate PSNR and SSIM. PSNR is used to measure the fidelity between the reconstructed image and the reference image. SSIM is used to assess the similarity between images.

For the task of staging the progression of PD, model performance was evaluated using metrics such as recall (rec), precision (pre), macro-averaging F1 score (f1), and accuracy (acc).

Comparison studies

We compared the proposed system with various deep learning models, including the Vgg1630, MobileNetV231, EfficientNet-b0, EfficientNet-b732, Vision Transformer33, ResNet50, and ResNet10134. We compared the performance of each model on the dataset used in this study.

Ablation studies

To systematically evaluate the impact of resolution manipulation and restoration strategies on the proposed framework’s performance, we designed a series of controlled experiments with the original high-resolution image set as the baseline, which is exactly the same as the input images used for the subsequent downsampling process. Building upon this baseline, we implemented five key variants to isolate the contributions of specific processing steps: first, we tested images directly downsampled to 1/2 and 1/8 of the original resolution to quantify performance degradation under reduced input quality; second, we examined 2× downsampled images restored to the original resolution via bicubic interpolation, a conventional upsampling method, to benchmark against classical techniques; third, we evaluated 4× downsampled images reconstructed using the WDSR algorithm, a state-of-the-art single-stage super-resolution model, to compare with advanced standalone methods; finally, we incorporated our proposed cascaded reconstruction approach to assess the added value of its modular design. All experiments maintained identical training and evaluation protocols, ensuring that observed performance differences could be attributed exclusively to the varying resolution processing strategies.

Assessment of pathological neuronal damage

Following TCS image acquisition, deeply anesthetized rats were perfused with physiological saline via cardiac perfusion, followed by perfusion with a 4% polyformaldehyde solution. The brain tissue was removed and fixed in 4% polyformaldehyde solution overnight. Conventional paraffin embedding and sectioning (4 μm) were performed, followed by standard Nissl staining using methylene blue. After staining, images were acquired using the Aperio GT 450 slide scanner. Find the substantia nigra region in ImageScope software, randomly select five fields of view, observe the condition of Nissl bodies, and use Image Pro Plus software to count the Nissl bodies and calculate the average value (Fig. 1A).

Statistical analysis

Data statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism 10 analysis system (San Diego, CA, USA, GraphPad Software). Differences between experimental groups were tested using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When the significance threshold P ≤ 0.05, the difference between groups was considered statistically significant.
