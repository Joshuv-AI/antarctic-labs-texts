Spatio-temporal differences in EEG dynamics during decision-making between online poker players with moderate-risk and high-risk gambling activity
Authors: Julie Giustiniani, Magali Nicolier, Florine Maylié, Lionel Pazart, Emmanuel Haffen, Damien Gabriel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Problem gamblers (PG) are often treated as a uniform group, though they vary in severity—moderate-risk (MR) and high-risk (HR)—as classified by the Canadian Problem Gambling Index (CPGI). This study investigated whether brain responses during uncertain decision-making differ by gambling severity. We hypothesized that the HR group would show distinct neural activity during outcome processing. A homogeneous sample of online poker players with PG was recruited and divided into MR (n = 15) and HR (n = 14) groups. Participants performed the Iowa Gambling Task while undergoing high-density EEG recording. Neural responses to outcomes were analyzed across three time windows (TW3, TW4, TW5). Both groups showed greater neural activity for losses than gains in TW3 and TW5. However, TW4 (220–290 ms post-outcome) revealed a brain map specific to loss processing in the HR group. This suggests greater neural sensitivity to negative outcomes in more severe cases of PG. These results indicate that PG is not a homogeneous disorder; HR individuals display heightened brain reactivity to losses, which may represent increased vulnerability and a potential biomarker for the progression toward gambling disorder.

Introduction

Gambling activity occurs along a continuum from recreational gambling to gambling disorder (GD), with each stage defined by the risks taken, the damage caused and the degree of loss of control over the gambling activity1. The Canadian Problem Gambling Index (CPGI) is a self-rated questionnaire which defines four categories of gambling activity severity according to the score obtained: no risk, low risk, moderate risk and high risk2. This self-report questionnaire is considered the “gold standard” for measuring problem gambling in the general population3. The moderate-risk and high-risk categories are often grouped together in a single “Problem Gambling” category2. The global prevalence of problematic gambling among adults is estimated at 2.43% for the moderate-risk category and 1.29% for the high-risk category4. Gambling activity is evaluated via the nine items of the CPGI according to two subdomains: problematic behaviours and adverse consequences5. The CPGI is not intended as a gambling disorder index, rather a continuous scale of problem gambling severity. However, many limitations of the self-administered questionnaire approach have been identified, particularly with regard to discriminating between people at low risk and those at future risk, and between those with at-risk gambling activity and those with genuine pathological gambling behaviour2,5. In addition, gamblers can minimise their difficulties and/or the seriousness of the consequences experienced or even deny their behaviour, leading to their difficulties being underestimated on the self-report. A lack of knowledge of gambling disorder, and insufficient awareness of problem gambling, together lead to the underestimation of problem gambling severity, and delayed detection and treatment6,7. Hence, it would be highly beneficial to find a marker that could be determined with minimal patient participation, to define the patient’s severity category and therefore the level of intervention required. However, before we can claim to have achieved this, we need to better understand, from an exploratory perspective, what distinguishes different groups of gamblers from each other.

We began this reflection in an earlier study by targeting the differences between neural activity generated in low-risk and in problem gambler groups during a decision-making task8. In this study, we used the Iowa Gambling Task (IGT), designed to evaluate decision-making ability under uncertainty, a capacity known to be impaired in GD9,10. At the start of the IGT, uncertainty is maximal, reflecting a complete lack of knowledge about reward or loss probabilities. Through successive choices, participants progressively acquire this information, shifting from decision-making under uncertainty to decision-making under risk11. To avoid methodological issues caused by the diversity of gambling practices, we decided to focus on one type of gambling activity and one specific population: Online Poker Players (OPP). Unlike non-strategic gambling, poker requires knowledge of rules, skills, mathematics and psychology12. Due to these different aspects, we did not expect OPP to exhibit differences according to risk category in their IGT performance8,13. However, neuroimaging techniques showed that in the early stages of outcome processing, subjects suffering from problem gambling displayed gain processing generated by the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), which was higher than in those with low-risk activity8. These data support the involvement of the reward system and reward hypersensitivity in Problem Gamblers, since the striatum is known to receive input from the ACC14. According to Bechara et al.15, impaired decision-making can result from several causes15: sensitivity to reward16, sensitivity to punishment17, and working memory deficit18. The results of our study are in line with those of a previous study using EEG16 and fMRI19, which showed hypersensitivity to reward. The differences observed in the identification of brain dysfunction as a source of decisional impairment could have been caused by some methodological differences such as GD severity or the main type of gambling activity studied20,21. Indeed, our previous study compared OPPs assessed as at low risk with problem gamblers. A focus on OPPs enabled us to avoid the pitfall posed by an overly heterogeneous population in terms of activity type or psychosocial data. OPPs are distinguished from other groups by socio-demographic specificity (young age, higher proportion of males, higher education level, well-paid employment), and by psychological specificity (higher impulsivity and better neuropsychological performance)22,23. However, among OPPs with problem gambling, there are moderate-risk gamblers, who do not necessarily have loss of control, and high-risk gamblers, some of whom probably have genuine GD.

In summary, the main objective of our exploratory study was to determine whether the severity levels of problem gambling in the gambling population could be reflected in specific neural responses generated when performing the IGT. Here, we propose to identify differences in the neural responses generated when performing the IGT between OPPs with moderate risk and those with high risk, to test two main hypotheses: 1/ that there is a continuum from low-risk to high-risk gambling with a positive correlation between risk of problem gambling and hypersensitivity to reward19; 2/ a switch from reward hypersensitivity to loss hypersensitivity when moderate-risk gamblers transition to high-risk gambling, in association with the appearance of loss chasing10,17.

Results

Sociodemographic and psychometric data

Regarding the sociodemographic data, the MR-group appeared to have a higher average age and to contain a higher proportion of parents than the HR-group. 25% of all participants reported harmful alcohol use, 57% were smokers and 14% presented harmful cannabis use. Previous substance use experiences and current substance use were recorded with no significant differences between groups. No significant psychometric variations were observed between groups in the BIS/BAS, AUDIT and Fagerström tests. We observed higher impulsivity in the HR-group than in the MR-group on the Non-Planning Impulsivity (NPI) scale, a BIS-10 sub-scale (Table 1).

Behavioural results in the IGT

A global improvement in performance was observed as subjects (both groups) made increasingly advantageous choices during the IGT (F (9,234) = 3.109; p = 0.0015). Post-hoc analysis revealed that the net score for blocks 9 to 10 was significantly superior to the first block (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1). However, no significant differences were observed for IGT performance between the two groups. No correlation was found between CPGI and the final or total net score.

Microstate analysis

Microstate analysis revealed eight different topographic maps that best represented brain responses during outcome processing (Fig. 2). Due to their latency and configuration, three maps were of specific interest. One topographic map, time window 3 [TW3] was observed between 140 and 220 ms after outcome for both groups. A specific time window 4 [TW4], was observed in the HR group during the loss condition between 220 ms and 290 ms. Another topographic map (Time window 5: TW5) was selected as a period of interest occurring close to P300, in the period that occurred between 290 ms and 600 ms after outcome, known to reflect cognitive processes related to outcome evaluation24.

The cluster maps obtained from microstate segmentation were fitted to the individual ERPs of each subject and each condition. In TW3, loss resulted in an increase in the maximum Global Field Power (F(1,26) = 19.160, p < 0.001, ŋp2 = 0.42) and mean Global Field Power (mean GFP: F(1,26) = 19.398; p < 0.001, ŋp2 = 0.43). In TW4, the maximum Global Field Power (F(1,26) = 4.71, p = 0.039, ŋp2 = 0.15), mean Global Field Power (F(1,26) = 4.850; p = 0.04, ŋp2 = 0.16), and number of Time Frame maxGFP (F(1,26) = 5.470; p = 0.03, ŋp2 = 0.17) were significantly higher in the HR group than in the MR group (Fig. 3).

A positive association exists between the severity of problematic gambling behavior (assessed using the CPGI and DSM-5 GD criteria) and TW4 activity. For the CPGI, significant correlations were observed for the max GFP (r = 0.48, p < 0.05), the mean GFP(r = 0.48, p < 0.05), and the number of Time Frame at maxGFP (r = 0.43, p < 0.05). Source localization

Source localisation applied to TW4 identified the right frontal gyrus and the occipital lobes as generators of this activity. Source localisation performed on TW3 identified the orbitofrontal cortex, the left temporal lobe and the right temporal gyrus as generators of the neural activity in this time window. In TW5, generators were localised to the occipital lobes, as well as to the inferior frontal gyrus (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to identify specific neural patterns appearing during IGT performance in two groups of online poker players, one group at moderate risk and another group at high risk of problem gambling, to identify whether players labelled “Problem Gamblers” present specific, distinctive, electro-clinical features. The neural activity recorded during the IGT showed significant differences between both groups. Loss processing in the HR group was at a higher level and lasted longer than in the MR group. These results confirmed our second hypothesis which suggested that when moderate-risk gamblers transition to high-risk gambling they switch from reward hypersensitivity to loss hypersensitivity10,17.

Participants recruited for the study were representative of OPP, as they were exclusively male, with a mean age of 30 years, a high level of education, and were mainly employed22,23. The whole population was also representative of OPP with regard to current and past substance use, with 25% of participants having harmful alcohol use, 14% harmful cannabis use, while 57% were smokers25. No significant differences were observed in past or current substance use between the two groups. As expected, we found a significant difference in GD status, with 93% of the HR group having received a diagnosis of GD, including 50% with mild to severe GD, versus 36% in the MR group, all with moderately severe GD. Finally, regarding the psychometric data, we observed that the HR group showed a significantly higher level of impulsivity on the BIS-10 non-planning sub-score. This observation is in line with a previous study which noted a higher level of impulsivity in GD with a correlation between GD severity and the specific non-planning component26.

No significant difference in IGT performance was observed between our two groups. This result is in line with our previous studies, in which we observed that OPPs at all risk levels showed no impairment of decision making in the IGT8,13. However, we observed that the improvement in performance during the IGT takes place more slowly and is smaller than in the No Gamblers group of our previous study13. So, IGT may not be a good tool for differentiating recreational gamblers or even at-risk gamblers from high-risk gamblers. The type of gambling, strategic or not, is a more decisive factor in achieving success in the IGT than the risk level of participants. Previous studies have shown that strategic gamblers displayed performance levels similar to healthy controls who did not gamble regularly, in contrast to non-strategic gamblers who showed significantly impaired decision-making in the IGT27.

Coupling high-density EEG with the computerised version of the IGT provides a dynamic picture of neuronal activity during the decision-making process13,16,24,28. Three Time Windows (TW) of interest associated with outcome processing were identified: 140-220ms (TW3), 220-290ms (TW4), and 290-600ms (TW5). TW3 and TW5 are the usual periods of interest as they correspond respectively to early and late outcome processing in the win and loss conditions. The TW3 generators, the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), the left temporal lobe and the right temporal gyrus, and the latency suggested that this activity could be linked to feedback-related negativity (FRN)29,30. Indeed, the OFC is a well-known generator of FRN, whose activation in the outcome process is linked to the cortical component of the “reward circuit”29. The OFC is also well known for its role in decision-making processes31, including cognitive flexibility and impulsivity control32, whereas the temporal lobe play a role in learning of visual information30. Altogether, these findings appear particularly relevant in the context of a visual laboratory task involving decision-making based on learning from successive experiences of reward and punishment. Both the latency and topography of TW5 are related to the P30029,33. The TW5 generators were identical to those identified in our previous studies, the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG)8 and the occipital lobe13. The functional microstate analysis method allows the identification of other brain responses, the TW4, that would be otherwise be overlooked34. TW4 appears to be associated with a brain map for the loss condition specific to the HR group. However, we note a loss of effect after applying the FDR correction, which can be explained by the methodological limitations inherent to the exploratory nature of our study. In addition, the TW4 generators, located in the right frontal gyrus and occipital lobes, suggest a transitional nature of TW4 activity occurring between TW3 and TW5. The analysis of TW4 fitted to the individual ERPs of each subject and each condition showed that a loss resulted in a longer brain response and demonstrated that the HR group displayed globally higher and longer responses than the MR group. IFG activity has been associated with GD and with the subjective perception of the urge to gamble35. Along these lines, the occipital origin may reflect visual awareness mechanisms36. The right Inferior Frontal Gyrus (rIFG) is a crucial structure for motor inhibition, which it exerts through subcortical projections to the subthalamic nucleus, as well as through its functional and structural connections with the presupplementary motor area (preSMA). Beyond its role in inhibition, an attentional and monitoring component is also debated37. The question of how to interpret increased activity within brain structures involved in inhibition, observed in a population known for high impulsivity, remains open. A study in adolescents showed that rIFG activity is higher in adolescents with substance use, highlighting that, for the same behavioral outcome, participants needed to recruit greater levels of brain activity to achieve similar performance to inhibitory task38. Thus, for equal performance on the IGT, the overactivation of the rIFG in loss contexts may reflect difficulties in inhibition, requiring compensatory overactivation to reach a comparable performance level. In addition, it appears on the TW4 map that this loss activity isn’t correlated to the severity of the gambling problem, whereas the reward activity is correlated to the severity. The question of sensitivity to loss and gain remains debated, as some studies report hyposensitivity while others, on the contrary, report hypersensitivity. Notably, we have seen that some authors describe hyposensitivity to both gain and loss39, whereas others, including our work, find hypersensitivity10,16,17. It appears that methodological factors may largely contribute to these contradictory results (choice of population and disorder severity, type of task or stimuli used, and the delay before receiving reward or punishment). However, the notion of hypersensitivity to gain has already been reported in populations with pathological gambling. This reward hypersensitivity seems to be present across all problematic gamblers8, with a positive correlation with disorder severity40, indicating an enhanced sensitization of the reward system in individuals with addiction41. Regarding the existence of hypersensitivity to loss, Linnet et al. (2010) had already identified that the money lost during the IGT by pathological gamblers was associated with increased activity in the left ventral striatum17. The IGT data were also similar between the gambler and control groups, which leads us to hypothesize that the hyperactivity observed from TW3 to the TW5 in the rIFG may compensate for the striatal activity generated by loss, as previously discussed.

Indeed, many authors have identified a correlation between striatal dopamine release, monetary loss, and gambling severity. This finding suggests that one component of loss processing is loss aversion in high-risk gamblers, who tend to be more sensitive to loss than to gain. Impaired salience attribution to impending losses, as well as loss avoidance and loss aversion, may contribute to loss chasing, which is one of the most significant factors in the development of GD42. Indeed, loss chasing is a symptom that is arguably unique to Gambling Disorder, characterized by the persistent continuation of gambling to recover previous losses43. Thus, the presence of TW4, indicating loss hypersensitivity in the high-risk group, within which chasing behavior is observed in 93% of individuals, may be associated with the emergence of loss-chasing behavior. Previous studies have linked loss chasing to heightened impulsivity, increased sensitivity to reward and loss, emotional dysregulation, and impaired decision-making. In addition, loss chasing has been described as a salient feature of decision-making under risk and uncertainty, processes that are strongly engaged during the IGT43. Furthermore, fronto-parietal hyperactivation in contexts involving extended delayed rewards has already been identified in individuals with GD44. More precisely, loss-chasing symptom is associated with brain networks involved in executive, motivational, and sensory processing43.

Our groups appear comparable in many ways; however, several limitations of our study should be noted. Firstly, the HR group had a younger average age. However, although this may introduce a potential bias, it is worth noting that these characteristics are typical of high-risk gambling groups. Previous studies have reported that such individuals are generally younger than low-risk gamblers, typically aged between 18 and 29 years, more often single, and with fewer children45. All of these features are reflected in our high-risk group, supporting its representativeness. Additionally, we recognize that our sample size was relatively small, and we acknowledge that the loss of effect on TW4 after applying the FDR correction is most likely attributable to this limitation. However, as highlighted in a recent review of the neurophysiological literature on problematic gambling populations46, studies in this field typically rely on similarly small samples, largely due to the difficulty of recruiting well-characterized clinical cohorts. In line with this, our study deliberately focused on problematic online poker players, a highly specific subgroup selected to reduce heterogeneity in behavioral and cognitive characteristics. While this approach improves sample homogeneity and internal validity, it inevitably results in smaller sample sizes. Finally, a limitation concerns the adaptation of the IGT for ERP analysis24. Although validated, this version may slightly modify decision-making processes, including the adoption of interactive strategies47. Nonetheless, the key stages of decision-making throughout the IGT remain preserved.

Our results confirmed that the Problem Gamblers Group is composed of two distinct populations, which display different neural activity. The high-risk population showed loss hypersensitivity, which could be an indicator of a transition to a GD. Regarding our previous study, we hypothesised that reward hypersensitivity could increase with the risk of the gambling activity whereas loss hypersensitivity indicated a GD. This study reveals a possible correspondence between specific brain activity and a risky stage of activity in the at-risk gambling population. Future research should focus on the reproducibility and simplification of the procedure to develop a biomarker that can identify with certainty the clinical stage, moderate risk or GD, present in a population of gamblers, using a larger sample.

Methods

Participants and recruitment

Twenty-nine volunteers over 18 years of age were recruited to participate in this study; all were right-handed and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. For recruitment of gamblers, we focused specifically on the online poker population, displaying an announcement and advertisement in the local press (Besançon; France). The advertisement asked for participants who “played poker online” to participate in our study. Another segment of the recruited population came from outpatients receiving treatment in our specialised behavioural addiction department (University Hospital of Besançon; France). Participants were divided into two groups according to problem gambling severity, as assessed by the Canadian Problem Gambling Index (CPGI)48,49. Fifteen participants, with a CPGI score of between 3 and 7, were assigned to the Moderate Risk group (MR group), and fourteen, with a CPGI score higher than or equal to 8, to the High-Risk group (HR group). No participants had any previous neurological diseases, traumatic brain injury, or stroke. Addictive habits were recorded, and a psychiatric evaluation was carried out. One participant in the MR group was withdrawn from the analysis due to a history of psychotic disorder and ongoing treatment with nalmefene and paliperidone, two treatments known for their impact on the dopaminergic system. As a result, fourteen participants were finally assigned to the MR group. Prior to participating in the study, participants received information on the aims and procedures of the experiment and gave written informed consent to take part. All participants received €85 at the end of the experiment in compensation.

Clinical and psychometric measures

Each participant was evaluated by a psychiatric addictologist, confirming the absence of non- inclusion criteria, and determining the presence of a GD according to DSM-550. Volunteers were then asked to complete several self-assessment questionnaires to check that the groups’ personality profile, drinking and smoking habits, and sociodemographic data were equivalent, and could not constitute bias. As tobacco and alcohol consumption were not excluded, their consumption level was verified by the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT)51 and the Fagerström Test52. The Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS-10) was employed to assess impulsivity and its subcomponents: cognitive (BIS-CI), motor (BIS-MI) and non-planning (BIS-NPI)53. The BIS/BAS (“Behavioral Inhibition System” and “Behavioral Approach System”), which evaluates appetitive and aversive motivation, is composed of three BAS sub-categories, Drive (BAS-D), Fun Seeking (BAS-FS) and Reward Responsiveness (BAS-RR)54.

Experimental tasks, the Iowa Gambling Task (IGT)

The virtual IGT used in this study was an electronic version of the IGT, adapted for the study of ERPs and analysis of brain activity sources24. The aim of the task is to win as much money as possible by making successive selections from four decks. The composition of decks, values, and reward/punishment schedules were predetermined in the same way as in the original version of the IGT. While the back of each deck looked identical, the decks differed in composition. Decks A and B were the disadvantageous decks; they provided immediate rewards, but in the long run yielded major economic losses. Decks C and D were the advantageous decks; they provided frequent small wins and smaller long-term penalties, which resulted in long-term gain. The subjects were not informed of the number of trials or of deck composition. To adapt the IGT to our French population, the currency used to play was converted from US Dollars to Euros. At the beginning of the IGT, participants received a virtual loan of €2,00024,55. Some changes had to be made to adapt the original IGT task in order to work with the EEG, for example, increasing the number of trials to 200, and lengthening the time interval between card selection and displaying the result (see56 for a complete description of modifications).

EEG recording

EEG signals were recorded using a 257-channel HydroCel Geodesic Sensor Net (Electrical Geodesic Inc.) during the IGT. Continuous recordings were made at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz. All channels were referenced to the vertex (Cz) and data collected with a high impedance amplifier (Net Amp 300 amplifier, Electrical Geodesics) using Net Station 4.5 software (Electrical Geodesics). Channel impedances were maintained below 100 kΩ. Subjects were instructed to limit body movements, eye blinks, and muscle contractions during task selection and reward feedback24.

Behavioral analysis

The 200 trials of the task were divided into 10 blocks of 20 trials. The individual net score was calculated by subtracting the number of disadvantageous decks from the number of advantageous decks obtained for each block57.

EEG analysis

EEG analysis was performed using Cartool Software 3.551. Data were bandpass filtered between 1 and 30 Hz (2nd order filters, with − 12 dB/octave roll-off), and a notch filter (12th-order Butterworth, 1-Hz stopband, implemented in Cartool) fixed at 50 Hz was applied to remove environmental artefacts. The stopband of the notch filter was narrow enough to avoid any attenuation or distortion in the analysed frequency range (1–30 Hz). The main time interval of interest in the IGT occurred after presentation of the reward screen. Epochs of 700 ms (100 ms prior to reward feedback – 600 ms following reward feedback) were analyzed. A baseline correction was applied using the 100ms prior to the presentation of the feedback (-100-0 ms). A semi-automatic artefact rejection method was used, with a fixed criterion of an amplitude of ± 100 µV. Remaining epochs were inspected visually, and any epochs that contained blinks, eye movements, or other sources of transient noise were removed manually from the analysis (mean number of epochs removed: 61 ± 39, min: 0, max: 145). Electrodes with an aberrant signal (e.g., excessive noise due to malfunctioning or a poor signal during data collection) were interpolated using a 3-dimensional spline algorithm (mean number of channels interpolated: 15 ± 7, min: 0, max: 26). A microstate analysis was performed to determine whether the global electric fields for the four conditions (win or loss in the IGT for the HR-group and the MR-group) differed58. Spatiotemporal segmentation was performed on the group-averaged responses from the displayed result to 600 ms following its display for each condition. Changes in electric fields occur when the configuration of the underlying generator has changed and suggest activation of different brain networks. An agglomerate hierarchical clustering (T-AAHC) was applied to segment the grand average ERP in both time and space. The range of clusters was 1–20. Microstates with > 85% similarity were combined into one class, while those with a duration of < 10 ms were smoothed and assigned to other microstates. The window half size of temporal smoothing was 3 with a strength of 10. To determine the optimal number of clusters, five criteria, namely Gamma, Silhouettes, Davies and Bouldin, Point-Biserial, and Dunn were used to independently evaluate the quality of each clustering and merged to derive a single synthetic meta-criterion.

Following the microstate procedure, a fitting procedure comparing the group-averaged data with the scalp topography of ERPs at individual level was performed. For each condition of each subject, we could then extract various parameters, such as the number of time frames associated with maximum global field power (TFmaxGFP), the mean GFP, and the maximum GFP (maxGFP) for each map59. Finally, a source localisation procedure was also performed by using a distributed linear inverse solution based on a low-resolution electromagnetic tomography (LORETA) model for the maps resulting from the segmentation analysis. These source estimations were computed from the averages of ERPs at all 256 electrodes into a solution space represented by a 3D grid composed of 5,018 nodes. These 5,018 nodes were selected from a grid equally distributed over the grey matter of the average brain provided by the Montreal Neurological Institute.

Statistical analysis

Statistics were performed using Statistica (StatSoft Europe, Hamburg, Germany) and R 3.4.1 (R Development Core Team) software. For the psychometric statistics, a t-test was used to test potential differences in psychometric scale between the two groups. A behavioural analysis was performed on the subjects’ performances. The net scores obtained for each block were submitted to a General Linear Model (GLM), with the factors group (MR or HR), and the blocks as repeated measures (1 to 10). If significant, a post-hoc Tukey test was run to assess differences. A t-test was used to assess potential differences in IGT performance between the two groups. In the fitting procedure, the topographic maps specific to either group (MR- group versus HR-group) and the results were analysed with a second GLM, including Group × Results. The dependent variables were the number of time frames max GFP, the mean GFP, and the maxGFP, in accordance with one of our a previous study8. For both GLMs, a Bonferroni post hoc correction was applied when necessary. Partial eta-squared (η²p) was computed for all fixed effects to quantify effect sizes. To account for the number of statistical tests performed across microstate maps and dependent variables, P-values were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR), following the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Residual normality was evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk tests. Some variables showed normal distributions, while others deviated moderately. Given the robustness of linear models to such deviations and the need for methodological consistency, all analyses were performed using the same model on untransformed data. The target TWs were analysed through Spearman correlations with both the CPGI and DSM-5 scores. Finally, a post-hoc sensitivity power analysis (R package pwr) indicated that with N = 28 (14 per group), α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, the minimal detectable effect size for between-group effects is Cohen’s d ≈ 0.90–1.00, indicating that only large effects can be reliably detected in this sample.
