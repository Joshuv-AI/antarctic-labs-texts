Pharmacogenomic analysis of low-density lipoprotein receptor 3’ untranslated region genetic variants influencing rosuvastatin efficacy in Chinese dyslipidemia patients
Authors: Keke Wang, Yihua Zhu, Yan Tian, Jingli Qin, Zhuo Wang, Guoqiang Zhang, Luyan Wang, Yanwei Zhang, Hong Yuan, Ningling Sun, Songnian Hu, Yayu Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesDyslipidemia is a crucial risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Although rosuvastatin is widely used, treatment response varies significantly due to genetic variation. This study investigated the pharmacogenomic impact of low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) 3’ untranslated region (UTR) variants on rosuvastatin efficacy in a Chinese Han adult cohort with dyslipidemia.MethodsA cohort of 113 Chinese participants receiving 10 mg rosuvastatin daily was sequenced for LDLR 3′UTR variants. Haploview was used to assess linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns and haplotype structures. Multivariate regression modeling was employed to assess the influence of genetic variants on therapeutic outcomes.ResultsSeventeen LDLR 3’UTR variants were identified. A crosspopulation comparative assessment revealed significant variation in allele frequencies across distinct ethnic groups. Five variants (rs14158, rs2738466, rs5742911, rs17249057, and rs17249064) were in complete LD (D’ = 1 and r2 = 1). CHANGE analysis revealed that rosuvastatin efficacy was significantly influenced by rs2738465, rs55903358, rs186461273, and rs751672818, while ANCOVA indicated that baseline triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and patient age, alongside rs2738467, rs143587805, and rs751672818 significantly impacted treatment response. Given the bias correction properties and well-established efficiency, results derived from ANCOVA were preferred. These findings were first reported, highlighting LDLR variants can be used as predictive markers for precision medicine for rosuvastatin in the Chinese population.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the role of LDLR 3’UTR as a critical pharmacogenomic locus. Our results advance understanding of genetic predictors for personalized statin therapy.

Introduction

Dyslipidemia is a crucial risk determinant for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), contributing to more than 40% of deaths among Chinese urban and rural residents [1]. Rosuvastatin is an important drug for the treatment of dyslipidemia, but the drug response varies widely. The main factor affecting the drug effect is genetic variation, partially attributable to genetic heterogeneity within cholesterol-regulatory pathways, particularly the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) locus [2–5].

Previous genomic investigations have been largely centered on elucidating the relationship between LDLR variants and dyslipidemia [5–30], while their associations with statin therapeutic responses remain underexplored. Our earlier work demonstrated that specific variants within the LDLR 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) significantly influence interindividual variability in atorvastatin efficacy [5].

This investigation systematically evaluates the pharmacogenomic influence of LDLR 3’UTR variants on rosuvastatin efficacy in Chinese Han adults with dyslipidemia. The findings advance understanding of genetic determinants in rosuvastatin pharmacodynamics. This investigation enables personalized therapeutic strategies through pharmacogenomic profiling, thereby supporting precision medicine implementation in cardiovascular disease management.

Methods

Study population

This cohort investigation recruited 113 Chinese Han adult participants receiving care at Tengzhou Central People’s Hospital from March to October 2024. Eligible subjects were administered 10 mg rosuvastatin daily with systematic clinical monitoring performed at 3-month intervals under standardized protocols supervised by qualified research personnel. This study, along with the previous study [5,31], is part of a continuing multicenter research project approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Authorization IDs: K23656, K24763), with documented informed consent obtained from all enrolled individuals before trial initiation. This observational design ensured compliance with international clinical research guidelines while maintaining participant confidentiality throughout the investigation period.

Data collection

Initial demographic data, including sex and age distribution, were systematically obtained through structured interviews employing a validated survey tool administered by certified personnel. Anthropometric evaluations comprising height and weight measurements were performed by clinical nursing staff using calibrated equipment, with BMI derived from the ratio of body weight in kilograms to the square of height in meters. Fasting venous blood samples collected through antecubital venipuncture by qualified healthcare professionals underwent biochemical analysis to quantify lipid parameters: triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) concentrations. All study protocols strictly adhered to internationally recognized ethical standards for human subject research as defined by the Declaration of Helsinki. Longitudinal monitoring of lipid profiles was implemented during periodic follow-up assessments, maintaining consistent laboratory methodologies throughout the observation period.

DNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from 2 ml peripheral blood samples obtained from all participants through venipuncture, utilizing a magnetic bead-based nucleic acid purification system (DP329, Tiangen Biotech, Beijing). Subsequent quantification of nucleic acid purity and yield was performed with fluorometric detection technology (dsDNA HS Assay, Yeasen Biotech, Shanghai), following standardized operational protocols. Massively parallel sequencing was conducted using a high-throughput platform (DNBSEQ-T7, MGI Tech, Shenzhen) to analyze DNA libraries prepared through hybrid capture methodology targeting pharmacogenomics relevant loci, with paired-end 150 nucleotide reads generated for subsequent analysis.

Variants calling and genotyping

The raw sequencing data underwent preprocessing using Trimmomatic (version 0.36) to eliminate adapter sequences, ambiguous nucleotide calls, and suboptimal base qualities, generating refined reads for downstream analysis [32]. Alignment of processed reads against the human reference genome (build GRCh37/hg19) was performed with the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner software package (BWA release 0.7.15) under standard alignment configurations [33]. Subsequent genomic refinement and variant detection were conducted through the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK version 3.8), implementing workflow steps including indel region optimization, base quality score recalibration, and haplotype-based variant identification with genotype probability modeling [34].

Statistical analysis

The analysis of linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns and haplotype architecture was performed utilizing Haploview software [35]. The normalization of relative differences of these lipids before and after treatment was performed through nonparametric rank-based inverse normal transformation using the RNOmni toolkit (v1.0.1.2) [36]. Genetic associations with lipid-lowering therapeutic responses were evaluated using a dominant model through dual analytical frameworks implemented in BLME package (v1.0.5) [37]: (a) CHANGE model: Bayesian linear mixed-effects analysis adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and target genetic loci covariates (excluding baseline lipids); (b) ANCOVA model: linear regression with post-treatment lipid levels as the dependent variable and baseline lipids added as covariates to the CHANGE model structure [38]. Subsequently, multitiered statistical inference were implemented sequentially: Contrast-Specific Wald t-tests with equal-tailed 95% confidence intervals (CIs), Type II Wald χ² tests using CAR package (v3.1.3) [39] for covariate significance, and empirical marginal mean comparisons between homozygous reference allele carries and mutant groups per genetic locus using EMMEANS package (v1.10.7) [40] with Tukey-Kramer adjustment for multiple testing [41]. R statistical environment (v4.3.3) [42] served as the computational backbone the above packages, other than Haploview. Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05.

Results

Personal characteristics of the study cohort

This study cohort comprised 113 participants, their personal demographics are outlined in Fig. 1. Males accounted for 79.65% (Fig. 1a), reflecting China’s ASCVD gender disparity. Age distribution showed 73.44% patients between 50 and 80 years (Fig. 1b), aligning with late-onset dyslipidemia patterns. Distribution of low-density lipoprotein receptor variants

The distribution and allele frequencies of LDLR 3’ UTR variants were systematically analyzed in the study cohort. A total of 17 distinct variants were identified, each present in at least five samples (Fig. 2).

Variants in the LDLR 3’ UTR identified in this study. LDLR, low-density lipoprotein receptor; 3’ UTR, untranslated region.

The carriers with the rs1433099 alternative allele accounted for 92.92%, with heterozygous and homozygous alternative allele genotypes observed in 34.51% (n = 39) and 58.41% (n = 66) of participants, respectively.

The carriers with the rs2738465 alternative allele occupied 81.42%, with a distribution of 46.02% heterozygous carriers (n = 52) and 35.40% homozygous alternative allele carriers (n = 40).

Notably, a haplotype block comprising five complete linked variants (rs14158, rs2738466, rs5742911, rs17249057, and rs17249064) demonstrated distinct genotypic stratification: 30.97% homozygous reference allele carries (n = 35), 49.56% heterozygous carries (n = 56), and 19.47% homozygous alternative allele carries (n = 22). The rs72658877 and rs55903358 displayed comparable heterozygous carriers (41.59%, n = 47; 40.71%, n = 46) and homozygous alternative allele carries (19.47%, n = 22 for both).

The frequencies of alternative allele carries between 20% and 50% included rs3826810 (23.01% heterozygous carries, n = 26; 2.65% homozygous alternative allele carries, n = 3), rs55971831 (47.79% heterozygous carries, n = 54; 0.88% homozygous alternative allele carries, n = 1), rs2738467 (26.55% heterozygous carries, n = 30; 0.88% homozygous alternative allele carries, n = 1), and rs186461273 (22.12% heterozygous carries, n = 25; 0.88% homozygous alternative allele carries, n = 1).

The alternative allele of the other four variants—rs35921663 (18.58%, n = 21), rs7258146 (12.39%, n = 14), rs143587805 (6.19%, n = 7), and rs751672818 (5.31%, n = 6)—were detected exclusively in heterozygous configurations, with no homozygous alternative allele carriers identified.

This comprehensive profiling highlights both the allelic diversity of LDLR 3’ UTR variants and the population-specific enrichment of certain genotypes.

Eight variants (rs2738465, rs14158, rs2738466, rs5742911, rs17249057, rs17249064, rs72658877, and rs55903358) exhibited elevated frequencies in our dataset compared to East Asian from 1000g and gnomad, while surpassing other populations. This comprehensive analysis reveals both conserved ethnic-specific allele patterns and unexpected regional deviations, highlighting significant genetic diversity across populations, emphasizing the necessity for ethnicity-specific clinical interpretation.

Linkage disequilibrium and haplotype analysis

Genetic association analysis revealed seventeen variants, comprising 15 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and two insertion-deletions (InDels). Following exclusion of the InDels, all remaining SNPs were analyzed by Haploview to evaluate LD. Analysis of LD patterns revealed five variants (rs14158, rs2738466, rs5742911, rs17249057, and rs17249064) demonstrating complete LD (D’ = 1, r2 = 1) as illustrated in Fig. 3a. Haplotype reconstruction identified two predominant haplotypes (GAATG and AGGCT) with estimated prevalence rates of 55.8 and 44.2%, respectively, within the study population as showed in Fig. 3b.

Results from the CHANGE and ANCOVA methods were inconsistent (Table 2). Covariates shared between analytical frameworks exhibited differing equal-tailed 95% CIs (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study delineates the pharmacogenomic landscape of personal profiles and LDLR 3’UTR variants, and their roles in rosuvastatin therapy efficiency.

A crosspopulation comparative assessment utilizing public databases revealed significant variation in allele frequencies across distinct ethnic groups, with notable disparities observed both between divergent populations and among individuals of shared ancestry. This analysis further highlighted marked divergence in polymorphic distributions within large-scale genomic databases, particularly demonstrating population-specific stratification patterns in allelic prevalence.

The complete LD among rs14158, rs2738466, rs5742911, rs17249057, and rs17249064 suggests a conserved haplotype block in this study, consistent with prior reports of LDLR structural [5], indicating this may be a common pattern in Chinese.

The results derived from the CHANGE and ANCOVA analyses were inconsistent (Table 2). Within the CHANGE framework, four variants—rs2738465, rs55903358, rs186461273, and rs751672818—demonstrated statistically significant associations with treatment efficacy (P < 0.05). In contrast, the ANCOVA model identified baseline TG and HDL-C levels, age, and the variants rs2738467, rs143587805, and rs751672818 were significantly associated with treatment efficacy (P < 0.05) (Table 2). These discrepancies typically stem the different assumptions that CHANGE and ANCOVA analyses taken. Given the bias correction properties and well-established efficiency, ANCOVA is preferred.

The differential rosuvastatin responses linked to rs2738465, rs55903358, rs186461273, and rs751672818 from the CHANGE method and rs2738467, rs143587805, and rs751672818 from the ANCOVA method underscore the complexity of rosuvastatin pharmacogenomics. However, the other eleven variants rs1433099, rs14158, rs2738466, rs5742911, rs17249057, rs17249064, rs72658877, rs3826810, rs55971831, rs7258146, and rs35921663 were not correlated with the therapeutic efficacy of rosuvastatin.

Our previous study reported rs2738467 T allele enhancing TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C levels reduction which was consistent with the results of this study obtained from the ANCOVA method and the rs1433099 C allele improving HDL-C levels after atorvastatin therapy [5]. A Brazilian cohort reported rs14158 had no discernible influence on the therapeutic efficacy of atorvastatin [43]. However, this study demonstrated rs14158 was not associated with rosuvastatin’s therapeutic response either. A study in the USA showed that rs5742911 was associated with poor simvastatin response in black patients but not in white patients [44]. However, in this study, no association was observed between rs5742911 and rosuvastatin efficacy.

These findings revealed LDLR 3’UTR variants exhibiting population-specific effects on different statins efficacy, advocating for integrating regional genetic data into precision medicine frameworks, optimizing lipid-lowering strategies.

Study strengths and limitations

This research presents several strengths, notably its investigation into the effects of LDLR 3’ UTR variants on rosuvastatin therapy in a population of Chinese Han adult individuals with dyslipidemia. The study provides valuable insights into the genetic factors impact the efficacy of rosuvastatin treatment, pinpointing individuals most likely to benefit from rosuvastatin therapy. As a result, this work lays a foundation for the implementation of personalized, precision medicine in clinical settings.

This study still has several limitations. Crucially, the sample size of the validation cohort, while comparable to similar exploratory biomarker studies, was limited. This may have compromised the statistical power to detect smaller effect sizes and potentially increased the susceptibility to false positive findings. Consequently, we explicitly acknowledge that the robustness and generalizability of these specific findings require confirmation in subsequent, adequately powered independent cohorts. Validation in such cohorts represents an imperative next step and is a primary focus of our ongoing research initiatives. Secondly, focusing exclusively on Chinese Han adult patients with dyslipidemia might restrict the applicability of the findings to other ethnicities or demographics. Thirdly, the present study aimed to explore the association between specific variants only in the 3’UTR of the LDLR gene and their effect on the efficacy of rosuvastatin treatment. Subsequent investigations should incorporate larger, more diverse cohorts to validate the generalizability of these preliminary observations and explore the role of whole LDLR variants across different populations. Finally, discrepancies were observed between the results obtained from the CHANGE and ANCOVA analyses. Although ANCOVA is generally preferred due to its ability to correct for bias and its well-established statistical efficiency, it has certain limitations stemming from its assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity. As the sample size increases, we plan to address these limitations by developing more appropriate linear models—such as applying transformations to the dependent variable or incorporating interaction terms between genetic variants and other covariates—to better satisfy the assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that LDLR 3’UTR variants are pivotal determinants of rosuvastatin responses in Chinese Han populations. These insights open new pathways for advanced studies and clinical applications, highlighting the importance of genetic profiling in tailoring treatment for dyslipidemia. Adopting a personalized approach to therapy it can enhance treatment precision and effectiveness.
