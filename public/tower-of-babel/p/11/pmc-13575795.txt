Whole-blood point of care biomarker testing following traumatic brain injury
Authors: Daniel P Whitehouse, Sophie Richter, Virginia FJ Newcombe
Published: 2026

In 2018, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) first approved a blood test to aid clinical decision-making regarding CT imaging in patients following mild traumatic brain injury (TBI).1 This was supported by the ALERT-TBI study, which demonstrated that predefined cut-offs of a combined serum ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase-L1 (UCH-L1) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) assay achieved a high sensitivity for predicting intracranial abnormality on CT scan following TBI.2 Since then, biomarker research has expanded considerably, including the development of point-of-care (POC) devices allowing rapid testing.3 While biomarker testing has been incorporated into mild TBI clinical guidelines in some countries it is not recommended in others on the basis of current evidence,4,5 and as a result, clinical adoption varies considerably worldwide. The current evidence base largely concerns plasma or serum-based assays requiring centrifugation prior to analysis, necessitating laboratory facilities, staff time and cost. In the acute period following injury, where time is critical, sample processing and analysis speed can also lead to important delays to care decisions impacting on both the individual patient and the wider system. Furthermore, centrifugation may not be feasible in resource-limited settings, limiting the applicability of serum/plasma biomarker assays. Whole blood testing, however, both avoids centrifuge requirement and provides testing methods that could be conducted directly at the bedside, improving acquisition and processing time, and creating a broader applicability to the assay.

It is in this context where the article ‘Whole-Blood Point-of-Care Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein and Ubiquitin C-terminal Hydrolase L1 Biomarkers after Traumatic Brain Injury in the US: A Prospective Diagnostic Prediction of Intracranial Injury on Head CTs’ is of particular interest.6 In this multicentre regulatory trial, recruiting through the Transforming Research and Clinical Knowledge in Traumatic Brain Injury (TRACK-TBI) network, Puccio et al. report a sensitivity of 96.5% (95% CI: 93.6, 98.1), a negative predictive value (NPV) of 96.5% (95% CI: 93.7, 98.1), and a 3.5% false negative rate for a whole blood POC GFAP/UCH-L1 panel to predict traumatic intracranial CT abnormalities following mild TBI. Of the false negatives, none required neurosurgical intervention. These results are comparable to the performance of plasma-based POC assays,3 and prior to publication, they informed the evidence to satisfy regulatory requirements. While the FDA granted approval for the whole blood GFAP/UCH-L1 assay in 2024 for assisting decisions regarding head CT imaging in adults with suspected mild TBI,7 it should be noted that current approvals do not extend to other clinical applications.

Several clinical questions remain despite regulatory approval. The assay is intended to help decide whether CT is needed, yet the study evaluates it exclusively in patients for whom clinicians had already decided to obtain CT. By excluding those where a CT image was felt not to be required, the population is enriched for patients at higher risk of intracranial injury. In addition, the population studied here had a near 30% CT-positive rate as compared to <10% reported rate in patients evaluated for TBI in US emergency rooms,8 and so may reflect a more “severe” mild TBI group than would be expected. This, in part, reflect the fact that all participants were recruited from Level 1 or 2 trauma centres. Predictive values likely differ in lower-risk patients, and whether the assay can safely reduce CT utilisation among patients in whom the need for imaging is uncertain remains unknown. The criteria that led to decision for CT is also not described within this paper which further affects ability to generalise to other populations where specific decision rules are used.

The timing of biomarker sampling is important. Biomarker measurements in the study by Puccio et al. were obtained at a median of 8 h after injury, and only after the decision to perform CT imaging had been made. Timing in relation to CT (i.e. before or after) was not stated. Consequently, the reported diagnostic performance may not reflect real-world use where biomarker results need to be available before imaging decisions are taken. Further prospective evidence is needed to validate their diagnostic performance and clinical safety at the point of clinical decision-making. In addition, realizing the full potential of POC biomarkers will likely require moving beyond a one-size fits all approach, toward threshold values that account for important biological differences such as sex and age. Both factors are of increasing importance given the changing demographics of TBI, with rising numbers of older adults being affected.

Improved understanding of how biomarkers behave less than 6 h after injury is also key for future clinical implementation. Rapid POC testing could be particularly valuable in geographically dispersed healthcare systems, where it may support transfer decisions and improve access to specialist neurotrauma assessment, as well as in the military or prehospital contexts. While their differing half-lives offer a theoretical benefit, especially in the initial minutes to hours after injury, the incremental value of combining GFAP and UCH-L1 remains unclear. GFAP alone has demonstrated similar sensitivity for detecting intracranial abnormalities on CT compared with the combined assay.2,9 These results favouring GFAP are perhaps unsurprising, given the typically late timing of blood sampling. However, in one study of the hyperacute phase (as little as 30 min after injury), where UCH-L1 might be expected to provide the most additional diagnostic value owing to its shorter half-life, GFAP continued to perform better.10 Studies of early biomarker sampling, as well as testing of the logistical challenges and assay performance in austere environments, are required.

The limitations mentioned do not diminish the technical achievement represented by whole-blood testing and value of the data presented in this paper, which demonstrate that rapid POC whole-blood GFAP/UCH-L1 testing without central laboratory processing is both feasible in the ER setting and may help inform decisions around CT imaging following mild TBI. The next challenge is to establish whether this technical advance translates into improved clinical decision-making and patient outcomes in the settings where it is intended to be used, which will require further studies using implementation science methodology to determine clinical utility, safety, cost-effectiveness and role within different TBI care pathways worldwide.
