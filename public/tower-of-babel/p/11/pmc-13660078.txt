Genotype-phenotype relationship in a cohort of 131 Chinese patients with myotonic dystrophy type 1
Authors: Li-Ying Pan, Jin-Wen Li, Hao Yu, Hai-Lin Dong, Sheng-Mei Zou, Zhi-Ying Wu, Gong-Lu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMyotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) is an autosomal dominant dynamic mutation disorder characterized by myotonia and muscle weakness. The genotype-phenotype relationship has not been clearly defined in Chinese patients.MethodsThe clinical and genetic characteristics of 131 patients from 105 pedigrees were retrospectively analyzed. Triplet-primed Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) and Flanking PCR were performed to analyze the CTG repeats in DMPK gene.ResultsAmong 131 DM1 patients, 64.9% were male, indicating a higher male admission rate, possibly due to limited diagnostic awareness in China. Notably, 11.5% of patients were not initially diagnosed in Neurology Department. The linear negative correlation between CTG repeat number and age of onset was no longer valid when CTG repeats exceeded approximately 400. Forced vital capacity (FVC) was negatively correlated with CTG repeat number, while no significant correlations were found with the Modified Scheltens score, creatine kinase, troponin T, lipid profile, glycosylated hemoglobin, fasting glucose, or BMI. In 16 families studied, CTG repeats expanded in 13 and contracted in 2. White matter lesions were detected in 97.9% of patients, predominantly in the supratentorial region, bilaterally affecting the frontal lobes, temporal poles, and deep white matter.ConclusionOur results provide insightful information on the clinical and genetic characteristics of the largest Chinese cohort of DM1 patients to date and clarify genotype-phenotype relationship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05180-1.

Introduction

Myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) is an autosomal dominant, multisystem-involved disease with a wide range of severity. It primarily affects skeletal muscles and also impacts ocular function, the cardiovascular system, respiratory system [1], the endocrine system, and the central nervous system. Specifically, the disorder is characterized by myotonia, weakness and atrophy, as well as baldness, cataract, arrhythmias, cardiac conduction disorders, respiratory impairment, insulin resistance or diabetes, erectile dysfunction and cognitive impairment. DM1 is caused by the expansion of an unstable cytosine-thymine-guanine (CTG) repeat in the 3’-untranslated region of the DMPK gene on chromosome 19q13.3 [2–4]. In DM1 patients, CTG repeats can range from 50 to several thousand repeats [2]. Based on the age at onset, DM1 patients are stratified into five different subtypes [5]: congenital form (mild to severe neonatal symptoms, including hypotonia, respiratory distress, sucking or swallowing difficulties, or skeletal deformities detected at birth or during the first month of life), infantile form (1 month − 10 years), juvenile form (10–20 years), adult form (onset between 20 and 40 years) and late-onset form (onset after 40 years).

DM1 is the most frequent adult muscular dystrophy in European, with a prevalence estimated between 1 in 10,700 and 1 in 7,400, which may be underestimated [6–9]. By contrast, DM1 is uncommon or even rare among the Asian population in Taiwan with the prevalence of 1/200,000 and the Black population in South Africa [10–12]. More than 20 countries have registered and collected information on DM1 patients for clinical trials and in-depth research [13, 14]. Studies in several countries have explored the genotype-phenotype correlation, enhancing understanding of the relationship between CTG repeat length, age of onset, and muscle weakness severity [15, 16]. However, clinical research on Chinese DM1 patients is limited [17–19], with existing studies focusing on either clinical manifestations or genetic information alone [17–20], rather than their correlation.

Neuroimaging studies have revealed brain involvement in DM1, with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showing ventricular enlargement, global atrophy and white matter lesions [21]. A previous study found that White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are associated with domain-specific cognitive impairment in DM1 patients [22]. Additionally, temporal and insular WMHs may be associated with global intellectual disability in adult DM1patients [22]. However, the association between brain morphological changes in DM1 and neuropsychological and clinical parameters, including CTG repeat lengths, remains unclear [23].

With this study, we aim to present clinical and genetic data from 131 Chinese DM1 patients, with a particular focus on the relationship between CTG repeat length and clinical parameters. It also explores the distribution of white matter lesions in Chinese DM1 patients using MRI.

Materials and methods

Participants and clinical scale assessment

One hundred thirty-one patients with genetically confirmed DM1 cases were recruited from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine between June 2015 and December 2024. This cohort encompassed the full clinical spectrum of non-congenital DM1, with patients classified as infantile, juvenile, adult-onset, or late-onset based on their age at symptom onset [5]. Demographic information, symptoms, signs, laboratory test results, and MRI data were collected (Supplementary Materials). Clinical evaluations and neurological examinations were conducted by two senior neurologists. This study was conducted with approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, following acquisition of written informed consent from all subjects or their legal proxies. For each of the 131 patients, muscle weakness was systematically evaluated using the standardized Muscular Impairment Rating Scale (MIRS), with details and evaluation criteria referenced from a previous study [15]. Moreover, cognitive assessments were conducted on 31 DM1 patients, using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment [24].

Brain imaging and White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) assessment

Forty-nine patients underwent 3.0T MRI (GE Signa HDx) with T2-FLAIR sequences acquired using these parameters: repetition time (TR) 8002 ms, echo time (TE) 144.9 ms, and field of view (FOV) 24 × 24 cm. WMHs were evaluated according to the Modified Scheltens scale [25], a 16-item scoring system (maximum 84 points) where elevated scores correlate with greater WMHs severity.

Genetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using a commercial Qiagen kit (Hilden, Germany). DMPK gene CTG repeat expansions were measured through combined triplet-primed PCR [26] and flanking PCR methodologies, with reagents supplied by Amplicon (Shanghai, China). Flanking PCR, which has a patent in China (CN202010807827.9), offers more accurate measurement of long-fragment CTG repeats compared to TP-PCR. After quality inspection, 2 ng of DNA was added to the reaction system for amplification (Reaction system: 1 µL of LongAmp Taq DNA polymerase, 5 µL of 5x GC buffer, 0.75 µL of 10 mM dNTPs, and 1 µL of each of the 10 µM specific primers, add ddH₂O to make up the system to 25 µL). The specific primers were: DMKf: 5’-GCCAGTTCACAACCGTCCGAGCGTGGGTC-3’ and DMKr: 5’-ACGCTCCCCAGAG CAGGGCGTCATGC-3’. The amplification program was as follows: pre-denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, followed by 38 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s and annealing/extension at 65 °C for 90 s, with a final extension at 65 °C for 10 min and holding at 4 °C. The CTG repeat size was determined by separating the flanking PCR products via capillary electrophoresis alongside a molecular weight marker of known sizes. By comparing the peak position of the amplified fragment with that of the reference ladder, the approximate fragment size was estimated. The CTG repeat number was then calculated using the following formula: (CTG)n = (measured fragment size − total length of the fixed flanking sequences) / 3.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 24. Categorical data were analyzed using the Chi-square test, and interval data were analyzed using the t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Interrelationships among clinical variables were assessed using correlation statistics. Within-group longitudinal changes in clinical and neuropsychological measures were evaluated using either the Wilcoxon signed-rank test or dependent t-test based on data distribution characteristics. Binary logistic regression modeling was employed to identify significant risk factors, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographics and clinical data

Among the 131 DM1 patients from 105 families, the majority were male (64.9%, 85/131), with a significantly higher proportion than females (χ²=11.61, p = 0.001). The average age of onset and age at clinical evaluation(± SD) were 31.98 ± 13.80 years and 43.39 ± 12.99, respectively (Table 1). Among the 131 patients, 119 (90.83%, 119/131) cases had typical symptoms including weakness of limbs, myotonia and muscular dystrophy, while the remaining 12 cases, all with CTG repeat numbers above 50 (Supplementary Materials), displayed no clinically apparent symptoms at assessment and were defined as “presymptomatic patients”. The 119 patients were categorized by age of onset, as shown in Fig. 1A. The distribution of CTG repeat number information (Fig. 1B), showed that most patients had repeats in two ranges: 50–150 and 650–1050. Limb weakness (86.26%, 113/131) and myotonia (73.3%, 96/131) were the most common symptoms, while cataracts and diabetes affected a smaller proportion of patients (Table 1).

First consultation department and misdiagnosis

Among the 131 patients, 122 had records of their first-visit department. Most first visited the Department of Neurology (88.5%, 108/122), while others visited Neurosurgery, Gynecology, Cardiology, General Internal Medicine, Ophthalmology, Endocrinology, Rheumatology, Orthopedics, Gastroenterology and Stomatology. The top four non-neurology departments were Gastroenterology, Orthopedics, Endocrinology and Ophthalmology (Fig. 1C).

Eight patients had a history of misdiagnosis: Two patients were misdiagnosed with myasthenia gravis (treated with hormones or immunotherapy), two as lumbar disc herniation, two as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, one as idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (underwent ventriculoperitoneal shunt with little effect), one as peripheral neuropathy, and one as ataxic leukodystrophy.

Correlation between CTG repeats and clinical characteristics

CTG repeat numbers were assessed in 100 patients’ blood samples, revealing a range of 58 to 1145 (Fig. 1B), with a median (interquartile range) of 642.50 (225.25-861.25). The remaining 31 patients all had CTG repeats exceeding 50 in the qualitative DMPK gene test, and were clinically diagnosed with DM1. It is noteworthy that female’s CTG repeats number was higher than male’s (Z=-2.005, p = 0.045). Presymptomatic patients had significantly lower CTG repeats than symptomatic patients (Z=-4.28 p < 0.0001). Regression analysis (Table 4) showed that an exponential function model, with a slope approaching 0 as CTG repeats increase, best fit the data, suggesting the correlation between CTG repeats and age of onset weakens beyond a threshold. Indeed, there was a negative correlation for CTG repeats < 400 (rho=-0.492, p = 0.011) but not for > 400 (p = 0.704) (Fig. 1D). Age at assessment was negatively correlated with CTG repeats (rho=-0.334, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1E). Due to small sample sizes, significant differences in CTG repeats between MIRS grades were only found between grade 1 and grade 5.

Correlation analysis between CTG repeat length and clinical indicators in this study

Linear correlation analysis showed a negative correlation between CTG repeats and FVC (rho=-0.317, p = 0.024) but no significant correlations with creatine kinase, troponin T, lipids, glycated hemoglobin A1c, fasting glucose, or BMI (Table 3).

Germline transmission of CTG repeats

Among the 16 pedigrees (Fig. 2), CTG repeats expanded in 13 families and contracted in 2 families (family 15 and family 16), while the germline transmission in Family 13 could not be verified as the proband’s affected mother was deceased. Anticipation was observed in all families except family 3, where parents had mild symptoms and two offspring with 5-fold higher CTG repeats were presymptomatic. Siblings in some families had similar CTG repeats (family 1 and 3), while others had totally different repeats (family 2 and family 13), indicating unstable germline transmission. Paternal transmission tended to show more expansion than maternal transmission, but the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 1F).

White matter lesion assessment

The brain MRI was performed in 49 patients. The WMHs were observed in 47 patients. Most had supratentorial WMHs (46 patients), with one having mild infratentorial WMHs. Supratentorial WMHs were most severe in the frontal lobe, followed by the temporal and parietal lobes (Fig. 3A). Temporal pole WMHs were found in 22 patients (46.8%, 22/47), who had higher Scheltens scores (Fig. 3B), but their CTG repeats were not higher (p = 0.374). No significant correlations were found between the modified Scheltens score and CTG repeats or MoCA, but there was a positive correlation with age at assessment. (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

This study explores the genotype-phenotype relationship in the largest cohort of Chinese DM1 patients with comprehensive clinical and genetic data, addressing a critical knowledge gap.

A key finding is the significant male predominance (64.9%) in our cohort, despite DM1 being autosomal dominant (expected 1:1 ratio). Previously studies on Chinese DM1 patients also reported more males [12, 18] unlike other countries [16, 27]. This may reflect admission bias: males often present with prominent myotonia, muscular weakness, and cardiac or respiratory symptoms, leading to more referrals to neurologists. In contrast females more commonly have cataracts, swallowing difficulties, and various metabolic or systemic issues (digestive, urinary, and thyroid disorders, obesity) that are easily misdiagnosed. This gender-specific pattern of clinical presentation aligns with previous findings that males with DM1 exhibit more severe muscular and cardiorespiratory involvement, whereas females are more frequently affected by cataracts, dysphagia, and systemic comorbidities [27]. Furthermore, our data highlight limited awareness of DM1 in China, with patients first seeking care in diverse departments (including stomatology and endocrinology), emphasizing its clinical heterogeneity. Misdiagnoses (e.g., peripheral neuropathy, myasthenia gravis) further indicate the need to improve awareness among neurologists.

Another important finding clarifies the debated threshold effect in the CTG repeat-age of onset correlation. While some studies propose a linear negative correlation without a plateau [4, 28–31], others suggest a threshold with unclear associations beyond it [12, 32, 33], and some use logarithmic models supporting the latter [34]. Our data strongly support a threshold hypothesis: the logarithmic model best fit the data, with a significant negative correlation for CTG repeats < 400 but no correlation for > 400. This hypothesis is supported by multiple studies and original data, with potential molecular mechanisms: DMPK RNA with CTG repeats exceeding the threshold may fail to be transported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm [35]. However, more recent studies utilizing the more precise small-pool PCR (SP-PCR) technique have demonstrated that the correlation with progressive phenotypes, such as muscle strength, persists even for very long CTG expansions [36, 37]. Therefore, the differences in the patterns we observed may partly reflect variations in CTG repeat length measurement methodologies, as well as differences in the extent to which confounding factors such as somatic mosaicism, interrupting variant repeats, and age were controlled. Future validation using gold-standard methods like SP-PCR is warranted.

We identified 12 presymptomatic patients with CTG repeats ranging 59–731, 9 of whom have fewer than 100 repeats. Unexpectedly, two siblings (Family 3) carried CTG repeats of 717/731 but remained asymptomatic at ages 43/39 with normal creatine kinase, echocardiography and electromyography. This phenotype-genotype discrepancy suggests factors beyond CTG repeats influence DM1 variability. Genetic modifiers such as different patterns of CCG/CTC/CGG/CAG interruption [38, 39], collectively termed variant repeats (VRs), which are known to stabilize the expanded DMPK allele in somatic cells and are associated with later age at onset and milder disease severity [40, 41], DNA repair gene MSH3 [42], DMPK gene methylation levels [43, 44] and polymorphism in MBNL [45]may account for this phenomenon. These cases demonstrate that CTG repeat length alone is not a fully reliable predictor of age of onset or disease severity in clinical practice.

Our study found a negative correlation relationship between CTG and Forced Vital Capacity (FVC), aligning with Poussel’s prior observations [46]. While this relationship requires further validation, it supports the emerging consensus that larger CTG expansions may predict poorer respiratory outcomes in DM1.

The occurrence of misdiagnosis in our cohort, including cases initially labelled as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) or myasthenia gravis (MG), underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by the phenotypic heterogeneity of DM1. While DM1 has a characteristic clinical picture, atypical or isolated presentations can mislead clinicians. In patients presenting with predominant distal limb weakness and atrophy—features overlapping with ALS—the diagnosis may be diverted if myotonic discharges are not detected on needle electromyography (EMG), or if myotonia is not clinically apparent. Similarly, ptosis and impaired eye closure, which are typical of MG, can also occur in DM1 due to facial muscle involvement. These cases highlight that in the absence of overt myotonia or genetic confirmation, DM1 can mimic a range of neurological conditions, reinforcing the importance of comprehensive clinical and electrophysiological evaluation, including a deliberate search for myotonia, in patients with unexplained weakness or multisystem involvement.

In addition to these relatively novel findings, our results replicate and extend several previous observations. First, 13.3% of the pedigrees in our study showed intergenerational contraction of CTG repeats, a proportion higher than the previously reported 6.4–10% [47, 48]. However, an important methodological consideration is that the parental repeat size, typically measured at an older age, may reflect age-related somatic expansion rather than the originally transmitted germline allele. This could potentially lead to an overestimation of true germline contraction events. Therefore, further large - scale studies are needed to confirm whether this higher contraction rate is specific to the Chinese population. Second, Our study found that many DM1 patients exhibited significant cardiac abnormalities, with conduction disorders being the most prevalent. This high prevalence aligns with large cohorts reporting cardiac involvement in up to 80% of patients [49]. The underlying mechanism is primarily an RNA gain-of-function toxicity. The expanded CUG repeat transcripts sequester muscleblind-like 1 (MBNL1) and upregulate CUG-binding protein 1 (CUGBP1), leading to dysregulated alternative splicing [50]. A key consequence is the increased inclusion of exon 5 in cardiac troponin T (cTnT), which alters the protein’s function and is implicated in the development of cardiac conduction defects [51]. This molecular pathology, alongside the disruption of ion channels and promotion of fibrosis, underlies the observed electrophysiological disturbances and structural remodeling in DM1 hearts [50]. Third, our analysis of WMHs revealed that they are most prevalent in the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes, consistent with prior research [21, 52]. Notably, 47.8% of our patients had WMHs in the temporal pole, with no involvement of the basal ganglia—a pattern that distinguishes DM1 from CADASIL and may serve as a radiological marker for DM1 [21, 53].

We also found a positive correlation between age at assessment and WMHs severity, supporting the idea that WMHs progresses gradually over time, as suggested by previous longitudinal studies [52, 54]. Importantly, WMHs severity showed no significant association with clinical indicators such as CTG repeats, MIRS, disease duration, cTnT or CK, indicating that WMHs are an intrinsic feature of DM1, independent of disease severity. Yet, we failed to find a correlation between WMH and cognitive test scores (MOCA), which may be explained by the small sample size and the limited sensitivity of these scales. In a diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)-based study [52], IQ estimate correlated positively with cingulum fractional anisotropy (FA), which demonstrated the relationship between white matter lesion and cognitive function in DM1. In contrast to the multivariate regression models employed by van der Plas et al. [17], which adjusted for covariates such as age, sex, and intracranial volume, our unadjusted correlation analyses may be subject to potential confounding biases. Future studies using similarly adjusted multivariate models could further clarify the independent relationship between WMHs and other disease features in DM1.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, this is a single-center study conducted in a tertiary hospital, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our results to the broader Chinese DM1 population. Second, the assessments were not uniform across all patients; key investigations such as brain MRI, echocardiography, and cognitive tests were performed only in subsets of the cohort. This inconsistent data collection may have affected the power to detect certain clinical-genetic correlations. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes longitudinal assessment of disease progression and its relationship with CTG repeat dynamics. Finally, although we analyzed CTG repeat size, we did not investigate potential genetic modifiers such as variant repeats, somatic mosaicism, or methylation status, which may influence phenotypic variability.

Overall, this study, based on the largest cohort of Chinese DM1 patients to date, clarifies key genotype-phenotype relationships specific to this population, including gender bias in clinical presentation, CTG repeat thresholds, and intrinsic white matter lesion features, providing critical insights for precise diagnosis and management of DM1 in China.
