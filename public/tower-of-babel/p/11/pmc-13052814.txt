Information-seeking behaviours, attitudes and practices on sexual and reproductive health among healthcare providers in Pakistan
Authors: Faiza Qureshi, Shahzil Abdur Rehman Malik, Asad Saulat Fatimi, Muhammad Abbas Raza, Syeda Kainaat Fatima, Sajjan Raja, Adam Khan Mohmand, Mahnoor Sarfraz, Akmal Shahzad, Abdul Rahim Farooq, Maha Muzzamil Chaipiwala, Rohaid Ali, Nimarta Heman, Sangeen Khan, Asaad Ahmed Nafees
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSexual and reproductive health (SRH) is a fundamental aspect of overall well-being, shaped not only by sociocultural and institutional barriers faced by patients, but also by healthcare providers’ information-seeking behaviours, attitudes and clinical practices. Evidence examining these provider-level determinants remains limited in low- and middle-income settings. This study aimed to assess healthcare providers’ knowledge, attitudes and practices related to SRH and to identify key obstacles and opportunities for improving service delivery.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from June 2023 to March 2024 across tertiary care hospitals in Pakistan. Healthcare providers completed a structured, self-administered questionnaire capturing sociodemographic information, SRH-related knowledge, attitudes, practices and perceived barriers to care. Descriptive statistics and Pearson’s χ2 tests were used to assess associations between categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression identified factors associated with positive SRH-related knowledge-seeking behaviours, attitudes and practices.ResultsAmong 319 participants, 59.6% reported counselling patients on SRH, though only 38.2% had attended relevant workshops. Fear of stigmatisation (60.8%), religious beliefs (48.6%) and lack of knowledge (47.3%) were reported as major barriers to SRH access. While participants generally expressed progressive attitudes, such as support for comprehensive sex education (86.5%) and youth access to contraception (75.6%), engagement in related practices remained limited. Positive SRH attitudes were significantly associated with employment in private institutions (OR 3.93, 95% CI 1.18 to 13.14), higher confidence in personal SRH knowledge (OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.08 to 3.58), and ease in discussing SRH with male guardians (OR 2.20, 95% CI 1.22 to 3.97).ConclusionsDespite encouraging attitudes among healthcare providers, substantial gaps in SRH education and proactive engagement persist. Addressing these deficiencies through structured training programmes, supportive institutional environments and policy-level reforms is essential to advancing SRH equity and quality in Pakistan.Keywords: Sexual Health, Health Services Accessibility, Community Health, Public Health

Introduction

Sexual and reproductive health (SRH) refers to the physical, emotional, mental and social well-being related to sexuality and reproduction and is recognised as a fundamental human right and a core component of universal health coverage.1 Despite global initiatives such as the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development Goals, progress towards equitable SRH access has been inconsistent, especially in resource-constrained settings in the Global South where health systems face persistent structural, sociocultural and workforce-related constraints. In these contexts, healthcare providers are often the first point of contact for individuals seeking SRH care. Their knowledge, information-seeking behaviours, attitudes and clinical practices can shape not only the quality of care delivered but also the willingness of clients to access and continue using these essential services, thereby directly driving SRH-related outcomes.2

Evidence from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) demonstrates that deficiencies in provider training, stigma related to sexuality and limited institutional support are key barriers to effective SRH service delivery, especially for adolescents and young adults. Studies have reported suboptimal healthcare provider knowledge of WHO-recommended guidelines for several SRH components, including basic emergency obstetric care, alongside negative provider attitudes toward young people’s use of contraception, which significantly restrict service access and utilisation.3 A scoping review of preconception care by Aynalem et al found that inadequate provider training significantly impedes service delivery.4

These provider-level and system-level gaps have tangible population-level consequences, as reflected in recent epidemiological trends across South Asia. In Pakistan, HIV outbreaks over the past decade have disproportionately affected adolescents and young adults, highlighting persistent failures in early sexual health education, risk recognition and the provision of youth-friendly SRH services. Notably, the 2019 outbreak in the Larkana district represents the largest paediatric HIV outbreak ever documented in Pakistan, with over 900 new infections identified between April and June 2019, of which 86% were children younger than 15 years.5

In Pakistan, where women constitute 47.8% of the population, SRH challenges are compounded by sociocultural norms, gender inequities and limited access to comprehensive sexuality education. Approximately 22% of Pakistani women cite fear of encountering impolite healthcare workers as one of the reasons for avoiding contraception services.6 Rather than turn to trained professionals, 53% of young women in Pakistan rely on peers and 26% on media for SRH-related information.7 Low contraceptive use among young married couples is also shaped not only by sociocultural constraints, such as limited autonomy and mobility, but by a broader environment in which healthcare providers may fail to offer supportive, non-discriminatory care.8 A 2018 study by Roberts et al further reinforces these concerns, revealing low levels of fertility awareness even among medical trainees.9

Existing literature in Pakistan has largely focused on patient-level barriers to SRH access, with limited empirical attention to healthcare providers’ information-seeking behaviours, attitudes and clinical practices related to SRH, despite their central role in shaping patient experiences and outcomes. Understanding how healthcare providers seek SRH-related information, perceive sensitive SRH issues and translate attitudes into practice is essential for designing effective training programmes, institutional policies and health system interventions.

This study therefore aimed to assess healthcare providers’ information-seeking behaviours, attitudes and practices related to sexual and reproductive health in tertiary healthcare settings in Pakistan, and to identify factors associated with both positive and limited engagement in SRH care. By addressing this gap, the study seeks to generate contextually relevant evidence to inform policy, structured training initiatives and institutional reforms to strengthen SRH service provision in Pakistan and similar LMIC settings.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study used a cross-sectional survey design and was conducted among healthcare workers from various tertiary healthcare centres located in major urban centres of Pakistan, including Karachi, Islamabad, Lahore, Peshawar and other metropolitan areas, from June 2023 to March 2024.10 This study was conducted in adherence with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology) guidelines and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was also conducted in accordance with the COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics) guidelines.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria comprised all healthcare workers, including physicians, residents, interns, medical officers, fellows, nurses and midwives, currently working at tertiary care hospitals in Pakistan. Students (medical, dental and nursing), and allied healthcare workers such as dentists, physiotherapists, pharmacists and laboratory/operating room technicians were excluded from this study.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not directly involved in the development of the research question, study design, recruitment or conduct of this study, as it focused on healthcare providers’ knowledge-seeking, attitudes and practices. Results will be disseminated through academic publications and conference presentations and will be shared with healthcare institutions to inform training and practice.

Sample size calculation

We calculated a minimum sample size of 323, assuming a precision level of 5%, a CI of 95% and an anticipated frequency of positive attitudes and adequate knowledge with respect to SRH of 70%, based on a similar study assessing the attitudes of healthcare workers’ attitudes toward sexual and reproductive health from Ethiopia.11

Data collection tool

After a thorough literature review, the study team adapted the data collection tool from a 2020 study by Tumwine et al, that investigated healthcare practitioners’ normative attitudes and practices towards SRH in Sweden.2 The questionnaire was developed in English, given that English is the primary language of documentation in hospitals in Pakistan, and all healthcare providers are required to have a working proficiency of English. Additional context-specific questions were adapted from a WHO questionnaire assessing knowledge of SRH in adolescents.12 Questions pertaining to perceptions of barriers to SRH were included from a 2022 study by Ooms et al that investigated healthcare workers’ perspectives on access to SRH in Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia.13 To ensure the validity of our modified questionnaire, it was reviewed for face validity by experts in the field, confirming that it appropriately measured the intended constructs.

Data coding

Each participant’s responses to Likert scale questions in sections 4, 5 and 6 of the survey were summed up to yield a composite score for that section, where responses were coded on a scale from 1 (being most negative) to 5 (being most positive). For these sections, a score that was greater than or equal to the median was defined as a high score, while a score that was less than the median was defined as a low score. Median-based categorisation was used due to the absence of validated cut-off thresholds for SRH-related composite scores. According to the outcome variables (knowledge seeking behaviours, perceptions and practices), high scores were interpreted as ‘active knowledge seekers’ or having positive attitudes or being more likely to take steps towards improving access to sexual and reproductive health and rights. The highest possible score attainable in the knowledge seeking section was 40, while for attitudes and practice sections it was 35.

Data collection procedure

The questionnaire was digitised using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). Non-probability convenience sampling was employed to recruit eligible participants. A group of Aga Khan University (AKU) medical students, who received standardised training on study objectives, ethical conduct, participant consent and use of the REDCap platform, served as data collectors, distributing the digital questionnaire both in person and online via email and social media platforms such as WhatsApp and Facebook. Where feasible, they administered the survey on-site, asking respondents to complete it using REDCap, and emailed the survey link to expand outreach.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata V.17 (StataCorp). Categorical data was summarised as frequencies and percentages. Data normality was primarily assessed through visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots and further evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test as an adjunct. Given the continuous data were not normally distributed, they were represented as medians and IQRs.

Binary logistic regression analyses were used to determine the predictors of positive knowledge-seeking behaviours, attitudes and practices. Variables that were selected into the multivariable model were those that crossed a significance threshold of p<0.25 on univariable analyses. All tests were two-sided, and a p value of <0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study enrolled 319 participants, with a slightly higher proportion of females (55.2%, n=176) than males (44.8%, n=143). Due to the use of convenience sampling across multiple sites, an exact response rate could not be calculated. The median age was 27 years (IQR: 25–32). Most respondents were unmarried (59.6%, n=190), and the majority had trained and worked in Pakistan. In terms of occupation, residents comprised the largest group (36.1%, n=115), followed by nurses and midwives (26.3%, n=84), interns (25.1%, n=80) and consultants (12.5%, n=40). A significant proportion of participants received their education from private institutions (58.3%, n=186) and were currently employed at private training facilities (86.2%, n=275), with a median of 3 years (IQR: 1–6) of clinical experience. AKU was the most common workplace and training site in the sample. Online supplemental table 1 provides a detailed overview of the sociodemographic and employment characteristics of the sample.

Self-reported knowledge of sexual and reproductive health

Most participants expressed confidence in their knowledge of SRH, with 73% (n=233) either agreeing (52%, n=166) or strongly agreeing (21%, n=67) with the statement. Only a small minority expressed disagreement (6%, n=19). While 38.2% (n=122) had attended SRH-related workshops, a larger proportion (59.6%, n=190) reported counselling patients on SRH. However, institutional support appeared limited, only 20.4% (n=65) noted that their departments had organised relevant sessions. The median duration of work related to SRH was 0 years (IQR: 0–2), indicating limited formal experience in the field. These data have been summarised in online supplemental table 2.

Knowledge of access to sexual and reproductive health Services

The study revealed that 79.3% (n=253) of respondents believe clients are reluctant to access SRH commodities. Key reasons selected by these respondents included fear of stigmatisation (60.8%, n=154), religious beliefs (48.6%, n=123) and lack of knowledge (47.3%, n=120). Additional barriers were myths (34.2%, n=87), low support from male partners (36.7%, n=93) and cost (29.5%, n=75). Potential solutions that were selected by participants to address reluctance in accessing SRH services included expanding client education for both males and females was crucial (64.3%, n=205), creating youth/adolescent-friendly health corners (32%, n=102), involving partners in SRH services (33.9%, n=108) and providing free family planning services (33.5%, n=107). Participants believed that clients who visited their health facilities were usually provided with the SRH-related services or commodities they requested (84%, n=268). If they were unable to provide services or commodities (16%, n=51), participants believed they were mainly due to their institution not offering family planning services (23.8%, n=12), services not being culturally or religiously acceptable (21.3%, n=11) or lack of knowledge of the service by healthcare workers (21.3%, n=11). Attitudes regarding sexual and reproductive health

The majority of respondents (82.4%, n=263) agreed or strongly agreed that inequality is responsible for poor maternal and neonatal health outcomes, all genders and sexual orientations should have equal access to healthcare and education (85.3%, n=272), men and women are equally affected by sexual violence (82.8%, n=264), the LGBTQ+ community should have equitable access to HIV/sexually transmitted infection (STI) care (82.1%, n=262), people should have access to comprehensive sexual education (86.5%, n=276) and youth should have access to contraception (75.6%, n=241). While still a majority, a relatively smaller proportion of respondents agreed or strongly agreed that abortion is a woman’s right (55.2%, n=176), and a large number of respondents reported being neutral on this topic (23.8%, n=76). On multivariable analysis, positive attitudes regarding SRH showed a statistically significant positive association with working in a private institution (OR 3.932 (95% CI 1.177 to 13.14)), smoking (OR 3.408 (95% CI 1.332 to 8.716)), confidence in knowledge of SRH (OR 1.97 (95% CI 1.084 to 3.577)), and ease in conversing with male guardian about SRH (OR 2.203 (95% CI 1.223 to 3.971)). Having older siblings was found to have a statistically significant negative association with positive attitudes regarding SRH (OR 0.554 (95% CI 0.321 to 0.953)) (online supplemental table 4).

Practices regarding sexual and reproductive health

A large number of respondents reported considering issues of sexual violence (49.2%, n=157), the LGBTQ+ community’s access to HIV/STI care (39.2%, n=125), equal access to SRH (41.7%, n=133), comprehensive sexual education (42.6%, n=136), youth access to contraception (41.4%, n=132), cervical cancer screening (40.8%, n=130) and access to abortion (36.7%,) to be important. However, while most of the respondents recognise the importance of these issues, few have taken steps to address them. In addition, a significant portion of respondents reported not thinking about issues like access to abortion (41.07%), and the LGBTQ+ community’s access to HIV/STI care (39.5%). Multivariable analysis showed that smoker status was statistically associated with being more likely to take steps towards improving access to sexual and reproductive health and rights (OR 3.422 (95% CI 1.416 to 8.271)) (online supplemental table 5).

Discussion

This study explored healthcare providers’ information-seeking behaviours, attitudes and practices related to SRH in tertiary healthcare settings in Pakistan. Our results highlight several interrelated gaps, including limited formal SRH training exposure, low engagement in active knowledge-seeking, particularly for stigmatised topics and a disconnect between generally positive attitudes and the translation of these attitudes into routine clinical practice. Together, these findings offer valuable insights for strengthening provider capacity and informing institutional and policy-level interventions aimed at improving SRH service delivery in Pakistan.

Overall, our findings indicate that healthcare providers in tertiary settings expressed high confidence in their knowledge of SRH. However, active information-seeking behaviour remains relatively limited, especially concerning sensitive or controversial areas such as LGBTQ+ health and abortion access. This aligns with existing literature reporting gaps in comprehensive SRH knowledge among medical trainees in Pakistan and other settings, identifying striking gaps in understanding of fertility knowledge among obstetrics and gynaecology medical residents worldwide.9 Multiple studies report that healthcare professionals realise the need for SRH-related programmes to tackle the cultural taboos and conservative societal norms, arguing that missing out on these has left them unprepared.14 15 These findings collectively suggest that confidence in SRH knowledge does not necessarily reflect objective competence and may coexist with substantial education gaps.

Interestingly, our analysis revealed a discrepancy between healthcare providers’ high self-reported confidence in their SRH knowledge and their limited engagement in formalised SRH training programmes or institutional learning sessions. This disconnect points toward a potential gap in recognising the need for continuous professional development (CPD) related to SRH. A previous study conducted in Pakistan showed a major number of physicians were at ease addressing SRH issues with their patient, but implied the importance of a structured training programme beginning from medical school.16 Another study assessing the impact of sexual and reproductive health and rights training programmes in low-income countries showed promising results indicating that those with higher knowledge-seeking behaviour performed better on test scores post-training, highlighting the importance of a positive attitude towards SRH education.17 Given this, institutions should adopt formalised, ongoing training mechanisms, such as mandatory CPD sessions, that emphasise areas of deficiency identified by providers themselves, particularly for contentious or neglected SRH topics.18

Our respondents identified significant perceived barriers limiting patient access to SRH services, notably fear of stigmatisation, religious beliefs, myths and inadequate partner support. This finding aligns with broader regional studies from South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, which underscore that provider perceptions and attitudes directly influence client utilisation of SRH services.19 20 Even though healthcare workers perceive SRH as a basic right, provision of these services is liable to the social and cultural norms.21 Our findings suggest that although healthcare providers understand patient-side barriers, they may underestimate their role in reinforcing or mitigating these barriers. Encouraging healthcare workers to critically reflect on how their attitudes and behaviours contribute to these perceptions is thus vital. Additionally, service unavailability emerged as a key structural barrier. Similar findings from East Africa have identified limited availability of SRH services as a primary constraint, underscoring the need for policy-level and infrastructural reforms to improve access.22

The participants’ generally positive attitudes towards equitable SRH access for diverse populations, including acknowledgement of rights to sexual education and contraception access for youth, are encouraging. These attitudes were significantly associated with employment at private institutions, comfort in discussing SRH topics and lifestyle factors such as smoking. While some of these associations (eg, private institution employment) may reflect differences in exposure to progressive institutional cultures or resources, other associations, such as smoking behaviour, might indirectly represent broader attitudinal openness or risk tolerance among certain providers.8 23 The association between smoking and more positive SRH-related attitudes and practices likely reflects unmeasured social or behavioural factors, such as broader social exposure or lower adherence to conservative norms, rather than a causal effect. This finding should be interpreted cautiously and does not suggest any benefit of smoking, but instead highlights the need for qualitative research to explore underlying drivers of provider openness and engagement in SRH care.

Globally, there have been mixed findings. Some studies report more acceptability towards sexual minorities and adolescents, while others show how different factors such as increased age of the healthcare worker and cultural influences are a reason for harbouring more negative perceptions.24,26 Another study from Taiwan showed that even though nurses had positive perceptions about LGBTQ+, they attributed the inability to approach such patients to lack of knowledge.27 These findings highlight an underlying need to integrate sensitisation training and targeted interventions for SRH provision, particularly in more conservative settings.

Even with the presence of some positive attitudes, unfortunately, there still is limited translation into proactive practices. Providers identified significant importance for addressing sexual violence, LGBTQ+ access to care and abortion; however, actual engagement or implementation steps remained minimal. These gaps between attitude and practice resonate with global literature indicating similar phenomena, where attitudinal shifts alone fail to prompt actionable change without structural support or clear institutional policies.1 Healthcare practitioners identify the lack of policies and infrastructure as a significant hurdle to improving SRH access.28 29 Practical measures could include creating structured referral pathways, supportive supervision mechanisms and formal guidelines to bridge this gap effectively.

Our findings hold important implications for informing policy changes and institutional regulations. First, our data emphasise the need to integrate comprehensive SRH training into existing medical curricula systematically. This would ensure that healthcare providers begin their professional journeys adequately equipped with evidence-based knowledge and skills to deliver non-discriminatory, empathetic SRH care. Additionally, institutional regulations should mandate periodic refresher training or CPD sessions, emphasising sensitive or stigmatised SRH issues. At the policy level, explicit guidelines promoting inclusive and stigma-free SRH service environments, backed by clear accountability and reporting mechanisms, can directly mitigate healthcare provider biases, promoting a more supportive environment for SRH care delivery.

Limitations

It is essential to acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, data were self-reported, which may have introduced social desirability and recall bias, particularly given the sensitive nature of the topics. Participants may have over-reported positive attitudes or practices related to SRH. To mitigate this risk, the survey was anonymous, self-administered and conducted using a secure digital platform, and participants were assured of confidentiality to encourage honest reporting.

Second, the study employed non-probability convenience sampling across tertiary care institutions, which limits the generalisability of the findings. The sample included a high proportion of participants from private-sector institutions, potentially reflecting more progressive institutional environments and greater access to training resources than in public-sector settings. As a result, the observed attitudes and practices may not be fully representative of all healthcare providers in Pakistan.

Third, our population subset was not restricted solely to healthcare providers with SRH routinely integrated into their daily practices. Inclusion of diverse healthcare roles, such as nurses, midwives and residents across various specialties, may have introduced variability in SRH knowledge, attitudes and practices unrelated to direct SRH exposure. While this heterogeneity limits the ability to generalise findings exclusively to SRH-focused practitioners, it offers insight into the broader landscape of SRH-related competencies across the healthcare workforce. This diversity reflects the multidisciplinary nature of real-world clinical environments, where providers from various backgrounds contribute to SRH care. Future research should specifically target healthcare workers whose routine responsibilities include SRH services to better understand nuanced training and attitude gaps.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings provide valuable evidence highlighting critical knowledge and practice gaps among healthcare providers, coupled with positive attitudes towards equitable SRH care in Pakistan. Our results underscore the urgency of targeted institutional and policy-level interventions. Structured educational programmes, sensitisation training and clear regulatory frameworks are necessary to bridge the divide between positive attitudes and actual SRH practices, ultimately enhancing patient access and outcomes within resource-constrained healthcare settings in Pakistan and comparable global contexts.
