Hip range of motion is differentially associated with pain sensitivity in individuals with and without knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Isabela Lima Nóbrega, Marialice Gyaraki da Silva, Filipe Estevão Sette, Natalia Aparecida Casonato, Roberta Freitas Tizziotti, Stela Marcia Mattiello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a multifactorial condition influenced by both mechanical and neurophysiological factors. Hip joint mobility may be associated to knee joint loading and pain processing; however, the relationships between hip range of motion (ROM), pain sensitivity, and muscle torque in individuals with KOA remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between knee pain sensitivity and hip rotator ROM in individuals with and without KOA. Additionally, it examined the association between hip rotation ROM and muscle torque and compared hip ROM between groups.MethodsThis cross-sectional comparative study included 38 participants (26 with KOA and 12 healthy controls). A comprehensive physical assessment was conducted, including evaluation of both hip and knee range of motion (ROM) across multiple hip movements (flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and internal and external rotation), measured using a digital inclinometer and a universal goniometer. Hip muscle torque was assessed using isokinetic dynamometry. Pain sensitivity was evaluated through Quantitative Sensory Testing, including pressure pain threshold (PPT), temporal summation (TS), and conditioned pain modulation (CPM). Group comparisons were performed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). Multiple linear regression models were used to examine the association between hip rotational ROM and pain sensitivity, including interaction terms for group.ResultsNo significant differences in hip or knee ROM were observed between groups. However, a significant interaction between total hip rotation and group was found for PPT at the knee joint line (p = 0.039; adjusted R2 = 0.372). Specifically, in the control group, greater hip rotational ROM was associated with higher PPT values (β = 0.068), indicating lower pain sensitivity. In contrast, in individuals with KOA, greater hip rotational ROM was associated with lower PPT values (β = − 0.047), indicating higher pain sensitivity. No significant associations were observed for muscle torque, temporal summation, or conditioned pain modulation.ConclusionsThe association between hip ROM and pain sensitivity appears to differ according to the presence of KOA. These findings suggest that proximal mobility may be differentially related to pain sensitivity measures depending on clinical status, supporting the need for further investigation into hip-related factors in individuals with KOA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10127-1.

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a disabling chronic condition characterized by articular cartilage degeneration, bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, and chronic inflammation, leading to pain, stiffness, and restricted movement in the affected joint, with higher prevalence among individuals over 60 years of age [1]. The knee is the most commonly affected joint, followed by the hip, with disease onset and progression influenced by multiple factors, including acute and chronic joint injuries, aging, obesity, and metabolic bone disease [2].

Pain is the most prevalent symptom in individuals with knee osteoarthritis (KOA), significantly impacting activities of daily living such as walking, sitting and rising, and ascending and descending stairs, thereby increasing the risk of falls in cases of high pain intensity [3, 4]. According to the systematic review by Fingleton et al. [5], individuals with KOA exhibit differences in pain experience, with symptom severity associated with alterations in nociceptive processing, characterized by both peripheral and central sensitization mechanisms. Alterations such as widespread pain and increased sensitivity to cold and heat may occur as a result of repetitive or intense nociceptive stimuli originating from the joint [6].

Recommended treatments for KOA involve an individualized approach focused primarily on pain reduction and lower limb rehabilitation, particularly targeting the thigh muscles and hip abductors [7–9]. Biomechanical evidence indicates that individuals with KOA demonstrate altered hip kinematic patterns during functional activities such as gait, including reduced internal rotation, movement asymmetries, and pelvic compensations, which are associated with increased knee joint loading [10–12]. The hip plays a crucial role in knee load distribution. Hip internal and external rotation movements are biomechanically interconnected and directly influence the knee adduction moment [12].

Studies have shown that limitations in hip range of motion (ROM), particularly in the rotational and sagittal planes, may contribute to increased knee adduction moment and lead to a less efficient gait pattern, potentially associated with KOA progression [11, 12].

Although previous studies have demonstrated associations between hip muscle strength, function, and pain in individuals with KOA [8, 13, 14], based on the reviewed literature, no studies have specifically investigated hip range of motion and its association with knee pain in this population.

Clinical assessments of hip ROM remain underexplored in individuals with KOA, limiting the understanding of the impact of these alterations on knee pain and joint overload. Furthermore, hip ROM restrictions may impair lower limb muscle activation and function, potentially contributing to the progression of pain and functional disability. Therefore, investigating the relationship between hip ROM and pain sensitivity may broaden clinical assessment strategies and support the development of new individualized physiotherapeutic interventions for individuals with KOA.

Methods

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the association between knee pain sensitivity and hip total rotational range of motion in individuals with and without KOA. Secondary objectives were to examine the association between hip rotational muscle torque and hip total rotational range of motion, and to compare additional hip and knee range of motion measures between individuals with and without KOA.

This was a cross-sectional study conducted in accordance with the STROBE Statement (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology), including individuals with clinically diagnosed KOA and healthy controls. The study followed the Brazilian National Health Council Resolution 466/2012 and was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of São Carlos. All participants provided written informed consent. The final sample comprised 40 participants, based on methodological recommendations for multiple linear regression models. Inclusion criteria:

The KOA group included men and women aged ≥ 40 years, with knee pain ≥ 4/10 on the Numeric Pain Rating Scale for at least 3 months and meeting the American College of Rheumatology clinical criteria for KOA [15]. Controls were ≥ 40 years, without KOA or knee pain, and were matched by age and sex.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria included recent knee surgery, intra-articular corticosteroid injections within 3 months, other rheumatologic conditions, BMI > 40 kg/m2, other diagnosed musculoskeletal disorders, clinical hip osteoarthritis, or greater pain in another body region than in the knee.

Procedures

Assessments were conducted at the Laboratory of Joint Function Analysis (LAFAr), UFSCar, in two visits (3–15 days apart). Pain intensity had to differ by less than 2 points between visits [16, 17]. Participants completed the Numeric Pain Rating Scale and underwent clinical and functional assessments.

Range of Motion (ROM) assessment

Hip and knee ROM were measured passively using a digital inclinometer [18] and universal goniometer (for hip abduction and adduction), following standardized positioning (Fig. 1): Hip flexion:supine (Fig. 1A); Hip abduction and adduction: supine, pelvis stabilized (Fig. 1B-C); Hip internal and external rotation: seated, knees at 90° (Fig. 1D-E); Knee flexion:prone (Fig. 1F); Knee extension: seated (Fig. 1G). Intra-rater reliability was assessed in a pilot sample (n = 8) using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC [1, 2]) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Because reliability varied across measures, only variables demonstrating acceptable reliability (ICC ≥ 0.70) and prespecified relevance to the primary study objective were included in the primary analyses. Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC)

The Brazilian version of the WOMAC evaluates three domains: pain, stiffness, and physical function. Each item is scored on a Likert scale from 0 to 4, with maximum scores of 20 for pain, 8 for stiffness, and 68 for physical function [19, 20]. For the analyses, the sum of the three domains was used, yielding a maximum total score of 96.

Quantitative Sensory Testing (QST)

QST included assessment of pressure pain threshold (PPT), temporal summation (TS), and conditioned pain modulation (CPM), performed in this order, from the least to the most discomfort [21, 22].

Pressure Pain Threshold (PPT)

PPT was assessed using a digital pressure algometer (OE-220, ITO Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation, Japan) with a 1 cm2 probe. Measurements were taken at the knee joint line (3 cm medial to the medial border of the patella) on the affected or most affected limb, and at a remote site (5 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle of the humerus) on the contralateral forearm [23]. Pressure was applied perpendicularly at a rate of 0.5 kgf/s. Participants pressed a stop button at the first perception of pain. In the KOA group, the most symptomatic knee was tested (involved limb),in the control group, the dominant side was defined as the involved limb.

Temporal Summation (TS)

Participants rated pain intensity (0–10 NPRS) in response to a single mechanical pinprick stimulus (Pinprick Stimulator 256 mN; MRC Systems GmbH, Germany) applied at the knee joint line. Subsequently, three series of ten stimuli at 1 Hz were delivered, with 30-s intervals between series. Pain ratings were recorded after each series. TS was calculated as the percentage of the maximum possible effect (%MPE) [24].

Conditioned Pain Modulation (CPM)

CPM followed the recommendations of Yarnitsky et al. (2015). A pressure stimulus was applied at the medial knee joint line and interrupted when pain reached 4/10 on the NPRS. The conditioning stimulus consisted of immersion of the contralateral hand in 4 °C water up to the wrist for one minute. Immediately afterward, a second pressure stimulus was applied and stopped at 4/10 pain. CPM was calculated as the difference between the first and second measurements; negative values indicated pain inhibition and positive values pain facilitation [25–27].

Muscle torque

Muscle torque was assessed using the Biodex System 4 Pro (Biodex Medical Systems Inc., Shirley, NY, USA). Participants were instructed to avoid physical activity for 24 h prior to testing. Bilateral concentric muscle torque was measured for hip abductors, adductors, and internal and external rotators, as well as knee flexors and extensors [28, 29].

Participants first performed three submaximal repetitions for familiarization at the same angular velocity used in the testing protocol. After a one-minute rest, they completed one set of five concentric/concentric contractions at low angular velocities: 30°/s for the hip and 60°/s for the knee [30]. A three-minute rest interval was provided between muscle groups [28].

All participants were positioned according to standardized manufacturer guidelines, with the trunk and contralateral limb stabilized to prevent compensatory movements.

For hip abductor and adductor testing, participants were positioned in side-lying, with the tested limb extended and parallel to the floor. The dynamometer axis was aligned near the greater trochanter, and the lever arm was secured at the distal third of the femur. The tested range was 0° to 30° of abduction from neutral [30, 31].

For hip rotator assessment, participants were seated with the hip and knee at 90° of flexion. The dynamometer axis was aligned with the knee joint line, and the lever arm was fixed 5 cm above the medial malleolus. The tested range was from 10° of internal rotation to 20° of external rotation.

For knee flexor and extensor evaluation, participants remained seated with hip and knee at 90° of flexion. The dynamometer axis was aligned with the lateral femoral epicondyle, and the lever arm was positioned 5 cm above the medial malleolus. The range tested was from 20° to 90° of knee flexion (0° representing full extension).

Statistical analysis

For the primary analysis, four multiple linear regression models were performed for the quantitative sensory testing outcomes: pressure pain threshold (PPT; local and remote), temporal summation (TS), and conditioned pain modulation (CPM). For each outcome, associations with total hip rotation, group status, and the interaction between total hip rotation and group were tested, with all models adjusted for sex. To account for multiple comparisons across the four quantitative sensory testing (QST) models, a Bonferroni correction was applied. Additionally, multicollinearity was assessed using the Generalized Variance Inflation Factor (GVIF) to ensure model validity (Detailed reliability results are provided in Supplementary Table S2).

The assumptions of multiple linear regression were verified through visual inspection of residuals. Normality was confirmed using histograms and Q–Q plots, as well as by the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated a normal distribution of residuals (W = 0.969; p = 0.373).

To investigate the association between hip rotator muscle torque and total hip rotation range of motion in individuals with and without KOA, two multiple linear regression models were performed using hip internal and external rotator muscle torque as outcomes. For each outcome, the associations with total hip rotation, group status, and the interaction between total hip rotation and group were tested, with all models adjusted for sex. Mean peak torque values were used and normalized to body mass ([Nm/kg] × 100) for data analysis [32]. Group comparisons for range of motion (ROM) were performed using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA). Age and Body Mass Index (BMI) were included as covariates to control for their potential confounding effects on joint mobility.

Results

Of the 143 individuals who initially expressed interest, most were excluded due to nonresponse or ineligibility. Sixty-two participants attended the first visit, after which 24 were excluded for clinical reasons or failure to meet diagnostic criteria, and 4 were excluded due to incomplete data. The final sample comprised 38 participants, including 26 individuals with KOA and 12 healthy controls. Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to determine whether the association between knee pain sensitivity and hip range of motion (ROM) differs between individuals with and without knee osteoarthritis (KOA). In the analysis of Model 1 predictors (pressure pain threshold—PPT), total hip rotation, considered independently, was not a direct predictor of pain sensitivity. However, the presence of a significant interaction between hip rotational ROM and group status indicated that the relationship between hip mobility and pain sensitivity varies according to the presence of osteoarthritis. Specifically, in healthy individuals, greater total hip rotation was associated with higher knee PPT values. In contrast, individuals in the KOA group exhibited an inverse relationship, characterized by lower PPT values as hip ROM increased. As illustrated in Fig. 2, these divergent interaction patterns suggest that the mechanisms underlying the mechanical modulation of pain may operate differently in osteoarthritic and non-osteoarthritic contexts.

From a biomechanical perspective, Powers [33] proposed that increased hip excursion, when not accompanied by adequate neuromuscular control, may lead to elevated distal joint stress, particularly at the knee.

Supporting this interpretation, Yang et al. [34] reported that individuals with mild KOA exhibit spatiotemporal gait alterations accompanied by compensatory neuromuscular adjustments. While these adaptations may initially enhance stability, they also modify frontal-plane joint moments and knee load distribution. Similarly, Pan et al. [35] identified altered movement patterns and joint moments at both the hip and knee in individuals with mild to moderate KOA, suggesting that proximal adaptations may negatively influence tibiofemoral mechanics.

From this perspective, we interpret that an increase in hip rotational ROM, within a system already sensitized by osteoarthritis, could act as a predisposing factor for elevated cumulative tissue loading. However, it is fundamental to consider that the data collected in the present study do not allow for the confirmation of this causal chain, given that direct assessments of motor control, reaction forces, or three-dimensional kinematics were not performed. Thus, while the cited literature provides a foundation for our theoretical interpretation, alternative explanatory pathways must be acknowledged; an increased hip ROM might instead represent a successful functional compensatory strategy to protect the knee joint, rather than a definitive indicator of increased mechanical loading.

Importantly, the sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S3) demonstrated that the interaction between full hip rotation and the knee osteoarthritis group remained consistent in direction and magnitude after adjusting for age, BMI, and pain intensity, although statistical significance was attenuated (p = 0.056). This finding suggests that the observed association may not be entirely independent of clinical and demographic characteristics and should therefore be interpreted with caution. However, the small variation in the regression coefficient between models corroborates a certain degree of consistency in the observed relationship, while highlighting the need for replication in larger samples. Similarly, although our findings provide important insights regarding the relationship between hip and knee range of motion in this population, the discrepancy in sample sizes between the groups represents a limitation that may compromise statistical power for detecting minor variances, as well as the stability of the regression models. Therefore, future research employing balanced sample designs is warranted to verify the robustness and external validity of our findings.

Regression models including remote PPT (contralateral forearm), temporal summation (TS), and conditioned pain modulation (CPM) did not demonstrate statistically significant associations with hip ROM. While local quantitative sensory tests typically reflect peripheral mechanical sensitivity and remote tests are used to screen for central alterations [36], our findings demonstrated a significant association exclusively with local PPT.

This specific pattern of results may suggest that proximal mobility is more actively intertwined with localized, peripheral mechanical pain sensitivity at the knee rather than with the evaluated central pain facilitation or inhibitory mechanisms. However, this trend must be interpreted with caution and should not be taken as definitive evidence against the involvement of central pathways. The absence of statistically significant associations in the remote PPT, TS, and CPM models may have been influenced by our limited sample size and restricted statistical power to detect small-to-moderate effects. Furthermore, because TS and CPM assess only specific, isolated aspects of nociceptive processing, they do not fully capture the entire complexity of central sensitization. Therefore, while our data potentially point toward a primary link with local mechanical sensitivity, they indicate a lack of association only within the specific central pain measures evaluated, without ruling out the potential contribution of broader central sensitization processes.

Regarding secondary outcomes, no statistically significant differences were found between the KOA and control groups for any hip ROM measures. Although the KOA group demonstrated slightly lower mean ROM values, these differences were minimal and unlikely to be clinically meaningful. These findings differ from those reported by Ro et al. [11], who observed reduced hip ROM in individuals with KOA during functional tasks. This discrepancy may be attributed to methodological differences, as ROM in the present study was assessed passively, whereas Ro et al. evaluated dynamic, task-based movement. In contrast, studies by Costa et al. [37] and Matsumura et al. [10] similarly reported no significant group differences during passive ROM assessments.

Kinematic studies further suggest that movement distribution may be more relevant than total available ROM. For instance, Yoon and Moon [12] identified altered hip rotational patterns, specifically reduced internal rotation and changes in the internal-to-external rotation ratio during gait in individuals with KOA. These findings emphasize dynamic movement behavior rather than static clinical measurements, which may explain the absence of group differences observed in the present study.

Together, these results indicate that hip ROM alterations associated with KOA may not be fully captured through static clinical assessments and may become more evident during dynamic tasks. However, the literature remains inconclusive, underscoring the need for studies integrating both static and dynamic assessments to better elucidate these mechanisms.

With respect to muscle torque, the regression models demonstrated overall significance; however, total hip rotational ROM was not significantly associated with torque production in either group. The initial hypothesis, that greater ROM would be linked to higher torque, was based on evidence suggesting that full-range training enhances strength gains [38]. Additionally, systematic reviews indicate substantial strength deficits in individuals with KOA, including reductions of 14–32% in hip abductor strength and marked deficits in rotational strength, with decreases of 46–47% in external rotation and 37–38% in internal rotation compared with healthy controls [39].

In the present study, however, passive hip rotational ROM was not associated with muscle torque. These findings suggest that although training through full ROM may enhance strength, passively assessed static ROM does not directly predict torque-generating capacity. Furthermore, torque assessment was conducted in standardized positions, which may not have captured mechanical disadvantages related to asymmetry. According to Cibulka et al. [40], muscle force production is influenced by joint position and movement symmetry. Future research should therefore examine the relationship between muscle torque and hip ROM using angle-specific and symmetry-dependent approaches.

The present findings provide relevant clinical insights into the relationship between hip mobility and pain sensitivity in individuals with and without KOA. The clinical relevance of these findings lies in the integration of accessible clinical measures with more advanced laboratory-based assessments. While hip ROM is a cost-effective, quick, and widely used measure in clinical practice, its interpretation has traditionally been limited to mechanical or structural perspectives. Our results suggest that hip rotational ROM may also be associated with aspects of pain processing.

Therefore, the evaluation of hip ROM, particularly in the transverse plane, may offer complementary information when assessing individuals with KOA, especially in cases where reported symptoms are disproportionate to structural findings. Integrating physical examination with measures reflecting pain sensitivity may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of patient presentation.

In addition, these findings may have implications for clinical decision-making and patient stratification. Hip ROM could potentially be incorporated into broader assessment frameworks aimed at identifying subgroups of individuals with distinct pain profiles, which may be relevant for tailoring management strategies. From a research perspective, the results support the need for future studies investigating whether interventions targeting hip mobility, motor control, or lower-limb biomechanics may be with changes in pain sensitivity outcomes. Longitudinal and interventional designs are particularly important to determine whether changes in hip ROM are associated with meaningful improvements in pain modulation and clinical symptoms. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of considering both mechanical and neurophysiological factors in the assessment and management of KOA, supporting the development of more integrated and individualized approaches in both clinical practice and research.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of some methodological limitations. Participants were clinically diagnosed with osteoarthritis according to Altman [15]. Although radiographic assessment could have complemented the clinical evaluation, it is important to consider that pain is not necessarily associated with morphological changes [41].

The study was able to detect large effect sizes; therefore, future studies with different sample size estimations are warranted to adequately examine small to moderate effects. Given the biomechanical interaction between pelvic motion and knee loading, pelvic kinematics should also be investigated to provide a more comprehensive understanding of hip–pelvis biomechanics.

Furthermore, the sensitivity analysis indicated that the primary interaction findings were influenced by model specification, with attenuation of statistical significance after adjustment for additional covariates. Although the stability of the regression coefficient suggests consistency in the direction of the association, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory.

Future research should adopt an integrated approach, combining static and dynamic assessments with kinematic and muscular analyses to further elucidate the relationship between joint mobility and pain sensitivity.

Conclusion

This study suggests that the association between hip rotational range of motion and pain sensitivity differs according to KOA status in individuals with and without KOA. These findings indicate that proximal mobility may be differentially related to pain sensitivity measures depending on clinical status and reinforce the need for further investigation into hip-related factors in individuals with KOA.
