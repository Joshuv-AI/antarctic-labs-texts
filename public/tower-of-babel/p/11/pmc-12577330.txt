Efficacy of vitamin C in COVID-19 management: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shuyi Zhou, Shijie Gao, Yujie Fang, Na Zhang, Guansheng Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCOVID-19 has posed a global burden, with vitamin C considered a potential treatment due to its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties. However, its efficacy remains uncertain. This study aims to evaluate the impact of vitamin C supplementation on COVID-19 patients.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, CBMdisc, WanFang Data, and CNKI from October 26, 2012, to October 1, 2023. The primary outcomes were 28-day mortality and in-hospital mortality, and the secondary outcomes were intensive care units (ICU) length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, length of vasopressor use, changes in SOFA score, and PaO2/FiO2 ratio. Random-effects models were employed to analyze the study outcomes. The Cochrane Systematic Review Guidelines and the GRADE system were utilized evaluated the risk of bias and the quality of evidence.ResultsA total of thirteen studies were included with sample of 12,062. Most of these included studies exhibited low to indeterminate bias risk. The quality of evidence ranged from very low to moderate across in included studies. The overall results indicated that non-substantial favorable impacts were observed in short-term mortality (risk ratio [RR]: 0.92, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.72 to 1.17, P = 0.415) and in hospital mortality (RR: 1.05, 95% CI: 0.95 to 1.16, P = 0.286), nor other secondary clinical outcomes.ConclusionAlthough the current application of vitamin C in COVID treatment and management, our findings revealed that vitamin C did not significantly improve COVID outcomes. More high-quality and multicenter trials are required to further elucidate the association between vitamin C and COVID-19.Trial registrationRegistration of systematic reviews: This study was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), under the registration number CRD42024527599.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11891-6.

Introduction

The Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by a highly contagious virus, has become the preeminent global health crisis in recent years, resulting in substantial fatalities and widespread contamination [1].

In the early stages of the pandemic, people sought alternative ways to enhance their immune systems against COVID-19 due to the lack of specific treatments and effective vaccines. Among these, Vitamin C, renowned for its immunomodulatory effects, emerged as a potential COVID-19 preventive agent. Google surveys revealed it as the second most popular nutritional supplement globally, with 17% to 37% of the population reporting its use between April-May 2020 and November 2020 [2]. The rationale for this widespread interest in vitamin C stems from the disease’s characteristic features: excessive oxidative stress, inflammatory storms, and compromised immunity.

COVID-19 patients tend to have low or very low levels of vitamin C, further highlighting its importance A preliminary study involving 22 patients found that most critically ill patients with COVID-19 in intensive care units (ICU) had vitamin C deficiency (average serum vitamin C level: 22.2 umol/L), and vitamin C deficiency was positively associated with COVID-19 mortality risk [3]. Another study involving 18 critically ill patients with COVID-19 in ICU showed that 94.4% of those patients had undetectable levels of vitamin C, with one patient having very low levels [4]. In March 2020, the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) stated that there was ‘no evidence to support intravenous high-dose vitamin C in the management of COVID-19 [5]. However, subsequent studies have suggested that intravenous administration (IV) of high-dose vitamin C may improve symptoms and outcomes in patients with severe or critical COVID-19, possibly by reversing the low vitamin C levels observed in these patients [6].

Vitamin C plays an extensive role in immune system, supporting various defense mechanisms, including maintaining epithelial barrier integrity, modulating the chemotaxis and antimicrobial activity of phagocytes, enhancing the activity of natural killer cells, and promoting the proliferation and differentiation of lymphocytes [7]. The antiviral properties of vitamin C also have been well documented [8–10]. Viral infections can induce intensive immune response, characterized by rapid and excessive expression of cytokines, leading to a cytokine storm. As an antioxidant, vitamin C can reduce cytokine release and alleviate tissue damage [8]. Mouse animal studies demonstrated that vitamin C could regulate antiviral cytokines production, such as interferons IFN-α/β [10]. Thus, vitamin C is often used in conjunction with other antiviral drugs, potentially aiding in suppressing viral infections and improving the survival rates [9]. Given its antioxidant, antiviral, and immune-boosting properties, vitamin C appears to be a promising adjunctive therapy, especially for critically ill patients with deficient vitamin C levels.

However, despite the clinical interest in vitamin C for COVID-19 management, its application has been hindered by inconsistent research findings [11–19]. This gap in evidence emphasizes the need for further research to clarify vitamin C’s role in the fight against COVID-19.

A recent meta-analysis of 3,429 patients reported that vitamin C was associated with a significant reduction in mortality (odds ratio [OR] = 0.64, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.51–0.80, P = 0.0001) and disease severity (OR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.43–0.80, P = 0.0006) [19]. Similarly, another meta-analysis involving 2,334 patients found that vitamin C supplementation reduced the risk of disease progression (risk ratio [RR] = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.135–0.873, P = 0.025) [17, 19]. Notably, dose and route of administration appear to influence outcomes: a meta-analysis of 15 studies (n = 2,490) demonstrated that high-dose intravenous vitamin C significantly reduced overall mortality (RR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.52–0.96, P = 0.03), whereas low-dose regimens showed no benefit (RR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.79–1.07, P = 0.46). Furthermore, intravenous vitamin C as monotherapy was associated with lower mortality (RR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.49–0.83, P = 0.006), while combination therapies did not yield significant effects, suggesting potential interactions or dilution of benefit in complex treatment regimens [15]. Despite these promising signals, other high-quality analyses have reported null findings. For instance, a meta-analysis of six RCTs (n = 572) found no significant effect of vitamin C on mortality, hospital length of stay, or ICU duration [11]. Similarly, another meta-analysis of 11 RCTs showed no improvement in in-hospital mortality or ICU stay [18]. A randomized controlled trial also reported that while high-dose intravenous vitamin C improved oxygenation (PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio; 95% CI: 33–122, P = 0.01), it did not reduce mortality [12]. These conflicting results, some showing benefit in mortality and disease progression, others reporting no significant clinical impact, highlight substantial heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, dosing strategies, and concomitant therapies. The inconsistency in evidence underscores the need for a comprehensive, up-to-date synthesis to clarify the true efficacy of vitamin C in the management of COVID-19.

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to address these research gaps and clarify the role of vitamin C in COVID-19 treatment by synthesizing previous data, enlarging the sample size, and enhancing statistical rigor. Our study can offer valuable insights for clinicians, policymakers, and individuals, informing better treatment decisions regarding vitamin C use in COVID-19 patient care and guiding informed vitamin C administration choices.

Methods

We adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines in this study, and performed PRISMA Checklist seen in the supplementary material. This study was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), under the registration number CRD42024527599.

Literature retrieval strategy

We performed a comprehensive literature searching for across several academic databases, including PubMed, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, and China Biology Medicine disc (CBMdisc), to retrieve prevalent literature. The free terms “Vitamin C OR Ascorbic Acid” AND “Novel Coronavirus or COVID-19” in addition to the medical subheading (MeSH) terms were employed in the literature searching to identify relevant articles published in both Chinese and English, spanning the period from October 26, 2012, to October 1, 2023. The detailed search strategies for each database have been added to the Supplementary Material. In addition, we also reviewed references in included articles and pertinent systematic review to further determine additional articles. Two investigators (SYZ and YJF) independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the included articles, and a third reviewer (NZ) resolved the disagreements.

Inclusion criteria

(1) RCTs and observational studies published in both Chinese and English, focusing on clinical trials investigating the association between vitamin C and COVID; (3) Studies focused on adult patients diagnosed with COVID-19; (3) The sole distinction between the intervention group and the placebo group was the administration of vitamin C therapy, aimed at assessing its effects on COVID-19 outcomes.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Duplicate reports, unpublished data, abstracts, letters, commentaries, popular science articles, conference reports, animal experiments, in vitro cell experiments, and low-quality articles were excluded from the analysis. (2) Studies lacked the methodological rigor to isolate and evaluate the independent effect of vitamin C on COVID-19 outcomes were also excluded.

Data extraction and evaluation

A pre-determined extraction table was used to extract data followed by a structured approach by two investigators (SYZ and SJG) independently. The findings were mutually cross-validated for accuracy upon completing the procedure, and discrepancies were resolved by a third researcher (NZ). The following data was extracted from included studies: first author’s last name and publication year, country, study design, sample size, participants characteristics, intervention and placebo protocol, and primary and secondary outcomes.

Quality evaluation

SYZ and SJG independently assessed the risk of bias and quality of included studies, NZ resolved disputes to guarantee the accuracy and consensus. The heterogeneity interpretation was used to determine the risk of bias among included studies, categorizing them into low, unclear, or high risk of bias categories. The GRADE-PRO framework was applied to evaluate the quality evidence, covering bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and other pertinent factors [20].

The primary outcomes in this study included short-term mortality (28-d mortality or 30-d mortality; M28/M30) in-hospital mortality (IHM), and secondary outcomes were length of Intensive Care Unit stays (ICU LOS, measured in days), length of hospital stays (Hospital LOS, measured in days), requirement for mechanical ventilation (MV), change in Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score (ΔSOFAS), the ratio of arterial oxygen partial pressure to fractional inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2).

Statistical analysis

R statistical software (version 4.3.0) with meta package (version 7.0–0) was utilized to perform Meta-analysis. Standardized mean differences (SMD) for ICU LOS, Hospital LOS and ΔSOFAS, along with RR for M28, IHM and MV, were recalculated based on the extracted data. The formulas for SMD and RR have been detailed in the supplementary material. The overall effect of heterogeneity among studies were determined using the random-effects model, evaluating both within-trial and between-trial variance [21]. The Knapp-Hartung adjustment method was applied to calculate the confidence interval of the effect value [22], and the variance in true effects is represented by τ2. For continuous outcomes, the combined results are reported as SMD and 95% CI, and RR was calculated with 95% CI for binary outcomes. The combined results are represented as SMD and 95% CI for continuous outcomes, and binary outcomes were assessed by RR with 95% CI. Cochrane’s Q test (with p < 0.10 indicating significance) and the I2 statistic, ranging from 0 to 100%, were used to evaluate between-trials heterogeneity. I² values were classified 0–40% = low, 30–60% = moderate, 50–90% = substantial, and 75–100% = considerable heterogeneity, based on the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [23].

To further explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we prespecified subgroup analyses based on study design (randomized controlled trials versus observational studies) and disease severity (critically ill, severe, or mild-to-moderate), as reported in the original studies. Outcomes examined in these subgroup analyses included IHM, M28, MV, and ΔSOFAS, with pooled effect estimates calculated using the same statistical models as in the primary analyses (RR for dichotomous outcomes and SMD for continuous outcomes). Subgroup analyses based on baseline vitamin C concentrations and on vitamin C dose (high versus low) were also planned. However, these analyses were not feasible because baseline plasma vitamin C levels were inconsistently reported, and dosing regimens varied substantially across studies depending on disease severity. For ICU LOS and Hospital LOS, the limited number of eligible studies precluded meaningful subgroup analyses.

Funnel plots were not generated due to the small number of included studies in each subgroup (fewer than ten), as visual asymmetry may be unreliable and misleading in such cases, according to PRISMA guidelines.

Results

Search strategy and study selection

A total of 4,303 articles were retrieved from electronic databases, and 3,899 articles were obtained after moving duplicates. Following title and abstract screening, this number was reduced to 215 articles, and a full-text review further narrowed it down to 13 studies [12, 24–35], in accordance with predefined criteria. At the full-text review stage, three studies were excluded because the effect of vitamin C could not be clearly distinguished from other interventions or combination therapies, consistent with our predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria. Risk of bias and quality assessment

Most of included studies exhibited a low to indeterminate bias risk, as detailed in Figs. 2 and 3. Regarding random sequence generation, the majority of included studies were rated as low risk of selection bias, whereas four of them were determined unclear risk bias [24, 26, 28, 33]. Around half of the studies had a low risk of performance bias and detection bias [12, 24–26, 29, 35], five studies demonstrated unclear risk [28, 30–33], and two studies presented a high risk for performance bias due to their open-label design [27, 34]. In regards to attrition bias, reporting bias and other bias, all studies were considered to have a low risk.Fig. 2Quality assessmentFig. 3Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias assessment

In general, four of included studies were assessed as very low evidence quality using the GRADE framework, with the exception of two studies that were determined to have a moderate. Further details of the GRADE assessment were provided in Fig. 4. The quality of evidence for M28 and ICU LOS were assessed as moderate. While, the evidence quality for IHM, Hospital LOS, MV, and ΔSOFAS were determined as very low primarily due to the inclusion of cohort studies. Efficacy outcomes

Short-term mortality

Five studies evaluated the effect of vitamin C administration on short-term mortality rate among COVID-19 patients [12, 24, 29, 33]. The results showed that a pooled RR of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.72 to 1.17, P = 0.415), indicating no statistically significant difference in the short-term mortality rate. In-hospital mortality

Seven studies reported on in-hospital mortality rates [12, 24, 26–28, 30, 34], with a combined RR of 1.05 (95% CI: 0.95 to 1.16, P = 0.286), showing no statistical significance. The heterogeneity was insignificant (I2 = 0%), demonstrating the consistency of findings across these studies (Fig. 5b).

ICU length of stay

The impact of vitamin C on the length of ICU stays was evaluated in two studies [12, 28]. The overall SMD was 0.30 (95% CI: −0.98 to 1.57, P = 0.207), with insignificant heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) observed (Fig. 5c). It should be noted that this analysis was based on only two studies, which limits the statistical power and generalizability of the findings.

Hospital length of stay

Two studies contributed data regarding the length of hospital stays [27, 30], providing a combined SMD of −0.53 (95% CI: −10.15 to 9.09, P = 0.611), which also indicated no statistically significant difference. However, a considerable heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 98%) across the analyses (Fig. 5 d). Given that this meta-analysis included only two studies, the results should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size and high heterogeneity.

Requirement for mechanical ventilation

Six studies examined the effectiveness of vitamin C in relation to the need for mechanical ventilation in COVID patient [12, 25–27, 30, 33]. The synthesis of these studies revealed a pooled RR of 1.29 (95% CI: 0.84 to 1.96, P = 0.185), indicating a non-significant trend towards a higher requirement for mechanical ventilation in the vitamin C group, although the difference was not statistically significant. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 84%) (Fig. 5e).

Change in sequential organ failure assessment score

Changes in the SOFA score were reported in three studies [12, 28, 31]. The pooled SMD was − 0.05 (95% CI: −0.58 to 0.48, P = 0.725), with no significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), (Fig. 5f).

Sub-group analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted by study design (RCTs vs. observational studies) and by disease severity (critically ill, severe, mild-to-moderate). No statistically significant differences were observed between RCTs and observational studies across outcomes. Similarly, subgroup analyses stratified by disease severity did not reveal significant effects of vitamin C treatment on any of the clinical outcomes. Due to the limited number of studies, subgroup analyses were not feasible for ICU LOS and Hospital LOS. The detailed forest plots of subgroup analyses are provided in the Supplementary Figures (Fig. S1–S5).

Discussion

A brief summary of findings and quality of included studies

Overall, our meta-analysis indicated that the administration of vitamin C did not show favorable impact on COVID-infected patients’ health outcomes, including short-term mortality, inpatient mortality, length of hospital stays, ICU stay, mechanical ventilation requirement, or SOFA score. Subgroup analyses did not reveal any statistically significant differences across disease severity groups (mild-to-moderate, severe, or critical illness). Additionally, when stratified by study design, no significant benefits of vitamin C supplementation were observed in either randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or observational cohort studies, indicating no consistent advantage of vitamin C treatment across subgroups.

In the terms of short-term mortality and in-hospital mortality rates, our results were consistent with several studies [11, 36]. Rawat, D., et al.‘s meta-analysis including 6 RCTs (n = 572) revealed that Vitamin C treatment didn’t reduce mortality (RR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.42 to 1.27; I2 = 0%; P = 0.27) [11]. In addition, Xie, Y., et al.‘s study including 57 reviews and 57 latest original studies demonstrated no significant reduction in mortality among COVID-19 patients treated with vitamin C (OR: 1.12, 95%CI: 0.44 to 2.86) [36]. However, there existed inconsistencies with studies conducted by Olczak-Pruc, M., et al., which involved a systematic review and meta-analysis involving 10 RCTs and 9 retrospective studies (N = 2,172). Their results revealed a significant reduction in mortality risk with vitamin C supplementation compared to the control group in RCTs (OR = 0.44; 95% CI: 0.25 to 0.76; P = 0.003), yet no significant change was observed in retrospective studies (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.37 to 1.68; P = 0.54) [37]. The discrepancies in the findings can be attributed to Olczak-Pruc et al. study’s subgroup analyses, as well as variations in ICU admission criteria across studies, which likely influenced disease severity and study outcomes [37].

In addition, Kow, CS, et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis incorporating 11 RCTs with 939 patients, demonstrating that vitamin C administration reduced overall mortality risk among COVID-19 patients (pooled OR = 0.53; 95% CI 0.30 to 0.94; I² = 22%), particularly among critically ill patients (pooled OR = 0.47; 95% CI 0.26 to 0.84; I² = 17%) [38]. However, our study did not perform similar subgroup analyses, which may partially account for the observed heterogeneity in results. Moreover, significant variations in dosing, ranging from 2 g to 24 g daily, were observed in the studies, whereas our study utilized doses between 6 g and 12 g. This discrepancy in dosing could be another reason for the differences in findings.

Regarding ICU and hospitalization durations, our findings were consistent with previous studies showing no significant benefits of vitamin C. Specifically, Lei, S., et al. and Xie, Y., et al. reported no substantial difference in ICU stay duration with or without vitamin C supplementation [17, 36]. Similarly, Rawat, D., et al.‘s meta-analysis and Song, C., et al.‘s mendelian randomization study, involving over 1,000,000 COVID-19 patients, found no reduction in hospitalization times [11, 39]. Our research also supports these results, indicating no significant effect of vitamin C on the need for mechanical ventilation or on SOFA scores among COVID-19 patients. Xie, Y, et al. ‘s study indicated that vitamin C did not effectively reduce the requirement of mechanical ventilation (OR, 0.73, 95%CI: 0.39 to 1.38) [36]. In addition, Lei, S, et al.‘s study demonstrated no significant reduction in SOFA scores among COVID-19 patients treated with vitamin C (SMD, −0.113, 95%CI: −1.186 to 0.961, P = 0.837) [17].

Strengths, limitations, and future research

Our findings offer valuable insights into the impact of intravenous vitamin C administration on COVID-19 clinical outcomes. By isolating the independent effect of vitamin C, we provide a more focused perspective on its role in COVID-19 treatment. Our research may inform healthcare providers and patients, enhancing outcomes and care.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations. First, some secondary outcomes—including ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, and change in SOFA score—were based on only two or three studies; pooled estimates may be imprecise, and heterogeneity metrics (I²) can be unstable or truncated, so these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Second, only PubMed, Embase, and three Chinese databases were searched, and other international databases were not included, which may have led to missed studies. Finally, meta-analyses based on very few studies have limited statistical power; while we included them to provide a more complete overview, caution is needed in interpretation. These limitations underscore the need for additional multicenter RCTs to confirm our findings.

In future COVID-19 clinical practice, vitamin C dosage and duration should be thoroughly documented. Furthermore, more multicenter RCTs are required to evaluate vitamin C’s role on COVID-19. Standardizing treatment protocols, including dosage, routes, and duration, will minimize variations. Subgroup analyses of patient demographics are crucial to understanding vitamin C’s efficacy in COVID-19 treatment.

In conclusion, our meta-analysis suggested that vitamin C supplementation did not show favorable effects on COVID-19 management. More high-quality studies are required to further explore vitamin C’s therapeutic potential in COVID-19.
