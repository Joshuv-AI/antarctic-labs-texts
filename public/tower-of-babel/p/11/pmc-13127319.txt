cJun-assisted loading of RelA DNA-binding promotes synergistic lung epithelial antimicrobial responses
Authors: Jezreel Pantaleón García, Keerthi Bhoda, Madeleine Burch-Eapen, Raquel Alonso Becerra, Paris Rodríguez Carstens, Daniela Covarrubias López, Jania Foncerrada Lizaola, Vikram V Kulkarni, Jose Martinez, Yongxing Wang, Scott E Evans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLower respiratory tract infections remain a leading cause of death worldwide, highlighting the need for host-directed therapies. We previously identified an inhaled immunostimulatory combination of Pam2CSK4 (Pam2) and CpG oligodeoxynucleotide ODN M362 (ODN) that synergistically protects against bacterial, viral, and fungal pneumonias by reprogramming lung epithelial defenses. Using RNA-seq, dual transcription factor ChIP-seq, and reverse phase protein array, we define the transcriptional basis of this synergy. Cooperative regulation by the AP-1 component cJun and the NF-κB subunit RelA is central to the Pam2+ODN response. cJun occupancy distinguishes synergistic genes, whereas RelA occupancy aligns with expression directionality. Promoter-proximal cJun occupancy facilitates enhanced RelA loading, enabling rapid transcriptional reprogramming that strengthens epithelial survival pathways and amplifies microbicidal effector programs. This AP-1–assisted NF-κB cooperation establishes inducible resistance in lung epithelium and provides a mechanistic foundation for host-directed therapies against respiratory infection.Subject areas: respiratory medicine, microbiology

Introduction

Respiratory infections remain a major cause of global morbidity and mortality.1,2,3 While global public health efforts have expanded access to vaccines against both coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and non-COVID-19 lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs),2 increasing antimicrobial resistance, the threat of emerging infections, and expanding immunocompromised populations necessitate novel LRTI management strategies.4,5,6,7

Host-directed therapies (HDTs) offer an appealing strategy to mitigate LRTIs, as an alternative or adjunct to pathogen-directed therapies.8,9,10 By harnessing intrinsic host defense mechanisms, HDTs can augment immune responses, ameliorate immunopathology, and restrict pathogen replication without directly targeting microbial viability.8,9,10,11 In efforts to develop epithelial-focused HDTs for vulnerable populations, our laboratory has reported a unique dyad of inhaled pattern recognition receptor agonists that broadly protects against LRTIs caused by Gram-positive and -negative bacteria, seasonal and pandemic viruses, and fungi.12,13,14,15,16,17,18 Comprised of a synthetic diacetylated lipopeptide, Pam2CSK4 (Pam2), and a class C CpG oligodeoxynucleotide, ODN M362 (ODN). Nebulized treatment with this combination (Pam2+ODN) enhances lung epithelium-dependent innate immune responses that reduce pathogen burden and increase host survival in a highly synergistic manner.15,19,20

Despite the robust and reproducible protective efficacy of Pam2+ODN, the molecular basis for its synergistic signaling machinery has remained incompletely understood. To define the regulatory framework underlying this response, we employed an integrated multi-omics approach to link transcriptional responses with chromatin accessibility and transcription factor (TF) occupancy. Here, we expand and identify a cooperative transcriptional mechanism in which the AP-1 component cJun facilitates the loading and functional engagement of the NF-κB subunit RelA at regulatory DNA elements. This cJun-assisted RelA occupancy drives greater-than-additive expression of antimicrobial and pro-survival genes, providing a mechanistic framework for how epithelial cells integrate multiple pathogen recognition receptor (PRR) signals to mount synergistic innate immune responses.

Results

Pam2CSK4 and ODN M362 synergistically protect lung epithelial cells against influenza A

The immunostimulatory treatment of lung epithelial cells with Pam2+ODN broadly protects against respiratory pathogens in vitro and in vivo.12,13,14,15,16,17,18,19,21 To demonstrate the synergistic interaction of the dual treatment, human bronchial epithelial (HBEC3-KT) cells were challenged with IAV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 after 4 h treatment with PBS, Pam2, ODN, or Pam2+ODN. The cells stimulated with Pam2+ODN (but not those cells treated with a single treatment) showed a significant reduction in pathogen burden 24 h after challenge (Figure 1A), as demonstrated by the expression of viral M2 protein using immunofluorescence imaging (Figure 1B). The antimicrobial effect of Pam2+ODN was associated with sustained reduction in virus-induced cytotoxicity at 8 days post infection (DPI) (Figure 1C), as demonstrated by increased uptake of neutral red viability staining (Figure 1D). No change in neutral red uptake was observed with any treatment in the absence of IAV infection (Figure S1A), enabling direct assessment of virus-induced cytotoxicity and cell survival across a wide range (MOI 0.001–10) of IAV challenges (Figures S1B–S1D). Compared to treatment with Pam2 or ODN alone, Pam2+ODN significantly improved both viral clearance and cell survival in a highly synergistic fashion (Figure 1E).

To disentangle the concurrent mechanisms of pathogen killing and host survival, we interrogated the interaction landscape between genes, TFs, and proteins in HBEC3-KT cells after 4 h of immunostimulatory treatment using a multi-Omics approach (Figure S2A). We performed independent analysis of our RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, and reverse-phase protein array (RPPA) datasets using our Omics-based interaction framework (OBIF) to reveal molecular drivers of Pam2+ODN’s phenotypic synergy. OBIF demonstrated robust model fitness (adjusted R2 > 0.60) (Figure S2B), strong discriminatory performance (ROC AUC >0.75) and conservative FDR selection (raw p-value cutoffs <0.05) (Figure S2C), enabling confident feature selection for downstream enrichment analysis (Figures S2D–S2E).

NF-kB and AP-1 regulate the lung epithelial transcriptional response to Pam2+ODN

We interrogated the transcriptional responses of HBEC-3KT cells 4 h after treatment with Pam2, ODN, or Pam2+ODN (relative to PBS) using RNA-seq (Figure 2A). Genomic mapping of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed broadly similar transcriptional landscapes across the immunostimulatory treatments (Figure 2B). However, Pam2 and ODN alone each elicited relatively imbalanced proportions of unique and shared DEGs between all conditions (Figure S3A). Pam2 treatment alone was biased toward gene induction, with the greatest proportion (Figure 2A) and number of uniquely increased DEGs (Figure S3B). In contrast, ODN treatment alone led to the smallest proportion (Figure 2A) and number of unique DEGs (Figure S3B). In comparison, Pam2+ODN led to the largest transcriptional response (339 DEGs) (Figure 2A), with a balanced proportion (∼50%) of unique genes (Figure S3A) that were increased and decreased (Figure S3B), suggesting a more extensive and globally reprogrammed transcriptional response. To better understand how individual and combined treatments shape lung epithelium’s transcriptional response, we used OBIF to identify feature clusters that capture both biological (Figure S3C) and statistical (Figure S3D) interaction patterns of combined treatments.

The majority of Pam2+ODN DEGs were found in two expression profiles (EPs) characterized by consistently increased (EP I) or decreased (EP II) gene expression across all treatments (Figure 2C). The prevalence of concordant profiles suggests a cooperative, rather than competitive, interaction when Pam2 and ODN are delivered together (Figure S3C). Such cooperative interactions resulted in non-additive responses (Figure S3D), including highly antagonistic and synergistic gene expression that cannot be explained by summing the effects of Pam2 and ODN alone (Figure 2D). Consistent with our prior experiments in mouse lung epithelium,19 functional enrichment analysis of these interaction-defined clusters identified NF-kB and AP-1 complexes as the top upstream regulators of Pam2+ODN DEGs (Figure 2E, left). However, to identify additional TF families potentially upstream of NF-kB and AP-1, we enriched for master regulators of NF-kB- and AP-1-containing networks. We did not find additional TF families upstream of the dual NF-kB and AP-1 mechanism (Figure S3E), supporting their central role in Pam2+ODN-induced antagonistic and synergistic gene expression downstream of histone activation changes (Figure 2E).

cJun and RelA are cooperatively activated after Pam2+ODN treatment

We evaluated the contribution of NF-kB (RelA, RelB, c-Rel, p50/Nfkb1, and p52/Nfkb2) and AP-1 (cJun, JunB, JunD, cFos, FosB, Fra1, and Fra2) subunits by integrating gene expression changes with TF activity predictions. None of the NF-κB subunits were differentially expressed in response to Pam2+ODN, and only JunD showed modest downregulation among AP-1 members (Figure 3A), indicating that pathway engagement occurs largely independent of TF transcriptional induction. Consistently, functional enrichment analysis predicted the activation of RelA/p50 and cJun across all treatments (Figure 3B), with p50 acting as a RelA heterodimerization partner. Other NF-kB (RelB, RelC, p52) and AP-1 subunits (JunB, FosB, cFos, Fra1) showed only treatment-specific activity or negligible (JunD, Fra2) activity prediction scores (Figure 3B). To independently assess TF activation, we performed RPPA profiling of 161 native and post-translationally modified proteins spanning multiple signaling pathways. Phosphorylated cJun (S73) and phosphorylated RelA (S536) emerged as the most strongly induced proteins after 4 h of Pam2+ODN stimulation (Figure 3C), exceeding the phosphorylation changes seen with either Pam2 or ODN treatment alone (Figure 3D).

ChIP-seq analysis further revealed cooperative global chromatin occupancy between cJun and RelA. Shared cJun-RelA (assisted) peaks (Figure S4A) across all treatments exhibited significantly greater assisted occupancy of both TFs when compared with cJun-only or RelA-only (unassisted) peaks (Figure 3E). Notably, RelA occupancy was substantially higher at cJun-assisted shared peaks relative to unassisted RelA-only peaks, whereas cJun occupancy was minimally improved in RelA-assisted shared peaks and remained similar to cJun-only unassisted peaks. These findings indicate a transcriptional hierarchy where cJun facilitates RelA recruitment, while RelA exerts limited influence on cJun recruitment. Consistent with a direct transcriptional role, shared cJun-RelA peaks were more preferentially localized to promoter-centric regions (∼21.5%) than unassisted cJun-only (∼5.3%) or RelA-only (∼12.7%) peaks (Figure S4B), supporting a model in which cJun-RelA co-occupancy directly shapes lung epithelial transcriptional responses across treatment conditions.

RelA chromatin occupancy after Pam2+ODN is highly associated with AP-1 motifs

To assess cooperative chromatin occupancy by cJun and RelA, we used OBIF to differentially analyze their ChIP-seq binding peaks after Pam2, ODN, or Pam2+ODN treatment. Within our ChIP-seq data, the largest subset of cJun peaks (∼38%, 16,487/43,452) was shared by all treatments (Figure S4C), whereas RelA peaks were predominantly treatment-specific (Figure S4D). Given the extensive overlap of cJun peaks among treatments, differential analysis did not reveal significant changes in global cJun chromatin occupancy (Figure S4E). In contrast, RelA chromatin occupancy exhibited extensive treatment-dependent accessibility changes (Figure 4A) with each condition showing a high proportion of uniquely increased peaks (Figure 4B), indicating distinct RelA chromatin occupancy programs induced by each treatment. Particularly, Pam2+ODN induced increases in RelA peaks that were predominantly synergistic, while decreases in RelA peaks were mostly antagonistic (Figure 4C). To examine whether treatment-specific RelA chromatin occupancy reflects cooperation with other TFs, we performed motif enrichment analysis of RelA peaks. RelA peaks induced by Pam2 or ODN alone were associated with a broad range of TF families and superfamilies, whereas Pam2+ODN-induced RelA peaks were largely restricted to the basic leucine zipper (bZIP) superfamily, predominantly AP-1 motifs (Figure 4D).

RelA peaks increased by Pam2 or ODN alone did not show significant enrichment for motifs from other TF families, suggesting that these gains in RelA occupancy likely occur independently of stable TF recruitment. In contrast, RelA peaks that were decreased following Pam2 or ODN alone remained broadly distributed across multiple TF families and superfamilies, indicating selective loss of RelA occupancy from pre-existing regulatory regions with other TFs. Strikingly, all Pam2+ODN RelA peak subsets exhibited strong specificity for AP-1 bZIP motifs (Figure 4D). To assess a direct interaction between RelA and cJun, we performed reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation of RelA and cJun in HBEC3-KT cell lysates after 4 h treatment with PBS or Pam2+ODN. In both directions, we observed co-precipitation (Figure 4E), demonstrating the mutual association of RelA and cJun. Consistent with our results of increased cJun-assisted RelA global occupancy (Figure 3E), we additionally observed increased immunoblotting of RelA after the immunoprecipitation of cJun after Pam2+ODN treatment (Figure 4E). These results support a TF assistance model where Pam2+ODN redirects RelA chromatin occupancy toward AP-1–associated regulatory elements without extensive cJun remodeling.

cJun and RelA occupancy regulate differential gene expression with Pam2+ODN

To define a mechanistic framework linking cJun and RelA chromatin occupancy to transcriptional responses in lung epithelial cells following Pam2+ODN stimulation, we integrated DEGs from RNA-seq with cJun and RelA peaks identified by ChIP-seq using a peak-gene association model (Figure 5A, top). The modulation of DEGs across immunostimulatory treatments showed a strong association with RelA and cJun peaks. Similar proportions of cJun-only, RelA-only, and shared cJun-RelA peaks were found across Pam2-, ODN-, and Pam2+ODN-responsive DEGs (Figure 5A), consistent with the cooperative nature (Figures 2B and 2C) and balanced contribution (Figures S3A–S3B) of Pam2 and ODN to the combined Pam2+ODN transcriptional response. Stratifying Pam2+ODN DEGs by expression direction (unresponsive, decreased, or increased) and interaction mode (additive, antagonistic, or synergistic) revealed that responsive genes exhibited significantly greater association with cJun-only, RelA-only, or shared cJun-RelA peaks than unresponsive genes. Notably, Pam2+ODN DEGs displaying synergistic expression (Figure S5A) showed the greatest association with shared cJun-RelA peaks than any other DEG subset (Figure 5A, bottom). To confirm that RelA and cJun chromatin occupancy—rather than peak-to-gene association alone—shapes Pam2+ODN-induced transcriptional gene regulation, we directly evaluated TF occupancy at their respective ChIP-defined binding regions.

The evaluation of RelA chromatin occupancy revealed significantly higher RelA occupancy at peaks associated with Pam2+ODN-responsive genes compared with unresponsive genes (Figure 5B). Among responsive genes, increased DEGs exhibited higher RelA occupancy than decreased DEGs, and non-additive DEGs (antagonistic and synergistic) showed greater RelA occupancy than additive DEGs. In comparison, cJun occupancy also displayed a higher enrichment across responsive versus unresponsive DEGs (Figure 5C), with minimal differences between increased and decreased DEGs. Notably, only synergistic DEGs exhibited the highest levels of cJun occupancy, distinguishing them from additive and antagonistic gene subsets. Together, these patterns indicate that cJun and RelA chromatin occupancy are broadly enriched at peaks associated with Pam2+ODN-responsive genes, with heightened cJun occupancy distinguishing synergistic from additive and antagonistic expression interactions, and elevated RelA occupancy associated with increased expression directionality.

In addition to peak-centric occupancy (predominantly distal), we independently evaluated promoter-centric (predominantly proximal) chromatin occupancy of RelA and cJun around transcription start sites (TSSs) of Pam2+ODN-responsive genes. Consistent with peak-centric analysis, Pam2+ODN-responsive genes exhibited increased cJun and RelA occupancy and stronger co-occupancy correlation (PCC >0.5, red) at their TSS than unresponsive genes, with synergistic DEGs having the highest levels of cJun-RelA occupancy and co-occupancy correlation (Figure 5D). Stratified analysis of Pam2+ODN DEGs revealed that only synergistic genes had significantly higher RelA and cJun occupancy at promoter regions, whereas other DEG subsets (increased, decreased, additive, and antagonistic) displayed TF occupancy comparable to the broader set of responsive genes (Figure 5E). Together, peak-centric and promoter-centric analyses reveal cooperative regulatory roles for RelA and cJun that distinguish Pam2+ODN-responsive genes from unresponsive genes.

cJun-assisted loading of RelA drives synergistic antiviral and pro-survival programs

To link these regulatory mechanisms to antiviral (Figures 1A and 1B) and pro-survival (Figures 1C and 1D) outcomes observed after Pam2+ODN treatment, we examined Pam2+ODN-induced gene programs and the functional consequences of NF-kB and AP-1 inhibition. Functional enrichment analysis identified Pam2+ODN-responsive DEGs associated with cell survival and antimicrobial defense (Figure 6A) among the most significantly enriched functions. These independently curated gene sets served as a reference to evaluate cJun and RelA co-occupancy in Pam2+ODN-mediated inducible resistance to influenza. Consistent with our promoter-centric analyses, antimicrobial defense (Figure 6B, top) and cell survival (Figure S5B, top) Pam2+ODN DEGs exhibited increased RelA and cJun chromatin occupancy at their TSS compared to unresponsive genes, with the highest promoter occupancy observed for both cJun and RelA at DUSP1 (Figure 6B, bottom) and AURKA (Figure S5B, bottom), both representative of synergistically regulated genes (Figure S5A). These data indicate that Pam2+ODN-induced gene programs are facilitated by promoter-level engagement and co-occupancy of both RelA and cJun.

To evaluate if RelA and cJun promoter co-occupancy is a distinctive feature of Pam2+ODN signaling, we quantified promoter-centric co-occupancy of RelA and cJun at Pam2+ODN-responsive genes (Figures 5D and 5E) and gene programs (Figure 6A) using the relative assistance ratio (RAR) of RelA to cJun co-occupancy (RelA:cJun RAR), defined as the ratio of RelA to cJun ChIP-seq tag density for each treatment, normalized to PBS-treated controls (Figure S5C). Consequently, increases (RAR>1) directly reflect enhanced NF-kB engagement relative to AP-1, providing a mechanistically grounded metric of cJun-assisted RelA promoter occupancy. Pam2+ODN treatment produced the highest RelA:cJun RAR values at upstream promoter regions (−500bp to 0 bp relative to TSS) of Pam2+ODN-responsive DEGs compared with Pam2 or ODN alone (Figure 6C, top). Moreover, effect-based synergy quantification demonstrated that observed RelA:cJun RAR for Pam2+ODN (EPam2+ODN) exceeded the expected additive effect derived from Pam2 (EPam2) and ODN (EODN) treatments. This indicates the synergistic enhancement of RelA-cJun co-occupancy at upstream promoter regions uniquely achieved during combined Pam2+ODN stimulation (Figure 6C, bottom). This cooperative occupancy pattern aligns with transcriptional programs associated with host survival and antimicrobial defense (Figure S5C).

To functionally assess the transcriptional hierarchy of NF-κB and AP-1 signaling to Pam2+ODN-mediated antiviral activity, we employed well-characterized pharmacologic inhibitors targeting each pathway. We examined how selective inhibition of AP-1 (using SP600-125) or NF-κB (using IMD-0354) affected Pam2+ODN antiviral efficacy across a broad (10−6–10−1 μM) inhibitor concentration range without compromising cell viability (Figure S5D). The inhibition of AP-1 with SP600-125 required relatively high concentrations (≥10−4 μM) to increase cellular susceptibility to influenza infection and attenuate Pam2+ODN-mediated antiviral protection (Figure 6D, left). In contrast, NF-κB inhibition with IMD-0354 led to a rapid and pronounced loss of Pam2+ODN antiviral activity, with increased susceptibility to infection observed at concentrations as low as 10−6 μM (Figure 6D, right). Notably, AP-1 inhibition did not reduce maximal antiviral protection to the extent observed with NF-κB inhibition, indicating that NF-κB signaling is the primary driver of the Pam2+ODN antiviral response, while AP-1 signaling plays a supportive role. These results served as functional validation of the transcriptional hierarchy—characterized by AP-1 assistance and NF-κB dominance— shown in our global occupancy analyses (Figure 3E) and Pam2+ODN-induced remodeling of RelA occupancy (Figure 4A) without corresponding changes in cJun occupancy (Figure S4E). Finally, immunofluorescence analysis performed 24 h after viral infection (MOI = 1) confirmed robust co-localization and activation of both phosphorylated RelA (S536) and phosphorylated cJun (S73) exclusively in Pam2+ODN-treated cells, coincident with influenza A NS1 expression (Figure 6E). Altogether, these findings link coordinated cJun-RelA activation to pro-survival and antiviral signaling through a defined transcriptional hierarchy and phosphorylation-dependent regulation in infected lung epithelial cells following Pam2+ODN treatment.

Discussion

Once regarded as immunologically inert, the respiratory epithelium is now recognized as a key regulator of immune responses in inflammatory and infectious lung diseases.22,23,24 Lung epithelial cells are sufficient to orchestrate pulmonary innate immune responses from the trachea and large conducting airway to the smaller respiratory airways and alveolar units.23,25,26 Hence, host-directed therapeutics that can harness lung epithelial immune responses against respiratory pathogens are an attractive strategy to address increasing antimicrobial resistance and emerging respiratory pathogens.8,9,10,22 Our current and previous work demonstrate that immunostimulatory exposure of lung epithelial cells to synthetic PRR ligands can treat and prevent infection by a wide array of respiratory pathogens.12,13,14,15,16,17,18,19,21 Pam2+ODN confers pathogen-agnostic protection involving both airway and alveolar cells, as assessed in vivo (mouse lungs), in vitro (human HBEC3-KT bronchial and murine MLE-15 alveolar type II cells), and ex vivo (primary human bronchial epithelial cells, primary murine tracheal epithelial cells, and primary murine alveolar type II cells).12,13,14,15,16,17,18,19,21 In this study, we provide a mechanistic framework that moves beyond pathway involvement to define how transcriptional synergy is implemented in human lung epithelium after Pam2+ODN stimulation.

Consistent with our prior work identifying critical roles of AP-1 and NF-kB in synergistic transcription in mouse and lung epithelium,19,21 this study reveals a cooperative regulatory framework in which RelA and cJun jointly shape Pam2+ODN-induced gene expression in human lung epithelial cells. Pam2+ODN-responsive genes were highly associated with RelA and cJun occupancy at both peak-centric and promoter-centric regulatory regions. Notably, increased co-occupancy of RelA and cJun at promoters distinguished Pam2+ODN-responsive genes from unresponsive loci, highlighting promoter-level cooperation as a defining feature of the synergistic response. We further identified genomic regions with shared cJun and RelA occupancy in lung epithelial cells, representing pre-existing shared peaks. In these sites, cJun facilitates RelA engagement, supporting a TF assistance model in which cJun enhances RelA occupancy rather than acting as an independent transcriptional driver. Pam2+ODN preferentially leverages these shared peaks to activate epithelial immune responses and promote antimicrobial defense. This mechanism aligns with previous observations that cJun can function as a gatekeeper TF, enabling recruitment or stabilization of other TFs at select regulatory elements following specific stimuli.27,28 While RelA occupancy can be remodeled by diverse treatments through association with multiple motif families,29,30,31 our data demonstrate that Pam2+ODN-induced synergistic gene expression occurs selectively at RelA peaks enriched for bZIP motifs, particularly those associated with AP-1 family members.

Our multi-omics analyses across human and murine epithelial systems, as well as mouse lung tissue, consistently demonstrate the activation of both TF families during synergistic Pam2+ODN responses.19 While cooperation between NF-κB and AP-1 has also been described in macrophage responses to Toll-like receptor ligands,29,32,33,34 this transcriptional hierarchy—characterized by AP-1 assistance and NF-κB dominance— has not heretofore been shown in the context of protective pattern recognition receptor signaling nor, to our knowledge, in other models.29,32,33,34 ChIP-seq analysis further revealed that an important determinant of this cooperation is baseline RelA-cJun co-occupancy in lung epithelial cells, which is selectively amplified at promoter-centric regions following Pam2+ODN stimulation. Interestingly, epigenetic remodeling of RelA occupancy at 4 h did not directly mirror transcriptional changes after Pam2+ODN stimulation. However, peak-to-gene annotation and enrichment analysis of such Pam2+ODN-responsive genes associated with cell survival and antimicrobial defense pathways exhibited increased RelA-cJun co-occupancy at promoter regions, suggesting that cooperative TF engagement primes transcriptional responses. In contrast, cJun occupancy did not exhibit widespread differential remodeling, despite its prominent presence at promoters of synergistically regulated effector genes. This likely reflects the broadly active nature of cJun in regulating cell cycle, metabolic, viability, and immune pathways across many contexts.35,36,37 However, differential cJun remodeling with Pam2+ODN remains a possibility at different timepoints not examined in this study.

Together, our findings support a model in which Pam2+ODN rapidly reprograms lung epithelial responses to respiratory pathogens through two coordinated mechanisms: (1) induction of transcriptional changes at genes poised by pre-existing RelA-cJun regulatory architecture and (2) selective remodeling of RelA chromatin accessibility through cJun-assisted engagement. This hierarchical and cooperative regulatory framework distinguishes synergistic from non-synergistic transcriptional responses and links TF co-occupancy at promoters of protective gene programs. Collectively, these mechanisms enable lung epithelial cells to integrate multiple PRR signals and execute synergistic antimicrobial and pro-survival transcription programs, providing mechanistic insight into how epithelial-directed immunostimulation can confer robust host protection against respiratory pathogens.

Limitations of the study

Previous studies show that Pam2+ODN-induced protection in lung epithelial cells involves cooperative mechanisms including reactive oxygen species (ROS), cytokine secretion, and antimicrobial peptide production.14,17,18 Our pharmacologic inhibition experiments were designed to evaluate the contribution of NF-κB and AP-1 to the transcriptional component of Pam2+ODN-mediated protection. The residual antiviral activity observed under NF-κB or AP-1 inhibition is consistent with the presence of additional, transcription-independent mechanisms—such as ROS—that contribute to protection, as demonstrated previously.14,17,18,21 These findings do not contradict the known role of ROS but rather underscore that Pam2+ODN engages multiple cooperative protective pathways. Although mechanistic studies were performed in human bronchial epithelial cells (HBEC3-KT), Pam2+ODN-mediated protection has been consistently demonstrated in human and murine models of both bronchial and alveolar epithelium in vivo, in vitro, and ex vivo,12,14,15,16,17,18,19,21,38 supporting a conserved epithelial protective mechanism. Here, we extend prior work by defining the transcriptional and epigenetic architecture underlying epithelial synergy, identifying an AP-1–assisted, NF-κB-dominant promoter regulatory program that integrates host survival and antimicrobial gene expression.

Experimental model and study participant details

Experiments were performed in immortalized human bronchial epithelial cells (HBEC3-KT) authenticated by the MD Anderson Characterized Cell Line Core Facility. Cell cultures were maintained in filter-sterilized growth medium (GM) of keratinocyte serum-free media supplemented with human recombinant epidermal growth factor and bovine pituitary extract, and 1 percent volume/volume (% v/v) of antibiotic-antimycotic (100X).21

Subculture of cells

Cells were cultured in 100 mm dishes with 10 mL of GM (replenished every 1–2 days) and maintained in a 37°C with 5% CO2 cell incubator until 80–100% confluency. Cell were passaged after a 5–10-min incubation with 1 mL of 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, further neutralized with 4 mL of 2% v/v fetal bovine serum in filter-sterilized phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Detached cells were centrifuged at 250 g for 5 min at room temperature (25 °C) and the resulting cell pellet was reconstituted in 10 mL of GM. Before plating, cell counting was performed using a 200 μL sample in a Scepter 3.0 handheld automated cell counter (60 μm sensors) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded into 100 mm dishes (in 10 mL GM) at a density of 2.5 x 106 cells/plate or 96-well plates (in 200 μL GM) at a density of 25,000 cells/well and maintained for 24 h in GM before beginning experimental procedures.

Infection model

Frozen stocks of influenza A/Hong Kong/1968 H3N2 at 1 x 107 plaque-forming units (PFU)/mL in Eagle’s minimal essential medium were kept at −80°C. Working solutions of virus were made fresh by thawing and diluting frozen stocks in filter-sterilized PBS as needed.19,39 Viral infectious challenges were performed in 96-well plates using the inner 60 wells to avoid edge effects. Cells first received a 4 h exposure of GM containing PBS, Pam2, ODN or Pam2+ODN (in the presence or absence of NF-kB or AP-1 inhibitors) and were subsequently infected with 50 μL of virus working solution at the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI), using the seeding density cell number for MOI calculations.

Method details

Exposure to transcription factor family inhibitors

Stock solutions of SP600-125 (AP-1 inhibitor) or IMD-0354 (NF-kB inhibitor) were reconstituted in dimethyl sulfoxide to a 250 μM concentration, aliquoted and stored at −80°C. Cells were exposed to 100 μL (for 96-well plates) of fresh GM at the indicated molarity concentrations 1 h prior to exposure to Toll-like receptor ligands and remained in the wells throughout the experiment.

Exposure to Toll-like receptor ligands

Stock solutions of S-[2,3-bis(palmitoyloxy)-propyl]-(R)-cysteinyl-(lysyl) 3-lysine (Pam2) or oligodeoxynucleotide M362 (ODN) were reconstituted in endotoxin-free water to a 240 μM concentration and stored at −80°C. Cells were exposed to 10 mL (for 100 mm dishes) or 200 μL (for 96-well plates) of fresh GM with either 1% v/v PBS, 4 μM Pam2 only, 1 μM ODN only, or both 4 μM Pam2 and 1 μM ODN (Pam2+ODN) and were followed up for the indicated timepoints per experiment.19 For loss of function studies in 96-well plates, cells first received 100 μL of fresh GM with the desired inhibitor concentrations for 1 h, and then the 200 μL volume of treatments was adjusted to 100 μl to maintain the constant molar concentration for 200 μL per treatment as described above.

Immunofluorescent staining

All the following steps were performed using fresh filtered sterile PBS or deionized water and performed at room temperature unless otherwise indicated. At the specified time points, cells were fixed for 25 min with 100 μL/well of 2% paraformaldehyde in PBS, washed 3 times with 200 μL/well of PBS, permeabilized with 100 μL of 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 25 min, washed 3 times with 200 μL/well of PBS, and blocked with 50 μL of 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS (blocking solution) for 25 min Staining solution was prepared from 5 mL of blocking solution and 25 μL (1:200 dilution) of fluorescently-labeled antibodies. For viral burden, the plates were stained with anti-influenza A virus M2 Alexa Fluor 546 antibody. For transcription factor studies, the plates were stained with anti-p-RelA (S536) Alexa Fluor 647, anti-p-cJun (S73) PE, and anti-influenza A virus NS1 Alexa Fluor 488 antibodies. After blocking, cells were stained by adding 50 μL/well of staining solution and incubated overnight at 4°C in a sealed plate protected from light. Stained cells were washed 3 times with 200 μL/well of PBS and stained for 10 min with 100 μL/well of a nuclear staining solution made of 5 μL of DAPI (1 mg/mL) in 10 mL of PBS per 60 wells. Lastly, cells were washed 3 times with 200 μL/well of PBS and kept in 100 μL/well of PBS protected from light for immediate imaging.

Cell viability and viral cytotoxicity

Cell viability was assessed using crystal violet following standardized Cold Spring Harbor Protocols.40,41 The neutral red uptake assay was used to evaluate viral cytotoxicity and treatment effectiveness against influenza A virus.40,41 At the specified time points, cells were stained with 100 μL/well of neutral red (40 μg/mL) in GM, and incubated for 2 h in the cell culture incubator (37°C and 5% CO2). After 2 h incubation, cells underwent visual inspection and brightfield cell imaging (described below). After imaging, neutral red medium was removed, and cells were washed once with 150 μL/well of PBS. Then, the wash solution was removed by gentle tapping of the inverted plate. A total of 150 μL/well of neutral red destain solution (50% v/v ethanol 96%, 49% v/v deionized water, and 1% v/v glacial acetic) was added and incubated under gentle orbital rotation for 10 min or until all neutral red was extracted as a homogeneous solution. Finally, absorbance of neutral red was measured at OD450 in a plate reader spectrophotometer.

Cell imaging

All cell imaging was performed using a Keyence BZX-810 automated fluorescence microscope with the Image Cytometer function using either a PlanApo 10x or 20x dry objective lens and equipped with the following filter sets: DAPI for nuclear staining, GFP for Alexa Fluor 488, TRITC for PE and Alexa Fluor 546, Cyanine5 for Alexa Fluor 647, or none for brightfield images.

Fluorescent cell imaging was performed with individual autofocus per field of view using the DAPI channel. Fluorescence intensities of influenza A virus were measured using BZX Image Cytometer analysis software (Keyence). Visual inspection of single fields of view for at least 5 random wells was used to standardize signal threshold levels for automated batch analysis of all fields of view per well in a plate. Total fluorescent intensity (sum of fluorescence intensity per pixel) of each field of view was measured for influenza A M2 to determine the total expression from 5 independent and non-overlapping fields of view per well. Representative images were pseudo-colored as indicated and overlaid.

Brightfield cell imaging was performed in a channel without filters (25 ms exposure) with individual autofocus per field of view before development of the neutral red uptake assay with methanol solution. Five independent and non-overlapping fields of view per well were taken and used for representation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

RNA-seq analysis

RNA extraction and library preparation were performed by Active Motif end-to-end services from frozen pellets of HBEC3-KT cells. Significant change of feature expression of gene transcripts between conditions in the RNA-seq experiment were analyzed by our Omics-Based Interaction Framework (OBIF) (limma-based) after reads alignment to the UCSC hg38 reference and trimming using the BasePair platform.19 Five replicates were analyzed for each treatment. Adjusted p-value (q-value) ≤ 0.20 was used for differential expression evaluation. RPKM data were imported into R for further analyses. All computational analyses were performed on non-zero log2 (RPKM + 1) quantile-normalized values per OBIF’s standards.19 Clustering by Expression Profiles was applied per gene based on expression directionality per exposure condition. Quantification of non-additive expression (synergistic or antagonistic) was performed by OBIF, including statistical evaluation of an interaction effect p-value <0.05. Quality control analysis and plots of p-value and false discovery rate (FDR) selection were done using the Beta Uniform Mixture (BUM) modeling functions incorporated in OBIF.

Transcription factor ChIP-seq analysis

For ChIP-seq, DNA extraction and library preparation were performed by Active Motif from frozen pellets of HBEC3-KT cells. Significant change of RNA expression between conditions in the RNA-seq experiment were analyzed by OBIF (limma-based) after reads alignment to the UCSC hg38 reference and trimming using the BasePair platform. Sequencing reads (150-mer) were generated for ChIP-seq experiments on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform. Tags were uniquely aligned to the human reference genome (hg38). Regions of enriched tags, also known as peaks, were used to define regions of DNA-binding that represent transcription factor binding sites (TFBS). The tags from each replicate were combined, and peaks form the pooled tags were determined using HOMER.42,43 Significant change of accessible sites between conditions in the ChIP-seq experiment were analyzed by OBIF (limma-based) after reads alignment to the UCSC hg38 reference and trimming using the BasePair platform. 5 replicates were used for each treatment. Adjusted p-value (q-value) ≤ 0.20 were used for differential expression evaluation. RPKM data were imported into R for further analyses. All computational analyses were performed on non-zero log2 (RPKM + 1) quantile normalized-values per OBIF’s standards.19 Clustering by Expression Profiles was applied per feature based on expression directionality per exposure condition. Quantification of non-additive expression (synergistic or antagonistic) was performed per OBIF’s standards on features whose interaction p-value <0.05. Quality control analysis and plots of p-value and false discovery rate (FDR) selection were done using the Beta Uniform Mixture (BUM) modeling functions incorporated in OBIF.

Reverse phase protein array analysis

We evaluated the expression of 161 regulatory proteins and phospho-proteins in our reverse phase protein array (RPPA) of HBEC3-KT cells as described before.19 Quality control analysis and plots of p-value and false discovery rate (FDR) selection were done using the Beta Uniform Mixture (BUM) modeling functions incorporated in OBIF.

Gene enrichment and motif analysis

Genes and TFBS-associated genes were analyzed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) (Ingenuity Systems, www.ingenuity.com) by our certified IPA analyst (JPG).44 Motif analysis of peaks was performed using HOMER and significant motifs were selected with an FDR <10%.45

ChIP-seq tag density profiles

We constructed tag density profiles of the ChIP-seq data by extending each mapped read to the 150 bp length into the 3′ direction relative to the strand and counting the distribution of tag counts over the genome. Tag normalization was done using HOMER with a default scale factor of 10 million/the total number of tags to obtain the normalized tag density profiles.45,46,47

ChIP-seq boxplots, aggregation plots, and heatmaps

Boxplots represent sequenced tag density (per bp) around (±250 bp) the center of binding sites or as detailed in the legend. The median is denoted by a horizontal line, and the mean is denoted by a ‘+’ sign. Density plots represent average sequenced tag density (10 bp bins) around (±500 bp or as detailed in the legend) the center of binding sites.46,47,48 Heatmaps show the correlation of ChIP tag densities between RelA and cJun ChIP-seq centered on specific sites as detailed in the legends. All plots were generated using HOMER and visualized in R or Prism.

Statistical analysis

Asterisks denote statistical significance as determined by an unpaired, two-tailed t-test unless otherwise noted. All graphs showing errors are means with SEM unless otherwise noted. All RNA-seq and ChIP-seq p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using their q-values.
