Endovascular treatment for symptomatic vasospasm following traumatic brain injury: illustrative case
Authors: Risa Kubota, Shigeki Nakano, Masaaki Kubota, Yosuke Tajima, Kyoko Aoyagi, Kentaro Horiguchi, Yoshinori Higuchi

ABSTRACT

The standard treatment for cerebral vasospasm (CV) following traumatic brain injury (TBI) has not been established. Consequently, the treatment protocol generally follows that for CV after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). Herein, we present a case of symptomatic CV following TBI that was successfully managed with endovascular treatments, including intra-arterial infusion of fasudil and balloon angioplasty. Furthermore, we review the recent literature on the treatment for CV following traumatic SAH.

Illustrative Case

A man in his 20s was found collapsed and covered with multiple contusions 2 days after anyone had had communication with him. The patient was transferred to the emergency department of our hospital. On arrival, he presented with disorientation and retrograde amnesia, and his Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was E3V4M6. Head CT revealed SAH, a right parietal bone fracture, cerebral contusions, and a small acute subdural hematoma (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced CT did not reveal any vascular abnormalities. After admission, the patient’s disorientation persisted and was accompanied by confused speech, self-directed conversation, and frequent tongue-clicking movements. On day 6 of hospitalization, his drowsiness and disorientation worsened, although his GCS score remained unchanged at E3V4M6, and no obvious motor deficits were observed. Contrast-enhanced CT was performed for further evaluation, revealing narrowing from the left internal carotid artery (ICA) to the middle cerebral artery (MCA), accompanied by reduced blood flow in the left MCA territory (Fig. 2). Based on these findings, emergency treatment for CV was performed on the same day. Under local anesthesia, fasudil hydrochloride was administered via intra-arterial infusion from the proximal M1 segment (Fig. 3A), resulting in dilatation of the MCA (Fig. 3B). However, repeat cerebral angiography performed the next day revealed recurrence of MCA stenosis. Percutaneous transluminal balloon angioplasty was performed for MCA stenosis, and the procedure was completed after confirming sufficient arterial dilatation on final angiography (Fig. 3E and F). After angioplasty, systemic fasudil hydrochloride and ozagrel sodium were administered intravenously. On day 13 of hospitalization, contrast-enhanced CT revealed improvement in vasospasm and restoration of blood flow in the left MCA territory (Fig. 4A). Although diffusion-weighted MRI revealed a few infarcts in the right frontal lobe due to the endovascular procedure (Fig. 4B), no associated neurological symptoms were evident. His level of consciousness gradually improved, and no extensive cerebral infarction was observed on MRI (Fig. 4C). On day 47 of hospitalization, the patient was transferred to a rehabilitation facility. He resumed normal daily activities after discharge. Discussion

Observations

In the present case, no focal neurological deficits such as motor weakness or aphasia were observed, and disorientation was the only significant symptom, encumbering differentiation from symptoms of cerebral contusion. CV secondary to head trauma is detected more frequently radiologically than aSAH; 1 however, determining the optimal timing of therapeutic intervention is challenging. In this case, in addition to narrowing of the major cerebral arteries, reduced perfusion was observed on CT perfusion imaging. These findings were suggestive of ischemia due to vasospasm and indicated a high risk of cerebral infarction, thereby necessitating therapeutic intervention. Intra-arterial infusion therapy was administered as the initial, less invasive approach; however, the vasodilatory effect was only temporary. Therefore, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) was performed on the following day, which was completed without complications. The patient successfully returned to normal activities without any residual deficits.

Traumatic CV (TCV) is a critical condition that can lead to delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) and subsequent poor outcomes. However, no established guidelines exist for its prevention or management, and the current practice is largely based on strategies for aneurysmal vasospasm. The pathophysiology of TCV differs from that of aneurysmal vasospasm. In addition to the presence of subarachnoid blood, traumatic vasospasm is thought to involve mechanical stretching of cerebral arteries, resulting in vascular smooth muscle hypercontractility, increased sensitivity to endothelin-1, and trauma-induced structural alterations of the vessel wall. 1 These mechanisms may lead to a more fixed or refractory vasoconstriction, particularly in proximal major cerebral arteries. The reported risk factors for TCV include the severity of TBI on admission (GCS score < 9), large amounts of SAH, younger age, and the presence of multiple concomitant injuries, such as subdural hematoma, intraventricular hemorrhage, or cerebral contusion. 1 Taneda et al. reported that among 130 patients with head trauma associated with traumatic SAH, DCI occurred in 7.7% of patients between days 4 and 16 postinjury, with the amount of SAH being a significant risk factor. 2 In contrast, a study of more than 14,000 patients demonstrated that neurosurgical intervention was required in only 0.0017% of cases of isolated traumatic SAH and mild TBI. 3 4 Thus, although traumatic SAH is a risk factor for TCV, risk assessment should not rely solely on its presence.

The reported frequency of TCV varies depending on the imaging modality. Detection rates range from 27% to 63% with transcranial Doppler ultrasonography (TCD) and from 5% to 18% with digital subtraction angiography (DSA). 5 6 Although TCD may overestimate vasospasm, its noninvasiveness and portability at the bedside constitute advantages. In contrast, DSA is more invasive and unsuitable for bedside use. However, DSA facilitates detailed assessment and immediate therapeutic intervention. In addition, contrast-enhanced CT and MRA, such as CT perfusion or arterial spin labeling, provide simultaneous evaluation of perfusion imaging, enabling assessment of ischemia. Even if radiological evidence of TCV is apparent, the incidence of symptomatic cases leading to neurological deficits or cerebral infarction has been reported to be relatively low (4%–17%) compared with aneurysmal vasospasm. 1 7

Only 50% of patients with mild to moderate TBI and untreated TCV achieved a favorable recovery. 2 8 Furthermore, regarding the prognostic impact of the location of the vasospasm, an investigation of 85 patients with head trauma revealed that the presence of vasospasm with reduced cerebral blood flow in the MCA or basilar artery was correlated with poor outcomes at 6 months, as measured using the Glasgow Outcome Scale. 9 Symptomatic vasospasm occurring in the proximal segments of the major cerebral arteries perfusing large brain areas, or the basilar artery, which perfuses the brainstem, may be associated with poor outcomes due to extensive cerebral infarction.

These characteristics of traumatic vasospasm complicate the determination of the optimal timing for therapeutic intervention. Specifically, given that traumatic vasospasm frequently includes asymptomatic or transient cases, performing invasive interventions solely based on radiological evidence of arterial narrowing may be excessive. However, delayed intervention carries the risk of missing the therapeutic window, potentially resulting in devastating infarction and poor prognosis. In this context, perfusion imaging provides a valuable tool, as it enables quantitative visualization of cerebral hypoperfusion. This modality may help distinguish cases of traumatic vasospasm that can be managed with observation from those that require timely therapeutic intervention. In patients with traumatic vasospasm following head injury, additional intervention should be considered when neurological symptoms persist or worsen and perfusion imaging demonstrates hypoperfusion in the territory of major cerebral arteries or diffuse cerebral hypoperfusion.

Given these pathophysiological differences, pharmacological vasodilator therapies may provide only transient or insufficient effects in traumatic vasospasm, particularly when fixed vasoconstriction involves proximal major cerebral arteries. In such cases, mechanical dilatation with PTA may be required to achieve sustained improvement in cerebral blood flow.

Considering the risk of vascular injury, PTA is generally reserved for such situations. 1 In patients with severe TBI, it is essential to confirm that the target vessel and adjacent regions are free from injuries that could increase procedural risk before implementing intra-arterial therapy or PTA.

Intravenous therapy for CV most commonly involves calcium channel blockers, although the reported dosage and duration of administration vary across studies. 20 In Japan, intravenous administration of ozagrel sodium is occasionally used for vasospasm following aSAH. In the present case, ozagrel sodium was administered intravenously at 80 mg/day for treating symptomatic vasospasm. Compared with endovascular treatment, intravenous administration is less invasive and may be a rational approach when vasospasm involves multiple or diffuse vascular territories.

Lessons

After a moderate TBI, evaluating the presence of CV is essential, even in the absence of obvious focal neurological deficits. In the event of fluctuations in the level of consciousness, neuropsychological symptoms, or suspicion of CV based on minimally invasive monitoring, assessing ischemia using perfusion imaging is recommended. If a high risk of severe vasospasm accompanied by perfusion reduction is identified, prompt therapeutic intervention, starting with the least invasive treatment, is recommended to improve clinical outcomes.
