CATVariant: a web server for integrated protein variant interpretation across sequence, structure, population, and clinical evidence
Authors: Khoa Ngo, Hajar Amini, Igor Vorobyov, Colleen E Clancy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficient interpretation of the structural, functional, and clinical impact of protein variants remains a longstanding challenge. This difficulty arises because evidence needed to interpret variant effects is distributed across sequence, structure, population, clinical, experimental, and literature resources. CATVariant (https://catvariant.khoa.ngo) is an open-access web server that addresses this gap by integrating heterogeneous evidence sources within a unified framework for protein variant interpretation. Given a protein name, protein structure, or list of variants, CATVariant retrieves, harmonizes, and analyzes residue-level information across multiple literature and data resources. The server generates an integrated evidence map linking sequence and structural views to variant tables, structural analyses, experimental measurements, population and disease context, literature associations, and evidence summaries. CATVariant supports rapid transition from a protein-wide overview to evidence-linked evaluation of specific variants within a unified evidence framework. Analyses of 6388 ClinVar-derived review-set variants across 22 proteins illustrate how integrated evidence supports variant prioritization. Across random pathogenic–benign variant pairs, pathogenic variants rank higher in 76% of comparisons. Analysis of variants in the hERG protein further shows how multi-scale evidence highlights vulnerable regions and generates testable hypotheses of channel dysfunction. Together, these analyses demonstrate rapid, transparent, and scalable prioritization of protein variants for mechanistic and experimental follow-up.

Introduction

The Human Genome Project and modern sequencing technologies have revealed extensive protein-sequence variation [1, 2], yet the functional consequences, particularly of single amino-acid substitutions, remain poorly understood [3–5]. A key question is whether a variant perturbs protein behavior and, if so, by what mechanism. A single amino-acid substitution can disrupt protein folding, stability, trafficking, binding, catalysis, gating, or allosteric coupling. The consequences of a protein variant depend on structural context, regional intolerance to variation, nearby pathogenic variants, and supporting experimental or clinical evidence. Together, these factors determine whether a variant is biologically informative, clinically relevant, or worthy of experimental investigation.

Currently, the evidence needed to interpret protein variants is dispersed across a wide range of specialized databases and analytical tools, including UniProt for curated protein features and functional annotation [6], ClinVar for clinical classifications and assertion history [7], and gnomAD [8] for allele frequency, ancestry distribution, and genetic constraint, meaning how depleted a gene or region is for variation in large population datasets [9]. Structural resources like the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [10] and AlphaFoldDB [11] provide experimental or predicted protein models. Computational variant-effect predictors, such as PolyPhen-2 [12], SIFT [13], REVEL [14], AlphaMissense[15], CADD [16], and phyloP [17], can further inform assessment, but prediction scores represent only one class of evidence and must be considered together with structural context in the protein, population-based frequency and constraint data, clinical variant classifications, and experimental measurements of variant effects. In practice, variant interpretation still requires repeated manual maneuvering across databases to align residue numbering, inspect structural context, compare nearby clinical and population variants, review functional measurements, and search the literature for supporting evidence. Manually integrating information across these resources is time-consuming, difficult to scale across full proteins or large variant sets, and poorly suited to reveal mutation-sensitive regions within proteins.

To bridge the divide between fragmented structural, population, clinical, and experimental evidences and mechanistic interpretation of protein variants, we developed “CATVariant” (Comprehensive Analysis Toolkit for Protein Variant Analysis; https://catvariant.khoa.ngo), an open-access web server for integrated protein variant analysis.

For each query, CATVariant maps variants onto the protein sequence and available three-dimensional structures, evaluates their relationship to annotated functional regions, examines nearby clinical and population variation, incorporates experimental measurements when available, and presents the results in a unified interactive report. The server performs analyses at the level of individual residues and larger protein regions, including identification of regions with unusually high concentrations of variants, evaluation of the local sequence and structural surroundings of each site, and summarization of the evidence available for each variant. Because many proteins harbor thousands of recorded variants, a central challenge is to identify those that warrant closer investigation because they likely alter protein function or contribute to disease. To address this, CATVariant integrates outputs from up to 15 variant-effect predictors (REVEL [14], AlphaMissense [15], PolyPhen-2 [12], SIFT [13], CADD [16], MutPred [18], ESM1b [19], EVE [20], VARITY [21], MetaLR [22], MetaSVM [22], phastCons [23], SpliceAI [24], Pangolin [25], and phyloP [17]), spanning complementary modeling strategies including conservation and constraint, protein language models, ensemble prediction, deleteriousness scoring, and splicing-based signals, together with structural context and experimental evidence when available, into a transparent variant priority framework. CATVariant presents evidence contributing to each variant’s priority score and highlights variants that warrant closer mechanistic or experimental follow-up. The output includes linked sequence and structural views, searchable variant tables, region-level summaries across the protein, disease and literature panels, and variant-level evidence summaries. By integrating dispersed evidence into a protein-centered view, CATVariant enables identification of mutation-sensitive regions, contextual evaluation of candidate variants, and prioritization of those warranting further investigation.

Methods and description

Input and data processing

CATVariant accepts multiple query formats to support flexible protein analysis. Users submit gene or protein identifiers as text queries, amino acid sequences for sequence-based matching, or user-uploaded protein structures for structural analysis. The server maps submitted queries to the corresponding protein entries and retrieve associated variant information for downstream analysis.

Users optionally provide a list of variants to restrict the analysis to specified positions. If no variant list is provided, CATVariant evaluates all variants mapped to the queried protein. Variant positions are integrated with sequence and structural information when available to support downstream annotation and interpretation.

To ensure secure access to submitted analyses, jobs may be protected using PIN-based access control. Uploaded files and generated results are retained on the server for up to 3 months before automatic removal. Typical analyses complete within ~30 min, depending on query complexity and server load. CATVariant provides online documentation describing the analysis workflow and interpretation of results. For large-scale or automated analyses, the platform supports programmatic access via a RESTful API and batch submission of queries.

Following user input, CATVariant retrieves the corresponding reference sequence, protein names, and organism information, together with curated structural and functional annotations from UniProt [6], including protein domains, transmembrane segments, predicted secondary structures, post-translational modifications, and active or binding sites. Variant records, including amino-acid substitutions and consequence annotations, are collected from UniProt [6], Ensembl [26], and PDBe [27]. Clinical enrichment is added through ClinVar [7], using a two-stage approach that first retrieves gene-level records and then performs targeted variant-level queries for unmatched substitutions. Population data are then layered onto the same variant set using user-selected gnomAD gene-level queries [9], which provide global and ancestry-specific allele frequencies, homozygote and hemizygote counts, and consequence annotations as descriptive population context (Supplementary Method S1). Gene-level and regional missense-constraint metrics are also retrieved and computed, enabling each protein to be viewed in the context of tolerance or intolerance to amino-acid-changing variation (Supplementary Method S2). When available, CATVariant integrates large-scale experimental studies that test the effects of different variants in the protein from MaveDB [28]. This allows experimentally measured effects, including trafficking and functional readouts, to be examined alongside structural and population evidence in the same interface. Literature support is incorporated through PubMed-linked dbSNP records [29] and LitVar [30], and gene-level disease and pharmacogenomic context are assembled from Open Targets [31], ClinGen [32], and PharmGKB [33]. Open Targets provides broad evidence linking genes to diseases across multiple public data sources [31]. ClinGen provides curated gene–disease validity, inheritance, and dosage-sensitivity context [32]. PharmGKB provides pharmacogenomic context relevant to drug response and drug-associated phenotypes [33]. For interpretation support, CATVariant also provides access to external clinical-interpretation resources in a separate panel distinct from the main priority score: AutoPVS1 [34] helps evaluate predicted loss-of-function variants, including cases in which transcript context or nonsense-mediated decay may affect the expected consequence, and InterVar [35] provides American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP)-based clinical interpretation support using standardized evidence criteria. CATVariant integrates computational variant-effect evidence by combining scores retrieved from external resources [6] with scores produced locally by the server. This workflow is designed to replace what would otherwise require repeated manual querying, identifier reconciliation, residue remapping, and cross-comparison across multiple separate databases and viewers.

Structure retrieval, selection, and mapping

CATVariant selects a single primary model for structural analysis in the following order depending on availability: user-uploaded structure, highest-coverage experimental model from the PDB [10], and then AlphaFold prediction [11, 36]. Canonical positions are mapped onto PDB residue numbering, and residue atomic coordinates are extracted for downstream analyses. CATVariant uses structural context in two ways (Supplementary Methods S3 and S4). First, hotspot analysis tests whether residues with elevated variant burden cluster together in the three-dimensional protein structure, revealing vulnerable regions that may not be obvious from the linear sequence alone. Second, residue interaction-network analysis identifies positions that may help transmit an effect from one part of the protein to another. These analyses strengthen interpretation by helping users connect variant location to possible mechanisms by which a variant modulates protein function.

Variant evidence integration and reporting

CATVariant organizes the assembled evidence into an interpretable, customizable reporting framework designed for rapid variant prioritization. After harmonizing the retrieved annotations onto a common residue-level representation, the server summarizes available evidence into an inspectable variant priority score and reports the contributing evidence classes for each variant (Supplementary Methods S5 and S6). The priority score can be tuned by the user to emphasize different evidence types according to the intended follow-up question. In the current default configuration, the score is driven primarily by computational variant-effect predictors and predictor-consensus terms, rather than by clinical labels or population annotations directly. This design reflects a practical limitation in variant interpretation: for the vast majority of variants, high-confidence clinical classifications and direct experimental measurements are not available, while population observations are often contextual and should not be treated as stand-alone evidence. CATVariant therefore uses established computational predictors not as definitive evidence, but as broadly available signals that can still help inform follow-up when stronger evidence is absent. These predictors capture complementary signal types derived from different modeling strategies, including evolutionary conservation and constraint, protein language model effects, splice disruption, supervised ensemble prediction, and broader deleteriousness features. The added value lies in combining this customizable priority score with structural context, population context, experimental measurements, disease and literature context, and separate clinical-interpretation resources within a shared protein-centered view. The priority score is intended to guide triage and subsequent review; it neither functions as a stand-alone clinical classifier nor as a replacement for underlying clinical or experimental evidence.

Results and web server features

Figure 1 summarizes the CATVariant workflow and the principal analytical layers available through the web server. For each query, CATVariant generates an interactive and bookmarkable results page that integrates synchronized sequence and structure visualizations with searchable variant tables, population summaries, disease and literature context, hotspot detection, residue interaction network analyses, and downloadable tabular and graphical outputs. The interface enables rapid navigation from protein-level context to detailed inspection of individual residues and variants within a single analytical environment. Typical analyses finish in <30 min for a single protein query. By integrating information from multiple data sources and analytical modules, CATVariant consolidates tasks that would otherwise require manual retrieval, separate analyses, and cross-comparison across multiple resources.

Overview of the CATVariant workflow and analytical framework. Users initiate an analysis by submitting a gene, protein identifier, or amino acid sequence, with the option to upload a protein structure. Analyses may include all variants associated with the queried protein or a user-provided list of specific variants. CATVariant automatically retrieves relevant information from genetic variant databases, literature and clinical records, experimental assay datasets, protein structure repositories, population genetics resources, and disease and pharmacological knowledge bases. The collected evidence is integrated into a multi-scale analytical framework spanning sequence annotation, population genetics, literature-derived evidence, three-dimensional structural mapping, molecular interaction networks, and variant-impact prediction. These analyses support functional consequence annotation, conservation and constraint profiling, demographic stratification, descriptive population profiling, three-dimensional variant mapping, identification of variant-enriched structural regions, local residue-environment characterization, interaction-network construction, pathway tracing among connected residues, and variant priority scoring. User-submitted variants are evaluated within the same integrated framework as previously reported variants, and results are presented in a unified analytical report.

Variant catalog and evidence-guided variant prioritization

Many proteins now have thousands of cataloged protein-altering variants, making “variant prioritization“ an important step for identifying variants that may affect protein function or disease relevance. Figure 4a shows the interactive variant catalog, which organizes variants together with key supporting evidence, including variant consequence, allele frequency for context, clinical records with source reliability, experimental measurements when available, and a customizable priority score summarizing multiple evidence sources. The table can be filtered by evidence category, enabling users to focus on variants meeting specific criteria without manually querying multiple resources. In the hERG example, the catalog highlights variants such as G628S, located near the channel selectivity filter. This variant is infrequent in population datasets, has a high follow-up priority score, shows strong trafficking-associated impact, and is linked to pathogenic clinical records reported for Long QT syndrome type 2 [40, 41].

Expanded variant views provide additional details such as subgroup-specific allele frequency and genotype counts. The highlighted examples show hERG (KCNH2) variants including G628S. This score is intended to help users decide which variants warrant deeper mechanistic or experimental investigation and is not presented as a final clinical classification. (c) The report also displays outputs from up to 15 established computational variant-effect predictors. These methods capture complementary signal types, including evolutionary constraint, protein language model effects, ensemble prediction, and splice disruption. This is particularly useful because, for the vast majority of variants, no reliable clinical classification or direct experimental measurement is available, making such predictions important for deciding what to investigate next.

Selecting a variant opens a detailed evidence panel summarizing the information contributing to its follow-up priority score and the additional context shown alongside it. This evidence-linked view distinguishes CATVariant from resources that provide only annotation summaries or a single pathogenicity estimate without supporting context (Supplementary Table S1). Figure 4b and c illustrates this functionality using the hERG variant G628S. The panel displays multiple evidence categories, including placement within functionally important regions, abnormal trafficking/activity experimental measurements, and outputs from up to 15 established computational variant-effect predictors (REVEL [14], AlphaMissense [15], PolyPhen-2 [12], SIFT [13], CADD [16], MutPred [18], ESM1b [19], EVE [20], VARITY [21], MetaLR [22], MetaSVM [22], phastCons [23], SpliceAI [24], Pangolin [25], and phyloP [17]). Because most variants lack high-quality clinical or experimental evidence, these predictors provide useful complementary signals, including evolutionary constraint, protein language model effects, ensemble prediction, and splice disruption. CATVariant integrates these evidence streams to support transparent prioritization of variants for mechanistic or experimental follow-up while presenting clinical records, experimental measurements, and other contextual evidence to provide comprehensive context for user-driven interpretation. Users can also customize the scoring during job submission by adjusting the weights assigned to individual evidence components.

Figure 5 evaluates the CATVariant evidence-guided prioritization framework against widely used stand-alone variant-effect predictors using curated ClinVar-derived review sets [7]. Because ClinVar records vary in review confidence and are not a clinical gold standard, this analysis is presented as a comparative prioritization benchmark rather than a measure of clinical classification performance, and includes only high-confidence classifications while excluding variants of uncertain significance and conflicting records. To ensure comparability across methods, the analysis retained only variants scored by all evaluated predictors. The resulting benchmark dataset comprised 6388 missense variants across 22 clinically relevant proteins. Figure 5a evaluates how well each method separates ClinVar-derived pathogenic/likely pathogenic and benign/likely benign review groups across the benchmark dataset using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Under the default weights (Supplementary Method S6), the CATVariant prioritization score achieved the highest AUROC (0.756, with 95% CI 0.707–0.797), compared with 0.723 (95% CI 0.678–0.772) for REVEL and 0.674 (95% CI 0.626–0.728) for AlphaMissense. These results indicate improved prioritization performance for the evaluated protein set under this comparative benchmark.

(b) Macro-averaged area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) across held-out benchmark proteins, reflecting within-protein enrichment of positive-class variants near the top of the ranked list. (c) Per-protein AUPRC values across the fixed benchmark cohort by protein, illustrating within-protein prioritization performance across diverse targets.

Figure 5b demonstrates performance using the AUPRC, which reflects the enrichment of clinically classified pathogenic variants among those ranked most highly by each method. CATVariant achieved the highest macro-averaged AUPRC (0.764 with 95% CI 0.658–0.852), compared with 0.751 (95% CI 0.646–0.846) for REVEL and 0.708 (95% CI 0.591–0.807) for AlphaMissense. CATVariant also showed consistent performance across individual proteins (Fig. 5c), achieving the highest AUPRC for most proteins in the benchmark. These results are consistent with the design of CATVariant, which prioritizes variants by integrating multiple evidence types within a protein-specific analytical framework rather than relying on a single predictor score.

To evaluate threshold-based performance independent of ranking, we performed leave-one-protein-out validation, in which the decision threshold for classification was determined using variants from all other proteins and then applied to the held-out protein. This approach avoids selecting thresholds using data from the same protein being evaluated. The held-out analysis indicated that the priority score maintains useful ranking performance under this framework. Detailed per-protein AUROC and AUPRC values, AUPRC differences relative to comparator methods, and results from the leave-one-protein-out validation are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

We additionally evaluated prioritization performance on a more stringent ClinVar-backed subset restricted to variants with at least two-star ClinVar review status, indicating concordant classifications from multiple submitters with assertion criteria provided (Supplementary Method S6 and Supplementary Figs S2 and S3). Under this stricter benchmark, CATVariant remained highly competitive with the strongest standalone comparator, achieving AUROC 0.956 (95% CI 0.912–0.989) and AUPRC 0.941 (95% CI 0.874–0.989), compared with REVEL AUROC 0.951 (95% CI 0.899–0.985) and AUPRC 0.943 (95% CI 0.858–0.988), and AlphaMissense AUROC 0.930 (95% CI 0.869–0.974) and AUPRC 0.907 (95% CI 0.809–0.976), although protein-level estimates for sparsely represented genes should be interpreted cautiously.

These results support the use of CATVariant as a variant prioritization framework that integrates multiple evidence types within a unified analysis environment. By combining computational predictions, structural context, population data, experimental measurements, and clinical annotations, the server provides an interpretable view of evidence relevant to variant assessment.

Disease, phenotype, reported context, and supporting literature

The disease, phenotype, reported context, and literature panels connect variant-level prioritization to broader biological and disease/phenotype context. Figure 6a summarizes disease or phenotype terms linked to the queried protein and displays associated variants, supporting publications, matched source databases, and evidence-support summaries for each term. Data from ClinGen [32] is used to provide curated gene–disease validity and inheritance context, which is useful when considering questions such as gene dosage sensitivity and whether loss of function is an established disease mechanism for a gene. Scoring framework by Open Targets [31] is included to provide broader disease-association context by aggregating evidence from genetics, literature, and other public resources. This score summarizes the overall strength of support for a gene–disease association across those sources. In the hERG example, Romano–Ward syndrome is shown as a matched ClinGen condition for KCNH2, with 225 linked variants and 32 supporting publications, whereas additional non-matched disease or phenotype terms from broader resources are optionally shown for context only. Figure 6b shows the literature panel, which provides a searchable view of variant-linked publications over time together with reported source-linked labels and brief summaries of reported findings. Users can query specific variants, review publication counts, and access supporting articles for each entry, enabling rapid inspection of published evidence associated with individual variants or protein regions.

Discussion and conclusion

Until now, the evidence required to evaluate protein-altering variants has been distributed across many resources and has been difficult to reconcile at the protein level. CATVariant addresses this gap by mapping variants onto protein sequence and structure, and by integrating structural, population, clinical, experimental, and literature evidence into a unified residue-level interpretation framework.

CATVariant is designed as an interpretive web server for variant prioritization and research follow-up rather than final clinical classification. Its primary purpose is to help users investigate variants whose impact remains uncertain by organizing diverse evidence streams into a practical framework for deciding what to study next. Until now, variant interpretation required manual collection of identifiers, residue mappings, population frequencies, clinical assertions, assay measurements, structural models, and literature references from multiple resources, followed by non-standardized reconciliation across incompatible formats and numbering schemes. CATVariant consolidates these processes into a single platform for analyses and interactive reporting. This is particularly useful for the large number of variants that lack reliable clinical classification and direct experimental characterization, where the main practical question is not whether a final diagnosis can be assigned immediately, but whether enough evidence exists to justify deeper computational or experimental follow-up. The framework supports identification of regions of mutational vulnerability and hotspots within proteins, evaluation of spatial relationships among implicated residues through residue-network analyses, and prioritization of variants for mechanistic investigation or experimental follow-up. An optional custom-structure pathway further extends CATVariant by allowing user-provided protein models to be analyzed within the same evidentiary context used for publicly available structures.

An analysis of the hERG protein illustrates the application of CATVariant. The platform identifies convergent signals near the pore and selectivity-filter architecture, links these regions to known functional and drug-binding residues, and supports progression from regional patterns to variant-level evidence evaluation. In this context, the server is intended to complement specialized resources including ProtVar [42], MISCAST [43], Missense3D [44], mutfunc [45], and MutationExplorer [46] rather than replace them (see Supplementary Table S1 for a comparison of CATVariant with related web resources).

CATVariant is an open-access web server that links variant catalogs to protein-level interpretation. By combining substantial workflow compression with integrated visual interpretation and transparent evidence-linked prioritization, the platform helps users move from large sets of variants of uncertain significance to evidence-supported, testable hypotheses for mechanistic or experimental follow-up without repeated manual reconciliation across multiple databases, numbering systems, and analytical tools.
