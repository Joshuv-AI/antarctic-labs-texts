Sex differences in efficacy and adverse events following bronchoscopic thermal vapour ablation for severe emphysema: results from a multinational real-world registry
Authors: Franziska C Trudzinski, Konstantina Kontogianni, Arschang Valipour, Theresa Klemm, Stephan Eisenmann, Joachim Ficker, Daniel Franzen, Wolfgang Gesierich, Christian Grah, Susanne Dittrich, Philipp Höger, Peter Hammerl, Martin Hetzel, Teresa Byczkowski, Manuel Feisst, Felix Herth, Kaid Darwiche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBronchoscopic thermal vapour ablation (BTVA) is a treatment option for selected patients with severe emphysema, inducing lung volume reduction through a localised inflammatory response. This study investigated sex-specific differences in outcomes and safety after BTVA.MethodsData from the multicentre BTVA post-market registry (December 2018–July 2024) were retrospectively analysed. Sex-specific outcomes at 3, 6 and 12 months were compared, and the effect of sex on residual volume (RV%) was assessed using an adjusted mixed-effects model.ResultsA total of 231 patients (105 women) were included. In women, significant improvements were observed in forced expiratory volume in 1 s at 3, 6 and 12 months (+60 mL, +70 mL, +20 mL), RV at 6 and 12 months (–0.38 L and –0.50 L), 6-Minute Walk Distance (6-MWD) at 3 and 6 months (+17.5 m and +23.5 m), and a consistent improvement in St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) at 3, 6 and 12 months (–9.3, –6.9, –9.1). In men, significant improvements were noted in RV at 3, 6 and 12 months (–0.32 L, –0.47 L, –0.32 L) and in SGRQ at 3 and 6 months (–4.8 and –3.7), but not at 12 months. An improvement in 6-MWD was not observed in men. Additionally, a significant time-by-sex interaction was observed for RV% (χ²=5.88, p=0.015), indicating distinct RV trajectories between the sexes.ConclusionsThe results indicate that women experienced greater improvements in lung function and SGRQ scores after BTVA, which may be explained by differences in perception and reporting of functional benefits, as well as potential biological factors such as a stronger inflammatory response.

Introduction

Bronchoscopic lung volume reduction using thermal vapour ablation (BTVA) is a minimally invasive treatment option for patients with advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and severe lung hyperinflation. During the procedure, heated water vapour is delivered via a flexible bronchoscope into targeted, overinflated areas of the lung. The thermal effect of the vapour causes localised ablation of lung tissue, triggering a localised inflammatory reaction (LIR).1 This is followed by tissue remodelling with scarring and shrinkage, resulting in a sustained reduction in both tissue and air volume within the treated segments.2 By reducing the most hyperinflated and diseased parts of the lung, adjacent, healthier lung regions are decompressed, improving overall lung mechanics and reducing the workload of the respiratory muscles. These physiological changes can lead to improvements in lung function, exercise capacity and quality of life. The procedure is a bronchoscopic lung volume reduction technique which, in contrast to endobronchial valve implantation that requires intact interlobar fissures, can also be applied in patients with existing collateral ventilation. Therefore, this procedure offers a less invasive alternative to surgical lung volume reduction, particularly for patients who are not suitable candidates for valve therapy. Its efficacy and safety have been evaluated in two prospective studies. In the first study by Snell et al, 44 patients with upper lobe-predominant emphysema were treated with unilateral BTVA, vapour dose of 10 cal/g lung tissue. At 6 months, forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁) improved by 141±26 mL, residual volume (RV) decreased by 406±113 mL and significant improvements were seen in St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ), 6-Minute Walk Distance (6MWD) and mMRC scores. Most adverse events (AEs) were respiratory and occurred within 30 days.1 The Sequential Staged Treatment ofEmphysema with Upper Lobe Predominance (STEP-UP) study was a randomised, controlled open-label trial. The treatment algorithm of the study required BTVA of one segment of an upper lobe during one session, followed by BTVA of two segments of the contralateral lobe during a second session performed 12 weeks later. 70 patients were randomised (46 treatment, 24 control). The treatment group showed a 14.7% relative improvement in FEV₁ and a 9.7-point reduction in SGRQ compared with controls. COPD exacerbation was the most common serious AE.3 Nevertheless, the clinical response to BTVA, as well as the extent of the LIR, remains heterogeneous and is often difficult to predict.4 The distinction between the desired immune response and immune-mediated side effects is challenging, as clinical efficacy is correlated with the extent of the inflammatory response. Patients who experience more pronounced respiratory symptoms within the first 30 days following BTVA tend to show greater efficacy, with COPD exacerbations and pneumonia being the most common adverse effects.5 Males and females differ in their inflammatory responses to both foreign and self-antigens, displaying distinct characteristics in innate and adaptive immune responses. Some of these immunological differences persist throughout life, while others emerge postpuberty and decline with reproductive senescence, suggesting that both genetic and hormonal factors play a role.6 7 As we enter the era of precision medicine, clinical decisions and therapeutic approaches are increasingly guided by an individual’s genetic, biological and demographic factors, including sex.8 Given that the mechanism of action of the BTVA procedure primarily relies on the inflammatory response of the treated individual, it is essential to further explore the influence of sex on treatment outcomes. The multicountry BTVA post-market registry was initiated in 2017 to investigate long-term treatment responses and complications of BTVA in patients with severe upper-lobe predominant emphysema. The current study aimed to examine the role of sex in relation to treatment response and potential AEs following BTVA within this large prospective registry. To analyse the procedure’s effectiveness and safety for both sexes, the data on lung function response, quality of life and complications were separately analysed for men and women at study enrollment, as well as at 3, 6 and 12 months postprocedure.

Methods

Study design

The BTVA post-market registry (Study ID CSP-2410 NCT03318406) is a prospective multicentre, multicountry (Germany, Austria, Switzerland), observational study initiated in 2017 to collect real-world data on patients undergoing BTVA for severe emphysema. The registry was designed to systematically evaluate the safety and effectiveness of BTVA under routine clinical conditions. It aims to enrol up to 300 patients with upper lobe–predominant emphysema treated at sites in the European Union and other selected regions. Patients are followed according to standard of care, with follow-up data collected for up to 5 years after treatment. Collected data include pulmonary function testing, exercise capacity, imaging and health-related quality of life assessed by the SGRQ. All serious and non-serious AEs are recorded throughout the study period to evaluate safety, with serious AEs adjudicated by an independent medical monitor to assess relatedness to the InterVapor device and procedure. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. For the present analysis, follow-up data up to 12 months of patients included between December 2018 and July 2024 were considered.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The registry included patients aged 18 years or older with heterogeneous emphysema, confirmed by CT showing a heterogeneity index of ≥1.2 in at least one target segment. Patients were excluded if they had severely impaired lung function (FEV₁ or diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) <20% predicted), were unable to walk more than 140 m in a 6-Minute Walk Test following optimised medical therapy, or had unstable COPD, defined as more than three COPD-related hospitalisations requiring antibiotics in the past year, any COPD-related hospitalisation within the previous 3 months, or daily use of systemic corticosteroids exceeding 5 mg prednisolone. Further exclusion criteria included significant comorbidities or medications associated with an increased risk of complications following BTVA (such as immune system disorders, clinically relevant immunosuppression, bleeding disorders, unstable cardiovascular conditions, asthma or alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency), recent initiation of morphine derivatives within the past 4 weeks, pregnancy or breastfeeding, severely diseased lower lobes (tissue-to-air ratio <11%), signs of active bacterial infection, the presence of large bullae (occupying more than one-third of a lobe) or paraseptal emphysema distribution in the target lobe, and recent respiratory infections or COPD exacerbations within 6 weeks prior to treatment consideration. For the current analyses, data were collected from patients in the registry who underwent at least one BTVA procedure with data available for at least one follow-up timepoint. The follow-up intervals and data refer specifically to the first BTVA procedure. It should be noted that not all patients had complete follow-up data at every time point. Furthermore, not all parameters were consistently recorded at each follow-up visit. This reflects the real-world clinical practice conditions of the study.

Patient selection and lung volume reduction procedures

Patients eligible for BTVA treatment presented with emphysema, a FEV₁ between 20% and 45% of predicted, a RV greater than 175% of predicted, and a DLCO of at least 20% of predicted. Prior to treatment, a dedicated software (IP3 V.3.4.3 Uptake Medical, San José, USA) was used to identify optimal target segments based on the highest emphysema index, segmental volume and heterogeneity index relative to the untreated ipsilateral lung lobes. The dose was predetermined in an individualised treatment plan for each case, ensuring that the vapour dosing for each individual was calculated using a predefined algorithm. This algorithm is based on the quantification of tissue mass from the baseline CT and targets a vapour dose of 8.5 cal/g of lung tissue. The procedure was performed under deep sedation or general anaesthesia. After positioning the BTVA catheter and occluding the selected segment using an integrated balloon, heated water vapour was delivered over a predefined duration according to the segment-specific vapour dose. Following the procedure, patients were closely monitored to proactively identify symptoms of localised inflammatory response—which may temporarily worsen clinical status—as well as to detect and manage potential complications.9

Follow-up

The following parameters were assessed at baseline and follow-up: Body plethysmography, 6-MWD, SGRQ score.10 Lung function tests, including spirometry, body plethysmography and diffusion capacity measurements, were performed in accordance with current and former guidelines.1113 Reference values from the Global Lung Function Initiative were used.14 15 For the current study, the baseline data and details of the procedure as well as the data from the 3, 6 and 12-month follow-up were analysed.

Adverse events

Only AEs that occurred within the first 6 weeks after the BTVA procedure were included in this analysis. COPD exacerbations were defined as the new onset or worsening of more than one respiratory symptom, including cough, sputum purulence, sputum volume, dyspnoea, wheeze or chest discomfort, lasting at least 3 days and requiring treatment with antibiotics or systemic corticosteroids. Episodes of shorter duration or symptom worsening not requiring antibiotic or systemic corticosteroid therapy were classified as mild exacerbations. The following specific AEs were recorded: COPD exacerbation, mild exacerbation, haemoptysis, pneumonia and pneumothorax. Various other events, including cough, pneumonitis, chest pain, upper respiratory tract infection, nausea/vomiting, were classified under the category Other. AEs, including severe AEs (SAEs), were documented for five patients that were still ongoing at study exit. In the database, the duration for these cases was recorded as ‘NA’, which could bias the analysis. To avoid this, the discharge date will be used as the end point for AE duration in these patients.

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as mean ±SD or as number and percentage. Baseline characteristics of male and female patients were compared using independent sample t-tests for continuous variables and χ² tests for categorical variables. To assess treatment response, the differences in lung function parameters, 6-MWD and SGRQ score between baseline and follow-up visits (at 3, 6 and 12 months) were calculated. These differences were analysed separately for men and women using paired samples t-tests. To assess the independent effect of sex on therapy response, a longitudinal data analysis of RV trajectories over time was performed using a linear mixed-effects model with time modelled as a continuous variable (‘duration’) and a random effect on the patient. This continuous approach was chosen to accommodate variable follow-up times across patients and to maximise the number of included observations. Fixed effects in the model included time, baseline value, sex, age, body mass index, number of procedures and the interaction of time and sex. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Due to the exploratory character of this study, all p values have to be interpreted in a descriptive sense and were not adjusted for multiplicity. No missing values were imputed. All analyses were conducted using R V.4.4.3.

Results

Baseline characteristics of men and women

A total of 231 out of 262 patients were included in the analysis (105 women and 126 men). Details on the BTVA procedure were not available for 13 patients. 18 patients were excluded due to missing follow-up data; thus, only patients with data from at least one of the planned follow-up visits at 3, 6 or 12 months were analysed. Follow-up data were available for 174 patients at 3 months, 179 patients at 6 months and 153 patients at 12 months (see online supplemental figure S1). The mean age at baseline was 64.48±6.96 years, with no significant age difference between women and men. At the time of study enrolment, the absolute values for pulmonary function tests—including (FEV₁ in litres), forced vital capacity (FVC in litres) and total lung capacity (TLC in litres)—were lower in women compared with men. However, when expressed as percentages of predicted values, a non-significant trend towards slightly higher FEV₁ was observed in women (31.78±7.75 vs 29.82±8.38; p=0.066). Additionally, FVC (%) was significantly higher in women (65.75±16.94 vs 60.45±16.28; p=0.017), and TLC was also greater (138.94±20.49 vs 128.78±20.12; p<0.001). RV%, however, was comparable between sexes. Regarding the SGRQ total score, no difference was found between women and men (see table 1).

BTVA procedure

There were no significant sex-related differences in the number of procedures performed, with women receiving 1.70±0.59 and men 1.58±0.64 procedures (p=0.153).

Clinical response at 3, 6 and 12 months

Follow-up data were available for 174 patients at 3 months, 179 patients at 6 months and 153 patients at 12 months. The clinical response at 3, 6 and 12 months was analysed. The difference between the values measured at these time points and the baseline values was compared separately for both sexes. At 3 months, women showed significant improvements in absolute FEV₁ (Δ+0.06±0.14 L; p<0.001), per cent predicted FEV₁ (Δ+2.83%±6.71%; p<0.001), SGRQ score (Δ –9.34±14.67 points; p<0.001) and 6MWD (Δ+17.45±66.44 m; p=0.035). Changes in RV, both in absolute and per cent predicted values, did not reach statistical significance. In men, significant improvements were observed in per cent predicted FEV₁ (Δ+1.30%±5.20%; p=0.019), absolute RV (Δ –0.32±1.28 L; p=0.022), per cent predicted RV (Δ –14.54%±50.27%; p=0.009) and SGRQ score (Δ –4.79±14.23 points; p=0.004). However, changes in absolute FEV₁ values and 6MWD did not reach statistical significance. At 6 months, women continued to show improvements in absolute FEV₁ (Δ+0.07±0.25 L; p=0.012), per cent predicted FEV₁ (Δ+2.57%±8.02%; p=0.007), as well as in absolute RV (Δ –0.38±0.85 L; p<0.001) and per cent predicted RV (Δ –11.73%±48.48%; p=0.040). In addition, a sustained improvement was observed in 6MWD (Δ+12.48±75.13 m; p<0.001) and SGRQ score (Δ –6.91±17.12 points; p=0.001). Men showed significant improvements in absolute RV (Δ –0.47±1.45 L; p=0.004), per cent predicted RV (Δ –22.13%±58.57%; p<0.001) and SGRQ score (Δ –3.68±13.12 points; p=0.017). Changes in other parameters remained non-significant: per cent predicted FEV₁ (Δ+1.02%±5.48%; p=0.085), absolute FEV₁ (Δ+0.03±0.19 L; p=0.199) and 6MWD (Δ+12.48±75.13 m; p=0.160). At 12 months, women maintained significant improvements in absolute FEV₁ (Δ+0.04±0.18 L; p=0.048), per cent predicted FEV₁ (Δ+0.33%±7.47%; p=0.010), absolute RV (Δ –0.50±1.35 L; p=0.003), per cent predicted RV (Δ –19.44%±71.36%; p=0.028) and SGRQ score (Δ –9.07±18.33 points; p<0.001). Changes in 6MWD (Δ+14.59±82.58 m; p=0.173) did not reach statistical significance. In men, only absolute RV (Δ –0.32±1.22 L; p=0.032) and per cent predicted RV (Δ –14.54%±49.81%; p=0.017) showed sustained and significant improvement. These findings indicate that women exhibited persistent improvements in FEV₁, RV and SGRQ scores up to the 12-month follow-up, as well as a statistically significant increase in 6MWD up to 6 months. In contrast, men demonstrated lasting improvements only in RV. While SGRQ scores improved significantly up to 6 months, both the reduction in RV and the gain in quality of life were consistently smaller in magnitude compared with women. Longitudinal data analysis of RV trajectories over time between sexes using linear mixed models

To assess the independent effect of sex on treatment response, a longitudinal data analysis of RV trajectories over time was performed using a linear mixed-effects model with time modelled as a continuous variable (‘duration’). The model was adjusted for time, sex, interaction between time and sex, age, body mass index, baseline RV and number of BTVA procedures. Baseline RV (χ²=129.30, p<0.001) and time (χ²=4.59, p=0.032) were both significantly associated with changes in RV following BTVA. Importantly, a significant interaction between time and sex was observed (χ²=5.88, p=0.015), indicating sex-specific differences in RV trajectories over follow-up. As illustrated in figure 1B, which displays absolute RV values (litres) as box plots at 3, 6 and 12 months, women demonstrated a more pronounced and sustained reduction in RV over time compared with men.

Adverse events

Only AEs occurring within the first 6 weeks after the procedure were included in the analysis. COPD exacerbations and haemoptysis were the most frequent AEs, accounting for 17.75% (41 cases) and 8.66% (20 cases), respectively, in the overall cohort. All other AEs were grouped under ‘Other’ and included dyspnoea (8.37%), cough (2.79%), pneumothorax (1.40%), pneumonitis (2.79%), mild exacerbation (0.93%), chest pain (1.40%), upper respiratory tract infection (0.93%) and nausea/vomiting (0.47%). Women reported nearly twice as many COPD exacerbations within 6 weeks after BTVA, with 25 cases (23.81%) compared with 16 cases (12.70%) in men (p=0.043). No significant sex differences were observed for the other AEs. AE outcomes were as follows: death in 5 patients (4.17%), ongoing at study exit in 1 patient (0.83%), resolved/recovered with sequelae in 6 patients (5.00%) and resolved/recovered without sequelae in 108 patients (90.00%). There were 111 cases with unknown outcomes. No differences in AE outcomes were observed between men and women (see table 3).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate sex-specific differences in treatment response and complications following BTVA within the international prospective BTVA registry. Over a follow-up period of up to 12 months, patients showed a moderate improvement in lung function with an overall acceptable safety profile. In sex-stratified analyses at 3, 6 and 12 months, women exhibited sustained improvements in FEV₁, RV and SGRQ scores throughout the 12-month follow-up, as well as a statistically significant increase in 6MWD up to 6 months. In contrast, men demonstrated lasting improvements only in RV. Although SGRQ scores improved significantly up to 6 months, both the reduction in RV and the gain in quality of life were consistently smaller in magnitude compared with women. Modelling of RV trajectories over time indicated a differential response pattern between the sexes across the entire 1-year follow-up. Additionally, women experienced almost twice as many COPD exacerbations as men within the first 6 weeks post-treatment. Taken together, these findings suggest sex-related differences in physiological response and complication profile following BTVA. Recent studies have reported sex-specific outcomes following ELVR using valves. Woytanowski et al described sex-related differences in post-procedural exacerbation frequency; however, this analysis was limited to a small cohort and has thus far only been published as an abstract.16 In contrast, a large prospective analysis from the German Lung Emphysema Registry by Atug et al did not demonstrate sex-related differences in exacerbation rates. Importantly, similar to the present study, this registry-based analysis evaluated both functional response and health-related quality of life. While functional response did not differ between women and men, women demonstrated a significantly greater improvement in quality of life.17 Both studies investigated ELVR by mechanical lobar occlusion using valves and therefore differ fundamentally from BTVA, which induces a localised inflammatory response. Consequently, the observed differences may reflect distinct underlying mechanisms rather than inconsistent findings. In particular, thermally induced parenchymal inflammation following vapour ablation may interact differently with sex-specific immunological or anatomical factors than lobar occlusion. These mechanistic differences should be considered when interpreting sex-related outcome differences across ELVR modalities. In our analyses, anatomical differences may contribute to the observed sex-related differences. Women have smaller airways18 and lungs, as well as approximately 10%–12% lower TLC due to different thoracic configurations—even with the same body size and weight.19 Although vapour dosing is calculated based on lung tissue mass, smaller airway dimensions in women may result in a higher proportion of vapour reaching distal airways and parenchyma, whereas larger airway lumina in men may lead to relatively less parenchymal exposure. Accordingly, the observed sex-related differences are likely multifactorial, reflecting the interplay of anatomical and immunological factors. It is surprising that in our study, no sex differences were observed for pneumonitis and pneumonia, which are considered classic SAEs related to the procedure. Furthermore, the number of reported AEs appears very low compared with previous studies. This may be due to the nature of the registry study, as well as potential documentation errors or uncertainty among investigators when classifying events as SAEs, given that these AEs can simultaneously represent desired effects of the procedure. As women not only demonstrated a better clinical response but also a higher frequency and severity of serious AEs, and since both the desired effects and AEs are primarily driven by the inflammatory responses of the treated individuals, these results suggest that a stronger immune response in women could also explain the different response rates. Sex plays a critical role in shaping the innate immune system. This influence is driven not only by genetic differences between males and females but also by variations in sex hormones, which affect the environment to which immune cells are exposed. Several genes encoding innate immune molecules are located on the X chromosome.20 Additionally, female hormones, such as oestrogen and progesterone, and male hormones, like testosterone and other androgens, are steroid hormones that regulate a broad range of biological processes,8 including key functions of the innate immune system. Testosterone, the male sex hormone, is generally immunosuppressive, whereas oestrogen, the female sex hormone, tends to enhance immune responses.21 Clinically relevant sex differences in immune responses are well-documented, with females generally exhibiting lower infection rates than males for various bacterial, viral and parasitic infections. Furthermore, the incidence of autoimmune diseases is notably higher in females compared with males. These patterns indicate that females may have a more pronounced immune reaction to both self and non-self molecular signals.6 8 It can be assumed that most of the women treated in the BTVA registry were in the postmenopausal phase. The higher susceptibility to infections in males is observed from birth to adulthood, suggesting that sex chromosomes and non-sex hormones have a major role in sexual dimorphism in innate immunity.20 These findings suggest a true biological effect, with women in the registry likely exhibiting a stronger immune response following treatment, as reflected by the higher number of COPD exacerbations reported within the 6-week post-treatment period. Their simultaneously better clinical outcomes further support that this difference is not due to gender-based reporting bias.

Our findings are consistent with known differences in immune responses between males and females, as observed in vaccination research, for example22—the optimal dosing, timing and management of the BTVA procedure may need to be sex-specific. For example, a higher dose could be considered for males to maximise therapeutic efficacy, while ensuring that AEs are kept at the lowest possible level throughout the treatment course.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, a considerable proportion of patients were lost to follow-up, partly due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which not only affected patient availability for follow-up visits but also introduced potential bias in outcome assessment. Second, data on relevant biomarkers that could have provided additional mechanistic insights into sex-specific immune responses were not available. Third, the management of serious AEs may have varied between individuals, particularly regarding the use of corticosteroids and antibiotics, which could have influenced both the clinical course and outcomes. In addition, for inpatients with ongoing AEs at study exit, the discharge date was used as the endpoint for AE reporting, which may have led to an underestimation of event duration or severity. Finally, differences in the perception and reporting of symptoms and AEs between men and women may have contributed to a reporting bias, potentially affecting the observed rates of AEs and patient-reported outcomes. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

Conclusions

These results show that women achieved significant and sustained improvements in lung function and quality of life measures at each follow-up, while men exhibited smaller, predominantly numerical, non-significant improvements across all time points. Since effects of BTVA are largely immune-mediated, these findings suggest that sex-related differences in immune responses seem to be playing a key role in treatment outcomes. Recognising and addressing these differences—as established in other immune-mediated conditions—could help optimise patient selection, risk assessment and treatment strategies for BTVA, ultimately improving safety and efficacy for both sexes.
