Efficacy of Open‐Ended Urinary Catheters in Preventing Catheter‐Related Bladder Discomfort: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Kohei Kobatake, Keisuke Goto, Tomoya Hatayama, Miki Naito, Kenshiro Takemoto, Shunsuke Miyamoto, Yohei Sekino, Hiroyuki Kitano, Akihiro Goriki, Keisuke Hieda, Nobuyuki Hinata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveCatheter‐related bladder discomfort (CRBD) is a common postoperative complication following urological procedures, causing significant distress and reducing patient satisfaction. Although pharmacological interventions have been investigated, a more definitive approach is warranted. This study evaluated whether an open‐ended Foley catheter (OEFC) reduces the incidence and severity of CRBD compared with a conventional Foley catheter (FC).MaterialsIn this exploratory, parallel‐group, single‐blind, randomized controlled trial conducted at Hiroshima University Hospital, 100 patients undergoing transurethral resection of a bladder tumor or robot‐assisted surgery for renal, renal pelvic, or ureteral malignancies were randomized 1:1 to receive either an OEFC or a standard FC. The primary endpoint was the incidence of CRBD at 6 h postoperatively. Secondary endpoints included the incidence and severity of CRBD and urethral pain, and the frequency of pharmacological intervention at predefined postoperative time points.ResultsNinety‐four patients were included in the full analysis set (47 per group). At 6 h postoperatively, CRBD incidence was significantly lower in the OEFC group than in the FC group (42.6% vs. 68.1%; p = 0.012). The OEFC group consistently demonstrated reduced CRBD incidence and severity compared with the FC group during the first 6 h. Urethral pain scores and analgesic use were comparable between groups. Subgroup analyses indicated that the preventive effect of OEFC was particularly notable in non‐transurethral resection of bladder tumor patients.ConclusionsOEFC placement represents a broadly applicable and effective strategy to reduce CRBD following urological surgery. Catheter tip design appears to play a pivotal role in CRBD prevention.

Introduction

Indwelling urinary catheters are often necessary during various surgical procedures, including urological surgeries. These catheters are typically inserted for a short period of 2–4 days in clinical settings; however, their use is associated with catheter‐related bladder discomfort (CRBD) in approximately 47% to 90% of cases [1, 2]. CRBD—manifesting as an urgent need to void or discomfort in the suprapubic area due to the presence of a urinary catheter 6—is a postoperative complication that commonly occurs immediately following urological surgeries, particularly transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT), as well as after gynecological and general surgical procedures [3, 4, 5].

For some patients, CRBD can be highly distressing, leading to increased postoperative pain, reduced satisfaction with the hospital experience, and potentially a greater workload for healthcare providers [6, 7]. Effective management of CRBD is crucial not only for improving patient outcomes but also for optimizing healthcare resource utilization [8]. Various strategies for the treatment and prevention of CRBD have been proposed; however, nearly all reported interventions with beneficial effects have been pharmacological in nature [9, 10, 11]. Existing options, such as antispasmodic agents and analgesics, often provide suboptimal relief and may cause adverse effects [8]. Furthermore, their use may be limited in patients with specific comorbid conditions.

The indwelling urethral catheter is a commonly used medical device that has undergone very few design modifications since its invention [12]. The Foley catheter (FC), currently the most widely used urinary catheter, has become the universal standard for bladder drainage in both women and men [13]. However, the tapered tip protruding above the balloon of the FC can press against the deflated bladder wall, potentially causing trauma to the bladder mucosa through contact damage with the catheter tip [14].

The open‐ended Foley catheter (OEFC), a variant of the conventional FC, features a shorter distance between the balloon cuff and the tip, as well as a blunt tip design—characteristics that confer several distinct advantages. It has been reported to reduce catheter obstruction after hypospadias repair [15] and to facilitate more efficient manual bladder irrigation for the removal of blood clots compared to the FC [16]. Moreover, the OEFC may minimize mucosal injury to the bladder. In a pilot study using an ovine model, bladders catheterized with the OEFC exhibited significantly less ulceration and inflammation than those catheterized with the conventional FC after 96 h of indwelling [13]. Although there are no clinical studies to support the animal research findings, the use of the OEFC possibly represents a simple, fundamental, and side effect‐free strategy for the prevention of CRBD. This study aimed to evaluate whether the OEFC reduces the incidence of CRBD compared with the conventional FC in patients undergoing urological surgery.

Methods

Study Design

This exploratory, parallel‐group, single‐blind (patient‐blinded) randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted in the Department of Urology at Hiroshima University Hospital to evaluate the OEFC against the standard FC. The trial was conducted in the Department of Urology at Hiroshima University Hospital. The study was approved by the institutional medical ethics review committee and registered in the Japanese Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT; registration number: jRCT1062240037).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Between July 24, 2024, and April 30, 2025, eligible patients aged ≥ 18 years scheduled for TURBT, robot‐assisted radical nephroureterectomy (RANU), or robot‐assisted radical or partial nephrectomy (RARN or RAPN) were enrolled after providing written informed consent. TURBT was curative for non‐muscle‐invasive bladder cancer, ensuring resection to the muscular layer. RANU, RARN, and RAPN followed standard oncological procedures.

Exclusion criteria included: pre‐existing bladder catheters or intermittent self‐catheterization, spinal cord injury or sensory impairment below the thoracic level, planned epidural anesthesia, dementia, hemodialysis, uncontrolled urinary tract infection, history of pelvic radiation, or any other investigator‐determined ineligibility.

Sample Size

The sample size was determined based on the precision of the confidence interval for the difference between groups. The incidence of the primary outcome, CRBD, in the FC group was anticipated to be 70%. In the absence of prior studies, a 20% difference in CRBD incidence between the two groups was considered clinically meaningful, i.e., a 50% incidence in the OEFC group. Based on these assumptions and a sample size of 41 participants per group, the half‐width of the two‐sided 90% confidence interval for the difference in incidence rates between the groups was 0.174, which was deemed acceptable for an exploratory study. Considering a potential dropout rate of 20%, the final sample size was set at 50 participants per group.

After obtaining written informed consent, participants were screened for eligibility. Those meeting all inclusion and no exclusion criteria were enrolled and randomized via an electronic data capture system. Each was assigned a unique study ID, recorded along with the randomization outcome on code, which was recorded along with the randomization results.

Device and Stratification

The FC used in this clinical trial was the Bardia Biocath Foley Catheter (C. R. Bard Inc., USA; approval number: 20400BZY00388000) which was the most commonly used type of FC in our facilities. The OEFC was the OPTICAP Open‐Ended Foley Catheter (Fuji Systems Inc., Japan; approval number: 220ADBZX00112000). The FC used in this study was composed of hydrogel‐coated latex, whereas the OEFC was made of silicone.

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the FC or the OEFC group using a stratified permuted block method. Stratification factors included sex (male or female) and the type of scheduled surgical procedure—either one involving direct bladder manipulation (TURBT or RANU) or one without direct bladder manipulation (RARN or RAPN). For patients undergoing TURBT, a three‐way 18 Fr FC or OEFC was inserted, while a two‐way 14 Fr FC or OEFC was used for those undergoing RANU, RARN, or RAPN. Each catheter was retained for a minimum of 6 h postoperatively and removed at a later time point at the discretion of the attending physician. If the urinary catheter remained in place at the predefined time points, all relevant parameters were recorded. The precise date and time of the catheter removal were not documented.

End Points and Assessments

The full analysis set (FAS) excluded patients not meeting key criteria, not receiving catheters, or lacking post‐randomization data. The primary endpoint was the incidence of CRBD at 6 h postoperatively. Secondary endpoints included the incidence and severity of CRBD at each predefined time point; the incidence and severity of urethral pain; the frequency and type of pharmacological interventions administered for CRBD or urethral pain; and the incidence of adverse events associated with the urinary catheter. The predefined time points for secondary endpoints were 0, 1, 3, and 6 h postoperatively, and, if the catheter remained indwelling, also at 24, 48, and 72 h. CRBD was assessed using the four‐point scale developed by Agarwal et al. [17]. Grade 0 was defined as no report from the patient upon interview; Grade 1 (mild discomfort) was revealed only through interviewing; Grade 2 (moderate discomfort) referred to a spontaneous complaint by the patient; and Grade 3 (severe discomfort) was characterized by excitement, great frustration, or attempts to remove the bladder catheter. The Wong‐Baker Pain Rating Scale (WBS), one of several facial‐expression scales used for pain assessment [18], was employed to evaluate urethral pain. All assessments were performed exclusively by the assigned nurses, and nursing assignments were rotated daily. Physicians were not involved in the evaluation process at any point. Throughout the observation period, nurses carried standardized assessment sheets detailing the evaluation items for both CRBD and urethral pain.

Analgesics were used when patients had CRBD or urethral pain symptoms and requested them. Patients informed nurses if they desired analgesics, which were then prescribed. Physicians were not involved in the medication administration process.

Statistics

Between‐group comparisons used unpaired t‐tests, χ 2 tests, and Mann–Whitney U tests (p < 0.05). Time‐dependent changes were analyzed using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) for binary outcomes and linear mixed models (LMMs) for ordinal CRBD scores. Models included time, catheter type, and their interaction as fixed effects, with patient ID as a random effect. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP software (SAS Institute Inc., RRID: SCR_008567). Figures were generated and plotted using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., RRID: SCR_002798).

Primary Endpoint

For the primary outcome, the incidence of CRBD at 6 h postoperatively was 42.6% (20/47) in the OEFC group, which was significantly lower compared to the 68.1% (32/47) observed in the FC group. This represents a difference in incidence of 25.5% (90% confidence interval: 9.2%–41.8%; p = 0.012). Additional Exploratory Analysis

The interaction between time and catheter type on CRBD incidence and severity within the first 6 postoperative hours was assessed using GLMM and LMMs, respectively (Tables S3 and S4). CRBD incidence significantly decreased over time (p < 0.001). Patients in the OEFC group consistently showed a significantly lower overall incidence than those in the FC group (p = 0.017). No significant interaction between time and catheter type was observed, indicating similar temporal trends between groups (p = 0.900) (Table S3). Likewise, CRBD severity within 6 h significantly decreased over time (p < 0.001). The OEFC group consistently showed significantly lower severity than the FC group (p = 0.008). Again, no significant interaction was observed, suggesting similar trends in both groups (p = 0.944) (Table S4).

Subgroup Analyses

To assess the consistency of the intervention effect across subgroups, exploratory subgroup analyses for CRBD were conducted. At the primary endpoint of 6 h postoperatively, we examined whether known risk factors for CRBD incidence—namely, male sex and TURBT [1]—influenced the preventive efficacy of the OEFC (Figure 4). The results demonstrated that the OEFC reduced the incidence of CRBD regardless of sex, with a more pronounced effect observed in patients who underwent non‐TURBT procedures compared with those who underwent TURBT. Subsequently, we investigated the incidence (Figure S1A) and severity (Figure S1B) of CRBD at 6 h postoperatively, stratified by non‐TURBT and TURBT procedures. In the non‐TURBT subgroup, the OEFC significantly reduced both the incidence and severity of CRBD compared to the FC, consistent with the overall findings. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two catheter types in the TURBT subgroup.

Discussion

Indwelling urethral catheters remain essential in modern medical care despite their notable design limitations. Although various catheter designs have been developed to reduce complications—such as catheter‐associated urinary tract infection, urethral injury, and obstruction—their impact on CRBD has remained unclear.

This exploratory, parallel‐group, single‐blind randomized controlled trial is the first to demonstrate that an OEFC—with a shorter distance between the balloon and tip, and a blunt tip design—significantly reduces CRBD incidence and severity during the early postoperative period (0–6 h) compared with a conventional FC.

It is recognized that the balloon of a urethral catheter can stimulate the trigone of the bladder, and that the catheter tip may come into contact with the bladder wall, contributing to mucosal irritation [19]. Since the OEFC and FC had the same balloon capacity, the reduction in stimulation of the bladder wall at the tip of the OEFC led to our results.

Although the FC and OEFC differed in material composition (hydrogel‐coated latex for the FC and silicone for the OEFC), the incidence and severity of urethral pain were comparable between the two groups. These findings clearly indicate that the observed reduction in CRBD incidence and severity is attributable to differences in catheter tip design. Furthermore, as reported in prior animal studies, the OEFC may induce less ulcer formation and inflammation of the bladder mucosa than the FC [13].

To date, no studies have directly compared the incidence of CRBD or urethral pain between latex and silicone catheters of identical shape. The present study clarifies that catheter material does not affect urethral pain. Modern latex catheters are typically coated with hydrogel or silicone elastomer to reduce the risk of cytotoxicity due to rubber‐derived eluates [14]. Silicone elastomer catheters, which are entirely composed of non‐latex material, often incorporate hydrophilic coatings to produce a low‐friction surface [20]. In addition to their biocompatible and non‐allergenic materials, they offer enhanced resistance to kinking and improved flow performance compared with conventional latex catheters [21]. However, a previous RCT demonstrated that these theoretical advantages did not result in clinical benefit after urethral reconstructive surgery [22].

TURBT is associated with a higher risk of CRBD compared with percutaneous nephrolithotomy and non‐urologic surgeries [1]. In the subgroup analyses, the incidence of CRBD at 6 h postoperatively was 63.6% in the OEFC group and 81.8% in the FC group among TURBT patients, and 24.0% in the OEFC group versus 56.0% in the FC group among non‐TURBT patients (Figure 4). Several previous clinical trials evaluating pharmacological interventions for the prevention of CRBD have also reported incidence rates at 6 h postoperatively [6, 7, 17, 23]. For instance, in patients undergoing TURBT, solifenacin reduced the CRBD incidence to 67.2% compared with 93.1% in the control group [6]. In patients undergoing other urological surgeries excluding TURBT, butylscopolamine reduced the incidence to 23.0% versus 40.0% in the control group [7]. Based on these findings, the use of the OEFC may serve as a primary strategy for CRBD prevention, offering efficacy comparable to that of pharmacological therapies.

In this study, the number of pharmacological interventions during catheterization did not differ significantly between groups. Acetaminophen was the most commonly used medication, consistent with previous reports from our institution and its affiliated center [24, 25]. Notably, muscarinic receptor antagonists—agents most frequently studied in clinical trials for the prevention of CRBD—were not used in this study. However, such pharmacological agents have been associated with side effects, including dry mouth and constipation [7]. The efficacy of OEFC appears comparable to outcomes reported in prior pharmacologic trials, though direct comparison was not made. Its broader application in clinical practice may contribute to reducing the overall costs associated with CRBD management.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center and included only patients undergoing urological surgeries, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the subgroup analyses were based on a relatively small number of patients, potentially resulting in limited statistical power. As this study did not compare catheters of identical design but different materials, the independent effect of catheter material on CRBD remains unclear and should be examined in future studies. Postoperative catheter insertions were performed by urologists. Although no insertion‐related complications occurred, the blunt tip may be unsuitable for patients with urethral stenosis.

In conclusion, use of the OEFC is a simple and safe strategy that reduces both the incidence and severity of CRBD compared with the FC. It should be considered whenever feasible to improve postoperative quality of life in patients undergoing various surgical procedures. The development of future urethral catheters with improved tip configurations to further minimize CRBD risk is highly desirable.
