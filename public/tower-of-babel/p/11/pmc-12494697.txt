Selection of optimal fabrication parameters of an innovative pressure sensor using fuzzy-AHP method based on sensor characteristics for robotic gripper
Authors: Ahad Khabbaz Bavil, Meltem Tekcin, Senem Kursun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With increasing demand for diverse sensors across various applications, the development of innovative sensors is crucial to advancing emerging technologies. Fabric-based sensors, valued for their flexibility and softness, are gaining popularity in fields like medicine and healthcare. This study presents a new method for designing and fabricating a textile-based resistive pressure sensor using pad printing and conductive inks, specifically for robotic grippers. Key fabrication factors include fabric type, ink composition, and the number of print passes. Sensor performance was assessed based on sensitivity, linearity, repeatability, and fatigue resistance. The main goal was to identify and optimize fabrication parameters to achieve the best performance within a robotic gripper using the fuzzy-AHP (Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process) method. The study was carried out in two phases. First, the optimal sample was chosen by evaluating general sensor properties through fuzzy-AHP modeling. In the second phase, to enhance the gripper’s performance when handling objects with varying surface hardness, the model incorporated sub-criteria of sensitivity and linearity across different ranges. Three scenarios were developed to identify the best sensor sample for gripping objects of low, medium, and high hardness. Across four rounds of fuzzy-AHP modeling, the sensor printed on polyamide-based taffeta label fabric with three print passes and 100% carbon nanoparticle ink delivered the best overall performance, surpassing other configurations.

Introduction

In recent years, extensive research has been dedicated to endowing robots with the sense of touch1–3. It is widely anticipated that, in the near future, virtually all robotic systems will integrate tactile sensing capabilities, thereby enabling them to perceive and engage with their surroundings in a far more refined and sophisticated manner 4. Tactile sensors can be classified through various criteria, such as their underlying sensing mechanisms, fabrication techniques, and transduction principles5. Additionally, tactile sensors can be categorized based on the specific robot components they are designed to replicate or cover5. Among the most vital components of tactile perception is the ability to sense force or pressure6. By incorporating pressure sensors into a robot’s structure, it gains the ability to detect the force exerted on specific parts of its body, thus providing it with a fundamental aspect of tactile feedback. In robotic grippers, the incorporation of tactile sensing significantly enhances the quality of object manipulation, especially when handling delicate or fragile items7,8. This tactile feedback allows robots to adjust their motion control dynamically in response to physical interactions with objects, which is crucial when robots operate in environments shared with humans9,10. In particular, robotic grippers equipped with tactile sensors are integral to advancements in haptic feedback systems for teleoperation and blind grasping11,12. Furthermore, substantial progress has been made in enabling robotic grippers to identify objects through tactile information, showcasing a remarkable leap in the application of touch-based perception in robotics13.

There exists a wide array of surface pressure sensors, with the most prevalent types being resistive14, capacitive15, and piezoelectric sensors16, each chosen based on the specific application requirements. Resistive pressure sensors operate on the principle of changes in electrical resistance resulting from applied pressure17. These sensors offer notable advantages, including high sensitivity and cost-effectiveness18. Flexible pressure sensors, in particular, possess desirable attributes such as exceptional flexibility, heightened sensitivity, and lightweight construction, which have made them especially attractive for applications in the development of electronic skin (E-skin) for robots19, as well as wearable, flexible sensing devices for human use20. Moreover, flexible pressure sensors have also been explored as tools for human–machine interfaces, opening new avenues for interaction between humans and machines21.

Textile-based touch sensors are a category of tactile sensors composed of fabrics, yarns, or fibers22. These flexible sensors can easily conform to the desired parts of a robot’s body, including curved surfaces23. Due to their inherent softness and wearability, fabric sensors have gained increasing traction across various industries, particularly in medicine and healthcare24. The integration of stretchable, fabric-based skins—incorporating force and thermal sensors—has been explored to cover portions of a robot, including its curved surfaces25. Fabric-based pressure sensors, in particular, have experienced significant growth in recent years, finding new applications in fields such as human motion capture, physiological activity monitoring, healthcare and rehabilitation, skin electronics, and human–machine interfaces22.

A sensor is a device that converts a physical measurement into an output signal, which can be interpreted by an instrument or observed by a user26. To effectively design, fabricate, and utilize sensors, it is essential to understand their performance characteristics27. Some of key sensor attributes include measurement range, resolution, sensitivity, linearity (or the linear transfer function), and repeatability27. In addition, the durability of a sensor reflects its acceptable operational lifespan28. The measurement range defines both the minimum and maximum values that the sensor can accurately measure26. Sensitivity indicates the extent to which the sensor’s output changes in response to a change in the input27. If the sensor behaves linearly, sensitivity is represented by the slope of the input–output graph within the sensor’s operational range26. For an ideal sensor, constant and significant sensitivity is a critical feature26. Furthermore, an increase in linearity enhances the sensor’s overall performance27. Repeatability (or reproducibility) refers to the sensor’s ability to generate consistent outputs when the same input is applied under identical conditions27. In the case of textile-based sensors, it is particularly important to assess the sensor’s fatigue over time and through multiple work cycles, due to their flexible and soft structures, which may be prone to wear29.

The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), which proposed by Thomas L. Saaty, is a decision-making methodology used by individuals and organizations to rank alternatives through pairwise comparisons in complex decision30. Comparisons are conducted using a scale of absolute judgments that quantifies the extent to which one element outweighs another in relation to a specific attribute31. It offers a structured, quantitative approach to assess the weight of decision-making criteria, leveraging the expertise of decision makers to estimate the relative importance of factors32. By performing these comparisons, AHP helps identify the best alternative, not by prescribing a “correct” decision, but by guiding decision makers toward the option that aligns most closely with their goals and understanding of the issue33. The key point is that applying the AHP involves two essential steps—designing a hierarchy and evaluating it—both of which rely heavily on the decision maker’s knowledge and judgment, meaning different people may create different hierarchies or produce different results even when using the same structure, due to the subjective nature of the process33. AHP provides a logical and comprehensive framework for structuring complex decisions, quantifying their elements, and evaluating potential solutions32.

In the AHP method, users begin by breaking down their decision problem into a hierarchical structure of smaller, more manageable subproblems, which can be analyzed independently. A typical hierarchy in the Analytical Hierarchy Process consists of three main levels—goal, criteria (which may be multi-tiered depending on the problem’s complexity), and alternatives—where criteria are first prioritized through pairwise comparisons to establish relative weights, and then alternatives are evaluated under each criterion, with the final scores computed by aggregating the resulting priority values. After establishing this hierarchy, decision makers assess each element by making pairwise comparisons based on its influence on the element above it in the hierarchy. These comparisons can be informed by both factual data and the decision makers’ judgments regarding the relative importance and meaning of the elements. AHP then converts these qualitative assessments into numerical values, enabling the comparison of elements across the entire problem. Each element in the hierarchy is assigned a numerical weight or priority, allowing for a logical and consistent comparison of diverse, and sometimes incomparable, factors30. This ability to quantify and compare such elements is what sets AHP apart from other decision-making methods34.

If the AHP method alone is not suitable for solving a particular problem, it can be integrated with other techniques35–39, such as the fuzzy-AHP method40,41. Fuzzy set theory has been integrated into the original AHP to better capture the inherent uncertainty and imprecision of human judgment, as relying solely on crisp values can misrepresent preferences and potentially lead to flawed decisions41. In fuzzy-AHP method, the use of interval-based criteria and scoring enhances the effectiveness of AHP in addressing complex real-world and engineering challenges33,42,43.

Although, AHP traditionally more famous for its use in management decision-making34,44, this method has recently found increasing applications across various engineering disciplines. Numerous studies have applied this method in diverse areas, including parametric optimization of non-traditional machining processes36,45–48, optimal machining parameter selection35,49–51, equipment selection strategies52,53, energy engineering54–56, vending machine location optimization57, tool cutting performance analysis58, effective paint removal process identification59, prioritization of high-energy machining operations60, meteorological modeling61,62, civil and structural engineering63,64, textile engineering65, manufacturing systems66,67, composite materials68,69, additive manufacturing parameter optimization70, advancements in environmental engineering and eco-efficiency71,72, software and data engineering73,74, marine technology75, and various other engineering disciplines. Unlike data-driven techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA) and other machine learning methods, which are suited for large-scale quantitative analysis and tasks like dimensionality reduction and pattern recognition76,77, Fuzzy-AHP is designed for structured evaluation and ranking of alternatives based on both qualitative and quantitative criteria, offering greater interpretability in decision-making contexts.

This study presents the design and fabrication of a novel fabric-based resistive pressure sensor utilizing the pad printing technique, intended for integration into robotic grippers. Key parameters in the pad printing process—such as the combination of conductive inks, fabric type, and ink layer thickness (determined by the number of successive printing passes)—were systematically varied, and corresponding sensor prototypes were produced. To determine the optimal fabrication parameters, the prototypes underwent pressure and cyclic loading tests. Performance characteristics, including sensitivity, linearity, fatigue resistance, and repeatability, were evaluated across all samples. The optimal configuration for robotic gripper applications was identified using the Fuzzy Analytical Hierarchy Process (fuzzy-AHP), based on the aforementioned performance metrics. An innovative approach was proposed through the use of the fuzzy-AHP method to enable the evaluation of sensor properties as continuous alternatives and the construction of comparison matrices between these continuous alternatives. Consequently, the study establishes a set of optimal parameters for the fabrication of fabric-based pressure sensors via pad printing.

Materials and fabrication method

The purpose of this experimental research is to design and fabricate an innovative type of textile-based pressure sensor that will be used in robotic grippers for industrial applications. The designed sensor will be covered the contact area of robotic gripper and objects to measure pressure between gripper and objects (Fig. 1). Pad printing technique has been used in the fabrication of this sensor. Pad printing is a widely used and cost-effective technique known for its flexibility and ability to print on diverse surface types and shapes78. Compared to methods like screen and inkjet printing, it offers more uniform ink distribution and higher resolution, resulting in enhanced surface conductivity79. In this process, conductive inks containing conductive nanoparticles including silver and carbon nanoparticles have been used. The pad printing machine utilized in this study to produce the samples is a standard commercial model, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. The important parts of pad printing machine are: a silicone pad, an ink container or in other word ‘the dye reservoir’, and a stereotype or cliché (Fig. 3b). The prepared ink is poured into the dye reservoir. The dye reservoir is then turned upside down and placed onto the cliché. The desired pattern is engraved on the cliché, which is a plate made of stainless steel. Thanks to the magnetic force from the magnets in the dye reservoir, the dye reservoir adheres to the cliché with optimal adhesion, preventing the evaporation of ink and solvent, and ensuring that the ink is spread correctly on the cliché. The pad is then pressed onto the pattern on the cliché. As a result, the pad takes the ink in the same pattern as the cliché and transfers it to the desired print location. The pad is pressed onto the desired area, and the pattern is printed onto the fabric surface78. For printing textile-based samples, silver conductive and carbon conductive inks were used. Silver conductive ink, which contains silver nanoparticles, and carbon conductive ink, which contains carbon nanoparticles, were purchased from Engineered Conductive Materials. To prepare the desired inks, silver and carbon conductive inks were precisely weighed using a digital balance and manually stirred for ten minutes. A thinner was then added to adjust the viscosity of the mixture. To achieve a homogeneous ink with optimal viscosity suitable for the pad printing process, the ink-thinner mixture was prepared at a weight ratio of 5:1 and further stirred for 30 min using a magnetic stirrer. The prepared ink was immediately transferred to the dye reservoir of the pad printing machine, and the sensor samples were subsequently printed. Due to the rapid nature of the printing process, no issues related to ink homogeneity were observed. Since the ink used in pad printing is a fundamental factor in the fabrication of the proposed pressure sensor, variations in the properties of these two inks, particularly in their conductivity, result in changes to the sensor’s performance. To identify the ink composition that provides the best performance for the proposed pressure sensor, particularly for compatibility with the robot’s gripper, several concentrations of two conductive inks—containing silver and carbon nanoparticles—were examined. To evaluate the impact of these varying concentrations on the performance of the printed samples, five different ink mixtures were tested: 100% Ag, 75% Ag-25% C, 50% Ag-50% C, 25% Ag-75% C, and 100% C.

To investigate the effect of fabric type as substrates of the proposed sensor on the performance of it, two types of fabrics were examined. Polyamide-based taffeta label fabric and sateen woven fabric were selected. The polyamide-based taffeta label fabric has a thickness ranging from 0.105 to 0.115 mm, with an average weight of 62 ± 5 g/m2. The sateen woven fabric, composed of 100% polyester and woven in a 4 × 4 Z pattern, has an average weight of 114.66 g/m2.

In the pad printing technique, the pad transfers a fixed amount of ink from the cliché to the printing area, applying pressure during each printing cycle. As a result, with an increased number of printing passes (or cycles), a larger quantity of electrically conductive ink is transferred to the fabric, causing the ink layer in the printed area to become thicker. By altering the amount or thickness of the conductive ink in the printed area, the conductivity of the printed samples changes accordingly. To examine the effect of the conductive ink quantity transferred to the printed samples, the parameter of consecutive print cycles (passes) was studied. In this research, samples with 1, 3, and 5 consecutive prints were fabricated to analyze the impact of the transferred conductive ink on sensor performance.

Finally, a total of 30 different samples with three different fabrication parameters: ink combination (5 combinations), print pass (3 types), and fabric type (2 types) were printed (Table 1). It is important to note that samples printed on sateen fabric with a single pass of 100% Ag, 75% Ag + 25% C, and 50% Ag + 50% C inks were excluded from this study due to their extremely high electrical resistance, which rendered them unmeasurable. Measurement of the printed sensor performance

The pressure sensor created for the robot gripper’s fingertips operates by sensing changes in pressure through variations in electrical resistance, classifying it as a resistive sensor. Consequently, the key factor to examine in the printed samples is the electrical resistance of the printed area. For this purpose, the electrical resistance of the samples was measured using the Instek GDM-8245 multimeter.

The function of the proposed pressure sensor is based on changes in electrical resistance at the contact area between two sensor layers, which occurs when the gripper’s fingertips make contact with an object and pressure is applied. Therefore, two samples with identical fabrication parameters were positioned face to face, with the printed areas of each layer connected to a desktop multimeter. The sensitivity of the designed pressure sensor is determined by the variation in electrical resistance measured in response to the applied force. More specifically, pressure sensitivity is calculated using the formula \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S=\frac{d\Omega /{\Omega }_{0}}{dP}$$\end{document}, where S represents the pressure sensitivity, dΩ/Ω₀ is the output signal, and P denotes the applied external pressure81. This formula quantifies how effectively a sensor detects and responds to small changes in the parameter it is intended to measure. Generally, sensitivity is a key characteristic of any sensor, defined as the ratio of the output change to the input change26.

The pressure sensor designed for the fingertips of the robot gripper must accurately measure the variation in pressure force between the gripper’s fingertips and the object. The linearity of the sensor’s measurements reflects the accuracy of its sensitivity calculation and overall performance27. This linearity, or the sensor’s linear response, is an important attribute determined by examining how closely the sensor’s output corresponds to changes in its inputs27. Typically, this evaluation involves analyzing correlations and checking the consistency of the sensor’s output in response to varying inputs. According to linear regression analysis principles, a higher R2 value—closer to 1—indicates that the measured results follow a more linear pattern82. Consequently, the proposed pressure sensor, made from the sample with the highest R2, demonstrates greater linearity and, as a result, superior performance. Moreover, the sensitivity and linearity outcomes observed in this study were evaluated in comparison with similar prior research, demonstrating consistency and alignment with previously reported findings83.

The proposed pressure sensor is designed to be mounted on the fingertips of the robot’s gripper, ensuring frequent and repetitive use. Therefore, the sensor must be durable enough to withstand numerous cycles without performance degradation. To assess the performance of the samples under consecutive, high-duty cycles, a cycling test was conducted. Methodology

When the robot’s gripper grasps an object, its fingertips exert a force on the object’s surface, while the object applies an equal but opposite force back on the gripper. This force is measured by the fabric pressure sensors embedded in the gripper’s fingertips, providing crucial data to control the grasping process. As the gripper’s fingers move, the force applied to the object’s surface begins at zero and gradually increases until it is sufficient to lift the object, depending on the surface hardness. The change in electrical resistance detected by the sensor, in response to the applied force, determines the sensor’s sensitivity. This sensitivity reflects the sensor’s ability to detect and respond to minor changes in the force it measures. Clearly, the pressure sensor’s sensitivity plays a vital role in the precise control of the robot gripper’s grasping action.

When the robot’s gripper grasps an object, the force applied by its fingertips starts at zero and gradually increases until it is sufficient to lift the object. As a result, the pressure sensor designed for the gripper’s fingertips must accurately measure the changes in pressure between the robot’s fingertips and the object. The linearity of the sensor’s measurements is a key indicator of its accuracy in sensing and performance. In other words, the sensor’s ability to provide a linear response—where its output closely matches changes in input—is crucial. This can be evaluated by examining how well the sensor’s output correlates with varying input forces, ensuring consistency and precision in its measurements.

Given that the robot gripper is intended for industrial applications, the pressure sensor designed for its fingertips must be capable of maintaining consistent performance during long-term, continuous operation. The sensor is composed of two layers that make direct contact under external force to measure the applied pressure. Over time, these layers can experience gradual wear and fatigue, which may impact the sensor’s performance during extended duty cycles. Therefore, resistance to fatigue is a critical feature of the sensor, ensuring that it continues to provide accurate measurements throughout prolonged use.

The primary function of the robot’s gripper is to grasp various objects repeatedly, with the pressure sensor in the gripper’s fingertips measuring the contact force between the fingertips and the objects based on their surface hardness. As a result, repeatability in measuring the resistance between the two layers of the proposed sensor under the same force is crucial for controlling the object grasping process. Repeatability is a key characteristic of any sensor and is determined by calculating the standard deviation of the measurements obtained from multiple readings under identical conditions.

An analysis of the experimental results, aimed at determining the optimal characteristics of the pressure sensor for the robot gripper, revealed notable variability in performance based on differing fabrication parameters. Specifically, ranking the samples according to the desired characteristics yielded inconsistent outcomes. For instance, a sample that excelled in one attribute often demonstrated subpar or mediocre performance in others. Consequently, selecting the ideal sample for integration into the robot gripper, along with the corresponding fabrication parameters, presents a complex challenge. This complexity is further exacerbated when considering the sensor’s performance in relation to its influence on the gripper’s ability to effectively handle a variety of objects. Hence, this study employs the Fuzzy-AHP technique to identify the optimal sample, leveraging its effectiveness in addressing complex decision-making processes. To achieve this, a Fuzzy-AHP model was developed. Table 3 illustrates the hierarchical structure of the proposed model. After defining the goal and structuring the hierarchy of the proposed Fuzzy-AHP model, the next step involved preparing the pairwise comparisons for all criteria and alternatives, in accordance with the principles of Fuzzy-AHP. Next, the pairwise comparison matrices were normalized, and the weights for all criteria, along with the scores for each alternative, were computed. Subsequently, the criteria weights were multiplied by the corresponding alternative scores, and the results were summed for each alternative. In scientific literature, no universal framework is typically used to prioritize the characteristics of sensors. Instead, the features of laboratory and industrial sensors are generally prioritized based on their intended applications and performance requirements. In this study, an attempt has been made to prioritize the key characteristics of a proposed pressure sensor, focusing on its role in controlling the movement of the robotic gripper fingers when objects are grasped. To achieve this, the desired sensor characteristics have been ranked in the following order: resistance to fatigue is ranked first, repeatability second, linearity third, and sensitivity last. Resistance to fatigue is considered crucial, as the sensor must maintain its functionality over extended and intermittent work cycles. The more fatigue-resistant the sensor is, the longer consistent performance can be provided without degradation. As a result, resistance to fatigue is regarded as the most critical characteristic for the intended application and is prioritized first. Repeatability, ranked second, is considered essential for ensuring consistent sensor output under the same measurement conditions, which is important for precise control of the robotic gripper. A sensor with high repeatability will improve the gripper’s ability to reliably handle various objects. Linearity is ranked third, as it directly influences the accuracy of pressure measurements between the robot’s fingertips and the object being grasped. As the gripper’s force gradually increases from zero to the point where the object is lifted, accurate pressure measurement is required. The linearity of the sensor ensures that its readings remain consistent and reliable during this process. Finally, the sensitivity is ranked last on the priority list. This does not imply that the high sensitivity of the designed pressure sensor has little impact on improving the control of the robotic gripper and its performance. Rather, it is considered more manageable compared to other sensor properties. Specifically, its sensitivity can be adjusted using programming tools without the need to modify the sensor hardware, which is not possible for other properties.

In this study, pairwise comparisons for the criteria in the proposed Fuzzy-AHP model were generated through an assessment of the relative importance of each characteristic compared to the others, following the standard AHP method. This evaluation was conducted using a scale from 1 to 9, with the following importance ratings: Fatigue resistance, Repeatability, Linearity, and Sensitivity. In this study, the attributes of sensitivity, linearity, fatigue resistance, and repeatability were assessed for all 27 samples. Each sample is treated as an alternative within the proposed fuzzy-AHP model and ranked according to these attributes, which serve as criteria in the model. Following AHP principles, a pairwise comparison of alternatives must be conducted for each sensor characteristic, generating a corresponding comparison matrix. However, due to the complexity of comparing real-valued measurements for each sample and constructing a comparison matrix based on traditional AHP methods, a Fuzzy-AHP approach is employed. In this methodology, each characteristic is evaluated through fuzzy logic, and the resulting fuzzy scores are used for comparison. Figure 7 illustrates the fuzzy-AHP modeling process and the corresponding relative scores for all evaluated attributes of the pressure sensors. In general, the measured range of each sensor characteristic was represented as a fuzzy set and subdivided into multiple fuzzy subsets. A Singleton membership function was then applied to assess these subsets. To conform with the standard methodology of Fuzzy-AHP modeling, the Singleton membership values were normalized to a scale of 1–9, rather than the conventional 0–1 range. A direct mapping approach was used, linking the sensor’s measured values to their corresponding fuzzy subsets within the defined numerical intervals. Each fuzzy subset was subsequently scored using the Singleton membership function. Figure 8 illustrates the fuzzy values corresponding to each sensor characteristic’s measurement range, along with their respective Fuzzy-AHP scores. For instance, the minimum and maximum sensitivity values across all samples define a numerical range, which is subdivided into five fuzzy categories: very low sensitivity, low sensitivity, medium sensitivity, high sensitivity, and very high sensitivity. Each category is then assigned a score according to fuzzy-AHP principles. All samples are subsequently classified and compared based on these fuzzy evaluations, allowing for the creation of a comparison matrix. This procedure has been similarly applied to the other characteristics. To convert the numerical ranges of sensor characteristics into fuzzy categories for evaluation, the full range of measured values for each characteristic was divided into fuzzy subsets. Each subset was then assigned a score based on the role and relative importance of that characteristic in achieving optimal sensor performance. For sensitivity, the total measured range across all samples was divided into five subgroups. The very low sensitivity subgroup was assigned a score of 1, while the remaining subgroups were scored accordingly. Since sensitivity carries the lowest weight in the proposed fuzzy model, the very high sensitivity subgroup received a maximum score of 5. For linearity, the R2 index was used to assess the linearity of pressure test results across all samples. The R2 scale, ranging from 0 to 1, was divided into four fuzzy subgroups. These were scored based on the importance of linearity in sensor performance, with very low linearity receiving a score of 1 and very high linearity receiving a score of 8. In the case of repeatability, the sigma index was used to evaluate repeatability. The sigma values from all samples were divided into five fuzzy subgroups. Given the critical role of repeatability, the highest sigma values (indicating poor repeatability) were categorized as very low repeatability and scored 1, while the lowest sigma values (indicating high repeatability) were categorized as very high repeatability and scored 9. To assess fatigue resistance, the fatigue rate index was employed. Since a lower fatigue rate indicates higher fatigue resistance, the fuzzy scoring was inversely assigned. A fatigue rate between 15 and 100% was classified as very low fatigue resistance and assigned a score of 1, whereas a fatigue rate between 0 and 1% was considered very high fatigue resistance and scored 9. Analysis of samples in different ranges

The developed fabric-based pressure sensor is designed to be integrated into a robotic gripper, enabling real-time measurement of the contact force between the gripper’s fingertips and the object being grasped. During the gripping process, the robotic fingertips apply an increasing force to the object’s surface, which, according to Newton’s third law, generates an equal and opposite reaction force on the fingertips. This contact force begins at zero and gradually increases until it reaches a level sufficient to securely lift the object. The surface hardness of the object plays a critical role in the force measurement process and directly influences the control of the robotic gripper’s fingertips. Softer surfaces undergo more deformation upon contact, which affects how the force is transmitted and perceived by the pressure sensor. Therefore, the effectiveness of the robotic gripper in handling objects with varying surface hardness is highly dependent on the sensor’s ability to accurately respond to these differences.

Given that pressure sensors exhibit varying performance characteristics across different force ranges, it is essential that the sensor’s optimal measurement range aligns with the specific functional requirements of the target application. To simulate the gripper’s interaction with objects of varying hardness, three distinct scenarios were defined—each representing a different pressure range—to evaluate how each sensor responds under different surface hardness conditions. For each scenario, the linearity and performance of all sensor samples were analyzed and assessed using the Fuzzy Analytical Hierarchy Process (Fuzzy-AHP), while the weights assigned to fatigue resistance and repeatability were kept constant across all scenarios. Based on this multi-range evaluation, the hierarchical structure of the Fuzzy-AHP model was revised to include four sub-criteria under both the sensitivity and linearity categories, resulting in a more comprehensive and adaptive framework for performance evaluation across diverse application conditions (Fig. 9). The inclusion of these sub-criteria reflects the fact that the relative importance of sensitivity and linearity can vary depending on the pressure range and specific use case. In contrast, the importance of repeatability and fatigue resistance remains consistent, and thus their weighting is fixed across all scenarios.Fig. 9The hierarchical structure of the proposed fuzzy-AHP model for scenarios A, B, and C.

Conclusion

This experimental study introduces an innovative resistive pressure sensor, fabricated on a textile substrate, designed for integration into robotic grippers to accurately measure gripping force. The sensor is produced using a pad printing technique, employing conductive ink contained with nanoparticles. The research focuses on identifying optimal fabrication parameters by evaluating the performance of various sensor prototypes, each manufactured under different conditions. To determine the best configuration for the proposed pressure sensor—intended for use on robotic fingertips—multiple samples were created, varying in fabric type, nanoparticle composition in the conductive ink, and the number of printing passes. Specifically, a polyamide-based taffeta label fabric and a woven satin fabric were used as substrates. The conductive ink was formulated with five distinct nanoparticle ratios: 100% silver (Ag), 75% Ag + 25% carbon (C), 50% Ag + 50% C, 25% Ag + 75% C, and 100% C. Each formulation was applied using one, three, or five sequential pad printing passes.

Sensor performance was evaluated based on key criteria: sensitivity, linearity, fatigue resistance, and repeatability. The influence of the fabrication parameters on each of these characteristics was thoroughly analyzed. A detailed description of the experimental procedures and the results obtained is presented in the article corresponding to the previous phase of this research80. However, the results revealed that optimizing for a single attribute often compromised others, making it difficult to identify a universally superior sample. Furthermore, evaluating the relative importance of each performance characteristic in the context of robotic fingertip force sensing added another layer of complexity to the selection process. To address this, the study employed the fuzzy-AHP, a robust multi-criteria decision-making framework, to identify the most suitable sensor configuration. Initially, the performance metrics were prioritized and weighted according to their relevance to pressure sensor effectiveness. The scores for each sample were then calculated, and the samples ranked accordingly.

Based on this comprehensive evaluation, Sample 23 emerged as the optimal choice. This sample was fabricated using three consecutive pad printing passes with ink composed entirely of carbon nanoparticles (100% C) on label fabric (polyamide-based taffeta label fabrics).

In the second phase of the study, three distinct application scenarios were introduced, reflecting different object surface hardness levels that the robotic gripper may encounter. These scenarios were analyzed using three variations of the fuzzy-AHP to evaluate sensor suitability: Scenario A considered objects with low surface hardness (soft materials). Scenario B focused on objects with medium hardness. Scenario C addressed objects with high surface hardness. In each case, sensor performance was assessed across four pressure ranges: 0.49–2.45 kPa, 2.45–12.25 kPa, 12.25–24.5 kPa, and 0.49–24.5 kPa. Object categories were prioritized based on their relevance to real-world robotic tasks, and corresponding weights were assigned. Across all scenarios, Sample 23 consistently demonstrated superior performance.

Compared to traditional evaluation methods, the propozed fuzzy-AHP framework enabled a more flexible and precise ranking of sensor configurations, particularly under varying pressure range scenarios. The introduction of sub-criteria within sensitivity and linearity categories significantly enhanced the model’s adaptability, allowing for context-dependent weighting based on desired performance of robotic gripper in taking objects with different surface hardness. This contrasts with prior studies that typically apply fixed weights regardless of application-specific demands.Critically, the use of sensor characters as continuous alternatives in the proposed fuzzy-AHP model marks a methodological innovation. While this approach increases modeling complexity, it offers greater fidelity in real-world sensor selection processes. This innovative approach also facilitates the application of fuzzy-AHP modeling in real-world engineering challenges where performance parameters are not discrete but vary across a continuum.

The overarching objective of this research was to determine the most effective sensor configuration and fabrication parameters for high-performance pressure sensing in robotic grippers. The selected sensor from Sample 23 will be subsequently integrated into the fingertips of a robotic gripper, covering the full contact area. During object manipulation, the sensor captures real-time contact force data, enabling dynamic control of the gripper fingers. Furthermore, the sensor fabricated from sample 23 will be subjected to real-world experimental evaluations. Assessment frameworks, including the development of a predictive sensor performance model, will be established through a series of independent validation experiments. In addition, an optimal calibration algorithm will be formulated to regulate and enhance the sensor’s performance during robotic gripper operation.

This work not only advances sensor design and optimization methodologies but also sets the stage for the next phase of research—real-world validation of the sensor-integrated gripper. Ultimately, the incorporation of this pressure sensor enhances the robotic gripper’s tactile sensing capabilities, enabling more precise and adaptive manipulation of diverse objects.
