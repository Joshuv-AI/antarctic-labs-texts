Bioluminescent monitoring of a co-culture model of pathogen growth to identify a novel probiotic bacterium
Authors: Phillip H. Gallo, Avni Kathju, Latha Satish
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveProbiotic bacteria can inhibit the growth of harmful bacterial species but may be limited in the bacteria they can counteract. We developed a novel co-culture assay in which candidate probiotic bacteria can be co-incubated with both Gram-positive and Gram-negative reporter pathogens that have been modified to emit light, so as to monitor pathogen growth simply by quantitation of emitted light from the culture. We used this assay to identify a novel probiotic bacterium with anti-microbial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens.ResultsWe isolated a novel bacterium from non-pasteurized milk and identified it as a strain of Lactobacillus delbrueckii. Testing in our assay confirmed that this bacterium was able to inhibit the growth of both Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, but in a delayed fashion, after allowing a period of early growth. This activity was not dependent on living cells but also a property of conditioned medium. We posited that this was because the pathogens stimulated the production of an inhibitory factor from the Lactobacillus, presumably a bacteriocin peptide. Pre-treatment of conditioned medium with a protease abolished this inhibitory activity, indicating that L. delbrueckii is stimulated to produce an antibacterial peptide in the presence of a bacterial pathogen.

Introduction

Infection following burn injury is the most common complication in burn wound patients and is the most cited reason for mortality, accounting for up to 75% of burn-related deaths [1]. Frank sepsis is the most common cause of death in patients of all ages [2], with the burn wound itself having been identified as the most likely portal of entry for the invading pathogens [3]. Numerous bacteria (and fungi) have been observed to exist within burn wounds in the form of biofilms [4]. Biofilm bacteria are highly resistant to both conventional antibiotics and to natural host immune responses and are also difficult to detect by standard microbiological culture [5]. These multiple factors make the treatment of burn wound infection a daunting problem. Systemic and topical antibiotics remain the standard therapy but may prove ineffective in the face of an overwhelming or polymicrobial infection. For this reason, alternative therapeutic strategies are a subject of great interest, including the use of probiotic bacteria as a means of countering burn wound pathogens.

Our lab has previously described a mouse model of burn wound infection leading to sepsis and death [6]. In this model, the use of the probiotic bacterium Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (formerly known as Lactobacillus plantarum) reduces mortality from a baseline of > 90% to < 10%. Our results suggested that probiotic therapy for burn wounds is highly promising, but our results were also limited. Another common pathogen in burn wounds (and many other wound types) is Staphylococcus aureus. Although L. plantarum was highly effective against P. aeruginosa in our mouse model, it did not show similar activity against S. aureus, potentially limiting its usefulness in clinical burn wounds. Multiple other potential probiotic bacteria have been reported, but in most cases their activity against S. aureus is weak or undetermined. For this reason, we developed a novel assay that would allow us to rapidly identify new probiotic strains of bacteria with strong activity against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive organisms, including especially S. aureus (and also Escherichia coli).

Methods

Isolation and identification of novel strains of probiotic bacteria

To obtain new candidate probiotic strains of Lactobacillus, non-pasteurized cow’s milk was obtained from a local grocery / co-operative. 100 µL of the cow’s milk was inoculated into MRS media and grown without shaking at 37°C for 8 hours, and then the culture was serially diluted onto MRS agar plates, incubating overnight at 37°C. Mixed cultures were obtained, and serial dilutions into MRS media and subsequent plating at 37°C anaerobically with MRS agar were used to colony purify multiple bacterial isolates, including bacterial isolate CM80. CM80 was determined to be Lactobacillus delbrueckii by 16S rDNA sequencing and similarity searching to the BLAST database. Briefly, genomic DNA was purified from overnight cultures of bacteria using the DNeasy prep kit (Qiagen Inc, Valencia, CA) following the protocol for Gram-positive bacteria. 16S rDNA PCR was done according to published protocols using an approximately 1.2 kb fragment of the 16S rDNA [7]; an aliquot of the resulting amplimer was directly sequenced using the sequencing primer 16-seqF (5’- GGAAACRGGYGCTAATACCG − 3’). Sequencing results were compared against the BLAST bacterial 16S rDNA database; the most similar sequence was L. delbrueckii subsp. indicus with 99% match, and confirmed the identity of our bacterial isolate as a strain of L. delbrueckii.

A co-culture assay to test the effect of candidate probiotic bacteria on the growth of light-emitting pathogens

We first examined the growth of bioluminescent methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA; Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA) when grown in co-culture with L. delbrueckii (CM80) by utilizing an automated in vitro assay measuring the growth of MRSA in a 96-well format. The TECAN Infinite 200 Pro Application Note was used to set up a growth curve for S. aureus strain Xen31, a clinical MRSA strain with bioluminescence, using a fully automated data collection system reporting luminescence and OD600 values approximately every 30 min for 13 h [8]. Briefly, 2 × 105 luminescent Xen31 cells were mixed with BHI media (400 µL final volume) and incubated as 100 µL reactions in triplicate in Greiner Bio-One CELLSTAR white flat bottom plates wrapped in parafilm. These plates were loaded into a TECAN Infinite 200 Pro and run using the programs specified in the Application Note collecting OD600 values every thirty minutes, except luminescence was also determined by adding a 500 ms luminescence acquisition with 100 ms delay at each time point. To screen the effect of the candidate probiotic bacterium in this assay, 10% of the media volume was replaced with either MRS broth (Difco) or MRS broth containing various concentrations of L. delbrueckii (CM80) cultures. Replacing 10% of the medium with MRS broth alone had no effect on MRSA growth (data not shown).

We then sought to determine whether co-incubation with L. delbrueckii could also inhibit the growth of E. coli. We employed a laboratory strain of E. coli (TOP10, Invitrogen) that was transformed with a previously published plasmid to express green fluorescent protein (GFP) [9]. The same co-culture assay was again performed as described above, wherein 2 × 105 fluorescent E. coli cells were co-incubated with varying quantities of CM80, with the only change being acquisition of fluorescence instead of luminescence. GFP acquisition was completed using the following settings: excitation 475, emission 520, top mode, optimal gain, number of flashes 10, settle time 5, lag time 0 microseconds, integration time 20 microseconds.

Statistical Tests:

Statistical significance was determined by using the Student’s t-test in MS Excel. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Lactobacillus delbrueckii inhibits the growth of MRSA

We first examined the effect of L. delbrueckii on the growth of Xen31 cells in our co-culture assay. The presence of CM80 resulted in decreased MRSA growth by the detection of bioluminescence (Fig. 1). CM80 on its own showed negligible growth under these co-culture conditions, and growth of an L. plantarum co-culture control had no effect on MRSA growth (data not shown). It appears that after approximately 3–5 h, some anti-Staphylococcal factor begins to decrease MRSA growth in a concentration-dependent manner. When approximately equal starting numbers of MRSA and CM80 are co-cultured, luminescence returns to control levels after approximately 10 h. However, at higher relative ratios of CM80 to MRSA, luminescence values remain depressed even after 13 h. We repeated this assay multiple times in triplicate and, at 13 h, we obtained colony-forming unit (cfu) counts to ensure that the decreased luciferase expression we observed was representative of an actual decrease in viable MRSA cell growth. This was done by serially diluting the co-culture samples in BHI media and plating on ChromAgar S. aureus plates. We found a > ten-fold reduction of MRSA in our co-culture model, confirming that the decreased luciferase activity we observed was in fact the result of greatly decreased bacterial growth (data not shown).

Lactobacillus delbrueckii inhibits the growth of E. coli

CM80 was then again used in our co-culture assays, but mixing fluorescent E. coli with increasing amounts of L. delbrueckii. As with MRSA, this resulted in decreased E. coli growth and accumulation, by the detection of total fluorescence (Fig. 2). Although GFP expression quickly saturates the assay at approximately 7 h when E. coli is grown by itself, the presence of 5-10-fold excess L. delbrueckii over E. coli results in a drop in fluorescence down to background levels. Again, this is a concentration- dependent phenomenon; at a 1:1 ratio of CM80/E. coli a lesser degree of inhibition was observed, but it is still present and strong. As with MRSA, we repeated this assay at 13 h and obtained cfu counts to ensure that the GFP expression was representative of E. coli growth. Colony plating of co-cultures with 5- and 10-fold excess CM80 showed essentially complete negation of E. coli growth in our co-culture model, with no detectable colonies at any dilution when samples were serially diluted and plated on LB agar plates plus chloramphenicol.

Pathogen inhibition by Lactobacillus delbrueckii is mediated by an induced peptide

The apparent delay in the onset of growth inhibition by CM80 led us to suspect that the bacterium was acting through an induced mediator, likely a bacteriocin peptide as has been described for other probiotic microbes [10]. We therefore next sought to determine if the induced anti-pathogenic agent was a peptide. We set up a co-culture of L. delbrueckii and MRSA in 100 mL LB media, collecting fractions every hour for 12 h; fractions were prepared by removing 1 mL of culture, centrifugation at 16,000*g for one minute, and the supernatant collected. Equal volumes of 50 µL supernatant (conditioned media) and 5*104 cfu MRSA in 50 µL 2X LB were then mixed and incubated at 37 °C, and assayed for a decrease in absorbance in a 96-well format. In this assay, reduced absorbance would be an indicator of inhibited bacterial growth.

As seen in Fig. 3A, fractions collected after 5–6 h of co-culture resulted in a large decrease of absorbance compared to MRSA-only control, indicating successful inhibition of pathogen growth by the conditioned media alone. The strongest fraction, fraction 6, was then pre-treated with Proteinase K (ProK) for 1 h at 50 °C (or left untreated as a control). Pre-treated and untreated control samples of fraction 6 were then again tested in the assay as described above. As seen in Fig. 3B, the proteinase K-treated fraction resulted in no impaired growth of MRSA, indicating that the induced agent is sensitive to proteolytic degradation, consistent with a bacteriocin peptide.

Discussion

Probiotic bacteria are of increasing interest as agents to advance human health in multiple disparate conditions [11]. We have previously shown that a probiotic bacterium (L. plantarum) can rescue a host from sepsis and death from P. aeruginosa infection following burn injury [6], but had little activity against other major pathogens, especially S. aureus. We were therefore interested to identify candidate probiotic bacterial species and strains that would have a broad range of anti-pathogen activity, encompassing both Gram-negative and Gram-positive organisms, so as to arrive at an improved probiotic therapy for burns and other wounds.

To accomplish this we have established a high-throughput assay using light-emitting reporter bacteria for the screening of novel candidate probiotic bacteria. This assay allows for rapid and inexpensive examination of a candidate bacterium’s inhibitory properties. We have used S. aureus and E. coli as the reporter organisms in this study, but other light-emitting pathogens of interest could be easily adopted to the same general protocol. The assay is equally useful for bioluminescent and fluorescent organisms. We have confirmed that the growth inhibition seen in our assay is truly the result of a reduction in viable bacteria (versus an attenuation of light-emission only) using conventional microbiological culture.

Our assay identified a novel strain of L. delbrueckii as a bacterium with both anti-Staphylococcal and anti-enteric pathogen properties. L. delbrueckii is commonly used in the dairy industry and has recently undergone extensive genomic characterization to identify the molecular pathways that may be critical for its probiotic activities [12]. One of these activities appears to be an induced protein bacteriocin that can inhibit the growth of MRSA bacteria independent of living bacterial cells.

Limitations

In this initial study we did not test conditioned medium from CM80 against E. coli, so it remains possible that L. delbrueckii inhibition of Gram-negative organisms proceeds via a different mechanism from S. aureus. It is also possible that L. delbrueckii can synthesize multiple bacteriocins against a range of pathogenic bacteria. Further studies will clarify these possibilities.

It is possible to contemplate the purification and characterization of the putative bacteriocin protein itself as a novel anti-infective agent, but in the setting of burn wound infection specifically the use of living probiotic bacteria still offers important advantages. Cultivation of large quantities of probiotic bacteria is cheap and readily accomplished. Application of the bacterial cells (as opposed to an individual bacteriocin protein) allows for multiple healthful pathways of action to manifest, not just the specific activity of the bacteriocin. Moreover, the burn wound milieu is replete with proteases that may be expected to quickly degrade an applied protein therapeutic, requiring repeated replenishment, whereas resident probiotic bacterial cells will persist and may propagate themselves after initial administration. For all the above reasons, the use of probiotic bacteria as an adjunctive therapy for burn wound infection (and other infections) remains an attractive option.
