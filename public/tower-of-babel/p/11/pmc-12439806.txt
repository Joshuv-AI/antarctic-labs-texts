X-CircuiT and conventional exercise in home-based cardiac rehabilitation for patients with coronary artery disease and risk factors: a randomized controlled non-inferiority trial
Authors: Xun Gong, Qing Ye, Wenliang Zhang, Yanan Shen, Zeng Cao, Yaoshan Dun, Suixin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study evaluated the effectiveness of Xiangya Hospital Circuit Training (X-CircuiT), a multi-component exercise program, as an alternative to conventional exercise (ConEx) in improving cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness within home-based cardiac rehabilitation (HBCR) for patients with coronary artery disease and risk factors (CADRF).Patients and MethodsThis randomized controlled non-inferiority trial consecutively enrolled patients with CADRF at Xiangya Hospital, China, between April and December 2020. Patients were assigned to a smartphone-guided X-CircuiT-HBCR or telephone call-guided ConEx-HBCR group for 12 weeks. Primary outcomes were cardiorespiratory fitness (peak oxygen uptake, V˙O2peak), and muscular fitness (30-sec arm curl and chair stand tests). The non-inferiority margins were 2.17 mL/kg/min, 2.70 reps, and 1.23 reps, respectively. Secondary outcomes included V˙O2 pulse, V˙O2 at ventilatory threshold, balance, flexibility, body mass index, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), self-efficacy, physical activity, safety, and exercise adherence.ResultsThis trial included 54 participants (median age 53 yr, 37% females), with 27 per group. Both groups showed significant improvements in fitness. There were no significant differences between groups in the improvements in V˙O2peak (mean difference, −0.13 [95%CI, −1.76 to 1.50] mL/kg/min), arm curl test (0.21 [−1.35 to 1.77] reps), and chair stand test (1.30 [−0.18 to 2.77] reps), with 95% CIs within the non-inferiority range. No significant differences were found in HRQoL, self-efficacy, and exercise adherence (all p > .05). No adverse events occurred.ConclusionsX-CircuiT is non-inferior to ConEx in HBCR for CADRF patients, suggesting it as an effective alternative or supplement in HBCR programs.Trial Registration NumberChiCTR2000032451

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of mortality worldwide. Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is a multidisciplinary and personalized approach that provides evidence-based secondary prevention therapies for patients with coronary artery disease and risk factors (CADRF) [1]. Despite the proven benefits of CR, participation and adherence rates among eligible patients remain suboptimal due to various barriers, including geographical, logistical and personal barriers [2–4]. In face of this, home-based CR (HBCR) programs, facilitated through smartphone and telemedicine platforms, have emerged as viable strategies to enhance patient participation and adherence [5]. These programs offer the flexibility to be implemented in nonclinical environments such as homes, community centers, and parks, mitigating geographical, logistical, and access-related challenges [6]. However, real-world implementation of telerehabilitation or remote HBCR also require consideration of several factors, including stakeholder engagement, infrastructure readiness, and tailored delivery strategies [7].

Exercise intervention is the cornerstone of the CR program. While there is extensive evidence supporting exercise programs for center-based CR, such programs for HBCR are comparatively limited [8,9]. The conventional exercise (ConEx) program commonly used in HBCR involves either a combined or isolated approach to aerobic and resistance exercises [10]. While these programs have demonstrated clinical efficacy, persistent limitations include exercise monotony, limited adaptability to individual patient needs, and reliance on specialized equipment [6]. Recent research has highlighted multi-component exercise as an alternative exercise program which could bring comparatively more effective results in improving cardiometabolic health, while improving overall exercise adherence [11]. Given the growing evidence, there is a need to develop and validate additional, alternative, or supplemental home-based exercise programs that can be remotely delivered, expanding the repertoire of effective options for HBCR.

To enhance physical fitness comprehensively, we developed a multi-component exercise program, named the Xiangya Hospital Circuit Training (X-CircuiT). This program comprises five distinct components: warm-up, aerobic training, Traditional Chinese Medicine-principle guided acupoint patting, elastic band resistance, and flexibility training. Each component is stratified into elementary, intermediate, and advanced stages for individuals with varying function capabilities [12]. X-CircuiT was shown to improve physical fitness in community-dwelling healthy older adults [13], and reverse frailty among community-dwelling older adults with pre-frailty [14]. Additionally, it offers unique advantages such as integrating traditional practices and a structured progression system, which showed good user engagement and adherence. To further enhance the feasibility and adherence of X-CircuiT in a home environment, we developed a smartphone application for X-CircuiT training. However, the potential of X-CircuiT as an alternative or supplemental exercise strategy within HBCR for patients with CADRF remains to be elucidated.

The study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of X-CircuiT as an alternative to ConEx in HBCR for improving cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness in patients with CADRF. We hypothesized that the smartphone-guided X-CircuiT would be non-inferior to conventional exercise programs within the HBCR context in improving cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness in patients with CADRF.

Patients and methods

Patients

Patients aged 25 to 80 with stable CAD and traditional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, family history, and smoking were recruited at the cardiac rehabilitation center of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, Changsha, China, between April 2020 and December 2020. Due to pandemic containment measures, the first participant was enrolled on April 30, 2020, nine days later than the recruitment start date specified in the registry. Exclusion criteria included: (1) absolute or relative contraindications to exercise training as described previously (e.g. recent change in the resting electrocardiogram suggesting significant ischemia, recent myocardial infarction, or other acute cardiac event, etc.) [15]; (2) high risk for cardiac events during exercise participation at home as described previously (e.g. presence of complex ventricular arrhythmias during exercise testing or recovery, etc.) [15]; (3) comorbidities affecting the three-month prognosis; (4) inability to exercise or conditions interfering with the exercise intervention. Detailed criteria are provided in Supplemental Material. The trial was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Furthermore, this trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (approval number 202004046), and all participants provided written informed consent. The trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR2000032451, Date of registration: 28 April 2020) and conducted at Xiangya Hospital, Central South University from April 2020 to April 2021. The study protocol is available in Supplemental Material.

Design and randomization

This study was a two-arm, parallel, randomized controlled non-inferiority trial. Participants were randomly assigned to either the X-CircuiT-HBCR group or the ConEx-HBCR group using a computer-generated sequence. Allocation was concealed using opaque, sealed, and sequentially numbered envelopes, administered by an independent researcher. While participants and physicians were aware of group assignments, outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded. Participants were instructed not to disclose their group allocation to the assessors. Three skilled cardiac rehabilitation specialists evaluated participants at baseline (week 0) and after the 12-week rehabilitation program. The study was adherent to CONSORT criteria of randomized clinical trials.

Intervention

Both groups underwent a 12-week comprehensive HBCR program, starting with one week in the hospital followed by 11 weeks at home. The program included educational sessions and exercise training conducted by a multidisciplinary team comprising physicians, dietitians, and cardiac rehabilitation specialists. Educational sessions covered self-management, nutritional counseling, physical activity counseling, psychological interventions, medications, and risk factor management. After one week of supervised exercises in the hospital, participants continued with home exercises at a similar intensity, performed three times/week for 11 weeks.

The ConEx protocol included aerobic and resistance training, based on the current guidelines [15,16], totaling 46 min per session, three sessions a week. Each session comprised a 5 min warm-up, 20 min aerobic exercise, 16 min resistance training with dumbbells, and 5 min cool-down. Exercise intensity was tailored to each participant, starting at 11–12 (light) and gradually increasing to 13–14 (somewhat hard) on the rate of perceived exertion scale or 40-60% peak heart rate. Each participant in the ConEx-HBCR group received regular guidance on exercise intensity and progression for their home exercises through three weekly telephone calls from a cardiac rehabilitation specialist.

The X-CircuiT protocol was conducted as previously described [12,14], with each session lasting 46 min. It included a 4.5 min warm-up, 6.5 min aerobic training, 6 min Traditional Chinese Medicine-principle guided acupoint patting, 15 min elastic band resistance, and 14 min flexibility training. After the first week, patients performed X-CircuiT at home utilizing a smartphone application developed by our team, which included instructional videos and difficulty settings (Supplementary Figure S1). Sessions were conducted three times a week with a day of rest in between. Three exercise intensities: elementary, intermediate, and advanced were set to accommodate different functional capacities, progressively increasing in complexity. Detailed information on the interventions is provided in Supplemental Material.

HBCR supervision and safety monitor

Before the exercise intervention, participants underwent comprehensive pre-screening, exclusion of those with absolute or relative contraindications to exercise training, and risk stratification according to established guidelines [15]. Exercise prescriptions were tailored to individual capacities and progressively adjusted based on participant feedback and physiological responses. During the intervention, heart rate and rating of perceived exertion were monitored for each session. Skilled cardiac rehabilitation specialists remotely supervised sessions via telephone and WeChat. They monitored participants’ self-reported heart rate and rating of perceived exertion score, providing personalized feedback and adjusting exercise intensity as needed. The research team offered immediate assistance for technical or health-related concerns, ensuring prompt issue resolution. All safety outcomes, including adverse events, were systematically documented throughout the study [6]. Moreover, previous studies have confirmed that remote cardiac rehabilitation is not only effective but also safe in long-term follow-up [17].

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included cardiorespiratory fitness measured through peak oxygen uptake (V˙O2peak), and muscular fitness (30-sec arm curl test and 30-sec chair stand test). Secondary outcomes included peak oxygen pulse, oxygen uptake at ventilatory threshold (V˙O2 at VT), balance fitness, flexibility fitness, body mass index (BMI), health-related quality of life (HRQoL), self-efficacy, physical activity, safety, and exercise adherence.

Assessments were conducted at baseline and after the 12-week intervention. Detailed procedures and assessment time points are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Cardiorespiratory fitness was measured via cardiopulmonary exercise testing using a cycle ergometer (Ergometer ERG 911 Plus) with a continuously incremented ramp protocol, in accordance with the American Heart Association’s Exercise Standards for Testing and Training [18]. (see Supplemental material: Section 7.1) Muscular fitness was evaluated using the 30-sec arm curl test and 30-sec chair stand test [19]. Balance fitness was assessed through the single leg stance test, functional reach test, and 2.4-m up-and-go test. Flexibility fitness was assessed using the back scratch test, modified trunk rotation test, and chair sit-and-reach test. Muscular, balance, and flexibility fitness tests were performed according to the Senior Fitness Test Manual (Second Edition) [20]. (see Supplemental material: Section 7.2) BMI was calculated using a TCS-200-RT electronic scale to measure height and weight. HRQoL was evaluated with the 12-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12) [21], while self-efficacy was measured using the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) [22]. Physical activity was assessed through the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF) [23].

Safety and exercise adherence were evaluated during follow-up. Exercise adherence was monitored and documented through weekly follow-ups via telephone and WeChat (a social media application). Follow-up plan

Three skilled cardiac rehabilitation specialists followed up with participants via telephone and WeChat. They evaluated health status, monitored exercise adherence and safety, and provided essential support and guidance. In the 11th week, participants were reminded of the final assessment scheduled for the 12th week.

Sample size

Considering clinical practice, the non-inferiority margin is set to be less than or equal to 50% of the difference between the ConEx-HBCR group and the control group without exercise intervention. For cardiorespiratory fitness, the X-CircuiT-HBCR program demonstrated a 25% improvement, with a V˙O2peak change of 0.46 ± 3.70 mL/kg/min in the control group[24] and 4.80 ± 3.70 mL/kg/min in the ConEx-HBCR group [25]. The non-inferiority margin for V˙O2peak was 2.17 mL/kg/min. With an α level of 0.05 (one-sided), a power of 80%, and a 10% dropout rate, 54 participants (27 per group) were required.

For muscular fitness, the change in the 30-sec arm curl test was −0.55 ± 3.36 reps for the control group [13] and 4.84 ± 3.23 reps for the ConEx-HBCR group [26]. The non-inferiority margin was 2.70 reps. With similar statistical parameters, 50 participants (25 per group) were required. Thus, to meet the statistical power for both fitness assessments, we included 27 participants per group, totaling 54 participants.

Statistics

Descriptive analysis of continuous variables was presented as median (IQR) and categorical variables as frequency (n) and percentage (%). Within-group differences were assessed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Independent t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests evaluated between-group differences in continuous variables, while Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. A generalized linear model was utilized to assess the noninferiority of the primary outcomes, taking into account the specified noninferiority margin. Per-protocol analysis was used to compare outcomes within and between groups. Intention-to-treat analysis was used for primary outcomes, assuming multivariate normal data distribution and random missing data, with multiple imputations applied to missing primary outcomes data [27].

Sensitivity analyses assessed the impact of attrition on primary outcomes, including best-case and worst-case scenario analyses for non-inferiority trials [28]. The best-case scenario analysis assumed favorable outcomes, applying the minimal clinically important difference for all participants lost to follow-up [29]. The minimal clinically important difference values for V˙O2peak, the 30-sec arm curl test, and the 30-sec chair stand test were 2.5 mL/kg/min [30], 2 reps [14], and 2 reps [14], respectively, calculated as half the standard deviation of the baseline measurements of participants [31]. The worst-case scenario analysis assumed unfavorable outcomes, assigning an improvement value of zero for all participants lost to follow-up [29]. Treatment effects were reported as between-group differences with 95% CI and probability values. X-CircuiT-HBCR was considered non-inferior if the lower limit of the two-sided 95% CI was within the predetermined non-inferiority margin [28]. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-sided). Statistical analyses were performed using JMP (Version 17.0) and R Statistical Software (Version 4.3.2).

Results

Trial flow

Out of 198 initially screened participants, 144 individuals were excluded: 90 refused to participate in CR, 11 preferred center-based CR, and 43 had a specific exercise program preference. Ultimately, 54 participants were included and randomly assigned to either the ConEx-HBCR group or the X-CircuiT-HBCR group. Each group had one participant drop out due to a preference for a different exercise program. Additionally, one participant from each group completed only the cardiorespiratory fitness assessment due to transportation constraints, and missing post-intervention assessments for muscular, balance, and flexibility fitness. The study flow chart is provided in Figure 1 and Supplementary Figure S2. Baseline characteristics, including demographics, clinical conditions, and fitness levels, were similar between the groups (Table 1).

Primary outcomes

After 12-week interventions, both the X-CircuiT-HBCR and ConEx-HBCR groups showed improvements in V˙O2peak, 30-sec arm curl test, and 30-sec chair stand test (Table 2). Comparisons between the groups revealed no statistical difference in V˙O2peak (mean difference, 0.12 [95%CI, −1.69 to 1.93] mL/kg/min, p = .898), the 30-sec arm curl test (0.56 [−1.27 to 2.39] reps, p = .541), and the 30-sec chair stand test (1.44 [−0.18 to 3.06] reps, p = .080).

In non-inferiority tests for the primary outcomes, including baseline-adjusted analyses, per-protocol analysis, intention-to-treat analysis, and sensitivity analysis, showed that the lower limit of the 95% CI of primary outcomes remained within the non-inferiority margin. This indicates that improvements in cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness in the X-CircuiT-HBCR group were not inferior to those in the ConEx-HBCR group (Figure 2).

Adverse events and adherence

No major adverse cardiovascular events were observed in either group during the study period. Reported symptoms included joint pain (17/54), dizziness (9/54), and chest discomfort (19/54), all of which were brief. Notably, chest discomfort did not occur during or within six hours after exercise, indicating it was not exercise-related. The ConEx-HBCR and X-CircuiT-HBCR groups completed 31 and 32 exercise sessions, respectively. The adherent rate was 72% in the ConEx-HBCR group and 80% in the X-CircuiT-HBCR group, with no significant differences in completed exercise sessions (p = .432) and adherence (p = .521).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the X-CircuiT program was not inferior to ConEx programs within HBCR for improving cardiorespiratory fitness and muscular fitness in patients with CADRF. This finding suggests that X-CircuiT may serve as an alternative or supplement to ConEx programs in HBCR for these patients.

X-CircuiT offers a unique, structured, multi-component exercise program developed by our team. It comprises warm-up, aerobic training, Traditional Chinese Medicine-principle guided acupoint patting, elastic band resistance, and flexibility training. This program effectively integrates modern exercise modalities with Traditional Chinese Medicine practices. X-CircuiT stands out for its simplicity and convenience, making it particularly suitable for home-based settings. Requiring only an elastic band, it is unaffected by weather, time constraints, location, or business travel. We have designed three exercise intensities to cater to patients with varying functional capacities, ensuring appropriate progression. Additionally, we developed a smartphone application for home exercise guidance, which enhances the standardization of the protocol. X-CircuiT includes a sitting position option for individuals with mobility impairments, broadening its accessibility [12–14]. Future studies should explore the application of X-CircuiT in people with different functional capacities and positions to further validate its efficacy and adaptability.

Cardiorespiratory fitness is a vital sign, with low levels linked to a higher risk of CAD and all-cause mortality [32,33]. ConEx programs in HBCR, which include aerobic and resistance exercises, have been extensively shown to enhance cardiorespiratory fitness, thereby lowering these risks [34–36]. Previous studies have demonstrated that multi-component exercise interventions can improve cardiorespiratory fitness, as assessed by the 2-min step test. This test is commonly used as an alternative to the 6-min walk test, especially in community settings and among older adults [37,38]. Our previous research found that X-CircuiT, a multi-component exercise program, significantly improved cardiorespiratory fitness, measured by 6-min walk test, in community-dwelling older adults without CAD [14]. In the present study, we used V˙O2peak, the gold standard measure of cardiorespiratory fitness, to evaluate this parameter directly. Following 12 weeks of intervention, patients in the X-CircuiT-HBCR group achieved significant improvements in V˙O2peak. Moreover, X-CircuiT was found to be non-inferior to ConEx in enhancing cardiorespiratory fitness among patients with CADRF. Our findings not only confirm that multi-component exercise intervention improve cardiorespiratory fitness, but also provide more precise and objective evidence supporting its effectiveness.

Overall improvement in physical fitness contributes to better long-term health outcomes [39]. Enhancements in muscular [40], balance [41], and flexibility fitness [42] have also been shown to be crucial for improving CAD risk factors and reducing the risk of CAD. This study demonstrated that X-CircuiT may also improve overall physical fitness in patients with CADRF. The observed improvements in 30-sec arm curl and chair stand tests in both the X-CircuiT-HBCR and ConEx-HBCR groups are notable, as they indicate that X-CircuiT can offer similar benefits to conventional models. This aligns with existing literature, which shows that structured multi-component exercise programs, when adequately supervised or guided, can achieve comparable outcomes in improving muscular fitness [43]. Notably, a recent study similarly demonstrated significant improvement in muscular fitness among community-dwelling older adults without CAD following a 12-week multi-component intervention [44]. Our results further support the effectiveness of multi-component exercise programs in improving muscular fitness across diverse populations and settings.

The results of this study suggest that the multi-component approach of X-CircuiT, incorporating both aerobic and resistance exercises, mirrors the benefits observed in ConEx. X-CircuiT incorporates elements of flexibility and Traditional Chinese Medicine, which may provide additional benefits for cardiorespiratory function. Furthermore, the lack of significant differences between the groups indicates that the integration of Traditional Chinese Medicine components, such as acupoint patting, does not detract from the effectiveness of the X-CircuiT program in improving physical fitness. Instead, these components may offer additional holistic benefits, such as stress reduction, which is not typically addressed in conventional exercise protocols [45]. Future studies should investigate whether these added components, such as acupoint patting, directly contribute to greater improvements in heart rate variability, respiratory function, or recovery time compared to conventional exercise programs. Poli et al. [46] observed no significant improvement in SF-12 scores following a 10-week multi-component exercise program consisting of aerobic exercise, resistance training, and flexibility training. In contrast, our study showed a significant improvement in SF-12 scores. This may be attributed to the inclusion of acupoint patting in X-CircuiT. As a component of Traditional Chinese Medicine, acupoint patting may help regulate autonomic nervous function and alleviate stress, thereby improving perceived quality of life [47]. This underscores the potential value of incorporating Traditional Chinese Medicine elements into modern multi-component exercise programs, offering a culturally tailored exercise program to enhance quality of life in patients with cardiovascular diseases undergoing CR.

Safety, convenience, and standardization are important considerations for home-based exercise programs [6,48]. During the study, no major adverse cardiovascular events were reported in either group. The exercise intervention was rigorously managed through pre-screening, remote monitoring, and routine follow-ups, with individualized exercise prescriptions. Participants also had direct support for technical or health concerns, ensuring safe and effective implementation. To represent a high-risk population, we included participants aged 75 and older, despite only one such participant, which precluded subgroup analysis. Older individuals face unique challenges with HBCR due to comorbidities, frailty, and resource limitations. Nonetheless, our previous research in pre-frail community-dwelling elderly (average age 72) demonstrated that X-CircuiT significantly reversed pre-frailty with high safety and compliance [14].

Furthermore, similarly to our previous research, we found X-CircuiT had achieved high adherence [14], this may be partly attributed the minimal equipment requirements for X-CircuiT, which could make it suitable for home-based environments. The smartphone application ensures standardization of home exercises and does not require direct guidance from cardiac rehabilitation specialists. Previous studies have demonstrated that HBCR based on information and communication technologies, such as smartphone application and web-based exercise platforms, is a cost-effective alternative to center-based CR [49,50]. The increasing use of artificial intelligence and devices like virtual reality in HBCR enhances patient participation and adherence by providing dynamic, real-time interactive communication and monitoring [51,52]. Future research should explore integrating X-CircuiT with artificial intelligence to investigate its potential in enhancing safety and adherence in HBCR.

This study has several limitations. First, the intervention period was only 12 weeks, allowing observation of only short-term effects. The sustainability of long-term effects remains to be observed. Second, the sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome measures, so caution is necessary when interpreting the secondary outcome measures. Third, the study aimed to validate the hypothesis that X-CircuiT is not inferior to ConEx within HBCR. This hypothesis relies on extensive research demonstrating that ConEx improves both short-term and long-term outcomes for patients with CADRF, leading to the decision not to include a blank control group. However, the lack of a control group means that other temporal and spatial factors could impact the results were not controlled for. Fourth, this study did not evaluate usability, which is crucial for assessing user experience and interface design of the application. Future research should include standardized usability assessments to evaluate user satisfaction and identify potential areas for optimization. Fifth, the study lacked objective, application-based engagement metrics. Future studies should incorporate objective tracking methods, such as application analytics or wearable devices, to more accurately measure user engagement. Sixth, digital inequity, including varying device access and digital literacy, may have affected participant engagement in HBCR programs. Although we provided comprehensive training, ongoing support, and a user-friendly application design to mitigate these issues, individual differences may still have introduced bias. Future research should consider alternative delivery approaches or stratify analyses by application usage metrics to better address digital barriers and improve accessibility.

Conclusion

Our results indicate that the X-CircuiT program is not inferior to ConEx programs within HBCR in improving cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness among patients with CADRF. No significant differences were observed in balance fitness, flexibility fitness, BMI, HRQoL, self-efficacy, and exercise adherence. X-CircuiT may serve as an effective alternative or supplemental exercise program within HBCR for this patient population.
