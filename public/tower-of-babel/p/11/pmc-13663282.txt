Subtypes and associated factors of prenatal depressive symptoms and their association with health-related quality of life among pregnant women: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lanting Huo, Xingfeng Yu, Anum Nisar, Yujia Ma, Yiping Nan, Lei Yang, Xiaowei Li, Xiaomei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPregnant women with prenatal depression could have distinctly heterogeneous constellations of symptoms. Classifying prenatal depressive symptoms into different subtypes using a person-centered approach and investigating their associated factors are beneficial for personalized assessment and management of the disorder. However, relevant evidence is still lacking.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in the obstetrics outpatient clinics of two university-affiliated, governmental, tertiary hospitals in Xi’an City, Northwestern China. A total of 1020 pregnant women were included in the study and administered with a researcher-designed sociodemographic, health-related and pregnancy-related information sheet, the 8-item Patient Health Questionnaire, the five-level EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 and the Pregnancy Stress Rating Scale. Latent profile analysis was used to classify prenatal depressive symptoms into different subtypes, while a multivariate binomial logistic regression model was employed to identify the associated factors of the different subtypes. Health-related quality of life, prenatal anxiety and stress were compared across subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms.ResultsPrenatal depressive symptoms were classified into three subtypes, including ‘minimal-to-mild symptoms’ (58.6%), ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ (17.1%) and ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ (24.3%). Multiple sociodemographic, health-related and pregnancy-related factors were associated with the subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms. Moreover, health-related quality of life, prenatal anxiety and stress differed significantly across subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms.ConclusionsPregnant women with depressive symptoms could be classified into distinct subgroups characterized by heterogeneous clinical manifestations. With further validation, investigating the relevant associated factors may become a useful addition to routine prenatal healthcare, facilitating the identification of women at risk of different subtypes and the implementation of early individualized management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09634-9.

Background

Gestation is a stressful life event that may contribute to various psychological problems to pregnant women, in both direct and indirect (via physiological and social pathways) manners. Evidence suggests that approximately one-quarter of women experience at least one psychological disorder during pregnancy [1, 2]. Among the common prenatal psychological disorders, depression is particularly important because of its high prevalence and far-reaching detrimental effects. Systematic reviews with meta-analysis suggest that the prevalence of prenatal depression in pregnant women may exceed 20% [3, 4]. Prenatal depression is associated with a variety of adverse outcomes, such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, fetal growth restrication, low birth weight, preterm birth, miscarriage and stillbirth [5–8]. It is also associated with long-term consequences, including long-lasting mental problems in mothers, poor growth and development in offspring, and impaired mother-baby bonding [9, 10].

Despite the growing body of evidence regarding prenatal depression, a long-standing challenge in the field is the considerable heterogeneity in the clinical presentation of the condition [11]. Specifically, pregnant women with prenatal depression may differ substantially in clinical presentation, disease course, longitudinal trajectory, and treatment responses [12–14]. A key explanation for such diversity or heterogeneity is that depression is a polythetic definition, meaning that individuals need to meet only a subset, rather than all, of the disgnostic criteria [14]. As such, behind a uniform diagnosis of prenatal depression, there are distinct subgroups with different constellations of the diagnostic and non-diagnostic symptoms [11]. Therefore, clustering pregnant women into homogeneous subgroups/subtypes is fundamental for personalized prevention and management of prenatal depression and for improving maternal and offspring well-being.

However, existing studies have predominantly adopted variable-centered clustering approaches to examine the subtypes of depression. Variable-centered approaches examine the relationships among variables under the assumption that the relationships between these variables are homogenous in a population, an assumption that has increasingly been challenged [15, 16]. Variable-centered approaches are also criticized for overemphasizing overall scores and averaged parameter variables, but neglecting the diverse combinations of individual symptoms [17–19]. By contrast, person-centered clustering approaches assume that individuals are heterogeneous concerning the variables in a population, and hence, enable the identification of meaningful subgroups that differ in their symptom profiles [20].

Among the typical person-centered clustering methods, such as latent class analysis, K-means and hierarchical clustering, the model-based latent profile analysis (LPA) is advantageous as it not only minimizes within-group and maximizes between-group indicator variability but also evaluates the accuracy and validity of classification based on objective statistical indicators [21]. LPA allocates samples into different profiles according to individuals’ responses to a set of variables using maximum likelihood estimations [22]. LPA has been utilized to explore the subtypes of various psychosocial problems, including perinatal depressive symptoms [11, 23–26]. However, to the best of our knowledge, existing studies have primarily focused on the subtypes of postnatal or perinatal depressive symptoms, leaving the subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms largely unexplored [27–29]. Identifying the factors associated with different subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms could facilitate the recognition of individuals vulnerable to specific subtypes, while health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is an important health outcome [30]. Although the factors associated with prenatal depressive symptoms and the correlation between the condition and HRQoL have been well-documented [3, 4, 31–33], whether these assoications differ across subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms remains unclear.

Given the limited evidence on the subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms, their associated factors and their relationship with HRQoL, this study aimed to identify distinct prenatal depressive symptom subtypes using the LPA, examine their associated sociodemographic, health-related and pregnancy-related factors, and compare HRQoL, prenatal anxiety and stress across the identified subtypes.

Methods

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [34].

Study design, setting and participants

A cross-sectional field survey was conducted in two university-affiliated, governmental, tertiary hospitals in Xi’an, Northwestern China, from July to August 2020. The target population comprised pregnant women attending routine prenatal care at the obstetrics outpatient clinics of the research sites. The inclusion criteria included: (1) aged ≥ 18 years; (2) without a clinical diagnosis of psychological illness; (3) with sufficient communication and interpretation ability; (4) consent to participate in this study. Pregnant women were excluded if they were participating in other studies. Using convience sample, eligible pregnant women were consecutively approached for participation during the study period. Based on the recommendation by Nylund et al., a minimum sample size of 500 is considered sufficient to identify the appropriate latent profiles. In the current study, 1020 participants were recruited [35].

Variables and measurements

Sociodemographic, health-related and pregnancy-related variables

Based on a comprehensive review of literature on factors associated with prenatal depressive symptoms, a researcher-designed sociodemographic, health-related and pregnancy-related information sheet was preliminarily developed. Each item in the preliminary instrument was evaluated for relevance using a four-point Likert scale ragning from 1 (‘highly irrelevant’) to 4 (‘highly relevant’) by an expert panel consisting of two obstetricians, two maternity nurses, one clinical psychologist and one senior researcher in perinatal psychological well-being. All experts rated each item as 3 or 4 point, indication satisfactory content validity. Following the expert review, one obstetrician suggested including history of gynecological diseases, dysmenorrhea and monocytosis. The final variables included age, educational level, employment status, monthly household income, relationship with partner and mother-in-law, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), history of gynecological diseases and dysmenorrhea, stage of pregnancy, gravidity, unplanned pregnancy, severity of vomiting symptoms, abnormal pregnancy indicators, and monocytosis.

Prenatal depressive symptoms

The 8-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8) was used to assess prenatal depressive symptoms. The PHQ-8, a shortened version of the PHQ-9, encompasses eight of the nine diagnostic criteria for major depression defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders - fifth Edition (DSM-5), with the item assessing suicidal and/or self-harm ideation in the PHQ-9 omitted because self-harm intention is not included in the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria [36, 37]. The PHQ-8 assesses the participants’ depressive symptoms during the past two weeks. Each item was scored on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), yielding a total score range of 0–24, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. A total PHQ-8 score of 0–4, 5–9, 10–14 and ≥ 15 was defined as minimal, mild, moderate and severe depression. The PHQ-8 has shown good reliability and validity among pregnant women [38].

Health-related quality of life

The five-level EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D-5 L) was used to assess HRQoL. The instrument includes a short descriptive questionnaire (with five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression) and a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) [39]. A utility index (UI) score can be calculated from the item scores by referring to the values of preference weights calculated for the Chinese general population [40]. The VAS provides an alternative to elicit an individual’s subjective rating of their overall well-being. The EQ-5D-5 L has exhibited good psychometric properties in pregnant women [40].

Prenatal anxiety

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale, developed by Spitzer et al. in 2006 [41], was used to assess prenatal anxiety. Rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘not at all’) to 3 (‘nearly every day’), each item of the GAD-7 assesses one of the typical anxiety symptoms the participants may have experienced during the past two weeks. The total score ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating worse conditions. The GAD-7 has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity in pregnant women [42].

Pregnancy stress

The 30-item Pregnancy Stress Rating Scale (PSRS) was used to assess pregnancy stress. The scale was developed by Chen et al. in 1983 [43]. It consists of three dimensions, including stress regarding maternal and infant health and safety, stress regarding maternal role identification, and stress regarding altered physical appearance and function. Items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (definitely no) to 3 (very severe), which could generate a total score of 0–90. Higher scores represent higher levels of perceived stress. The original Chinese version of the PSRS had good psychometric properties [44].

Procedures

Upon identifying a potentially eligible participant, the principal investigator explained the aims and procedures of the study and invited the pregnant woman to participate. Interested participants were asked to provide written informed consent and were subsequently administered with the research questionnaires. Using individual face-to-face interviews, the principal investigator asked the questions following the protocol and recorded the participants’ responses in the printed survey questionnaires. All data collection was completed by the principal investigator, thereby maximizing the consistency of the collected data.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Health Science Center, Xi’an Jiaotong University (reference identifier: 2020 − 1373) and permissions were obtained from the participating hospitals. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants before data collection. Participants’ rights and safety were protected by adhering to national laws, local regulations, the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional policies.

Statistical analysis plan

The IBM SPSS v22.0 and Mplus v8.3 were used for data analysis. Continuous data with normal distribution were presented as means and standard deviations, whereas categorical data as counts and percentages. Latent profile analysis was used to categorize the participants into different subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms based on the heterogeneous patterns of the eight symptoms. Following the guidelines from Nylund et al. [35], our research team specified two potential profiles and subsequently increased the number of profiles till the model fitness reached an optimal level. Eight coefficients were used in combination to examine model fitness, including log-likelihood (LL), Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size-adjusted BIC (SSA-BIC), Entropy, Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMRT), bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and the relative frequency of the smallest class [45]. A superior model would have smaller LL, AIC, BIC and SSA-BIC values, an Entropy value of > 0.8, significant results of LMRT and BLRT, and a relative frequency of the smallest class value of ≥ 5%.

A two-step procedure was employed to investigate the associated factors of different subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms. Analysis of variance (ANOVA), the Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were employed as univariate analytical strategies to explore the differences in sociodemographic, health-related, and pregnancy-related variables across subtypes. A multinomial logistic regression model was developed to identify the factors associated with different subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms. To avoid incorrectly excluding potentially important variable when the relationship between an associated factor and the prenatal depressive symptoms subtypes is confounded by other variables, the significant level for the univariate analysis was set at α = 0.1 [46]. By contrast, the statistically significant level for multivariate analysis was set at α = 0.05. The ANOVA with post-hoc multiple comparisons was used to compare the differences in HRQoL, prenatal anxiety and stress across profiles, with a statistically significant level of α = 0.05.

Results

Of the 1465 pregnant women who were invited for participation, 1020 agreed and completed the survey questionnaires. The majority of the participants were aged 23–35 years, with a mean age of 30.09 ± 3.48 years. Slightly more than half of the participants were in the third trimester, and two-thirds of them were primiparous. Participants with unplanned pregnancies accounted for 42.8% of the total sample. Regarding prenatal psychological characteristics, 55.7% and 2.8% of the participants had mild-to-moderate or major depression (PHQ-8 total score of 5–14 and ≥ 15, respectively). A total of 264 (25.9%) and 12 (1.2%) participants had mild-to-moderate or major anxiety (GAD-7 total score of 6–15 and ≥ 16, respectively). Mild or moderate levels of stress were observed in 88.2% of the sample.

The latent profile analysis generated four latent profile models, with the number of profiles ranging from two to five (Table 2). Given the non-statistically LMRT result, Model I was first excluded. Althoug Models III and IV showed lower LL, AIC, BIC and SSA-BIC and higher Enttopy values, they were excluded because the smallest class in both models accounted for less than 5% of the sample. Therefore, a three-profile model (model II) was selected.

Distinctly heterogeneous depressive symptoms were observed across profiles. As is shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1, profile 1 has the lowest scores in all symptoms, and was hence labeled as the ‘minimal-to-mild symptoms’ subtype. Profile 2 is characterized by its highest scores in the symptoms of sleep problems, low energy, appetite change and concentration, and was therefore labeled as the ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ subtype. By contrast, profile 3 was labeled as the ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ subtype, considering its highest scores in the symptoms of depressed mood, low self-esteem and psychomotor problems. The three profiles accounted for 58.6%, 17.1% and 24.3% of the total sample, respectively.

Concerning the severity of prenatal depression, minimal depression (PHQ-8 total score of 0–4) accounts for the majority (67.9%) the ‘minimal-to-mild symptoms’ subtype, while mild and severer levels of depression (PHQ-8 total score of ≥ 5) dominate the ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ (100%) and ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ (94.0%) subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Associated factors of different prenatal depressive symptoms profiles

Univariate analyses demonstrated that employment status, income level, relationship with partner, relationship with mother-in-law, history of dysmenorrhea, stage of pregnancy, unplanned pregnancy, abnormal pregnancy indicators and severity of vomiting were associated with different subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms, at a significant level of α = 0.1 (Table 1).

The results of the multivariate multinomial logistic regression model are presented in Table 3. The analysis showed that an antecedent history of dysmenorrhea (odds ratio: 2.08, 95% confidence interval: 1.38–3.13) and earlier stages of pregnancy (odds ratio: 3.63, 95% confidence interval: 2.12–6.20 for ‘3–6 months’ vs. ‘> 6 months’; odds ratio: 1.71, 95% confidence interval: 1.07–2.74 for ‘< 3 month’ vs. ‘> 6 months’) were risk factors, while a close relationship with the mother-in-law (odds ratio: 0.54, 95% confidence interval: 0.29–0.99, vs. ‘general’) was a protective factor for the ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ subtype (Table 3). Meanwhile, a lower income level (odds ratio: 3.70, 95% confidence interval: 1.62–8.46 for ‘< 3000 Chinese Yuan per month’ vs. ‘> 5000 Chinese Yuan per month’), an antecedent history of dysmenorrhea (odds ratio: 1.80, 95% confidence interval: 1.25–2.59), an earlier stage of pregnancy (odds ratio: 1.99, 95% confidence interval: 1.18–3.34 for ‘3–6 months’ vs. ‘> 6 months’), unplanned pregnancy (odds ratio: 1.65, 95% confidence interval: 1.15–2.38), and abnormal pregnancy indicators (odds ratio: 3.69, 95% confidence interval: 1.17–11.64) were risk factors, while being employed (odds ratio: 0.64, 95% confidence interval: 0.42–0.97) was a protective factor for the ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ subtype.

Furthermore, compared with those in the ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ group, participants in the ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ group had significantly lower EQ-5D-UI (mean difference: -0.02, 95% confidence interval: -0.04-0.00) and EQ-5D-VAS (mean difference: -3.05, 95% confidence interval: -4.96–-1.14) scores as well as a significantly higher GAD-7 score (mean difference: 1.02, 95% confidence interval: 0.42–1.63), indicating worse HRQoL and higher levels of prenatal anxiety in the latter group.

Discussion

Because of the complexity of the pathophysiology and the diversity of the symptoms among individuals with prenatal depression, it is important to classify prenatal depressive symptoms into different subtypes. In the current study, prenatal depressive symptoms were classified into three subtypes using latent profile analysis, including the ‘minimal-to-mild symptoms’ (58.6%), ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ (17.1%) and ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ (24.3%) subtypes. Multiple sociodemographic, health-related and pregnancy-related factors were found to be associated with these subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms. Moreover, HRQoL, prenatal anxiety and stress also differed significantly across the subtypes.

Prenatal depressive symptoms could be classified into three distinct subtypes

Prenatal depressive symptoms were classified into three subtypes, namely the ‘minimal-to-mild symptoms’, ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ and ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ subtypes. Although no existing study has clustered prenatal depressive symptoms using a person-centered approach, the findings of the current study were similar to previous studies on perinatal depression [28, 29]. It is worth noting that the item scores for sleep problems, low energy and appetite change were the highest across all subtypes, which was congruent with the existing evidence [47, 48]. This might be partly explained by the shared manifestations of depressive disorders and normal pregnancy, during which the natural physiological changes of several hormones, such as increased levels of estrogen, progesterone and cortisol, would raise these symptoms [49]. This interpretation is futher supported by a recent perinatal hormonal change stimulation study, in which lethargy, fatigue and appetite change were among the earliest observed symptoms among participants [50].

Notably, the prevalence of clinical depression (PHQ-8 total score of ≥ 10) was significantly higher in the ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ group compared to the ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ group (Supplementary Fig. 1). One possible explanation for this findings is that the two subtypes are characterized by different dominant symptom profiles. The ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ subtype is characterized by high scores in depressed mood and anhedonia, which are the two core symptoms required for the clinical diagnosis of major depressive disorder according to the DSM-5 criteria [51]. Such findings indicate that pregnant women with different constellations of depressive symptoms have different severity of psychological problems, which further highlights the value of classifying prenatal depressive symptoms into different subtypes in maintaining perinatal psychological well-being.

Multiple sociodemographic, health-related and pregnancy-related factors could be associated with the subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms

Sociodemographic and health-related factors

The result of this study suggested that unemployed or low-income pregnant women are more likely to have ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’. Existing evidence consistently supports the associations of unemployment and low income with an increased risk of prenatal depression [3, 52]. Some researchers suggest that housewives are more likely to develop prenatal anxiety and stress, which shared overlapping melancholic symptoms with depression [53]. Furhtermore, unemployment and low income could result in greater economic burdens, more family conflicts and less social interactions, which might further lead to melancholic depressive symptoms [53, 54]. Pregnant women who maintained a close relationship with their mothers-in-law seem less likely to have ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ in the current study. A good relationship with the mother-in-law has been frequently reported to be associated with lower levels of depression among Chinese pregnant women because mothers-in-law generally play important roles in the perinatal period of Chinese women [28, 31, 55]. Wang et al. reported that having the grandmother identified as the primary family caregiver was associated with a higher level of perceived family support in Chinese pregnant women and new mothers, which might be considered as a possible underlying mechanism [56]. However, cautions should be taken when generalizing this finding to other countries as such result is rooted in the Chinese familistic culture.

It was found in the current study that pregnant women who had an antecedent history of gynecologic diseases are more likely to experience mild-to-moderate levels of both psychosomatic and melancholic depressive symptoms. Although relevant evidence is lacking, pregnant women may worry that a history of gynecologic diseases could cause adverse pregnancy outcomes, which would consequently lead to psychological burdens. Nevertheless, due to the lack of evidence, a firm conclusion regarding the association between a history of gynecologic diseases and prenatal depression cannot be drawn until further evidence becomes available.

Pregnancy-related factors

This study found that pregnant women at the earlier stages of pregnancy are more vulnerable to mild-to-moderate levels of both psychosomatic and melancholic depressive symptoms than those in the third trimester. Although a systematic review suggests that the pooled prevalence of prenatal depression was the highest in studies that recruited pregnant women in the third trimester [3], recent longitudinal evidence supports the findings of the current study [57]. To clarify the association between the stage of pregnancy and the subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms, more empirical evidence is desirable. The gradually decreasing trend in depressive symptoms in the current study could be interpreted as reflecting the gradual adaptation to the stresses of pregnancy. Women with unplanned pregnancies or abnormal pregnancy indicators were found to have a higher risk of experiencing ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ in this study, which is supported by existing evidence [3, 31, 58, 59]. In general, people find it more difficult to cope with unexpected and undesired events [60]. Similarly, pregnant women might be more likely to experience psychological problems following an unplanned pregnancy. It is likely that pregnant women would be experiencing psychological distress when encountering obstetric complications, considering the adverse influences on themselves and their offspring [61].

Level of HRQoL, prenatal anxiety and stress differs across the subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms

HRQoL is a multidimensional concept that reflects the overall well-being of human beings, while prenatal anxiety and stress are common companying psychological problems of prenatal depressive symptoms. The results of this study suggested that pregnant women in the ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ and ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ groups tended to have poorer HRQoL as well as higher levels of prenatal anxiety and stress compared to those in the ‘minimal-to-mild symptoms’ group. The close associations between HRQoL, prenatal anxiety and stress, and prenatal depressive symptoms have been frequently reported in existing studies [4, 62, 63]. However, there are substantial overlaps among measures of depressive symptoms, anxiety and stress, which might have influenced the associations between the prenatal depressive symptoms subtypes and these outcome variables. Further study could use bifactor modelling to quantify the disorder-specific associations. The results also showed that pregnant women suffering from ‘mild-to-moderate melancholic symptoms’ had worse HRQoL and a higher level of prenatal anxiety than those belonging to the ‘mild-to-moderate psychosomatic symptoms’ group. Such results are consistent with the proposed differentiated severity of depressive symptoms across subtypes and, to some extent, provide evidence supporting the internal validity of the originally proposed latent profiles.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to classify prenatal depressive symptoms using a person-centered approach, which contributes novel evidence to the existing body of knowledge. Moreover, several sociodemographic, health-related and pregnancy-related factors associated with different subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms were identified, which may facilitate the early identification of high-risk individuals. Finally, HRQoL, prenatal anxiety and stress were compared across subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms, providing evidence for the internal validity of the proposed latent profiles of prenatal depressive symptoms.

Despite its strengths, the findings of the current study should be interpreted with caution in light of the following limitations. First, even though the sample size is relatively large, the research participants were recruited from two local university-affiliated, governmental, tertiary hospitals using a convenience sampling approach, which could have limited the representativeness of the research participants, and as a result, the generalizability of the findings to other regions and primary healthcare settings may be limited. Second, a large proportion of the research variables were measured in a subjective manner, which might result in reporting bias. Third, as part of a doctorate dissertation, the data collection of this cross-sectional study was limited to July to August (summer days), 2020 as scheduled. As a result, the potential influences of seasonality on the results remain uncertain until further evidence becomes available [64]. Fourth, due to the nature of a cross-sectional design, the causal relationship between the associated factors and subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms was not established. In addition, due to the lack of existing studies on the subtypes of prenatal depressive symptoms, the attempt to compare our findings with those from other populations could not be achieved, and as a result, the impacts of cultural context on the classification of prenatal depressive symptoms subtypes and their associated factors remain unclear. Finally, external validation was not performed in this study, which could downgrade the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, further studies are encouraged to validate the findings of this study in broader regions and clinical settings.

Conclusions

Pregnant women with depressive symptoms could be classified into distinct subgroups characterized by heterogeneous clinical manifestations. With further valiation, investigating the relevant associated factors may become a useful addition to routine prenatal healthcare, facilitating the identification of women at risk of different subtypes and the implementation of early individualized management.
