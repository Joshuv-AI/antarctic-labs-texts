Roxadustat alleviates cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury by regulating fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial function
Authors: Xialin Li, Yan Hu, Jinqing Li, Yingfeng Shi, Qin Zhong, Xiaoyan Ma, Daofang Jiang, Shasha Zhang, Shougang Zhuang, Na Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to elucidate the role and function of roxadustat, a small molecule hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) stabilizer, on cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) in vivo and in vitro.MethodsWe investigated the effects of roxadustat (10 mg/kg) in a cisplatin-induced AKI model in mice. After a 48-hour pretreatment with the intraperitoneal injection of roxadustat, cisplatin was administered at a dose of 20 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection. We also examined the role of roxadustat (10 μM) on HK-2 cells stimulated by cisplatin (20 μg/mL).ResultsPretreatment with roxadustat significantly improved kidney function in the AKI model, as evidenced by reductions of Scr and BUN, and alleviated cisplatin-induced kidney morphology lesions. Roxadustat also remarkably decreased the expression of KIM-1 and NGAL. Furthermore, roxadustat significantly abolished the expression of Bax and cleaved caspase 3, two apoptosis marker proteins. Mechanistically, our study demonstrated that roxadustat could alleviate the deficiency of fatty acid oxidation (FAO) induced by cisplatin, as evidenced by the improvement of lipid accumulation and metabolic disorders in the roxadustat-treated group. Moreover, roxadustat effectively blocked mitochondrial damage, as evidenced by reduced mitochondrial swelling and fragmentation in the roxadustat-treated group. Simultaneously, roxadustat also partially rescued the expression of mitochondrial fusion proteins, including OPA1, MFN1, and MFN2. In vitro, roxadustat also effectively alleviate injury, apoptotic responses, FAO disorders, mitochondrial damage caused by cisplatin.ConclusionWe demonstrated that roxadustat exerts a protective role in cisplatin-treated mice and HK-2 cells, which might be due to amelioration of FAO deficiency and suppression of mitochondrial damage.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, roxadustat, fatty acid oxidation, mitochondrial function, cisplatin

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI), a severe clinical syndrome, occurs in approximately half of the hospitalized patients worldwide, and is associated with high mortality rates [1,2]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that AKI is responsible for about 1.7 million deaths annually [3]. Without effective therapies, patients who survive the AKI remain at a high risk of developing chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1,2,4]. In hospital-acquired situations, AKI typically results from sepsis, surgery or drugs [2]. Among these, chemotherapeutic drugs (e.g. cisplatin) that have been linked to the development of AKI deserve our attention. Cisplatin is a widely used chemotherapeutic agent to treat various solid tumors [5–9], and has many side effects, including dose-dependent nephrotoxicity, which is linked to the development of AKI [10–12]. Thus, the prevention of cisplatin-induced AKI is of great clinical significance. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unveiled, necessitating further studies to develop new therapies for cisplatin-induced AKI.

The pathogenesis of AKI is complex and remains incompletely understood. Mounting publications revealed that inflammation plays a crucial role during AKI, including increased levels of cytokines and chemokines [13], activation of MAPK, AMPK/SIRT1/NF-κB, and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways following cisplatin treatment [14]. Additionally, cisplatin also has been demonstrated to induce oxidative stress by enhancing the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and upregulating the expression of NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) [15]. Moreover, autophagy and apoptosis were two important mechanisms in developing AKI. While autophagy is regarded as a positive factor following cisplatin treatment, apoptosis, in which proximal tubular cells become central events in the development of various kidney diseases to maintain cellular homeostasis, is a negative factor in AKI [16,17]. It is noteworthy that the administration of QPI-1002, an apoptosis inhibitor, enhances the protective effects during cisplatin-induced AKI [18]. In addition, a number of further mechanisms have been proven to happen during cisplatin-induced AKI in recent years, including alterations in FAO and mitochondrial dysfunction.

Emerging evidence has demonstrated that FAO disorders occur in AKI, which is characterized by lipid accumulation and energy deficiency [19–21]. Long-chain fatty acids need to be transported into the mitochondria for subsequent catabolism. Carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1A (CPT1A), an enzyme that combines fatty acids with carnitine, holds a dominant function in this process [19]. Furthermore, peroxisomal acyl-coenzyme A oxidase 1 (ACOX1) represents a crucial downstream factor that is highly expressed under normal conditions [22]. FAO primarily occurs in mitochondrion [23], the indispensable organelles for the production of energy.

Another important mechanism underlying cisplatin-induced AKI is mitochondrial dysfunction. It is known that the kidney consumes 10% of the oxygen supply in the body to perform its functions, owing to the high energy demand for reabsorption and secretion, which predominantly occur in proximal tubule epithelial cells [21,22]. This process mainly occurs within the mitochondria. Thus, mitochondria damage and dysfunction are regarded as the leading causes of acute kidney injury. Mitochondrial impairment in morphology and dynamics was observed in cases of cisplatin-induced kidney damage. The NAD+-dependent SIRT family deacetylases, especially the mitochondrial matrix-residing SIRT3 protein, play a vital role in regulating mitochondrial dynamics [24]. SIRT3-dependent inhibition increases expression of the fusion-promoting protein optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) [25]. OPA1 is responsible for the fusion of mitochondrial inner membrane (MIM). Mitofusin 1 (MFN1) and mitofusin 2 (MFN2) are localized in the mitochondrial outer membrane (MOM) [23]. OPA1, in conjunction with MFNs, has been demonstrated to facilitate mitochondrial fusion and other functions. Moreover, this homeostasis can be modified by p53 [24].

Roxadustat can stabilize the level of HIF by restraining prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD) enzymes. Emerging evidence has demonstrated that roxadustat also efficiently decelerated the progression of fibrosis in vivo animal models [26,27]. Moreover, recent publications indicated the therapeutic effects of roxadustat in AKI. In a contrast-induced AKI animal model, pretreatment with roxadustat significantly increased the level of BNIP3-mediated autophagy and mitigated kidney injury [28]. In addition, roxadustat effectively enhanced ferroptosis via Akt/GSK-3β/Nrf2 pathways in folic acid-induced AKI, thereby facilitating the recovery of kidney function [27]. Roxadustat is reported to ameliorate cisplatin-induced AKI by mitigating inflammation and tubular cell apoptosis in the kidney [29]. Another paper denoted that the reno-protective effects of roxadustat in the I/R-induced AKI murine model depended on the suppression of mitochondrial dysfunction [30]. Nevertheless, the specific mechanisms by which roxadustat alleviates the AKI are still unclear.

Here, we explored the role of roxadustat using both in vivo and in vitro experiments during cisplatin-induced AKI. Notably, we provided evidence that roxadustat restored lipid metabolic homeostasis through upregulation of pivotal enzymes, thereby mitigating intracellular lipid deposition. Furthermore, roxadustat preserved mitochondrial dynamics by rescuing cisplatin-induced downregulation of mitochondrial fusion-related proteins.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and reagents

Roxadustat was purchased from Selleckchem (Houston, TX, USA). The primary antibodies used in this study are as follows: anti-kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1; R&D Systems; BAF1817), anti-cleaved caspase 3 (Cell Signaling Technology; #9664), anti-Bcl-2 associated X protein (Bax; BD Bioscience; 556467), anti-neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL; Santa Cruz Biotechnology; sc-515876), anti-CPT1A (Abcam; ab128568), anti-HIF-1α (Abcam; ab228649), anti-p53 (ABclonal Technology; A3185), anti-MFN2 (ABclonal Technology; A19678), anti-MFN1 (ABclonal Technology; A21293), anti-ACOX1 (Proteintech; 10957-1-AP), anti-OPA1 (Proteintech; 27733-1-AP), anti-β-actin (Sangon; D190826-0100), anti-TNF-α (Servicebio; GB11188) anti-IL-18 (ABclonal Technology; A16737) anti-IL-1β (Servicebio; GB11113). Secondary antibodies, including anti-mouse (A0216) and anti-rabbit (A0208), were sourced from the Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (WB3100), protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P002), enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (P10300), and penicillin-streptomycin (C100C5) were provided by NCM Biotech (Suzhou, China). Immobilon-P polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (ISEQ00010, 0.22 μm) were obtained from Millipore (Billerica, USA) for protein immunoblotting. Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with F12 was provided by Life-iLab Biotech (Shanghai, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS; F101-01) was sourced from Vazyme Biotech (Nanjing, China). Serum creatinine (Scr) (Cat: C011-2-1), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (Cat: C013-2-1), reduced glutathione (GSH) (Cat: A006-2-1) levels were quantified using commercial kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Malondialdehyde (MDA) (Cat: S0131S) and Oil red O staining (ORO) kit (Cat: C0158S) were acquired from Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Histopathological analyses utilized periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining (Cat: G1008) and hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining kits (Cat: G1005) from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Isoflurane (R510-22-10), which was used for anesthesia, was provided by RWD Life Science (Shenzhen, China).

Animal experiments

Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Tongji University (No. TJBB00624103). C57BL/6J male mice weighting 20–25 g were obtained from Huachuang sino (Taizhou, China) and were used in this study. All mice are provided with adequate food and water, and live under 12-h light-dark cycle. All mice were acclimated to this environment for 7 days before experiments. To explore appropriate drug concentration, mice were treated with four doses of roxadustat (0, 1, 5, or 10 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection before cisplatin administration (20 mg/kg). To investigate the effect of roxadustat, mice were divided into 4 groups: (1) Sham group, intraperitoneally injected with an equal volume of saline and DMSO (n = 6); (2) Sham + roxadustat group, intraperitoneally injected with an equal volume of saline and roxadustat (10 mg/kg) (Selleckchem, Houston, TX, USA) dissolved in DMSO (n = 6); (3) Cisplatin group, intraperitoneally injected with an equal volume of cisplatin and DMSO (n = 6); and (4) Cisplatin + roxadustat group, intraperitoneally injected with an equal volume of cisplatin and roxadustat (n = 6). To confirm the role of roxadustat against cisplatin-induced AKI, cisplatin exposure was prolonged to 72 h, and the mice were randomly divided into four groups, with grouping details and drug doses as described above. All mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups. Roxadustat was dissolved in DMSO at 50 mg/mL and then diluted to 1 mg/mL with sterile PBS. Roxadustat was given 48 h before injection of cisplatin/saline. In addition, after 48 h of cisplatin/saline treatment, all mice were sacrificed by exsanguination under anesthesia with inhaled 5% isoflurane. We ensured that all groups received the same concentrations of saline and DMSO. Serum was collected for the measurement of Scr and BUN and kidney tissues were taken for protein evaluation and histological analysis.

Cell culture and treatments

Human tubular epithelial cells (HK-2) cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in DMEM with F12 containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After starved overnight, HK-2 cells were pretreated with or without roxadustat (10 μM) for 24 h and then incubated with or without cisplatin (20 μg/mL) for 24 h before harvesting. All the in vitro experiments were repeated at least three times.

Immunoblot analysis

Protein samples were obtained from kidney tissue, which were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Then, after centrifugation at 4 °C,12000 rpm for 15 min, followed by absorption the supernatants and denaturation at 100 °C for 10 min, we obtained ideal protein samples for detection. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE Gel electrophoresis (8%-12%) and transferred to PVDF membranes. Subsequently, we blocked these membranes with 5% defatted milk for 1 h at room temperature. Then, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After that, the membranes were washed with TBST for 30 min and incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Eventually, we used an ECL kit to detect the expression levels of proteins. Densitometry analysis was carried out by using the Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Kidney function assay

Blood samples were collected and centrifugated. After adding the chromogenic reagent, the absorbance was measured with microplate reader under different OD values. According to the datasheets, their levels could be calculated.

Pathological assessment

After being fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h, these specimens were embedded in paraffin. For histology analysis, sections (3-μm-thick) were stained with PAS solution and H&E solution, respectively, following the manufacturer’s standardized protocols. Tubular injury index was examined using a semiquantitative grading system based on three key morphological features: tubule dilation, epithelial necrosis, and brush edge loss. Tubular injury index was scored by the percentage of damaged tubules: none = 0, <30% = 1, 30%–60% = 2 and >60% = 3. n = 6 per group were included. Each image was counted individually and 15–20 fields were randomly selected for quantified analysis. All assessments were done blindly.

Immunohistochemistry staining

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was implemented according to procedures described in our previous studies [31,32]. The tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and then repaired with citrate buffer. After being blocked with 10% goat serum at room temperature for one hour, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, including NGAL, HIF-1α, IL-18, IL-1β, and TNF-α. The kidney tissue sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, and then subjected to DAB for chromogenic reactions, with nuclei stained with hematoxylin. Finally, the images were captured using a light microscope (Leica, DM3000). n = 6 per group were included. For quantification, 15–20 fields were randomly selected for analysis, and the images were examined using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

ORO staining

The 10 μm thick frozen sections were prepared for staining. Then, the staining solution was added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, the areas with lipid deposition were specifically stained red and were considered as positive areas. After that, images were visualized using a light microscope (Leica, DM3000). n = 6 per group were included. For quantification, 15–20 fields were randomly selected for analysis, and the images were examined using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Transmission electron microscope

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) was carried out to observe the lipid accumulation and mitochondria morphology. Approximately 1 mm3 of kidney cortex tubes from different groups were collected. After being embedded in a fixative solution, tissue cubes were used for standard ultrastructural analysis. Then, images were visualized using a TEM (Hitachi, HT7800). n = 6 per group were included. For quantification, 10–15 fields were randomly selected for analysis, and the mitochondrial size and area were examined using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Kidney MDA and GSH assays

After grinding the kidney tissue into kidney homogenate, kidney MDA and GSH were detected respectively by MDA and GSH assay kit according to the manufacturers’ recommended instructions.

Statistical analysis

All values were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between unpaired two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-test for multiple comparisons was used for groups of three or more. For all analyses, P-value <0.05 was considered significant. The statistical analyses were conducted by using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Roxadustat improved renal function and kidney injury in a murine model of cisplatin-caused AKI

To elucidate the role of roxadustat in the cisplatin-induced AKI, we first conducted a dose-gradient experiment in mice. Mice were pretreated with different doses of roxadustat (0, 1, 5, or 10 mg/kg) before cisplatin administration. We found that a dose of 10 mg/kg roxadustat could effectively reduce Scr levels (Supplementary Figure 1). Subsequently, we established a model of mice by pretreating with roxadustat at a dose of 10 mg/kg. After the 48-h pretreatment, cisplatin was administrated intraperitoneally. Compared with the mice in control groups, the Scr and BUN levels were increased significantly in the cisplatin-injected group. The pretreatment with roxadustat could successfully reduce the Scr and BUN levels in the mice subjected to cisplatin (Figure 1a, b). Then, the histological analyses of the kidneys of mice were conducted. It was observed that tubule atrophy, dilation and vacuolization in the specimens from the cisplatin-injected group, and the renal tubular injury index was significantly higher in this group (Figure 1c). However, these pathological lesions caused by cisplatin could be reversed by pretreatment with roxadustat (Figure 1c, d). In addition, it was demonstrated that the administration of roxadustat alone did not result in any alterations to kidney function or morphology (Figure 1a-d).

We also detected the levels of the biomarkers of AKI, including KIM-1 and NGAL. As shown in Figure 1e, the protein expression levels of KIM-1 and NGAL were evidently increased in cisplatin-induced mice. Conversely, administration of roxadustat could dramatically decrease the levels of KIM-1 and NGAL (Figure 1e-g). Consistently with the results of immunoblotting, IHC staining also indicated an increase of NGAL expression in the tubular epithelial cells of the kidney from cisplatin-injected mice compared to the sham group, while the pretreatment with roxadustat dramatically reduced the expression of NGAL. To investigate the function of roxadustat on AKI at different timepoints, we extended the cisplatin treatment duration to 72 h. The cisplatin-induced AKI manifested significantly elevated Scr levels, which were attenuated by roxadustat pretreatment (Supplementary Figure 2A). PAS staining revealed significant tubular injury, brush border loss, and epithelial cell necrosis in the cisplatin-injected group, while roxadustat administration ameliorated the tubular injury (Supplementary Figure 2B). Furthermore, the tubular injury index revealed alleviation of tubular damage in the roxadustat pretreatment group compared with the cisplatin group (Supplementary Figure 2C). Thus, the data demonstrated that roxadustat improved the renal function and pathological damage in the cisplatin-induced AKI, suggesting a protective role of roxadustat.

Roxadustat ameliorated cell apoptosis and activated HIF-1α in the kidney of cisplatin-induced AKI mice

As accumulating papers have reported that apoptosis is an indispensable mechanism of AKI [33], we also tried to find out whether roxadustat exerts its protective effects by regulating apoptotic responses. We detected the protein levels of Bax and cleaved caspase 3, which exert fundamental role on start up apoptotic program. Immunoblot analysis showed that both Bax and cleaved caspase 3 were minimally expressed in the normal mice kidneys, while its expression level was increased in the kidney of cisplatin-induced AKI (Figure 2a-d). The roxadustat, however, substantially decreased the level of Bax and cleaved caspase 3 (Figure 2a-d). The previous publications have demonstrated that tumor-inhibiting factor p53 could directly regulate apoptosis as an upstream molecule [34,35]. Therefore, we detected the level of p53 to prove whether roxadustat could mediate apoptosis by regulating the expression of p53. Then, we found that p53 was elevated in cisplatin-injected mice group, while administration of roxadustat significantly decreased its expression (Figure 2a, b). These findings indicated that roxadustat could suppress apoptosis by mediating p53 in the kidney of cisplatin-induced AKI. IHC staining revealed that roxadustat, as the regulator of HIF-1α, up-regulated the positive areas of HIF-1α with or without cisplatin treatment (Figure 2e, f). Furthermore, in the cisplatin-treated group, MDA was found to be upregulated, while GSH was downregulated, indicating elevated levels of oxidative stress. However, roxadustat pretreatment mitigated these imbalances (Figure 2g, h). In summary, these results suggested that roxadustat raised the expression level of HIF-1α and may protect from the cisplatin-induced AKI by reducing apoptosis and alleviating oxidative stress.

Roxadustat mitigated deficiency of fatty acid oxidation in the kidney of cisplatin-induced AKI mice

Recent studies revealed that cisplatin-induced AKI can induce lipid metabolic disorders [19,36]. CPT1A and ACOX1 were identified as key enzymes of FAO, which exhibited high expression levels under normal conditions [37]. Thus, we set out to determine whether roxadustat would regulate lipid metabolism to improve the cisplatin-induced AKI. The ORO staining showed that there was no lipid droplet accumulation in the sham group, while the accumulation of lipid droplets was significantly increased in the kidney of cisplatin-induced AKI mice. The pretreatment with roxadustat effectively inhibited this abnormal lipid accumulation, which demonstrated that the ORO staining positive area manifested a notable decrease (Figure 3a, b). The TEM observed that the increased number of lipid droplets in the cisplatin treatment group was reversed by roxadustat administration, indicating that the protective function of roxadustat in cisplatin-induced AKI depended on its regulatory function on fatty acid metabolism (Figure 3a, c). Furthermore, immunoblotting was employed to investigate the protein levels of FAO-related enzymes CPT1A and ACOX1. The levels of CPT1A and ACOX1 were found to be significantly diminished in the kidney treated with cisplatin, indicating the lipid metabolism disorders induced in the cisplatin-caused AKI. The pretreatment with roxadustat restored the expression of CPT1A and ACOX1 to the relatively higher levels (Figure 3d-f). Collectively, these results indicated the possibility that roxadustat may attenuate the cisplatin-induced AKI by regulating lipid metabolism disturbance.

Roxadustat ameliorated mitochondrial injury in the murine model of cisplatin-induced AKI

Considering the key role of mitochondrial function in AKI, we further investigated the relationship between roxadustat and mitochondrial function [38]. Mitochondria, behaving as major energy metabolism organelles in the kidney, were damaged in cisplatin-induced AKI [39]. In the sham groups of the current study, we observed mitochondria appear as long strips or short rod-shaped and have clear double-membrane structures as well as regularly arranged cristae. The cisplatin-caused mitochondrial swelling, cristae rupture, mitochondrial fragmentation, and smaller size and area (Figure 4a-c). Interestingly, roxadustat partially restored these abnormalities in mitochondria. In addition, we evaluated whether roxadustat pretreatment had impacts on mitochondrial dynamics or not. The mitochondrial fusion-related proteins, including OPA1, MFN1, and MFN2, were highly downregulated in cisplatin-injected mice, while the protein levels of these fusion-related proteins in their roxadustat pretreated littermates were dramatically increased (Figure 4d-g). Taken together, the results demonstrated that roxadustat can alleviate the cisplatin-caused AKI by inhibiting mitochondria damage.

Roxadustat alleviated cisplatin-induced renal tubular cell injury in vitro

To confirm the effect of roxadustat in renal tubular cells, we pretreated HK-2 cells with roxadustat (10 μM) for 24 h before being exposed to cisplatin (20 μg/mL) for an additional 24 h. Immunoblot analysis demonstrated that cisplatin significantly upregulated NGAL expression, whereas roxadustat pretreatment effectively mitigated this elevation without altering basal NGAL levels (Figure 5a, b). Cisplatin-induced AKI exhibited activation of apoptosis, as evidenced by increased cleaved caspase-3 expression. Notably, roxadustat pretreatment substantially attenuated this pro-apoptotic response (Figure 5c-d). Mechanistically, cisplatin exposure downregulated key lipid metabolic enzymes CPT1A and ACOX, which were rescued by roxadustat (Figure 5e-g). Furthermore, cisplatin disrupted mitochondrial dynamics through suppression of mitochondrial fusion proteins OPA1, MFN1, and MFN2, while roxadustat restored their expression levels (Figure 5h-k). These in vitro findings corroborate our in vivo observations, demonstrating that cisplatin triggers tubular cell injury via apoptosis activation, lipid metabolic dysregulation, and mitochondrial dynamics impairment. Roxadustat pretreatment exerts cytoprotection by modulating these pathological cascades.

Discussion

Currently, roxadustat has already passed Phase III clinical trials and has been approved for the treatment of anemia in CKD patients [40,41]. Roxadustat exerts its function by elevating endogenous EPO to normal physiological levels [42]. Recently, roxadustat was also reported to exert a beneficial influence on rescuing kidney fibrosis in hypoxia-related nephropathy [43]. Several evidence have supported the protective roles of roxadustat in AKI within different causes [27–29]. However, the specific roles and underlying molecular mechanisms of roxadustat in AKI are still ambiguous. In this study, we further examined the renoprotective effects of roxadustat and the underlying mechanism in a cisplatin-caused mouse model of AKI and cisplatin-stimulated HK-2 cells. Our data revealed that roxadustat could significantly improve kidney function, attenuate histological alternations and inhibit apoptotic response induced by nephrotoxic drugs. Roxadustat also decreased the number of lipid droplets by rescuing the expression of FAO-related proteins, suggesting that roxadustat could regulate metabolic disorders. Moreover, roxadustat attenuated mitochondria damage and restored the expressions of mitochondria fusion membrane proteins.

Previous publications have demonstrated the renoprotective effects of roxadustat in AKI [30,44,45]. The researchers have found that the pretreatment by roxadustat would effectively inhibit the injuries in cisplatin-caused AKI [29]. Our data also verified the protective effect of roxadustat in cisplatin-induced AKI. When compared with the mice that only received saline before the administration of cisplatin, the kidney function was improved and the pathological changes were alleviated in the group of mice with pretreatment with roxadustat.

As known to all, AKI which is characterized by rapid loss of kidney function and associated with insufficient kidney energy supply could influence a series of processes, including inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress [46,47]. We confirmed that the levels of inflammatory factors, including IL-18, IL-1β, and TNF-α, were elevated during cisplatin-induced AKI, while roxadustat administration effectively suppressed this tendency (Supplementary Figure 3A-D). Apoptosis plays an indispensable role in the AKI [38,48]. Early research indicated that roxadustat suppressed apoptosis under pathological conditions, such as hypoxia-induced injury [43,45]. Consistently, our findings revealed that roxadustat effectively reduced the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins including Bax and cleaved caspase 3, indicating the protective effects of roxadustat in cisplatin-induced AKI may be dependent on the inhibition of apoptosis. As the marker of DNA injury, p53 was confirmed to influence apoptosis, cell cycle arrest and senescence in AKI [49]. In this research, the elevated expression of p53 caused by cisplatin was decreased via pretreatment with roxadustat, thus we hypothesized that roxadustat may alleviate cisplatin-induced AKI by inhibiting apoptosis in a p53-dependent manner.

Emerging evidence has shown that FAO protects kidney tubules from acute injury by supplying sufficient energy. Defects in FAO pathways have been identified to enhance the development of acute kidney injury and renal fibrosis [21,50]. FAO-related enzymes involving CPT1A and ACOX1 are downstream substrates of AMPK signaling pathways, which were activated via lipid accumulation and injury [51]. Moreover, several studies have considered that CPT1A plays a central role in protecting and maintaining the normal levels of FAO in rat models [52,53]. CPT1A is responsible for long-chain fatty acids catabolism, while ACOX1 is mainly in charge of short-chain fatty acids [54]. Both are necessary for FAO and are downregulated during AKI. Thus, we wondered whether the roxadustat protects the kidney by regulating the FAO pathways. In our research, ORO staining and TEM images revealed increased lipid droplet levels induced via cisplatin. This phenomenon was consistent with previous findings [19,20]. In this study, the lipid droplet accumulated in the tubular epithelium of cisplatin-administrated mice, while roxadustat successfully reduced the abnormal lipid droplet accumulation. The downregulation of FAO rate-limiting enzymes during cisplatin-induced AKI also were rescued by the pretreatment with roxadustat. Evidenced by the restoration of expression levels of key enzymes of FAO in vivo and in vitro, we originally reported that roxadustat may protect the kidney by inhibiting FAO disturbance in cisplatin-induced AKI. Although the present study helps indicate that roxadustat could improve FAO during cisplatin-induced AKI, further experimental verification is still needed, especially with energy metabolism.

Various previous studies have demonstrated that mitochondria are the predominant organelles producing energy in the kidney and the most vulnerable targets for AKI [55,56]. Cisplatin, a classic chemotherapeutic drug with nephrotoxicity, induces tubular cell dysfunction by activating oxidative stress and inhibiting adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, leading to disorders of mitochondria homeostasis and then inducing detrimental kidney outcomes [57]. Mitochondria imbalanced homeostasis was reflected in mitochondrial dynamics disturbance. Mitochondrial dynamics is significantly destructed during cisplatin-induced AKI, as evidenced by the altered expression levels of proteins involved in mitochondrial fusion and fission [58]. Previous research indicated that mitochondrial fusion proteins involving OPA1, MFN1 and MFN2 were regulated by Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways in AKI [59]. Cisplatin administration leads to a reduction in the expression of these mitochondrial fusion proteins in the kidney, contributing to the mitochondrial dysfunction observed in cisplatin-caused AKI [60]. Some researchers have found that roxadustat could reduce the mitochondrial damage in ischemia/reperfusion-induced and contrast-induced AKI [28,30], however, there are no similar reports in cisplatin-induced AKI. We speculated that roxadustat may also prevent mitochondrial damage in the cisplatin-induced AKI. We observed that after the treatment with cisplatin, the size and area of mitochondria shrank significantly, which was in accordance with the previous paper [61]. While pretreatment with roxadustat effectively facilitated mitochondria homeostasis, which was manifested in the recovery of mitochondrial morphology. Furthermore, mitochondrial fusion disorders and mitochondrial dysfunction occurred during cisplatin-caused AKI in vivo and in vitro, evidenced by decreased expression of OPA1, MFN1 and MFN2. Roxadustat facilitated the upregulation of mitochondrial fusion proteins. Thus, our findings suggested the therapeutic role of roxadustat in cisplatin-induced AKI may be exerted by regulating mitochondrial morphology and mitochondrial fusion proteins to alleviate mitochondrial disorders.

However, this research contains a few limitations, first, due to the preciousness of clinical renal biopsy specimens, we have not conducted related trials; second, we did not use knockout mice to investigate the importance of specific pathways for FAO, but we plan to conduct these experiments in the future. Another limitation is only a single time point of cisplatin-induced AKI was evaluated, in future research, it would be beneficial to evaluate additional time points, such as 72 h, to further explore the therapeutic effects of roxadustat.

Our current study demonstrated that pretreatment of roxadustat exerted a protective effect on the kidney through a variety of novel mechanisms, including improved kidney function, attenuation of apoptosis, as well as reduction of lipid droplet accumulation and recovery of mitochondria morphology. Besides being identified as an important drug for the treatment of anemia, roxadustat is also suggested as an effective agent for treating AKI based on our results. As one of the small molecule agents, roxadustat may hold a clinical potential for the treatment of patients with AKI. Further studies and related clinical trials investigate the efficacy and safety of roxadustat for the treatment of AKI also still in demand. Taken together, our findings showed the renoprotective effects of roxadustat in cisplatin-induced AKI, providing a theoretical basis for the application of roxadustat in clinical.
