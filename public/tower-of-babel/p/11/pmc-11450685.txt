Novel Metal-Free Nanozyme for Targeted Imaging and Inhibition of Atherosclerosis via Macrophage Autophagy Activation to Prevent Vulnerable Plaque Formation and Rupture
Authors: Lingjie Wang, Xiaoqian Zhang, Hongrong Zhang, Xiaozhe Wang, Xiaofeng Ren, Wei Bian, Caiyun Shi, Jingying Wang, Liping Li, Ruiping Zhang, Hua Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Atherosclerosis is a primary cause of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, with the unpredictable rupture of vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques enriched with lipid-laden macrophages being able to lead to heart attacks and strokes. Activating macrophage autophagy presents itself as a promising strategy for preventing vulnerable plaque formation and reducing the risk of rupture. In this study, we have developed a novel metal-free nanozyme (HCN@DS) that integrates the functions of multimodal imaging-guided therapy for atherosclerosis. HCN@DS has demonstrated high macrophage-targeting abilities due to its affinity toward scavenger receptor A (SR-A), along with excellent photoacoustic and photothermal imaging capabilities for guiding the precise treatment. It combines mild photothermal effects with moderate reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation to treat atherosclerosis. This controlled approach activates autophagy in atherosclerotic macrophages, inhibiting foam cell formation by reducing the uptake of oxidized low-density lipoproteins (oxLDL) and promoting efferocytosis and cholesterol efflux in macrophages. Additionally, it prevents plaque rupture by inhibiting apoptosis and inflammation within the plaque. Therefore, this metal-free nanozyme holds great potential for reducing the risk of atherosclerosis due to its high biosafety, excellent targeting ability, dual-modality imaging capability, and appropriate modulation of autophagy.

Introduction

Atherosclerosis-induced cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases have emerged as the leading cause of disability and mortality worldwide.1 The majority of fatal events resulting from atherosclerosis, particularly ischemic strokes, are primarily attributed to emboli originating from vulnerable plaque rupture.2,3 Macrophages, which account for over 80% of all cellular components in vulnerable plaque, differentiate into foam cells following scavenger receptor-mediated phagocytosis of deposited lipid and cellular debris. Simultaneously, they release various pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ), thereby promoting the progression of atherosclerosis.1,4−7 It is crucial to prevent the formation of vulnerable plaques in order to achieve effective treatment outcomes in managing atherosclerosis.8 Clinically, current therapies for atherosclerosis, such as statins and antiplatelet agents, primarily focus on reducing risk factors like cholesterol levels and preventing thrombosis, respectively. Despite effectively slowing disease progression, the limited clinical application of these therapies is attributed to poor targeting and side effects, as they fail to directly address the underlying mechanisms of plaque vulnerability and rupture.9,10

The lysosome-mediated autophagy, a ubiquitous intracellular degradation process in eukaryotic cells, is currently receiving increasing attention for its role in inhibiting macrophage apoptosis and promoting plaque stability.7,11−13 The dysfunction of macrophage autophagy is commonly involved in the progression of atherosclerosis,7 making reactivation of this process significantly therapeutically promising for treating atherosclerosis. Phototherapies, such as photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT), have gained considerable attention in the field of tumor therapy due to their minimally invasive nature and ability to induce lesion ablation.14,15 The effectiveness of phototherapies in preventing the progression of atherosclerosis has been fully confirmed by inducing apoptosis and autophagy in macrophages or macrophage-derived foam cells.16,17 Various nanomaterials, including gold nanomaterials, carbon nanotubes, quantum dots, metal oxides, and semiconducting polymers, have been used as photothermal conversion agents for PTT.18 However, their potential issues, such as inevitable thermal ablation damage to surrounding normal tissues caused by excessive thermal exposure, undesired inflammation resulting from heat diffusion, and insufficient tissue penetration of short-wavelength light, limit their application of PTT in arteriosclerosis treatment.19,20 These concerns prompt us to explore innovative mild photothermal conversion agents that can effectively activate macrophage autophagy while minimizing undesirable biological effects, inhibiting the progression of atherosclerosis.

Recently, nanozymes have gained significant attention in disease diagnosis and treatment due to their advantages, including robust catalytic activity to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), good photothermal properties, high stability, and simple and high-yield preparation.21,22 Nanozymes also exhibit excellent photothermal and photoacoustic imaging properties owing to their broad-spectrum absorption characteristics, which guide precise treatment. Therapeutic strategies for atherosclerosis utilizing nanozymes, such as porous manganese substituted prussian blue (PMPB) nanocube, platinum and cerium bimetallic nanoraspberry, Cu-MOFs, etc., have demonstrated efficacy through suppressing the inflammatory and regulating the microenvironment of lipid metabolism.14,23,24 However, it is important to note that the presence of metal elements in most nanozymes can potentially lead to biological toxicity, thus significantly limiting their clinical application. Consequently, there is a need for developing a metal-free nanozyme with precise targeting capabilities and mild photothermal properties for both atherosclerosis therapy and imaging, which presents significant challenges alongside promising prospects.

In this study, we developed a novel metal-free nanozyme (HCN@DS) composed of hollow carbon spheres for photothermal/photoacoustic imaging-guided mild PTT and moderate ROS generation in the treatment of atherosclerosis (Scheme 1). Dextran sulfate (DS) has been incorporated to specifically target scavenger receptor type A (SR-A), which is expressed on the surface of atherosclerotic macrophages or macrophage-derived foam cells. The HCN@DS demonstrates remarkable efficacy in identifying and internalizing atherosclerotic macrophages, making it an ideal candidate for near-infrared II (NIR II) photothermal and photoacoustic imaging due to its exceptional tissue penetration capability and higher maximum permissible energy for skin irradiation. A mild photothermal effect (<45 °C) is used to reactivate autophagy of macrophages or foam cells, thereby inhibiting apoptosis and necrosis, suppressing inflammation, and activating signaling pathways that facilitate cholesterol efflux in macrophages. The concomitant moderate generation of ROS further augments autophagy in atherosclerotic macrophages. Consequently, this approach promotes plaque stability and inhibits the progression of atherosclerosis.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Reagents

Chitosan (CS) and Dextran sulfate (DS) were acquired from Shanghai Aladdin Biochem Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Aniline, pyrrole, and ammonium persulfate (APS) were obtained from Energy Chemical (Anhui, China). Triton X-100 was procured from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Acrylic acid was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of HCN@DS

Hollow carbon nanospheres (HCNs) were fabricated following the details provided in a previous report.25 By the typical protocol, the APS solution was prepared and cooled in an ice box. Subsequently, 1 mg mL–1 Triton X-100 solution was prepared and 0.38 mL of aniline, and 0.29 mL of pyrrole were incorporated. Then, APS was added to the mixed solution, and stationary for 15 h to obtain the final product. The solvent was extracted through filtration, and the product was then washed using DI water until the filtrate became colorless. The black powder obtained after drying at 60 °C was placed in a tube furnace and heated at 1000 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere for 2 h to produce HCNs. 2 mg of HCNs and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid were blended with a 1 mg/mL CS solution (5 mL), and the cross-linking process was completed by adding 30 μL of glutaraldehyde. After stirring for 4 h, the HCN@CS was centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 8 min, washed three times with ultrapure water, and dispersed in 5 mL ultrapure water. Then, 10 mg DS powder was added to the mixture and magnetically stirred for 1 h in an ice–water bath to avoid overheating due to the exothermic reaction.

Characterization of HCN@DS

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100f, JEOL) was utilized to examine the morphology and structure of the HCN@DS. The UV–vis absorption spectra were obtained using a spectrophotometer (UH5300, HitachiLimited). The hydrophilic diameter and zeta potential were obtained using dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Zetasizer Nano, Malvern, UK). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Thermo Scientific Escalab 250Xi) was conducted to characterize the elemental composition of HCN@DS.

PA Imaging Performance of HCN@DS

All the PA signals were collected on Vevo 3100 and Vevo LAZR-X, (FUJIFILM VisualSonics). HCN@DS solutions of different concentrations, ranging from 0 to 1000 μg/mL, were filled into capillaries and placed in a Petri dish. A 1205 nm laser was used to irradiate the sample, and the resulting photoacoustic signals were recorded.

In Vitro Phototherapeutic Effects of HCN@DS

The photothermal effects of HCN@DS were investigated by recording the temperature changes of HCN@DS in various concentrations (0, 25, 50, 100, and 150 μg/mL) under 1064 nm-laser irradiation (1 W cm–2) for 5 min with an IR thermal camera.

Enzyme-Mimic Activities of HCN@DS

The peroxidase (POD) activity was assessed using 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) as a substrate. After incubating HCN@DS in the HAc-NaAc buffer (pH 4.6) with H2O2 (25 mM) and TMB (0.5 mM) at room temperature for 10 min, the supernatant was obtained by centrifugation at 10000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, the oxidation of TMB was measured using a UV spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 652 nm. The catalase (CAT) activity was determined using a dissolved oxygen analyzer, wherein 1 mM H2O2 was mixed with HCNs and HCN@DS before being introduced into HAc-NaAc buffer at pH 4.6. The O2 concentrations were determined at different time intervals. The superoxide anion detection kit was employed to assess oxidase (OXD) activity by detecting the generation of superoxide anions. According to the instructions of the superoxide anion kit, standard solutions with different concentrations were prepared, mixed well, centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min, absorbance values were measured, and standard curves were drawn. PH value of buffer solution was 4.6, TMB concentration was 0.5 mM, H2O2 concentration was 25 mM, and superoxide anion production with or without DS was measured.

Confirming the Formation of Foam Cells

RAW264.7 Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. The cells were stimulated with 100 μg/mL Ox-LDL in serum-free 1640 medium for 24 h to induce foam cell formation.

Oil Red O (ORO) Staining

The activated RAW 264.7 cells were stained with oil red dye and double distilled water (3:2) for 20 min and then examined using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

MRSI gene expression was examined in activated RAW264.7 cells to ascertain the formation of foam cells. Total RNA was extracted from macrophage-derived foam cells using Trizol reagent and then purified. PCR amplification was conducted with Quant studio 3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and D30 (Eppendorf BioPhotometer, Germany). CDNA was obtained from 1 ng RNA by reverse transcription. β-actin was used as the internal parameter. The relative expression level of MRS1 was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Western Blot Analysis

Western blotting was employed to validate the existence of foam cells, as evidenced by the presence of MRS1 protein. The cells were treated after protein presentation, and proteins were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). The membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and washed with TBST thrice. After that, the membranes were incubated with 1:1000 dilution of primary antibody in TBST with 1% BSA at 4 °C on a shaker overnight, followed by incubation with 1:5000 dilution of secondary antibody in TBST with 1% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using a Gel imaging system (Alphalmager MINI) and densitometry of the Western blot was analyzed and normalized by β-actin using ImageJ. The primary and secondary antibodies used were MSR1 Rabbit pAb and ABflo 594-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, respectively.

Immunofluorometric Analysis

The immunofluorescence method was utilized to corroborate foam cell formation. The activated RAW 264.7 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at 4 °C and rinsed with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS. The cells were then blocked with 0.1% normal goat serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with a primary antibody (1:200) at 4 °C overnight. Afterward, a secondary antibody was applied to the cells for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, DAPI was added to the cells for 30 min before imaging with a confocal microscope. The primary and secondary antibodies used were MSR1 Rabbit pAb and ABflo 594-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, respectively.

In Vitro Targeting Ability

Confocal laser scanning microscopy was utilized to observe the targeting efficacy of the HCNs and HCN@DS on activated and unactivated RAW 264.7 cells. The cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and incubated with 1640 medium for 24 h. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with a fresh medium containing the FITC-labeled HCNs or HCN@DS (at a concentration of 80 μg/mL) for 4 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed thrice with PBS, and the nuclei were then stained with DAPI for 10 min. Once the residual DAPI was eliminated with PBS, the fixed cells were observed through a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Cell Viability and Apoptosis Analysis

Cell viability of HCN@DS was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay. Activated RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 104 cells/well and incubated overnight. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with 100 μL of the medium containing HCN@DS at different concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μg/mL. The cells were then exposed to 1064 nm laser (1 W cm–2) irradiation for 10 min and incubated for an additional 4 h in the dark. The CCK-8 working solution (10 μL) was added to each well, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The optical density of the cells at 450 nm was read on a microplate reader 2 h later. Additionally, the cytotoxicity of the HCN@DS was also examined using a live/dead cell staining assay. The activated macrophages with different treatments were incubated with Calcein-AM (5 μM) and propidium iodide (10 μM) for 25 min, following which they were washed with PBS and observed under fluorescence microscopy.

For the in vitro cell apoptosis assay, the activated Raw 264.7 cells were incubated in the medium containing various treatments and then exposed to laser irradiation for 5 min. The cells were then collected, and stained using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit, and the cell apoptosis was measured using FACSCelesta. Finally, the data were analyzed using FlowJo software. (Flow Jo, LLC/Becton, Dickinson, and Company, Ashland, OR).

Autophagy Induction Experiment

After RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate and incubated for 24 h, HCN@DS was introduced to the wells at 200 μg/mL and subjected to 1064 nm-laser irradiation for 5 min. To investigate autophagy formation, all the cells from each group were gathered for Western blot analysis, as well as to calculate the LC3-II/I ratio and protein expression of p62 and LAMP1.

Animal Protocols

ApoE–/– mice (6–8 weeks old, male) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed in a temperature and humidity-controlled animal facility with a 12-h light/dark cycle. The mice were acclimatized to the new environment and fed a standard laboratory chow diet for 1 week, with their body weights being recorded weekly. Afterward, all mice were fed a high-fat diet for 16 weeks to induce atherosclerotic plaque. The mice were randomly divided into three groups: (I) Control; (II) HCN@DS; (III) HCN@DS+Laser. All mice were treated with saline (group I), and HCN@DS (group II–III) via tail vein injection. At 4 h postinjection, the lesion region of group III was exposed to a 1064 laser irradiation at a power density of 1 W cm–2 for 5 min. The treatment was administered three times within 4 weeks, and the body weights of the mice were measured once per week. Here, the atherosclerosis mice model was employed to investigate the targeted recognition of HCN@DS and assess its in vivo PA imaging performance. The mice were intravenously administered with HCN@DS, and at 4 h postinjection, PA imaging was performed on the experimental mice using an excitation wavelength of 1205 nm.

In Vivo Antiatherosclerotic Efficacy and Biological Safety

Upon treatment completion, the heart arteries of the mice were collected for histological and immunohistochemical analysis, and their aortas were removed for ORO staining. To ensure safety, the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were taken, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and prepared for H&E staining. Furthermore, blood was collected to measure the blood biochemical indicators in the various groups.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and analysis of variance and presented as mean values ± standard deviation. Comparisons between groups were performed by independent sample Student’s t test and analysis of variance. All analyses were repeated three times in three independent experiments (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001)

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of HCN@DS

HCN@DS was synthesized via the polymerization-carbonization process and subsequent layer-by-layer modification (Scheme 1). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed the well-defined hollow spherical shape of HCN@DS (Figure 1A) with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 140 ± 10 nm (Figure 1B). Scanning transmission electron microscopy imaging (STEM, Figure 1C) and Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX, Figure S1) spectroscopy images clarified that S, Na and N element were distributed in the HCN@DS, indicating the successful coating of CS and DS on HCN@DS. The elemental composition was further proved by the XPS survey (Figure 1D and Figure S2). The HCN@DS exhibited banding energy peaks of S 2p, Na 1s, O 1s, N 1s, and C 1s, respectively. The Zeta potentials of HCNs, CS, HCNs-CS, DS, and HCN@DS were −14.4 mV, 18.6 mV, 4.31 mV, −25.3 mV, and −20 mV, respectively (Figure 1E). It was observed that upon cross-linking the CS and DS, the products exhibited different zeta potentials during each synthesis stage. All these results proved that HCN@DS was successfully constructed by layer-by-layer modification.

(E) Zeta potential changes during the preparation process of HCN@DS. (n = 3). (F) POD-like, (G) CAT-like and (H) OXD-like activities of HCNS and HCN@DS. The significant absorption peak of HCN and HCN@DS can be observed, as shown in Figure 1F, owing to the oxidation of colorless TMB to blue oxTMB by H2O2 through the POD-like activities of HCN and HCN@DS. The CAT-like activities of HCN and HCN@DS were also investigated to elucidate the differences in activity between the two nanostructures (Figure 1G). Both structures demonstrated efficient O2 production within 30 min in the presence of H2O2. The OXD-like activities of HCN and HCN@DS were further evaluated by quantifying the concentration of O2–, as oxygen reduction reaction catalysis is associated with OXD. As shown in Figure 1H, an elevation in the generated O2– concentration was observed with increasing levels of both HCNs and HCN@DS, indicating their resemblance to OXD activities. These findings demonstrate that the multienzyme-like properties of HCN@DS effectively promote ROS generation. ROS have been extensively investigated in the context of atherosclerosis therapy. Notably, the strategic utilization of ROS as a trigger for targeted drug delivery has demonstrated efficacy; however, dysregulated ROS expression can aggravate atherosclerotic progression.26,27 HCN@DS possesses the inherent capacity to generate a moderate ROS, which is crucial for enhancing macrophage autophagy and cholesterol efflux, thereby inhibiting the progression of atherosclerosis.28−30 The optical absorption capabilities of CS, DS, HCNs, and HCN@DS were evaluated using UV–vis absorption spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 1I, HCN@DS exhibits strong light adsorption in the visible to NIR-IIrange, making it a promising candidate for PTT, photothermal imaging (PTI) and photoacoustic imaging (PAI). The temperature changes of HCN@DS in different concentrations under 1064 nm-laser irradiation (1 W cm–2) were captured, exhibiting a concentration and time-dependent behavior (Figure S3A,B). The temperature of the 150 μg/mL HCN@DS dispersion increased dramatically by 40 °C within 2 min, whereas the temperature of the PBS solution only rose 5.2 °C under the same conditions. Furthermore, HCN@DS exhibited remarkable photothermal stability, with its performance remaining consistent after four heating–cooling cycles (Figure S3C). These findings demonstrate that HCN@DS is capable of rapid photothermal conversion, making it a viable PTT agent. Motivated by the favorable PTT performance of HCN@DS, we further explored its PA properties. Figure S4A,B illustrate the PA images and respective signal intensities from various HCN@DS solutions with concentrations ranging from 0 to 800 μg/mL under 1064 nm laser excitation. The PA signal enhancement displays a linear correlation to the concentration of HCN@DS.

Confirming the Formation of Foam Cells

At the initial stage of atherosclerosis, monocytes migrate to the intima of arteries and become activated macrophages.1,4,31 These macrophages express scavenger receptors and phagocytize liposomes, leading to the formation of foam cells via scavenger receptors.1 The Oil Red O (ORO) staining method, known for its high specificity in detecting intracellular neutral lipids, was employed to illustrate the formation of foam cells.32 It was clear from (Figure 2A) that macrophages treated with Ox-LDL had a significant accumulation of lipids compared to those that were untreated. This was further confirmed by the ImageJ analysis (Figure 2B). Macrophages possess the scavenger receptor 1 (MRS1), which facilitates its uptake and breakdown of acetylated LDL. This factor is responsible for the creation of foam cells in atherosclerosis.33 Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), Western blot (WB), and immunofluorescence staining experiments were performed to verify the gene and protein expression of MRS1. As illustrated in Figure 2C, the level of MRS1 gene expression was notably elevated in macrophages that were treated with Ox-LDL. WB analysis demonstrated that the expression of MRS1 was significantly increased after 24 h of Ox-LDL stimulation, indicating that the protein of MRS1 was highly expressed on the cell membrane of pro-inflammatory macrophages (Figure 2D and Figure S5). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining of MRS1 results (Figure 2E,F) further confirmed the successful formation of foam cells.

In Vitro Targeting Ability of HCN@DS on Activated Macrophages

Subsequently, the targeting capability of the HCN@DS toward activated macrophages was assessed using a confocal laser scanning microscope image. HCN@DS labeled with FITC were coincubated with activated and normal macrophages, respectively. As shown in Figure S6A,B, the activated macrophages showed the highest uptake of HCN@DS, which was evidenced by a solid green fluorescence signal. This was attributed to the high expression of SR-A on the surface of the activated macrophages, to which DS has an affinity. The uptake of HCN@DS by unactivated macrophages was relatively low; however, it still surpassed the uptake of HCN due to the limited expression of SR-A on macrophages. Quantitative analysis of the relative fluorescence intensities further confirmed that the activated macrophages had a higher uptake efficiency of HCN@DS. Additionally, the macrophages activated with HCN@DS showed a progressive rise in fluorescence signal intensity over time (Figure S6C,D). The collective evidence demonstrates that HCN@DS has a particular capacity to target activated macrophages.

In Vitro PTT Treatment and the Mechanism of Elimination of Foam Cells

The cytotoxicity of HCN@DS was evaluated using a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, wherein foam cells were incubated with different concentrations of HCN@DS for 24 h, both with or without 1064 nm-laser irradiation. At the concentration of 100 μg/mL, the cell viability exposed to HCN@DS is around 80%, indicating its high biological safety. Upon exposure to 1064 nm irradiation, cell viability decreased in a concentration-dependent manner following HCN@DS treatment (Figure 3A). Live–dead costaining (Figure 3B) and flow cytometry (Figure 3C) were performed to analyze the effect of PTT in vitro. As expected, HCN@DS+Laser group achieved the highest apoptotic rate, indicating a potential approach to induce macrophage apoptosis. However, massive apoptosis or necrosis could increase the risk of plaque rupture. A safe and effective strategy to treat atherosclerosis should prevent excessive cell apoptosis and necrosis while reducing intracellular lipid levels. HSP27, a newly discovered antiatherosclerotic biomarker, has been found to inhibit cell apoptosis through both the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways.34 Additionally, it has been observed to increase the expression of ATP-binding cassette transporters (ABCA1) and promote the efflux of cholesterol from THP-1 macrophage-derived foam cells by activating the signal pathway.35 Thus, we studied the alterations in both proteins’ expression in the activated cells to investigate the effects of different treatments on foam cells. As shown in Figure 3D–F, HCN@DS+Laser group significantly increased the expression of HSP27 and ABCA1 proteins, while the HCN@DS alone and control groups showed a minimal difference. These results indicated that moderate-photothermal PTT therapy would be an ideal strategy for treating atherosclerosis. Autophagy of macrophages can be promoted during atherosclerosis development, with the efflux of internalized cholesterol reducing lipid accumulation and slowing its progression.36,37 Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) production is an indicator of cellular autophagy, and a moderate level of ROS production can foster autophagy.38,39 In our study, the capability of HCN@DS with or without 1064 nm-laser irradiation to promote ROS production in activated macrophages was verified. It was observed that a small amount of ROS was present in activated macrophages treated with HCN@DS alone and HCN@DS+Laser group (Figure 4A and Figure S7), while no ROS was detected in the control group. Autophagy can be evidenced by the conversion of cytosolic LC3 (LC3-I) to membrane-bound LC3 (LC3-II), the rise of autophagy lysosomal associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1), and the breakdown of p62.40 Our results, as illustrated in Figure 4B,D, demonstrated an upregulation of p62 protein expression, which may result from autophagy activation leading to the efflux of ox-LDL from macrophage-derived foam cells, subsequently triggering further upregulation of p62 protein.41−43 Autophagy, a sophisticated process regulated by various mechanisms, was activated as confirmed by the elevated LC3-II/I ratio and LAMP1 expression in the HCN@DS and HCN@DS+Laser groups, despite the interference of ox-LDL accumulation in the culture medium with p62 degradation, as shown in Figure 4B,C,E. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that a modest elevation in temperature can generate a moderate ROS, which may further enhance autophagy, thereby inhibiting the progression of atherosclerosis.

In Vivo PA/PT Imaging

An animal model with atherosclerotic diseases was established using ApoE–/– mice fed a high-cholesterol diet to perform multimodal imaging of atherosclerosis plaques in vivo using HCN@DS. Ex vivo PA imaging was conducted and further confirmed the targeted capability of HCN@DS to atherosclerotic plaques (Figure 5B). Owing to its photothermal properties, HCN@DS can be employed for PTI to monitor the accumulation and localized heating of drugs. By using an IR thermal camera, it was observed that the temperature of the arterial area of the HCN@DS group had increased to 44.3 °C after 5 min of irradiation with a 1064 nm laser. In contrast the temperature of the PBS group only rose by 0.3 °C (Figure 5A). This finding further suggests that mild photothermal can be utilized for the accurate treatment of atherosclerotic mice.

In Vivo Therapeutic Efficacy in Atherosclerotic Mice

To investigate the antiatherosclerosis efficacy of HCN@DS, the ApoE–/– mice were fed with a high-fat diet to establish the atherosclerotic model. After that, atherosclerotic mice were divided into three groups: the control group, HCN@DS group, HCN@DS+Laser group. All groups were administered a four-week treatment. As the therapy finished, the images obtained from the anatomical microscope indicated that the carotid arteries of the control group had more significant atherosclerotic plaques than HCN@DS+Laser group treatment groups (Figure 5C). ORO staining of the whole aortas (Figure 6A and Figure S8A) and the aortic root sections (Figure 6B and Figure S8B) revealed that the HCN@DS+Laser group had the most effective antiatherosclerosis effect, which was comparable to the other two groups. The HCN@DS group was also able to delay the progress of atherosclerosis, though not as much as the HCN@DS+Laser group. We have previously demonstrated that HCNs are catalytically active, and this may be the cause of the production of partial ROS, which in turn leads to cellular autophagy and inhibits the formation of foam cells. As illustrated in Figure 6A, the control group had the most aggravated atherosclerosis progression, with a plaque area covering approximately 20% of the entire aorta. The aortic root was cross-sectioned and stained with hematoxylin and eosin to evaluate the aortic pathology morphology. As shown in Figure 6B, the HCN@DS+Laser group had the least amount of atherosclerotic lesions compared to the other groups, indicating a remarkable therapeutic effect. The effectiveness of various groups in preventing atherosclerosis was evaluated by analyzing the plaque-to-aorta ratios in H&E-stained sections. As illustrated in Figure 6B and Figure S8C, the control group had a plaque area of 47.91%, while the HCN@DS and HCN@DS+Laser groups had a significantly reduced plaque area of 34.86% and 28.73%, respectively, demonstrating a successful inhibition of plaque progression. Through Masson’s trichrome staining, the amount of collagen fibers in a tissue sample can be quantified, which indicates the thickness of the fibrous cap in the plaque.44 A more considerable amount of collagen fibers implies that the plaque is more stable and less prone to rupture, thus allowing for a determination of its stability. As illustrated in Figure 6B and Figure S8D, the collagen content decreased to 13% in the HCN@DS group and 25.3% in the HCN@DS+Laser group, demonstrating a significant antiatherosclerosis efficacy. Immunohistochemical staining of cross sections of aortas from mice with different treatments was conducted to analyze the composition of plaques. Macrophages, which accumulate and proliferate in plaques, are known to be the driving force behind the formation of foam cells and vulnerable plaques in atherosclerosis progression. Thus, the macrophage content was assessed by staining the aortas with anti-CD68 antibody, a marker of macrophages. Results showed that treatment with HCN@DS and HCN@DS+Laser reduced the macrophage content compared to the control group (39.9%), to 24.6% and 19.2%, respectively (Figure 6B and Figure S8E). This result indicates that the HCN@DS group and HCN@DS+Laser group treatment successfully alleviated macrophage infiltration. Apart from macrophages, another factor in the development of atherosclerotic lesions is the proliferation of smooth muscle cells (SMCs). Therefore, we employed antialpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) antibody, a marker of SMC, to assess the SMC content of the aorta cross-section.45 The results, as shown in Figure 6B and Figure S8F, indicated that the SMC content decreased to 7.25% and 4.29% in the HCN@DS and HCN@DS+Laser treatment groups, respectively, compared to the control group (14.42% SMC). Evidence of antiatherosclerotic effects was found in both treatment groups, and the mild PTT group was observed to stabilize the plaque further and facilitate the regression of atherosclerosis.

Biosafety Assessment

The efficacy of the therapeutic protocol was evaluated by measuring the mice weights during the treatments, as shown in Figure S9. No significant weight change was observed in the three groups, indicating the safety of the protocol. To further verify this, serum levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), total cholesterol (TC), and triglyceride (TG) were measured at the end of the treatment. The results showed that LDL, TG, and TC in the HCN@DS and HCN@DS+Laser treated groups were notably lower than those in the PBS group, while a slight increase in HDL was observed (Figure S10). These in vivo findings suggest that HCN@DS and HCN@DS+Laser can improve blood-lipid levels and have antiatherosclerosis effects, indicating the safety of the therapeutic protocol. The analysis of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 in the serum demonstrated a significant reduction across all three groups, indicating a robust anti-inflammatory effect (Figure 7A–C). The histological examination of major organs in all groups revealed no significant differences between the treated groups and the control group, and there were no observable tissue abnormalities (Figure 7D). These results suggest that the mild-temperature PTT strategy could be an effective and safe approach for preventing atherosclerotic plaque progression in vivo.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we successfully developed a novel metal-free nanozyme (HCN@DS) by utilizing hollow carbon nanospheres (HCNs) and dextran sulfate (DS), enabling specific targeting and inhibition of atherosclerosis through photoacoustic/photothermal imaging-guided autophagy activation in mice model. This metal-free nanozyme selectively accumulated within atherosclerotic plaques through specific binding to SR-A receptors expressed on the surface of macrophages or macrophage-derived foam cells. It exhibited excellent photoacoustic/photothermal imaging performance for precise treatment guidance. A mild phototheromal effect derived from this nanozyme was observed to induce autophagy of and provide cellular protection against apoptosis and necrosis, as well as stimulate ABCA1-dependent cholesterol efflux in macrophages via HSP27 upregulation, thereby inhibiting the progression of atherosclerosis. Additionally, a moderate release of ROS from this nanozyme was found to enhance cell autophagy and reduce lipid deposition of arterial intima. Furthermore, it demonstrated excellent biocompatibility both in vivo and in vitro. These findings suggest that HCN@DS holds promise as a potential strategy for the secure and precise treatment of atherosclerosis, thereby establishing a foundation for further investigation into such methods.
