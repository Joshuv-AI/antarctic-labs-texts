Ideality and reality collision: how perceived professional environment shapes Chinese medical undergraduates’ subject interest
Authors: Guobiao Li, Yi Yang, Liying Guo, Guojing Zhao, Shijie Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubject interest among medical students-an intrinsic motivation reflecting specialty preference-typically exhibits considerable variation. As curricular factors offer limited explanations for changes in college students' interests, existing studies have gradually shifted the research horizon from the curriculum environment to the overall professional environment. This study aimed to examine how undergraduate medical students’ perceived professional environment affects subject interest, with professional identity formation analyzed as a mediating factor.MethodsIn this study, we classified medical students' professional environments into three domains: medical school environment, regulatory environment, and social environment. Using a sample of 1,648 clinical medicine students from 14 universities across 6 Chinese provinces, we assessed: (1) the effects of perceived environmental factors on subject interest, and (2) the mediating role of professional identity formation.ResultsAll perceived professional environments significantly predict subject interest, and students’ professional identities mediate the relationship between the perceived professional environment and subject interest. Although perceived medical school and the social environment positively predict students’ professional identity, the perceived regulatory environment negatively predicts professional identity. Heterogeneity analysis indicated that male students and those with lower grades, higher annual score ranks and clinical experience performed better in terms of subject interest. The effect of the perceived social environment on professional identity formation was significant only for graduates. The effect of the perceived medical school environment on subject interest was significant only for freshmen.ConclusionsFindings reveal that a supportive professional environment is an effective model for increasing medical students' professional identity formation and subject interest. Further research is needed to explain other factors that may influence the causal relationship between the career environment and professional interest.Keywords: Subject interest, Medical education, Professional environment, Professional identity formation

Background

Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, China has strengthened healthcare workforce development, with undergraduate medical programs expanding continually. By 2021, the annual output of medical bachelor's degree graduates reached 302,039, maintaining a steady growth trajectory Ministry of Education of PRC, [33]. This expansion may dilute medical students' intrinsic subject interest through intensified competition for educational resources and clinical training opportunities. Concurrently, rising graduate numbers amplify employment pressures, potentially shifting motivation toward extrinsic factors (e.g., job security) rather than genuine academic curiosity. As prospective healthcare practitioners, medical students' subject interest extends beyond educational outcomes [5]. Recent research has established significant correlations between professional development and subject interest [48] Quinlan, and [36]. However, empirical evidence from China shows that medical students demonstrate objectively low levels of subject-specific interest [30], including those who initially made interest-driven specialty selections [54]. The question remains: what is the current status of specialty interest among students who initially demonstrated strong medical aspirations? What factors are associated with it?

While curricular design shapes students' subject interest [50, 51], environmental transitions play a complementary role in directing interest development [4]. The campus environment remains students' primary sphere of academic engagement, where pedagogical elements exert immediate influence on subject interest [29, 47]. However, Quinlan et al. [37] reported that students' interest is particularly associated with profession-specific contexts. Medical education presents unique epistemological characteristics within disciplinary taxonomy, distinguished by its applied nature and clinical rigor [8]. Unlike theoretical disciplines, medical training necessitates continuous navigation through complex clinical practice environments and professional socialization processes. This disciplinary particularity manifests conspicuously in medical students' interest through two distinctive mechanisms: first, early professional commitment establishes clear developmental trajectories; second, dual exposure in academic medical centers and clinical settings provides intensified professional enculturation compared with nonapplied disciplines. In addition, social contract theory in medical education [11] formalizes the critical relationship between clinical environmental interactions and professional identity development. This contractual framework underscores the reciprocal obligations inherent in medical students' engagement with healthcare ecosystems, thereby reinforcing the environmental determinants of sustained subject interest.

Research examining how professional environments shape medical students' subject interest remains scarce, with literature offering predominantly fragmented analyses of professional identity. Within this limited scholarship, the learning environment has emerged as a recognized conceptual framework. [15] highlighted that institutional support systems in medical education and clinical training settings substantially influence psychological development—particularly subject interest—and professional identity formation. While medical students in clinical environments do not hold the formal physician status, their experiential learning exposes them to workplace conditions remarkably akin to actual medical practice. The holistic professional environment invariably influences how medical students view their possible future careers. These include career status and socioeconomics [20], COVID-19 [9], and media coverage. Crucially, the regulatory environment's dual function requires particular attention—not only does it establish fundamental competencies for medical practice, but it also constructs an interpretative framework for understanding professional obligations (Drolet and [14, 49]. For example, the UK General Medical Council (GMC) has clarified the competency expectations and requirements for medical practitioners through professional guideline standards; the Chinese Medical Doctors' Association (CMDA) has also adopted a Charter for the Development of Medicine in the Millennium in response to the commercialization of healthcare and the lack of professionalism and has published a Declaration of the Chinese Physician [52], which sets out the professional values of medical students and doctors.

To identify the factors influencing medical students' subject interest, this study designed measures that fit the Chinese context and answered three questions: first, do professional environments outside of the classroom structure predict medical students' subject interest? Second, to what extent does the perceived environment influence professional identity formation? Professional identity formation implicitly involves an individual's identification with the values, emotions, and cognitive experiences of a particular profession, which are strongly linked to various components of an individual's interest [28, 42] Ainley and [3]. Third, do these relationships suggest a mediated pathway to increased subject interest? While combining previous explorations of the role of medical school contexts and other medical professional settings on students' subject interest, this study attempts to contribute to unraveling the black box of how environmental factors play a role in subject interest.

Literature and hypothesis development

Subject interest

The development of interest, defined as the psychological dynamics underlying an individual's orientation toward specific activities during environmental interactions [22, 41], has been classified into four stages by [23]. Two salient features are affective and value associations [28]: (1) affective associations are the positive emotional experiences of individuals during the activity, and (2) value associations are the high level of recognition of the value of the task by the individual. Previous research in higher education has examined factors, including individual experience, age, professional autonomy, and learning environments (Fryer & Bovee, [18]). Recent studies have reported inconsistent findings. Many consider the curriculum to be the primary driver in increasing student interest (Fryer and Bovee, [18]; [50, 51]. However, Quinlan and Corbin's ([38]) qualitative research demonstrates that courses may either strengthen subject interest or prompt students to recognize misalignment with their current major. [38] suggested that interest evolution depends on environmental support. Against the backdrop of institutions' growing focus on alignment between career services, collaboration, curriculum and job competency, profession-related elements of the environment (rather than course structure-related learning environments) are receiving attention from scholars [36]. Nevertheless, few studies have explored the mechanisms by which professional environmental factors play a role, especially for major factors that are as closely linked to vocation as medicine.

Perceived professional environment and its impact on subject interest

Established research often conflates professional environment with work/organizational environment, collectively framing them as employee-perceived psychological climate [20] encompassing occupational nature and income. When examining the medical student population, two critical points emerge. First, medical students’ perceived psychological climate predominantly stems from medical schools and hospitals, a construct described in existing research as the learning environment [15]. The learning environment, comprising medical schools’ supportive and clinical components, plays a pivotal role in professional identity formation [31]. Second, however, career concepts extend beyond specific institutions. Both occupational nature and compensation systems are governed by broader societal or industry-level factors. Societal demands and transformations disrupt all professions, as evidenced by the reciprocal relationship between the social environment and the medical profession [11], a dynamic reflecting a profession's current status, societal valuation, economic position, and trends. Guidelines and codes that are common to the profession form a regulatory environment that maintains stable and consistent identification with the profession (Drolet and [14]. Unfortunately, Mak et al.'s review (2021) identified only one study in medical education that focused on the professional regulatory environment. In summary, this study divided the professional environment into two levels: organizational (learning) and societal (regulatory and social).

Interest changes depend on how effectively the environment supports it [38]. One possible scenario is that students may develop ambivalent views, frustration, and stress about their subjects and professions through interaction with environments, leading to a decline in interest [15]. Interest-major congruence theory [6] posits that favorable professional environments enhance students' cognitive development. Students demonstrate a lower likelihood of changing majors and a greater probability of achieving satisfaction and success when the professional environment aligns with their interests [5]. Hollan ([25]) maintains that individuals with specific professional interests possess unique potential to adapt to environmental demands. Once a student's attention is drawn to a particular topic, the environment may reinforce the importance that students place on that topic and awaken an individual's affective experience, generating greater enthusiasm for learning [43]. Professional identity formation and its mediating role

It is generally accepted that professional identity is defined as a person's recognition and understanding of the beliefs, values, attitudes, and roles associated with his or her professional group, including the perception of self rather than others (Adam et al., [2]; Reees and Monrouxe, [39]). Professional identity formation is the process by which individuals and different elements of their lives shape their professional identity (Curess et al., [12]). For the sake of measuring professional practice, [45] argued that definitions of professional identity and professional identity formation need to be expanded and more inclusive. While definitions from different perspectives are useful, [31] concluded that professional identity and its formation are at least professional norms for expected behaviors and attitudes. In the medical profession, [53] argued that to clarify medical professional identity, a rich description of professional identity should involve the recognized professional attributes of the medical profession. The emphasis on medicine's professional attributes stems from the challenge medical education faces in fostering professional identity, which arises from the exclusion of medical humanities [35]. In other words, humanistic attributes are at the core of medical professional identity, and medical students who lack humanistic attributes are not good doctors.

Although [44] make it clear that medical professional identity and medical professionalism are completely different and interrelated concepts, there is a lack of delineation of conceptual boundaries [40]. From an empirical perspective, medical professional identity formation is an integral part of medical professionalism (ABIM, [1]; [13, 19]. Baldwin (2006) conceptualized medical professionalism as a process of ideological construction under the value orientation of the medical profession. Similarly, the formation of professional identity and professionalism are both adaptive development processes [27]. Current research on medical students’ professional identity has focused on undergraduate students, possibly because, for most medical students, the transition from an outsider identity to a medical professional identity begins in late adolescence [24]. As a major psychological struggle for college students, an important aspect of medical students' self-identity is their identification with the professional role of medicine. In summary, this study concluded that medical professionalism is based on strengthening the identity of medical professional attributes [26]. Medical professional identity formation is a combination of professional and personal selves and is largely influenced by how medical students assess their professional roles and responsibilities in light of the professional environment [17, 44].

A recent review [31] noted that the formation of professional identity in medicine is related to the individual, the professional environment (academic and clinical), and the profession in general. The ability of perceptions of professional environments to predict interest has been widely recognized in Western contexts [17]. Hendlman et al. ([21]) reported that the learning environment is a context for medical students' professional identity formation. [24] emphasized campus support to strengthen medical students' professional identity formation. Professional identity formation involves an individual's judgment of the values, emotions, and cognitive experiences of a particular occupation, a connotation that coincides with that of medical students' professional interests [28, 42] Ainley and [3]. [34] emphasized that one of the tasks of medical students in developing their professional identity is to face changes in their idealized doctor in harmony with the reality of modern medicine. The result of this harmonization may reveal value misalignment between personal and medical career paradigms, potentially generating negative subject perceptions [15]. In other words, perceptions of the professional environment generate a connection between medical students and the meaning of medical careers, which in turn helps them set goals and strive to make them realizable [36], which is precisely the process of interest formation. Methods

Setting and aim

The data used in this study were obtained through random distribution in May 2023 at 14 universities in Zhejiang Province, Shandong Province, Liaoning Province, Anhui Province, Shanxi Province, and Hunan Province, China. A total of 2764 participants took part in the questionnaire, and after eliminating the questionnaires that were obviously invalid, were logically contradictory, and had a high number of missing questions, 2424 valid questionnaires were obtained, with a validity rate of 87.7%. This study applied profession-interest congruence theory to examine how the professional interest of students who chose medicine due to subject interest was influenced by the professional environment. We asked students if they chose medicine because of their interest and used this to screen a sample of 1648 who met the requirements.

Survey procedure and measures

This study integrates China's localized medical education context with international scholarship, aligning with Standards for Basic Medical Education in China (Clinical Medicine, 2022 edition). Instrument development followed: literature synthesis of professional environment frameworks, several rounds of expert discussion and modification with data testing.

The dependent variable is subject interest. The respondents were asked, "How interested are you in the medical profession at this time?" and based on the result of a 5-point Likert scale, which is scored from 5 to 1 (5 = very interested, 1 = very uninterested).

The independent variables in this paper are perceived professional environment, which are classified into perceived medical school environment (α = 0.95), perceived regulatory environment (α = 0.95), and perceived social environment (α = 0.92). The perceived medical school environment (Curess, 2006) was measured in four dimensions: school climate, relevant stakeholder involvement, organizational setup, and school support; the perceived regulatory environment (Drolet and [14] measured medical students' knowledge of medical laws, professional standards in clinical medicine, and industry guidelines; and the perceived social environment [52] was measured in seven dimensions: global change, national needs, cultural traditions, social values, media coverage, the compensation system, and major emergencies. The perceptions of the professional environment were based on the results of a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 5 to 1 (5 = very consistent, 1 = very inconsistent).

The mediator variable is professional identity formation, which includes medical students' identification with the four dimensions of humanitarianism, sense of responsibility, virtue of the medical profession, and pursuit of excellence. Professional identity formation (α = 0.93) is the result of a 5-point Likert scale, which is scored from 5 to 1 (5 = very consistent, 1 = very inconsistent). Instead of directly using settings from other disciplines, our description was inspired by several studies and designed for medical students. On the one hand, [31] highlight professional identity and its formation as expected patterns and attitudes. On the other hand, professional identity is part of professionalism (Dann et al., [13]), and humanistic attributes are considered crucial to professional identity formation in medical education [35].

The control variables included gender [38], grade [6], political affiliation [52], place of domicile [47], annual score rank (Fryer and Bovee, [18]), and clinical experience [38]. The control variables were described in Table 1.

Results

Analysis of variance

ANOVA and t tests revealed no significant differences in subject interest by grade, political affiliation or place of domicile. Male students (M = 4.24, SD = 0.78) were slightly more interested in the medical program than female students were (M = 4.15, SD = 0.82, p < 0.05). Students with clinical experience (M = 4.22, SD = 0.82) were slightly more interested in the medical profession than were those without (M = 4.14, SD = 0.79, p < 0.1). Post hoc comparisons of annual score ranks revealed that 1 > 2 (SD = 0.35, p < 0.001), 1 > 3 (SD = 0.28, p < 0.001), 1 > 4 (SD = 0.27, p < 0.05), 2 < 4 (SD = 0.23, p < 0.05), 2 < 5 (SD = 0.23, p < 0.001), 3 < 4 (SD = 0.08, p < 0.001), 3 < 5 (SD = 0.08, p < 0.001), and 4 < 5 (SD = 0.06, p < 0.001).

Predictors of subject interest

Ordered logistic and hierarchical regression analyses were used to investigate whether the perceived professional environment predicted subject interest when controlling for the main demographic variables. The perceived regulatory environment (β = 0.447, p < 0.01), perceived medical school environment (β = 0.578, p < 0.01) and perceived social environment (β = 0.461, p < 0.01) significantly predict subject interest (Table 4), supporting hypotheses 1, 2 and 3. Following Baron and Kenny’s ([7]) causal steps approach to assess medication, we assessed whether students’ professional identity in medical careers mediated the relationship between the perceived professional environment and subject interest. As shown in Model 2, the perceived medical school environment (β = 0.236, p < 0.01) and perceived social environment (β = 0.209, p < 0.01) also significantly predict students’ professional identity. However, the perceived regulatory environment (β = −0.025, p < 0.05) negatively predicts professional identity. Finally, in Model 3, the indirect effect of professional identity was significant (β = 0.584, p < 0.01), and the perceived medical school environment (β = 0.439, p < 0.01), perceived social environment (β = 0.351, p < 0.01) and perceived regulatory environment (β = 0.471, p < 0.01) also significantly predict students’ subject interest, which means that hypotheses 4 and 6 were supported and that Hypothesis 5 was not supported. Thus, students’ professional identity mediated the relationship between the perceived professional environment and their subject interest.

Heterogeneity analysis

Subject interest is staged and changed [23]. Many studies focusing on subject interest [6, 38] and medical students [15, 21] have yielded differentiated results across grade levels, providing a basis for heterogeneity analysis. Therefore, this study was conducted to analyze heterogeneity in the formation of subject interest and professional identity for each of the five grades of clinical medical students.

The results (Table 5) revealed that gender differences appeared in subject interest only in year 3–4 cohorts, effects varied exclusively among freshmen, annual score ranking impacted subject interest at all grades. The effect of clinical experience appeared in the second year at the time of initial exposure to the clinical setting and in the graduating cohort. Heterogeneity is equally present in theoretical models. Specifically, the effect of the perceived regulatory environment on subject interest was significant at every grade level, whereas the effect on professional identity formation was significant only for graduates. The effect of the perceived medical school environment was significant for both subject interest and professional identity formation for students at every grade level. The perceived social environment had a significant impact primarily on professional identity formation among clinical medical students and significantly influenced subject interest among both second-year students and graduates. The effect of professional identity formation on subject interest was significant at any grade level.

Discussion

The findings suggest that the perceived professional environment of undergraduate clinical medicine students positively predicts subject interest (Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3). Regression analysis demonstrated that the perceived medical school environment and social environment stimulated professional identity formation, which positively mediated the relationships among the perceived medical school environment, perceived social environment, and subject interest (Hypotheses 4, 6). Contrary to our expectations (Hypothesis 5), the perceived regulatory environment inhibited professional identity formation; thus, professional identity formation exerts a negative mediating effect on the relationship between the perceived regulatory environment and subject interest.

The regulatory environment defines the institutions governing vocational operations, which are inseparable from social factors. Value and cultural mismatches constitute primary components of societal mismatches [10]. Individuals who perceive such mismatches typically assess their willingness to adapt. Unwilling individuals tend to withdraw from organizations when external opportunities exist while resorting to constructive advice, proactive action, and impression management in the absence of external opportunities. The British General Medical Council established societal expectations for medical practitioners' competence through professional guidelines and legislation, enforcing expected professionalism among medical students via coercive powers [49]. The laws and professional standards of China's medical profession emphasize the duties and responsibilities of the medical profession and professional ethics, but there is a lack of safeguards for the rights of the medical profession. This disparity triggers responsibility avoidance, diminished professionalism, and educational or career attrition among medical students [44, 46]. These findings explain the perceived regulatory environment's significant negative impact on professional identity formation.

Support from the school environment has been widely recognized in existing studies [15] Fryer and Bovee, [18]; [38]. However, few studies have focused on undergraduate students, and a gap stems from divergent training programs across countries and regions. For instance, we validated Hendelman and Byszewski’s ([21]) assertion regarding the need for repeated surveys to assess the impact of the learning environment. They emphasized that effective support systems—including deans, medical schools, clinical departments, and all clinical training sites—must address student development to cultivate medical professionalism in master's programs. In China, where medical students constitute a significantly large population, major selection occurs immediately after the college entrance exam (Ministry of Education of PRC, [33]). Given this critical period for developing professional identity [16, 24], our study examined the relationship between undergraduate education and professional identity formation. It was found that medical school environmental support enhances students' professional interest through their internalization of medical career values. This suggests that institutional educators should integrate professional education and career development [32, 40], to foster stable subject interests through enhanced medical identity formation.

The perceived social environment is a key integrative concept in this study, and the influence of the Chinese sociocultural context cannot be overlooked. The observed positive impact on medical students' subject interest and professional identity supports the conclusions of Chui et al. (2022), who identified societal expectations as a primary external factor that facilitates or constrains the development of physician professionalism. Drawing on cases involving medical students and physicians in Taiwan, China, Chui et al. (2022) reported that the COVID-19 pandemic strengthened physicians' sense of responsibility for public health, as well as their demand for and interest in medical professional knowledge—both in formal education and continuing education—and skills.

Implications for practice

Subject interest can be both innate and cultivated [23]. For interest related to personal traits, it is beneficial for students to identify them as early as possible and develop a broad understanding of the realities associated with those interests. Secondary education should initiate preliminary career planning through various activities that foster objective understanding of themselves, realistic profession-specific awareness, and socio-structural literacy. This process helps them establish a connection between their individual, professional, and professional identities, not only on the basis of personal ideals but also on the basis of societal reality. For students who lack a clear self-perception and have limited knowledge of career planning and academic majors, deepening their understanding of current subject interest is crucial. This can help prevent impulsive decisions from being withdrawn from studies due to insufficient information. In addition, broadening their awareness of diverse domains may be necessary. Self-knowledge can be enhanced through learning about others, whereas subject interest and competence can be fostered by improving the alignment between one's self-concept and the chosen subject or career.

Student subject interest should be prioritized in higher education. In addition to formal curricula, opportunities such as interprofessional studies and flexible transfer policies offer students avenues to explore different academic disciplines. In China, the limited exposure to specialized knowledge during the first two years of medical education is misaligned with the timing of major transfers, which typically occur at the end of the first year. Structural mismatch may exacerbate students' disidentification with their chosen majors and intended careers. An increase in medical students’ subject interest depends, to some extent, on support from the external environment, which is closely linked to professional experiences such as clinical experiences. Therefore, the training of future physicians in medical schools must involve multiple stakeholders to facilitate medical students' understanding of the realities of the medical profession.

Limitations and implications for research

A longitudinal design is needed to further clarify the causal relationship between students' subject interest, perceived professional environment and professional identity formation, particularly for studies that track students from secondary school through higher education. While China's experience offers valuable insights for regions or countries with similar contexts, caution should be exercised to avoid uncritical application. Our measures were tailored to the characteristics of medical education and may not be applicable to students in other academic disciplines. Further research conducted in other countries will enhance the generalizability of the findings. This study focused exclusively on undergraduate students in clinical medicine. Understanding how families support medical students is particularly important, given the importance of the influence of family expectations on subject and career choices, as well as the high financial cost of medical education.

Conclusion

This study concludes that the subject interest of undergraduate clinical medicine students is closely associated with the professional environment. To enhance students' subject interest and promote employability, universities must extend their focus beyond curricular content and pedagogical methods to actively foster the development of student interest and professional engagement. The findings indicate that even students with a strong interest in medical disciplines may be affected by the medical professional environment, which is often closely related to their identification with the medical profession. The formation of professional identity is a key factor in the transformation of medical students into members of the professional community. The results show that the perceived medical school environment and broader social environment provided a more supportive atmosphere—compared with the perceived regulatory environment—for medical students to identify with the profession and continue pursuing their medical aspirations. Students' attitudes toward the environment play a critical role in cultivating a stable subject interest. Therefore, scholars and policymakers may need to reconsider medical education by elevating profession-guided student formation as a central pedagogical tenet within medical training frameworks.
