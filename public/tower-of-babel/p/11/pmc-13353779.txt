Immune evasion in locally advanced mismatch repair-deficient microsatellite instability-high colorectal cancer: Reduced T-cell infiltration and upregulation of epithelial IDO1 expression
Authors: Sabina Niyazova, Christine Sers, Hendrik Bläker, David Horst, Wolfgang Schmitt, Zehra Karadeniz, Simon Schallenberg, Alexej Ballhausen, Dominik P Modest, Colin M Krüger, Eric P M Lorenz, Michael Hünerbein, Arndt von Kirchbach, Armin Jarosch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly-stage colorectal cancer (CRC) with confirmed microsatellite instability generally has a favorable prognosis associated with pronounced immune infiltration. However, some patients still develop metastases. The underlying mechanisms, particularly those related to the tumor immune microenvironment, remain incompletely understood. The study included tissue samples from 217 patients with dMMR/MSI-H CRC, comprising 89 stage III/IV and 128 stage I/II cases. Tissue microarrays and immunohistochemical analyzes were performed for all cases. We evaluated immune markers identifying T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, natural killer cells, macrophages, immunosuppressive markers, and immune checkpoint targets in epithelial and stromal compartments, and additionally performed a cohort-derived immune infiltration score (IIS). The survival analysis assessed the prognostic impact of immune markers stratified by tumor stage. Stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRCs showed significantly higher CD3⁺ T-cell and natural killer cell levels, higher IIS metrics across all regions, and higher CD4⁺ T helper cell levels in the stroma. Stage III/IV cases exhibited increased epithelial expression of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1. Given the limited number of stage IV patients, an additional stage III vs. stage I/II comparison was performed, revealing that these immune differences were already evident at the level of nodal progression. In addition, CD3⁺, CD4⁺, and CD8⁺ T cells and the IIS showed varying prognostic associations across tumor stages. These findings suggest that the overall immunogenicity and prognostic relevance of immune markers in dMMR/MSI-H CRC depend on tumor stage. Immune profiling could be used for early-stage patient stratification and also suggests the potential benefit of early immunotherapeutic intervention.

Introduction

The development of CRC is driven by a complex interaction of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors, with three dominant mechanisms: chromosomal instability (CIN), microsatellite instability (MSI), and the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP). MSI, which occurs in approximately 15%–20% of non-metastatic (stage I/II) CRCs, 1 , 2 develops from defects in the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) system. 3 , 4 These defects, referred to as mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR), arise through inactivation of key MMR genes such as MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2, leading to replication errors that accumulate particularly pronounced in microsatellites. 3 , 5 , 6 Tumors with instability in two or more specific microsatellite loci are classified as microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), strongly associated with dMMR. 3 Clinically, MSI-H/dMMR is enriched in right-sided colon carcinomas, whereas rectal cancer represents only a small fraction, approximately 5%, of dMMR/MSI-H CRC cases. 2

Up to one third of dMMR/MSI-H CRCs are associated with Lynch syndrome (3%–5% of all cases of CRC), driven by germline mutations in one of the MMR genes. 3 The remaining cases occur sporadically and are almost exclusively caused by silencing of the MLH1 promoter, 7 a hallmark of CIMP, which represents an epigenetic pathway marked by widespread hypermethylation of CpG islands in gene promoter regions. 4

Due to frequent genetic errors, dMMR/MSI-H CRC usually exhibits an elevated tumor mutational burden (TMB). 8 , 9 The resulting neoantigens enhance immunogenicity and underlie the recruitment of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). 8 , 9 As a result, dMMR/MSI-H CRC demonstrates a substantially higher density of TILs compared to mismatch repair-proficient/microsatellite stable (pMMR/MSS) CRC, underscoring a robust anti-tumor immune response. 8 Concurrently, dMMR/MSI-H CRC is associated with significantly improved survival outcomes in early-stage disease compared to MSS CRC. 1

Consequently, dMMR/MSI-H CRC shows pronounced sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI), both in locally advanced and metastatic settings. 10–13 This finding has significantly influenced therapeutic strategies for metastatic dMMR/MSI-H CRC and may pave the way for establishing neoadjuvant immunotherapy in locally advanced disease. 12 , 13 At the same time, dMMR/MSI-H tumors account for only 3%–5% of metastatic CRCs, 14 and their frequency is also lower in stage III disease than in stage I/II tumors, with approximately 8%–12% vs. 15%–20%. 1 , 2 Clinically, this is highly relevant, since lymph node involvement worsens prognosis, while pathological responses to conventional chemotherapy appear limited in dMMR/MSI-H CRC, supporting the need to explore alternative strategies such as immunotherapy already at a locally advanced stage. 12 , 13 , 15 , 16

These observations suggest that progression to locally advanced or metastatic disease may occur despite the generally high immunogenicity associated with dMMR/MSI-H CRC.

We therefore hypothesize that primary tumors of locally advanced or metastatic (stage III/IV) dMMR/MSI-H CRC are less immunogenic than their early-stage (stage I/II) counterparts, potentially facilitating immune evasion and metastasis.

To address the lack of detailed immune profiling data, we focused on two groups of dMMR/MSI-H CRC patients: those presenting with stage III or stage IV at initial diagnosis and those with stage I or stage II. For each tumor, we analyzed formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue from both the invasive front and tumor center using 12 immune markers: CD3, CD4, CD8, CD11c, CD20, CD56, CD68, CD163, forkhead box protein P3 (FoxP3), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1). By comparing the immune profiles of stage III/IV and stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRCs, we aimed to assess whether immunogenic differences exist that could drive nodal and distant metastatic progression. Additionally, we incorporated dMMR/MSI-H CRC patients’ survival data to examine the prognostic relevance of the immune markers.

Materials and methods

Study cohort

The study cohort consisted of tumor tissue samples from 283 patients with histologically confirmed CRC. This retrospective, multicenter study analyzed oncologic resections performed between 2009 and 2021 at Charité—Universitätsmedizin Berlin, Immanuel Hospital Rüdersdorf, Immanuel Hospital Bernau, St. Hedwig Hospital Berlin, and Oberhavel Kliniken Oranienburg and Hennigsdorf. Study procedure and analysis were conducted between 2021 and 2025.

This retrospective, non-interventional study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki using archived diagnostic tumor tissue and routinely collected clinical data. All data were pseudonymized before analysis. The study included only adult patients (≥18 y) and was approved by the local ethics committee of Charité—Universitätsmedizin Berlin (EA4/046/21), which waived the requirement for additional informed consent.

The cases were identified using a local pathology archive. All selected tumor samples were reviewed and re-evaluated at the Institute of Pathology, Charité—Universitätsmedizin Berlin. All tumors included in this study were classified as either dMMR, confirmed by IHC, or MSI-H, confirmed by PCR-based microsatellite instability testing. Throughout this study, we refer to the cohort as dMMR/MSI-H.

Of the 283 initially identified dMMR/MSI-H CRC cases, 217 met all tissue-related inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. Parameters used for verification of case eligibility and clinical and tumor-related characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Patients who received neoadjuvant therapy were excluded. Primary tumor location was categorized as right-sided (including cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, and transverse colon) or left-sided (including splenic flexure, descending colon, sigmoid colon, and rectum). Due to the predefined exclusion of patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy, which is frequently administered in rectal cancer according to guidelines, only one rectal carcinoma was included in the final evaluable cohort. In this case, neoadjuvant treatment had been declined by the patient.

Clinical follow-up data were collected until March 2025. Overall survival (OS) data were available for 187 patients, and progression-free survival (PFS) data were available for 114 patients, with median follow-up times of 53.78 months (~4.5 y) for OS analysis and 31.88 months (~2.7 y) for PFS analysis. These data were obtained from the cancer registry of the Charité Comprehensive Cancer Center (CCCC) and from the surgical centers where the patients were treated (Immanuel Hospital Rüdersdorf, St. Hedwig Hospital Berlin, and Oberhavel Kliniken Oranienburg and Hennigsdorf).

Tissue microarray construction and Immunohistochemistry

Tissue microarrays (TMAs) were constructed from FFPE tumor samples. Each tumor was represented by six punches: three from the invasive margin (IM) and three from the tumor center (TC), each 1.5 mm in diameter. The sections were mounted on SuperFrost Plus slides (R. Langenbrinck GmbH). In total, 24 TMAs were constructed: 10 for stage III/IV dMMR/MSI-H CRCs (n = 109 tumors) and 14 for stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRCs (n = 155 tumors). Liver tissue was included as a negative control in each TMA to verify staining specificity and serve as a reference point.

Representative punches were selected under microscopic examination of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections. Cases were excluded if none of the three punches from either the IM or TC region contained adequate tumor tissue, but rather adjacent healthy or fatty tissue, or had insufficient tissue quantity. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed semi-automatically using a Leica Bond III autostainer (Leica Biosystems). The following antibodies were applied: CD3, CD8, CD4, CD11c, CD20, CD56, CD68, CD163, FoxP3, IDO1, PD-1 and PD-L1. Heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) was conducted using appropriate buffers (e.g., citrate or ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-based), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A detailed list of antibody information and functions of the corresponding proteins is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

The final cohort available for statistical analysis included 89 tumors from stage III/IV dMMR/MSI-H CRC cases and 128 tumors from stage I/II cases. Image analysis

Stained TMA slides were digitized using a Pannoramic Slide Scanner (3D Histech, Budapest, Hungary) to enable quantitative assessment of immune cell infiltration in both the IM and TC regions. The quantification of immune cell populations was performed using the open-source software QuPath (v0.5.1) for bioimage analysis. 17 Epithelial and stromal compartments within each TMA punch were manually annotated, supported by QuPath's semi-supervised machine learning framework to ensure consistent and accurate tissue classification. Positive cells in immunohistochemically stained TMA punches were detected using QuPath's “positive cell detection” algorithm, applied separately to the epithelial and stromal compartments. The final parameters used for cell detection are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The complete image analysis workflow is illustrated in Figure 3.

Panel A demonstrates the QuPath digital analysis workflow showing the original stained punch section (left), tissue compartment classification into epithelium and stroma (middle), and positive cell detection (right). Panel B illustrates the cell classification system with color-coded detection for all 12 immune markers: epithelial (positive/negative: red/orange) and stromal (positive/negative: purple/green) compartments for standard markers, intensity-based scoring for IDO1 H-score (1 + 2 + 3 + positive/negative in epithelium: yellow, dark orange, red/light orange, 1 + 2 + 3 + positive/negative in stroma: light green, middle green, dark green/neon green) and tumor/immune cell distinction for PD-L1 (positive/negative tumor cells: red/orange, positive/negative immune cells: green/blue).

TMA punches with major artifacts or poor staining quality were excluded. In cases where the artifacts were localized, they were manually removed to retain the remaining evaluable tissue.

To normalize immune cell counts relative to tissue composition, the number of positive cells was divided by the total number of cells, yielding the percentage of positively stained cells. For each immune marker (except IDO1 and PD-L1), four representative expression values per tumor were calculated by averaging the percentages of positively stained cells across all evaluable punches in each compartment and region: epithelial and stromal areas in the invasive margin (ETIM, STIM) and tumor center (ETTC, STTC).

For IDO1 and PD-L1, specialized scoring systems were used. IDO1 expression was assessed using the H-score method, which integrates both the staining intensity and the proportion of positive tumor and immune cells. PD-L1 expression was evaluated using four established scoring systems in both IM and TC: the tumor area positivity (TAP), tumor proportion score (TPS), immune cell score (IC), and combined positive score (CPS). 18 In our dataset, CPS values exceeded 100 due to the underlying formula. CPS values are typically capped at 100 in clinical or pathological routine, a convention acknowledged in our interpretation.

Cohort-derived immune infiltration score

To quantify the antitumor immune response, we focused on the densities of CD3⁺ and CD8⁺ T lymphocytes, which are key components of the adaptive immune system and are established independent prognostic markers in CRC. 19 , 20 To derive a robust metric, we calculated a cohort-derived immune infiltration score (IIS) based on the proportions of intraepithelial CD3⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells within the invasive margin and tumor center (ETIM, ETTC), respectively. Each of the four relative values was converted into a cohort-specific percentile. The mean of these percentiles was then utilized to derive the final IIS for each tumor. This approach was adapted from the Immunoscore, 21 which is based on standardized whole-slide assessment and validated thresholds, whereas our metrics are derived from cohort-specific distributions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyzes were performed using R (version 4.4.3).

Differences in immune cell proportions, IIS, IDO1 H-scores, and PD-L1 scoring parameters between stage III/IV and stage I/II were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and visualized using boxplots. Due to the limited number of stage IV cases, stage III and IV tumors were combined for the main comparison, with additional pairwise analyzes of stage III vs. stage I/II and stage IV vs. stage I/II to explore stage-related differences further. P-values were adjusted separately for each comparison using the Benjamini‒Hochberg (BH) method.

Kaplan‒Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to compare OS and PFS between stage III/IV and stage I/II.

Prognostic associations of immune markers were assessed by univariate Cox proportional hazards models for OS and PFS. Multivariate Cox models included the respective immune marker, tumor stage (stage III/IV vs. stage I/II), their interaction term, and adjustment for age, sex, BRAF status, and adjuvant therapy. Cox model p-values were BH-adjusted within OS- and PFS-specific test families.

Only associations with p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

OS and PFS analyzes included only patients with available OS or PFS data, respectively. To additionally assess potential bias related to incomplete OS/PFS availability, patients with and without available OS/PFS data were compared across baseline characteristics using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, followed by multivariate logistic regression analyzes.

Results

Lymphoid infiltration is increased in early-stage dMMR/MSI-H CRC, while immunosuppressive markers are enriched in the epithelial compartment of locally advanced dMMR/MSI-H CRC

To characterize differences in the tumor immune microenvironment, we quantitatively compared immune marker expressions between stage III/IV and stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRCs in all four regions of interest: ETIM, STIM, ETTC, and STTC. A full summary of all 53 immune marker parameter Wilcoxon rank-sum test results (12 markers x four/eight regions + IIS = 53) is provided in the Supplementary Table 3a. CD3⁺ T cells were significantly more abundant in stage I/II tumors across all evaluated regions (p < 0.01). Within the T-cell population, CD4⁺ T helper cells were significantly elevated in the stromal compartments of stage I/II tumors (STIM: p = 0.0098; STTC: p < 0.001), with no significant differences observed in the epithelial compartments. We did not observe significant differences between CD8⁺ cytotoxic T-cell proportions in any compartment, although the epithelial compartment in the tumor center showed a trend toward higher levels in stage I/II tumors (p = 0.08). However, the IIS was significantly higher in stage I/II tumors compared to stage III/IV tumors (p = 0.001), consistent with a more robust T-cell-mediated immune contexture. Given the absence of clear differences in CD8⁺ T-cell proportions, the observed IIS difference was likely influenced predominantly by the CD3 component. Further, CD56⁺ natural killer (NK) cells demonstrated significantly higher proportions in stage I/II tumors in all regions (p < 0.001). Thus, lymphoid cells, specifically CD3⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T helper cells, and CD56⁺ NK cells, exhibited a significantly reduced presence in stage III/IV tumors.

In contrast, CD11c⁺ dendritic cells were significantly enriched in the epithelial compartments of stage III/IV tumors (ETIM: p = 0.002; ETTC: p < 0.001), with no corresponding differences in the stroma. A strongly compartment-specific pattern was also observed for IDO1 expression: IDO1 H-scores were significantly higher in the epithelial compartments of stage III/IV tumors (ETIM and ETTC: p = 0.003), while IDO1 expression in stromal compartments, thus not in tumor cells, was significantly elevated in stage I/II tumors (STIM: p = 0.001; STTC: p = 0.002). For PD-L1, only the TPS in the tumor center differed significantly between the groups, being higher in stage III/IV tumors (p = 0.023). No significant group differences were observed in TAP, IC, or CPS metrics. These findings demonstrate that while stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRC exhibits a higher T-cell infiltration and IIS, stage III/IV dMMR/MSI-H CRC upregulates potentially immunosuppressive markers in epithelial compartments.

To further dissect stage-related differences, we performed pairwise comparisons of stage III vs. stage I/II and stage IV vs. stage I/II. The stage III vs. stage I/II comparison reproduced the findings of the main stage III/IV vs. stage I/II analysis, showing identical directional changes and similar significant differences for the same markers and tumor regions, including CD3⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells, CD11c⁺ dendritic cells, CD56⁺ NK cells, IDO1 expression, PD-L1 TPS, and IIS. In contrast, the stage IV vs. stage I/II comparison did not remain significant after BH correction, although the uncorrected results showed similar directional trends for CD3, CD56, IDO1, and IIS (Supplementary Table 3b, Supplementary Table 3c).

T-cell infiltration shows a stage-dependent prognostic effect in dMMR/MSI-H CRCs

To provide clinical context for the immunological differences observed between stage III/IV and stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRC, we first compared survival outcomes between these two groups. While OS showed a nonsignificant trend towards shorter survival in stage III/IV cases (p = 0.12), PFS was significantly worse in the stage III/IV group (p < 0.001) (Figure 5). To evaluate the prognostic relevance of immune-related markers across the entire dMMR/MSI-H CRC cohort, we first performed univariate Cox regression analyzes for OS and PFS. We next constructed multivariate Cox regression models adjusted for age, sex, BRAF status, and adjuvant therapy, including an interaction term between immune marker expression and tumor stage (stage III/IV vs. stage I/II), to test whether the prognostic value of immune markers differed between stage III/IV and stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRCs. Consistent statistically significant associations are summarized below, with complete results provided in Supplementary Table 5a and Supplementary Table 5b. Finally, we applied BH corrections.

In univariate analyzes, higher proportions of epithelial PD-1⁺ cells in the epithelial compartments and higher IIS were associated with improved overall survival, whereas a higher IDO1 H-score in STTC correlated with a worse overall survival, unadjusted for any co-variates. However, none of these associations remained significant after BH correction.

For PFS, several markers showed unadjusted associations in individual compartments, including CD8, CD68, CD163, IDO1, and PD-L1 scores. After BH correction, the negative associations of IDO1 in ETTC and STTC (ETTC: HR = 1.016, 95% CI = 1.008–1.024, p = 0.005; STTC: HR = 1.026, 95% CI = 1.009–1.044, p = 0.048) and PD-L1 TPS in IM and TC (TPS IM: HR = 1.021, 95% CI = 1.007–1.035, p = 0.048; TPC TC: HR = 1.02, 95% CI = 1.007–1.033, p = 0.048) with PFS remained significant, whereas the other univariate signals were inconsistent and restricted to individual regions.

In multivariate analyzes, no adjusted main effect remained significant for OS after BH correction. In contrast, several significant interaction terms indicated stage-dependent prognostic effects: CD3⁺ T cells in ETIM (HR = 0.848, 95% CI = 0.727–0.988, p = 0.035), CD4⁺ T cells in STIM (HR = 0.829, 95% CI = 0.701–0.98, p = 0.028), CD8⁺ T cells in ETIM and ETTC (ETIM: HR = 0.833, 95% CI = 0.724–0.958, p = 0.011; ETTC: HR = 0.696, 95% CI = 0.503–0.964, p = 0.029), and IIS (HR = 0.901, 95% CI = 0.85–0.956, p < 0.001) showed stronger protective effects in stage III/IV tumors compared to stage I/II tumors. In contrast, IDO1 in STIM (HR = 1.078, 95% CI = 1.018–1.141, p = 0.01) showed a significantly different survival association in stage III/IV than in stage I/II.

For PFS, CD163⁺ cells showed a negative adjusted association in ETIM and STIM, although this did not survive BH correction. Significant interaction terms were observed for CD4⁺ T cells in the stromal compartments (STIM: HR = 0.865, 95% CI = 0.783–0.956, p = 0.005; STTC: HR = 0.887, 95% CI = 0.814–0.968, p = 0.007), CD11c⁺ cells in STTC (HR = 0.908, 95% CI = 0.847–0.973, p = 0.006), CD20⁺ cells in ETIM (HR = 0.686, 95% CI = 0.477–0.986, p = 0.042), and IIS (HR = 0.942, 95% CI = 0.898–0.987, p = 0.013), indicating stage-dependent differences in prognostic relevance, again with a stronger protective effect in stage III/IV compared to stage I/II.

Taken together, the prognostic analyzes suggest that T-cell–related markers, including CD3, CD4, CD8, and IIS, showed significantly better prognostic effects in stage III/IV tumors than in stage I/II tumors. Immunosuppressive markers, including IDO1, PD-L1, and CD163, were associated with poorer outcomes.

Since PFS data were available for only 114 of 217 patients, cases with and without available PFS data were compared, assessing potential systematic differences in missingness (Supplementary Table 6a). Missingness was significantly associated with treatment site, indicating that the availability of PFS data differed between hospitals, whereas no major differences were observed for the other examined baseline characteristics.

For OS, with data available for 187 of the 217 patients, missingness was also significantly associated with treatment site, while sex showed a borderline univariate difference that was not confirmed in the logistic regression analysis (Supplementary Table 6b).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine whether there are differences in the immunological landscapes of stage III/IV and stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRCs and how individual immune markers affect prognosis at different stages of disease progression in dMMR/MSI-H CRC.

The additional stage-stratified analyzes demonstrated that the immune differences identified in the main stage III/IV vs. stage I/II comparison were consistently reproduced in the stage III vs. stage I/II comparison. By contrast, only a small number of stage IV tumors were available, and the stage IV vs. stage I/II comparison did not remain significant after BH correction. Consequently, our findings are primarily informative for locally advanced stage III disease, whereas conclusions regarding metastatic disease should be interpreted with caution.

Our immunohistochemical analysis revealed that CD3⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T helper cells, and CD56⁺ natural killer cells, associated with effective antitumor activity, were consistently more abundant in stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRCs. The compartment-specific increased levels of these immune cell populations reflect their known biological distribution, e.g., T helper cells, predominantly in stromal regions. This spatially resolved immune infiltration underscores a more immunologically active tumor microenvironment in stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRCs. This is further supported by an elevated IIS in stage I/II cases. These findings align with previous literature indicating that strong immune cell infiltration correlates with limited tumor progression in dMMR/MSI-H CRC. 8 , 22 The significant CD3 findings and the corresponding IIS differences are likely not solely explained by cytotoxic T-cell infiltration. In our cohort, CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells showed only limited differences between stage III and I/II. While CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells are widely regarded as central mediators of antitumor immunity in CRC, 9 previous studies have also reported that CD3⁺ T-cell density can provide equal or even stronger prognostic information than CD8⁺ T-cell density, including in dMMR/MSI-H CRC. 20,23–25 Thus, our findings suggest that the stage-associated immune shift in dMMR/MSI-H CRC may involve a broader T-cell compartment. Besides CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, CD3 staining also captures CD4⁺ T-helper cells, regulatory T-cell subsets, and other non-CD8⁺ T-cell populations that may contribute to antitumor immune responses. 23 , 24 In addition, CD45RO⁺ memory T cells, which were not assessed in the present study, include both antigen-experienced CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells and have been linked to improved immune control in CRC. 20 , 26 Future studies, including multi-marker analyzes such as multiplex immunohistochemistry or double staining approaches, will be required to further dissect the cellular composition of the CD3⁺ T-cell infiltrate and clarify the mechanisms underlying these stage-associated differences.

The PD-L1 expression did not differ significantly between stage III and stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRCs. Although tumor proportion scores appeared significantly elevated in the tumor centers of stage III cases, no differences were observed for the other scores. PD-L1 is a well-established functional marker of adaptive immune resistance in several cancer entities, including dMMR/MSI-H CRCs. 27–31 The limited stage-associated differences observed in our cohort, therefore, do not exclude a functional role of immune checkpoint signaling in disease progression.

Epithelial PD-1 was significant in univariate OS models but did not persist after correction or multivariate adjustment, likely reflecting intraepithelial T-cell infiltration rather than a distinct biological phenomenon. 28 As immunohistochemical assessment was limited to a single marker per slide, spatial colocalization with PD-L1-expressing cells could not be determined.

IDO1 displayed a compartment-specific expression pattern: Stromal IDO1 expression was higher in stage I/II dMMR/MSI-H CRCs, whereas epithelial expression dominated in stage III cases. IDO1 is an IFN-γ-inducible enzyme that degrades tryptophan to kynurenine, leading to immunosuppression. 27 , 32 In the tumor microenvironment, IDO1, expressed by tumor cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, 33 suppresses CD8⁺ cytotoxic T-cell function and promotes regulatory T-cell (Treg) expansion. Previous reports have also indicated higher IDO1 expression in the context of other tumor suppressive markers in CRC. 34 , 35

Early-stage stromal IDO1 probably originates from immune-regulatory cells responding to active local immunity, whereas epithelial IDO1 in advanced tumors may arise from intrinsic tumor signaling or recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, consistent with reduced immune infiltration and IFN-γ activity. Supporting this, Pflügler et al. found that high epithelial IDO1 correlated with worse outcomes, while stromal IDO1, particularly when epithelial levels were low, was associated with better immune activation and prognosis. 35 IDO1 has also been implicated as a molecular integrator of immune escape and vascularization during metastasis. 33 Together, these findings suggest that IDO1's role in tumor progression is shaped by its spatial distribution and immune context, and that compartment-specific effects should be considered when targeting IDO1 in dMMR/MSI-H CRC.

Adjusted survival analyzes indicated negative associations between CD163⁺ M2-type macrophages and PFS in epithelial and stromal invasive margin compartments. Although these associations did not remain significant after BH correction, the observed direction of prognostic effect is consistent with the well-established association between M2-type macrophages and unfavorable prognosis in CRC, including MSI-H CRC. 36 , 37

CD3⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T helper cells, CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, and IIS showed stage-dependent differences in prognostic impact across OS and PFS analyzes, with generally more favorable associations observed in stage III/IV than in stage I/II tumors. As noted at the outset, the limited number of stage IV cases indicates that these observations are likely driven primarily by locally advanced tumors.

Ballhausen et al. showed that dMMR/MSI-H tumors are shaped by selective pressure against highly immunogenic frameshift-derived neoantigens, although certain peptides, such as those from TGFBR2, persist because of their dual functional and immunogenic roles. This suggests that some tumors retain targetable neoantigens despite immune editing. 38 In contrast, impaired immune responses in advanced tumors may result from immune escape mechanisms such as B2M mutations, which disrupt Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I presentation and render tumors invisible to cytotoxic T cells. 38 , 39 These cases highlight that reduced antigen presentation, rather than neoantigen absence, may explain reduced immune reactivity. Integrating B2M mutation status and neoantigen profiling into stratification strategies could further improve the understanding of immunogenic diversity in dMMR/MSI-H CRC.

This may also clarify why some advanced tumors, despite their stage, show a more favorable prognosis, potentially driven by retained immunogenic mutations like those in TGFBR2, CASP5, or ACVR2A. 40 Whether these specific mutations directly underlie the observed survival benefit of stage III/IV dMMR/MSI-H CRCs remains an open question and presents a promising avenue for further investigation. These findings support combining immune checkpoint inhibition with “off-the-shelf” neoantigen vaccines, as proposed by Roudko et al. 39 particularly in cases with shared, immunogenic alterations. These mechanisms are not only relevant for understanding prognosis but also for therapeutic responsiveness. Although dMMR/MSI-H CRC is generally considered highly immunogenic, recent clinical data show that only 30%–50% of patients respond to ICIs. As highlighted by Sahin et al., this limited efficacy is often due to intrinsic or acquired resistance mechanisms, including impaired antigen presentation (e.g., B2M or HLA mutations), disruptions in IFN-γ signaling (e.g., JAK1/2 mutations), or an immunosuppressive microenvironment shaped by TGF-β and WNT pathways. These factors may prevent adequate immune engagement despite the presence of abundant neoantigens. 40 , 41

Our findings contribute to these previously observed immunosuppressive mechanisms by demonstrating that stage III dMMR/MSI-H CRCs are characterized by reduced T-cell infiltration and increased epithelial expression of immunosuppressive markers such as IDO1 and PD-L1. This stage-associated shift in immune contexture suggests that mechanisms that attenuate effective immune-mediated control emerge already during nodal progression, likely contributing to variable responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibition. Understanding these resistance mechanisms will be essential to improve therapeutic efficacy in advanced disease. Our data underscore the need to better define the immunobiological determinants shaping treatment responsiveness throughout disease progression. Moreover, the observed CD3/CD8 discrepancy suggests that stage-associated immune remodeling may involve broader T-cell populations beyond cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells, emphasizing the need for more refined immune profiling. Notably, immune activation in early-stage dMMR/MSI-H CRC appeared more pronounced than in locally advanced disease, suggesting that checkpoint inhibition may be most effective when applied before immunosuppressive features become dominant. This supports the potential benefit of immunotherapy in localized dMMR/MSI-H CRC, beyond its current use being largely restricted to metastatic settings.

This study had limitations related to its methodology and sample structure. Single-marker immunohistochemistry on serial TMA sections limited the assessment of co-expression patterns, functional immune states, and cell–cell interactions, while TMAs may not fully capture intratumoral spatial heterogeneity. In addition, some markers showed low epithelial abundance, e.g., FoxP3 and CD163, limiting interpretability in these compartments. The analysis was quantitative and descriptive, without insight into functional immune states or cell‒cell interactions. PD-L1 CPS assessment on TMA cores requires cautious interpretation, as values may exceed 100 in small tissue areas with variable composition, and should be viewed as cohort-specific associations rather than direct equivalents of whole-slide assessment. Nevertheless, comparable TMA-based approaches have been previously reported, supporting the feasibility of this method. 42–44

The incomplete availability of outcome data, particularly for PFS, limits uniform longitudinal follow-up. No major systematic differences were observed for baseline characteristics, although residual bias cannot be excluded, and the reduced availability of PFS data still limited the sample size for subgroup and marker-specific analyzes.

Although the cohort was analyzed as colorectal cancer, only one rectal carcinoma was included, reflecting both the generally lower frequency of dMMR/MSI-H in rectal cancer 45–47 and the exclusion of neoadjuvant-treated cases to avoid treatment-induced changes in immune infiltration. Therefore, the study is primarily informative for colon cancer, while rectal cancer-specific conclusions remain limited.

In summary, our findings highlight both stage-associated differences in the immune landscape of dMMR/MSI-H CRC and stage-specific differences in the prognostic relevance of immune markers. The observed reduction in T-cell infiltration, together with increased expression of immunosuppressive markers in stage III tumors, suggests that immune remodeling occurs already during nodal progression. These results highlight the importance of spatially resolved immune profiling for understanding disease heterogeneity and support further investigation of immunotherapeutic strategies earlier in the disease course.

Future studies should explore whether immunomodulatory targets such as IDO1 are functionally active even at early stages, potentially before overt immune escape mechanisms evolve. These observations call for advanced approaches that integrate functional assays with spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed imaging to better understand immune cell interactions and resistance mechanisms in dMMR/MSI-H tumors.

Ultimately, a more nuanced view of the immuno-oncological spectrum of dMMR/MSI-H CRC is critical for improving prognosis and personalized treatment strategies.
