Long-term results of the HD0607 trial on interim PET-adapted treatment of advanced-stage Hodgkin lymphoma beyond 10 years of follow-up

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundIn advanced-stage classic Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL), risk-adapted therapeutic strategies have proven to be safe and effective, although long term efficacy data are lacking. Furthermore, some concern persists about the risk of late occurrence of second primary malignancies (SPM) or cardiovascular events (CVE) in survivors.MethodsHD0607 is a phase-2 multicenter prospective clinical trial enrolling stage IIB-IV patients with untreated cHL. All subjects started treatment with 2 ABVD cycles and subsequently underwent an interim PET (PET-2). PET-2 positive patients [Deauville score (DS) 4–5] switched to BEACOPP escalated/baseline (4 + 4 cycles), while PET-2 negative patients (DS ≤ 3) were treated with 4 more ABVD cycles. Patients with a large nodal mass (LNM, defined as ≥5 cm in diameter) at baseline and both PET-2 and end-of-chemotherapy (EoT) PET negative scans were randomly allocated to receive consolidation radiotherapy (cRT) or no further therapy (NFT). This clinical trial has been registered in the European Union Drug Regulating Authorities Clinical Trials Database under the EudraCT number 2007-007168-94 and is publicly available on the EU Clinical Trials Register.FindingsOut of 782 enrolled subjects, 630 (81%) had a negative while 150 (19%) a positive PET-2. After a median follow-up of 11 years (IQR 7–13 years), 102/630 patients with a negative PET-2 (16.2%) and 57/150 (38.0%) with a positive PET-2 progressed or relapsed. The 10-years PFS and OS were 83% and 96% vs. 58% and 85% (p < 0.0001) for PET-2 negative and PET-2 positive patients, respectively. Patients randomized to NFT did not have an increased risk of relapse or death (PFS HR 0.67, 95%CI 0.31–1.43; OS HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.09–2.68). During the follow-up 28 (3.6%) and 16 (2.1%) patients developed a SPM and CVE, respectively.InterpretationAfter more than a decade of follow-up the HD0607 trial confirmed: i) the long-term predictive value of PET-2; ii) the persistent and sustained remission rate of PET-2 negative patients; iii) no PFS benefit consolidation RT over a non-FDG avid LNM; iv) the cumulative low incidence of SPM and CVE in the enrolled patients.FundingThis work was supported by 10.13039/501100005010Associazione Italiana per la Ricerca sul Cancro (AIRC), IG n.2013 to AG, by Associazione Italiana Lotta alla Leucemia (AIL) sezione di Bergamo and by a Research Grant of Cassa di Risparmio di Cuneo. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Before undertaking this long-term follow-up analysis of the HD0607 trial, we performed a structured literature search in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, complemented by manual review of reference lists from relevant articles and conference proceedings (ASH, ASCO, EHA). The search covered studies published from January 1, 2005 to June 30, 2024, without language restrictions.

Search terms included combinations of: “advanced-stage classic Hodgkin lymphoma,” “PET-adapted,” “interim PET,” “PET-2,” “ABVD,” “BEACOPP escalated,” “RATHL,” “SWOG S0816,” “HD0607,” “Brentuximab Vedotin,” “checkpoint inhibitors,” “second primary malignancies,” “cardiovascular toxicity,” and “long-term follow-up.”

We included prospective randomized or phase II/III clinical trials evaluating PET-2–adapted strategies in newly diagnosed advanced-stage classic Hodgkin lymphoma (as-cHL). Retrospective series, pediatric-only cohorts, and studies lacking outcome stratification by interim PET response were excluded.

The available evidence consistently demonstrated that escalation to BEACOPP in PET-2–positive patients improved progression-free survival compared with continued ABVD (HD0607, RATHL, SWOG S0816). Medium-term follow-up (5–7 years) confirmed durable disease control with PET-adapted approaches. However, none of the available studies reported long-term follow-up beyond 10 years, and robust data on late toxicities—particularly second primary malignancies (SPM) and cardiovascular events (CVE)—remained scarce.

Across randomized trials, pooled progression-free survival for PET-2–negative patients treated with ABVD-based regimens ranged between approximately 80–85% at 5 years, while PET-2–positive patients escalated to BEACOPP achieved 3–5-year PFS rates of approximately 55–70%, superior to historical non–PET-adapted ABVD cohorts. Risk of bias across the pivotal trials was low to moderate, given the prospective design, predefined PET interpretation criteria (Deauville scale), and, the blinded centralized scan review for PET scans; however, heterogeneity in escalation protocols and limited long-term safety reporting were notable limitations.

At the time of study conception, no published data had reported outcomes beyond 10 years for PET-2–adapted strategies in a large prospective cohort with systematic assessment of long-term morbidity and mortality.

Added value of this study

This study provides the longest follow-up (more than 11 years) reported to date for a large prospective PET-2–adapted cohort in advanced-stage cHL.

Our findings demonstrate a sustained long-term disease control, with 85% of PET-2–negative patients remaining progression-free and 58% of PET-2–positive patients achieving durable remission after escalation to BEACOPP. Overall survival reached 96% across the entire cohort. Importantly, long-term safety outcomes were favorable, with very low cumulative incidences of second primary malignancies (3.6%) and cardiovascular events (2.1%) after median follow-ups of 8.7 and 7.0 years, respectively.

By providing mature efficacy and safety data, this study addresses a critical gap in the literature: whether PET-2–guided intensification maintains long-term benefit without incurring unacceptable late toxicity. The results confirm that a flexible-intensity PET-adapted approach achieves durable disease control while limiting long-term morbidity compared with intensified regimens.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together with previous PET-adapted trials (HD0607, RATHL, SWOG S0816), the present long-term results support PET-2–guided therapy as a safe, effective for advanced-stage classic Hodgkin lymphoma.

In clinical practice, these findings reinforce the use of interim PET to personalize treatment intensity—escalating therapy only in the approximately 15–20% of patients with PET-2–positive disease—thereby minimizing exposure to overtreatment and long-term toxicity in most patients.

From a policy perspective, PET-adapted ABVD-based strategies remain particularly relevant in settings where novel agents such as checkpoint inhibitors are not reimbursed or widely available, and the demonstrated long-term safety profile strengthens the global applicability of this approach, including in medium- and low-income countries.

Introduction

Positron Emission Tomography (PET), associated with computed tomography performed early during treatment after 2 cycles (PET-2) of ABVD (doxorubicine, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine) or escalated BEACOPP (bleomycin, etoposide, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, procarbazine, and prednisolone) chemotherapy, is the most powerful predictive factor on treatment outcome in advanced-stage classic Hodgkin Lymphoma (cHL), even superseding a number of classic prognostic indexes and individual factors, including IPS (International Prognostic Score), mediastinal bulk, and extra-nodal disease.1, 2, 3 As a consequence, as soon as robust criteria for PET-2 interpretation became available,4,5 a Blinded Independent Central Review (BICR) for a timely central review of PET images by experts feasible, and thanks to a WEB-based informatic tool for PET image BICR,6,7 a PET-2 driven strategy with a flexible chemotherapy intensity was proposed for the treatment of advanced-stage cHL.

Thanks to the above-mentioned facilities, a PET-2 adapted strategy was proposed in the early 2000s in several prospective clinical trials in advanced-stage cHL. This strategy, based on an early chemosensitivity assessment of the disease with PET-2, was aimed at exploring the efficacy of a therapeutic approach consisting in treatment intensification to escalated BEACOPP in patients showing a chemoresistance to ABVD (with a positive PET-2), or, vice versa, in a treatment de-escalation with ABVD or to a reduced number of escalated BEACOPP cycles for patients with an early response to escalated BEACOPP and a negative PET-2. The results of these trials confirmed a better outcome for advanced stage cHL patients compared to historical controls treated with a non-PET-adapted ABVD regimen.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 In patients with a positive PET-2 switching to BEACOPP escalated after 2 ABVD cycles, a long-term follow-up of five years or more confirmed a durable response after treatment intensification, with very few patients relapsing beyond three years of follow-up, and only a slight reduction of ABVD efficacy overtime for patients with a negative PET-2 continuing with ABVD or AVD for a total of six cycles.9,11 On the other hand, patients treated upfront with BEACOPP escalated switching to ABVD after a negative PET-2 had an unchanged long-term disease control compared to a non-PET adapted strategy, while receiving a reduced treatment burden and experiencing a much lower treatment-related toxicity.14, 15, 16 Notably, patients starting with BEACOPP escalated or BrECADD (brentuximab vedotin, etoposide, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, dacarbazine, and dexamethasone), treated with a reduced number of cycles from 6 to 4 after a negative PET-2 sustained an unchanged treatment efficacy compared to patients treated with the original block of 6 CT cycles, leading to an unchanged long-term disease control for the overall population of patients included in the trial, or, surprisingly, even a better overall long-term disease control compared to the classic non-PET adapted BEACOPP escalated regimen.15,16 In addition, de-escalation strategies from initial BEACOPP-like chemotherapy consistently results in higher PFS rates in comparison to escalation from initial ABVD treatment.14, 15, 16

No data exist, at the time of this writing, on long-term outcomes (10 years or more) of clinical trials in advanced stage cHL adopting a PET-adapted therapeutic strategy. We herein report the long-term results of the GITIL/FIL HD0607 clinical trial after a patient observation spanning beyond ten years.

Methods

Treatment plan

The treatment details of the patients enrolled into the HD0607 trial (NCT identifier 00795613, EudraCT code 2007-007168-94) on behalf of the Italian multicenter GITIL-FIL cooperative group have been previously described in detail.13 The trial was launched in June 2008 in 25 Italian and 1 Israeli clinical site. Briefly, after a complete physical examination and blood test sampling, (1) a contrast-enhanced (Ce)CT scan of chest and abdomen, (2) a whole-body PET/CT and (3) a bone marrow trephine biopsy was performed on consecutively admitted, intermediate (stage IIB) or advanced-stage (III-IV) patients, with a biopsy-proven cHL. Upon signing a written informed consent all the patients completing the above staging procedures have been prospectively enrolled in the HD0607 trial.

Enrolled patients were treated with 2 cycles of ABVD, and an early interim PET was performed afterwards (PET-2), 11–13 days after the end of the second ABVD course. PET-2 positive patients with a scan scored 4 or 5, according to the Deauville 5-point scale (DS) underwent treatment intensification by a random assignment to 4 courses of escalated + 4 courses of baseline BEACOPP or the same regimen supplemented by Rituximab, 375 mg/m2 iv, given on day 1 of each BEACOPP course. Patients with a negative PET-2 (with a DS 1–3) continued the standard ABVD treatment for a total of 6 courses. Enrolled patients presenting at baseline with a large nodal mass (LNM), defined as a single or multiple conglomerated nodes with the largest diameter measuring at least 5 cm and no more than 10 cm. In CeCT scan and a negative restaging PET after 6 ABVD cycles, were randomly allocated to receive consolidation radiotherapy (cRT, 30 Gy) on the site of a LNM detected at diagnosis or no further therapy (NFT). Aims of the long-term updates

The aim of assessing the long-term results of the HD0607 trial after a very long follow-up spanning over more than 10 years (until September 2025) consist in evaluating the long-term efficacy of the above-mentioned PET-2 adapted therapeutic strategy and in assessing the cumulative incidence of the most clinical relevant late toxicities, such as second primary malignancies (SPM) and cardiovascular events (CVE) in the enrolled patients. In the context of this long-term follow-up study, the present report will focus on (1) the disease status (relapse or continuous complete remission—CCR), Overall Survival (OS), Progression-Free Survival (PFS) at the last follow-up; (2) the details of eventual further lines of therapy, (3) the type and cumulative incidence of SPM and (4) the long-term cumulative incidence of CVE. As such, the endpoint of the present analysis were the 10-year PFS and OS, as well as the cumulative incidence of SPM and CVE.

Statistical analysis

The statistical design underpinning the HD0607 trial was designed to produce a benefit, in terms of 3-year PFS, of a PET response adapted strategy compared to historical controls of ABVD-treated patients. The calculation of the sample size was reported in the former publication.13 For the present analysis, continuous variables were compared with Student’s t-test and categorial variable with Fisher’s exact test. Normal distribution of age was verified by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Time-to-event data were analyzed with the Kaplan–Meier method, with death as the only event for OS estimates and death, progression or relapse as being considered events to calculate PFS since the registration date. Hazard ratios (HR) for intergroup comparisons were estimated with Cox’s regression analysis after assessing for the validity of the proportional hazard assumption. Cumulative incidence, with 95% confidence interval (CI), for SPM and CVE were evaluated since the registration date with the Fine and Gray method, utilizing non-SPM deaths and non-CVE deaths as competing events, respectively. No formal adjustment for multiple testing was applied as the analyses were limited to prespecified, clinically relevant time-to-event endpoints. Analyses were carried out with R version 4.5.0 and R Studio version 2025.05.0 for Mac, with p-values ≤ 0.05 being considered as statistically significant.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects prior to their participation in the study. The study was approved by the Santa Croce e Carle Hospital institutional review board (identification number Emato 16), and has been registered in the European Union Drug Regulating Authorities Clinical Trials Database under the EudraCT number 2007-007168-94 and is publicly available on the EU Clinical Trials Registry.

Role of the funding source

The supporting funders had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Long-term outcomes

After a median follow-up time of 11 years (IQR 7–13 years), a total of 47 (6.0%) deaths and 159 (20.3%) progression events have been recorded, with 91 (11.6%) individuals experiencing progression before the achievement of a complete response (CR), and 68 (8.7%) patients relapsing after the completion of chemotherapy. Among the latter, 10 (1.3% of the total enrolled patients, 14.7% of the relapses) subjects had a late relapse (i.e., occurring after at least 5 years from the obtainment of a CR). Considering the entire intention-to-treat population, the median PFS and OS have not been reached, whereas the 10-year PFS and OS were 78% (95% CI 75–81) and 94% (95% CI 92%–96%), respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B).

Data on second-line therapy was available for 136 of the 159 (85.5%) patients who experienced progression or relapse. Among the remaining 23 patients, 2 died before initiating salvage treatment, and 21 had incomplete clinical information. Seventy patients (51.5% out of the evaluable 136 relapsed patients) required a third and 32 (23.5% a fourth line of treatment). Salvage therapies are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. The more commonly used second line of therapy was IGEV (Ifosfamide, Gemcitabine, Vinorelbine, Prednisolone, n = 56, 41.2%) followed by platinum-based chemotherapy (n = 38, 27.9%), high-dose sequential chemotherapy (n = 18, 13.2%) and BEGEV (Bendamustine, Gemcitabine, Vinorelbine, Prednisolone n = 10, 7.4%). Overall, the second line therapy led to a complete or partial remission in 61 (44.9%) and in 15 (11.0%) out of 136 relapsed patients, respectively (see Supplementary Table S1). Regarding the use of targeted agents, 57 (41.9%) patients received brentuximab-vedotin, and 9 (6.6%) received the anti PD-1 checkpoint inhibitors pembrolizumab (5) or nivolumab (4). One hundred and five (77.2%) and 30 (22.1%) patients received autologous and allogenic stem cell transplantation, respectively.

Furthermore, an exploratory analysis evaluating the long-term impact of the Ann-Arbor stage at baseline (IIB to IVB) was performed, showing how stage IV subjects experienced the worst outcomes, with a 10-year PFS and OS of 70% (95% CI 65–77) and 89% (95% CI 85–93), respectively (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Long-term impact of PET-2 and of radiotherapy on LNM

All but two enrolled patients but two (780 patients) received 2 cycles of ABVD and underwent an interim PET (PET-2) assessment (Fig. 1). Two patients died during the first 2 courses of ABVD from disease progression and cardiac failure, respectively. Six-hundred and thirty patients (80.8%, Fig. 1) had a negative PET-2, 520 (82.5%) of them had a continuous CR, 55 (8.7%) relapsed after CR entry, 47 (7.5%) never entering CR had a progressive disease, 4 (0.6%) died in remission and 3 patients withdrew their consent early after study entry. Conversely, among the 150 patients with a positive PET-2, 81 (80.7%) had a continuous CR, 13 (8%, DS 4 n = 8 and DS 5 n = 5) relapsed after achieving a CR, 44 (10.7%, DS 4 n = 17 and DS 5 n = 27) progressed, 2 (0.6%) died in remission and 10 patients withdrew early from the study. All but one of the 10 late relapses occurred in the PET-2 negative subgroup. The 10-year PFS and OS were 83% (95% CI 80–86) and 96% (95% CI 94–98) vs. 58% (95% CI 51–67) and 85% (95% CI 80–92) for patients with negative vs. positive PET-2, respectively (Fig. 2A and B). Even after shifting to an intensified treatment schedule, patients with a positive PET-2 (i.e., DS 4–5) had almost 3-fold higher risk of progression, relapse or death (HR 3.16, 95% CI 2.30–4.34, p < 0.0001) and death (HR 3.84, 95%CI 2.13–6.92, p < 0.0001), compared to subjects with a negative PET-2. Long-term incidence of second primary malignancies (SPM)

After a median time from enrollment into the study of 8.7 years (range 1–14.9 years), 28 (3.6%) patients developed a SPM (Table 2) with a 10-year cumulative incidence of SPM of 2.8% (95% CI 1.7–4.2, Fig. 4A). Patients who developed SPMs were older, with a median age of 42.5 vs. 31 years (p = 0.0015). Out of the 28 SPM reported, the most frequently recorded diagnosis was non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) in 8 patients (28.6% and 1.1% of the intention-to-treat population), with the following breakdown: B-cell lymphoma (6 patients) T/NK-cell lymphoma (2 patients). The second most recorded SPM was breast cancer in 5 subjects (17.9% and 0.6% of the intention-to-treat population), followed by colon cancer in 4 patients, (14.3%, 0.5%), skin cancer in 3 patients (2 melanoma and 1 squamous skin carcinoma, 10.7%, 0.4%), urothelial carcinoma, renal cancer and thyroid carcinoma all three detected in 2 patients (7.1%, 0.3%), germinal tumor of the testis and B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B ALL) in 1 patient (3.6%, 0.1%). Notably, breast carcinoma occurred in 4 patients who did not receive RT and in only 1 treated with mediastinal RT to baseline LNM. Two patients treated with chemotherapy without RT developed thyroid carcinoma. The patient who developed B-ALL one year after treatment end had a positive PET-2 and received four courses of BEACOPP escalated followed by 4 courses of BEACOPP baseline (Table 2). However, neither myelodysplastic syndrome nor acute myeloid leukemia have occurred so far. Noteworthy, patients who intensified treatment (HR 1.90, 95% CI 0.71–5.07, Fig. 4C) and those who received RT (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.14–2.34, Fig. 4E) did not show a higher cumulative incidence of SPM compared to the entire population of patients. Long-term incidence of cardiovascular events (CVE)

Sixteen (2.1%) patients developed CVE, including 3 (0.4%) who experienced more than one event (Table 2). The median time to CVE was 7.0 years (range 0.1–14.29 years) and the 10-year cumulative incidence on CVE was 2% (95% CI 1.1-3-3, Fig. 4B). Patients who developed CVEs were older compared to the whole population (p = 0.0025), and the median age at enrollment was 38 years (IQR 34–49.5). Ten patients developed congestive heart failure (CHF), accounting for 62.5% of the CVEs and 1.3% of the enrolled population and the median time from enrolment to CHF was 5.5 years. Three (19%, 0.4% of the intention-to-treat population) patients experienced acute coronary syndrome (ACS) after a median time from diagnosis of 7.5 years. Other reported CVEs occurred in 6 patients, 2 atrial fibrillation, 2 sudden cardiac death, 1 myocarditis and 2 not specified CVE occurred after a median time of 6.94 years (Table 2). Of note, out of 16 patients experiencing CVE, five (31.3%) received cRT, 3 (18.8%) were shifted to BEACOPP after a posotove PET-2. One patient had a sudden death for an acute heart failure 2 days after the enrollment. All the 3 individuals with ACS did not receive RT. Patients who intensified treatment for a positive PET-2 (HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.33–4.11, Fig. 4D) and those who received cRT (HR 1.29, 95% CI 0.29–5.67, Fig. 4F) did not show a higher cumulative incidence of developing CVE. We found a higher rate of CVE in patients who received salvage therapy (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3).

Discussion

PET-2 guided therapeutic strategies, which either intensify ABVD after a positive scan (I-PET-A) or de-escalate a BEACOPP-based approach in patients with a negative PET-2 (I-PET-B) have proved to be superior to standard ABVD chemotherapy or non-inferior to non-PET-guided escalated BEACOPP regimens for the treatment of advanced-stage cHL,10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 with favourable outcomes being confirmed after a relatively long follow-up, spanning over five years or more.9,11,14 However, no clinical trial has reported evidence on the effectiveness and safety of these strategies after a follow-up period that extends beyond 10 years.9,11,14 The present analysis was conceived with a twofold aim: (1) to explore the ability of a PET-2 adapted strategy to maintain a long-term disease control in patients switching to a more aggressive treatment for a positive interim scan, and (2) to explore the long-term safety of such an approach in terms of morbidity (with a focus on the incidence of SPM and CVE) and mortality.

The HD0607 trial, alongside the RATHL8 and the SWOG 081610 trials is a notable example of the I-PET-A therapeutic strategy, which proved to be feasible both in clinical trials and in routine practice thanks to the availability of robust, shared, and simple criteria for a PET-2 interpretation with visual assessment,4 and to the feasibility of on-time central review of PET-2 images.7 Thanks to this strategy, most of PET-2 negative patients enrolled in clinical trials with a follow-up of 3–7 years so far are still in continuous CR: 79%–81% are disease-free after 7 years of follow-up in the RATHL study, 76% after 5 years in the SWOG 0816 trial, and 83% after 10 years of follow-up in the HD0607 trial. On the other hand, PET-2 positive patients enrolled in the same studies had a 7-year PFS of 65.9% (RATHL trial), 5-year PFS of 66% (SWOG 0816 trial) and 10-year PFS of 58% (HD0607), respectively, with a significant improvement of 3-year PFS compared to the patients with a positive PET-2 and a non-PET adapted therapeutic strategy.1,2,5 The comparison among these 3 trials, however, should be taken with caution due to different criteria of inclusion and different follow-up duration among the studies: (1) Stage IIA- IVB and age 18–79 years in RATHL with a follow-up of 7 years, (2) Stage IIIA-IVB age 18 to 60 and follow-up of 5.9 years in SWOG 0816 and (3) stage IIB-IVB, age 18–60 and follow-up of almost 11 years in HD0607 trial. Of note, both the RATHL and HD0607 trials, reported a plateau of PFS in PET-2 negative patients, a finding that was not replicated in the SWOG 0816 trial, possibly to the relatively shorter follow-up duration in the latter (5 years).

A notable finding emerging from the present analysis of the HD0607 trial is the rarity of PFS events being recorded beyond the 5-year follow-up mark, with only 10 individuals (1.3%) out of the 782 patients enrolled in the study experiencing late relapses.

On the topic of late toxicities, this update describes an overall limited incidence of SPM and CVE, with a 10-year cumulative incidence of 2.84% and 2.0%, respectively. Regarding SPM, non-Hodgkin Lymphomas had the highest incidence, accounting for almost 30% of the recorded malignancies, while no cases of acute myeloid leukemia or myelodysplastic syndrome were reported. Concerning CVE, on the other hand, heart failure was the most commonly reported event by a wide margin, representing more than 60% of the total CVEs, in keeping with the toxicity profile of anthracycline-containing regimens.19 Of note, therapy escalation to BEACOPP or cRT had no impact on the incidence of both SPM and CVE, whereas an increase of SPM was seen in patients who received further lines of therapy (although such data needs to be carefully evaluated in light of the relatively low number of total SPM events). These findings highlight the favourable long-term safety profile of the I-PET-A strategy, and, regarding the incidence of SPM, are in keeping with other PET-guided clinical trials. Specifically, the recently published long-term follow-up of the RATHL trial reported a 7-year cumulative incidence of SPM of 5.5% and 2.5% in patients that continued with 4 AVD cycles or that were escalated to 4 BEACOPP cycles, respectively.9 Similarly, in the AHL2011 trial,14 the 5-year cumulative incidence of SPM was 3.2% in the standard arm and 2.2% in the experimental arm, whereas the SWOG0816 study reported a higher incidence of SPM in patients who were escalated to BEACOPP (14%) when compared to those who continued with ABVD (2%) after 5 years of follow-up.11 Such comparisons are to be considered explorative due to their indirect nature, the differences in follow-up length between studies and the lack of a defined cumulative incidence analysis in the latest available reports of the SWOG0816 and AHL2011 studies.

By looking at the long-term results of the HD0607 study, as well as at those achieved in the other cited trials some key concepts emerge: (1) the need of more effective first-line therapeutic options in advanced-stage cHL, specifically for stage IV patients which have the (comparably) lowest probability of being cured with standard ABVD; (2) the strong prognostic impact of early chemosensitivity (or lack thereof) demonstrated by the PET-2 scan; (3) the favourable long-term toxicity profile of the PET-guided ABVD approach, especially concerning SPM. Considering the first two points, it is worth noting that the approaches currently in use for advanced-stage cHL (N-AVD, BV-AVD and BrECADD) have shown promising results, achieving better disease control when compared with the respective control arm.16,18,20 Furthermore, both BV-AVD and N-AVD were not designed as PET-guided schemes,18,20 although the prognostic impact of the PET-2 scan remains significant in the context of BV-AVD,21 with it being indeterminate in patients receiving N-AVD22 and a diver for therapy de-escalation in subjects receiving BrECADD.16 Regarding the third point, while the data presented here is incomplete as it fails to capture the whole spectrum of late toxicities associated with the treatment of cHL, it can still be taken as reassuring for a population of patients who can realistically expect to be cured of their malignancy, especially when combined with the data coming from other long term follow-ups of clinical trials. Moreover, one can speculate that the same safety profile will be observed with the modern schemes, as the chemotherapy backbone remains largely unchanged and no significant late toxic events have emerged from the single-agent usage of BV or checkpoint inhibitors.23, 24, 25, 26

As evidenced by various studies with a PET-guided designs, as well as by previous reports of the HD0607 trial, the PET2-guided approach has some limitations, considering the non-zero rate of individuals with early response who become PET-positive at the end of treatment, and the influence that novel drugs (namely checkpoint inhibitors) can exert on PET scans raising concern for false positives. Interestingly, both these constraints could be improved upon by complementing PET-2 scans with biomarker evaluation, such as cfDNA or TARC measurements.27, 28, 29, 30

This analysis has some limitations. First of all the treatment backbone adopted in HD0607 reflects the therapeutic standards at the time of trial design and is not fully representative of current first-line strategies incorporating brentuximab vedotin or checkpoint inhibitors. Moreover, despite the long observation period, the assessment of late toxicity was focused on SPM and CVE whereas other clinically relevant outcomes, including fertility, endocrine dysfunction, pulmonary toxicity, neuropathy, and patient-reported quality of life, were not systematically captured. In addition, the relatively low number of SPM and CVE events limits the statistical power to detect moderate differences according to treatment escalation, RT, or subsequent salvage therapies. Furthermore, incomplete information on post-progression treatments in a subset of relapsed patients may have limited the attribution of late adverse events to first-line therapy vs. subsequent treatment exposures. Finally, although we report the longest follow-up of a PET2-guided trial to date, 10 years of follow-up may still be insufficient to capture all treatment-emergent late toxic events. These limitations notwithstanding, the mature follow-up of HD0607 provides important reassurance regarding the durability of disease control and the long-term safety of a PET-2–adapted ABVD-based strategy. These results, along with the recently published long-term follow-up of the RATHL trial, contribute to the establishment of PET-2 guided ABVD as a robust curative option for cHL and as a useful benchmark for evaluating newer first-line regimens.
