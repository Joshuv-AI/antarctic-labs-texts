Preparation, Biocompatibility, and In Vitro Bioactivity of High-Entropy Bio-Piezoelectric Ceramics
Authors: Huaizhang Gu, Yuanxun Li, Yunfei Kai, Xiaojuan Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A high-entropy strategy has emerged as a promising approach to enhance the functional properties of piezoelectric ceramics for biomedical applications. For this reason, we have designed two novel high-entropy ceramics, (Bi1/2Na1/2)(Zr1/3Sn1/3Ti1/3)O3(BNZST) and (Bi1/2Na1/2)(Zr1/4Sn1/4Hf1/4Ti1/4)O3(BNZSHT), which were synthesized via a two-step solid-state reaction. The phase structure, surface morphology, biocompatibility, and in vitro bioactivity were assessed. The results showed both ceramics adopted perovskite structures. BNZST and BNZSHT ceramics had relatively even crystallite sizes and element distribution, as well as achieving piezoelectric (d33 ≥ 78 pC/N) properties. In vitro tests confirmed a high relative cell growth rate (RSG, >80%) after co-culturing BNZST or BNZSHT ceramic with murine fibroblasts L929 for more than 3 days. In particular, the surface with electric charge enhanced L929 with more extensive, widespread, and dense proliferation for the BNZST ceramic compared to ceramics without BNZST or unpolarized BNZST. The above indicated that multi-element doping and entropy stabilization established a novel pathway for developing a high-entropy bio-piezoelectric ceramics with high biocompatibility and bioactivity, providing the possibility for their use in bone repair materials.

1. Introduction

Piezoelectric biomaterials have revolutionized bone tissue engineering by transducing mechanical stimuli into endogenous electrical signals, thereby mimicking the electromechanical microenvironment of natural bone [1,2,3,4,5,6]. In living bone, mechanical stresses during daily activities generate endogenous piezoelectric signals (primarily from collagen), which are believed to play a crucial role in bone remodeling by stimulating osteoblast proliferation and differentiation, thereby guiding bone repair and adaptation to the mechanical load. Conventional bioceramics, such as barium titanate (BaTiO3) and zinc oxide (ZnO), exhibit piezoelectric coefficients (d33 ≈ 50–200 pC/N) exceeding that of collagen–hydroxyapatite composites in native bone [7,8,9,10,11,12]. However, their clinical adoption remains hampered by intrinsic limitations, such as low fracture toughness causing brittle failure under cyclic loading and insufficient bioactivity delaying osseointegration [13,14]. While biocompatible polymers like polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) offer flexibility, their weak piezoelectric response (d33 < 30 pC/N) and inadequate osteogenic efficacy constrain therapeutic outcomes [15,16,17].

High-entropy piezoelectric ceramics represent a transformative design strategy aimed at overcoming the key limitations of established piezoelectric materials [18,19,20]. This approach seeks to synergistically achieve high piezoelectric coefficients (d33), elevated Curie temperatures (Tc), and good thermal stability. In contrast, conventional high-performance materials like lead zirconate titanate (PZT, d33 ≈ 500 pC/N) face challenges due to lead toxicity and thermal instability at high temperatures (Tc typically < 400 °C) [21,22]. Conversely, environmentally friendly bismuth-layered ceramics, such as CaBi2Nb2O9 (CBN), exhibit excellent thermal stability with very high Tc (>900 °C) but are hindered by intrinsically low piezoelectric activity (d33 < 25 pC/N) [23,24]. The high-entropy paradigm, by incorporating multiple principal elements, offers a promising route to bridge this performance gap and tailor a more balanced functional property profile. Recent breakthroughs leverage configurational entropy engineering (ΔS ≥ 1.5R) to disrupt structural constraints and enhance electromechanical responses. The high-entropy strategy has proven effective in structurally engineering bismuth-layered ceramics. A representative study by Qi et al. showed that co-doping CBN with Na+, Ti4+, and Ta5+ (resulting in a configurational entropy, ΔS, of 1.78R–2.34R) led to the formation of two-dimensional amorphous interlayers approximately 1 nm thick. These interlayers disrupted the inherent in-plane (a–b) polarization constraint of the layered structure, enabling the development of out-of-plane polarization components. This structural modification yielded a significantly enhanced piezoelectric coefficient (d33 ≈ 30 pC/N) while preserving an ultra-high Curie temperature (Tc > 900 °C). This property combination surpassed the performance of all bismuth-layered ceramics reported prior to that work; in perovskite systems, energy-harvesting applications can benefit from the high-entropy system, which can stabilize a five-phase coexistence to maintain d33 > 200 pC/N with <10% variation from 25 to 120 °C and output current densities of 15.46 μA/cm2, while bismuth ferrite-based systems like 0.6BiFeO3-0.4(Ba0.2Sr0.2Ca0.2Bi0.2Na0.2)TiO3 (0.6BF-0.4BSCBNT) can utilize a morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) design to achieve d33 ≈ 80 pC/N alongside a large strain of 0.50% at 100 kV/cm [19,20,25,26,27,28,29]. These advances highlight how an entropy-mediated local disorder (e.g., lattice distortions, phase coexistence, and defect engineering) enables unprecedented synergy between piezoelectricity, thermal robustness, and functional stability. The efficacy of entropy engineering in simultaneously enhancing piezoelectricity and thermal stability underscores its strategic potential for perovskite-type high-entropy bio-piezoceramics, where biocompatibility and electromechanical performance must coexist for implantable medical devices. To our knowledge, numerous lead-free high-entropy piezoelectric ceramics with non-equimolar or composite configurations have been reported, featuring structures such as bismuth-layered, perovskite (ABO3), or rhombohedral. However, there are few reports on systems that simultaneously satisfy all the following criteria: an equimolar single-phase composition, a rhombohedral structure, and high-entropy doping at the A/B-sites. In this study, two novel high-entropy rhombohedral phase structure (ABO3) ceramics in equimolar single composition systems have designed, synthesized via two-step solid-state reaction and the phase structure, surface morphology, in vitro bioactivity, biocompatibility was assessed.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Preparation

High-entropy bio-piezoelectric ceramic BNZST with mixed entropy ΔS = 1.791R and BNZTSH with ΔS = 2.079R was prepared via a solid-state reaction, where the ΔS values were calculated according to the following formula [30,31,32]: (1)ΔS=−R[∑a=1nxalnxaA-site+(∑b=1nxblnxb)B-site+3(∑c=1nxclnxc)C-site

Stoichiometric raw material powders of Bi2O3 (≥99.9%), Na2CO3 (≥99.9%), BaCO3 (≥99.9%), ZrO2 (≥99.9%), TiO2 (≥99.8%), SnO2 (≥99.9%), and HfO2 (≥99.5%) purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) were primary ball milled in ethanol for 12 h to obtain uniform and fine particles mixing, calcined in a furnace at 850 °C for 4.5 h, and sintered at 1050 °C for 4 h with a heating rate of 3 °C/min. According to the same process, the pre-sintered mixing powders were secondary ball milled, later compressed into the diameter of a 11 mm disk after binding using wax as the powder binder in a pressure machine under the pressure of 5 MPa for 5 s, subsequently calcined at 850 °C for 4.5 h to eliminate the binder, and sintered at 1050 °C for 4 h at the end.

2.2. Characterization

The phase composition of high-entropy bio-piezoelectric BNZTS and BNZTSH samples was analyzed using X-ray diffraction (Holland) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at a scanning speed of 2°/min. The surface morphology and elemental distribution were examined using a Hattichi S-3400N scanning electron microscope and its supporting spectrum analyzer (SEM/EDS) (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

2.3. Electrical Property Evaluation

For electrical property evaluation, ceramic pellet samples were meticulously planarized on opposing faces to ensure surface parallelism. Silver electrodes were screen-printed onto both surfaces prior to poling. Polarization was performed at room temperature in silicone oil under electric fields spanning 0.5–2.5 kV/mm. The piezoelectric constant was quantified via a quasi-static d33 meter (SA1303A, Wuxi Shiao Co., Ltd., Wuxi, China). For biological performance assays, the silver electrodes on the polarized sample surfaces were cleaned by a grinding method to avoid affecting the accuracy of biological performance tests, like in cellular activity.

2.4. In Vitro Biological Property Evaluation

In vitro biocompatibility was assessed using murine fibroblasts L929 lineage (Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), with RSG quantified via CCK-8 assay [33,34,35]. The L929 cell line is a well-established model extensively applied in evaluating the biocompatibility of diverse medical materials, including polymers, metals, and inorganic substances [36,37,38]. A cell culture was performed using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Corning, NY, USA) as the basal medium. The growth medium was prepared by supplementing the DMEM with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell, Wuhan, China), 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin (Biosharp, Beijing, China), and routine passages were carried out using trypsin (Biosharp, Beijing, China). All cultures were incubated at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 with the medium being refreshed every 48 h. The value of RSG was calculated according to the formula as follows:RSG (%) = (As − Ab)/(Ac − Ab) × 100%(2) where As is the absorbance value (OD value) of the experimental group; Ac is the absorbance value (OD value) of the positive control group; and Ab is the absorbance value (OD value) of the blank control group. The polarized BNZTS and BNZTSH groups underwent meticulous preparation to evaluate the effects of polarization on cellular responses. Ceramic disks (Ø11 × 1 mm) were sterilized via sequential immersion in 75% (v/v) ethanol for 24 h and triple rinsing with PBS within 24-well plates, followed by aseptic soaking in fresh PBS for an additional 24 h. Murine fibroblasts L929 (3500 cells/disk) were seeded on the cathodically polarized surfaces of specimens. Cultures were maintained in an α-MEM medium (90% v/v) and supplemented with fetal bovine serum (10%) and penicillin–streptomycin (1%) at 37 °C/5%CO2 with 90% humidity, with medium replenishment every 48 h. The quantification of cell viability was performed via the CCK-8 assay at 1, 3, and 5 days after seeding. Following rinsing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were incubated with CCK-8 reagent—diluted 1:10 in culture medium—for 2 h, protected from light. Thereafter, 100 µL aliquots were transferred to a 96-well plate. The ab-sorbance of these aliquots was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a BioTek microplate reader. At 72 h, calcein-AM/propidium iodide staining (30 min) differentiated viable (green, 490 nm excitation) and necrotic cells (red, 545 nm excitation) using a fluorescence microscope. Where cytoskeletal morphology assessment after 3 days, fixed cells (overnight fixation) were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (5 min), and observed and analyzed via SEM imaging.

2.5. XRD Refinement

The Rietveld refinement of the XRD data was performed using the open-source software General Structure Analysis System II (gsas2 5438 (64 bit)) to obtain accurate structural parameters. The refinement strategy followed a sequential and constrained approach to ensure stability and convergence. Initially, the scale factor and background, modeled with a Chebyshev polynomial function, were refined. This was followed by the refinement of the lattice parameters to correct peak positions. Subsequently, the profile parameters (U, V, W) and asymmetry were refined to accurately model the peak shapes. Finally, the anisotropy atomic displacement parameters (Uaniso) for all atoms were included in the refinement. The refinement process was monitored through the weighted profile R-factor (Rw), and the final fit, showing a good agreement between the observed and calculated patterns, has been presented in the revised manuscript. This stepwise method ensured a relatively physically meaningful and stable refinement outcome.

3. Results

3.1. XRD Analysis

Figure 1a shows XRD patterns of the piezoelectric ceramics BNT, BNZT, BNZST, and BNZSHT. The standard cards of the rhombohedral crystal structure phase (PDF#46-0001, R3c) of BNT are presented by vertical lines for reference. All the ceramics showed peaks corresponding to rhombohedral phase structure. The well-matched diffraction peaks to the standard BNT phase and the absence of impurity phases in pure BNZT, BNZST and BNZSHT ceramics indicated that a single ion or multiple ions of Zr2+, Sn4+, and Hf4+ successfully integrated into the BNT lattice (Figure 1b and Figure 2). Obviously, the diffraction peaks in this range of 45–47° generally shifted towards small angles as the number of doping elements increased, such as BNZT compared to BNT or BNZSHT compared to BNZST, and the width of the diffraction peak widened or narrowed as the types and ratios of the doped ions (the radius 0.605 Å for Ti4+, 0.79 Å for Zr4+, 0.69 Å for Sn4+ and 0.71 Å for Hf4+) in BNZT, BNZST, and BNZSHT ceramics. According to the Scherrer formula [36], which is used to estimate crystallite size from X-ray diffraction data, a smaller full width at half maximum (FWHM) of a diffraction peak corresponds to a larger particle size [39]. Therefore, the peak shift and the changes in peak width from BNZT to BNZSHT ceramics compared to BNT indicated a change in the lattice parameters due to the incorporation of larger ions (e.g., Zr4+, Sn4+, Hf4+) into the B-site of the lattice, leading to lattice expansion. The high-entropy compositions (BNZST and BNZSHT) exhibit altered lattice parameters compared to pure BNT, leading to a reduction in crystallographic symmetry. This lower symmetry facilitates enhanced polarization under an applied electric field, which is a key factor contributing to their superior piezoelectric response [40,41].

3.2. SEM/EDS Analysis

From Figure 3, it can be seen that the BNZT ceramic grains were loosely stacked. Although BNZST grains have a certain number of pores at some grain connections, the grains were overall denser than BNZT, with more complete crystal growth and more uniform grain size. BNZSHT also exhibited a similar structure of dense grain stacking, but the uniformity of the grain size was not as good as BNZST. The BNZST ceramics contained a certain number of circular pores, which may have affected its ferroelectric properties and facilitated the infiltration of osteoblasts and murine fibroblasts, promoting the improvement of biological properties [42,43].

From Figure 4, it can be seen that the Bi, Na, Zr, Ti, and O elements in the BNZT ceramics were unevenly distributed throughout the entire region. However, the element distribution uniformity of Bi, Na, Zr, Ti, Sn, and other elements in the BNZST and BNZSHT ceramics was improved throughout the region, which to some extent reflected that “high entropy” was conducive to the formation of a single-phase solid solution structure [18,44,45]. Among them, the element distribution uniformity of the BNZST ceramics was the highest, followed by the BNZSHT ceramics.

3.3. Piezoelectric Performance Analysis

From Figure 5, it can be seen that the piezoelectric constant was d33(BNZST) > d33(BNZSHT) > d33(BNZT). According to the relationship d33 = 2Q33ε0εrPs [46], the piezoelectric coefficient (d33) depends not only on the spontaneous polarization (Ps) but is also closely tied to the relative permittivity (εr) and is strongly influenced by the electro-stritive coefficient (Q33). Therefore, a high ferroelectric performance (characterized by a large Ps) does not guarantee a high piezoelectric response. Assuming that the Q33 values of the three ceramics were similar due to their equal vacuum dielectric constant ε0 and subequal Ec values, as long as the relative dielectric constant εr of BNZST ceramics was much higher than that of BNZSHT and BNZSHT, and slightly higher than that of BNZT, it could be possible to show that the piezoelectric constant of BNZST ceramics was sequentially higher than that of BNZSHT and BNZT. The SEM analysis (Figure 3) revealed that the BNZST ceramic contained a greater density of pores compared to the other two compositions. It is proposed that the increased pore-boundary interfaces enhanced dielectric polarization through scattering mechanisms, leading to a higher relative permittivity. This, in turn, contributed to the observed larger piezoelectric coefficient (d33) for BNZST, consistent with reported models [47,48,49].

3.4. RSG Analysis

The relative cell growth (RSG) values for BNZST, BNZSHT, and BNZT, as shown in Figure 6, exhibited a general increasing trend over the culture period. On the first day, the RSGs of the three were close, while on the third day, the RSG of the BNZST negative electrode was the highest, followed by the unpolarized RSG; the RSGs of the negative and unpolarized surfaces of BNZSHT were relatively small compared to BNZST, while BNZT had the smallest RSG. On the fifth day, the RSGs of the BNZST negative electrodes and unpolarized surfaces were relatively close, still relatively high compared to other groups, followed by the RSGs of the BNZSHT negative electrodes and unpolarized BNZSHT, with BNZST as the lowest. This indicated that the material had a certain degree of biocompatibility and could promote cell proliferation on the materials [50,51,52], but the magnitude of the growth rate was not determined by a single factor condition [53,54].

3.5. Live/Dead Cells Staining Analysis

Based on XRD, SEM, d33, RSG, and other analyses, we selected the high-entropy piezoelectric ceramic BNZST with good piezoelectric properties and RSG for cell staining experiments to analyze its biocompatibility and bioactivity. The proliferation quantity and proliferation status of cells can reflect the biocompatibility and biological activity of materials [53,54,55]. From Figure 7, it can be seen that the negative electrode surfaces of the polarized BNZST (Figure 7b) had a higher cell density compared to the control group (Figure 7a) without the piezoelectric ceramic samples and unpolarized BNZST (Figure 7c), indicating that the piezoelectric ceramics with charges (micro-currents) were beneficial for cell proliferation and that the piezoelectric ceramic BNZST had good bioactivity [54].

3.6. Fixed Cells SEM Analysis

From Figure 8, we also conducted SEM analysis after co-culturing murine fibroblasts L929 with high-entropy bio-piezoelectric ceramic BNZST for 3 days and found that L929 cells can proliferate on a large range of polarized BNZST cathode surfaces. Compared to the control group (without ceramics) and the non-polarized BNZST group, cells co-cultured with the polarized BNZST samples displayed a more favorable morphology—characterized by clear outlines, rounded cell bodies, and uniform size. These cells also exhibited superior adhesion to the ceramic surface and a broader distribution across it. These observations collectively indicate the enhanced bioactivity of the electrically polarized high-entropy BNZST ceramic [9,10,54].

4. Conclusions

The high-entropy rhombohedral structure of bioceramics BNZST and BNZSHT, synthesized via solid-state reaction, exhibited phase-pure rhombohedral structures with uniform grain morphology and homogeneous elemental distribution, confirming successful entropy-driven suppression of phase segregation. These materials achieved exceptional electromechanical properties, including a piezoelectric coefficient (d33 ≥ 78 pC/N). In vitro biocompatibility assessments demonstrated high relative cell growth rates (RSG > 80%), while surface charge engineering via electrical polarization significantly augmented cellular proliferation, yielding denser and more extensive cell layers on the polarized BNZTS substrates compared to the unpoled controls. These results validated that multi-element doping and entropy stabilization could establish a robust pathway to design high-entropy piezoelectric bioceramics with synergistic electromechanical performance and bioactivity, positioning them as promising candidates for self-powered bone regeneration devices.
