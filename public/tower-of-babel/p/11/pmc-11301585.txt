Mechanical compression of the carotid artery by the pharynx in the retropharyngeal space during swallowing and the induction of stenosis and embolic stroke: illustrative case

The carotid artery (CA) is surrounded by various structures, such as muscles, hyoid bone, thyroid cartilage, and the pharynx. Mechanical compression of the CA by the hyoid bone or thyroid cartilage can induce ischemic stroke during swallowing or neck rotation.1, 2 However, no report has described the development of CA stenosis and distal emboli secondary to mechanical compression of the CA by the pharynx during swallowing. We herein report the first case in which mechanical compression of the CA in the retropharyngeal space by the pharynx and its dynamic movement induced stenotic change and finally embolic stroke.

Illustrative Case

A man in his 70s was transferred to our hospital 1 hour after presenting with left-sided hemiplegia and motor aphasia. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance angiography showed infarction of the frontal lobe and occlusion of the left distal middle cerebral artery (Fig. 1A and B). After the administration of tissue plasminogen activator, the neurological deficits were resolved. The next day, the patient developed worsened motor aphasia, and MRI revealed an infarction in a new territory of the left frontal lobe. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the neck showed that the left common carotid artery (CCA) was trapped by the hyoid bone and thyroid cartilage and that the internal carotid artery (ICA) ran medially to the external carotid artery in the retropharyngeal space (Fig. 1C–E). Furthermore, CTA showed moderate stenosis with ulceration in the anteromedial aspect of the CCA to ICA, where this region was in contact with the pharynx (Fig. 1F–I). Cervical plaque T1-weighted MRI demonstrated an unstable plaque with hyperintensity (Fig. 1J). Neck CTA performed 7 years previously showed that the CA had been trapped at that time; however, no plaque had been present (Fig. 1K). To investigate the association between CA stenosis and swallowing, angiography during swallowing of a contrast agent was performed. This examination showed dynamic compression of the left CCA and ICA in the posterolateral direction by the pharynx during swallowing, despite the fact that the CA on the healthy right side moved slightly anteromedially (Fig. 2A–H, Video 1). These results indicated that the mechanical compression of the pharynx had induced the stenotic changes in the CA and distal emboli. Three weeks later, carotid endarterectomy was performed. During this procedure, the hyoid bone and thyroid cartilage were repositioned away from the CCA without resecting them, the retropharyngeal ICA was transposed to its normal location, and endarterectomy of the intraplaque hemorrhage with rupture was performed (Fig. 2I–K). Finally, the retropharyngeal space was closed with sutures to prevent the transposed CA from returning to its original position. The patient developed no ischemic events postoperatively, and angiography showed that the left CA now moved anteromedially during swallowing (Fig. 2L–O, Video 2). CTA at 12 months postoperatively showed that the CCA and ICA were still in their normal anatomical positions (Fig. 2P and Q). Discussion

Observations

We report a rare case in which the CCA was trapped by the hyoid bone and thyroid cartilage and the ICA ran medially to the external carotid artery in the retropharyngeal space. This unique position of the CA contributed to mechanical compression of the CA by the pharynx and its repetitive dynamic movement, which induced stenotic change in the CA and finally embolic stroke. To our knowledge, this is the first report to describe mechanical compression of the CA in the retropharyngeal space by the pharynx during swallowing, resulting in the development of stenotic change and distal emboli.

Many reports have addressed the association between ischemic stroke and hyoid bony structures.1, 2 The CA can be directly damaged by the hyoid bone. Even if the CA is not in direct contact with the hyoid bone at rest, it can still be injured by repetitive contact with the hyoid bone during swallowing or neck rotation.1, 2 In the latter case, diagnosis requires evaluation of the dynamics of the hyoid bone and CA as well as CT or MRI examinations. In some reports, the dynamics of the hyoid bone were evaluated by carotid ultrasound, dynamic MRI, or CTA, and the findings of these studies are summarized in Table 1.1, 3–5 In our case, the CCA was trapped by the hyoid bone and thyroid cartilage, and we considered the possibility that these bony structures had injured the CA and induced ischemic stroke. However, we ultimately determined that the development of stenotic change and distal emboli was associated with the pharynx rather than these structures for the following reasons. First, the plaque in our case was located on the anteromedial side, in contact with the pharynx. In a previous report describing stenosis of the CA and twisted CA, the plaque was usually located on the posteromedial side;6 this differs from our case. Furthermore, it was not located on the lateral side in contact with the hyoid bone and thyroid cartilage. These facts suggested that the stenotic change was induced by the pharynx. Next, we investigated the association between CA stenosis and movement of the pharynx during swallowing. Angiography during swallowing of a contrast agent demonstrated that dynamic movement of the pharynx during swallowing compressed the CA posterolaterally, which differed from the normal anteromedial motion on the healthy side and the phenomenon described in previous reports.7 We accordingly determined that the mechanical compression of the CA by the pharynx and its dynamic movement for more than a 7-year period had induced stenotic change and finally embolic stroke.

In our case, the risk of recurrent stroke was considered high with medical treatment alone because 2 embolic strokes had occurred and the CA was being dynamically compressed with each swallow. A previous report described the case of a retropharyngeal CA in which a CA stent was compressed and fractured by the hyoid bone.8 Therefore, to prevent recurrent stroke, it is important to eliminate repetitive compression of the CA by direct surgery rather than endovascular treatment and to remove the unstable plaque. We transposed the bony structures, CCA, and ICA to their normal positions and then performed carotid endarterectomy. Postoperative angiography showed that the CA moved anteromedially during swallowing, and the patient developed no ischemic events postoperatively, which supported our diagnosis and treatment.

In a previous report, the incidence of a retropharyngeal CA was 15.5%.9 Furthermore, the retropharyngeal CA was positioned at the level of the hyoid bone in all patients, suggesting that the ICA might be held in the retropharyngeal position by the hyoid bone.9 Thus, although a retropharyngeal ICA becoming trapped by the hyoid bone, as in our case, is not uncommon, no reports have described stroke induced by mechanical compression of the CA in the retropharyngeal space by the pharynx. Therefore, it is possible that this phenomenon has been overlooked until now. In the future, the anatomical interaction between the retropharyngeal CA and surrounding structures, as well as the dynamics of these structures, should be investigated to clarify whether this phenomenon induces CA stenosis and ischemic stroke.

Lessons

Movement of the pharynx during swallowing can be a risk factor for CA stenosis in the retropharyngeal space. It is important to evaluate the anatomical interaction between the CA and surrounding structures, as well as their dynamics, to ensure appropriate diagnosis and treatment.
