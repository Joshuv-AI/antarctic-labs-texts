Exploring therapeutic targets of curcumin for knee osteoarthritis via the gene–gut microbiota–plasma metabolite axis: An observational study
Authors: Haibo Zheng, Hehua Wei, Bing Tian, Zilan Xu, Di Su, Haozhao Lü, Xianqiu Li, Hao Ni, Longlong Wei, Xueshen Mo, Kun Hou, Zhiqiang Zhang, Mengnan Yu, Feipan Long, Wei Li, Xiangbin Rong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent, chronic, degenerative joint disorder characterized by a multifaceted pathogenesis. Conventional risk variables inadequately account for its evolution, leading to heightened focus on systemic causes. Dysbiosis of gut microbiota associated with immunological, metabolic, and inflammatory abnormalities, can influence joint health through the “gut-joint axis.” This study investigated the correlation between OA and gut microbiota dysbiosis to identify novel therapeutic targets. This study utilized 211 gut microbiota characteristics as exposures, 1400 plasma metabolite concentrations as mediators, and Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) data for patients with knee osteoarthritis (KOA) as outcomes. Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses were performed via inverse variance weighting, with robustness verified using pleiotropy and heterogeneity assessments. We identified the intersection of differentially expressed genes from the transcriptome analysis of OA patients with genes exhibiting significant causal associations derived from MR analysis. The intersecting genes were then analyzed through Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and gene ontology enrichment analysis, as well as molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. Our analyses indicated that the Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, exhibited a negative correlation with KOA, potentially mitigating the condition by reducing the glycine-to-serine ratio. Sensitivity analyses validated the robustness of these associations. Reverse Mendelian randomization analyses (rMRa) provided no evidence of reverse causality. The integration of GWAS and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) data revealed that 1 gene had a substantial association with KOA, suggesting it as a potential therapeutic target for KOA. Molecular docking analysis and molecular dynamics simulations were employed to forecast potential drug–target interactions, therefore substantiating the therapeutic potential of curcumin. Our study provides insights into the development of personalized therapeutic strategies for KOA and identifies preliminary possible targets for curcumin.

1. Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a common and formidable degenerative joint disease globally, significantly affecting individuals’ health and quality of life.[1] OA predominantly affects weight-bearing or frequently utilized joints, including the knees, hips, and hands. As per pertinent research, the global population of OA patients reached 595 million in 2020, representing 7.6% of the total worldwide population, with an overall rise of 132.2% since 1990. By 2050, knee osteoarthritis (KOA) cases are anticipated to increase by 74.9%, hand OA by 48.6%, hip OA by 78.6%, and other forms of OA by 95.1%.[2] Core pathological changes include progressive deterioration of articular cartilage, accompanied by subchondral bone sclerosis and remodeling, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation, ultimately leading to joint pain and dysfunction. Presently, despite the availability of diverse clinical treatment modalities, they exhibit notable limitations: nonpharmacological interventions are limited in halting disease progression; pharmacological treatments (including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and glucocorticoids) are restricted and constrained by adverse reactions; and late-stage joint replacement surgery encounters challenges such as significant trauma, elevated costs, and surgical risks.[3] Consequently, there is an imperative necessity to investigate more effective prevention and treatment methodologies. In recent years, as research on the pathogenesis of OA has advanced, the gut microbiota has emerged as a focal point of investigation, serving as a crucial “intermediate hub” in disease control. A multitude of studies have established that the relationship between gut microbiota and OA is multifaceted rather than a singular pathway. At the immune level, dysbiosis can trigger the host’s innate immune response, facilitating the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, thereby intensifying local joint inflammation.[4] At the metabolic level, microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), tryptophan metabolites, and bile acid derivatives, circulate through the bloodstream to the joints, directly influencing the anabolic/catabolic equilibrium of chondrocytes.[5] Concurrently, microbial dysbiosis undermines the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier, resulting in endotoxin translocation and systemic low-grade inflammation, which creates a “synergistic effect” with local joint damage and accelerates the progression of OA. Seymour et al discovered[6] that abnormal tryptophan metabolism mediated by gut microbiota is significantly associated with arthritis, with indole production capable of inducing collagen-induced arthritis by activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor. Wei et al established[7] that microbial dysbiosis enhances intestinal mucosal permeability, resulting in elevated blood endotoxin levels that can expedite the progression of KOA through the TLR4/NF-κB pathway. Berthelot et al further indicated[8] that alterations in plasma metabolites, such as SCFAs and bile acids caused by microbial dysbiosis, can directly affect articular cartilage, up-regulate the expression of catabolic genes like MMPs, inhibit type II collagen synthesis, and ultimately lead to cartilage matrix degradation. Moreover, the epigenetic interplay between gut microbiota and the host genome may influence the host’s vulnerability to OA by regulating the expression of inflammation-related genes, offering a new perspective on disease heterogeneity.[9] Curcumin, a natural compound derived from the Zingiberaceae plant Curcuma longa, has been thoroughly investigated for its therapeutic efficacy in OA and has emerged as a potential candidate for disease intervention. Numerous in vitro studies have demonstrated that curcumin can markedly diminish the expression of pro-inflammatory factors, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, by inhibiting signaling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK, while simultaneously up-regulating anti-inflammatory factors like IL-10, thus mitigating synovial inflammation.[10,11] Curcumin can diminish chondrocyte death, enhance the synthesis of extracellular matrix components such as type II collagen and proteoglycan, and decrease the activity of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1, MMP-3, MMP-13), hence delaying cartilage degeneration.[12] Research utilizing animal models has substantiated that curcumin intervention can enhance joint swelling and pain metrics in the OA model mice while also diminishing cartilage degradation and osteophyte development.[13] Preclinical evidence indicates that[14] serum levels of curcumin metabolites in OA patients are favorably linked with cartilage volume, providing preliminary support for its in vivo efficacy. Nonetheless, the practical application of curcumin is constrained by its low bioavailability and swift metabolism in vivo, with the gut flora potentially serving as a crucial factor in overcoming this constraint.[15] Recent studies have uncovered a bidirectional regulatory relationship between curcumin and gut microbiota[16]:curcumin enhances microbial equilibrium by regulating gut microbiota composition, increasing beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus while diminishing pro-inflammatory bacteria like Escherichia coli; conversely, gut microbiota metabolizes curcumin through reduction and demethylation, producing more active metabolites such as tetrahydro curcumin, thereby prolonging its efficacy. The interaction between curcumin and gut bacteria may be a crucial mechanism for its biological activity. Nevertheless, contemporary research regarding the impact of this relationship on OA remains insufficiently systematic and comprehensive. In conclusion, current research has indicated that gut microbiota contributes to the advancement of KOA through metabolites, inflammation, and several mechanisms, while curcumin exhibits anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective properties in OA. Nonetheless, it remains ambiguous whether the interaction between curcumin and gut microbiota influences KOA through the regulation of plasma metabolites, as well as the specific important bacteria, metabolites, and molecular pathways implicated. This study seeks to elucidate the regulatory function of the gut microbiota–plasma metabolite axis in the progression of KOA, determine whether curcumin improves KOA by regulating gut microbiota composition and plasma metabolite profile, and identify the fundamental mechanism underlying the synergistic effects of curcumin, gut microbiota, and plasma metabolites on KOA, thereby offering a novel theoretical foundation and potential intervention targets for targeted therapies addressing the “gut-joint axis” in KOA.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

The general approach of our study can be intuitively presented in Figure 1. MR analysis was performed by a GWAS to evaluate the causative relationships among 211 gut microbiota characteristics, 1400 plasma metabolic variables, and KOA. MR analyses delineate risk factors utilizing genetic variation, and legitimate instrumental variables (IVs) in causal inference must adhere to 3 fundamental assumptions[17]: Assumption of associativity: IVs exhibit a robust association with the exposure factor being investigated. Independence assumption: IVs are independent of any confounders that exist between exposure factors and outcomes. Exclusivity assumption: IVs can influence the result solely via exposure factors (Fig. 2). The subsequent study design encompassed the following steps: Transcriptome analysis: the GSE114007 dataset was acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Gene functional annotation: We developed interaction networks of overlapping genes and subsequently utilized the gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases to conduct functional annotation of the genes. Molecular docking: We obtained the molecular structures of the vasoactive intestinal peptide-receptor 1 (VIPR1) protein and curcumin from the RCSB and PubChem databases, then modeled the ligand–receptor interactions using the CB-Dock2 protein–ligand docking platform. Molecular dynamics simulation: We employed YASARA software to simulate the molecular dynamics of the docking complex and examine its dynamic binding mechanism.

2.2. Data sources

Data about gut microbiota: We utilized genome-wide association study (GWAS) data from MiBioGen, which encompasses extensive, multi-ethnic, genome-wide meta-analyses regarding the relationship between human genetic diversity and the gut microbiome.[18] Following the adjustment for age, sex, technical variables, and genetic principal components, Quantitative Microbiome Trait Loci (mbQTL) analyses yielded GWAS summary statistics for 211 microbial taxa, encompassing 9 phyla, 16 classes, 20 orders, 35 families (3 of which remain unclassified), and 131 genera (12 of which are unclassified). Plasma metabolite data originate from published literature.[19] The summary-level data utilized in the papers is available for download and has obtained ethical permission from the relevant institutions associated with each GWAS. OA data for the knee was obtained from the IEU Open GWAS Project database, identified by ID: ebi-a-GCST007092. The sample size was 417,596 individuals, and the study population consisted of a European demographic. The Medical Research Council Integrative Epidemiology Unit in the United Kingdom built the database to collect biological samples for illness research. Transcriptome analysis data are sourced from the GEO database, established by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), which serves as a gene expression repository. KOA Transcriptome Data ID: GSE114007 comprises RNA sequencing data from knee cartilage tissues of 18 healthy individuals and 20 patients diagnosed with KOA. KEGG, created by the Kanehisa Laboratory at Kyoto University, Japan, delineates gene pathways and molecular interactions to elucidate gene functions within biological systems. The GO system consolidates information from organism-specific databases, including FlyBase (a worldwide cooperation), the Saccharomyces Genome Database (Stanford), and the Mouse Genome Database (Jackson Laboratory). These databases provide genome-related information for particular species, while GO establishes standardized terminologies to delineate gene functions across 3 categories: biological process, molecular function (MF), and cellular component. The molecular structure of the substance curcumin was obtained from the PubChem database, which is endorsed by the National Institutes of Health and maintained by the NCBI. Data regarding the VIPR1 protein was obtained from the Research Collaboration for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) protein database, which is overseen by the RCSB. The chosen protein complex structure comprises the protein encoded by the VIPR1 gene, identified by the ID 7ALO, originating from the organism Homo sapiens. 2.3. Experimental methods

2.3.1. Instrument selection

All single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with exposure were retrieved from the Ensemble database at https://rest.ensembl.org/info/variation/homo_sapiens. In Mendelian randomization analysis, SNPs significantly associated with gut microbiota (P < 1e−05) and plasma metabolites (P < 1e−05) were chosen as IVs for exposures. For reverse Mendelian randomization analysis, SNPs substantially correlated with specific KOA phenotypes (P < 5e−08) were chosen as instrumental factors. We conducted a clumping operation on the exposure data, which included gut microbiota data, plasma metabolite data, and KOA data as exposure factors for linkage disequilibrium analysis, utilizing the clump_data function (exposure_data, clump_kb = 10000, clump_r2 = 0.001). To evaluate the strength of IVs and mitigate weak IV bias, the F statistic was calculated for each IV. SNPs with F > 10 were considered legitimate IVs, therefore mitigating weak instrument bias. R2 is determined using the formula[20]:R2=βexposure 2βexposure 2+seexposure 2⋅ samplesize exposure , and the F statistic is calculated[21] by F=R2⋅( samplesize exposure -2)1-R2. Here, βexposure represents the regression coefficient, Seexposure is the standard error, and exposure is the sample size. This study encompasses 1531 SNPs associated with gut microbiota, 34,843 SNPs linked to plasma metabolites, and 26 SNPs related to KOA. All these SNPs were evaluated based on certain criteria and subsequently utilized in Mendelian randomization analysis (Supplementary file 1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q101). Ultimately, allele data was compared and rectified utilizing the SNP2eaf function to mitigate the impact of alleles on the results and to guarantee the precise calculation of effective allele frequencies.

2.3.2. Statistical analyses

MR analysis was performed with the Two Sample MR package. Five methodologies were employed: MR Egger regression, weighted median (WM), inverse variance weighted (IVW), simple mode, and weighted mode. The IVW results constituted the primary foundation for interpretation. To guarantee the reliability and robustness of the results, we employ heterogeneity and pleiotropy, visualizing the data through leave-one-out plots, funnel plots, forest plots, and scatter plots. The heterogeneity is assessed via the MR-Egger and IVW techniques, which depend on Cochran Q statistic. Pleiotropy was analyzed using MR-Egger regression and MR-PRESSO. All analyses in this experiment were conducted using R 4.4.1 (https://www.r-project.org/). The data in the article is approximated to 3 decimal places. A biostatistician from Guangxi University of Traditional Chinese Medicine evaluated the statistical methodologies employed in this study.

2.3.3. MR analysis

We methodically investigated the causal relationships between gut microbiota and KOA, as well as between plasma metabolites and KOA. The filtering criteria were as follows: The relationship between gut microbiota and KOA required a P value of <.05; while the association between plasma metabolites and KOA necessitated a P value of less than .01. The direction of the odds ratio (OR) must correspond with the direction of beta (β). The P value for the pleiotropy test must exceed .05.

2.3.4. Reverse MR analysis

The gut microbiota acquired through IVW filtration served as the outcome, whereas KOA functioned as the exposure. Subsequently, reverse Mendelian randomization analysis was conducted using the inverse variance weighting method, MR-Egger regression method, WM method, simple model method, and weighted mode method. The P value derived from the IVW approach served as a benchmark. If P > .05, reverse causality is absent.

2.3.5. Mediation analysis

The gut microbiota and plasma metabolites, filtered and significantly correlated with the outcome, were reanalyzed using MR analysis to assess the mediating effect of plasma metabolite involvement. The regression coefficient value β (effect size), indicating the strength of the association between exposure factors and outcomes in the MR analysis results, was employed as a criterion.[22] The outcomes of MR analyses from gut microbiota to plasma metabolites are represented as β1, while the outcomes from plasma metabolites to KOA are represented as β2; the results from gut microbiota to KOA are used as the total effect value (βAll). The mediating effect value is calculated as β1 × β2, the mediating effect share is the ratio of the mediating effect value to the total effect value, and the direct effect value is determined by subtracting the mediating effect from the total effect.[23]

2.3.6. Conversion of IVs to genes

SNPs in the gut microbiota, significantly correlated with the plasma metabolite–KOA axis, were identified following thorough screening. The RS coding of the summarized SNPs was subsequently utilized on the g:Profiler toolset website to associate the information with gene names.[24] (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/snpense).

2.3.7. Transcriptome analysis of osteoarthritis of the knee

The search query “OA AND RNA-seq” was employed to perform the search. The dataset “GSE114007” was chosen for transcriptome analysis following filtration. The gene expression series (GSE) files in this dataset, each identified by a GSE number, were subsequently downloaded. The GEO platform (GPL) file contains information regarding the chip type, probe sequence and position, gene symbols, and other data. These 2 files were subsequently processed by a Perl script to generate the gene expression matrix file, where each row denotes a gene, each column represents a distinct sample, and the value indicates the gene’s expression level in the respective sample. The limma package was subsequently utilized to perform a linear model analysis of the microarray data, followed by differential expression analysis of the gene expression data to provide a summary file of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Heatmaps and volcano plots were generated for visualization.

2.3.8. Obtaining the intersecting genes

To identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets, intersecting genes were derived by intersecting differentially DEGs from transcriptome analyses, serving as potential disease biomarkers and candidate therapeutic targets, with genes identified through MR analyses that exhibited robust causal relationships. The interrelationships among several gene lists were illustrated using Venn diagrams with the VennDiagram program.

2.3.9. Functional enrichment analysis (GO, KEGG)

We conducted enrichment analysis to elucidate the activities of the intersecting genes. The interaction network of the intersected genes was obtained from a website (https://string-db.org/). The minimum interaction score is established at 0.400 to conceal unlinked nodes, while the maximum number of interactors is capped at 200. Next, we imported these interacting genes into Cytoscape software and sorted them using the CytoNCA plugin with “Degree” as the indicator. The relationships were analyzed via the “clusterProfiler” program for functional enrichment analysis. Bubble plots and histograms were created for visualization purposes.

2.3.10. Molecular docking

We conducted automated blind docking of the molecular structures of proteins expressed by intersecting genes with the drug curcumin on a website (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php). The results encompassed visible molecular conformations, docking scores (binding energies), interacting residues of small molecules with proteins, and data regarding the potential location of the receptor-binding pocket.

2.3.11. Molecular dynamics simulation

We conducted molecular dynamics simulations of component-target combinations with the lowest binding energy using the YASARA platform to replicate the dynamic docking of proteins with Chinese medicinal compounds in real scenarios. The protein complexes and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) molecules were solubilized in a TIP3P explicit water molecule model with periodic boundaries, utilizing an Amber14 force field, 0.9% NaCl, a temperature of 310 K, and a pH of 7.4. The simulations were conducted over 75 nanoseconds (ns), with a time step of 2.5 femtoseconds (fs) and a sampling period of 100 picoseconds (ps).

3.3. Results of the intermediary effects

The analysis of intermediation effects on 20 plasma metabolites indicated that glycine and serine mediated the causative relationship between the Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, and KOA. The glycine-to-serine ratio was determined as the primary modulator of this causal association. β1 = −0.129, β2 = 0.044, mediator effect = −0.005, βAll = −0.071, representing 7.99% of the total effect, and direct effect = −0.066. The glycine-to-serine ratio exhibited a favorable correlation with outcome progression (β2 > 0, OR = 1.045 > 1). The Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, was found to diminish the prevalence of KOA by decreasing the glycine-to-serine ratio (Fig. 7).

3.4. Results of obtaining intersecting genes

Transcriptome data from patients with KOA were evaluated, yielding 2593 DEGs (Supplementary file 2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q101) The SNPs of the Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, were translated into gene names, resulting in the identification of 9 genes (Supplementary file 3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q101). VIPR1 was identified from the 2 gene sets utilizing the Venn diagram software. This investigation could not identify other significantly associated genes as potential therapeutic targets. The primary reason is that these genes satisfy the criteria for significant association in only one dimension: they are either SNP-associated genes or exhibit differential expression in the transcriptome; however, they lack the confluence of “causal association + differential expression in the transcriptome” that characterizes the VIPR1 gene. In the absence of dual evidence, these genes cannot provide a comprehensive regulatory chain linking gene function to disease phenotype, rendering them unsuitable as prospective therapeutic targets for KOA at this time. Heatmaps were employed to illustrate the expression patterns and variations of genes across different samples. Volcano plots were employed to illustrate genes exhibiting significant expression variations and distinct expression change tendencies (upregulation or downregulation), while Venn diagrams were utilized to depict the intersection, as shown in Figures 8, 9, and 10. These results further substantiate the potential involvement of VIPR1 in the pathogenesis of KOA while also presenting novel directions for future investigation. Despite curcumin consumption being minimal in European populations, VIPR1-related pathways may be stimulated by alternative environmental factors (such as dietary fiber intake) or endogenous ligands, consequently affecting the amounts of beneficial microbiota like Lachnospiraceae. Future research should encompass larger-scale cross-population investigations to validate the universality of VIPR1 as a pivotal regulatory node in the microbiota-joint axis and its significance for personalized treatment.

3.6. Docking results of the VIPR1 protein-curcumin drug

4. Discussion

4.1. Lachnospiraceae UCG001 and knee osteoarthritis

We confirmed by MR that the Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, exhibited a negative correlation with KOA. Previous studies have demonstrated that an imbalance in the gut microbiota can initiate a cascade of events. The intestinal barrier typically inhibits the entry of gut microbiota and its metabolites into systemic circulation; however, gut dysbiosis compromises barrier integrity. Zhang et al[25] revealed thatthe altered composition and function of gut microbiota in pathological conditions can compromise the intestinal barrier by enhancing intestinal permeability and influencing the expression of tight junction proteins. This permits bacteria and their metabolites to infiltrate the bloodstream, resulting in inflammation and tissue damage, exemplified by the entry of the bacterial metabolite lipopolysaccharide (LPS) into circulation. LPS is a potent immune activator that swiftly stimulates the immune system upon entering the bloodstream, prompting immune cells to secrete several inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α and IL-1β, thus initiating chronic inflammatory reactions.[26] Babaei et al[27] investigated the correlation between systemic inflammatory markers and localized knee synovitis, reporting an association between systemic and localized joint inflammation, as well as a link between localized microenvironmental alterations in the joints and systemic inflammation. Hotta et al[28] reported that inflammation diminishes the expression of anabolic genes, including COL2A1, ACAN, and SOX9, in chondrocytes, while elevating the levels of catabolic factors such as ADAMTS-5 and MMP-13, thereby disrupting the metabolic equilibrium of chondrocytes. Inflammation affects the extracellular matrix synthesized by chondrocytes, elevating the production and activity of degrading enzymes, such as MMPs, which dismantle the cartilage matrix, resulting in further thinning and surface irregularities of the cartilage.[29] Based on the negative correlation observed in the study, it can be hypothesized that the Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, may inhibit detrimental processes and diminish the risk of KOA through multiple mechanisms. The regulation of intestinal immunity may stimulate intestinal immune cells to generate specific cytokines that regulate systemic immunity and prevent inflammation triggered by excessive immunological activation. To preserve the integrity of the intestinal barrier, it interacts with intestinal epithelial cells to stimulate the expression of tight junction proteins, augment the efficacy of the physical barrier, and obstruct the entry of harmful substances like LPS into the bloodstream; it may also generate antimicrobial substances to inhibit the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria, maintain the balance of the gut microbiota, and indirectly safeguard the functionality of the intestinal barrier.[30] From a metabolite standpoint, this enterobacterium may generate metabolites exhibiting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, including SCFAs. SCFAs provide energy to intestinal epithelial cells, facilitating their growth and repair. Additionally, they modulate immune cell activity, inhibit inflammatory mediators, and diminish systemic inflammation, thus inhibiting the progression of KOA.[31,32]

4.2. Glycine and serine

This study revealed that the Lachnospiraceae family, specifically the species UCG001, decreased the ratio of glycine-to-serine, thereby hindering the progression of KOA. From the standpoint of the inflammatory response, glycine exhibits a clear anti-inflammatory effect; it activates GlyR ion channels, diminishes intracellular calcium levels, modulates chloride influx, lowers the concentrations of pro-inflammatory mediators and cytokine, and influences the NF-κB signaling pathway to decrease inflammatory cell infiltration and reduce the concentrations of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines.[33] Moreover, glycine attenuates the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines by preventing necrosis, obstructing pyroptosis, and blocking the initial activation of inflammatory cells.[34] The Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, decreased this ratio, potentially through an adaptive defensive mechanism. It may modulate the intestinal metabolic microenvironment, diminishing the excessive absorption of glycine by inflammatory cells and enabling glycine to manifest its anti-inflammatory effects at critical locations. The Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, may influence the gut’s pH and redox potential by modifying its metabolites. Studies indicate that SCFAs, including acetic, propionic, and butyric acids, generated by intestinal microbes, can profoundly influence the intestinal milieu.[35] The Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, may enhance the secretion of short-chain fatty acid. SCFAs lower intestinal pH, inhibit pathogenic bacteria, and foster a favorable environment for glycine metabolism and absorption; concurrently, they influence intestinal epithelial cells, regulate metabolism processes, and indirectly mitigate the excessive absorption of glycine by inflammatory cells. Moreover, glycine participates in metabolic activities that indirectly facilitate the circumstances or intermediates necessary for the synthesis of SCFAs. Glycine is involved in the gluconeogenesis pathway, generating carbohydrates[36] like glucose, which gut microorganisms can digest to yield SCFAs. Furthermore, the gut microbiota may regulate bile acid metabolism, with alterations in bile acid species and concentrations impacting the structure and function of the gut microbial population, potentially modulating glycine uptake by inflammatory cells.[37] Consequently, this gut microbiota may facilitate the utilization of glycine in anti-inflammatory metabolism, preserve the integrity of the joint milieu, and alleviate inflammatory damage to articular cartilage. At the cellular metabolic level, serine participates in phospholipid synthesis, influencing cell membrane stability and functionality.[38] In KOA, articular chondrocytes are compromised by oxidative stress and inflammation, leading to diminished cell membrane stability. The Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, diminishes the glycine-to-serine ratio, potentially modifying the phospholipid synthesis pattern in chondrocytes and influencing serine flux into the phospholipid synthesis pathway. This alteration enables chondrocytes to synthesize more stable phospholipids, thereby enhancing cell membranes resistance against inflammation and oxidative stress, decreasing external injuries, and postponing the advancement of KOA. Moreover, glycine and serine are essential components of one-carbon metabolism, which is intricately related to DNA synthesis and repair, as well as cellular proliferation and differentiation.[39] Alterations in the glycine-to-serine ratio influence one-carbon metabolism, hence affecting the proliferation and repair capabilities of chondrocytes. When the ratio is appropriate, one-carbon metabolism provides sufficient methyl donors for chondrocyte proliferation, enhances DNA synthesis and cell division, and aids in the repair of damaged cartilage; concurrently, it supports the maintenance of normal epigenetic modifications within the cell to ensure proper chondrocyte function.[40] The Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, decreases the glycine-to-serine ratio, possibly by precisely regulating one-carbon metabolism and facilitating chondrocyte repair and joint tissue preservation, hence inhibiting the progression of KOA.

4.3. Mechanisms of VIPR1 gene regulation

This study demonstrates that VIPR1 exerts a regulatory influence on the Lachnospiraceae family, specifically the UCG001 genus, in relation to the glycine-to-serine ratio and KOA association system. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that VIPR1 participates in hormone signaling and neuroactive ligand–receptor pathways to sustain homeostatic equilibrium. It functions as a receptor for vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), and the specific interaction of the 2 initiates downstream signaling. GO enrichment analysis indicated that VIPR1-associated genes were significantly enriched in the G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway coupled to cyclic nucleotide second messengers and the modulation of adenylate cyclase. VIP interacts with VIPR1, hence modifying intracellular cyclic nucleotide second messenger levels (e.g., cAMP) and regulating cellular physiological activities. VIPR1 exhibits diverse MFs, including the binding of peptide hormones, facilitating precise recognition and attachment to these hormones, activating G protein-coupled signaling, and significantly contributing to the regulation of normal functions in the intestines, joints, and various tissues and organs. The VIP-VIPR1 signaling system in the intestine may influence intestinal microecology and maintain the equilibrium of gut microbiota. Research indicates that this signaling pathway regulates the secretory function of intestinal epithelial cells[41] and the activity of immune cells,[42] thus providing an optimal environment for the survival of the gut microbiota. The Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, typically preserves an appropriate glycine-to-serine ratio and protects knee joint health. The pathological process of KOA disrupts this balance. From the standpoint of the neuroactive ligand–receptor pathway, the inflammatory milieu is an important characteristic of KOA, with numerous inflammatory molecules, including TNF-α and IL-1β, being released, thereby disrupting the VIP–VIPR1 signaling pathway and obstructing signaling. Moreover, the mRNA and protein levels of VIPR1 are markedly diminished in the joint tissues of individuals with KOA, which directly undermines the regulatory functions of VIP on articular chondrocyte- and gut-associated tissues.[43] Abnormalities in signaling pathways inside the colon may disrupt intestinal microecology and influence the abundance and activity of the Lachnospiraceae family, specifically the species UCG001. An imbalance in intestinal microecology disrupts the Lachnospiraceae family’s genus UCG001’s capacity to decrease the glycine-to-serine ratio, resulting in a deviation from the usual range of this ratio. An abnormal glycine-to-serine ratio disrupts one-carbon metabolism and collagen synthesis in chondrocytes, inhibits their anabolic capacity, accelerates articular cartilage degeneration, and facilitates the progression of KOA. Alterations in the glycine-to-serine ratio in Lachnospiraceae UCG001 may also influence the neuroactive ligand–receptor pathway. The gut microbiota and its metabolites influence the neurological system and joint tissues through the gut–brain and gut–joint axes.[44,45] An aberrant ratio of glycine-to-serine may result in the production of many atypical metabolites. These chemicals infiltrate the circulation and may influence nerve endings or immune cells, affecting the release of VIP and the function of VIPR1, further aggravating the disturbance of the neuroactive ligand–receptor pathway and establishing a detrimental cycle.

4.4. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations

This work anticipated the potential binding location of curcumin’s molecular structure to VIPR1 and performed molecular dynamics modeling of the binding site. The molecular docking findings indicate that curcumin’s molecular structure resembles the binding of VIPR1, potentially modulating VIP activity and enhancing signal transduction. Curcumin’s occupancy of the VIPR1 binding site may enhance the regulation of cyclic nucleotide second messengers, resulting in stabilized levels of crucial intracellular signaling molecules like cAMP, which subsequently regulate cellular processes.[46] In articular chondrocytes, a steady signaling pathway facilitates the expression of genes associated with cartilage matrix synthesis, enhances chondrocyte anabolism, and postpones cartilage degeneration. From a MF standpoint, VIPR1 executes various functions, including peptide-hormone binding and hormone binding. The interaction of curcumin with VIPR1 may influence these functions. Curcumin may enhance the affinity of VIPR1 for particular peptide hormones, hence optimizing signaling efficiency; conversely, it may also modify the interaction of VIPR1 with downstream effector molecules. Curcumin may alter the gut microecological environment by influencing the VIP-VIPR1 signaling pathway and its MF. The VIP-VIPR1 signaling pathway significantly regulates the secretory function of intestinal epithelial cells and the activity of immune cells, while curcumin intervention may enhance this regulatory mechanism. Curcumin’s modulation of VIPR1 function may stimulate intestinal epithelial cells to secrete increased quantities of chemicals advantageous to the Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001. The Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, effectively reduces the glycine-to-serine ratio, keeping it within a reasonable range. Molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that the binding conformation of curcumin to VIPR1 remained stable during the simulation, exhibiting minimal fluctuations in the root mean square deviation. This indicates that curcumin can persistently occupy the VIPR1 binding site and provide structural protection for its modulatory effects. root mean square fluctuation analysis indicated altered amino acid residue flexibility in the binding region of VIPR1 to curcumin, and conformational alterations in this region may influence the interface of VIPR1 with other molecules. Binding free energy estimates demonstrated a robust interaction between the 2, confirming that the binding was stable and secure. These results elucidate the mechanism of curcumin’s interaction with VIPR1 from a multifaceted perspective, encompassing structural and energetic dimensions, and may provide a theoretical foundation for the creation of novel pharmaceuticals or treatment techniques. The identification of VIPR1 carries significant biological implications: as a receptor for VIP, its aberrant expression could facilitate the development of KOA by influencing intestinal immune homeostasis and microbiota composition. Despite the generally low consumption of curcumin among European populations and the scarcity of direct studies connecting curcumin to specific microbiota like Lachnospiraceae, the findings indicate that VIPR1 may serve as a conserved molecular target that facilitates the relationship between microbiota-host interactions and joint inflammation, even with minimal curcumin exposure. In such cases, genetic variations in associated pathways might indirectly influence KOA susceptibility by altering the gut microbiota.

4.5. Limitations of this study

This study has advanced the understanding of curcumin’s mechanisms in KOA and gut microbiota; nonetheless, some limitations persist that require attention. Firstly, the causal inferences in this study mainly depend on Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis, lacking validation through cellular investigations and animal models. The inhibitory mechanism of the Lachnospiraceae family UCG001 genus on OA remains ambiguous, and quantitative investigations of this bacterial genus, its metabolic products, and associated pathways have yet to be conducted. Secondly, the data from the GWAS primarily derive from European populations. Conversely, the transcriptomic data are presumed to be from North American patients, but this is not expressly mentioned in the relevant articles. This variance in population origin may compromise the study outcomes. While genes exhibiting substantial correlations may be retained, this concern raises doubt on the reliability of the findings and directly constrains the generalizability of the study’s conclusions. Divergences in gut microbiota composition, genetic predispositions, and environmental influences (including diet and lifestyle) among various racial and geographical populations may influence the gut–joint axis, thereby affecting the generalizability of the findings. Thirdly, regarding VIPR1, there is currently inadequate in vivo experimental data to elucidate the effects of anomalies in the VIP-VIPR1 pathway on the gut microbiome and the UCG001 genus of the Lachnospiraceae family, and the pertinent feedback mechanisms remain unvalidated. Fourthly, studies on curcumin predominantly remain theoretical, lack in vivo experimental validation, and fail to consider its pharmacokinetic properties, dosage, or potential adverse effects. This study has not yet received clinical validation. Future extensive clinical trials with diverse populations are essential to evaluate the therapeutic potential of the UCG001 genus within the Lachnospiraceae family and curcumin and to validate the universality of the microbial and genetic associations identified.

5. Conclusion

This study investigated the therapeutic efficacy of curcumin in the treatment of KOA through the gut microbiota–plasma metabolite axis. The Lachnospiraceae family, genus UCG001, had a negative correlation with KOA, which was mitigated by reducing the glycine-to-serine ratio. VIPR1 regulated this system. Molecular docking and simulations demonstrated that curcumin interacts with VIPR1. This binding may regulate cyclic nucleotide second messengers, streamline signal transduction, and transmission, and alter gut microecology, potentially serving as a therapy for KOA.
