The Barriers and Enablers to the Use of Alternative Approaches to Restrictive Practices: A Scoping Review and Causal Loop Diagram
Authors: Stephanie L Bennetts, Genevieve Pepin, James J Lucas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe use of restrictive practices in acute mental health services has long been a contentious issue globally, with well‐documented harms for both service users and the practitioners required to implement them. Existing research examining what influences the uptake of alternative approaches could benefit from synthesis through a qualitative systems thinking lens, enabling deeper insights into key leverage points and the development of dynamic hypotheses. This study aimed to review the literature and develop a Causal Loop Diagram (CLD) to visualise how interconnected pathways within the literature describe the potential elimination of restrictive practices in adult acute mental healthcare services.DesignA scoping review informed by Arksey and O'Malley's framework was undertaken, combined with a qualitative systems thinking approach to synthesise findings into a CLD.MethodsA systematic search and inclusion strategy identified 20 relevant articles exploring barriers and enablers to alternative approaches to restrictive practices. Key findings were extracted and synthesised, then mapped into a CLD comprising four domains: (1) peer support, relationships and communication; (2) staff capacity and the clinical environment; (3) practitioner decision‐making processes; and (4) transforming practice away from the status quo.ResultsThe CLD illustrates how multiple, interdependent factors interact to influence the use of restrictive practices. It highlights the dynamic relationships between relational practices, workforce capacity, environmental conditions and clinical decision‐making. The model provides a systems‐level representation of the ‘topography’ of the literature and identifies potential feedback loops and leverage points for change, supporting the development of hypotheses for reducing restrictive practices.Patient or Public ContributionNo patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this research.Keywords: eliminating, mental health care services, qualitative, quantitative, reducing, restrictive practices, review

Background

Restrictive practices, also referred to as restrictive interventions, are used by mental health practitioners when a person in care becomes agitated or aggressive and staff aim to prevent the situation from escalating further. Although restrictive practices take many forms, this paper focuses specifically on those most commonly used in Victoria, Australia: physical restraint and seclusion. These practices are generally applied in adult acute mental health settings to manage behaviour perceived as threatening or unsafe (Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018).

Efforts to reduce and ultimately eliminate restrictive practices have gained considerable momentum both within Australia and internationally (Goulet et al. 2017; Hernandez et al. 2017; Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018). In the United States, media scrutiny and subsequent congressional attention in 1998 highlighted the harms associated with these practices, prompting investment in and adoption of the Six Core Strategies (LeBel et al. 2014). This framework, designed to minimise conflict, violence and the use of restraint and seclusion (Huckshorn et al. 2005), demonstrated success in reducing both the frequency and duration of these interventions (Wieman et al. 2014) and has since been implemented across multiple countries (LeBel et al. 2014). In Victoria, the Safewards model was trialled across several public mental health services and was shown to support more recovery‐oriented approaches to care (Fletcher et al. 2019). Despite these advances, the use of restraint and seclusion continues to be widespread in mental health services globally. While researchers such as Lawrence et al. (2021) have examined restrictive practices in forensic mental health contexts, there remains limited research exploring alternatives within adult acute mental health settings, which is the focus of this paper.

Growing awareness of the harms associated with restrictive practices has contributed to a shift in public and institutional attitudes, with decreasing tolerance for their use. In Australia, the Royal Commission into Victoria's Mental Health System (2021) formally recommended that restrictive practices be phased out of acute mental health services within a decade (State of Victoria 2021). Although debates surrounding restrictive practices have often centred on staff safety, the human rights implications for service users have received comparatively little attention and remain a significant concern (McSherry and Maker 2020). While alternatives to restrictive practices are available, including frameworks such as Safewards (Bowers 2014), reliance on restrictive interventions persists.

The use of restrictive practices in acute mental health services in Australia appears to be at a critical juncture. Although there is substantial literature examining restrictive practices, there are no scoping reviews that synthesise findings across studies while applying a systems thinking perspective (Leischow and Milstein 2006). The development of a Causal Loop Diagram (CLD) (Darabi and Hosseinichimeh 2020) offers a way to examine the barriers and enablers influencing the adoption of alternative approaches. This study aims to address that gap by creating such a CLD.

A CLD (Bennetts et al. 2024; Lucas et al. 2022) is a widely used systems thinking tool that helps illustrate complex social issues and is grounded in the broader field of systems dynamics (Barbrook‐Johnson and Penn 2022). Its value within a scoping review lies in its ability to integrate qualitative and quantitative insights by mapping feedback loops that describe system behaviour (Dhirasasna and Sahin 2019). CLDs also provide a visual representation of qualitative findings, making it easier to understand how different elements within a system interact and offering a foundation for future simulation modelling (Barbrook‐Johnson and Penn 2022). Combining systems thinking, CLD development and scoping review methodology provides an innovative approach to examining the factors that shape the use of alternatives to restrictive practices and supports the development of dynamic hypotheses for future research (Newberry and Carhart 2024).

Author Group Positioning

The author team is made up of professionals from a range of disciplines, including allied health, social work, occupational therapy, disability and social inclusion. Each member shares a commitment to improving the wellbeing of both mental health service users and the practitioners who support them. The group also includes individuals with lived experience of mental ill health, who have accessed mental health services within their own communities and have worked inside mental health systems. Their combined perspectives strengthen the relevance and grounding of this work.

Review Question

The question guiding this scoping review and CLD creation was: What are the barriers and enablers to the use of alternative approaches to restrictive practices in adult acute mental healthcare services?

Methods

Scoping reviews are a methodology in health and social research that allow for the review, synthesis and assessment of emerging evidence (Peterson et al. 2017). Therefore, a pragmatic and rigorous scoping review structure is needed to ensure the quality of the review. In this scoping review, Arksey and O'Malley (2005) six‐step framework was applied: (1) identifying the review question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) charting the data, (5) collating, summarising and reporting the results and (6) consultation.

Step 1: Identifying the Review Question

All authors (SLB, JJL, GP, SM, RC) collaborated to refine the review question and develop the search strategy. A university librarian, who was not otherwise involved in the project, reviewed the strategy for completeness and accuracy. Search terms were selected based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1) and included synonyms, Boolean operators and relevant MeSH or subject headings. Searches were conducted in CINAHL, MEDLINE and APA PsycInfo via EBSCOHost, with terms adapted for each database (see Table S1). The search was run and refined in September 2023 using the following key terms: (‘restrictive practices’, ‘restrictive intervention’, ‘physical intervention’, restraint, coercion, reactive, seclusion or constraint) combined with (barrier or enabler* or obstacle* or facilitator) and (‘mental health setting’ or ‘mental health care setting’ or psychiat or ‘emergency department’ or ‘mental health practice’) and (reduc* or eliminat*). This approach ensured comprehensive coverage of relevant literature.

Step 2: Identifying Relevant Studies

The inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1) were developed using the SPIDER tool (Cooke et al. 2012; Page et al. 2021), which is particularly suited to qualitative research and helps address limitations in database indexing that often favour quantitative studies.

Step 3: Study Selection

After removing duplicates (n = 1271), one reviewer (SLB) screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining records (n = 2218) against the eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant articles (n = 164) were then retrieved and assessed in detail by the same reviewer. A second reviewer (JJL) was consulted throughout, following recommendations by Levac et al. (2010), to confirm decisions and validate extracted information. A third reviewer (GP) was available to resolve disagreements. This process balanced efficiency with methodological robustness and reduced the likelihood of individual bias. Covidence, an online systematic review platform, was used to manage screening and selection, and the process was reported in line with PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Page et al. 2021). Ultimately, 20 articles met the criteria for inclusion.

One reviewer (SLB) also evaluated the methodological quality of each included study using Dixon‐Woods' (2006) six‐question framework, which considers: (1) clarity of the research question, (2) adequacy of the literature search, (3) appropriateness of study selection, (4) quality appraisal, (5) data extraction and (6) development of a synthesising argument (Depraetere et al. 2021; Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006). This framework is well suited to synthesising diverse bodies of literature. A second reviewer (JJL) again provided oversight. No studies were excluded based on quality; instead, quality assessments were used to interpret the strength and limitations of the findings.

Step 4: Charting the Data

Data extraction was carried out using a structured coding sheet that captured study characteristics (such as authors, publication year, sample size, country and service setting). Key findings, including direct participant quotations, were also recorded. The first author (SLB) additionally extracted information on the direction and polarity of relationships between factors identified in each study, following recommendations by Poix and Elmusharaf (2023). Most data extraction was completed by one reviewer (SLB) and checked for accuracy by a second reviewer (JJL) using NVivo (Version 20). Any disagreements were resolved with input from a third reviewer (GP). The PAGER framework was applied to identify Patterns, Advances, Gaps and Evidence for Practice and Research recommendations across the included literature (Table S1 PAGER Framework).

Step 5: Collating, Summarising and Reporting the Results

The extracted data from the 20 studies were analysed to identify how mental health service users and practitioners described the barriers and enablers to using alternatives to restrictive practices in acute mental health settings. A spreadsheet was developed to map each study's factors and the relationships between them. Similar factors were grouped into broader domains that reflected key areas influencing the use of alternative approaches. In the CLD, dashed black lines were used to indicate potential connections that were not explicitly demonstrated in the included studies but were considered plausible based on the wider literature. These inferred links were included only after careful discussion to ensure they added value to the model. The CLD was developed using the Systems Thinking in Community Knowledge‐Exchange (STICK‐E) platform (Hayward et al. 2020). An iterative process was used, with two authors refining the factors, domains and linkages until agreement was reached.

Step 6: Consultation

Although Arksey and O'Malley (2005) describe consultation as optional, this review incorporated it by presenting the findings to local stakeholder groups. These consultations informed co‐design activities undertaken as part of the first author's PhD research, the outcomes of which are published elsewhere (Bennetts et al. 2024).

Ethical Considerations

As this study involved a scoping review and the development of a causal loop diagram based solely on previously published literature, no human participants were involved and no identifiable data were collected. Ethical approval was therefore not required.

Following PRISMA guidelines, the search and screening process identified 3489 records. After removing duplicates (n = 1271), the remaining titles and abstracts were screened, resulting in the exclusion of 2054 records. Full‐text review of the remaining articles led to 20 studies meeting the criteria for inclusion in this scoping review (Figure 1; Table 2). All included studies were published between 2014 and 2023. Of these, nine studies (45%) used quantitative designs, eight studies (40%) used qualitative methods, and three studies (15%) employed mixed‐methods approaches.

Characteristics of the included studies and variables used in the CLD.

Countries

Four (20%) included studies were undertaken in Australia. The remaining articles reported studies from Brazil (5%; n = 1), China (5%; n = 1), Germany (15%; n = 3), Netherlands (5%; n = 1), New Zealand (5%; n = 1), Nigeria (5%; n = 1), Norway (5%; n = 1), Spain (5%; n = 1), UK (15%; n = 3) and USA (15%; n = 3). The included article pool is northern‐hemisphere dominant (70%; n = 14).

Participant Ages and Gender Identities

There were approximately 5045 mental health service user voices, and 1326 practitioner voices represented across the included studies. Participant ages ranged from < 12–108 years of age. There were reports of more women than men in all studies (Women: n = 1005; Men: n = 390). Nine articles (n = 9; 45%) did not explicitly report the gender of participants.

Restrictive Practice Type and Participant Perspective Category

Articles reporting the restrictive practices of both seclusion and restraint (physical/mechanical) were most represented (n = 16; 60%), followed by restraint (physical and mechanical, n = 6; 30%) and seclusion (n = 2; 10%). Many of the articles focused solely on the perspective of the practitioners (n = 55%), followed by the reporting of the service user's perspective on its own (n = 3; 15%) and the combination of the practitioner and service user's perspective (n = 3; 15%). Moreover, three articles (15%) discussed the researchers' evaluations of implementation programs to reduce/eliminate restrictive practices. The nursing profession was represented by far the most (n = 14; 70%), compared to other health practitioners such as physicians, social workers, psychiatrists and occupational therapists respectively (n = 1; 5%).

The Causal Loop Diagram: Factors, Domains and Interconnections

The CLD (Figure 2) offers a visual representation of the different barriers and enablers to the alternative use of restrictive practices in adult acute mental healthcare services. The CLD was constructed to illustrate the connections between the different constructs that emerged during data collection and analysis. This approach followed the work of Poix and Elmusharaf (2023), who demonstrated how CLDs can be used to visually represent relationships grounded in qualitative findings. The CLD contains 22 factors distributed around the objective of the ‘Reduction in use of Restrictive Practices’. The CLD is also built around four subsystems identified via pink colour coded text: (1) Peer support, relationships and communication, (2) Staff capacity and the clinical environment, (3) The decision‐making process of the practitioner and (4) Transforming practice away from the status quo. Not all domains and factors involved contribution from every article, but each article contributed to at least one factor or domain. It is important to note that these domains are not considered mutually exclusive, but rather interdependent—each having an influence on the other (Figure 2; Table 3).

Peer Support, Relationships and Communication

Peer support, relationships and communication were reported across seven articles (Aluh, Ayilara, et al. 2023; Aluh, Onu, et al. 2023; Antonsson et al. 2023; Aragonés‐Calleja and Sánchez‐Martínez 2022; Badouin et al. 2023; Brown et al. 2022; Paradis‐Gagné et al. 2021; Wilson et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2014). This domain relates to the service users' access to peer support and the quality of communication and therapeutic relationships on the ward where they could possibly be subjected to restrictive practices. The CLD constructs that arose from the articles relate to communication, the therapeutic relationship between the service user and the practitioner, the availability of support, and the empathy of nursing staff. Badouin et al. (2023) are one of the first studies of its kind to examine the relationship between peer support availability and its effects on the use of restrictive practices. They discuss the lack of availability of peer support as a contributing factor to an increased incidence of the restrictive practice of restraint among their participants in the control group. Moreover, Aragonés‐Calleja and Sánchez‐Martínez (2022) found that by increasing the therapeutic relationship between the service user and the practitioner, the need for any restrictive practice is greatly diminished. Both studies highlight the great importance of building rapport with service users (Antonsson et al. 2023) when they are at their most vulnerable, and the additional benefit of de‐escalating aggressive behaviour.

The importance of open‐dialogue and clear communication was described by Aluh, Ayilara, et al. (2023); Aluh, Onu, et al. (2023) as pivotal for ensuring the avoidance of restrictive practices. Service users and their families need to feel seen and heard and have patience extended to them in a time of crisis, which often accompanies an acute hospital admission. Wharewera‐Mika et al. (2016) take this point a step further by outlining the significance of cultural awareness and sensitivity when considering approaches to avoid using the more specific restrictive practice of seclusion:

It basically comes down to the approach…treating people right, the way you'd like to be treated…Kanohi ki te kanohi (face to face). Not six or seven (staff) standing around any individual, what it does is it freaks them out, it just raises their anxiety.

In addition, both Yang et al. (2014) and Antonsson et al. (2023) discuss the importance of showing empathy and providing trauma‐informed care in avoiding the use of seclusion and restraint in acute mental health services:

[P27] Creating a therapeutic environment greatly influences and benefits the relationship between the service user and staff, and it is feasible…not everything can be solved by pills. If you manage to create that bond and that person maybe tells you what is happening to them, then you can help them without medication.

Staff Capacity and the Clinical Environment

Staff capacity and the clinical environment were reported across six articles (Aragonés‐Calleja and Sánchez‐Martínez 2023; McKeown et al. 2019; Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018; Norvoll et al. 2017; Wharewera‐Mika et al. 2016; Wilson et al. 2018). This domain not only relates to the organisational and structural factors surrounding staff in acute mental health services, but also relates to the actual environment and clinical space that is occupied within this service that may enact restrictive practices. The CLD constructs that form this domain are: staff availability, administrative tasks, leadership power and control and staff training and knowledge. McKeown et al. (2019) describe a multitude of staffing factors contributing to aggressive behaviour that resulted in the use of restraint. These include a lack of on‐the‐job training due to insufficient staffing levels, compounded by the burden of the ever‐growing expectation of non‐clinical tasks (McKeown et al. 2019). Furthermore, Aragonés‐Calleja and Sánchez‐Martínez (2023) describe the lack of training and knowledge around de‐escalation techniques and conflict management as a contributor to the use of restrictive practices in the first place.

In terms of shifting the focus from reduction/elimination of restrictive practices to prevention, nurse participants from the study conducted by Wharewera‐Mika et al. (2016) discussed the importance of developing strong leadership to enact real change in practice: ‘Consistent sustainable implementation…[and] leadership towards organisational changes…[requires] effective leadership (p. 262).’ In addition to strong leadership from practitioners, the suggestion of person‐centred practices to further strengthen the staff capacity and therefore avoid the use of restrictive practices is also needed. Muir‐Cochrane et al. (2018) explain how they found that restrictive practices were seen in direct contrast to the principles of person‐centred care: ‘…the client isn't at the centre of the care when they're getting restrained (p. 1514).’ However, balancing the needs of the service user and safety of the clinical environment is also an issue, especially when both perspectives need to be effectively accommodated and integrated when considering alternative approaches to restrictive practices (Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018):

We're too overrun by risk (FG1). I think it [risk adversity] paralyses our decision‐making or the decision‐making of clinicians anyway…I think it feeds more restrictive practices (p. 1515).

In addition to balancing the needs and safety of service users and practitioners, Wilson et al. (2018) discuss the physical aspect of the ward environment having an impact on the use of restrictive practices. Participants from this study suggested specific ward rooms to aid with calming people down and providing activities such as arts and crafts to distract service users when their behaviour may start to escalate (Wilson et al. 2018).

The Decision‐Making Process of the Practitioner

The decision‐making process of the practitioner was reported across four articles (Aluh, Ayilara, et al. 2023; Aluh, Onu, et al. 2023; Efkemann et al. 2022; Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018; Norvoll et al. 2017). This domain relates to the practitioners' attitudes to restrictive practices, their fears and the moral concerns and implications on the decision‐making process to apply restrictive practices. The CLD was informed by the following constructs: post‐coercion apology, ambivalence to the use of restrictive practice, staff feelings of fear and attitudes towards restrictive practices. Efkemann et al. (2022) reported that there was an increased likelihood of implementing restrictive practices if the practitioner's attitudes leant more favourably towards their practice. Interestingly, Norvoll et al. (2017) described this phenomenon in the context of a moral justification towards guiding the decision towards the use of restrictive practices in individual cases. Thereby, recognising the relation of a practitioner's own experiences and judgements and thus how this effects the likelihood of implementing restrictive practices.

Muir‐Cochrane et al. (2018) and Aluh, Ayilara, et al. (2023); Aluh, Onu, et al. (2023) described the juxtaposition of fear and maintaining a therapeutic relationship between service user and practitioner. In Muir‐Cochrane et al. (2018) study, when. practitioners were asked about the elimination of restrictive practices, they were met with fear and apprehension:

alright this is what we're going to do [ban the use of seclusion and restraint] and you're left standing there thinking ‘what are we going to do now?’ (p. 1513)

In Aluh, Ayilara, et al. (2023); Aluh, Onu, et al. (2023)'s study practitioners described the importance of the post‐coercion apology and how it is imperative to maintaining trust and the therapeutic relationship between service users and practitioners.

Transforming Practice Away From the Status Quo

Transforming practice away from the status quo was reported across 16 articles (Aluh, Ayilara, et al. 2023; Aluh, Onu, et al. 2023; Aragonés‐Calleja and Sánchez‐Martínez 2023; Baumgardt et al. 2019; Blair et al. 2017; Chong et al. 2023; Duxbury et al. 2019; Gerace and Muir‐Cochrane 2019; Godfrey et al. 2014; Hamilton et al. 2023; Mann‐Poll et al. 2020; McKeown et al. 2019; Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018; Norvoll et al. 2017; Vedana et al. 2018; Wharewera‐Mika et al. 2016; Whitecross et al. 2020). This domain relates to the implementation of evidenced based practices to reduce and or eliminate the use of restrictive practices, maintaining safety for service users and practitioners and the regulation and legislative policies that occur on acute wards regarding restrictive practice implementation. The constructs that made up this domain were: strategies to reduce restrictive practices, implementation of evidence‐based practices to reduce restrictive practices, home consultation and advocates, detriment to staff and service users, regulation and legislation and resources for mental health.

The adoption and evaluation of evidence‐based practice programs to reduce and eliminate restrictive practices was a significant aspect of the findings. Baumgardt et al. (2019) discussed the success of reducing restrictive practices (physical restraint and seclusion) through the implementation of the Safewards program. Moreover, Hamilton et al. (2023) also confirmed the positive effects of programs such as that of Safewards in increasing communication and patient‐centred approaches, as well as increasing therapeutic relationships. Whitecross et al. (2020) evaluation of the psychiatric behaviour of concern (psy‐BOC) model found not only a reduction in the use of restrictive practices (physical restraint and seclusion) but also an increased use of sensory modulation for reducing aggressive behaviours by service users.

Gerace and Muir‐Cochrane (2019) highlighted that nursing staff only felt moderately safe on acute inpatient wards and that maintaining safety was a key aspect of their concerns regarding the prospective reduction and or elimination of restrictive practices. McKeown et al. (2019) echoed these sentiments by detailing the impact of low staffing levels on the ability to reduce the escalation of aggressive behaviours:

Two staff on here were seriously injured and the patient summoned help because there was no staff around…three staff are still off sick because they were traumatised by it, it was a serious, serious assault. And it makes you wonder, had there been more staff (p. 13).

A component of increasing safety in acute wards, is the use of de‐escalation techniques. Aragonés‐Calleja and Sánchez‐Martínez (2023) discussed how the use of verbal de‐escalation should be the first option when trying to avoid the use of restrictive practices. Furthermore, Godfrey et al. (2014) discussed how they determined the reduction of mechanical restraint through training staff on the use of de‐escalation techniques and the implementation of a response team.

Legislative policy placement on acute wards was another key issue that recurred within the literature. Aluh, Ayilara, et al. (2023); Aluh, Onu, et al. (2023) discussed how regulation and legislation were of importance to service users for preventing the abuse of restrictive practices and maintaining guidelines for the justification of their use. Whilst some practitioners were also in agreement within their study of having laws to regulate the use of restrictive practices, other practitioners were more supportive of the individual hospital having their own policies and procedures to guide reducing the use of restrictive practices.

Discussion

The aim of this scoping review was to map the existing literature on the barriers and enablers to using alternatives to restrictive practices in adult acute mental health services. The review covers literature from the past 9 years and provides a comprehensive examination of how relationships, the clinical environment, and therapeutic practices influence the uptake of alternatives to restrictive practices. Following the review, the findings were synthesised into a causal loop diagram (CLD). This visual tool was developed to support understanding of the complex interactions shaping the use of alternatives in acute mental health settings. Practitioners highlighted the need for additional training in alternative approaches and emphasised workplace safety, while service users stressed the importance of relationships and communication with practitioners as central to feeling safe and supporting de‐escalation of aggressive behaviour.

Across the literature, practitioners consistently identified the need for enhanced training, stronger leadership and safer working conditions. In contrast, service users emphasised the importance of communication, therapeutic relationships and peer support in preventing escalation. These perspectives differ but are complementary, pointing to the layered nature of restrictive practice use and the role of organisational and workforce factors in shaping clinical practice.

Within the CLD, the domain with the greatest number of outgoing connections to other constructs was Transforming practice away from the status quo (n = 2), followed by the other domains (n = 1). This suggests that maintaining safety is central to reducing the use of restrictive practices. The domain receiving the most connections was also Transforming practice away from the status quo (n = 5), followed by peer support, relationships and communication (n = 4), staff capacity and clinical environment (n = 4) and the moral scope of the practitioner (n = 4). This pattern indicates the importance of resourcing mental health services effectively and ensuring that evidence‐based approaches inform a range of strategies to reduce restrictive practices.

Overall, service users largely discussed barriers and enablers from the perspective of peer support, relationships and communication. In contrast, practitioners focused more on staff capacity, the clinical environment, the moral scope of practice and transforming established practices. These different perspectives show how factors influencing restrictive practice use operate differently for service users and practitioners.

Peer support, relationships and communication were particularly important for service users. They emphasised the value of having peer support available on the ward (Badouin et al. 2023), which contributed to increased feelings of safety and a reduced likelihood of escalation (Oster et al. 2016). Recognition of cultural diversity and sensitivity was also identified as important for strengthening therapeutic relationships and influencing the use of restrictive practices (Wharewera‐Mika et al. 2016). This aligns with findings from Lawrence et al. (2024), who reported improved confidence and emotional wellbeing among service users when a person‐centred approach was used to reduce restrictive practices.

From the practitioner perspective, strengthening staff capacity and improving the clinical environment were seen as starting with leadership and the development of effective practices that promote alternatives (Wharewera‐Mika et al. 2016). Investment in training and ongoing support for staff was also identified as a key strategy for overcoming barriers (Aragonés‐Calleja and Sánchez‐Martínez 2023). However, organisational and clinical factors must be considered alongside the moral scope of the practitioner. A major barrier arises when practitioners hold attitudes that favour restrictive practices (Efkemann et al. 2022). This becomes particularly problematic when staff feel unsafe and believe there are no viable alternatives (Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018). Lawrence et al. (2024) similarly found that trauma‐saturated organisational environments, characterised by ongoing threat, cumulative stress and a sense of vulnerability, influence how nurses respond in escalating situations. These conditions can limit perceived options and make seclusion appear necessary. In contrast, the use of evidence‐based approaches such as Safewards has been identified as an enabler, supporting ward safety and reducing restrictive practices (Baumgardt et al. 2019).

While similar reviews have been conducted in other sectors, including the National Disability Insurance Scheme in Australia (Leif et al. 2023), this review is among the first to examine both service user and practitioner perspectives within adult acute mental health services. The use of a CLD as an implementation tool (Hovmand 2014) is also novel in nursing education, as it has not previously been used to visually demonstrate the factors influencing the use of alternatives to restrictive practices. Mapping approaches such as CLDs provide a high‐level overview of system interactions (Jalali et al. 2017), helping practitioners and managers identify leverage points where change can be applied, such as staffing levels, which may in turn affect restrictive practice use.

Nursing leadership is critical in shaping the culture and conditions that support alternative approaches. The findings indicate that leaders play a central role in fostering therapeutic and trauma‐informed environments where communication, empathy and relational safety are prioritised. Effective leadership involves modelling evidence‐based approaches such as Safewards, creating workplaces where staff feel psychologically safe to use de‐escalation strategies and addressing fear‐driven decision making that contributes to restrictive practices. Leaders are also responsible for embedding reflective practice and supervision structures to support staff in managing moral distress and building confidence in non‐coercive approaches. The literature shows that when leadership is inconsistent or absent, restrictive practices are more likely, particularly in environments marked by high stress and ongoing threat.

Workforce capacity also emerged as a key factor influencing restrictive practice use. Practitioners reported challenges such as inadequate staffing, heavy administrative demands, limited training opportunities, and workplace cultures characterised by high levels of trauma and risk aversion. These conditions reduce opportunities for therapeutic engagement and increase reliance on restrictive practices. Addressing workforce capacity involves ensuring adequate staffing ratios that allow time for relational care and de‐escalation, along with sustained investment in training focused on communication, conflict management and trauma‐informed care. Workforce planning must also recognise the emotional demands of acute mental health nursing and provide support to reduce burnout and fear‐based responses. Improvements to the physical environment, including calming spaces and sensory rooms, can also help reduce triggers for escalation. Policies that align safety requirements with person‐centred care are essential for balancing risk management and therapeutic engagement. By addressing these workforce issues, nursing leaders can support the consistent and sustainable use of alternatives to restrictive practices.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

A scoping review approach was selected given the wide range of literature relating to the reduction or elimination of restrictive practices, and the need to focus specifically on the barriers and enablers to using alternatives rather than intervention outcomes, which are more typical of systematic reviews. Repeating this review with revised inclusion criteria may allow for deeper exploration of the diversity of practitioner perspectives. In addition, the final CLD represents the authors' interpretation of the available primary and grey literature. It is therefore not a comprehensive model and should be viewed as an initial framework that can be built on to develop more detailed and complex system representations (Poix and Elmusharaf 2023).

Further research is needed to explore co‐designed approaches that bring together both mental health practitioners and service users to develop a portfolio of strategies that support the use of alternatives to restrictive practices.

Conclusion

The synthesis of the 20 included studies indicates that the barriers and enablers to using alternatives to restrictive practices are complex and interconnected, shaped by a range of individual, organisational and system level factors. Across the studies, both service users and practitioners described how structural, cultural, and relational influences can either support or limit the uptake of non‐restrictive approaches. This review brings together the perspectives of both groups, providing a consolidated account of the layered and often compounding factors that influence practice in acute mental healthcare settings internationally. By drawing these findings together, the review offers a clearer basis for addressing the persistent challenges that restrict the broader adoption of alternatives to restrictive practices.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

The reduction and eventual elimination of restrictive practices remains a key priority across mental health services internationally. Understanding these services from a systems perspective can be supported through the use of CLDs. CLDs make it possible to identify leverage points within the system and highlight where targeted changes may contribute to reducing the use of restrictive practices. There is clear support in the literature for implementing alternatives, and CLDs offer a practical visual tool to strengthen this work. To progress towards elimination, it is important to prioritise co‐design approaches alongside system‐level evaluation.
