Non‐Physician Contributors to Patient Satisfaction in a Japanese Primary Care: A Cross‐Sectional Secondary Analysis of Patient Satisfaction Surveys
Authors: Keita Morikawa, Takayuki Ando, Shun Tezen, Tadao Okada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatient satisfaction is a key indicator of healthcare quality. While physician‐related factors are well studied, less is known about how non‐physician staff contribute to satisfaction in primary care. This study examined the association between patient satisfaction and multidisciplinary staff involvement in a Japanese primary care clinic.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of patient satisfaction survey data collected from 2019 to 2022. The primary outcome was overall satisfaction with the visit, dichotomized as “highly satisfied” versus “less than highly satisfied.” Modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors estimated prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariable models included satisfaction with non‐physician staff, patient demographics, and physician‐related satisfaction via principal component analysis. Sensitivity analyses included consultation duration and access to care.ResultsAmong 1415 patients, higher satisfaction with the demeanor of nursing staff (PR 2.06, 95% CI 1.39–3.05) and waiting time care (PR 1.43, 95% CI 1.33–1.54) were significantly associated with greater overall satisfaction. Receptionist demeanor also showed a modest but significant association (PR 1.30, 95% CI 1.05–1.61). In the sensitivity analysis, these associations persisted for nursing staff (PR 1.64, 95% CI 1.20–2.24) and waiting time care (PR 1.08, 95% CI 1.02–1.14), while the receptionist association was attenuated (PR 1.15, 95% CI 0.97–1.35).ConclusionsBeyond physician‐related factors, nursing staff demeanor and waiting time care were found to be associated with patient satisfaction. These findings support the importance of multidisciplinary contributions to patient‐centered care in primary care clinics.

Introduction

Patient satisfaction is a critical indicator of healthcare quality and is gaining increasing attention. Reflecting how well healthcare services meet patient expectations, patient satisfaction is influenced by various factors, such as individual needs, values, and characteristics [1]. Although subjective, patient satisfaction is a key concept for assessing care quality and building trust in healthcare institutions. High levels of patient satisfaction are associated with numerous positive outcomes, such as fewer complaints, lower mortality rates, improved treatment adherence, and greater trust and loyalty toward healthcare providers [2, 3, 4, 5]. These associations emphasize the vital role of patient satisfaction in not only promoting patient well‐being but also enhancing the operational success of healthcare systems.

A systematic review found that the quality of healthcare services is the most significant determinant of patient satisfaction, reinforcing its importance as an evaluation tool [6, 7]. Previous studies have often focused on factors such as physician behavior, waiting duration, and patient demographics when examining patient satisfaction. Waiting time for consultations and the behavior of physicians are known to have a significant impact on patient satisfaction [8, 9, 10]. For example, on the patient side, factors such as age, gender, and educational background have been shown to be associated with satisfaction [11]. However, every healthcare professional involved in a patient's visit, from nurses to medical clerks, can influence their overall satisfaction through staff‐patient interaction. Recently, there has been increasing interest in understanding the roles and importance of various healthcare professionals, including receptionists, in modern primary care [12, 13, 14]. However, few studies have explored how the role of different healthcare workers affects patient satisfaction, particularly in primary care outpatient settings.

This study aimed to fill this gap by investigating how the role of different healthcare professionals impacts patient satisfaction in primary care clinics. Understanding how different healthcare professionals contribute to patient satisfaction can help develop multidisciplinary strategies for improving care quality, which will ultimately benefit both patients and healthcare providers.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study is a secondary analysis of patient satisfaction surveys conducted annually at Kameda Family Clinic Tateyama (KFCT) as part of ongoing quality improvement initiatives. KFCT is located in Tateyama City, a remote suburban municipality in Japan characterized by a population density of 410.3 individuals per square kilometer and an aging rate of 40.9% (aged ≥ 65 years) as of 2020. The clinic is a community‐based, outpatient‐only facility that serves as a teaching site for a family medicine residency program and sees approximately 200 outpatients per day.

Figure S1 illustrates the typical outpatient consultation process at KFCT. Upon arrival, patients present their health insurance card to the reception staff. New patients are required to complete a medical history form. After registration, patients wait to be called by the nursing staff, who measure vital signs and conduct a brief triage assessment, the results of which are shared with the physician prior to consultation. The nursing team comprises both registered nurses and non‐licensed nursing aides, with a structured escalation protocol in place.

Attending physicians or supervised residents conduct consultations. If additional diagnostic tests, such as blood tests or imaging, are required, the nursing staff provides appropriate instructions. After the consultation, patients proceed to the billing counter and receive a printed prescription, which they fill at a community pharmacy. The clinic does not dispense medications in‐house.

Data Collection

In KFCT, each year, during a designated one‐week period in the fall, a self‐administered questionnaire was distributed in person to all outpatients visiting the clinic. The survey design and implementation process were consistent across the four study years (2019–2022). Due to logistical considerations and the need to balance feasibility with routine clinical operations, the target sample size was set at approximately 400 respondents per year, following discussion within the clinic's Clinical Quality Improvement Committee. Although the exact number of questionnaires distributed and the response rate were not systematically recorded, the target represents roughly 4% of the clinic's unique annual outpatient population (approximately 10,000 patients per year). This analysis uses pooled cross‐sectional data from four consecutive survey cycles.

Measurement

Data for this study were obtained through a structured, anonymous, self‐administered questionnaire. The questionnaire first asked whether the respondent was the patient or an accompanying individual. In cases of proxy responses, the respondent was required to indicate their relationship to the patient. Demographic items included the patient's gender, age, and municipality of residence. The remaining items assessed various dimensions of the patient's experience during the outpatient visit. Patients were asked to evaluate the following components of their visit using five‐point Likert scales ranging from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied): demeanor of the receptionist, guidance to the waiting room, demeanor of the nursing staff, demeanor of the physician, physician's knowledge and skills, clarity of the physician's explanation, consultation duration, waiting duration, waiting time care, access to healthcare services, and overall satisfaction with the visit.

Outcome and Variables

The primary explanatory variables were satisfaction with three non‐physician care domains: demeanor of the receptionist, demeanor of the nursing staff, and the wating time care. These factors were selected a priori to reflect modifiable aspects of multidisciplinary care, particularly interpersonal interactions outside the examination room.

In accordance with previous research, covariates included patient age, gender, survey year, and satisfaction with physician‐related communication, skills, and access to healthcare [11, 15]. All satisfaction‐related variables were dichotomized in the same manner as the outcome.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and satisfaction responses. Frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables, and medians with interquartile ranges were used for ordinal satisfaction items due to their non‐normal distribution.

We first conducted univariate modified Poisson regression analyses with robust standard errors to estimate crude prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals for the association between each variable and the primary outcome. This method was chosen instead of logistic regression because the prevalence of the outcome (high overall satisfaction) exceeded 10%, and odds ratios would therefore overestimate relative risks [16, 17]. Modified Poisson regression is an established method for estimating PRs directly in cross‐sectional studies with common binary outcomes.

For the multivariate model (Model 1), we adopted a theory‐driven approach guided by prior literature and empirical correlation analysis. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated across satisfaction variables (Figure S3). A strong correlation (r = 0.86) was observed between “demeanor of the receptionist” and “guidance to the waiting room.” To minimize multicollinearity and model redundancy, we excluded “guidance to the waiting room” and retained “receptionist demeanor” as the more comprehensive construct. Similarly, “satisfaction with waiting time” was not included in the model due to its high correlation (r = 0.79) with “waiting time care.” Since waiting time itself is often less modifiable in busy clinical settings, we opted to include “waiting time care” as a more actionable and relational variable. To adjust for physician‐related communication and interpersonal quality while mitigating multicollinearity, we conducted principal component analysis (PCA) on three highly correlated physician‐related variables (demeanor of the physician, physician's knowledge and skills, and clarity of the physician's explanation). Conceptually, PCA reduces several correlated measures to a single index that captures their shared variance—here, the latent construct of physician‐related satisfaction—thereby simplifying interpretation and limiting multicollinearity. The first principal component, which explained over 85% of the total variance, was retained as a composite physician satisfaction score. Given its strong right‐skewed distribution, we dichotomized this score at the 80th percentile to identify those reporting high physician satisfaction.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis that included all additional candidate covariates—specifically satisfaction with consultation duration and access to care—in the multivariate model [18, 19, 20]. We also evaluated multicollinearity in the final and sensitivity models using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF), adjusted for degrees of freedom. For clarity, GVIF is a collinearity diagnostic that quantifies how much a coefficient's variance is inflated by correlations among predictors—values near 1 indicate little collinearity, whereas larger values suggest potential redundancy. We considered a GVIF of less than 5 as indicating a low possibility of multicollinearity among explanatory variables. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Cases with missing values on two or more variables were excluded. For records missing a single variable, multiple imputation was conducted using predictive mean matching. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2. This study was approved by the Kameda Medical Center Research Ethics Comittee (Approval Number: 23‐083).

To address potential multicollinearity among physician‐related satisfaction variables—specifically, the demeanor of the physician, the physician's knowledge and skills, and the clarity of the physician's explanation—we conducted a PCA. The first principal component explained 87.1% of the total variance and showed high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.92), indicating that these items represent a unidimensional construct. (Table S2) All three items demonstrated high loadings on the first component (|loadings| > 0.55). The distribution of the composite score was strongly right‐skewed. Therefore, a composite physician satisfaction score was generated based on this component. For analytical clarity and interpretability, we dichotomized this score by identifying the top 20% of respondents as having “high physician satisfaction,” while the remaining 80% were categorized as “lower satisfaction.”

Table 2 and Figure 2 indicate the results of the univariate modified Poisson regression analysis. Among the non‐physician staff variables, high satisfaction with the demeanor of the receptionist (PR 3.89; 95% CI 3.16–4.78) and the demeanor of the nursing staff (PR 6.55; 95% CI 4.70–9.13) were all strongly associated with greater overall satisfaction. Patients who reported high satisfaction with the waiting time care (PR 2.10; 95% CI 1.94–2.27) and consultation duration (PR 3.18; 95% CI 2.70–3.74) also had higher overall satisfaction with the visit. High physician‐related satisfaction, defined as scoring in the top 20% of the PCA‐derived composite score, was strongly associated with overall satisfaction (PR 6.61; 95% CI 5.13–8.52). Access to healthcare services was one of the strongest predictors (PR 5.35; 95% CI 4.41–6.49). Demographic factors such as sex and age class, as well as survey year, were not significantly associated with satisfaction.

In the multivariable modified Poisson regression model (Model 1), high satisfaction with the demeanor of the nursing staff (PR 2.06; 95% CI 1.39–3.05), waiting time care (PR 1.43; 95% CI 1.33–1.54), and high physician‐related satisfaction (PR 2.61; 95% CI 1.96–3.49) was all independently associated with greater overall satisfaction (Table 2 and Figure 2). Satisfaction with the receptionist also remained statistically significant (PR 1.30; 95% CI 1.05–1.61), whereas demographic factors (age, gender) and survey year were not significantly associated.

In the sensitivity analysis (Model 2), which additionally adjusted for consultation duration and access to care, associations remained significant for the demeanor of the nursing staff (PR 1.64; 95% CI 1.20–2.24), waiting time care (PR 1.08; 95% CI 1.02–1.14), and physician‐related satisfaction (PR 1.65; 95% CI 1.29–2.11). Furthermore, consultation duration (PR 1.28; 95% CI 1.14–1.44) and access to healthcare services (PR 2.66; 95% CI 2.12–3.34) were also significantly associated with high satisfaction. In this model, the association with the demeanor of the receptionist was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (PR 1.15; 95% CI 0.97–1.35).

Multicollinearity was assessed using Generalized Variance Inflation Factors (GVIF), and all variables had adjusted GVIF values well below the conventional threshold of 5, indicating no concerning collinearity in either model (Table S3).

Discussion

This study examined how various aspects of multidisciplinary care influence patient satisfaction in a Japanese primary care setting. Among a broad set of patient experience indicators, we identified three key factors consistently linked to overall satisfaction: the demeanor of the nursing staff, the care provided during the wait, and satisfaction related to physicians. These associations remained robust in sensitivity analyses, which also highlighted the importance of access to healthcare and consultation duration.

Although physicians have traditionally been considered central to shaping patient satisfaction, our findings underscore the independent and substantial contribution of non‐physician staff, particularly nursing staff. Patients who expressed high satisfaction with the demeanor of nursing staff were almost twice as likely to report high overall satisfaction, even after adjusting for physician‐related factors and patient demographics. This result aligns with systematic reviews highlighting relational aspects of care—such as empathy, courtesy, and attentiveness—as major determinants of patient experience, regardless of the provider's role [21]. Receptionist demeanor also emerged as a potentially relevant factor in patient satisfaction. As the first staff member encountered during a visit, the receptionist may influence patients' initial impressions. In the primary model, high satisfaction with receptionist demeanor was associated with greater overall satisfaction, although this relationship weakened and lost statistical significance in the sensitivity model. This may reflect overlapping effects with other variables, such as waiting care or access, or a potential ceiling effect in ratings.

In our clinical setting, nurses and assistants engage with patients early in the care process, helping to clarify concerns, collect preliminary information, and manage patient flow. These interactions appear to serve as pivotal touchpoints that shape perceptions of quality, trust, and safety—findings that resonate with international literature on nurse practitioners (NPs) and medical assistants (MAs) in primary care settings [22]. Although MAs' specific responsibilities and credentials differ across countries, their frequent early and interpersonal contact with patients mirrors the roles of Japanese primary care nurses. Notably, few previous studies have quantitatively isolated the contribution of these support roles using multivariable models that account for physician and system‐level factors. Our results thus provide novel evidence supporting team‐based models of care that not only emphasize the physician‐patient dyad but also recognize the value added by nursing and clerical staff.

Our findings suggest that care provided during the waiting period—such as updates, brief interactions, or reassurance—has a strong association with patient satisfaction. This aligns with previous research indicating that patients' perceptions and expectations about waiting matter more than objective duration [9, 23, 24]. For example, Zhang et al. found that when actual wait time met expectations, satisfaction was preserved—even if the wait was objectively long [10]. Similarly, positive communication during the wait was found to reduce dissatisfaction caused by delays, emphasizing the importance of interpersonal engagement while waiting [25]. These results suggest that waiting time care is not merely a logistical issue but a relational opportunity. Investing in meaningful patient contact during waiting periods—by nurses or clerical staff—may be an effective strategy to enhance patient experience without requiring structural changes to reduce wait times.

Access to care was among the strongest predictors of patient satisfaction in our study, consistent with previous findings [19, 21]. Although we did not distinguish between dimensions of access—such as timeliness, geographic proximity, or cost—our results suggest that system features like short waiting durations and the availability of same‐day appointments (via duty doctors) likely contributed to higher satisfaction. While structural factors matter, prior research indicates that interpersonal aspects—such as staff communication—may outweigh geographic or financial accessibility [26]. Together, these findings highlight the importance of timely, coordinated access as a foundation for patient‐centered care. Our findings support previous research showing that longer consultation durations improve patient satisfaction [18]. However, earlier studies suggest that it is not just the length, but the quality of communication that matters most [27]. Patients probably value feeling heard and understood, even within a limited amount of time. Notably, in this study, satisfaction was measured based on perception of consultation duration rather than the actual length. Therefore, it is unclear whether longer visits truly took place or if patients simply felt they had enough time with their doctors.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the single‐site (rural) setting limits generalizability. Third, the survey did not collect key patient and physician background factors such as reason for visit, comorbidities, ADL, frequency of visits, or physician characteristics, leaving potential for residual confounding. Fourth, the number of questionnaires distributed and the response rate were not recorded, raising the possibility of selection bias. This study is notable because it used a multi‐year design, leading to a relatively large sample size. Furthermore, because the respondents' age distribution was similar to the study area's population composition, potential sampling bias can be reduced, thereby enhancing the reliability of the results.

Conclusion

This study highlights that patient satisfaction in primary care is shaped not only by physician‐related factors but also by the quality of interactions with non‐physician staff and system‐level elements. Specifically, the demeanor of nursing staff, care during the waiting period, consultation duration, and ease of access to care were all independently associated with higher satisfaction. These findings underscore the importance of a multidisciplinary, patient‐centered approach in improving patient experience. Future studies should explore how team‐based care and workflow redesign can enhance satisfaction across diverse clinical settings.
