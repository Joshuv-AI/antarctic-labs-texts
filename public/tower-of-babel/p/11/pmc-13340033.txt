Unprecedented rearranged triterpenoids with seven-membered rings from Camellia formosensis and structurally diverse bioactive triterpenoids
Authors: Shiou-Ling Li, Yong-Han Hong, Tsong-Long Hwang, Chia-Hung Yen, Chaisak Chansriniyom, Miao-Chu Tseng, Po-Chun Chen, Sin-Hao Huang, Hsueh-Wei Chang, Tai-Hsin Tsai, Ho-Cheng Wu, Hsun-Shuo Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Camellia formosensis is the only endemic Camellia species in Taiwan with established commercial cultivation. Despite its ecological importance and cultural prominence, the phytochemical composition and pharmacological properties of this species remain largely uncharacterized. This study fills this knowledge gap through systematic phytochemical investigation and comprehensive bioactivity profiling. Systematic phytochemical investigation of C. formosensis afforded ten structurally novel triterpenoids, cameterpenes A–J (1–10), together with eight known compounds (11–18). Notably, four of these new compounds (1–4) feature an unprecedented rearranged skeleton incorporating a seven-membered ring. X-ray crystallographic analysis (Cu Kα) provided conclusive evidence for the absolute configuration of 1, 7, and 9. ECD analysis supported the assignment of the absolute configurations of 2–4. A plausible biosynthetic pathway for compounds 1–4 is proposed, involving oxidative ring-opening followed by recyclization. Comprehensive bioactivity screening revealed structure-dependent pharmacological activities. Among the oleanane-type triterpenoids (12–14), 12 showed the strongest antiproliferative activity against Ca9-22 oral cancer cells (IC50: 20.11 ± 0.95 µg mL−1), 13 demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory activity by suppressing superoxide anion generation in fMLP/CB-stimulated human neutrophils (IC50: 3.40 ± 1.19 µM), and 14 exhibited exceptional inhibition of RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation (IC50: 18.6 nM) without cytotoxicity. Lupane-type triterpenoid 16 exhibited comparable anti-osteoclastogenic activity (IC50: 27.4 nM) without cytotoxicity. These activities collectively target the interconnected processes of oxidative stress and inflammation underlying bone loss, chronic inflammatory conditions, and cancer progression. The active triterpenoids offer integrated therapeutic potential against inflammation-driven pathologies. Overall, this study establishes C. formosensis as a promising source for the development of multifunctional pharmaceuticals and nutraceuticals targeting inflammation-mediated diseases.

Introduction

Theaceae represents a botanically and economically significant plant family, with its members serving as globally important beverages, ornamentals, and sources of traditional medicines. Among these, tea stands as one of the most widely consumed beverages worldwide and occupies a central role in East Asian culture. Commercial tea production predominantly derives from Camellia sinensis, its botanical varieties, and hybrid cultivars. Extensive pharmacological investigations have documented a broad spectrum of biological activities associated with tea, including anticancer, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-obesity, cardioprotective, and neuroprotective properties.1–10 Phytochemical studies have further revealed that tea elaborates a rich array of structurally diverse secondary metabolites, encompassing catechins, flavonoids, phenolic acids, alkaloids, amino acids, and carbohydrates.11,12 The genus Camellia, comprising over 200 recognized species, constitutes the largest genus within the family Theaceae and exhibits remarkable phytochemical diversity.13 Taiwan harbors 14 native Camellia species, of which seven are endemic to the island.14Camellia formosensis, the only endemic Camellia species in Taiwan, has traditionally been consumed as a beverage tea and has recently gained commercial prominence through the development of two emerging tea cultivars, Taiwan Tea No. 18 and Taiwan Tea No. 24.15 Beyond its cultural and agricultural significance, C. formosensis represents a valuable genetic resource for tea breeding programs, offering opportunities to broaden genetic diversity and reduce dependence on limited parental lineages. C. formosensis is a small evergreen tree endemic to Taiwan, distributed across the central, southern, and southeastern regions of the island at elevations ranging from 800 to 1800 m.16 Morphologically, it is characterized by glabrous winter buds and ovaries, although these features are not consistently diagnostic for taxonomic discrimination.17 Its systematic placement has been further clarified through molecular phylogenetic analyses.18 Previous studies only reported that the hot-water extract of fresh leaves from C. formosensis exhibits antioxidant activity.19 However, aside from this finding, no detailed chemical investigation of this plant has been conducted. To address this significant gap in knowledge, the present study reports the isolation and structural elucidation of chemical constituents from the leaves of C. formosensis, together with a comprehensive evaluation of their biological activities, with the aim of establishing a phytochemical basis for the pharmacological potential of this underexplored endemic species.

Osteoporosis, meaning “porous bone”, is a skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration, resulting in increased bone fragility and fracture risk. It affects approximately 18% of the global elderly adult population and 37% of postmenopausal women.20,21 The pathogenesis of osteoporosis is closely associated with dysregulation of osteoblast and osteoclast activity.22 Osteoblasts, derived from mesenchymal stem cells, synthesize extracellular matrix proteins, thereby strengthening bone tissue and promoting new bone formation. In contrast, osteoclasts originate from hematopoietic stem cells and resorb bone by secreting acid and proteolytic enzymes to degrade mineralized bone matrix.23,24 Excessive osteoclast activation, or an imbalance in which bone resorption outpaces bone formation, contributes to the development of osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and Paget's disease.25–28 Therefore, targeting osteoclast differentiation and function represents a promising therapeutic strategy for managing these skeletal disorders. Several reports from epidemiological studies have revealed that tea consumption has osteoprotective effects and reduces the risk of osteoporotic fractures in the elderly population.29–31 Consequently, we evaluated the anti-osteoporotic properties of compounds isolated from C. formosensis to determine whether C. formosensis holds potential for osteoporosis management.

Inflammation is an innate immune response that protects the human body against infection and injury. However, chronic inflammation is associated with a wide range of diseases, including autoimmune disorders, rheumatoid arthritis, asthma, psoriasis, and neurodegenerative diseases.32 Neutrophils, as the first line of defense in the innate immune system, play a crucial role in the immune response by migrating to sites of infection or injury and releasing cytotoxic mediators such as superoxide anions and granule proteases.33,34 However, excessive or prolonged neutrophil activation can cause tissue damage, perpetuate chronic inflammation, and contribute to disease progression. Therefore, the development of therapeutic agents that can attenuate inflammatory responses represents a promising strategy for managing inflammation-related diseases. Notably, tea and its bioactive constituents have been reported to possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties potentially relevant to the treatment or prevention of inflammation-driven conditions, including osteoporosis.3–5,35,36 In light of this evidence, the anti-inflammatory activity of compounds isolated from the leaves of C. formosensis was also evaluated in the present study.

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a malignant tumor arising from the mucosal epithelium of the oral cavity and accounts for approximately 90% of all oral malignancies worldwide.37 The global incidence of OSCC is projected to rise by 65% by 2050, underscoring its growing public health significance.38 Major etiological risk factors include tobacco smoking, betel quid and areca nut chewing, alcohol consumption, and human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. Although surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy are standard treatment modalities, the overall prognosis of OSCC remains poor, with approximately 50% of newly diagnosed cases presenting at advanced TNM stage III or IV and a five-year survival rate of less than 50%.37 Therefore, the identification of new therapeutic agents for OSCC remains an important goal. Several studies have shown that natural products and their bioactive constituents can suppress OSCC cell growth through mechanisms such as apoptosis induction, cell cycle arrest, reactive oxygen species generation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and DNA damage.39–41 Accordingly, the anticancer potential of compounds isolated from C. formosensis was evaluated using Ca9-22 oral cancer cells in the present study.

Experimental

General experimental procedures

Optical rotations were measured on a Jasco P-2000 polarimeter (Jasco, Kyoto, Japan), and IR spectra (ATR) were acquired with a Jasco FT/IR-4600 spectrometer (Jasco, Kyoto, Japan). 1D (1H, 13C, DEPT) and 2D (COSY, NOESY, HSQC, HMBC) NMR spectra were recorded on a Varian Unityplus-400 spectrometer (Varian, Inc. Vacuum Technologies, Lexington, MA, USA) operated at 400 (1H) and 100 MHz (13C), a Varian Mercuryplus-400 spectrometer (Varian, Inc. Vacuum Technologies, Lexington, MA, USA) operated at 400 (1H) and 100 MHz (13C), and a Varian VNMRS-600 spectrometer (Varian, Inc. Vacuum Technologies, Lexington, MA, USA) operated at 600 (1H) and 150 MHz (13C). The UV and ECD spectra were acquired by a Jasco-V-530 UV/vis spectrometer (Jasco, Kyoto, Japan) and a Jasco J-815 CD spectrometer (Jasco, Kyoto, Japan). The low-resolution mass spectra were obtained with POLARIS Q Thermo Finnigan (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Chicago, IL, USA), Waters ZQ 4000 (Waters, Milford, MA, USA), and VG Quattro GC/MS/MS/DS (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) mass spectrometers. The EIMS were taken on a JEOL JMS-700 mass spectrometer (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). HRESIMS were recorded on a Bruker APEX II mass spectrometer (Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany) and a VARIAN 901-MS (Varian, CA, USA). Silica gel (70–230 and 230–400 mesh; Silicycle, Quebec, Canada) was used for column chromatography (CC), and silica gel 60 F254 (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and RP-18 F254s (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) were used for thin layer chromatography (TLC) and preparative TLC, respectively, visualized by spraying Ce2(SO4)3 aqueous solution. Further purification was performed by medium-performance liquid chromatography (MPLC; ceramic pump: VSP-3050; EYELA, Kyoto, Japan) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC: pump L-7110, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan; UV/VIS detector UV-975, JASCO, Tokyo, Japan; integrator LC-NetII/ADC, JASCO; column: ChiraDex®, 5 µm, 100 Å, LiChroCART® 250-4, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany).

Plant materials

The leaves of Camellia formosensis were collected in July 2021 in Liouguei District, Kaohsiung City, Taiwan, and identified by Prof. Hsun-Shuo Chang. A voucher specimen (Chen 6741) was deposited with the herbarium of the College of Pharmacy, Kaohsiung Medical University, Kaohsiung, Taiwan.

Extraction and isolation

Dried leaves (6.5 kg) of C. formosensis were extracted three times with EtOH at room temperature, and concentrated under reduced pressure to yield an EtOH extract (832 g). The EtOH extract was suspended in water and partitioned with ethyl acetate (EtOAc) to give a water layer and an EtOAc layer. The EtOAc layer (130.0 g) was subjected to column chromatography (CC) (silica gel, n-hexane–acetone 100 : 0 to 50 : 50, then washed with 100% acetone and 100% methanol) to yield sixteen fractions (Fr. 1–16). Fr. 7 was subjected to silica gel (n-hexane–EtOAc, 9 : 1) to afford 12 fractions. Fr. 7-3 was introduced to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–EtOAc, 12 : 1) to provide ten fractions. Fr. 7-3-3 was applied to prep. TLC (n-hexane–EtOAc, 20 : 1, four times) to afford 3. Then, Fr. 7-8 was subjected to MPLC (RP-18, MeOH–acetone, 1 : 6) to obtain nine fractions. Fr. 7-8-8 was applied to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 8 : 2 : 1) to yield 13 fractions. Compound 9 was obtained from Fr. 7-8-8-11 by CC (silica gel, n-hexane–EtOAc, 6 : 1). Fr. 7-8-8-11-8 was subjected to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 6 : 2 : 1) to provide five fractions. Fr. 7-8-8-11-8-3 was purified by HPLC (Chiral, H2O–MeOH, 1 : 10, flow rate 0.5 mL min−1, tR = 6 min) to get 10. Fr. 7-8-9 was subjected to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–acetone, 10 : 1) to afford 10 fractions. Fr. 7-8-9-7 was purified by HPLC (RP-18, MeOH-acetonitrile, 9 : 1, flow rate 1 mL min−1, tR = 15 min) to get 18.

Fr. 9 was subjected to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–EtOAc, 8 : 2) to obtain eight fractions. Fr. 9-5 was applied to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–EtOAc, 5 : 1) to afford seven fractions. Fr. 9-5-5 was subjected to MPLC (RP-18, H2O–acetone, 1 : 5) to yield 11 fractions. Fr. 9-5-5-5 was introduced to MPLC (RP-18, H2O–acetone, 1 : 4) to get four fractions. Compound 7 was purified from Fr. 9-5-5-5-3 by MPLC (RP-18, H2O–acetone, 1 : 4). Fr. 9-7 was subjected to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–acetone, 7 : 1) to provide ten fractions. Fr. 9-7-6 was subjected to MPLC (RP-18, H2O–acetone, 1 : 3) to afford 12 fractions. Fr. 9-7-6-8 was applied to MPLC (RP-18, H2O–MeOH, 1 : 10) to afford six fractions. Fr. 9-7-6-8-3 was subjected to MPLC (silica gel, n-hexane–EtOAc, 5 : 1) to afford seven fractions. Compound 14 was purified from Fr. 9-7-6-8-3-4 by MPLC (RP-18, H2O–MeOH, 1 : 8). Compound 1 was obtained from Fr. 9-7-6-11 by CC (silica gel, n-hexane–CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 4 : 2 : 1). Fr. 9-7-7 was subjected to MPLC (RP-18, H2O–acetone, 1 : 3) to afford eight fractions. Fr. 9-7-7-4 was applied to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 5 : 1 : 1) to provide six fractions. Fr. 9-7-7-4-2 was subjected to MPLC (RP-18, H2O–MeOH, 1 : 20) to yield four fractions. Fr. 9-7-7-4-2-2, eluted with CH2Cl2–EtOAc (20 : 1), was further fractionated by MPLC to afford 15 along with 12 fractions. Fr. 9-7-7-4-2-2-9 was subjected to MPLC (RP-18, H2O-acetonitrile, 1 : 15) to yield seven fractions. Fr. 9-7-7-4-2-2-9-3 was applied to prep. TLC (CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 10 : 1) to obtain two fractions. Compound 4 was purified from Fr. 9-7-7-4-2-2-9-3-A by MPLC (silica gel, CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 18 : 1). Compound 8 was obtained from Fr. 9-7-7-4-2-2-12 by prep. TLC (CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 10 : 1, two times). Fr. 9-7-7-5 was applied to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 5 : 1 : 1) to provide 12 fractions. Fr. 9-7-7-5-7 was subjected to MPLC (RP-18, MeOH-acetonitrile, 1 : 15) to provide 17. Fr. 9-7-7-5-7-2 was purified by prep. TLC (CH2Cl2–acetone, 15 : 1) to get 2. Fr. 9-7-7-6 was subjected to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 5 : 1 : 1) to afford 12 fractions. Fr. 9-7-7-6-12 was purified by MPLC (silica gel, n-hexane–EtOAc, 4 : 1) to get 12. Fr. 9-7-7-6-12-5 was then applied to MPLC (silica gel, n-hexane–acetone, 6 : 1) to afford 11 fractions. Fr. 9-7-7-6-12-5-9 was further separated with MPLC (silica gel, n-hexane–acetone, 7 : 1) to give 13 and five fractions. Fr. 9-7-7-6-12-5-9-6 was subjected to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–EtOAc, 4 : 1) to get six fractions. Compound 5 was purified from Fr. 9-7-7-6-12-5-9-6-4 by MPLC (silica gel, CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 20 : 1). Fr. 9-7-7-6-12-6 was applied to MPLC (RP-18, H2O–MeOH, 1 : 17) to give compound 6 along with four fractions. Fr. 9-7-7-7 was subjected to CC (silica gel, n-hexane–CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 5 : 1 : 1) to get 16.

Bioactivity assays

Inhibition of RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation

To determine the safe concentration range for subsequent osteoclast differentiation assays, a cell viability assay was performed. The RAW264.7 murine macrophage cell line (BCRC60001; Bioresource Collection and Research Center, Hsinchu, Taiwan) was used as osteoclast precursor cells. RAW264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 20 000 cells per well in 96-well plates and cultured in complete medium (10% FBS in DMEM) with or without the tested samples for the indicated durations. At the indicated time points, 10% resazurin solution was added to each well and incubated for 2.5 hours. Fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em = 540/590 nm) was measured to assess cell viability. For osteoclast differentiation, RAW264.7 cells were seeded at 750 cells per well in 48-well plates and maintained in complete culture medium. After three days, osteoclastogenesis was induced by replacing the medium with fresh medium containing 50 ng mL−1 receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL), with or without the tested samples at concentrations previously determined to be non-cytotoxic. After two additional days, the medium was replaced with fresh RANKL-containing medium (with or without test samples) and cultured for another two days. At the end of the differentiation period, the cells were collected for TRAP staining to evaluate osteoclast formation. TRAP staining was performed using a commercial kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. After medium removal, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% formalin for 20 minutes. Following additional PBS washes, 150 µL of TRAP staining solution was added to each well and incubated for 2.5 hours. Stained cells were imaged using a light microscope, and osteoclast differentiation was quantified using ImageJ software. Total TRAP-positive area per well was analyzed to assess differentiation.

Anti-inflammatory activity of fMLP/CB-induced superoxide anion and elastase release assays

The ability of tested compounds to modulate superoxide anion generation and elastase release by neutrophils was evaluated according to the methods previously reported by co-author Professor Tsong-Long Hwang.33,42 The superoxide generation assay was based on the superoxide dismutase (SOD)-inhibitable reduction of ferricytochrome c. The elastase release assay was performed by measuring the changes in elastase substrate MeO–Suc–Ala–Ala–Pro–Val-p-nitroanilide. Human neutrophils (6 × 105 cells per mL) were pre-incubated with ferricytochrome c (0.5 mg mL−1) at 37 °C, and then test compounds were added for 5 min before activation by fMLP (0.1 µM) in the presence of cytochalasin B (1 µg mL−1). The absorbance at 550 nm was monitored continuously using a spectrophotometer. Human neutrophils (6 × 105 cells per mL) were pre-incubated with elastase substrate (methoxysuccinyl-Ala–Ala–Pro–Val-p-nitroanilide, 100 µM) at 37 °C, and then test compounds were added for 5 min before activation by fMLP (0.1 µM) in the presence of cytochalasin B (0.5 µg mL−1). The absorbance at 405 nm was monitored continuously using a spectrophotometer. The results are expressed as a percentage of the rate of elastase release in the fMLP/CB-activated neutrophils treated with DMSO (solvent control, 0.1%). PI3K inhibitor LY294002 was used as a positive control for the neutrophil assays. All assays were repeated three times. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Student's t-test was used to compare the test compound with DMSO control. A probability less than 0.05 was considered significant. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Antiproliferative effects on Ca9-22 oral cancer cells

Cell cultures

The human oral cancer cell line Ca9-22 (JCRB Cell Bank, Osaka, Japan) was cultured in DMEM/F12 (3 : 2) medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and P/S antibiotics. The cells were incubated in humidified air at 37 °C with 5% CO2.43

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using the ATPlite™ luminescence assay kit (#6016941, PerkinElmer Life Sciences). The Ca9-22 cells were plated at a density of 2000 cells per well in a 96-well plate for 72 h viability assays. After 24 h of seeding, cells were treated with the drugs for 72 h and subsequently incubated with ATP assay reagent for 1 h in the dark. Cellular ATP content was measured by a luminometer (Berthold Technologies GmbH).43

Results and discussion

In the current study, we aimed to explore the metabolites from the leaves of C. formosensis and evaluate their bioactive potential in anti-osteoporotic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-oral cancer applications. Herein, we report the structural elucidation of four new rearranged seven-membered ring triterpenoids (1–4), six new olean-type triterpenoids (5–10), together with eight known compounds (11–18) from the leaves of C. formosensis (Fig. 1). To provide further insight into the formation of rearranged seven-membered ring triterpenoids, a plausible biosynthetic pathway for compounds 1–4 is also proposed. Furthermore, selected isolates were evaluated for their inhibitory effects on RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation, anti-inflammatory activity as assessed by fMLP/CB-induced superoxide anion generation and elastase release, and antiproliferative effects against Ca9-22 oral cancer cells.

Structures of compounds 1–18.

Structure elucidation

Compound 1 was a whitish amorphous powder. Its molecular formula was identified as C30H48O4 by HRESIMS (m/z 495.34433, [M + Na]+, calcd for C30H48NaO4, 495.34448) with seven degrees of unsaturation. The hydroxyl (3318 cm−1) and carbonyl (1782 cm−1) groups were observed in the IR spectrum, respectively. The 1H NMR spectrum (Table 1) presented singlet signals of eight methyl groups (δH 0.79, 0.86, 0.94, 0.96, 0.96, 1.05, 1.11, and 1.36) consistent with an oleanane-type triterpene skeleton. The 1H NMR signal of H-3 (δH 3.22), the COSY correlation between H-2 (δH 1.65) and H-3 (δH 3.22), and the HMBC correlations of H-23 (δH 0.79) and H-24 (δH 0.96) with C-3 (δC 78.5) (Fig. 2) confirmed the presence of a hydroxyl group attached to C-3. Based on the 13C NMR data, C-11 (δC 101.3) and C-13 (δC 85.0) were determined to be substituted with two and one oxygen atoms, respectively. The IR absorption band at 1782 cm−1, the 13C NMR signal of C-12 (δC 176.2), and the HMBC correlations of H-11 (δH 5.61) with C-9 (δC 59.0), C-10 (δC 36.3), C-12 (δC 176.2), and C-13 (δC 85.0) (Fig. 2) indicated that an ε-lactone ring was formed between C-11 and C-13 through a bridging oxygen atom. Meanwhile, six of the seven degrees of unsaturation were accounted for by the identified functional groups and rings, with the remaining one attributed to a pyran ring formed between C-11 and C-13 via an oxygen bridge. The relative configuration of OH-3 was assigned as equatorial orientation based on the large coupling constant of H-3 (J = 11.2 Hz) and the NOESY correlation between H-3/H-5 (δH 0.70) (Fig. 3). Additionally, the equatorial orientation of H-11 was assigned based on the small coupling constant of H-11 (J = 3.2 Hz) and the NOESY correlations between H-11/H-25 (δH 1.05), H-26 (δH 1.36) (Fig. 3). The absolute configuration of 1 was unambiguously assigned as shown in Fig. 4 based on single-crystal X-ray diffraction data [Cu Kα radiation, absolute structure parameter = −0.1(2)]. Therefore, compound 1 was established as (3S,5R,9R,11S,12S,18R,25S,26R,27S,28R) and designated as cameterpene A.

Compound 2 was isolated as a whitish amorphous powder and showed a molecular formula of C30H46O5 by HRESIMS (m/z 509.32355 [M + Na]+, calcd for C30H46NaO5, 509.32375). The physical and NMR spectroscopic data of 2 suggested a similar structure to 1, except that CH3-23 in 1 was replaced by an aldehyde group in 2. The HMBC correlations of H-24 (δH 9.81)/C-3 (δC 77.0), C-4 (δC 52.7), C-5 (δC 56.2), and H-23/C-24 (δC 207.1) confirmed the presence of an aldehyde group at C-23 (Fig. 2). The NOESY correlations between H-24/H-25 (δH 0.98) indicated the relative configuration of H-24 as an equatorial orientation (Fig. 3). Given their shared chemical skeleton, NOESY correlations, and biogenetic relationship, 2 was assigned the same configuration as 1. Based on the spectroscopic data, compound 2 was elucidated as a new triterpenoid and named cameterpene B.

Compound 3 was obtained as a whitish amorphous powder and showed a molecular formula of C32H50O5 by HRESIMS (m/z 537.35480 [M + Na]+, calcd for C32H50NaO5, 537.35505). Comparison of the IR and NMR spectra of 3 with those of 1 revealed an additional IR absorption band at 1728 cm−1 and NMR signals at δC 21.2 and δC 170.7, indicating the presence of an acetoxy group in 3. The acetoxy group was located at C-3 based on the HMBC correlations (Fig. 2). The large coupling constant of H-3 (J = 11.4 Hz) and the NOESY correlations between H-3/H-5 (Fig. 3) identified the relative configuration of H-3 as axial, indicating an equatorial orientation of OAc-3. The stereochemistry of 3 was determined to be identical to that of 1, based on their biogenetic relationship and comparative analysis of NOESY correlations. Accordingly, compound 3 was characterized as a new rearranged triterpenoid and trivially named cameterpene C.

The 1H and 13C NMR data of 4 closely resembled those of 3 (Table 1), with two notable exceptions: the absence of a methyl group and the presence of an oxymethylene group at C-28. The assignment of the oxymethylene group at C-28 was supported by key HMBC correlations observed between H-28 (δH 3.30, δ 3.76)/C-16 (δC 32.5), C-18 (δC 38.9), C-22 (δC 29.3) (Fig. 2). The proposed structure was further corroborated by HRESIMS analysis, which afforded a molecular formula of C32H50O6, as well as IR absorptions at 3443 cm−1 (hydroxyl group) and 1785 and 1734 cm−1 (carbonyl groups). The stereochemistry of 4 was determined to be identical to that of 3, based on their biogenetic relationship and comparative analysis of NOESY correlations. Therefore, compound 4 was elucidated as cameterpene D.

After the planar structures and relative configurations of compounds 2–4 had been established, their absolute configurations were assessed by electronic circular dichroism (ECD) analysis. In the ECD spectra, compounds 2–4 displayed negative Cotton effects at 208 and 236 nm, showing a pattern closely matching that of compound 1 (Fig. S31). Accordingly, compounds 2–4 share the same absolute configuration as 1, which was explicitly determined as 3S,5R,9R,11S,12S,18R,25S,26R,27S,28R. Compound 5 was purified as a whitish amorphous powder. Its molecular formula was identified as C32H52O5 by HRESIMS (m/z 539.37039 [M + Na]+, calcd for C32H52NaO5, 539.37070), indicating seven degrees of unsaturation. The hydroxyl (3402 cm−1) and carbonyl (1723 cm−1) groups were observed in the IR spectrum. The 1H NMR data showed signals of eight tertiary methyl groups at δH 0.80, 0.87, 0.91, 0.97, 1.00, 1.00, 1.20, and 1.42 (Table 2), indicative of an oleanane-type triterpene skeleton. The COSY correlation between H-2/H-3 and the HMBC correlation of H-23 (δH 0.80) and H-24 (δH 1.00) to C-3 (δC 78.1) confirmed the presence of a hydroxyl group at C-3 (Fig. 2). The HMBC showed correlations between H-18 (δH 1.50) and H-27 (δH 0.97) to C-13 (δC 82.9), which supported the presence of an additional hydroxyl group at C-13. The seven degrees of unsaturation and δC 202.8 indicated a carbonyl group at C-12. Additionally, the large coupling constants of H-3 (δH 3.19, J = 11.4 Hz) and H-11 (δH 6.13, J = 12.3 Hz) and NOESY correlations of H-3/H-5 (δH 0.73) and H-11 (δH 6.13)/H-25 (δH 1.00) and H-26 (δH 1.42) suggested an equatorial orientation of OH-3 and OAc-11 (Fig. 3).

The H-3 of 5 was assigned the axial orientation, which is consistent with the previously reported 3β,11α-diacetoxy-13β-hydroxyolean-12-one that shares a common biosynthetic origin with 544 On the basis of the above spectroscopic data, compound 5 was established as cameterpene E.

The 1H and 13C NMR data (Table 2) of 6 were nearly identical to those of the known compound 13, 13,26-epoxy-3β,11α-dihydroxyolean-12-one,45 the only difference being the presence of a carbonyl group at C-11 (δC 184.8) in 6 in place of the hydroxyl group (OH-11) observed in 13. The IR spectrum of 6 displayed two carbonyl absorption bands (1729 and 1668 cm−1), further supporting the presence of one additional carbonyl group in 6 compared to 13. The configurations of 6 were proposed, as in the case of 13, according to the NMR spectra (Fig. 3). Taken together, the spectroscopic data allowed the structure of 6 to be fully determined, and it was trivially named cameterpene F.

Compound 7 was a colorless needle. Its molecular formula was identified as C32H52O5 by HRESIMS (m/z 539.37042 [M + Na]+, calcd for C30H52NaO5, 539.37070). The IR absorptions at 3420, 1737, and 1712 cm−1 indicated the presence of hydroxyl and carbonyl groups, respectively. The 1H and 13C data for 7 (Table 2) were similar to those of 5, except for the substituents on C-3, C-11, and C-12. The HMBC correlation between H-3 (δH 4.48)/C-2 (δC 23.4), C-4 (δC 37.8), C-23 (δC 27.9), and H-24 (δH 0.88)/C-3 (δC 80.9) indicated an acetoxy group at C-3. The HMBC correlations from H-12 (δH 3.51) to C-11 (δC 210.9), C-13 (δC 81.6), C-14 (δC 44.6), and H-18 (δH 1.15), H-27 (δH 1.40) to C-13 suggested the two hydroxyl groups attached to C-12 and C-13. Additionally, a carbonyl group located at C-11 was confirmed by HMBC correlations (Fig. 2) between H-9 (δH 3.15) and H-12/C-11. Compound 8 was obtained as a whitish amorphous powder, whose 1H and 13C NMR data (Table 2) closely resembled those of the known compound 3α-acetoxy-28-hydroxy-12-oleanene-11-one,46 suggesting that both compounds share the same carbon skeleton. The coupling constant of H-3 (J = 11.7 Hz) revealed that H-3 occupied an axial position, placing the OAc-3 group in an equatorial orientation, which was further corroborated by the NOESY correlation between H-3 (δH 4.51)/H-5 (δH 0.80) (Fig. 3). The molecular formula was determined to be C32H50O4 by HRESIMS, which exhibited a sodiated adduct at m/z 521.35980 [M + Na]+ (calcd for C32H50NaO4, 521.36013), corresponding to six degrees of unsaturation, and the IR spectrum further supported the structure by disclosing a hydroxyl absorption at 3464 cm−1 and carbonyl stretching bands at 1738 and 1708 cm−1. These collective spectroscopic analyses allowed the complete structural determination of 8, which was therefore named cameterpene H.

Plausible biosynthetic pathways of 1–4

The plausible biosynthetic pathways of compounds 1–4 are proposed in Scheme 1. Biogenetically, the known compound 12, 3β,11α,13β-trihydroxyolean-12-one, is considered the precursor of compounds 1–4. The pathway is initiated by oxidation at C-11 to generate an 11,12-diketone intermediate (A). Baeyer–Villiger oxidation subsequently transforms the ketone at C-11 into an ester (B), which is then reduced to a hydroxyl group, yielding intermediate C. Further dehydration at C-11 converts C to D, eliminating H2O to generate a double bond between C-9 and C-11. Protonation of the double bond in D generates a secondary carbocation in E, which accepts a lone pair of electrons from the C-13 hydroxyl group. This electron transfer facilitates ether bond formation between C-11 and C-13, leading to the production of 1. Acetylation of OH-3 in 1 affords 3, which undergoes further oxidation at C-28 to introduce a hydroxyl group, yielding 4. In addition, oxidation at C-24 in 1 produces intermediate F, which is subsequently transformed to 2 through further oxidation at C-24.

Anti-inflammatory activity of fMLP/CB-induced superoxide anion generation and elastase release

The anti-inflammatory activity was evaluated by measuring fMLP/CB-induced superoxide anion generation and elastase release. Conclusion

In this study, the phytochemical investigation of the leaves from C. formosensis led to 10 new triterpenoids, cameterpenes A–J (1–10), together with eight known compounds (11–18). Most notably, cameterpenes A–D (1–4) represent a unique class of rearranged oleanane-type triterpenoids featuring an atypical seven-membered C-ring with an ε-lactone and an 11,13-oxygen bridge. Distinct from the Δ14 double bond of serratene-type and the C-27 methyl migration of dubosane-type triterpenoids, compounds 1–4 constitute the first instance of C-ring expanded triterpenoids in the Camellia genus.55–60 Furthermore, the isolation of a cyclo-anhydride precursor along with these compounds supports a plausible biosynthetic pathway involving Baeyer–Villiger (BV) oxidation. Unlike the common BV oxidations observed in rings A, B, or D, our findings suggest a rare oxidative expansion occurring specifically at ring C.61–65 Taken together, these results expand the chemical diversity of C. formosensis and provide valuable markers for chemotaxonomic studies of the Camellia genus.

In the bioactivity evaluation, 13 showed the strongest anti-inflammatory activity by suppressing superoxide anion generation (IC50: 3.40 ± 1.19 µM), while other compounds showed weak effects. In contrast, 14 and 16 exhibited potent dose-dependent inhibition of RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation (IC50: 18.6 nM and 27.4 nM, respectively) without cytotoxicity. Although 12 and 15 showed some inhibition of RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation, their effects were weaker than those of 14 and 16. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of anti-osteoclastogenic activity for these triterpenoids. Based on the bioactivity profiles, the structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis was conducted. The SAR discussion suggested that the lupane-type triterpenoids were generally more active than the oleanane-type analogues in the osteoclast differentiation assay, whereas the rearranged cameterpene scaffold showed weak activity. Within the lupane series, comparison of 15 and 16 suggested that the hydroxyl group at C-20 may be more favourable for anti-osteoclastogenic activity than the hydroxyl group at C-16. Conversely, within the oleanane-type triterpenoids, the higher activity of 14 compared to 12 suggests that the Δ12 double bond and the hydroxymethyl group at C-28 are important for anti-osteoclastogenic potential. In the anti-inflammatory activity assay, only compound 13 exhibited significant inhibition of superoxide anion generation (IC50: 3.40 ± 1.19 µM), suggesting that the 13,26-epoxy moiety may be associated with this activity. In the oral cancer assay, 12 demonstrated the strongest antiproliferative effect against Ca9-22 cells (IC50: 20.11 ± 0.95 µg mL−1). Overall, the isolated triterpenoids displayed distinct and selective bioactivities across the tested models.

In summary, this study comprehensively demonstrates the anti-osteoclastogenic, anti-inflammatory, and antiproliferative activities of C. formosensis extracts. Notably, four triterpenoids were identified as exhibiting previously unreported anti-osteoclastogenic activity. Although bone loss-preventive effects have been reported for other Camellia species, such as C. sinensis and C. sinensis var. assamica, the active triterpenoids identified in C. formosensis are structurally distinct from those previously reported in the literature.66–70 These findings provide additional evidence that mature leaves of C. formosensis are a valuable source of bioactive triterpenoids and warrant further pharmacological investigation in inflammation-related disease models.
