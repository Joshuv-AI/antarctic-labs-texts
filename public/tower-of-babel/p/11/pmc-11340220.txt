A modified open surgical revision technique for malfunctioning peritoneal dialysis catheter
Authors: Shilong Xiang, Xiaohui Zhang, Yaomin Wang, Guangjun Liu, Xishao Xie, Fei Han, Jianghua Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgrounds: The malfunction of peritoneal dialysis (PD) catheter is still an intractable problem. A modified open surgical revision technique with suturing fixation and without catheter removal for malfunctioning catheter was developed to evaluated the efficacy and safety between simultaneous catheter replacement technique.Methods: A total of 167 PD patients with malfunctioning catheter were retrospectively reviewed. For the salvage of PD catheters, patients underwent modified open surgical revision (group A) or simultaneous catheter replacement (group B). The baseline characteristics before operation, perioperative condition, complications and outcomes were compared between both groups.Results: Patients of group A showed significantly shorter operative time (67.4 ± 22.1 versus 82.8 ± 21.1 min, p = 0.009), less postoperative pain score within 24 h (median 0.0 versus 2.0, p < 0.001), quicker start of PD (1.06 ± 0.31 versus 1.89 ± 0.89 days, p < 0.001), shorter length of stay (9.89 ± 5.11 versus 12.55 ± 7.37 days, p = 0.020) than group B. In terms of complications, the incidence of recurred catheter malfunction in group A was significantly lower than those in group B (1/114 versus 12/53, p < 0.001). There were no significant differences in mechanical complications (bloody effluent, dialysate leakage, and hernia) and early peritonitis between the groups. The group A patients had a favorable catheter survival rate compared with group B (log-rank, p = 0.004).Conclusions: Our modified open surgical revision technique is a safe, simple and fast method, and offers a better outcome with minimal risk of recurrence of catheter malfunction without additional cost and equipment. This technique is worthy of clinical application.

Introduction

Peritoneal dialysis (PD), a safe and effective treatment option for patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), has become more common as a renal replacement therapy. The success of PD largely depends on the presence of a functional and durable catheter, which is regarded as the lifeline of PD patients. Catheter malfunction, evident as mechanical failure in dialysate inflow or outflow, remains the second most common reason for PD failure, accounting for 12.7–35% [1–4]. The most common causes of catheter malfunction are catheter tip migration, omental wrapping, or catheter occlusion [5]. Several techniques have been developed to avoid these complications: suturing fixation [6] of the catheter to prevent catheter migration, and omentopexy [7] or omentectomy [8] to prevent omental wrapping. For patients with inadequate drainage or perfusion, a number of conservative methods have been recommended firstly by guidelines [9], including motion, laxatives, intra-catheter urokinase, forced catheter flushing, and guidewire manipulation. However, the conservative management is of a high failure rate and ineffective in the long run [10], and surgical intervention is often inevitable. Historically, simultaneous replacement of the catheter was the standard surgical treatment of choice [9, 11]. This method not only brought more trauma due to creating a new incision and tunnel, but also was subject to all of the potential complications of a new catheter, such as dialysate leakage, infection, and recurrence of catheter malfunction [9]. More recently, the advances in laparoscopic surgery technique have made it more feasible to rescue malfunctioning catheters under direct vision [12]. The laparoscopic technique is minimally invasive, is able to resolve the wrapped omentum and intraluminal occlusion, and can perform suturing fixation to prevent the recurrence of migration [10, 13–16]. Nevertheless, the procedures seem to be relatively complicated under general anesthesia with higher costs, and require expensive equipment and specially trained personnel. Thus, the laparoscopic technique is tough to be popularized and not the first choice in developing countries [17].

The success rate of revision technique varies greatly between procedures as well as operators [16, 18–22]. Nowadays, catheter placement by nephrologist has been widely encouraged to enhance the utilization of PD [23, 24]. The PD catheters are inserted by nephrologists in a large proportion of PD centers in China. Therefore, the malfunction of PD catheter is still a relatively intractable problem for these centers. In our center, a modified open surgical revision technique for malfunctioning PD catheter with a catheter fixation procedure and without catheter removal has been developed based on the perspective of nephrologists. This study aimed to describe the technique and to assess its efficacy, safety, and complications compared with simultaneous catheter replacement technique and to share our experience of the modified open surgical revision technique.

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively investigated the charts of total 167 patients referred to our hospital with malfunctioning PD catheter in whom nonsurgical treatment modalities (such as motion, laxatives, intra-catheter urokinase, forced catheter flushing and guidewire intervention) had failed between June 2008 and September 2022. All patients were counted once and only the first episode of catheter malfunction was reviewed. Patients with confirmed catheter infections at malfunction were excluded from the study. Catheter malfunction was manifested as poor outflow of dialysate (more than 30 min/2L exchange), obstruction to inflow and outflow, leading to insufficient PD [9, 10]. After documentation of poor inflow or outflow, all cases were evaluated using a plain abdominal X-ray to check the position of the catheter tip. Catheter malposition was defined as migration of the catheter tip out of the true pelvis, as detected by abdominal radiography. All patients after operation were followed up till September 2023 or until catheter removal or death. Peri-operative and follow-up data were collected and analyzed. Before the catheter revision surgery, patients were fully informed of the characteristics of modified open surgical catheter revision and simultaneous catheter replacement technique. According to their voluntary choice of surgical method, all patients were divided into two groups: 114 cases in the modified open surgical catheter revision group (group A), and 53 cases in the simultaneous catheter replacement group (group B). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University.

Surgical technique

Prophylactic intravenous antibiotic, usually cefazolin, was used in half to one hour before operation. All the surgeries were operated in the laminar-flow operating-rooms with the laminar-flow air disinfection.

Modified open surgical catheter revision method

Patients were maintained in a supine position with routine sterilization after removing the Titanium adapter and transfer set. The original PD catheter was located in the abdomen. Under local anesthesia or conduction anesthesia (rectus sheath nerve block), a 3–4 cm vertical paramedian incision in length 1 cm below the original wound was made. After dissection from the skin to the anterior rectus sheath layer by layer, a vertical incision approximately 3 cm long was made through the posterior sheath and peritoneum to enter the peritoneal cavity. The key incision required clutching with clamps to stabilize the peritoneum layer. Along the original catheter insertion site, the intra-peritoneal portion of the catheter was pulled out by hand for inspection. Meanwhile, blunt dissection of the omental adhesion or clearing of the fibrin or blood clots were operated if necessary. The treated catheter was introduced into the pouch of Douglas using large ring forcep, then loop ligature and fixation with 4–0 prolene suture was made to the peritoneum and the medial border of the posterior rectus sheath at the lower edge of incision. Catheter patency was tested by flushing, after which the peritoneum was sealed with 3–0 nylon and all wounds were closed in layers. Simultaneous catheter replacement method

The simultaneous catheter replacement-removal procedures underwent the removal of original malfunctioning catheter and replacement of the new catheter afterwards by conventional surgical means. Briefly, under local anesthesia or conduction anesthesia (rectus sheath nerve block) in supine position, incision was made along the original wound, and the deep cuff was dissected free from the surrounding rectus muscle. After separation of the superficial cuff from the surrounding tissue through the exit, the PD catheter was removed. To insert the new catheter, a 2–3 cm vertical paramedian incision on the opposite side was made from the skin to anterior rectus sheath. A tiny incision was then made through the posterior sheath and peritoneum to enter the peritoneal cavity. The new Tenckhoff catheter, the same type as the removed catheter, straightened over malleable metal stylet, was advanced toward the pelvis blindly. After satisfactory test of flow function, the stylet was pulled and the purse-string suture was tied. A new exit site was created by a standard technique. Eventually, all incisions were sealed in layers.

Protocol for commencing dialysis

Early flush was performed postoperatively on the same day after the salvage surgery in both groups. If the flushing drainage was not with obvious hemorrhage or leakage, routine dialysis was then initiated on the following day with a low fill volume of 1000 mL. The standard dose of 2000 mL was applied instead after one week gradually.

Data collection

Variables that were retrospectively collected included: (1) Baseline characteristics, such as gender, age, body mass index (BMI) and primary disease, history of hemodialysis, renal transplantation and abdominal surgery;

(2) Previous PD catheter relevant details, such as original PD catheter type, previous catheter-related infections and hernia, previous catheter insertion method and PD modality at malfunction, interval between original catheter insertion and malfunction, previous malfunction causes leading to revision or replacement and catheter tip position;

(3) Perioperative parameters, such as operative type, operative time, anesthesia methods, postoperative pain within 24 h evaluated by the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), hospitalization expenses calculated in US $, length of stay, onset time of PD;

(4) Complications, such as recurrence of catheter malfunction including catheter migration, catheter occlusion and omentum wrapping, dialysate leakage, bloody effluent, organ injury, hernia, hydrothorax, early tunnel infection and early peritonitis; The early catheter-related infections, defined as infections occurred within 1 month after catheter salvage surgery, were more associated with operation procedures [25];

(5) Clinical outcomes, such as successful catheter function, catheter survival, causes of catheter removal, and patient mortality. All patients were followed up till September 2023 or until catheter removal or death. Successful catheter functioning was defined as the good functioning of the catheter after surgery, with good patency of inflow and outflow of dialysate, without the need for a new maneuver, salvage or removal of the same. Catheter survival was defined as all mechanical functional catheters without the need for removal due to catheter malfunction, dialysate leak, hernia, hydrothorax, catheter-related infections, inadequate solute clearance or ultrafiltration failure. Patients were censored for renal transplantation, recovery of renal function, and for patient voluntary change to hemodialysis and death with functioning catheter, whose catheters were removed due to catheter-unrelated reasons.

Statistical analysis

Summaries of continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and as medians with interquartile ranges for skewed distribution data; categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages). Continuous variables were compared with the use of Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U-test (for skewed data), and categorical data with the use of Pearson’s chi-square test. Multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression models were performed, and factors that showed a significant association (p < 0.10) after univariate analysis (age, BMI) or was of considerable theoretical relevance (sex) were entered into the multivariate Cox regression analysis (Supplementary Table S1). And multivariate linear regression analysis was conducted to adjust the effect of multiple variables of age, sex, BMI and to identify significant determinants of response perioperative parameters and surgery type (Supplementary Table S2). The cumulative PD catheter survival estimates were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival analysis was compared using a log-rank test. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

In total, 167 patients with catheter malfunction in whom had catheter salvage surgery were enrolled into this study during this period. The salvage surgery were operated on 114 patients by modified open surgical revision method (group A) and in 53 patients by simultaneous catheter replacement method (group B). Patient baseline characteristics of the two groups were summarized in Table 1. Compared to patients of the group B, patients of group A were significantly younger (49.21 ± 15.86 years in group A versus 56.75 ± 16.66 years in group B, p = 0.005) and fatter (21.75 ± 3.41 versus 20.64 ± 2.41, p = 0.034); had a significantly lower proportion of hypertensive nephropathy leading ESKD (0.9% versus 9.4%, p = 0.008). No significant differences were found in gender, distribution of primary diseases (such as glomerulonephritis, diabetic nephropathy, other etiologies), history of abdominal surgery, hemodialysis, renal transplantation.

Complications

The summary of postoperative complications showed in Table 4. The PD treatment was successful in 113 patients (99.1%) in group A and in 41 patients (77.4%) in group B (p < 0.001) during follow-up. At 1 month after PD start, the catheter was functioning in 49 patients in group B, significantly lesser than in group A (p = 0.037). Catheter malfunction was the most important mechanical complication that recurred in 12 (22.6%) patients in group B, while it was obviously less common in group A (p < 0.001). Further analysis of the causes of catheter malfunction, there was no recurrence of catheter migration (p < 0.001) and omentum wrapping (p = 0.031), which were remarkable lower in group A. And there was only 1 case of group A that developed catheter occlusion following peritonitis occurred at 61 days after modified revision and switched to hemodialysis at last. The rate of hydrothorax after salvage of group A was significantly lower than group B (p = 0.031). There were no other significant differences in mechanical complications (bloody effluent, dialysate leakage, and hernia) and early peritonitis when comparing the two groups. There was no occurrence of organ injury and early tunnel infection in both groups.

Clinical outcomes

The follow-up period was 36.37 (14.98–59.95) months and 41.57 (17.80–82.25) months for group A and group B respectively, with no statistical difference (p = 0.410). Most of patients in both groups were treated with CAPD. The details of outcomes were shown in Table 5. There was no surgical mortality that occurred in either group. The catheters were significantly less removed due to the overall catheter removal reasons (p < 0.001), including the catheter-unrelated removal (p = 0.020) and catheter-related reasons (p < 0.001) in group A compared with group B. According to the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, after adjusting for the factors of age, sex, BMI, the good prognosis of group A still existed (Supplementary Table S1). Excluding deaths unrelated to catheter, patient voluntary PD withdrawal, renal recovery and renal transplantation (accounting for 29 patients in group A and 23 patients in group B), the patients in group A had a favorable true catheter survival rate compared with group B, demonstrated by the log-rank test (p = 0.004) (Figure 2).

Discussion

Catheter malfunction is still a common problem in PD patients and can occur at anytime during PD treatment. In our study, catheter malfunction came up as early as on the day after catheter insertion, and as late as more than 6 years later. Similar to other reports [1–4], the rate of catheter malfunction in our center was about 9–10%, and the reasons were mainly due to catheter tip migration, omental wrapping, or catheter occlusion. A series of conservative treatments often failed to restore catheter drainage in a large part of patients [10], and salvage surgery was necessary.

Our study showed that the simultaneous catheter replacement method took more time, with more peritoneal trauma and more pain, longer delay in start of PD. The biggest weakness of this method was that the recurrence rate of catheter malposition was especially high after operation. Laparoscopic rescue approach, with omentectomy, omentopexy, or fixation, except in the scenario of extensive peritoneal adhesions or technical error of the previous insertion surgery, has been reported to be efficient the treatment of omental wrapping and prevention of recurrence [10, 13–16]. However, there were several barriers to wide uptake of these techniques, especially in developing countries. These procedures involved complicated laparoscopic suturing techniques and multiple working ports increasing the risk of dialysate leakage. Operating for laparoscopic rescue procedure was often not readily available, as it required expensive equipment and specially trained personnel with higher costs, and unsuitable for high-risk uremic patients with intolerance to general anesthesia. It was the main reason that the laparoscopic rescue approach was not the first choice in our center. Moreover, recurrent catheter malfunction after revision was still possible. Hence, we created a modified open surgical revision technique for malfunctioning PD catheter without catheter removal based on the perspective of nephrologists.

The ideal salvage surgery should be safe and easy to perform, with a high success rate, ability to prevent recurrence, and short recovery time. In the laparoscopic salvage procedures, fixing the catheter with nonabsorbable sutures to the abdominal wall have been reported to prevent catheter migration again effectively [15, 21]. The suturing fixation procedure was also applied in our study. Our results indicated that the modified open surgical revision method with a catheter fixation procedure could be safely and efficiently performed by nephrologists with appropriate training and experience for the management of PD catheter malfunction. The 1-month catheter survival and technique survival rates of our patients were 100 and 99.0%, respectively, which compared favorably to previous series of catheter manipulation [19, 26–28], laparoscopic salvage [10, 15, 16, 20, 21], and open surgical revision [22, 29]. Kim et al. [22] and Li et al. [29] introduced minilaparotomy techniques to correct malfunctioning catheters. However, unlike the present reports, our study compared the modified salvage method with the simultaneous replacement method, and included much more sample size. The most distinguishing features between our modified technique with the others were the exact incision site and additional catheter fixation site. As the original incision of conventional open surgical insertion method was about 9–10 cm vertically above the upper border of pubic symphysis, in order to reach the catheter in abdomen by finger insertion, lower incision point generally makes a wider incision line (3 cm), and the catheter fixation site was about 6–7 cm above the edge. One explanation of the fixation site was that the anatomical distribution of the greater omentum in the peritoneal cavity is less at that position [30, 31]. Our results showed that the catheter fixation at the ventral abdominal wall was useful in lowering the rate of catheter tip migration of 0% and omental wrapping rate 0%. Only 1 case developed catheter occlusion following peritonitis.

Interestingly, there were 4 cases with catheter malfunction that were found to be caused by technical errors of the previous insertion surgery during the salvage procedure, in which 2 cases were due to the extraperitoneal placement of the initial catheter, and in which another 2 cases were due to kinking of the catheter. These patients were all successfully underwent the salvage procedures without creating another incision. This was another unexpected advantage of our procedure over laparoscopic surgery.

Dialysate leakage was also an early complication after salvage, leading to the interruption of routine PD for some time [32]. In our experience, as long as the incisions of posterior rectus sheath and peritoneum were sutured exactly, early restart routine dialysis gradually from a low dialysate volume might not increase the risk of leakage. Our data showed that the onset time of PD in both the minilaparotomy and replacement groups were followed by about 1–3 days after operation, the incidence of leakage was very low and there was no significant difference between two groups.

It was necessary to provide evidence of the safety of a novel technology. The present results indicated that our modified salvage technique was a safe procedure. There were no operative complications such as organ injury or uncontrollable bleeding associated with the procedure. One reason might be that our technique would also, by allowing direct vision of the abdominal cavity, lessen the risk of visceral injury compared to the simultaneous catheter replacement technique. There was no procedure-related mortality that occurred in the study. Our data did not observe increased risk of hernia, and early catheter-related infections, including tunnel infection and peritonitis.

Our study had several limitations. The primary limitation of this study was its retrospective design which might lead to the loss of data and limited sample size. And the time line changed over time which could alter data and outcome. This study might not be completely free of bias. This technique still need to be validated by further randomized controlled trials. Moreover, although all the salvage procedures were performed in a standardized fashion by the surgeons with sufficient training, the surgeons were not the same during the study period, which might have impacted on the outcomes.

Notably, the improved results of this study are not owing to a single new method, but to a series of modifications on the standing shoulder effect. In conclusion, our modified open surgical revision technique is safe, simple, fast, familiar and of low learning curve to nephrologists. Meanwhile, the presented technique offers a better outcome with minimal risk of recurrence of catheter malfunction without additional cost and equipment. Our findings will provide an insight for clinicians in selecting PD catheter salvage method for patients with catheter malfunction.
