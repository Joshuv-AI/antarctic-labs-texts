Salusin‐β, Arterial Stiffness, and Heart Rate Variability Influence the Blood Pressure Response to High‐Intensity Interval Training Among Older Adults With Hypertension: A Randomized Control Trial
Authors: Alireza Ghardashi‐Afousi, Linda S. Pescatello, Maryam Asvadi Fard, Aida Sabouri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSalusin‐α and ‐β are two isoforms with opposing effects on the etiology of hypertension. We examined the effects of salusin‐α and ‐β, carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV), and heart rate variability (HRV) on the blood pressure (BP) response to high‐intensity interval training (HIIT).MethodsEighty older adults with hypertension (n = 80, 59.8 ± 6.1 yr, 27.2 ± 1.4 kg/m2, 158.0 ± 11.9/98.7 ± 6.1 mmHg) were randomized to HIIT or control (CTR). HIIT was performed for 12, 1.5 min intervals at 85%–90% heart rate reserve (HRR) separated by 2 min at 50%–55% HRR for 12 weeks.ResultsSystolic (SBP 17.7 ± 4.4 mmHg) and DBP (6.9 ± 2.3 mmHg) decreased after HIIT versus CTR (ps = 0.001). Salusin‐α increased 144.9 ± 34.3 pg/mL after HIIT versus CTR (ps = 0.001); while salusin‐β (308.7 ± 98.7 pg/mL) and the salusin‐β/α ratio (0.89 ± 0.31) decreased after HIIT versus CTR (ps = 0.001). cfPWV decreased 336.7 ± 81.3 cm/s after HIIT versus CTR (p = 0.001). High‐frequency power (HF 7.1 ± 2.1 nu) and SDNN (6.8 ± 1.4 ms) increased after HIIT versus CTR (ps = 0.001). Low‐frequency power (LF 14.2 ± 3.2 nu) and LF/HF ratio decreased 1.1 ± 0.4 after HIIT versus CTR (ps = 0.001). After HIIT, salusin‐β (β = 0.468), LF (β = 0.189), HF (β = −0.176), and VO2peak (β = −0.150) accounted for 82% of the variance in the SBP response (p < 0.0001), whereas the salusin‐β (β = 0.549), cfPWV (β = 0.346), VO2peak (β = −0.186), and HF (β = −0.179), accounted for 72% of the variance in the DBP response (p < 0.0001).ConclusionSalusin‐β, cfPWV, HRV and VO2peak appear to be implicated in the BP benefits of HIIT among older adults with hypertension.

Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) is a major contributor to cardiovascular disease (CVD), which affects nearly a billion people worldwide and ranks as the foremost cause of mortality and disability [1]. The development of HTN is multifaceted, requiring coordination among several regulatory systems, particularly the cardiovascular and central nervous systems (CNS). The hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN) within the CNS plays a pivotal role in regulating blood pressure (BP) and sympathetic nervous activity [2].

Recently discovered, salusins are bioactive peptides—28 amino acids in salusin‐α and 20 in salusin‐β—produced in multiple tissues such as the heart, endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and the CNS [3, 4]. Administering salusin‐β into regions like the PVN or rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM) has been shown to enhance sympathetic drive, elevate mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR), and stimulate arginine vasopressin (AVP) secretion [5, 6]. Silencing salusin‐β in the PVN attenuated sympathetic excitation, inflammation, NADPH‐oxidase‐derived reactive oxygen species (ROS), MAP, and cardiovascular remodeling in spontaneously hypertensive rats [7, 8, 9]. Arterial stiffness is an independent predictor of CVD events and all‐cause mortality [10] and is associated with higher levels of salusin‐β and lower levels of salusin‐α [11, 12, 13]. Pulse wave velocity (PWV) is a measure of arterial stiffness that is increased in adults with HTN [14, 15]. Heart rate variability (HRV) reflects autonomic control of the heart, indicating sympathetic–parasympathetic balance [16] which often skews toward heightened sympathetic tone in hypertensive populations [17].

Exercise training is a universally recommended nonpharmacological therapy for HTN that improves arterial stiffness, PWV, HRV, and cardiorespiratory fitness [18, 19]. High‐intensity interval training (HIIT) has gained attention as an efficient approach to managing HTN, offering a time‐saving substitute for conventional aerobic, resistance, or combined exercise methods [20]. HIIT improves cardiorespiratory fitness, systolic and diastolic BP, carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV), HRV, and norepinephrine (NE) in adults with HTN [18, 19, 21]. Nonetheless, the role of salusin‐α and ‐β on BP in response to HIIT, particularly in adults with HTN, have yet to be adequately identified.

The purpose of our randomized control trial (RCT) was to investigate the influence of salusin‐β, cfPWV, and HRV on the BP response to HIIT among adults with HTN. We hypothesized the BP response to HIIT would be associated with decreased plasma levels of salusin‐β and cfPWV, and increased VO2peak and HRV in patients with HTN.

Methods

Participants

A total of 180 participants were recruited from the Department of Hypertension in Imam Khomeini Hospital and a community recreation center in Tehran, where participants underwent an evaluation and were enrolled if they met the eligibility criteria for this RCT. Of these, 80 participants qualified (47 male and 33 female). Eligibility criteria included individuals diagnosed with mild hypertension (systolic BP: 140–159 mmHg, diastolic BP: 90–99 mmHg) or moderate hypertension (systolic BP: 160–179 mmHg, diastolic BP: 100–109 mmHg) [22]; BMI ranging from 25 to 30 kg/m2, age between 50 and 70 years, and no engagement in exercise programs during the past 6 months. Participants were excluded if they smoked or used e‐cigarettes, had a history of myocardial infarction, angioplasty, coronary artery bypass, chronic heart failure, or cardiac arrhythmias; suffered from mobility‐impairing conditions like osteoarthritis; were diagnosed with liver or kidney disorders, diabetes mellitus, or secondary hypertension (such as obstructive sleep apnea); or experienced a hypertensive emergency within the past year. All study procedures adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee at the University of Tehran (IR.UT.SPORT.REC.1402.124). Participants were fully informed—both verbally and in writing—about the purpose of the research, the measurement methods, and any potential risks or benefits before signing the informed consent during their first visit.

To control for external variables, participants were instructed to avoid intense exercise, caffeine, and tobacco products for a minimum of 24 h prior to testing, and to fast overnight before attending laboratory sessions scheduled between 7:00 and 9:00 a.m. Random assignment to either the HIIT or control (CTR) group was carried out via an online tool (https://www.randomizer.org/), with stratification according to age and sex. To maintain equal distribution, block randomization with a block size of two was implemented.

Overview of the Study Design

The study overview is depicted in Figure 1. All participants underwent a baseline assessment at Visit 1 including measurements of fasting blood sampling, body mass index (BMI), resting BP, HRV, and cfPWV, and cycle ergometer graded exercise test to determine peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) at Imam Khomeini Hospital by the same trained investigator at the same time of day of all subjects in CTR and HIIT groups. The HRV was assessed with subjects lying supine on a bed for 10 min, after that a continuous ECG was obtained for 20 min. cfPWV was assessed with subjects lying supine on a bed for 10 min and then ECG electrodes were affixed to the carotid and femoral arteries. Participants then underwent a peak graded exercise test on a cycle ergometer to determine VO2peak. Subsequent to Visit 1, HIIT performed 12 weeks of supervised exercise training that included 12 intervals, 1.5 min at 85%–90% of HRR separated by 2 min at 55%–60% of HRR. CTR was advised to engage in their normal activities of daily living during the 12‐week intervention without exercise training. Forty‐eight hours following the last session of training, in Visit 2, all measurements performed in Visit 1 were repeated for both groups. Additionally, each participant was instructed to adhere to their regular dietary habits throughout the intervention period and complete the necessary forms. The data from these forms were analyzed using CompEat Pro dietary analysis software (Nutrition Systems, Banbury, UK) (Table 1). Furthermore, participants adherence to physical activity was monitored by the Actigraph wGT3x‐BT accelerometer. They were instructed to wear the accelerometer on their non‐dominant wrist throughout the day. As well as, instructed participants to aim for between 7 and 9 hours of sleep each night. All measures were performed by a specialist who was blinded to the patients' history.

Cardiorespiratory Fitness

VO2peak was measured both pre‐ and postintervention using a graded cycling test with an ergometer connected to an indirect calorimetry system for respiratory gas analysis (ZAN 600CEPT, Ergorespiratory, ZAN Mesgerate GmbH, Oberthulba, Germany). A week before the main exercise evaluation, every participant completed a brief, low‐intensity practice session to familiarize themselves with the testing procedures. After a 5‐min warm‐up on the cycle ergometer, each participant continued pedaling. During the initial 2 min, men cycled at a workload of 30 watts while women cycled at 20 watts, after which the resistance was increased by 10 watts every minute until the participant could no longer maintain a constant cadence of 40 rpm. VO2peak was measured as the highest average value of VO2 over a 30‐s period during the exercise test. This measurement was determined based on the following criteria: (1) a peak respiratory exchange ratio of at least 1.1, (2) achieving 90% of predicted maximum HR using the formula [208 − (0.7 × age)], and (3) observing a plateau in VO2 despite an increase in workload [23].

Exercise Intervention

Participants performed 12 weeks of supervised HIIT sessions, cycling three times per week on specific days (Saturday, Monday, and Wednesday) between the hours of 09:00 a.m. and 12:00 p.m. Participants exercised on a 894E Monark Ergomedic Peak Bike (Varberg, Sweden), completing 12 intervals of 1.5 min/session at an intensity corresponding to 85%–90% of their heart rate reserve (HRR). These intervals were followed by a 2‐min rest period at 50%–55% HRR. Before and after each session, 10‐min warm‐up and cool‐down periods were allocated, during which HR was maintained at 40% HRR. HRR was determined from the exercise test as: [(maximal heart rate − resting heart rate) × (intensity of exercise)] + resting heart rate, according to the Karvonen method [24]. Maximal and resting HR were obtained from peak of the exercise test. Following each 2‐week exercise intervention, all participants conducted an exercise test to ascertain their updated maximal HR for the purpose of adjusting exercise intensity. The velocity and watts of the ergometer bicycle were consistently controlled during the 12‐week intervention to ensure each session was conducted at the intended HRR. In order to maintain the prescribed exercise intensity, all patients utilized a HR monitor (Polar Beat, Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland) throughout HIIT.

Blood Sample and Biochemical Analysis

Venous blood was drawn from the antecubital region between 09:00 and 11:00 a.m. following an overnight fast, both at baseline and 48 h after the final HIIT session. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C and subsequently frozen at −80°C for later biochemical analysis. Salusin‐α levels were measured in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA, MBS161541), salusin‐β (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA, MBS163956), AVP (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA, MBS700417), and NE (Abcam, Berlin, Germany, ab287789) were measured by ELISA. The intra‐assay coefficients of variation for salusin‐α and salusin‐β were both below 8%, while the inter‐assay coefficients were under 10%.

Office Blood Pressure

Systolic and diastolic BP were measured during Visits 1 and 2. Following a 5‐min supine rest period, an automated sphygmomanometer (Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan) measured brachial artery BP on the nondominant arm. Three measurements were recorded, each 1 min apart, and their mean was calculated for analysis.

Arterial Stiffness

Measurements of arterial stiffness were conducted in compliance with American Heart Association recommendations [25]. cfPWV was measured using an autonomic oscillometric instrument (AtCor Medical, Solingen, Germany) [26, 27] between 24 and 48 h after the last intervention session for HIIT and CTR groups. The participants were instructed to remain in a resting state for 10 min while lying supine on a bed. Next, a cardiac sonography microphone was attached to the left parasternal boundary of the fourth internal space, and a BP cuff was wrapped around the right arm. ECG electrodes were then affixed to the carotid and femoral arteries. To determine cfPWV, the distance between the carotid and femoral arteries (L) is divided by the time interval between the pulse wave's appearance at these two sites (Tcf), following the formula: cfPWV = L/Tcf.

Heart Rate Variability

A Holter HR monitoring device (3‐channel Medilog Digital Holter recorder FD3, Oxford, USA) with a 1024 Hz sample rate was used to evaluate HRV. After lying down on a bed and remaining in a resting position for 10 min measurements were taken. ECG electrodes were connected to the participant's chest and their HR were monitored for 20 min. A commercial device and computer were used to analyze HRV (Oxford Instruments, with Excel ECG Replay System‐Rel 8.5). Artifact removal from ECG recordings was performed by a trained operator who inspected and corrected irregular beats as needed, under cardiologist supervision. Data were included only if ectopic beats accounted for < 15%, and R–R intervals affected by ectopic beats were excluded and replaced using linear interpolation prior to spectral analysis. HRV indices, such as frequency domain parameters including low frequency (LF), high frequency (HF) and the LF/HF ratio, reflect sympathetic activity, parasympathetic activity and sympathovagal balance, respectively. Time domain analysis of HRV includes the standard deviation of R–R intervals (SDNN), which reflects parasympathetic activity within short‐term assessment periods. SDNN, LF (0.04–0.15 Hz), HF (0.15–0.4 Hz), and the LF/HF ratio—were calculated using MT‐210 Analysis Software Version 1.0.0 (Schiller).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 25.0; SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL, USA), while data visualization and graph creation were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 9, Boston, MA 02110, USA) was used to create the graphs. Statistical significance was p < 0.05 with results reported as the mean ± SD. A Shapiro–Wilk test confirmed the distribution of all outcome variables was normal. Two‐way repeated measure of ANCOVA (RMANCOVA) tested differences in salusins, AVP, NE, cfPWV, and HRV within and between HIIT and CTR over time corrected with Bonferroni post hoc analysis when necessary. In these analyses, covariates included sex, age, and BMI. Partial eta‐squared (η 2 p ) values were calculated to estimate the effect sizes. Multivariate linear regressions examined relationships of changes in salusin‐β, cfPWV, HRV, and VO2peak to the BP response to HIIT. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values under 5.0 confirmed that multicollinearity among the independent variables was minimal in both the multivariate regression and linear mixed models. Moreover, in these regression models, the combined partial contributions of each independent variable to the total variance exceeded the model's total R 2 value, indicating that each predictor played a significant role in explaining variations in the BP outcome [28].

Blood Pressure

An interaction was detected for changes in systolic (group × time, p < 0.001, η 2 p = 0.800) and diastolic BP (group × time, p = 0.001, η 2 p = 0.672) over time. Systolic and diastolic BP decreased from baseline after HIIT (ps < 0.05) with no change in CTR (ps > 0.05, Table 2). Thus, systolic BP decreased by 17.69 ± 4.37 mmHg more and diastolic BP decreased by 6.90 ± 2.35 mmHg more after HIIT versus CTR (ps = 0.001). There was an effect of age (systolic BP, p = 0.001, η 2 p = 0.156 and diastolic BP, p = 0.043, η 2 p = 0.170), BMI (systolic BP, p < 0.001, η 2 p = 0.485, and diastolic BP, p = 0.001, η 2 p = 0.290), or sex (systolic BP, p = 0.283, η 2 p = 0.016 and diastolic BP, p = 0.408, η 2 p = 0.009) as covariates.

Discussion

We are the first to investigate the effects of salusins on the BP response to HIIT among adults with HTN. We hypothesized BP would decrease in response to HIIT, partially explained by decreased plasma levels of salusin‐β. Following HIIT, we found SBP decreased ~17 mmHg and DBP ~ 7 mmHg, while salusin‐β decreased ~309 pg/mL compared to CTR. Furthermore, we found 82.4% of the variance in the SBP response to HIIT was explained by decreased salusin‐β (42%) and LF (20%), and increased HF (26%) and VO2peak (22%); while 72.4% of the variance in the DBP response to HIIT was explained by decreased salusin‐β (50%) and cfPWV (23%), and increased HF (21%) and VO2peak (20%). Consistent with our hypothesis, our new finding is that salusin‐β was the primary correlate of the BP response to HIIT, explaining 42% to 50% of the variance in the BP response to HIIT.

The observed decrease in SBP and DBP of 17/7 mmHg resulting from HIIT in adults with HTN, respectively, confirmed similar findings by Izadi et al. [18], Molmen‐Hansen et al. [19], and Clark et al. [29] of the antihypertensive benefits of HIIT. To put our findings in a clinical context, a 10 mmHg reduction in SBP among patients with baseline levels of SBP of 160 mmHg or more reduces the risk of CVD mortality by 15% [30, 31].

To the best of our knowledge, the influence of salusin‐β on the BP response to exercise has not been studied. Our novel finding of decreased levels of salusin‐β accounting for 42% of the decrease in SBP and 50% of the decrease in DBP responses to HIIT among adults with HTN provides new mechanistic insight into the BP benefits of HIIT. In support of our finding, Zhang et al. [6], Chen et al. [5] elevated salusin‐β levels were observed in the RVLM—a key vasomotor region involved in sympathetic regulation and BP control—which was associated with heightened renal sympathetic nerve activity and increased release of AVP and NE from the neurohypophysis in spontaneously hypertensive rats [32]. The AVP projects from the PVN to RVLM, which in turn mediates the effects of salusin‐β on sympathetic nervous system activation in hypertensive rats [5]. Furthermore, Alpsoy and colleagues found that diminished vagal tone in patients with HTN contributed to increased salusin‐β, and thus, augmented sympathetic tone in patients with HTN [33]. Collectively, these studies in rats [5, 6] and humans [33] suggest that the decreases in BP after HIIT we found may be partially attributed to decreased salusin‐β that shifted the sympathovagal balance to parasympathetic activation.

Pan and colleagues found that higher levels of endogenous salusin‐β in spontaneously hypertensive rats contributed to endothelial dysfunction by inhibiting the activation of endothelial nitric oxide (eNOS) synthesis, eNOS, and nitric oxide (NO) levels. Also, salusin‐β stimulated NADPH oxidase activation on the endothelial cell membrane and increased ROS production. ROS production in turn inhibited eNOS synthesis, decreased the release of NO, and increased vasoconstriction. They then found eNOS activity and eNOS and NO levels were restored by salusin‐β silencing in the arteries of spontaneously hypertensive rats [8]. The findings of Pan and colleagues suggest decreased levels of salusin‐β may have increased levels of NO which also contributed to the decreases in BP we observed after HIIT in adults with HTN.

In further support of our findings, Alpsoy and colleagues found plasma levels of salusin‐β were higher in nondipper than dipper individuals with HTN [33]. They also found nighttime ambulatory SBP and HR were increased along with an attenuated decline in nighttime BP, all of which were associated with increased levels of salusin‐β. They concluded increased levels of salusin‐β were a suitable candidate biomarker to predict nondipper status which shifted the autonomic balance in favor of the sympathetic nervous system activation at night [33].

In addition to salusin‐β, other correlates of the BP response to HIIT that emerged included HF accounting for 21%–26%, LF 20%, cfPWV 23%, and VO2peak 20%–22% of the variance in the BP response to HIIT. The autonomic nervous system, particularly sympathovagal balance, plays an important role in BP regulation [34]. Consistent with our findings related to the HRV domains of HF and LF, Deng and colleagues performed a meta‐analysis in patients with HTN and reported increased HF and decreased LF resulting from aerobic exercise training associated with decreases in BP of 4.8/2.9 mmHg [35]. Songsorn and colleagues demonstrated a whole‐body HIIT intervention was effective in improving HRV by reducing sympathetic nerve activity and increasing the parasympathetic nerve activity shifting the sympathovagal to parasympathetic activation among healthy, physically inactive young adults [36]. Additionally, in this study, the high‐intensity period of HIIT compared to the low‐intensity period stimulated NO synthesis to greater levels due to higher shear stress. The bioavailability of NO is not only a potent vasodilator that decreases peripheral vascular resistance but also promotes parasympathetic nervous system activity and decreased BP [37]. The studies of Deng et al. [35] and Songsorn et al. [36] further support the premise that the antihypertensive effects of HIIT may be partially attributed to factors that shift in the sympathovagal balance to parasympathetic activation, such as decreased salusin‐β and increased NO levels.

In support of our finding related to cfPWV, Zhou et al. [38] performed a meta‐analysis in patients with HTN and found decreased cfPWV associated with decreases in SBP/DBP of 5/4 mmHg in patients with HTN. Oliveira and colleagues demonstrated HIIT had a superior effect than traditional training in decreasing cfPWV by increasing peripheral NO bioavailability that attenuated the reflected wave to the aorta and reduced BP [39]. In a meta‐analysis, Way and colleagues reported that HIIT resulted in a more pronounced decrease in cfPWV compared to moderate‐intensity continuous aerobic training. This effect may stem directly from diminished elastin degradation and lower collagen buildup in arterial walls, or indirectly through enhanced antioxidant capacity, reduced inflammatory markers, and decreased sympathetic nervous system activity [40].

In our study, VO2peak increased by 4 mL/kg/min in response to the HIIT. Our findings confirm reports in the literature that HIIT promotes greater improvements in VO2peak than aerobic exercise training [41]. Improvements in VO2peak following HIIT of the magnitude we found are associated with decreased SBP/DBP of 4.7/4.4 in patients with HTN [42]. Engel et al. [43] postulated HIIT decreased BP via increased VO2peak through increased physiological hypertrophy without myocardial fibrosis in spontaneous hypertensive rats.

Evidence suggests that plasma levels of salusinsalusin‐α have a negative correlation with arterial stiffness. Our results indicate that plasma levels of salusinsalusin‐ significantly increased after a12‐week HIIT program versus the CTR group. Fujie et al., demonstrated that plasma levels of salusinsalusin‐α increased in elderly healthy subjects during aerobic exercise training (45 min at 60‐70% VO2peak, 8 weeks), and there was a negative correlation with PWV [44]. Salusinsalusin‐α is a peptide with anti‐atherosclerotic and anti‐inflammatory effects. Research has shown that angiotensin II (ATI) promotes the expression of Janus kinase‐2 (Jak‐2) as a critical BP regulator (Ang II). Importantly, Ang II both increases arterial stiffness and controls BP [45].

NE is typically used as an indirect index of sympathetic activity, which is regulated by baroreceptor sensitivity, while NO bioavailability is associated with vagal neuron activity. Casey et al. indicated that a decrease in catecholamine levels and α‐adrenergic density induced by endurance exercise training may affect the anterograde/retrograde shear rate. Improvement in NE sensitivity and receptor responsiveness leads to lower resting sympathetic activity and improved BP control [46]. Over a 12‐week intervention period, we observed a decrease in plasma levels of AVP and NE in the HIIT group versus CTR. Previous research has suggested that AVP can enter the brain through carrier‐mediated transport across the blood‐brain barrier [5, 47]. Therefore, the reduction in plasma levels of salusinsalusin‐α following 12 weeks of HIIT may be linked to a decrease in AVP, resulting in a lower concentration of AVP available for transport into the brain. This decrease in AVP within the brain is associated with reduced projections to the RVLM and a decrease in sympathetic activity in older adults with hypertension.

Limitations and Strengths

There are several limitations in the present RCT. The sample size was relatively small with a lack of racial/ethnic and age diversity, so the generalizability of our findings is limited. The 70 % adherence rate in our study may have influenced on BP, salusin‐α, salusin‐β, AVP, NE, HRV, cfPWV, VO2peak [48]. Although we excluded patients with CVDs and diabetes, we did not evaluate microvascular atherosclerosis plaque. Since salusin levels are associated with atherosclerosis [49], the possibility remains that subclinical atherosclerosis could have influenced salusin levels in the study participants. Our study had several strengths. This RCT is the first to examine salusin‐β following HIIT in patients with HTN to provide mechanistic insights into the antihypertensive benefits of HIIT. We implemented stringent eligibility criteria to ensure that the study groups were comparable with respect to baseline prognostic factors such as HTN class and BMI. The randomization process was performed by an online tool and block randomization method that helped us to ensure a balanced sample size across groups by sex and age.

Conclusion

This is the first RCT to investigate the effects of salusins on the BP response to HIIT among adults with HTN. HIIT resulted in clinically meaning decreases in SBP of 17 and DBP of 7 mmHg among adults with HTN. The decreases in BP we observed were partially attributed to decreased salusin‐β that emerged as the primary correlate in addition to increased HF, decreased LF, decreased cfPWV, and increased VO2peak. Further investigation is needed to more definitively determine the mechanistic insight into the antihypertensive benefits of HIIT among patients with HTN.
