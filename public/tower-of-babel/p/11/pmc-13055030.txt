Receptor-binding specificity and antigenic properties of a genotype D1.1 A(H5N1) influenza virus isolated from a human
Authors: Ahmed Kandeil, Pradeep Chopra, Sean D Ray, Balasaheb K Ghotekar, Charlene Ranadheera, Trushar Jeevan, Thomas Fabrizio, Adam Rubrum, Geert-Jan Boons, Stephen Mark Tompkins, Nathalie Bastien, Richard J Webby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClade 2.3.4.4b A(H5N1) influenza viruses continue to expand their host range and pose increasing public health concerns. A case of severe human infection with genotype D1.1 A(H5N1) with HÀ substitutions was diagnosed in British Columbia. It is important to monitor receptor-binding specificity and antigenicity of emerging variants of circulating influenza A(H5N1) viruses. To evaluate potential human adaptation of this virus, we enriched HA-variant subpopulations and assessed sialic acid receptor usage via glycan microarray, solid-phase assays, and biolayer interferometry. All variants bound exclusively to avian-type sialic acid receptors. The antigenicity of the tested A(H5N1) variants was covered by candidate vaccine viruses.KEYWORDS: Influenza, H5N1, Hemagglutinin, emerging variants, receptor-binding specificity

The unprecedented activity, including an expanding host range, of clade 2.3.4.4b highly pathogenic avian influenza A(H5N1) viruses is cause for public health concern. Circulating clade 2.3.4.4b A(H5N1) viruses have been reported in humans in Canada, the USA, and Mexico [1]. As of October 2025, the 2.3.4.4b viruses have caused 73 laboratory-confirmed human infections and 2 deaths. Most of the reported human cases in North America had known exposures to either poultry or dairy cattle [2]. Recent seroprevalence studies in several countries have indicated that human infections with the clade 2.3.4.4b A(H5N1) viruses are underestimated; true infection rates among at-risk populations range from 2% to 7% [3–5].

In November 2024, a severe infection of an adolescent girl with a genotype D1.1 A(H5N1) virus was reported in British Columbia [6]. The hemagglutinin (HA) glycoprotein of the infecting virus (A/British Columbia/PHL-2032/2024 [H5N1; BC]), which was sequenced from clinical material, identified the amino acid substitutions E190D and Q226H (H3 numbering), 2 positions previously implicated in sialic acid receptor interactions. Although these substitutions were detected in clinical samples at frequencies of only 28% for E190D and 35% for Q226H, their presence raised concerns that they are signs of viral adaptation to human sialic acid receptors [6]. Furthering these concerns was the identification of the E190E/D mixture in clinical samples obtained from a fatal human infection in Louisiana [7].

Position 190 within the HA 190-helix acts as a critical structural element of the sialic acid receptor–binding site that affects receptor-binding avidity and virus antigenicity and fitness. Switching receptor-binding specificity by acquiring asparagine at HA positions 190 and 225 was a critical factor in the A(H1N1) pandemics [8,9]. Assessing receptor-binding specificity is important for risk assessments of zoonotic influenza viruses.

Here, we characterized the receptor-binding specificity of subpopulations of H5N1; BC virus isolated through plaque purification. Receptor usage was examined by glycan microarray technology and a solid-phase–based assay [10]. Additionally, we analyzed the antigenic relations between the BC subpopulations and World Health Organization–recommended candidate vaccine viruses (CVVs). We isolated 4 populations of BC viruses that had variations in their HA sequences from a virus stock (from the original clinical sample) that had been passaged twice in MDCK (Madine-Darby canine kidney) cells. These virus subpopulations had the following amino acid substitutions within HA: (1) E190; (2) D190; (3) G159, K169, and D190; and (4) D159, K169, and D190, which was representative of the consensus-level sequence from the parental virus stock. We did not detect any viruses containing HA 226H and observed only a few instances (0.5%) of this mutation in parental stocks. However previous deep mutational scanning showed that the presence of a histidine residue at position 226 did not affect cellular entry in the context of a 2.3.4.4b HA [11].

All tested purified BC viruses bound only to “avian-type” receptors (α2,3-sialosides) based on the 3 assays we used: glycan microarray (Figure 1(a and b)), solid-phase–binding assay (Figure 1(c)), and biolayer interferometry (Figure 1(d)). None of the BC viruses showed any adaptation to “human-type” receptor specificity (α2,6-sialosides). Compared to the A/Vietnam/1203/2004 virus, BC viruses exhibited broader receptor-binding specificity and can accommodate both α1,3-fucosides (e.g. Figure 1(a) H, L) and bisecting N-acetyl-glucosamine (GlcNAc, e.g. Figure 1(a) I, M). Unlike the earlier strain, which primarily recognized biantennary N-glycans with α2,3-sialosides at elongated N-acetyl-lactosamine [Gal(β1,4)GlcNAc, LacNAc] chains, BC viruses bound simpler structures containing 1 or 2 sialyl Lewisx (SLex) moieties (e.g. Figure 1(a) O vs. P). We did, however, observe reduced avidity of most tested viruses to a glycopolymer functionalized with avian-like receptors (Figure 1). Of note, full adaptation of avian influenza viruses to humans involves not only the acquisition of binding to the α2,6-sialosides but also the loss of binding to the α2,3-sialosides, thereby providing a potential reason for selecting these subpopulations [12]. The observed avidity differences not only reflected virus binding to host receptors but also underlined the importance of the nature of the synthetic glycopolymers used, including glycan length, valency, density, and flexibility, to capture the full spectrum of viral-binding behaviours [13–15]. All viruses, regardless of HA substitutions, reacted with postinfection ferret antisera raised against 2 tested CVVs: A/Astrakhan/3212/2020 (H5N8) and A/Ezo red fox/Hokkaido/1/2022 (H5N1), showing that the residue changes had limited antigenic effects (Table S1). Interestingly, the loss of a glycosylation site caused by the N169 K substitution was associated with a 2- to – 3-fold increase in reactivity to CVV ferret antisera. This may reflect the unmasking of nearby antigenic epitopes that become more accessible to antibody binding after removal of the glycan shield. Neutralization titres for the BC viruses were generally within a 3-fold range observed for the tested CVVs (Table S2).

Receptor-binding specificity of British Columbia A(H5N1) D1.1 variants isolated from a human. (a) Structures of N-glycans (A–U) printed on the microarray. (b) Glycan array–binding analysis of the receptor-binding specificities of 5 A(H5N1) viruses and an A(H1N1) virus. (c) Solid-phase binding assay of 4 British Columbia A(H5N1) D1.1 variants. (d) Biolayer interferometry binding assay showing the preferences of different British Columbia A(H5N1) viruses to 2,3-SLN, rather than 2,6-SLN, receptors. The BC virus with the E190 substitution exhibited a lower preference for avian-type sialic acid receptors than did the variant with the D190 substitution.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that the genetic changes observed in the British Columbia’s human isolate of genotype D1.1 A(H5N1) influenza viruses have not acquired human-type receptor specificity or altered antigenic properties. Together, these results underscore the importance of monitoring receptor binding and assessing the antigenicity of emerging variants of circulating H5N1influenza viruses.
