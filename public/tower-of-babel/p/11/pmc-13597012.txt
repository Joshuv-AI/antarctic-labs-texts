Laparoscopic Management of Isolated Fallopian Tube Torsion in Pregnancy
Authors: Thomas Joyce, Oluwasemilore Oluwagbenro, Isabel Sanchez, Marino Poliseno, Nisha Lakhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Isolated fallopian tube torsion (IFTT) is a rare gynecologic emergency that poses a diagnostic challenge during pregnancy due to its nonspecific clinical presentation and overlap with more common abdominal pathologies such as ovarian torsion and appendicitis. We report the case of a 20-year-old primigravid woman at 28 weeks gestation who presented with acute right lower quadrant pain. Diagnostic laparoscopy revealed a necrotic, torsed right fallopian tube associated with a paratubal cyst. We also reviewed the current literature on laparoscopic management of IFTT.Methods:A systematic review following PRISMA guidelines identified seven additional cases of laparoscopic management of IFTT in the second or third trimester, yielding eight cases for analysis.Results:The mean maternal age was 28 years (18–36 years), and the average gestational age was 29 weeks (24–35 weeks). Right-sided abdominal pain was present in 100% of cases, with acute onset in 87.5% and associated nausea and vomiting in 75%. Ultrasonographic imaging consistently identified a pelvic mass with preserved ovarian Doppler flow; MRI was utilized in 75% of cases. The most common preoperative diagnosis was ovarian torsion (62.5%). Entry techniques included Palmer’s point (42.9%), Lee-Huang point (42.9%), and one case utilizing umbilical-entry. Salpingectomy was performed in 87.5% of patients due to tubal necrosis. All reported pregnancy outcomes progressed to term.Conclusions:This case and review support laparoscopy as a safe and effective management strategy for IFTT in the second and third trimesters, offering favorable maternal and fetal outcomes when performed with appropriate surgical technique and multidisciplinary coordination.

INTRODUCTION

Isolated Fallopian Tube Torsion (IFTT) is a rare gynecologic pathology characterized by torsion of the fallopian tube along its own axis, independent of ovarian involvement. Its incidence has been reported to be approximately 1 in 1.5 million women,1 thus incidence in pregnancy is even rarer. Structural abnormalities such as paratubal cysts, hydrosalpinx, and malignancy may contribute to the pathogenesis of the condition.2 This often presents acute abdominal pain, nausea, and/or vomiting. As a clinical entity, the presentation is often nonspecific lacking clinical signs, symptoms, imaging or laboratory findings that are pathognomonic in nature.2,3

Since its initial description in 1933, several case reports have been documented in the literature, with a small minority presenting during pregnancy.4 IFTT is often misdiagnosed for other differentials of an acute abdomen, such as appendicitis and ovarian torsion when encountered clinically.5 Diagnostic accuracy is further challenged secondary to anatomical shifts in the setting of the gravid uterus.6 Utility of ultrasound to accurately diagnose IFTT is only reported in 30% of cases.7 Other modalities, such as Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and computer tomography (CT) may also be utilized but are limited by low sensitivity.8 If left without intervention, IFFT may result in deleterious effects to the pregnancy such as fetal loss, preterm labor, tubal necrosis, and maternal morbidity.9

Advances in minimally invasive surgery have increasingly made laparoscopy the preferred approach for many nonobstetric surgical emergencies during pregnancy. Laparoscopy in pregnant patients has been shown to be as safe as laparotomy when performed with appropriate precautions. It offers well established benefits: shorter hospital stays, less postoperative pain, faster return to activity, and lower risk of wound complications or incisional hernias.10 Tenets of surgical management of IFFT include successful detorsion or salpingectomy of the affected tube.11 However, due to the extreme rarity of IFTT, evidence guiding the optimal diagnosis and management in late pregnancy has been limited. Several recent reviews have synthesized the available literature on IFTT, including Gulino et al., who conducted a systematic review of laparotomy and laparoscopic cases in both pregnant and nonpregnant women,12 and Jo et al., who reported a case of IFTT during pregnancy with an accompanying literature review of surgical approaches.6 While these studies provide important foundational knowledge, our analysis makes a distinct contribution by restricting inclusion to laparoscopic cases occurring specifically in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. This focus highlights the feasibility of minimally invasive surgery even in advanced gestation, with particular emphasis on technical considerations for entry site selection, intraoperative management, and maternal–fetal outcomes. By narrowing our scope, we aim to provide practical, procedure-oriented insights for surgeons faced with this rare but challenging scenario. In this report we will discuss a 20-year-old primigravid woman in the 28th week of pregnancy who presented with abdominal pain, nonspecific imaging, and upon diagnostic laparoscopy was noted to have an IFTT. We further systematically review the state of the current literature on a laparoscopic approach in the management of IFTT in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A systematic review of the literature was conducted following the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines. Utilizing PubMed, Medline, SCOPUS, and Google Scholar, we searched for articles published in English between inception to Jan 1, 2024, that addressed isolated fallopian tube torsion in pregnancy during the second and third trimesters. Search terms included combinations of “isolated tubal torsion,” “fallopian tube torsion”, “pregnancy”, “third trimester”, “second trimester”, “tubal torsion in pregnancy”, “laparoscopy”, and “minimally invasive surgery”. Given the rarity of the condition, only case reports were identified (Figure 1).

PRISMA diagram for study selection.

Articles were included if they were published in English, described a pregnant patient in the second or third trimester, reported a confirmed diagnosis of isolated fallopian tube torsion (IFTT), and documented management with a laparoscopic approach. Exclusion criteria consisted of nonpregnancy-related cases, management by laparotomy, cases of ectopic pregnancy, first-trimester presentations, or reports that did not represent true IFTT. A total of 549 records were identified through PubMed, Medline, SCOPUS, and Google Scholar. Following title and abstract screening, 492 records were excluded. Twenty-four full-text articles were then assessed for eligibility, of which 17 were excluded (11 describing laparotomy management, 3 not pregnancy-related, and 3 not true IFTT). Seven publications met inclusion criteria and were included in the review. With the addition of our institutional case, a total of eight cases comprised the final analysis. Case Description

A 20-year-old primigravid woman at 28 weeks of gestation presented via ambulance with acute onset right lower quadrant pain that began earlier that evening. She described the pain as initially constant and sharp, which later became intermittent. She also reported persistent nausea with episodes of vomiting, chills, and intolerance to oral intake. Pain was aggravated by movement, without attributable relieving factors.

On initial presentation, the patient was in moderate distress, likely secondary to pain. Her abdomen was soft, though she had right lower quadrant tenderness with minimal guarding. Her overall abdominal examination was considered benign. She was afebrile and hemodynamically stable throughout her hospital course. Obstetrically, electronic fetal monitoring demonstrated a fetal tracing with moderate variability, positive accelerations, and occasional variable decelerations. Given her gestational age her tracing was deemed overall reassuring. Her tocometer demonstrated absence of uterine contractions. Laboratory evaluation, including hematologic studies, serum chemistry, and urinalysis, was unremarkable, with normal hemoglobin, no leukocytosis, and no electrolyte abnormalities. Targeted ultrasonography, including obstetric, gynecologic, abdominal, and renal studies, were obtained to identify possible structural causes of her pain.

Obstetric sonography was reassuring for fetal wellbeing. Renal ultrasound demonstrated mild right-sided hydronephrosis without evidence of obstruction, consistent with physiologic changes of pregnancy. Abdominal ultrasound visualized a normal gallbladder, hepatobiliary system, and spleen; however, the appendix could not be identified, likely due to limitations from the gravid uterus. Transvaginal ultrasound demonstrated a 4 cm simple-appearing cystic structure in the right adnexa with preserved ovarian blood flow. To better assess the appendix and characterize the adnexal mass, an MRI was performed, which showed a normal appendix and reconfirmed the right adnexal lesion.

Despite supportive care, including intravenous acetaminophen and narcotics, the patient’s abdominal pain persisted. With concern for adnexal torsion, most likely ovarian in origin, a decision was made by the gynecology team to proceed with diagnostic laparoscopy. The risks and benefits of surgery were thoroughly explained to the patient, who elected to proceed through shared decision-making.

Laparoscopic entry was achieved via Lee-Huang point using the Hasson technique, with two lateral accessory ports placed under direct visualization. Intraoperatively, the right fallopian tube was noted to be cystic, torsed, and necrotic (Figure 2). The right ovary appeared grossly normal (Figure 3). A laparoscopic salpingectomy was performed. Given the technical challenges posed by the gravid uterus and the necrotic appearance of the tube, detorsion was deferred in favor of excision. Pathological examination demonstrated an infarcted salpinx. The patient made a full recovery and was discharged on postoperative day 2, following extended obstetric monitoring. She subsequently delivered a healthy term infant without obstetric complications.

Literature Review/Results

Our search yielded seven publications reporting laparoscopic management of isolated tubal torsion in pregnancy that met inclusion criteria. These case reports, published between 2011 and 2023, each described a single patient; three also included a broader literature review component. All cases involved IFTT in the second or third trimester. With the addition of our institutional case at 28 weeks’ gestation, the series comprised eight patients for analysis (Table 1).

The average maternal age was 28 years (range: 18–36 years). All but one patient (Chohan et al., 201113) were primigravid. The mean gestational age at presentation was 29 weeks (range: 24–35 weeks gestation). All patients presented with right-sided abdominal pain. Seven of the eight were described as acute in nature (87.5%), with one case (Akalin et al., 202314) having a more subacute presentation with worsening pain over the course of 10 days. Additionally, six cases (75%) involved nausea and vomiting, mimicking the presentation of ovarian torsion or acute appendicitis. All patients were afebrile.

Physical examination findings were nonspecific across cases. Most patients had localized lower abdominal tenderness, sometimes with mild guarding or rebound. Uterine contractions were reported in two patients (25%), though fetal heart rate tracings were reassuring in all cases. Mild leukocytosis was reported in 50% of patients (n = 4 cases), with the highest WBC reported by Akalın et al. (13,130/μL).14

All patients underwent obstetric ultrasonography to assess the fetus and placenta. Targeted gynecological or abdominal ultrasound was used in all cases. In most cases, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) was ordered to further delineate pelvic structures (i.e. appendix, ovary, etc.,) due to anatomic limitations (n = 6; 75%). MRI corroborated ultrasonographic findings, with the exception of Takeda et al., 2020 with findings suggestive of IFTT.15

The most common preoperative diagnosis was adnexal torsion, presumed to be ovarian in origin (n = 5, 62.5%). Two cases were suspected to be acute appendicitis (n = 2 25%), while only one case (Takeda et al., 202015) accurately considered IFTT preoperatively. Secondary differentials included appendicitis (25%) and ovarian torsion (12.5%). Most surgeries occurred within 24–48 hours of hospital admission, after imaging and obstetric consultation. Importantly, once the decision for surgical exploration was made, prompt operative management was pursued.

All eight cases were managed with surgical intervention, and notably, all were approached with minimally invasive surgery. No case required conversion to open laparotomy intraoperatively. The laparoscopic approach was sufficient to both diagnose and treat the condition despite the challenges posed by advanced gestational age. Several different entry strategies were employed. Seven of the eight cases described their method of entry. Palmer’s point was utilized in 42.9% of cases (n = 3). Lee-Huang point was utilized in 42.9% of cases (n = 3). One case (12.5%) entered through the umbilicus. Seven of the eight cases utilized traditional multiport laparoscopy. Takeda et al. utilized laparoendoscopic single site surgery for their approach.15

Intraoperative findings confirmed the diagnosis of right-sided isolated fallopian tube torsion in all cases. Laparoscopy revealed in 50% of cases the etiology to be paratubal cysts (n = 4) and 50% of cases reported hydrosalpinx (n = 4). In seven of the eight cases (87.5%), a salpingectomy was performed given apparent necrosis in all cases. In Chohan et al., a detorsion was performed.13 In Macedo et al., a concurrent appendectomy was performed at time of salpingectomy.16 Histopathological examination confirmed the intraoperative findings.

Outcomes were uniformly favorable. All pregnancy outcomes reported progressed to term with delivery of healthy neonates. Six patients (85.6%) had vaginal deliveries, and two (14.2%) underwent cesarean section for unrelated obstetric indications. Macedo et al. did not disclose their patient’s obstetric outcome.16

DISCUSSION

Isolated Fallopian Tube Torsion (IFTT) is a rare gynecologic emergency that often presents clinically as an acute abdomen. In the nonpregnant population, it is estimated to have an incidence of 1 in 1.5 million women.1 It can be extrapolated, given this incidence rate and limited literature, that IFTT could be even more rare in the pregnant population. Its nondescript findings on sonography and MRI make it difficult to discern the entity from other etiologies such as ovarian torsion and appendicitis.3 Interestingly, all eight cases in our series, including our institutional report, involved right-sided IFTT. A right-sided predominance has been noted in the broader adnexal torsion literature and may reflect anatomic and physiologic factors. The sigmoid colon provides partial stabilization to the left adnexa, limiting torsional mobility, whereas the right adnexa has greater freedom of movement.1,17 Additionally, the higher frequency of right lower quadrant pain leading to surgical exploration may contribute to diagnostic detection bias.17

Historically, IFTT is most often managed with laparotomy.12 However, laparoscopic procedures in later stages of pregnancy are safe and can be considered.10 Laparoscopic management offers distinct advantages in managing IFTT. It allows definitive diagnosis and treatment of torsion while minimizing maternal morbidity. The accumulated case experience shows that even up to 35 weeks’ gestation, laparoscopic detorsion or salpingectomy can be performed successfully, with resolution of symptoms and continuation of the pregnancy to term. Key technical considerations include alternate site entry to establish pneumoperitoneum safely, adjusting trocar placement to the gravid anatomy, and maintaining diligent maternal-fetal monitoring perioperatively. In selecting laparoscopic entry points during pregnancy, anatomic considerations are paramount to avoid uterine injury and ensure adequate visualization. Palmer’s point, located in the left upper quadrant, provides safe access above the uterine fundus and is particularly advantageous in patients with a markedly enlarged uterus or prior midline abdominal surgery where periumbilical adhesions may be present.10,17,18 It also offers reliable distance from the gravid uterus, thereby minimizing the risk of inadvertent uterine injury. In contrast, the Lee-Huang point, a midline supraumbilical entry, provides a direct central view of the pelvis and upper abdomen, which can be beneficial in later gestation when the uterus elevates into the upper abdomen and displaces adnexal structures.6,17,18 Umbilical entry can be safely employed in select patients when the uterine fundus remains below the umbilicus and intra-abdominal space is sufficient.10,18 The choice among these techniques is guided by gestational age, uterine size, patient habitus, and the surgeon’s familiarity with each approach.6,10 Innovative techniques also warrant further study. For example, Takeda et al. (2020) described the successful use of laparoendoscopic single-site surgery at 31 weeks’ gestation, suggesting that advanced minimally invasive approaches may be feasible in select cases.15 Further research is needed to further identify the best approach.

Although the focus of our review is on the laparoscopic approach, it is worth emphasizing that outcomes in IFTT are generally favorable, when timely intervention is undertaken. Even in laparotomy cases, almost all patients and fetuses do well.12 However, delays in diagnosis and intervention can result in disastrous effects to the pregnancy such as fetal loss, preterm labor, and maternal morbidity.9 The proposed mechanisms of fetal loss include maternal sepsis from necrotic adnexal tissue,9 preterm labor triggered by peritoneal inflammation17 and impaired uteroplacental perfusion secondary to maternal systemic compromise.16,17 These risks underscore the importance of prompt diagnosis and surgical intervention. Notably, in all laparoscopic cases analyzed, timely management resulted in preservation of pregnancy with favorable neonatal outcomes.9,16 As mentioned in the other literature in this topic, in skilled surgical hands, laparoscopic management of IFTT is feasible and should be considered in management.

For clinicians, early recognition of IFTT during pregnancy requires maintaining a high index of suspicion when patients present with acute right-sided abdominal pain, often accompanied by nausea and vomiting.1,14 The condition poses diagnostic challenges because ultrasound and MRI findings are frequently nonspecific.3,7,8 Diagnostic pitfalls include preserved ovarian blood flow on Doppler studies, which may falsely reassure providers and delay further evaluation.7,15 Delayed diagnosis increases the risk of tubal necrosis and adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm labor and, rarely, fetal loss.9,12 Prompt surgical evaluation remains essential to minimize maternal morbidity while preserving favorable obstetric outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Laparoscopic management of isolated fallopian tube torsion during pregnancy is a safe and effective alternative to laparotomy, even in the third trimester. Early surgical intervention with careful technique minimizes maternal morbidity while preserving pregnancy outcomes. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for IFTT in pregnant patients presenting with acute abdomen and atypical imaging findings, recognizing that preserved ovarian Doppler flow and nonspecific imaging can obscure the diagnosis.
