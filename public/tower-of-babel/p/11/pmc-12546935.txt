Detection of pre-seismic magnetic field anomalies using Swarm satellite data: a case study of the 2025 Mw7.7 Myanmar earthquake
Authors: Homayoon Alimoradi, Habib Rahimi, Angelo De Santis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates magnetic field anomalies as precursors to the Mw7.7 Myanmar earthquake (March 28, 2025) using Swarm satellite data. We analyzed vector magnetic field (X, Y, Z) measurements over the Dobrovolsky region during the 10 days preceding the earthquake, applying cubic-spline filtering to identify residual anomalies. Our results revealed Y-component anomalies in 22 of 85 half-orbits, detectable up to 8 days before the event. Magnitude estimates were obtained using four empirical equations based on anomaly parameters such as duration, amplitude (“energy”), timing, and satellite–epicenter distance. Among these, the distance-based relation provided the most accurate estimate (M ≈ 7.2), showing reasonable agreement with the observed magnitude (M = 7.7). Interestingly, anomaly energy values consistently clustered within a narrow range (570–577), which may represent a characteristic signature of earthquake-related anomalies. These findings suggest that satellite-based magnetic anomalies could contribute to short-term earthquake forecasting systems, although further validation with larger datasets is needed to assess the robustness and general applicability of the approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20901-1.

Introduction

Earthquake prediction remains one of the most challenging problems in seismology. Despite substantial efforts, no reliable method exists to predict the time, location, and magnitude of major earthquakes with low uncertainty. While the general public often expects short-term predictions, the economic and social implications of false alarms, especially in urban regions with critical infrastructure, significantly limit the practicality of such uncertain forecasts1,2.

Nonetheless, research into earthquake precursors continues to expand. These precursors refer to observable physical or chemical changes—especially in the lithosphere, atmosphere, or ionosphere—that precede seismic events3. Although no universal precursor has been confirmed, satellite and ground-based observations have detected several anomalies associated with large earthquakes, such as ultra-low frequency (ULF) electromagnetic emissions, thermal infrared (TIR) anomalies, and ionospheric disturbances in electron density and magnetic field variations4–15.

A widely discussed framework for explaining such earthquake-related anomalies is the Lithosphere–Atmosphere–Ionosphere Coupling (LAIC) model. According to this concept, stress accumulation in the lithosphere can trigger a chain of processes, including radon emanation, atmospheric ionization, and changes in conductivity, which can propagate upward and produce detectable disturbances in the ionosphere and the geomagnetic field. This coupling mechanism provides a plausible physical basis for interpreting magnetic and ionospheric anomalies as possible precursors of seismic events16–18.

The increasing availability of satellite data and cloud-based analysis platforms (e.g., Google Earth Engine, NASA Giovanni) has accelerated the detection of such anomalies19. Notably, the DEMETER satellite mission (2004–2010) provided pioneering observations of seismo-ionospheric phenomena, while the currently active Swarm constellation continues to advance global monitoring in this field20–22. Previous studies have already provided evidence that Swarm satellite magnetic data can reveal potential precursory signatures of strong earthquakes. For instance, Alimoradi et al. (2024)7 reported a statistical correlation between magnetic field anomalies in Swarm data and parameters such as earthquake magnitude, time-to-event, and satellite–epicenter distance. More recently, Özsöz and Pamukçu (2025)23 and Özsöz et al. (2024)24 analyzed Swarm data over Turkye and Greece, confirming time-dependent magnetic anomalies before several M ≥ 6 earthquakes. Together with earlier theoretical models25,26, these findings emphasize the potential of Swarm-based observations as a valuable tool for short-term earthquake precursor research, and provide the context for the present Myanmar case study. Empirical evidence suggests that parameters such as Total Electron Content (TEC)4, electromagnetic wave propagation, and outgoing longwave radiation (OLR)11,12 may undergo detectable variations prior to significant earthquakes.

On March 28, 2025, a Mw 7.7 strike-slip earthquake struck the Sagaing region of Myanmar, causing widespread damage across several countries and resulting in over 5,000 fatalities27–29. Myanmar lies at the complex junction of four tectonic plates, including the highly active Sagaing Fault, a known source of destructive earthquakes30 (Fig. 1). The recurrence of large events along this fault highlights the urgent need for effective short-term warning tools.

Recent studies by Alimoradi et al. (2024)7 using Swarm satellite data have revealed that certain magnetic field anomalies, especially in the Y component, are statistically correlated with earthquake magnitude, satellite-epicenter distance, and time before the event. These studies introduced four empirical equations that estimate earthquake magnitude based on anomaly features such as duration, amplitude (“anomaly energy”), and observation timing (see Data Processing). These empirical relations were specifically developed using Swarm vector magnetic field (VFM) anomalies. Their applicability to other ionospheric parameters, such as plasma density (Ne) or electron temperature (Te), has not yet been systematically tested. Future investigations may extend these equations to a broader set of parameters within a multi-parameter framework.

This study aims to investigate the presence of pre-seismic magnetic field anomalies before the March 28, 2025 Mw7.7 Myanmar earthquake by analyzing Swarm vector magnetic field data over the 10-day period preceding the event. Our objectives are to test whether the detected anomalies are consistent with the earthquake’s seismic parameters, to evaluate their potential role as short-term precursors within the framework of satellite-based earthquake monitoring, and to apply recently developed empirical equations for magnitude estimation to this case study. The novelty of this work lies in its application of these empirical relations to a new large earthquake, and in highlighting that the anomaly “energy” values consistently fall within a narrow range, which may serve as a potential diagnostic feature of earthquake-related anomalies.

In this work, we adopt an empirical statistical approach, applying previously developed equations to Swarm magnetic anomalies in order to evaluate their potential as short-term earthquake precursors.

Results and discussion

Vector magnetic field (VMF) data—including all three components (X, Y, Z) and the total magnetic field intensity (F)—were analyzed using observations from the Swarm satellite constellation. During the 10-day period preceding the Myanmar earthquake (Mw 7.7), 85 half-orbits from Swarm Alpha, Bravo, and Charlie passed over the Dobrovolsky region (as defined in Eq. 1 in the Methods section). Among these, 22 half-orbits showed detectable magnetic anomalies (Table 1), representing a significant proportion of possible precursory activity.

A detailed analysis was conducted on Swarm Alpha’s orbit 63,682, which passed within ~ 50 km of the earthquake epicenter on March 24, 2025—four days prior to the event (Fig. 2). This orbit exhibited a pronounced magnetic anomaly in the Y component, consistent with earlier findings that this component is the most sensitive to earthquake-related signals7,39. Given the satellite’s velocity, which covers approximately one latitudinal degree in 15 seconds19,31, this anomaly lasted for about 135 s.

Based on Table 4—and as observed in Table 3— we notice that, in general, magnitude averages do not depend on the satellite. In addition, M1, M2 and M4 underestimate the real magnitude, while M3 overestimates that value. When we exclude the estimates taken in geomagnetic disturbed times and too far from the earthquake epicentre (at distances larger than 1000 km), final averages are usually a little better. Table 4 confirms that the best magnitude estimate was obtained using Eq. (3). It is also important to note that the present analysis is restricted to magnetic field anomalies. Applying and validating these equations with additional parameters, such as plasma density (Ne) and electron temperature (Te), remains an important task for future research and may improve the robustness of earthquake precursor detection in a multi-parameter framework. Considering the actual magnitude of the Myanmar earthquake (7.7) and the estimated magnitude from Eq. (3), which is approximately 7.2, it can be claimed that this is a reasonable estimate, because a typical uncertainty in magnitude estimation is 0.540, while those taken with Eqs. (2) and (5) underestimate the real magnitude, by almost a unit. On the other hand, the method based on Eq. (4), overestimates the real magnitude by about 1.5. Why this latter Equation overestimates the magnitude could be due to its use of the amplitude (or “energy”) of the anomaly, that could depend on the satellite altitude, that usually decreases with time, causing a little larger amplitude of the magnetic anomaly and, in turn, an overestimation of the resulting magnitude.

However, this Eq. (4), which is the only one that is based on the anomaly “energy” (or amplitude), has an interesting feature. As shown in Table 3, the energy values of the detected anomalies mostly fall within a specific range of 570 to 577 (14 out of 22). Although the magnitudes estimated using anomaly energy involve a greater margin of error, the fact that the energy of the identified anomalies for this earthquake consistently lies within the 570–577 range may, if confirmed in other cases, serve as a distinguishing feature between earthquake-related anomalies and other types. This finding could also be considered a promising subject for future research.

Another noteworthy point concerns the anomaly identified in orbit number 63,682, which passed directly over the causative fault of the earthquake and the area that experienced the highest intensity. This overlap indicates that the detected anomaly, which lasted for 138 s, corresponds precisely to the region of maximum seismic intensity. Therefore, it can be confidently stated that this magnetic anomaly was associated with pre-seismic processes and appeared four days before the mainshock.

As shown in Fig. 5, the causative fault of the earthquake lies along the longitude of 96° E and between the latitudes of 16° N to 25°N, which is the area of highest intensity according to the USGS shake map. On the other hand, the path of orbit 63,682, as shown in Fig. 2, also crosses longitude 96°E, and the magnetic anomaly appears between latitudes 27°N and 18°N, demonstrating a strong spatial correlation.

Based on this, it can be concluded that the satellite successfully detected a magnetic anomaly caused by the earthquake-related processes four days prior to the event. Although this method is still in development and has certain limitations, the findings suggest that with more precise monitoring of known active faults and higher-resolution data acquisition in seismically active regions, there is potential to advance the science of earthquake precursors and move closer to the goal of earthquake prediction.

Overall, these results confirm the presence of significant magnetic field anomalies prior to the Myanmar earthquake, and demonstrate that empirical models based on Swarm data can provide useful estimates of earthquake magnitude. However, while promising, such anomalies must be interpreted with caution due to the influence of external geomagnetic activity and orbit geometry. Continued validation with larger datasets and diverse seismic events remains essential.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of satellite-based magnetic field measurements in detecting precursory phenomena related to seismic events. Using data from the Swarm satellite mission, we identified significant magnetic anomalies in the region surrounding the Myanmar earthquake epicenter, which occurred up to ten days before the event. These anomalies were detected in multiple components of the magnetic field (X, Y, Z), with the Y-component showing the most pronounced changes. The correlation between the detected anomalies and the earthquake magnitude is promising, with the magnitude estimates based on these anomalies aligning closely with the actual earthquake magnitude. The analysis further highlights that magnetic anomalies detected in the vicinity of active fault zones could serve as valuable indicators of pre-seismic processes, potentially advancing the field of earthquake prediction. While further research is needed to refine the methods and validate the findings in different seismic regions, this study marks an important step toward understanding the role of magnetic field anomalies as precursors to earthquakes. Future studies should analyze a larger dataset of M7 + earthquakes to validate empirical equations, and understand why some are better than others, and why one (M3) provides an overestimate of the actual magnitude. With improved data resolution and monitoring, magnetic field data may one day contribute significantly to early earthquake warning systems a few days in advance.

Materials and methods data

The Swarm constellation, launched in late 2013, consists of three satellites (Alpha, Bravo and Charlie) designed to study the Earth’s magnetic field22. The satellites operate in different orbits: Alpha and Charlie fly in nearly identical orbits at approximately 450 km altitude, while Bravo orbits at around 512 km. This configuration enables simultaneous measurement of aligned current flows in the radial direction of the field31 and long-term monitoring across a full solar cycle (11 years). Each satellite carries precision instrumentation, including VMF magnetometers for 3D magnetic field measurements; electric field instruments for ionospheric plasma characterization, accelerometers, and GPS receivers. These observations are provided as b1 level data, which include calibrated time series formatted for specific use, such as magnetic field measurements taken by all three Swarm satellites. The research objectives dedicated to this mission are: (1) Studies of core dynamics, geodynamic processes, and the mantle-core interaction; (2) Mapping of lithospheric polarization and its geological interpretation; (3) Determining the 3D electrical conductivity of the mantle; and (4) Investigating the electric currents flowing in the magnetosphere and ionosphere. However, one challenging aspect is the separation of different sources in the core, lithosphere, ionosphere, magnetosphere, and so on, which together contribute to the overall measured magnetic field32. Some of the main results obtained so far by the Swarm satellite mission include: mapping of the lithospheric magnetic field with a high resolution of 250 km33, the effect of ocean tides on the geomagnetic field, and the electromagnetic induction generated by ocean circulation34,35.

In this paper, we used VFM level 1-b data, with a sampling rate of 1 s, from 10 days before the earthquake to the day of the event, along paths passing through the Dobrovolsky region. According to studies conducted, most of the precursor phenomena have been observed in this region37,38.

In addition to the magnetic field observations, we also used geomagnetic indices Kp and Dst, which are standard measures of global geomagnetic activity. The Kp index quantifies planetary-scale magnetic disturbances on a quasi-logarithmic scale from 0 (quiet) to 9 (extreme storm), while the Dst index represents the intensity of the equatorial ring current in nanotesla (nT). In this study, we used the auxiliary Kp and Dst data provided directly by the Swarm mission. Although the F10.7 solar radio flux index is commonly used as an indicator of solar activity, it was not included in this study. The F10.7 index does not always correlate directly with ionospheric disturbances caused by geomagnetic storms, and high F10.7 values (typically above 110–150 SFU) can significantly reduce the amount of usable Swarm data41. For these reasons, we focused on Kp and Dst indices, which more directly reflect geomagnetic conditions relevant to distinguishing seismic-related anomalies. Nevertheless, future multi-parameter approaches could include the F10.7 index to provide complementary information.

Data processing

First, the Swarm magnetic field data are downloaded from the Swarm satellite website (https://earth.esa.int/eogateway/missions/swarm) according to the magnitude and date of each earthquake. Subsequently, the following analysis is performed on the data.

Using VFM, all measurements of the magnetic components X (north), Y (east), and Z (vertical) are considered. All the paths passing through the Dobrovolsky region from 10 days before the earthquake until the day of its occurrence are taken into account. First, the derivative with respect to time is calculated for each of these paths. This method eliminates most of the slow fluctuations in the magnetic signal. Then, the best-fit curve, obtained using the cubic-spline algorithm with knot points set at 6 s, is applied to the paths, and we subtract the time series. The residuals obtained are then plotted. The scale remains constant within the range, allowing for comparison of the residuals of one path with another. These residuals are highly sensitive to any abrupt changes in the original signal39. For magnitude estimation, we applied four empirical relations previously introduced by Alimoradi et al. (2024)7. These equations relate anomaly features such as duration (Du), distance from the epicenter (Dist), anomaly amplitude normalized to the orbit average (“energy”, En), and the time of occurrence before the earthquake (Time), to the estimated earthquake magnitude. For each identified anomaly, four parameters were determined: (1) duration (Du), which represents the length of the detected anomaly along the satellite ground track and was converted into seconds according to the Swarm orbital velocity; (2) distance (Dist), the horizontal ground distance between the anomaly location and the earthquake epicenter; (3) anomaly “energy” (En), calculated as the amplitude of the anomaly divided by the average amplitude of the entire half-orbit; and (4) time (Time), the number of days before the earthquake when the anomaly was observed. These values were then substituted into Eqs. (2)– (5) to provide four independent magnitude estimates for each anomalous half-orbit. Since 22 anomalous half-orbits were identified, this procedure yielded 88 magnitude estimates in total.

Ionospheric parameters are influenced by solar activity. Geomagnetic indices, such as Kp and Dst, serve as indicators of solar activity. Generally, geomagnetic disturbances have less impact at mid-latitudes. The criterion for calm geomagnetic indices is usually defined as (Kp < 3, |Dst| < 20 nT)42–44. These indices were applied as criteria to exclude anomalies occurring during disturbed geomagnetic conditions, ensuring that only candidate seismic-related anomalies within quiet periods were considered. For each satellite half-orbit, the average values of these indices were calculated and used to assess whether geomagnetic conditions were sufficiently quiet (Kp < 3, |Dst| < 20 nT) or disturbed. As a result, to distinguish between potential ionospheric seismic disturbances and solar geomagnetic disturbances, Kp and Dst indices were analyzed.

The main difference between the effects of the ionosphere caused by geomagnetic storms and the ionospheric effects potentially caused by seismic events is that the former has a global impact and is observed worldwide, whereas the latter is localized and only observed near the earthquake’s epicenter43,44.
