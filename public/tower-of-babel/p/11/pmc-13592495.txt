Information needs of patients considering epilepsy surgery: A scoping review
Authors: Maren Kimura, Shahab Marzoughi, Ashley Truesdale, Diane L. Lorenzetti, Colin B. Josephson, Derek Payne, Churl‐Su Kwon, Nathalie Jette
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEpilepsy surgery is a recommended treatment for individuals with drug‐resistant epilepsy. However, patients often face significant challenges in decision‐making due to a lack of accessible and high‐quality information.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review to identify the information needs of people considering surgery for epilepsy. Nine databases were searched between 1990 and 2025 to identify research relevant to this review. We included studies that described the information needs of people considering epilepsy surgery and that evaluated available resources to support this decision. Abstract screening, full text review, and data charting were done in duplicate. Risk of bias assessment was completed by one reviewer.ResultsWe identified 2949 studies, of which 18 were included, addressing the following topics: patient informational needs (n = 12), resources designed to support patients through epilepsy surgery decision‐making (n = 5), and resource readability (n = 2). Themes of information needs included discussion of risks and benefits, personalized prognosis, medical assessments needed to determine eligibility, and procedure‐specific outcomes. Six studies identified the importance of involving epilepsy specialists in information sharing, and six highlighted the importance of patient testimonials. Multiple studies suggested current resources lack clarity and consistency, and two identified average readability of available resources to be at or above grade 12 level. Five studies evaluated resources including a mixed reality education tool, a caregiver intensive information program, use of a decision coach, and a patient information program. Providing patient‐centered information can reduce anxiety/depression and increase quality of life for patients and caregivers.SignificanceThis review highlights critical gaps in the quality and accessibility of information for individuals considering epilepsy surgery. Patient testimonials emerged as a powerful tool in supporting informed decision‐making. Future efforts should focus on developing culturally responsive, readable, and patient‐centered resources tailored to diverse epilepsy populations.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy can have profound impacts on an individual's life, with increased risk of premature mortality, poor mental health outcomes, and decreased quality of life. For most people, antiseizure medications can control seizure occurrence, mitigating negative impacts of epilepsy on life expectancy and quality of life. However, one third of people with epilepsy will be drug‐resistant, meaning they will continue to have seizures despite two adequate trials of different antiseizure medications. 1 For patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy, surgery has been shown to promote seizure remission, reduce associated mortality and morbidity, and improve quality of life. 2 , 3 , 4 Increased success of seizure‐free status postsurgery is associated with earlier referrals after diagnosis of drug‐resistance is established. 5 Current guidelines suggest that surgery should be considered as a treatment in these cases, but despite positive outcomes, surgery remains underutilized. 6

The underutilization of epilepsy surgery has been attributed to a variety of reasons, including patient fears regarding surgery risks and a lack of high‐quality resources to inform and guide patients and their caregivers in making these decisions. Interest in epilepsy surgery has been shown to change when additional information on benefits and complications is provided, reinforcing the role of patient education in decision‐making. 7 , 8 Because misconceptions about the risk of surgery are prominent, 9 it is important that information shared with people living with epilepsy is accurate and informed by their needs. Information resources that focus on epilepsy surgery are highly variable in the content provided, readability, and information quality and often fail to address the specific needs of this patient population.

To address the above gaps, the primary aim of this study was to conduct a scoping review to identify the information needs of people considering epilepsy surgery. This includes the specific type of information content, timing and delivery of information, and quality of information desired by patients, caregivers, and family members. Our secondary aims were to identify the barriers and facilitators that exist in deciding to undergo epilepsy surgery and establish what elements are needed when creating a resource to support patient decision‐making about epilepsy surgery. Findings from this scoping review could inform the future development of high‐quality resources that could help patients and their care partners with epilepsy surgery decision‐making.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protocol and registration

This scoping review was registered in Open Science Framework (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/3U25Y). We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐SR) guidelines for reporting. 10 This study did not require ethics approval, because it only analyzes existing published data.

Search and information sources

Our search strategy (Appendix S1) incorporated the following databases: American Psychological Association PsycInfo (Ovid), CINAHL (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature; EBSCO), Education Research Complete (EBSCO), EMBASE (Ovid), MEDLINE (Ovid), SocINDEX (EBSCO), Social Services Abstracts (ProQuest), Sociological Abstracts (ProQuest), and Web of Science (Clarivate). We searched these databases from 1990 to March 2025 to capture studies released after the introduction of clinical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for epilepsy, because MRI has been instrumental in the surgical evaluation process. 11 Furthermore, this captures most studies that have been completed since the creation of the internet, and our literature review suggested the most common place for patients to access resources was online. Our search was not limited by language. Reference lists of included studies were reviewed to identify additional sources.

Eligibility

Our research question was guided by the Population, Concept, Context Framework. We included studies that addressed informational needs of patients or caregivers considering surgical intervention for epilepsy management. Additionally, we included studies that discussed information‐based resources that are available to epilepsy patients regarding surgery. Studies that focused on information needs of health care providers were excluded in addition to abstracts, conference proceedings, protocols, case series, case reports, editorials, opinion pieces, and dissertations.

Selection of evidence

Titles and abstracts were screened in duplicate with disagreements addressed by consensus or using a third reviewer if consensus could not be reached. The full text of eligible studies was also screened in duplicate, with disagreements resolved through consensus or with a third reviewer. All screening was completed by members of the research team using Covidence software.

Data charting process and data items

Data charting was completed by two independent reviewers, with disagreements resolved through consensus. Due to the nature of the scoping review, a narrative synthesis was undertaken. For all included studies, we used calibrated forms for data charting. We collected the following study characteristics: year of publication, data collection years, country, study type, sample size, and demographic characteristics of the study population. When available, we collected specific data on the type of surgical intervention being considered and the type of epilepsy of the patient population. For qualitative studies, data outlining information needs, facilitators and barriers to decision‐making, and descriptions of resources were collected to address information needs of this patient population. Additionally, information on the perceived or objectively scored quality of information resources was collected. For clinical trials, we also collected a description of the resource being evaluated, intervention target, and measured outcome.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the studies. A narrative analysis was done to synthesize the findings.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias assessment was completed by one reviewer for all included studies using design‐appropriate tools. Assessment of study quality was completed by a single reviewer. To enhance consistency, the reviewer followed standardized assessment frameworks as follows. The Risk of Bias in Non‐Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS‐I V2) 12 tool was used to appraise interventional studies, and the published algorithm was followed to assess the overall degree of bias. For cross‐sectional studies, risk of bias was categorized by subheading using the Appraisal Tool for Cross‐Sectional Studies (AXIS). 13 Low risk of bias was assigned when all questions within a given AXIS subheading were adequately addressed. Moderate risk of bias was assigned when one or two subquestions within a subheading were not addressed, and severe risk of bias was assigned when more than two subquestions were not addressed. AXIS scoring was applied as a point per item method, assuming equal weighting of items. Finally, Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) 14 was followed for qualitative studies and reported by each question in the tool to identify sources of bias.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed and is presented in Figure 2A–C. The nine qualitative studies were appraised using the CASP checklist. All qualitative studies demonstrated at least one potential source of bias, most commonly related to insufficient reporting of the relationship between the researchers and participants, which limited assessment of the research team's influence on data collection and interpretation. Five cross‐sectional studies were evaluated using the AXIS‐I tool. All cross‐sectional studies exhibited methodological bias, most frequently due to small, unjustified sample sizes that were identified as limiting data analysis. No study was able to provide information on nonresponders, limiting assessment of response bias. The ROBINS‐I tool was used to assess risk of bias for interventional studies. Primary sources of bias among these studies included failure to identify and control for confounding variables and lack of blinding both participants and researchers to intervention. We were unable to complete risk assessment for one study 27 outlining development of an implementation science model for patient decision‐making as no validated appraisal tool was identified.

Four studies focused on information needs of caregivers for pediatric patients. 17 , 18 , 23 , 29 Themes of informational needs for this population encompassed the same categories. In addition, one study emphasized the need for information on how children are prioritized for surgery and the limitations of assessments and surgical procedures that are related to a pediatric patient's age. 23 Additionally, another study emphasized how providing increased education on epilepsy and management can decrease caregiver burnout and improve quality of life for both caregivers and patients. 31

Across studies that included caregiver perspectives, the psychological burden of caregiving was addressed in five. 17 , 18 , 23 , 25 , 31 One study evaluated the impact of addressing information needs on burnout and found that addressing informational needs of caregivers led to increased quality of life and feelings of empowerment toward providing care. 31

Characteristics of information resources

Ten of the 12 studies that addressed informational needs commented specifically on what is needed in a resource to support decision‐making or how this information should be delivered. 7 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 23 , 25 , 28 , 31 Six studies highlighted the value and need of including testimonials from previous patients. 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 25 , 28 Vicarious experiences were identified as being especially valuable if patients shared the same specific illness or potential procedure. Six studies also mentioned the importance of incorporating clinical data presented by field experts in epilepsy alongside patient testimonials. 19 , 22 , 25 , 27 , 28 , 29 Three studies mentioned the importance of a multimedia format utilizing analog, digital, and video means of delivering information. 15 , 19 , 23 Three studies that included family members as participants suggested there is also a desire for family‐specific interventions. 19 , 25 , 31

Quality of resources

In general, most studies highlighted the heterogeneity of information available for people considering epilepsy surgery and how high‐quality information is usually lacking. Available resources were considered too broad and not specific enough to fit individual patient circumstances. Increased descriptions of the surgical process from preprocedure assessment to potential complications during recovery increased patients' confidence in their decision‐making. 15 Patients who received conflicting information were also driven to seek clarification through online sources including social media 15 , 23 ; however, accuracy of this information was identified as being difficult to discern. 15 , 31 Two studies explored the readability of patient‐focused web‐based resources addressing epilepsy surgery. The first found the Flesch–Kincaid Reading Ease score to be 37.35 (interquartile range [IQR] = 26.80–14.53) and the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level to be 12.20 (IQR = 10.87–13.91) across 82 websites. 20 Similarly, a second study found that the average readability across 49 websites was found to score at or above college level when using seven distinct readability formulas. 24 Additionally, one qualitative focus group study found that patients were overwhelmed by information that was provided and suggested the need for a glossary of terms to be provided when discussions of surgery emerged. 15

Clinical trials

Five clinical trials aimed to evaluate existing resources that were developed to support the decision‐making process surrounding epilepsy surgery. 22 , 26 , 29 , 30 , 31 Three studies addressed intensive information programs, with one addressing patients 26 and two focusing on caregivers. 30 , 31

The information program designed for adult patients found significant reductions in depression and anxiety following use. 26 Another mixed reality tool found that when compared to teaching with a rubber brain model, adult patients had increased comprehensibility and a decrease in presurgical anxiety. 22 The authors hypothesized that the change in presurgical anxiety could have resulted from the use of three‐dimensional MRI imaging in the mixed reality tool, enabling a deeper understanding of brain anatomy and the procedure, ultimately leading to a reduction in fear.

Among the two information programs for caregivers, one showed a similar decrease in anxiety and depression as seen among patients. 30 The other also indicated added benefit through decreased difficulty in managing epilepsy, increased knowledge about epilepsy, and increased quality of life among caregivers following use. 31

A final study focused on pediatric patients through the implementation of a personal decision coach for parents, showing significant improvement in decisional conflict, uncertainty, and preparedness for decision‐making. 29 No studies we identified directly engaged pediatric patients.

DISCUSSION

Our scoping review found 18 studies published between 1990 and 2025 that addressed the information needs of people living with epilepsy deciding whether to undergo surgery for treatment. Of these studies, only 12 addressed the specific information content required to make this decision. Due to the limited number of studies and limited sample sizes, we were unable to derive conclusions about how information needs vary across patient populations (pediatric vs. adult) or by surgery type. Overall, the low number of studies we found suggests greater research is needed to fully understand the information that patients and their caregivers need to feel supported and empowered in making decisions about epilepsy surgery.

The studies found addressing information content were generally consistent with high overlap in the topics that were identified as necessary for making an informed decision. However, existing resources were suggested to be of lower quality, with poor readability scores, which may explain why participants in one study found the information presented to them to be overwhelming and indicated the need for a glossary of terms to aid in understanding the content presented. 15 It is suggested that health materials be written at a grade 6 level 32 to be usable for patients, and both readability studies suggested the majority of epilepsy surgery decision‐making tools were written at a grade 12 level or higher. 20 , 24 Similar findings were highlighted in another readability analysis of health education materials for traumatic brain injury and epilepsy, where of 405 resources only 6.2% were found to be at an appropriate grade level. 33 For information to be presented in a more usable format, it was suggested by some studies that a multimedia format would be helpful, with both printed materials and the opportunity to speak face‐to‐face with epilepsy specialists. 19 , 23 There was also an emphasis on the need to hear from past surgery patients and have ongoing peer support throughout the decision‐making process. 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 25 , 28

Our review also highlights unique information needs among subpopulations. Studies published in the United States specifically commented on the need for information on navigating insurance to aid the decision‐making process about epilepsy surgery. 17 , 18 Furthermore, it was suggested that additional information that recognized child development is needed to support pediatric patients. 23 These findings suggest that information resources should be adapted to the specific sociocultural context they are intended to be used in. Gender differences have also been reported in the medical decision‐making process, with a recent study in Parkinson disease patients suggesting that there may be gender differences in the decision‐making process for deep brain stimulation. 34 These differences were not specifically investigated in relation to epilepsy surgery, but similarities in procedures make it plausible that similar experiences may exist for people with epilepsy. Accordingly, targeted culturally responsive materials may enhance decision‐making; however, the long‐term impact on epilepsy surgery utilization remains uncertain.

Although the included studies span 1999–2024, we were unable to identify meaningful changes in patient information needs over time due to the absence of longitudinal studies and repeated assessments within the same populations. None of the included studies explicitly examined how information needs change in response to advances in surgical techniques and diagnostic technologies. However, recurring themes, particularly the value of patient testimonials and peer experiences, were consistent across timeframes, suggesting that similar informational needs have persisted. In addition to medical advances, the growing role of digital media, social platforms, and educational technologies represents an important gap in the existing literature and should be considered to understand how information delivery methods have influenced decision‐making over time.

Multiple studies highlight different stages patients go through when deciding to undergo surgery. This includes the initial referral, assessments for eligibility, the time needed to decide to undergo surgery, and postoperative recovery. In the development of information‐based resources, it is important that each of these stages is discussed, and support is available throughout all stages. 15 , 17 , 23 , 25 Sources of information should also be tailored to personalized conditions and prognosis. Generalized testimonials or information provided in resources is not always relevant to individual patients and can hinder decision‐making. 15 , 28 Rather, resources are more beneficial when patients can relate to the content shared; for example, speaking with patients of the same age or specific epilepsy diagnosis. 15 , 31 Several prediction tools including nomograms exist to help counsel patients considering epilepsy surgery about surgical risks and outcomes. 6 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 In addition to seizure reduction, these tools focus on specific outcomes of surgery such as memory decline and mood changes that may be identified as more important to specific patients. 35 , 36 , 39

Inconsistency in communication and quality of information provided to patients is not an experience unique to people living with epilepsy. High degrees of variability and resources that are not designed to meet the literacy and numeracy needs of patients considering surgery have been shown to lead to poor satisfaction with care and feelings of being overwhelmed by this decision across a variety of neurosurgical procedures. 40 When interviewed, neurosurgical patients who had unmet information needs and lacked knowledge about their procedure experienced anxiety, confusion, and lack of understanding about their medical care across preoperative, procedural, and recovery stages. 40

The specific information needs identified through this scoping review are aligned with decision‐making needs for other neurosurgical procedures. The need for multimodal information resources in neurosurgery is reinforced by a systematic review showing that the use of multiple modalities for patient education increased patient satisfaction and knowledge while decreasing anxiety. The value of peer support and patient testimonials has also been emphasized across other neurological procedures, including deep brain stimulation for Parkinson disease. 34

There are strengths to this scoping review. First, a robust search strategy using broad search terms within an expansive number of databases was used to capture a large scope of literature addressing information needs of people living with epilepsy. Furthermore, we did not limit the search to any languages or regions of the world. However, this review is not without limitations. First, the number of studies was limited despite our broad search strategy. This, in combination with heterogeneous study populations and small sample sizes, meant we were unable to discriminate between the needs for different patient populations or across varying surgical procedures. Second, despite the large number of resources captured in readability analyses, few studies assessed impact of patient education interventions and information sources. Although interventional studies captured a broad spectrum of outcomes, including depression, anxiety, quality of life, and decisional conflict, these were not applied to all subpopulations. Third, although we did not exclude studies based on language, databases searched primarily indexed English language content, and all included studies were published in English and from high‐resource countries. The geographic representation of included studies does not capture perspectives from other regions with a high burden of epilepsy, such as Africa or Latin America. As our review suggests information needs vary by sociocultural context, our findings may have limited generalizability.

This scoping review revealed limited studies addressing information needs of patients considering epilepsy surgery. However, the 18 studies that were included provide a foundational understanding of the information needs of people considering epilepsy surgery. Our study suggests that resources would be strengthened by incorporating patient testimonials, adapting information to specific sociocultural contexts, and use of multimodal delivery. Future studies should explore how information is best delivered, how resources can be tailored to meet the needs of specific sociocultural populations, and the types of peer support or testimonials that are useful in this decision‐making process. Beyond qualitative studies, additional research should be directed at evaluating how specific resources and their delivery impact decision‐making among specific subpopulations, including adult and pediatric patients as well as caregivers, in both low‐ and high‐resource settings.
