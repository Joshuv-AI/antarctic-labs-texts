Artificial neural networks (ANN) and response surface methodology (RSM) for optimizing antioxidant and α-amylase inhibitory compound extraction from Ballota limbata
Authors: Namra, Haroon Iftikhar, Alev Yüksel Aydar, Dildar Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ballota limbata (syn. Otostegia limbata), a medicinal plant with various ethno-therapeutic uses, was investigated for the recovery of its bioactive compounds using sustainable extraction strategies. This study compared microwave-assisted extraction (MA) and conventional heat-assisted extraction (HA), employing 70% hydro-ethanol as the solvent and optimizing both techniques via response surface methodology (RSM) based on a 3-factor-3-level Box-Behnken design and Artificial Neural Network (ANN), comprising three input variables and one hidden layer. The response factors included total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), DPPH radical scavenging activity (RSA), and α-amylase inhibitory activity (AAI). MA under optimized conditions (power 220 W, time 20 s, solvent-to-solid ratio (SSR) 40 mL/g) yielded TPC, TFC, RSA, and AAI as 3.27 mg gallic acid equivalents/g (GAE/g) dry weight (DW), 2.43 mg rutin equivalents/g (RE/g) DW, 2.16 mg ascorbic acid equivalents/g (AAE/g) DW, and 60.99%, respectively; while HA under optimized conditions (temperature 57 °C, time 150 min, SSR 40 mL/g) produced 3.85 mg GAE/g DW TPC, 2.40 mg RE/g DW TFC, 2.50 mg AAE/g DW RSA, and 86.22% AAI. Statistical modeling showed that MA and HA both were reliable, with low p-values, strong fit statistics, and small differences between predicted and observed values. The yield of bioactives obtained from HA was higher than MAE, but MAE was more efficient in terms of time, which aligns directly with green chemistry principles. Both ANN and RSM performed well; however, ANN had superior predictive accuracy with higher R2, lower root mean square error (MSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). The independent t-test confirmed that there is a significant difference (p < 0.001) between HA and MA when compared for AAI. Thus, the study provided valuable insights into sustainable phytochemical recovery from B. limbata and supported the industrial application of MA for rapid, eco-friendly extraction of medicinal plant constituents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-37738-x.

Introduction

Plants are an important reservoir of structurally diverse compounds that are bioactive in nature and offer sustainable opportunities for the discovery of novel therapeutic agents1. These phytochemicals are often characterized by complex and unique molecular structures that have played a central role in traditional medicines for centuries and continue to serve as an essential resource in modern drug discovery2.

Ballota limbata Benth. (syn. Otostegia limbata), a member of the Lamiaceae family, has attracted increasing ethnopharmacological and phytochemical interest. This plant is widely distributed across South and Central Asia, North Africa, and the Himalayan belt, especially in the northern hilly regions of Pakistan3.

In ethnopharmacology, the leaves of B. limbata have been used to manage different diseases, including eye inflammation, infections, wounds, and have antioxidant, antiseptic, and nutritive potential4–8. Many important secondary metabolites are present in B. limbata, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, and terpenoids. Limbatolides A–C and flavonol glycosides and flavones have also been identified and isolated from its roots and stems 3–6,9−13. Pharmacological effects, including anti-ulcer, antispasmodic, and sedative effects, are associated with these phytochemicals. Although a lot of literature is available, the optimal conditions for the extraction of phytochemicals from B. limbata leaves remain insufficiently explored14.

In the field of natural product research, the development of an efficient extraction method is of great importance. Commonly used conventional methods, such as maceration and Soxhlet extraction, have many drawbacks, including excessive solvent use, long extraction time, and thermal degradation of the heat-sensitive phytochemicals15–17. Therefore, alternative techniques such as ultrasound- and microwave-assisted extraction are investigated with growing interest18–20. These new methods are useful in saving solvent and extraction time, improving efficacy, and preserving sensitive phytochemicals from thermal degradation.

Microwave-assisted extraction (MA) uses microwaves for heating the sample. It increases the pressure inside the cell matrix that helps in improving mass transfer, making the overall extraction process efficient. It is a promising alternative to conventional heating methods that depend on external heating, which can degrade thermolabile natural products during the extraction process16,17,21. The extraction efficacy also depends on the solvent system used for the purpose. Hydro-ethanolic solvents are commonly used for bioactive compound extraction from plant sources. By intelligently tailoring the water-ethanol ratio, the polarity of the hydro-ethanolic solvent can be optimized for efficient extraction of a wide range of phytochemicals22,23. Due to these reasons, hydro-ethanol is preferred over absolute ethanol. Numerous studies support this trend. For instance, a recent study compared hydro-ethanol of various ratios with neat ethanol and water and found hydroethanol more effective in extracting bioactive compounds24.

Besides technique and extraction medium, systematic optimization of extraction conditions is also important to make the extraction process as efficient as possible. In this connection, response surface methodology (RSM) is considered highly effective. It is a statistical tool that can efficiently be used to study the impact of different independent variables on the extraction25. Unlike traditional one-factor-at-a-time approaches, RSM enables precise identification of optimal extraction conditions while minimizing experimental runs, time, and resource consumption26–30. Another technique that is gaining increased attention due to rapid developments in the field of artificial intelligence (AI) is what is known as the artificial neural network (ANN). It is a computationally based learning system that aims to mimic the human brain’s neurological processing capabilities. As a data-driven self-adaptive technique, an ANN requires minimal presumptions regarding the process being studied. The value of an ANN lies in its capacity to learn and generalize the behavior of any complicated and nonlinear process31,32. In literature, ANNs have been used to model a number of food processes with success33,34.

As noted above, despite its well-documented ethnomedicinal applications and phytochemical diversity, limited efforts have been directed toward optimizing the extraction of bioactive metabolites from B. limbata leaves. To address this gap, the present study aimed to systematically compare MA and HA using a hydro-ethanolic solvent system and RSM and ANN optimization. The goals were to illustrate the potential of MA and HA methods for the effective extraction of bioactive compounds from B. limbata leaves with systematic optimization. The success of the methods was monitored based on various response factors, including total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), radical scavenging activity (RSA), and alpha-amylase inhibitory activity (AAI) of the leaf extracts of B. limbata. Beyond methodological optimization, this investigation also holds significant biomedical relevance, as it contributes to the identification and development of novel, safe, and efficacious plant-derived bioactive agents for the management of oxidative stress–mediated disorders and metabolic diseases such as diabetes mellitus. By elucidating the antioxidant capacity and α-amylase inhibitory potential of B. limbata leaf extracts obtained under optimized extraction conditions, the study supports the growing paradigm of utilizing phytochemicals as complementary or alternative therapeutic interventions. Such botanicals may offer multifaceted mechanisms of action, improved biocompatibility, and reduced adverse effects compared to synthetic drugs, thereby reinforcing their potential role in preventive nutrition, functional foods, and evidence-based phytopharmaceutical development. Thus, the study aimed to establish an efficient and reproducible extraction protocol, thereby providing a foundation for future pharmacological and nutraceutical applications of B. limbata leaves with optimal bioactive compound recovery.

Methods and materials

Chemical reagents

All reagents and solvents used in this study were of analytical grade. Aluminum chloride (AlCl3), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), and sodium nitrite (NaNO2) were procured from Merck (Germany). Folin–Ciocalteu (FC) phenol reagent was obtained from Scharlau (Spain). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), gallic acid, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), sodium chloride (NaCl), rutin, sodium dihydrogen phosphate (NaH2PO4), disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), starch, and alpha-amylase enzyme (of Aspergillus oryzae origin) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany).

Sample Preparation

The fresh plant sample of B. limbata was obtained from Abbottabad district, Pakistan, and was identified by a qualified botanist, Dr. Abdul Majid, Hazara University, Mansehra, Pakistan and a specimen of the plant has been placed in the Department of Chemistry, F. C. College, Lahore, Pakistan, under the voucher FCD-1291. The leaves were separated from the twigs and dried in the shade for several days. These dried leaves were pulverized using an electric grinder, and the resulting material was sieved through a 100-mesh screen to ensure uniform particle size. The powdered plant material was stored in airtight polyethylene bags and stored under refrigeration until further experimental use.

Single-factor experiments

Single-factor experiments were conducted to find the influence of individual extraction parameters (independent variables). In these experiments, one factor varied while maintaining all other variables constant. This helped determine appropriate ranges for each factor, which were applied in the RSM optimization design. Single-factor studies were performed for both HA and MA.

For HA, conducted in a shaking incubator, 1 g of leaf powder and 30 mL of hydro-ethanolic solution were added to a 250-mL conical flask. The effects of four key parameters were investigated: (i) extraction time (30–150 min), (ii) extraction temperature (30–60 °C), (iii) solvent-to-solid ratio (20–40 mL/g), and (iv) percentage of ethanol in hydro-ethanol (50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, and 100% v/v). During these experiments, the shaking speed was kept at 200 rpm.

For MA, the factors evaluated included (i) microwave power (220–660 W), (ii) irradiation time (20–100 s), (iii) solvent-to-solid ratio (20–40 mL/g), and (iv) ethanol percentage in hydro-ethanol as described for HA. While working in microwave treatment of the samples, precautions must be taken, as in the cases of prolonged irradiation, boiling may occur, which can easily be controlled by giving a short interval.

After extraction, the mixtures were cooled and filtered. The solvent was evaporated using a rotary evaporator. The concentrated extracts were then collected in pre-weighted vials and weighed to find the extraction yield. The percentage yield (% w/w) was determined relative to the initial mass of the dry plant material used for extraction.

Design of experiment for optimization (RSM and ANN)

Based on the outcomes of the one-factor-at-a-time experiments (OFAT), factors were selected for optimization. For RSM, a Box-Behnken Design (BBD) was generated on the software (Design Expert version 13, Stat-Ease, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, US) that contained 17 experimental runs with 5 repeated runs at the center point to check accuracy and consistency. To minimize bias, all experiments were performed in random order. The optimization focused on four main response factors related to antioxidant and enzyme inhibition activities: total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), DPPH radical scavenging activity (RSA), and α-amylase inhibitory activity (AAI).

The ANN model in this study was built using the Box-Behnken Design with 17 experimental runs and three input variables. The experimental data were divided into three sets: for training (11 samples), testing (3 samples), and validation (3 samples). The models’ parameters were adjusted by using the training set so the network could perform calculations. The test set was used to evaluate a model’s ability to predict unseen data, while the validation set was used to assess network stability and prevent overfitting. To train the network, the Levenberg–Marquardt optimization algorithm was employed in conjunction with the back propagation learning method. The ANN model was developed as a multilayer perceptron using MATLAB software (version R2025b, US). Based on the results obtained from the experiment, it was determined that a Feedforward Backpropagation algorithm separate ANN models with a 3–10–1 architecture was developed for each response (TPC, TFC, RSA, and AAI) shown in Fig. 1.

Mathematical modelling and statistical analysis

According to RSM, a second-order polynomial regression model was used to explain how the independent variables relate to the measured responses in the three-factor experimental design35.

ANOVA (analysis of variance) was used to find the adequacy of a model and the statistical significance of each term. Different interactions of independent variables, including linear and quadratic effects on responses, were examined. Model performance and goodness-of-fit were assessed using p-value, F-value, coefficient of determination (R²), adjusted R², and standard error36. The model was considered adequate if the lack-of-fit was not significant (p > 0.05) and the coefficient of determination was close to 1.00, indicating high predictive accuracy. The predictive abilities of both the RSM and ANN strategies were compared using a separate dataset of experiments based on RMSE (root mean square error), MAE (mean absolute error), and R2 for each output parameter. An independent t-test was used to compare the means of TPC, TFC, RSA, and AAI under optimum conditions obtained by HA and MA (IBM, SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States).

Assay for total phenolic content (TPC)

The measurement of TPC present in B. limbata extracts from HA and MA was done using the Folin-Ciocalteu method described by Martins et al. 37, with some minor changes. In summary, 100 µL of the extract or reference standard (gallic acid) was combined with 2 mL of deionized water, then 200 µL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent was added. After 8 minutes’ incubation at room temperature, 2 mL of sodium carbonate solution was mixed. The absorbance of the mixture was measured at 765 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer. The gallic acid calibration curve straight line equation (y = 0.0025x + 0.0579; R² = 0.9917) was used to calculate TPC as mg gallic acid equivalents/g (GAE/g) dry weight (DW).

Assay for total flavonoid content (TFC)

For the estimation of total flavonoid content (TFC) of B. limbata leaf extracts, an aluminum chloride colorimetric method described by Marinova et al. (2005)38, was used with a few adjustments. Briefly, 1.0 mL of plant extract or standard (rutin) was added to a 10-mL volumetric flask and diluted with 4 mL of distilled water. After dilution, 300 µL of sodium nitrite (NaNO2) solution was added to the mixture, which was then incubated for 5 min. After the incubation, 300 µL of aluminum chloride (AlCl3) solution was added, and the mixture was incubated for 6 min at 25 ℃. After the second incubation, 2.0 mL of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was added, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 15 min. By adding distilled water, the volume was made up to the mark. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 510 nm. The rutin calibration curve straight line equation (y = 0.0012x – 0.0049; R2 = 0.9985) was used to calculate TFC as mg RE/g dry weight (DW).

Assay for radical scavenging activity (RSA)

The free radical scavenging activity of the extracts was measured using the DPPH assay, following the method described by Brand-Williams et al. (1995)39, with few changes. A stock solution of DPPH was prepared by dissolving 15.8 g of it in 100 mL of methanol, and a working solution was prepared from the stock solution by diluting 5 mL of it with 15 mL of methanol. Then, 0.4 mL of plant extract or standard solution was combined with 3.0 mL of the DPPH working solution in a test tube. The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min to prevent photo degradation. The absorbance was then recorded at 517 nm against the reagent control. Radical scavenging activity (RSA) was expressed as milligrams of ascorbic acid equivalent per gram of dry extract (mg AAE/g DW) using ascorbic acid calibration straight line equation (y = 1.2354x − 9.214; R2 = 0.9988).

Assay for α-amylase inhibitory activity (AAI)

The inhibitory activity of the extracts against α-amylase was determined following the method of Saeed et al. 40,41, with minor modifications. Briefly, stock solutions of 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS), phosphate buffer (pH 6.7), soluble starch, and α-amylase enzyme were prepared fresh before the assay. For the reaction, 500 µL of extract solution was mixed with 500 µL (1 mg/100 mL; ≥150 units/mg) of enzyme solution in a glass test tube and pre-incubated at ambient temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of soluble starch solution was added as the substrate, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 3 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 1 mL of DNS reagent, followed by heating in a boiling water bath (100 °C) for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture was diluted with 9 mL of distilled water. Controls were prepared using distilled water in place of the extract, while blanks contained extract and enzyme without starch to account for background absorbance. The absorbance of samples, controls, and blanks was measured at 540 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer, and the percentage α-amylase inhibitory activity was calculated using the reported Eq. 41. The acarbose standard was used for comparison, and its calibration curve equation was y = 0.3824x + 29.628; R2 = 0.9411. Results and discussion

With the aim of discovering a green and efficient method for obtaining therapeutically active extracts from the leaves of the medicinal herb B. limbata, two techniques were investigated in the current study, including heat-assisted (HA) and microwave-assisted (MA) extraction. Their results and discussion are given below.

Heat-assisted extraction (HA)

HA single-factor experiment results

For single-factor experiments, TPC was selected since phenolics not only include simple phenols such as phenolic acids but also flavonoids. Moreover, phenolics, including flavonoids, have a considerable contribution to antioxidant and other bioactivities. Thus, TPC has an overall reflection of the bioactivities assessed in this study. Single-factor experimental results of the HA are shown in Fig. 2. The effect of four factors was studied on the extraction of phenolic compounds from B. limbata leaf extracts. Shaking speed was kept constant at 200 rpm.

Fig. 2Results of HA single-factor experiment demonstrating the influence of individual variables on phenolic compound extraction.

Results of HA single-factor experiment demonstrating the influence of individual variables on phenolic compound extraction.

Effect of temperature

During extraction, temperature exhibited a strong influence on TPC yield as shown in Fig. 2(a). Phenolic recovery increased as the temperature rose from 30 °C to 40 °C, where the maximum was observed. This increase can be attributed to enhanced solvent diffusivity, reduced viscosity, and improved solubility of phenolic compounds at moderate heating, which facilitates their release from disrupted plant cell walls. Ghafoor et al. (2019) reported the same effect on plum and mahelab fruits, in which total phenolic content sufficiently increased with temperature increase42. However, a decline in TPC was observed beyond 40 °C, most likely due to the thermal degradation of phenolic constituents, a phenomenon commonly occurring at high temperatures as previously reported by Antony and Farid (2017)43. These findings suggested 40 °C to be an optimal temperature for phenolic extraction from B. limbata leaves using 70% hydro-ethanol solvent.

Effect of time

Extraction time strongly affected TPC yield, as shown in Fig. 2(b). At shorter extraction times, between 30 and 90 min, phenolic release appeared to be incomplete, which might be because of the slow diffusion rate of phenolics from the plant matrix to the hydro-ethanol solvent. The highest TPC yield appeared at 120 min, showing that this duration allowed enough time for the solvent to penetrate and dissolve the phenolics in it. At longer extraction time (150 min), TPC decreased, which might be because prolonged heating can degrade phytochemicals, as previously reported by Tan et al. (2013) that TPC in Lawsonia inermis stem was decreased on prolonged heating44. Based on these findings, an extraction time of about 120 min is optimal under these conditions.

Effect of solvent-to-solid ratio (SSR)

Figure 2(c) illustrates that SSR is a highly influential factor on TPC. The phenolic content at low SSR (10–20 mL/g) was low. This effect was also noticed by Voung et al. (2013), who found that the solvent ratio affected the TPC of Carica papaya leaves45. With the increase in SSR from 30 to 40 mL/g, the extraction efficacy improved, which might be because more solvent was available to facilitate the draining of bioactives. When the solvent ratio increased from 40 mL/g, the increase in TPC slowed and levelled off at 50 mL/g, likely due to equilibrium. The same kind of trend was also reported by Vo et al. (2025) for Elsholtzia ciliata, where TPC reached equilibrium after 1:60 g/mL 46. Thus, SSR has to be optimized as using a large solvent amount is not practical for large-scale processes because of cost and saturation issues.

Effect of hydro-ethanol ratio

As Fig. 2(d) demonstrates, the extraction yield of TPC was highly dependent on the composition of hydro-ethanol. The TPC yield (2.7 mg GAE/g DW) peaked at a water-to-ethanol ratio of approximately 30–40%, confirming that a specific solvent polarity is optimal for extracting an appropriate range of phenolic compounds from B. limbata leaves. This is due to the varying polarities of the target compounds themselves. A higher water content in the solvent (lower ethanol percentage) favors the extraction of more polar compounds, such as phenolic acids and glycosides. However, as ethanol concentration increases, the overall polarity of the medium decreases, which can improve the solubility of less polar compounds, such as certain flavonoid aglycones47. Therefore, the peak in the graph represents the ideal balance of polarity for the most efficient extraction of all the bioactive compounds present in the plant sample.

In HA of B. limbata leaves, phenolic and flavonoid recovery showed clear dependence on both solvent-to-solid ratio (SSR) and extraction temperature. Microwave-assisted extraction (MA)

MA single-factor experiments

The results of the MA single-factor experiments are exhibited in Fig. 3.

Fig. 3Results of the MA single-factor experiment demonstrating the effect of independent variables on the extraction of total phenolic content.

Results of the MA single-factor experiment demonstrating the effect of independent variables on the extraction of total phenolic content.

MA-Effect of microwave power

The yield of bioactive compounds depended strongly on microwave power, as shown in Fig. 3(a). Higher power improved extraction, which might be due to improved mass transfer. It can be inferred that microwaves caused fast internal heating, which led to superheating and pressure inside plant cells, breaking cell walls and releasing bioactive compounds48. Beyond the optimal power of around 330 W, the TPC yield declined. This likely happened because too much heat can degrade the sensitive phenolic compounds, reducing the benefits of higher extraction49. Thus, the peak in the graph shows the point where extraction is maximized without causing too much thermal damage to the compounds.

MA-Effect of microwave irradiation time

From Fig. 3(b), it can be inferred that irradiation time affects TPC. Within the first 20 to 40 s, an increase in time sufficiently increases the yield of TPC, likely because microwaves can quickly heat the sample and increase internal pressure, leading to the release of bioactives from plant cells into the solvent50. After about 40 s, the TPC yield starts decreasing because after the optimal extraction point, further exposure to microwaves starts degrading heat-sensitive phytochemicals. This suggests that the optimal irradiation time is 40 s, after which degradation becomes faster than extraction, so the final extract contains less TPC51.

MA-Effect of solvent-to-solid ratio (SSR)

Figure 3(c) illustrates that the extraction efficiency was strongly influenced by SSR. A significant rise in TPC yield was observed when SSR was increased from 10 to 50 mL/g, highlighting the interaction between solvent volume and extraction efficiency. At higher solvent ratios, better mass transfer happens because an appropriate amount of solvent is available to solubilize the target biomolecules52. This steep gradient promotes the efficient diffusion and dissolution of bioactive compounds into the solvent.

MA-Effect of hydro-ethanol ratio

Figure 3(d) shows that, similar to HA in Fig. 2(d), MA resulted in a non-linear relationship between the hydro-ethanolic ratio and TPC yield. More water in the solvent helped extract polar phenolics, but in MA, effective extraction also relied on how well the solvent absorbed microwave energy and produced heat. The sharp increase in yield at higher ethanol concentrations suggests that ethanol’s ability to absorb microwaves and break down cell structures played a key role in releasing phenolic compounds, along with solvent polarity53. Highest yield (1.45 mg GAE/g DW) was obtained in 80% ethanol in hydro ethanol.

These single-factor analyses helped determine the most favorable parameters for the extraction process. Based on these findings, three sets of conditions were shortlisted for further experimental design. This selection provided a strong foundation for achieving higher efficiency and guiding process optimization.

Fit statistics of both techniques (HA and MA).

In evaluating the model performance for both HA and MA, several statistical parameters provide insights into accuracy, reliability, and predictive power.

The standard deviation (Std. Dev.) is used to check the variation of individual data points from the average. Lower standard deviation demonstrates that the experimental data points are closer to the model’s predictions, ensuring higher precision. The coefficient of variation (C.V. %) expresses the standard deviation as a percentage of the mean. Lower values of C.V.% show that the model is reliable and consistent. For biological and chemical studies, a C.V. % less than 10 is generally considered desirable25.

The coefficient of determination (R²) is used to check the variation in the model’s data. If R² is close to its maximum value, that is, 1, the model fits the data well. For a more balanced measurement, the number of predictors (adjusted R²) is also considered. Predicted R² is used to check how well the model can predict new data. When the values of predicted and adjusted R2 are close, it means that the model is likely reliable and can handle new information effectively54.

Adequate Precision (Adeq Precision) is used to measure the signal-to-noise ratio. When this value is greater than 4, the model is considered strong enough to proceed reliably through the experimental design space55.

Collectively, these parameters confirm that the developed models are valid. These are also helpful in guiding the design process, supporting in making informed decisions56,57.

There is a positive interaction A×C: the beneficial effect of SSR on TPC becomes larger at longer extraction times (or conversely, the time effect on TPC is amplified when SSR is higher). Mechanistically, more solvent (higher SSR) increases concentration gradient and mass transfer, and when combined with longer contact time, the solvent has more opportunity to leach phenolics from the matrix, hence the synergy25. Thus, maximize SSR to raise TPC, particularly when using longer extraction times, but watch solvent usage and downstream concentration costs.

In TFC-HA, SSR is the only significant term: raising SSR increases flavonoid yield. Moderate temperature and time were favorable within the studied range.

RSA-HA is also driven mainly by SSR, consistent with RSA being correlated with the extractable phenolic and flavonoid pool. No significant temperature or time linear term remained, indicating antioxidant capacity is captured largely by the compounds extracted when SSR increases.

For AAI-HA, SSR (A) and temperature (B) both have large, positive effects: increasing these increases the percent inhibition. This suggests enzyme-inhibitory constituents are more efficiently extracted with greater solvent volume and benefit from moderate heating that increases solubility/availability. Time (C) has a small but significant negative effect: longer heating reduces inhibition, which might be due to thermal degradation or structural modification of specific inhibitory molecules.

Thus, for HA, it would be effective to maximize SSR and use moderate-to-high temperature within the safe range, but overly long extractions should be avoided.

In TPC-MA, SSR (A) is again the main positive driver like HA. Time (C) exerts a small negative linear effect: longer microwave irradiation slightly decreases TPC, consistent with thermal/oxidative degradation under microwave energy. Thus, it is suggested to increase SSR to raise TPC in MA, but unnecessarily long irradiation times should be avoided.

In TFC-MA, SSR (A) is the sole significant predictor; power and time were not significant here. The practical implication is that SSR optimization is the lever for flavonoids under microwave conditions. RSA-MA also tracks SSR (A) closely, and phenolic/flavonoid extraction (driven by SSR) appears to govern radical scavenging capacity. In AAI-MA, SSR (A) and microwave power (B) both have negative coefficients: increasing SSR reduces the extraction of compounds with alpha-amylase inhibitory activity. The higher microwave energy might degrade or modify inhibitor structures. Time (C) did not appear as a significant linear term for AAI in MA. The practical implication is to maximize AAI in MA; lower SSR should be used, and high microwave power should be avoided.

Thus, SSR is the most consistent and powerful factor for TPC, TFC, and RSA in both HA and MA. This is possibly because a higher solvent volume increases mass transfer, solubility, and contact with intracellular pools of phenolics/flavonoids. AAI behaves differently in the two techniques. Under HA, the extraction of α-amylase inhibitory compounds benefits from higher SSR and moderate temperature, but suffers from a long time, suggesting that extraction of stable inhibitory species favored by heat and solvent, but over-exposure causes loss. Under MA, the extraction of α-amylase inhibitors declines with increasing SSR and power, indicating (a) dilution effects (higher SSR), and (b) power-induced degradation or structural alteration of specific inhibitors under microwave irradiation.

Absence of B (temp in HA and power in MA) in antioxidant models (TPC/TFC/RSA) implies that temperature/power were not significant linear predictors for those measures in the studied ranges. SSR dominated antioxidant metrics; energy input mainly affects enzyme inhibitor extraction (AAI). Interaction A×C in HA TPC points to synergy: more solvent plus longer time produces disproportionately larger TPC gains, but it is notable that too long (C large) reduces AAI, so a trade-off exists between maximizing TPC and preserving inhibitory activity.

Comparison of RSM and ANN for MA

For microwave-assisted extraction (MA), the observed experimental means were slightly different from predicted values for all responses, as shown in Table 3. The benefits of ANN were not limited to lower error rates; its model accuracy translated directly into higher confidence for all critical metrics associated with bioactive compound recovery, especially when maximizing antioxidant and inhibitory capacities in plant extracts. Additionally, the ANN’s learning algorithm enabled nuanced mapping of nonlinear variable effects, which is crucial for optimizing extraction time, power input, and solvent-to-solid ratios in MA workflows. These practical advantages echo broader meta-analyses and recent literature, not only validating ANN’s technical superiority but also confirming its reliability for predicting outcomes in phytochemical extraction processes, including process control and industrial scaling. Comparison of HA and MA

The present research shows that MA is more efficient for extracting bioactives from B. limbata than HA and is more sustainable because it reduces extraction time and lowers energy use. Therefore, it can be inferred that MA is a better alternative to traditional thermal extraction methods as it is a more sustainable and scalable approach for obtaining antioxidant-rich phytochemicals. Both techniques used the same solid-to-solvent ratio, but MA was more effective because of quick heating and cell disruption that improved mass transfer, while also preventing compound breakdown and reducing solvent waste by avoiding prolonged heat exposure63.

Under optimized conditions, MA and HA showed good performance in recovering bioactive compounds from B. limbata leaves. The observed values for MA were 3.27 mg GAE/g (TPC), 2.43 mg RE/g (TFC), 2.16 mg AAE/g (RSA), and 60.99% (AAI), whereas for HA, they were 3.85 mg GAE/g, 2.40 mg RE/g, 2.50 mg AAE/g, and 86.22%, respectively. The results show that HA improved TPC, RSA, and AAI more than MA, but both methods had similar results for TFC. Although HA produced higher bioactivity yields, MA required much less time, reaching similar extraction efficiency in just seconds. These findings make MA a more efficient and sustainable alternative to conventional methods for the recovery of bioactive compounds from plants and fit with green chemistry principles64. Conclusively, MA is a promising and environmentally friendly method for extracting plant chemicals, which has been demonstrated by a growing number of studies41,65,66. The importance of the current study also stems from exploring practical applications of bioactive botanicals for the treatment of oxidative stress and diabetes, which are affecting a growing number of populations around the globe and require effective and safer alternatives to synthetic drugs67.

The α-amylase inhibitory potency, expressed as IC₅₀, followed the order acarbose (0.47 mg/mL) < HA (0.69 mg/mL) < MA (1.01 mg/mL), indicating strong enzyme inhibition by the plant extracts, particularly under heat-assisted extraction. These results showed that the IC₅₀ (mg/mL) values of both MA and HA extracts were higher than that of the standard drug acarbose, indicating lower inhibitory potency. This outcome is expected, as acarbose is a purified compound, whereas plant extracts are complex mixtures containing both active and inactive constituents. Notably, the HA extract exhibited a lower IC₅₀ value than the MA extract, suggesting a comparatively stronger α-amylase inhibitory potential, likely due to more efficient extraction or stabilization of enzyme-inhibitory phenolic constituents under conventional heating conditions.

Conclusions

This present study optimized and compared microwave-assisted extraction (MA) and heat-assisted extraction (HA) for the recovery of compounds from Ballota limbata leaves using hydro-ethanol. Both methods were validated under optimal conditions, which were 220 W for 20 s with a solvent-to-solid ratio of 40 mL/g for MA, while heating at 57 °C for 150 min with the same ratio 40 mL/g for HA. Although HA gave higher values for TPC, RSA, and AAI, MA recovered similar amounts of TFC. Under optimized conditions, MA achieved flavonoid yields comparable to HA in a fraction of the much less time, supporting its potential as a green and scalable extraction technique. Its quick process and lower heat help protect the environment and keep compounds intact. Commercial-level use of MA could improve sustainability, increase output, and lower costs, supporting eco-friendly natural product recovery. The overall higher R values for all outputs were greater than 0.98, indicating that the ANN models are more reliable in understanding the relationship between the input variables and the outputs. The points closely follow ideal linear trends in all graphs, confirming accurate predictions with minor deviations. To confirm MA as a sustainable, industrially viable extraction technology and to establish the broader reliability of the modeling approach, future work should expand the experimental design to enable external ANN validation, employ HPLC-MS for compound-specific identification, determine IC50 values for alpha-amylase inhibition, and conduct a full life-cycle assessment with energy metrics.
