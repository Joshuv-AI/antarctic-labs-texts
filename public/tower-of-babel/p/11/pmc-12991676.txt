Predictors of ineffective platelet transfusion in surgical ICU: A retrospective study
Authors: Jin Fang Chen, Wei Wei Liu, Zhen Hai Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Platelet transfusion is commonly utilized in the surgical intensive care unit (ICU) to provide hemostatic support for patients with severe thrombocytopenia or bleeding. Ineffective platelet transfusion is described as either the expected platelet count increase or insufficient hemostasis following transfusion. This study sought to identify risk factors and assess their predictive value in improving patient outcomes. This was an observational, retrospective study. Clinical information was gathered from 302 platelet transfusion episodes that occurred in the surgical ICU at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, between June and December 2022. Based on the clinical bleeding improvement indices (drainage volume, hemoglobin level) and the 24 hour-corrected count increment, the patients were divided into effective and ineffective groups. Binomial logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors for ineffective transfusion after univariate analysis was screened for significant variables (P < .05). The predictive performance of these factors was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Patients were separated into 2 groups: effective (n = 192) and ineffective (n = 110). Baseline comparisons demonstrated significant differences in surgery frequency (≤1 vs >1), liver disease, intraoperative bleeding (≤1600 vs >1600), procalcitonin (PCT) level, C-reactive protein level, splenomegaly, and age (≥65 vs <65 years) (P < .05). The total red blood cell transfusion volume and other indicators showed no intergroup differences (P > .05). Univariate logistic regression revealed a significant association (P < .05) between platelet transfusion refractoriness and these variables, including the total red blood cell transfusion volume. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified surgeries (>1) (odds ratio (OR) = 1.997, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.196–3.335; P = .008), splenomegaly (OR = 3.912, 95% CI: 2.277–6.720; P < .001), and PCT (OR = 1.030, 95% CI: 1.013–1.047; P < .001) as independent risk variables (P < .05). ROC curve analysis revealed that the area under the curve of the model was 0.706 (95% CI: 0.645–0.766), with a Youden index of 0.346, corresponding to an optimal cutoff value of 0.338, sensitivity of 66.40%, and specificity of 68.20%. The number of surgeries (>1), splenomegaly, and PCT level were independent risk factors for ineffective platelet transfusion, and the constructed model had moderate predictive efficacy, providing a reference for early clinical identification and intervention.

1. Introduction

Perioperative platelet transfusion is a crucial supportive treatment for surgical intensive care unit (ICU) patients. It was especially significant in patients with substantial intraoperative bleeding, thrombocytopenia, or compromised hemostatic function. It is essential to preserve perioperative hemostasis, reduce the risk of severe bleeding, and enhance the treatment outcomes.[1] However, inefficient platelet transfusion remains a major clinical problem in this group; it is defined as failure to achieve the desired increase in circulating platelet count or sufficient hemostatic impact within 24 hours post-transfusion.[2] The reported prevalence ranges from 22 to 50%, and it is strongly associated with greater surgical bleeding, extended ICU stay, higher medical expenditures, and a poor prognosis, putting a strain on perioperative treatment and patient recovery.[3,4]

Immune and non-immune mechanisms work together to cause ineffective platelet transfusions.[5] Alloimmunization from repeated transfusions and platelet-specific antibodies (human platelet antigen antibodies and human leukocyte antigen antibodies) are immune factors that decrease efficacy and accelerate platelet clearance.[6] Non-immune factors, which are associated with the pathophysiology of critical illness and include hypersplenism, infection, fever, drugs, massive hemorrhage, and systemic inflammation, predominate in surgical ICU patients. In particular, hypersplenism increases splenic platelet sequestration/destruction; infection/inflammation causes consumption and functional impairment; fever modifies metabolism/survival; and some medications disrupt aggregation/adhesion.[6–9] These elements work in concert to cause inefficient platelet transfusion.[5]

Against the backdrop of empirically dominated clinical platelet transfusion and the lack of systematic research into immune/non-immune factors driving ineffective platelet transfusion in surgical ICU patients who present with high trauma, severe inflammation, and complex comorbidities, in contrast to existing single-factor or general ICU-focused studies.[10] This study tried to identify and evaluate perioperative risk variables for transfusion refractoriness, providing a solid foundation for early detection of vulnerable patients and the optimization of tailored transfusion methods.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and study population

This retrospective case-controlled study was carried out from June 2022 to December 2022 in the surgical ICU at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The study included patients who received platelet transfusions in the surgical ICU. There were 113 patients in total, and 302 platelet transfusion episodes were noted. It should be noted that a single patient receiving several therapeutic platelet doses simultaneously was counted as one episode. The 302 cases were divided into 2 groups by this outcome: the effective group (n = 192) and the ineffective group (n = 110).

Inclusion criteria: received apheresis platelet transfusion in the surgical ICU, underwent surgical treatment during hospitalization, and complete clinical data were available.

Exclusion criteria: admission to the surgical ICU but not platelet transfusion; no surgical treatment during the index hospitalization; and incomplete clinical data.

2.2. Sample size calculation and bias control

2.2.1. Sample size calculation

The events per variable technique is currently the most commonly applied strategy, with “events” denoting the dependent variable’s less frequently occurring subcategory.[11] Based on pertinent studies, platelet transfusion inefficiency among patients in the ICU is around 35%.[12] In this study, the significance level (ɑ) was set at 0.05 (two-tailed) and power was set at 80%. After the initial univariate analysis, 8 independent variables were included in the regression analysis, with the events per variable fixed at 11. The event group required 88 cases, calculated as 11 and multiplied by 8. The total sample size (including both the control and event groups) was 251 cases, derived from 88 cases divided by 35%. Because some samples were excluded due to insufficient clinical data, the study allowed for a 20% sample loss rate to ensure that the final effective sample size was at least 251 cases. The total number of patients needed was approximately 314, computed as 251, divided by (1–20%). The final sample included 302 patients, which met the analysis requirements: 110 in the event group and 192 in the control group, with a roughly 1:2 ratio between the 2 groups. Additionally, there was no need to handle missing data, as samples with incomplete clinical data were excluded during the study design phase in line with the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

2.2.2. Bias control measures

This study used several ways to lower bias: Strict inclusion/exclusion criteria were set for ICU patients. This helped reduce selection bias; A digital health record system was used to extract objective indicators. These included corrected post-transfusion hemoglobin (Hb) levels, which were determined by deducting the red blood cell transfusion volume within 24 hours, as well as the pre- and post-transfusion platelet counts. The aim of this action was to reduce information bias; Data on possible confounding factors were collected synchronously. Leukocyte reduction filters and antibiotics have been used; and After univariate analysis, parameters with P < .05 were included in the logistic regression equation. This helped control for confounding bias.

2.3. Indicators and evaluation

2.3.1. Calculation of efficacy indicators

Laboratory parameters and bleeding improvement metrics were the 2 categories of indicators used to assess the efficacy of platelet transfusion.[9] Laboratory criteria defined refractoriness as 1-h corrected count increment (CCI) < 7.5, 24-hour CCI < 4.5, or 1-hour percentage platelet recovery < 20%.[13,14] Hb level and drainage volume were the 2 primary bleeding improvement metrics. The efficacy of platelet transfusion was evaluated in this study using 24-hour CCI in conjunction with the bleeding improvement indicators (drainage volume and Hb fluctuations) mentioned above.[9] The following are the standardized quantitative definitions of the indicators are as follows:

2.3.1.1. Calculation of 24 hour-CCI[15]

The therapeutic dosage had a platelet count of 2.5 × 1011. The DuBois formula was used to calculate the patient’s body surface area[16]: BSA=0.007184 × Height (cm)0.725× Weight (kg)0.425.

2.3.1.2. Quantitative criteria for drainage volume change

The hemostatic effect at the surgical site was assessed by comparing changes in drainage volume. These modifications were compared with the baseline values prior to transfusion[17,18]: Significant reduction: >10% decrease in 24-hour drainage volume compared with pre-transfusion baseline; Increase: >10% increase in 24-hour drainage volume compared with the pre-transfusion baseline; and No improvement: Fluctuation range of 24-hour drainage volume within ± 10% of the pre-transfusion baseline.

2.3.1.3. Quantitative criteria for Hb level fluctuation

Increments from a 24-hour red blood cell transfusion were excluded to correct all changes in Hb levels, in an effort to remove confounding variables such as allogeneic blood transfusion and volume resuscitation. These modifications were compared with the baseline values prior to transfusion[18,19]: Increase: >5 g/L increase in Hb level compared with pre-transfusion baseline; Stable: Fluctuation range of Hb levels within ±5 g/L compared with the pre-transfusion baseline; and Progressive decrease: >5 g/L decrease in Hb level compared to the pre-transfusion baseline.

2.3.2. Efficacy evaluation for non-hemorrhagic patients

In the absence of overt bleeding and indwelling drainage devices, transfusion efficacy was determined directly by the 24 hour-CCI threshold, excluding drainage volume and Hb level[13,20]: Effective transfusion: 24 hour-CCI > 4.5; Ineffective transfusion: 24 hour-CCI ≤ 4.5.

2.3.3. Efficacy evaluation for hemorrhagic patients

Efficacy was comprehensively evaluated based on the combination of 24 hour-CCI, drainage volume change, and corrected Hb level fluctuation, with 24 hour-CCI as the primary threshold and clinical indicators as supplementary criteria.[20]

2.3.3.1. Effective transfusion

Transfusion was defined as effective if either of the following criteria was met[13,21]: Criterion 1:24 hour-CCI > 4.5, regardless of drainage volume change and Hb level fluctuations; Criterion 2:24 hour-CCI ≤ 4.5, accompanied by a significant reduction in drainage volume and a stable Hb level; Criterion 3:24 hour-CCI ≤ 4.5, accompanied by a significant reduction in drainage volume and increased Hb levels.

2.3.3.2. Ineffective transfusion

Transfusion was defined as ineffective when 24 hour-CCI ≤ 4.5 and any of the following criteria was satisfied[13,20,21]: Criterion 1: Increased drainage volume, regardless of Hb level fluctuation; Criterion 2: No improvement in drainage volume, regardless of Hb level fluctuation; and Criterion 3: A significant reduction in drainage volume accompanied by a progressive decrease in Hb level.

The quantitative thresholds for drainage volume change and Hb level variation were determined in accordance with the need for improved efficacy evaluation in perioperative surgical ICU patients as well as common threshold settings in relevant clinical research.[17,18] All assessment criteria were developed in accordance with applicable clinical recommendations and prior research findings.[13,20]

2.4. Platelet product specifications

The Guangzhou Blood Center consistently provided platelet products for this investigation. There were approximately 250 to 300 mL of each apheresis platelet unit. It was a strict requirement that the platelet count per unit be at least 2.5 × 1011. A single treatment dose was contained in a single bag. Following collection, platelets were maintained for up to 5 days at a steady temperature of 22 ± 2°C. Cell viability was maintained by constant oscillations.

2.5. Study endpoints

2.5.1. Primary study endpoint

The primary study endpoint was a composite index of 24 hour-CCI, drainage volume and Hb level, which served to identify risk factors for refractoriness to platelet transfusion and assess platelet transfusion efficacy. (detailed criteria in Sections 2.3.2 and 2.3.3).

2.5.2. Secondary study endpoints

Changes in coagulation and inflammatory laboratory markers within 24 hours of transfusion, such as procalcitonin (PCT), C-reactive protein (CRP), and white blood cell count; and correlations between clinical variables and the effectiveness of platelet transfusions, such as intraoperative bleeding volume and splenomegaly.

2.7. Statistical analyzing

SPSS 26.0 (Chicago) were performed using for statistical version 26.0. An independent-samples t-test was used to compare normally distributed continuous data (x̄±s). The Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyze non-normally distributed data [M (Q1, Q3)]. The χ2 test was used to compare the categorical variables (n [%]). P < .05 for significance. Univariate logistic regression was used to screen variables associated with platelet transfusion ineffectiveness. Variables with a P-value of <.05 were subjected to multivariate analysis. A prediction model was created using significant multivariate factors, and its performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis [area under the curve (AUC), Youden index, optimal cutoff, sensitivity, and specificity]. Newly adjusted models were constructed by sequentially removing one independent variable at a time. By comparing the AUC, Hosmer-Lemeshow statistics, and regression coefficients between the original and modified models, the model stability and robustness were verified.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

This retrospective observational study included 302 platelet transfusion cases in the surgical ICU between June 2022 and December 2022. Participants had a median age of 55.98 ± 13.98 years (range: 25–87 years). This study included 215 males and 87 females. The primary finding of this study was the inefficiency of platelet transfusions. It was defined by a dual evaluation system: 24-hour CCI ≤ 4.5 plus clinical hemostatic failure. The 302 cases were divided into 2 groups by this outcome: the effective group (n = 192) and the ineffective group (n = 110). The baseline characteristics of the 2 groups are presented in Table 1. Most variables showed no statistically significant intergroup differences (P > .05). Surgery frequency (≤1 vs >1), liver disease, intraoperative blood loss (≤1600 vs >1600), PCT level, CRP level, splenomegaly, and age (<65 vs ≥65 years) were the 7 variables that showed significant intergroup differences (all P < .05).

3.2. Univariate analysis

All variables in Table 1 were subjected to univariate logistic regression analysis. Eight factors were found to be significantly (P < .05) linked to platelet transfusion ineffectiveness. Multivariate logistic regression analysis incorporated the following 8 variables: surgery frequency (≤1 vs >1), liver disease, intraoperative blood loss (≤1600 vs >1600), PCT, CRP, splenomegaly, age (<65 vs ≥65 years), and total erythrocyte transfusion. The ineffectiveness of platelet transfusion was not significantly correlated with other indicators (P > .05) (Table 2).

3.3. Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Logistic multiple regression analysis was conducted to establish platelet transfusion efficacy (1 = ineffective, 0 = effective) as the dependent variable, and 8 modifiers that were significantly different (P < .05) in the preceding single-variate evaluation as independent parameters.

Before the formal analysis, the assumptions of the model were verified. The Box-Tidwell test confirmed the linear relationship between continuous independent variables and the dependent variable (all interaction terms, P > .1). The variance inflation factor (VIF) test showed VIF values for all 8 variables, both < 5. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test showed χ2 = 4.403, P = .819 (P > .05). The results of the 8-factor full model showed that 3 factors were identified as independent risk factors for ineffective platelet transfusion (all P < .05), including surgery frequency (≤1 vs >1), PCT, and splenomegaly. The other 5 factors showed no statistical correlation with the study outcome after adjusting for confounding factors (all P > .05) (Table 3).

3.4. ROC curve of the prediction model

The results of the ROC curve analysis indicated that when the 3 risk factors were pooled, the predictive value peaked (Youden index = 0.346), with an ROC-AUC of 0.706 and sensitivity and specificity of 66.40% and 68.20%, respectively. Among the individual factors, splenomegaly had the highest predictive value. Its AUC was 0.614 (95% CI: 0.547–0.681, P = .001). PCT also had a significant predictive value, with an AUC of 0.570 (95% CI: 0.502–0.639, P = .042). In contrast, the number of surgeries had no significant predictive value for the outcome. Its AUC was 0.563 (95% CI: 0.495–0.630, P = .071 > 0.05) (Table 5 and Fig. 1).

3.5. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis was used to examine the stability and reliability of the three-factor model as well as the contributions of each variable. It created new modified models by eliminating one independent variable at a time. The original and modified models were compared using the basic indicators (Hosmer-Lemeshow test p-values, the regression coefficient change, and the absolute AUC change). The study results showed that the Hosmer–Lemeshow test P-values of the 3 modified models and the original model were all >0.05. When the core variables (splenomegaly and PCT) were eliminated, the regression coefficient change was >10% and the absolute AUC change was >0.05 when the core variables (splenomegaly and PCT) were eliminated. The regression coefficient (splenomegaly and PCT) stayed constant (change < 10%) and the AUC changed by just 0.02 (<0.05) when the auxiliary variable (surgery frequency > 1) was eliminated (Table 6).

4. Discussion

This study aimed to predict ineffective platelet transfusions in surgical ICU patients during the perioperative period. A three-factor prediction model was built, which included splenomegaly (dichotomous), number of surgeries (dichotomous), and PCT (continuous). According to ROC curve and sensitivity analyses, splenomegaly, PCT, and the number of surgeries were risk factors for ineffective platelet transfusion. The prediction model built using these 3 factors showed moderate accuracy (AUC = 0.706) and reliability. These findings provide useful insights into clinical management.

The sensitivity and ROC analysis results were in agreement. The three-factor model’s AUC (0.706) was greater than the AUCs of the individual key variables (splenomegaly: AUC = 0.614; PCT: AUC = 0.570), indicating that the multi-factor combined prediction outperformed the single-variable prediction. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test P-value for each model was >.05. This implies that the model fits properly. Sensitivity analysis also revealed that eliminating PCT or splenomegaly resulted in coefficient shifts of >10% and AUC changes of ≥0.05. Only a minor AUC change (0.02) and coefficient shifts of <10% resulted from the elimination of the number of surgeries. This means that in surgical ICU patients, splenomegaly and PCT were important predictors of ineffective platelet transfusion.

Repeated surgical damage increases the risk of infection, weakens immunity, and may cause whole-body inflammation. All of these factors worsen platelet function.[22] Multivariate regression analysis proved the number of surgeries was an independent risk factor for ineffective platelet transfusion (P < .05). However, the single-factor ROC analysis showed that it could not predict the results alone (P > .05). The sensitivity analysis also showed that it did not improve the effect of the core model. This conflicting result shows that many factors led to ineffective platelet transfusions. The number of surgeries performed was a weak, independent risk factor. Its influence only showed that other factors such as splenomegaly and PCT were controlled. This was not sufficient to predict the results alone. However, adding it to the model is still helpful. This slightly improved the prediction accuracy when paired with the key variables. The “key variable + helper variable” model had extra benefits for predicting ineffective platelet transfusion.

Splenomegaly was shown as one of the risk variables for ineffective platelet transfusion in this study’s multivariate logistic analysis, which was consistent with findings from previous studies.[23,24] When this indicator was eliminated, the performance of the model declined significantly. Spleen structure and function were abnormally changed during splenomegaly. It often indicates immunological problems, persistent inflammation, or elevated blood pressure in the portal vein of patients in the surgical ICU. The relationship between splenomegaly and ineffective platelet transfusion was nearly statistically significant (OR = 1.730, 95% CI: 0.97–3.07, P = .060), according to a meta-analysis.[5] More platelets were collected in the splenic sinusoids due to splenomegaly. This weakens the body’s response to platelet transfusion and decreases the number of functional platelets in the blood.[7] Additionally, in surgical ICU patients, splenomegaly frequently coexists with other illnesses in surgical ICU patients. Liver cirrhosis and serious infections are among these conditions. These were recognized as risk factors for platelet transfusion that did not work.[24] This further proves that splenomegaly is a key variable in the prediction model.

PCT level is a measure of inflammation in the body. The severity of infection and tissue damage were directly correlated with its severity.[25] According to this study, PCT level is also a separate risk factor for platelet transfusion failure. When used as a single variable for prediction, it performed well (AUC = 0.570). Interestingly, the regression coefficient for splenomegaly changed by 19.7% when PCT was removed. This implies that they worked together to predict the risk of ineffective platelet transfusions. It could be caused by PCT’s ability to show inflammation all over the body. Inflammation is a major contributing factor to ineffective transfusion.[24] Inflammatory chemicals are released during severe inflammation. These drugs decrease the transfusion effect and have an impact on platelet function.[26] Jiang et al discovered an inverse relationship between PCT levels and platelet counts in patients with sepsis. To assess prognosis, they routinely recommended checking both parameters.[27] PCT should direct platelet transfusion in infectious diseases according to the 2025 American Association of Blood Banks Guidelines.[20] Splenomegaly is associated with abnormal local immune sequestration, whereas PCT shows inflammation throughout the body. They may enhance overall risk assessment and clinical transfusion evaluation when combined with clinical signs.[25,28]

Several investigations have indicated that the risk of thrombocytopenia was much greater in surgical ICU patients than in medical ICU patients.[1] Underlying illnesses, infection, and surgical stress all contributed to this. According to earlier research, between 22% and 50% of general ICU patients received an ineffective platelet transfusion.[3,4] With an incidence of 36%, this study was limited to surgical patients. These surgical patients’ postoperative platelet consumption was impacted by underlying illnesses, infections, and inflammatory responses brought on by surgical trauma. With PCT and splenomegaly as core variables and surgery frequency as an auxiliary variable, the three-factor model had significant clinical application value. The degree of postoperative inflammation may be directly reflected by PCT. Increased platelet sequestration and destruction were closely linked to splenomegaly. The dimension of risk assessment was further enhanced by the frequency of surgery. The 3 factors worked together. They allowed for the early detection of the risk of ineffective platelet transfusion, assisted doctors in optimizing customized platelet transfusion procedures, and provided useful references for clinical diagnosis and therapy.

This study has some limitations that future studies should address. It makes use of data from a single center in a few cases. This hinders the widespread application of these findings. Multicenter studies with larger data sets are required to retest the model. The influence of variables may be twisted by unknown factors that could affect the results, such as patients’ other illnesses or the length of time the platelets were stored. The efficacy evaluation criteria in this study were developed based on the relevant clinical guidelines and prior research findings. To ensure consistency in assessment, these standards included distinguishing between patients who were bleeding and those who were not, as well as establishing precise figures for Hb changes and drainage volume. However, these standards can still be improved. This evaluation system should be improved in future studies. It can modify the weight of each index, incorporate additional risk factors into the three-factor model to improve prediction accuracy, and offer resources to direct customized transfusion regimens. Using only the 24 hour-CCI is simple for non-bleeding patients, but it might not accurately reflect how well the body stops bleeding in complicated circumstances. Physicians should use 24 hour-CCI in conjunction with Hb and drainage volume tests for patients with bleeding. Further investigation into the interplay between splenomegaly induced platelet retention and PCT-related whole-body inflammation may result in novel approaches for the prevention and treatment of ineffective platelet transfusions in surgical ICU patients.

5. Conclusions

This study developed a three-factor model (number of surgeries, PCT level, and splenomegaly) to predict unsuccessful perioperative platelet transfusion in surgical ICU patients. In clinical practice, dynamic PCT monitoring helps identify at-risk patients early and supports the development of customized transfusion strategies, such as cross-matched platelet transfusion and infection control, for surgical ICU patients with splenomegaly and multiple surgeries. These interventions improve perioperative outcomes, decrease ineffective transfusions, and increase transfusion efficacy.
