Virtual care is a double-edged sword: Adjusting preventive care service delivery in community health clinics during COVID-19
Authors: Dea Papajorgji-Taylor, Christina R. Sheppler, Carmit McMullen, Patrick J. O’Connor, Rachel Gold
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurpose:Qualitatively examine how community health clinics delivered cardiovascular disease (CVD) preventive care during COVID-19 using virtual care and related adaptations.Methods:Semi-structured interviews were conducted in 2021–2022 with nine clinician leaders from four clinics across the country that share an electronic health record. Interviews covered: (1) the pandemic’s impact on preventive care delivery, (2) the adaptation and role of virtual care, and (3) management of CVD risk in this context. Collected data were analyzed using a thematic analysis approach.Results:Staff reported substantial pros and cons to virtual care for CVD risk management. It was seen as useful for general check-ins with patients. However, without current laboratory results and vital measurements, providers found it challenging to assess patient CVD risk and direct care accordingly.Conclusion:Findings add to the evolving evidence base that the necessary pivot to virtual care during the pandemic spurred innovations but also created limitations and delays in delivering appropriate preventive care.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic led to substantial restructuring of healthcare delivery.[1234] Notably, virtual care greatly increased during the pandemic and continues to be used at rates far higher than before.[5678] Virtual care allows patients to receive aspects of medical care while alleviating barriers to in-person appointments, e.g. transportation difficulties.[9] Conversely, increased reliance on virtual care may exacerbate healthcare disparities due to patients’ and organizations’ differential access to and capacity to engage in telehealth.[1011] Clinic staff perspectives on virtual care and its impacts on care delivery during the pandemic are relatively understudied, especially in community health clinics. It is important to understand these impacts because community clinics serve socioeconomically vulnerable patients who are most likely to experience disparities in access to telehealth, but at the same time, they potentially could benefit greatly from telehealth due to difficulties accessing in-person care.[1213] Such understanding can also inform how primary care physicians deliver quality cardiovascular preventive care via telehealth, especially when serving socioeconomically vulnerable communities. Insights into the challenges faced and solutions implemented by community clinics could also enhance primary care providers’ ability to adapt to changing healthcare landscapes, improve patient outcomes, and contribute to mitigating cardiovascular health disparities. These analyses explore how four community-based clinics delivered general and cardiovascular disease (CVD) preventive care during the height of the pandemic (2020–2021) using virtual care and other related adaptations.

Methods

Data were collected from clinics that participated in an effectiveness trial that took place in community clinics and tested implementation of a clinical decision support and shared decision-making tool (CV Wizard) to improve CVD risk reduction through preventive care; details previously reported.[14] Because these analyses were conducted as part of this trial of CV Wizard, they address its use during virtual care.

Qualitative data were collected during fall 2021 and winter 2022 from four primary care community clinics located in Alaska, Ohio, and Oregon. The clinics share an electronic health record (EHR) platform through membership in OCHIN, Inc. Clinic A provides a full range of primary care services to >5,000 people; Clinic B delivers culturally appropriate care to >47,000 people in two neighboring counties; Clinic C is in a rural frontier community of 2,000 people; and Clinic D is in a metropolitan area whose service area spans 12 neighborhoods with a diverse mix of residents. They are all Federally Qualified Health Centers.

We invited clinic leaders and providers who served as clinical champions for CV Wizard at each clinic to participate in 30-min semi-structured phone interviews, recorded with permission. Participants were selected based on (1) clinic roles, (2) availability to participate, and (3) experience delivering preventive care virtually. We interviewed nine staff: one from Clinic A, two from Clinic B, two from Clinic C, and four from Clinic D, including eight primary care providers (four of whom also had formal leadership roles), and a nurse care manager. The interview guide covered topics related to their experience providing CVD risk management care during the pandemic, how their approach to doing so was affected by the transition to virtual care, and when applicable, their use of CV Wizard in virtual visits.

All recordings were transcribed professionally and analyzed by two researchers (DP-T, CM) using a thematic analysis approach.[1516] DP-T developed a coding dictionary based on interview topics and a preliminary review of transcripts. DP-T and CM subsequently refined the coding dictionary and DP-T applied it to the full dataset. The thematic analysis assessed how clinics adapted to delivering preventive care virtually and how they incorporated decision-making tools for addressing patient CVD risk factors during the pandemic. All interview materials and processes were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at Kaiser Permanente Northwest, where the study was conducted. The clinical trial is registered on Clinicaltrials.gov under the identifier NCT03001713. This clinical trial was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board Review Board at Kaiser Permanente Northwest. Initial approval was provided on March 2018.

Results

Perspectives on how each clinic adapted care delivery in response to the pandemic are described below, in three themes: (1) the pandemic’s impact on care delivery; (2) the role of virtual care in this context; and (3) management of CVD risk. Impact on care delivery

The provider from Clinic A noted that deferrals of in-person appointments resulted in a decrease in preventive care service delivery in the early pandemic period (spring 2020). Vital sign measurements (e.g. blood pressure) and routine laboratory results (e.g. HbA1c screenings) were difficult to obtain due to limited in-person encounters. Without updated clinical data, providers could not accurately assess patients’ CVD risk factors or need for specific services (e.g. prescriptions). Once clinic leaders felt it was safe to resume preventive services in person based on federal COVID guidelines, the clinic had a backlog of patients. Many returning patients had acute needs resulting in elevated need for intervention, e.g. statins or blood pressure medications.

The two providers from Clinic B reported that primary care services were put on hold intermittently from March 2020 through fall 2021. To be responsive to COVID surges and local transmission rates, the clinic fluctuated between deprioritizing nonurgent services, and then trying to catch up and address the backlog of appointments when it was safer to do so. During this time, many preventive care activities, such as blood work, physical exams, and pap smears, were referred to specialty care, such as gynecology.

Clinic C providers mentioned that during the pandemic’s first 8–9 months, the clinic limited its delivery of preventive care services. Support staff (front desk, medical assistants) generally worked remotely, while providers remained in the clinic, although factors, such as staff members’ health or caregiving responsibilities, also determined who worked remotely. The few in-person visits conducted were typically reserved for acute concerns. The clinic also implemented curbside visits, setting up a drive-through system: patients would park, call the front desk to check in, and then were greeted by staff in personal protective equipment to assess symptoms. Using portable sheds, tents, and imaging equipment let staff provide some services outside the office setting, though these were typically used for acute rather than preventive care.

Interviewees from Clinic D noted that in-person care decreased substantially at the beginning of the pandemic and remained reduced at the time of the interviews (fall 2021). For the first 6–10 months of the pandemic, the clinic almost exclusively conducted video and telephone visits with very limited in-person visits, then gradually returned to providing a limited number of in-person visits. During this time, it was challenging to engage with patients who lacked access to technology. In addition to the limited availability of in-person visits, patients were less likely to seek in-person care during this period, making it difficult to conduct screenings or physical exams.

Use of virtual care

Reliance on virtual services allowed Clinic A to provide access to care and maintain contact with patients. However, they relied on outdated or patient-reported home readings to assess CVD risk, given the limited ability to collect such information directly. The Clinic A provider also noted that medication refill requests were challenging to address without current clinical information.

For Clinic B, participants noted that due to the great reduction in the number of patients going into the clinic, providers relied on delivering care virtually, although a lot of preventive care was placed on hold at various times depending on the COVID surge levels, then resume when there was an opportunity to prioritize it again. In modifying cardiovascular disease risk during virtual care, one provider noted that it was easier to address CVD risk because the interaction with the patient is limited to verbal or visual, so providers could spend more time to discuss the patient’s risk factors, address behavioral changes, and utilize the cardiovascular risk calculator to review recommendations for lowering their risk levels.

In Clinic C, patients were scheduled to have virtual visits or virtually enhanced home visits based on their technology access and use of the EHR’s patient portal. The clinic already offered some virtual services before the pandemic due to the large catchment area of their rural population, which eased their ability to integrate virtual care into home visits during the pandemic. In virtually enhanced home visits, a nurse care manager went to the patient’s home and the provider joined the visit virtually. The nurse would collect vitals, perform a physical assessment, and engage with the provider via instant chat. These encounters were scheduled as virtual visits, so the provider and nurse care manager could join using a secure link via the EHR. This “hybrid” method increased interaction with some patients who had historically been less likely to go into the clinic and reduced COVID exposure risk while enabling continuing to deliver care.

In Clinic D, virtual care appeared to increase access to care for patients who might not go to the clinic due to transportation issues. Furthermore, patients with reliable internet access reported finding virtual visits more convenient than in-person visits. However, one provider noted that patients with transportation barriers also often lacked dependable internet access and so were more likely to rely on phone visits, which limit provider–patient interaction. One provider said that the challenges of virtual care were worse among non-English speaking patients and those with low literacy, because virtual visits had to rely more on written materials (typically in English) such as patient portal messages. One Clinic D provider found that virtual visits were a helpful way for addressing smoking cessation as they enabled more frequent check-ins by giving the patient scheduling flexibility. Grant funding enabled Clinic D to give patients tools for automatically uploading blood pressure or glucose readings to the EHR. Patients for whom such automated technology was challenging wrote down monitor readings and shared them at upcoming visits.

Challenges and adaptations of CVD risk management

In Clinic A, interviewees reported that monitoring CVD risk was deprioritized due to competing acute needs such as COVID or mental health needs. The lack of current clinical data also impeded CVD risk management. Clinic A’s participant explained that when inputs to CVD risk calculators included potentially inaccurate home readings or measurements from the last in-person visit, they could not identify needed next steps in the treatment plan.

Clinic B participants noted that during the pandemic, if a patient presented with acute issues, a given encounter would focus on that need, rather than reviewing the patient’s CVD risk. However, one Clinic B participant found it easier to use CVD risk tools during virtual visits because the provider had more time to review the tool’s recommendations compared to in-person visits.

A Clinic C participant noted that differences in medical assistant and provider workflows prepandemic impacted the use of CVD risk tools and discussion of CVD risk factors with patients during virtual visits: some providers actively used risk calculators, while others relied on their medical assistants to input data or share calculator results. All Clinic D participants found CVD risk assessment tools useful for virtual care, particularly for patients with reliable video connection and higher CVD risk. They found that the ability to show risk assessment results and recommendations via screen sharing helped to engage patients with the information even more than when it was shared on paper at in-person visits.

Clinic D staff who were interviewed had differing perspectives on how CVD risk was addressed during the pandemic. One participant felt that providers continued to review patients’ blood pressure and cholesterol levels at the same level as prepandemic. They noted that even during acute visits, there were opportunities to talk about CVD risk reduction, specifically when discussing changes in people’s lives as a result of COVID, such as limited opportunities for exercise or changes in eating habits. In contrast, another participant felt that the loss of physical exams and laboratory testing disrupted the monitoring and management of medication adherence and CVD risk factors, which by necessity shifted their focus to discussing lifestyle interventions and self-monitoring with patients. Sometimes this discussion was followed by coordinating care services at another visit, such as independent laboratory scheduling, coordinating with a nurse for point-of-care measurement, or going into the clinic for a blood pressure check. Other providers in Clinic D described deprioritizing preventive care to focus on competing, acute needs. One stated that sheltering in place to limit COVID transmission led patients to increased smoking and unhealthy diets or decreased physical activity. These behavioral changes had negative effects on CVD risk, but addressing this was less of a priority for some physicians because other issues were more urgent.

Discussion

These findings demonstrate how preventive care and the use of virtual modalities evolved during the COVID-19 pandemic and suggest mechanisms of impact on CVD prevention. At the height of the pandemic, video and telephone visits were a primary method for providing care. Virtual care was seen as useful for general check-ins with patients but not for patients with high CVD risk. For these patients, providers perceived a negative impact of reliance on virtual care because in-person visits allowed the provider to collect laboratory results needed to prioritize recommendations and engage meaningfully in conversations about risk reduction.

Some providers found it hard to use CVD risk assessment tools remotely. Many patients could not access the patient portal through a smartphone or computer, making it challenging for them to consider written recommendations from risk calculators. Others noted that this information could be difficult to understand when patients access it outside of a visit where results could be discussed. Most providers who used CVD risk assessment tools remotely only did so a few times because they prioritized addressing acute issues over preventive care.

These case studies demonstrate the different ways that clinics adapted preventive care practices during the pandemic. Some providers adjusted to using self-reported patient data. Others focused on discussing behavioral changes and CVD risk. Supplemental funding from a grant lets one clinic equip patients with home monitoring devices to capture vital data. One clinic found innovative ways to deliver patient care in the form of virtually enhanced home visits or curbside visits. Understanding how community clinics have utilized and supplemented virtual platforms for cardiovascular preventive care can help primary care providers optimize resource utilization and reduce healthcare costs without compromising quality. It can also provide guidance on how providers can most effectively respond to crises like COVID-19. By studying how community clinics adapted their cardiovascular preventive care practices, primary care providers can develop greater resilience and flexibility in managing future challenges or emergencies.

Limitations

The strain of the pandemic on clinics time and resources impacted our ability to recruit and interview a large number of participants, and to interview participants from more diverse roles. This may have resulted in selection bias. However, this may be minimal as interviews came from staff in diverse clinical and geographic settings.

Conclusion

Results highlight how the COVID-19 pandemic impacted community clinic’s ability to deliver preventive care services in general, and CVD risk management in particular. Using virtual visits transformed healthcare delivery in both positive and negative ways, such as improved access for some types of patients and care, but increased barriers for others in terms of patients’ access to care and providers’ access to current vital sign measurements and laboratory results.
