Antimicrobial resistance of Salmonella isolates from cattle and horses with salmonellosis in Hokkaido, Japan
Authors: Yoshimasa SASAKI, Shotaro SUZUKI, Nobuyuki KUSABA, Nahid RAHMAN, Chihiro AIKAWA, Masashi OKAMURA
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, reported cases of salmonellosis in cattle and horses in Hokkaido, Japan, have increased. To optimize antimicrobial therapy, we assessed the antimicrobial susceptibility of Salmonella isolates from these animals. Among 112 bovine isolates, high resistance rates were observed for streptomycin (92.0%), ampicillin (88.4%), and tetracycline (79.5%), while resistance rates for kanamycin (8.9%), ciprofloxacin (1.8%), gentamicin (0.0%), and colistin (0.0%) were low. Additionally, 18.8% of the bovine isolates were resistant to cefotaxime, and all of these cefotaxime-resistant isolates carried blaCMY-2. Among 16 equine isolates, no resistance was observed to cefazolin, cefotaxime, gentamicin, nalidixic acid, ciprofloxacin, colistin, or chloramphenicol. Gentamicin and kanamycin are recommended as the first-line treatment for salmonellosis in cattle, with fluoroquinolones as the second choice.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, cattle, equine paratyphoid, horse, salmonellosis

Salmonellosis in livestock may be asymptomatic or may result in clinical illnesses of varying severity [4, 11, 14, 17]. Bovine salmonellosis is an important disease caused predominantly by Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovars Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium) and S. Dublin [4, 6]. In Japan, bovine salmonellosis caused by certain serovars, including S. Typhimurium and S. Dublin, is designated as a notifiable disease under the Domestic Animal Infectious Disease Control Law. Equine salmonellosis caused by S. Abortusequi is called equine paratyphoid and is a notifiable disease under the same law. S. Abortusequi is associated with abortion in mares, neonatal septicemia, and polyarthritis [5, 14]. Salmonellosis diagnosed in cattle and horses should be reported by veterinarians to the government. Recently, the number of reported cases of bovine salmonellosis has increased. In particular, the number of reported cases in Hokkaido increased sharply from 246 in 2018 to 560 in 2023. In 2023, the reported cases in Hokkaido accounted for 87.6% (560/639) of the cases in Japan. In 2023, 62.1% of dairy cows raised in Japan and 21.1% of beef cattle raised in Japan were reared in Hokkaido, according to the Statistical Survey on Livestock of the Japanese Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry, and Fisheries (JMAFF) (https://www.maff.go.jp/j/tokei/kouhyou/tikusan/index.html). The number of reported equine paratyphoid cases was less than 10 per year from 2009 to 2021; however, 17 cases were reported in 2022. Of these 17 cases, 15 were reported in Hokkaido.

Efficient strategies for Salmonella management on farms should be developed to decrease the number of salmonellosis cases in cattle and horses. Moreover, effective antimicrobial treatment for salmonellosis is required to ensure the rapid recovery of affected animals and prevent bacterial transmission to other animals. Antimicrobial agents should be selected based on the antimicrobial susceptibility of the bacterial isolates from the affected animal. However, in clinical practice, treatment often needs to begin before the results of antimicrobial susceptibility testing are available. Understanding the antimicrobial resistance profiles of Salmonella isolates from cattle and horses with salmonellosis is crucial for effective antimicrobial selection. Therefore, we investigated the antimicrobial resistance profiles of Salmonella isolates obtained from cattle and horses affected by recent salmonellosis outbreaks in Hokkaido, Japan.

We requested the Hokkaido Government to provide one Salmonella isolate for each outbreak of salmonellosis in livestock that occurred in Hokkaido between 2018 and 2023, specifically one isolate per affected animal per outbreak. These diseased animals were diagnosed with salmonellosis in accordance with the guidelines for diagnosis of infectious diseases published by JMAFF (https://www.maff.go.jp/j/syouan/douei/katiku_yobo/attach/pdf/byokanshishin-11.pdf). If multiple outbreaks of salmonellosis occurred on the same farm within 3 months, we asked that they be considered caused by the same Salmonella clone and requested only a single isolate to be provided. As a result, we received 128 Salmonella isolates from 112 cattle and 16 horses. These isolates were collected from livestock hygiene centers in 13 of the 14 subprefectures in Hokkaido (designated A to M) and sent to Obihiro University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine (Table 1).

Among the bovine isolates, 26 were S. Typhimurium, 77 were the monophasic variant S. Typhimurium (4:i:-), 8 were S. Dublin, and 1 was the monophasic variant Salmonella (4:-:1,2). Of the 26 S. Typhimurium isolates, 24 were from 21 dairy farms in nine subprefectures (A to I), and 2 were from beef farms in subprefecture D. Among the 77 monophasic variant S. Typhimurium (4:i:-) isolates, 62 were from 47 dairy farms in 11 subprefectures (B to L), and 15 were from 11 beef farms in six subprefectures (C to F, J, and M).

For the equine isolates, six S. Abortusequi isolates were obtained from six horses with equine paratyphoid on four horse farms in subprefecture F from 2021 to 2022. Additionally, 10 monophasic variant S. Typhimurium (4:i:-) isolates were collected from 10 horses with non-paratyphoid salmonellosis on six horse farms in subprefecture J from 2019 to 2022.

At the university, 88 monophasic variant Salmonella isolates were confirmed to be monophasic variant S. Typhimurium using the multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) method reported by Hong et al. [7]. To investigate the relationship between the regions where S. Typhimurium, its monophasic variant (4:i:-), and S. Dublin were isolated, multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was performed. MLST was carried out as described by Stepan et al. [19]. The nucleotide sequences of seven housekeeping genes (aroC, dnaN, hemD, hisD, purE, sucA, and thrA) were amplified and sequenced for MLST. The sequence data was submitted to the MLST database (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk/) to determine allele profiles and sequence type (ST) assignments. For this analysis, one isolate per serovar was randomly selected from each region using random numbers generated in Microsoft Excel 2021.

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of antimicrobials against the 128 isolates were determined by broth microdilution on dry plates (Eiken Chemical, Tokyo, Japan). Susceptibilities to ampicillin (1–128 mg/L), cefazolin (1–128 mg/L), cefotaxime (0.5–64 mg/L), streptomycin (1–128 mg/L), gentamicin (0.5–64 mg/L), kanamycin (1–128 mg/L), tetracycline (0.5–64 mg/L), nalidixic acid (1–128 mg/L), ciprofloxacin (0.03–4 mg/L), colistin (0.12–16 mg/L), chloramphenicol (1–128 mg/L), and trimethoprim (0.25–16 mg/L) were tested. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality control strain. The breakpoints for ampicillin (32 mg/L), cefazolin (8 mg/L), cefotaxime (4 mg/L), streptomycin (32 mg/L), gentamicin (16 mg/L), kanamycin (64 mg/L), tetracycline (16 mg/L), nalidixic acid (32 mg/L), ciprofloxacin (1 mg/L), colistin (4 mg/L), chloramphenicol (32 mg/L), and trimethoprim (16 mg/L) were adopted from the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [2] and the Japanese Veterinary Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring System [11]. The double-disk synergy test was conducted using ceftazidime (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg) with clavulanic acid, cefotaxime (30 µg), and cefotaxime (30 µg) with clavulanic acid disks (Eiken Chemical Co.) to detect the presence of extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL) in cefotaxime-resistant isolates [3]. Additionally, these cefotaxime-resistant isolates were further analyzed to detect plasmid-mediated AmpC β-lactamase genes using multiplex PCR [15]. The nucleotide sequences of the detected β-lactamase genes were confirmed by Sanger sequencing, as described by Noda et al. [13].

All statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.1. The differences between the proportions were tested using Fisher’s exact test, with a P-value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

The highest resistance rate among bovine isolates was for streptomycin (92.0%), followed by ampicillin (88.4%), tetracycline (79.5%), chloramphenicol (68.8%), trimethoprim (58.9%), cefazolin (35.7%), cefotaxime (18.8%), nalidixic acid (13.4%), kanamycin (8.9%), and ciprofloxacin (1.8%) (Table 2). All isolates were susceptible to gentamicin (MIC: ≤2 mg/L) and colistin (MIC: ≤1 mg/L). Twenty-one isolates were resistant to cefotaxime (MIC: 8–64 mg/L) and harbored blaCMY-2. ESBL production was not detected in any of these cefotaxime-resistant isolates using the double-disk synergy test. Resistance rates to cefazolin, cefotaxime, and nalidixic acid were significantly higher in S. Typhimurium than in the monophasic variant S. Typhimurium (4:i:-). In contrast, the resistance rates to streptomycin and trimethoprim were significantly lower in S. Typhimurium than in the monophasic variant S. Typhimurium (4:i:-). Regarding the relationship between S. Typhimurium and S. Dublin, the resistance rate to nalidixic acid was statistically lower in S. Typhimurium than in S. Dublin.

Among the 26 S. Typhimurium isolates from cattle, six (23.1%) were susceptible to all 12 antimicrobials tested, whereas 19 (73.1%) were multidrug-resistant (Table 3). The most common antimicrobial resistance profile was resistance to ampicillin, cefazolin, cefotaxime, streptomycin, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol (nine isolates). Of the nine isolates exhibiting this resistance profile, six were isolated from two neighboring subprefectures (E and F). Among the 77 monophasic variant S. Typhimurium (4:i:-) isolates from cattle, one (1.6%) was susceptible to all 12 antimicrobials tested, whereas 74 (96.1%) were multidrug-resistant. The most common antimicrobial resistance profile was resistance to ampicillin, streptomycin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim (30 isolates). Of the 30 isolates exhibiting this resistance profile, 24 were isolated from three neighboring subprefectures (D, E, and F). The second most common antimicrobial resistance profile was resistance to ampicillin, cefazolin, streptomycin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim (13 isolates). Of the 13 isolates exhibiting this resistance profile, 9 were isolated from two neighboring subprefectures (B and D). All 15 monophasic variant S. Typhimurium (4:i:-) isolates from beef cattle were multidrug-resistant. All eight S. Dublin isolates were multidrug-resistant. Of the eight S. Dublin isolates, six were resistant to ampicillin, cefazolin, cefotaxime, streptomycin, tetracycline, nalidixic acid, and chloramphenicol.

In this study, over 90% of the 112 bovine Salmonella isolates tested were multidrug-resistant, with most showing resistance to ampicillin, streptomycin, and tetracycline. Various antimicrobial resistance profiles were observed, some with regional specificity. Typically, cattle are born on farms, sold at livestock markets, transferred to different farms, and raised on several farms before being sent to slaughterhouses. S. Typhimurium and its monophasic variant have a broad host range and are common serovars in humans [18], food animals [1, 8], and wildlife [10, 21]. This broad host range is considered a factor responsible for the transmission of S. Typhimurium and its monophasic variant from cattle to horses [12] or other livestock through wild animals [21]. Arai et al. [1] investigated the STs and antimicrobial resistance of 160 monophasic variant S. Typhimurium (4, [5],12:i:-) isolates from cattle in Japan between 2008 and 2017. They found all isolates to be ST34, with over 90% resistant to ampicillin, streptomycin, and tetracycline, and reported that the ST34 isolates from pigs, humans, and cattle are genetically closely related. In our study, of the 17 monophasic variant S. Typhimurium (4:i:-) isolates from subprefecture J, 7 exhibited resistance to ampicillin, streptomycin, and tetracycline. The spread of Salmonella in cattle farms in Hokkaido is likely due to the transportation of infected cattle. This may result in the presence of clones with specific antimicrobial resistance profiles distributed regionally. To confirm this hypothesis, further collection of isolates from livestock and wildlife and genetic analysis, such as whole-genome sequencing, is needed.

Administration of ampicillin, streptomycin, or tetracycline to cattle with salmonellosis may fail to alleviate symptoms and result in the selection of multidrug-resistant organisms. Therefore, these antimicrobials should not be administered. Antimicrobial resistance profiles varied depending on the region where infected cattle were found and the serovar that the bacteria belonged to, but resistance rates were low for kanamycin (8.9%), ciprofloxacin (1.8%), gentamicin (0.0%), and colistin (0.0%). Third-generation cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones have been classified by the World Health Organization as “critically important antimicrobials in human medicine” [20]. In Japan, these antimicrobials have been designated as second-choice drugs by the JMAFF. Therefore, although fluoroquinolones are likely to be effective against bovine salmonellosis, they should not be prioritized. Gentamicin is approved for administration to cattle under 3 months of age by the JMAFF. Sarashina et al. [16] reported that calves with salmonellosis recovered after oral administration of 1.0 mg/kg gentamicin twice daily for 3 days. Therefore, gentamicin should be the first-choice drug for cattle under 3 months of age. For cattle over 3 months of age, the use of kanamycin is approved, and it should be administered prior to the administration of the second-choice drugs mentioned above. Subsequently, the optimal drug should be selected based on the results of antimicrobial susceptibility testing of the isolate from the salmonellosis case once the results are available.

None of the equine isolates were resistant to cefazolin, cefotaxime, gentamicin, nalidixic acid, ciprofloxacin, colistin, or chloramphenicol. The administration of chloramphenicol to food-producing animals has been prohibited by the JMAFF. Cefazolin, gentamicin, and oxolinic acid (one of quinolones) are effective against equine salmonellosis. Grandolfo et al. [5] reported that four symptomatic foals infected with S. Abortusequi were treated with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (20 mg/kg once a day) and gentamicin (6 mg/kg once a day) intramuscularly for 10 days. Three foals recovered completely without permanent sequelae, although one foal had permanent signs of polyarthritis.

Although antimicrobial therapy may facilitate clinical recovery in cattle with salmonellosis, it has been criticized for failing to limit the fecal shedding of bacteria or for having a positive effect on the duration of the fecal shedding of bacteria [6]. Moreover, administration of antimicrobials to cattle may lead to the acquisition of antimicrobial resistance by pathogenic bacteria in humans and cattle. Antimicrobial resistance poses a major threat to human health. Kumagai et al. [9] reported that beef is a potential source of human non-typhoidal salmonellosis based on an analysis of outbreak surveillance data in Japan. We reported that S. Typhimurium was detected in 3.2% (8/250) of the rectal contents of healthy dairy cattle [17]. In addition, we characterized 102 Salmonella isolates from patients with enteritis between 2019 and 2022 and found that S. Typhimurium and its monophasic variant accounted for 13.7% (14/102) of the isolates, 71% (10/14) of them were multidrug-resistant, and two S. Typhimurium strains were resistant to cefotaxime and possessed blaCMY-2 [18]. In the present study, 21 S. Typhimurium isolates were found to be resistant to cefotaxime and harbored blaCMY-2. To maintain the efficacy of antimicrobial therapy against bovine salmonellosis and human non-typhoidal salmonellosis, it is necessary to monitor the drug resistance status of Salmonella isolates from bovine and equine salmonellosis cases so that veterinarians can easily select the appropriate antimicrobials.
