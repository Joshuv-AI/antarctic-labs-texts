Estimated labor market outcomes of people progressing from preclinical to early‐stage Alzheimer's disease in the United States
Authors: Maria Prados, Carlos Acosta, Soeren Mattke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONSecondary prevention treatments for Alzheimer's disease (AD) that are currently in late‐stage clinical trials may preserve productivity and workforce participation.METHODSUsing the nationally representative Health and Retirement Study waves from 1996 to 2020, we estimated changes in labor force participation, annual earnings, and social assistance payments between incident cases of cognitively impaired and statistically matched cognitively normal individuals.RESULTSAmong 20,717 respondents (aged 50 to 79), 5232 developed mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia. Disease onset was associated with a five‐percentage‐point (p.p.) reduction in workforce participation, annual earnings losses of US$8233 (23%) and US$5616 (18%) for men and women who remained in the labor force, respectively, and an increase of 3.5. p.p. male and 5.6 p.p. female social assistance beneficiaries.DISCUSSIONOnset of cognitive impairment was associated with statistically significant and meaningful reduction in workforce participation and earnings and increased participation in social assistance programs.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, cognitive impairment, earnings, labor, workforce

BACKGROUND

Having proven their ability to reduce the progression of early‐stage Alzheimer's disease (AD), defined as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and mild dementia due to AD, in phase 3 trials, 1 , 2 the amyloid‐targeting treatments lecanemab and donanemab are now approved for clinical use in multiple jurisdictions. The same treatments, as well as a third, trontinemab, are currently also in late‐stage clinical trials for preclinical AD, that is, cases with the AD pathology but without objectifiable cognitive impairment. 3 As the trial results in early‐stage AD have shown that the efficacy of the treatments is higher in the earliest‐stage cases with limited tau burden, 2 these data suggest that earlier treatment in the disease trajectory or secondary prevention, that is, preventing or delaying symptomatic disease, might be a successful treatment strategy.

If proven effective and approved, treatments for preclinical AD will be subject to intense scrutiny in connection with their economic value, as the experience with treatments for early‐stage AD has shown. 4 − 6 An exploratory analysis suggests that even a broad population screening and treatment program that reduces the risk of onset of clinical disease could potentially meet conventional cost‐effectiveness criteria with a lifetime treatment cost of up to US$54,257 when used in the full 55 to 79 population. 7 Targeting the intervention to clinical populations at greater risk of progression could further improve cost‐effectiveness. 8 Treatment of preclinical AD might generate greater value than the treatment of patients with early‐stage AD because treating early‐stage AD maintains patients in a mildly disabled state with elevated resource use for longer, whereas secondary prevention would reduce the risk of developing disability and the associated cost, akin to reducing cholesterol prior to a major cardiovascular event as opposed to after it. For example, Robinson et al. 9 estimated, based on United States (U.S.) General Evaluation of Resource Use and Cost of Alzheimer's Disease Study data, that monthly medical and social care costs associated with MCI, as the earliest stage of cognitive impairment, were already US$1098 and US$194 in 2017 dollars, respectively.

The aforementioned study defined value in terms of reduced direct medical and formal social care costs, loss of quality‐adjusted life‐years, and caregiver burden. An important additional contributor to the value of these treatments, however, could be the effect on labor market participation and productivity. Labor market effects are not traditionally considered in health technology assessment but have been proposed by International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research in its Value Flower as a novel component of treatment value. 10 People with preclinical AD are younger, healthier, and less cognitively impaired than early‐stage AD patients and thus are more likely to be in the labor force than early‐stage AD patients. Here, we use a U.S. nationally representative survey to estimate the association between incident cognitive impairment and labor market participation, annual earnings, and social assistance payments.

METHODS

Overview

We quantified the association of U.S. labor market outcomes with the transition from normal cognitive functioning to cognitively impaired but no dementia (CIND), as a proxy for MCI, or mild dementia of any etiology. We further used published estimates for amyloid positivity by age to project the proportion of changes that may be due to AD, as the data did not contain biomarker confirmation of amyloid pathology. We focused on labor force participation, labor earnings, and benefits received from the U.S. Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) and/or Social Security Supplemental Security Income (SSI). Both programs provide monthly payments to people with a disability that stops or limits their ability to work or to older adults with little or no income or assets. 11 , 12 While SSDI eligibility is tied to work history and Social Security contributions, SSI is strictly needs‐based. 12 We compared changes in these outcomes for incident cases of cognitive impairment and matched cognitively normal counterparts in the Health and Retirement Study (HRS). 13

Data and analytic sample

We used data from the RAND version of the HRS, 14 a nationally representative longitudinal study of older adults aged 50 and over in the United States. This biennial survey contains information on demographics, socioeconomic status, health and healthcare utilization, family, income and wealth, employment, and retirement. Full data on cognitive test scores are available since 1996, and we used data from then to 2020, the most recent available at the time of the analysis. Estimates using the HRS are representative of the U.S. national level based on sample weights. 13 The RAND HRS Longitudinal File is a cleaned, easy‐to‐use, and streamlined data product containing information from the HRS, including variables on demographics, health, health insurance, Social Security, pensions, family structure, retirement plans, expectations, and employment history, as well as imputations for income, assets, and medical expenditures.

As labor force participation and earnings decline strongly for older ages, we restricted the sample to adults aged 50 to 79. This resulted in 36,470 respondents who participated on average in 5.5 HRS survey waves. The sample only included individuals with normal cognitive function in their first survey wave. Respondents who scored below normal cognitive function in their first survey wave or with missing cognitive information in their first wave (n = 7017 unique respondents) could not be included in the estimation because the timing of their cognitive decline could not be ascertained. Participants who had not participated in the labor force in any HRS wave were not in our analytic sample (7815 respondents). We were unable to determine the incidence of cognitive decline for 693 participants due to missing cognitive scores. The cognitive scores we used to identify or rule out incident early‐stage cognitive impairment were not available for proxy respondents (n = 204 who always had proxy individuals), and 24 respondents were excluded from the analysis due to having other missing variables. The main analytic sample thus consisted of 20,717 unique respondents and 120,674 observations. The incident subsample was composed of those who – when observed in following HRS waves – developed incident MCI or mild dementia. The respondents without incident cognitive impairment formed the subsample from which the individuals were matched for the comparative analysis. There was no one‐to‐one correspondence between sample size and number of observations, as we did not restrict the sample according to the number of times each respondent was observed in the data. Outcome variables

For this study, the outcomes are labor force participation, annual labor earnings, and program participation in SSDI or SSI. We estimated the associations with average annual earnings conditional on participating in the labor force. We also estimated the associations with average annual earnings for the full incident group, which captures changes in earnings while working and foregone earnings as a result of exiting the labor force. Monetary values of interests are deflated to 2024 USD using the annual all‐items US Consumer Price Index (all urban consumers, not seasonally adjusted). 15 We winsorize annual earnings at the 99th percentile. 16

Cognitive classification

The HRS utilizes a brief cognitive screening tool, the modified Telephone Interview for Cognitive Assessment (mTICS), to measure cognitive functioning. The mTICS scores do not represent a clinical diagnosis but allow the identification of cognitive impairment using tests of immediate and delayed recall of 10 words, five trials of serial 7s, and backward counting. The RAND HRS includes a variable that sums total word recall, serial 7s, and backwards counting from 20, resulting in a range of 0 to 27. 13 We use the Langa–Weir classification 17 to distinguish different stages of cognitive health: A score of 12 or greater is defined as normal cognitive functioning, a score of 7 to 11 indicates CIND, a proxy for MCI, and a score of 6 or less indicates dementia. Following the approach by Green et al., 18 , 19 we further distinguish the severity of dementia using the measures of psychiatric symptoms and functional abilities included in the HRS, as in Prados et al. 34 To measure psychiatric symptoms, we use the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CESD) scale included in the RAND HRS files, 13 which ranges from 0 to 8, to generate a binary indicator of depressive symptoms and anxiety: A CESD score of 4 and higher indicates symptoms of depression and anxiety. An individual is classified as having functional difficulties if he/she is dependent for at least two of the following instrumental activities of daily living: managing money, shopping for groceries, preparing hot meals, using the telephone, and taking medications. Among those who have a cognition score of 6 or less, a respondent is categorized as having mild dementia if he/she does not have a psychiatric and functional problem, as moderate dementia if the respondent has either a psychiatric or functional problem and severe dementia if the respondent has both. 19

Incident cases were identified using respondents for whom two consecutive waves of data are available and selecting those with normal scores in the first wave and a score consistent with MCI or dementia in the second. In the main analysis, we only retained incident cases of MCI or mild dementia. Excluding the incident cases of moderate or severe dementia, which might not be truly incident cases, represents a more conservative approach. In the sensitivity analysis, we included those additional cases who transition directly to moderate or severe dementia from a normal cognitive status.

Analytic approach

We estimated associations of developing early‐stage cognitive impairment due to any etiology on labor market outcomes using a matching approach. We estimated the differential change in labor force participation, labor earnings, and social assistance payments between incident cases of cognitive impairment and matched cognitively normal counterparts. We estimated the associations separately by sex because of lower labor market participation and earnings of women. Subsequently, we extrapolated the results of the survey population to the U.S. population to obtain a national estimate. We then applied estimates for the hypothetical effect size of a preclinical treatment to reflect its potential value.

Matching algorithm

In observational studies, a simple comparison between two groups that differ on an exposure may not identify its average effect correctly if other variables that are correlated with both the exposure and the outcome of interest (confounders) are not well balanced between both groups. As age, chronic disease, and socioeconomic characteristics are risk factors for cognitive impairment and also influence labor market outcomes, we used a matching estimator that created a synthetic comparison group by matching on other observable characteristics. In other words, we compared labor market outcomes in respondents with incident cognitive impairment with those that resemble the incident cases in all observable aspects other than disease onset. With this non‐parametric method, we estimated the impact of disease onset only in affected individuals compared to non‐afflicted “statistical twins.” This approach differs from controlling for observable characteristics in a regression that computes average effects in the entire sample. The difference is most relevant because the incident and non‐affected groups differed substantially in their characteristics.

We used the nearest‐neighbor matching estimator in Abadie and Imbens. 20 This approach allowed us to match exactly on a subset of covariates that are of first‐order importance for incidence and outcomes. In this large sample, this matching approach achieved a good‐quality comparison group with the advantage of increased transparency and no need to rely on model choice compared with propensity score matching. 21 , 22 We matched the incident cases in the survey – starting at the wave with incident cognitive impairment and for the rest of their time in the survey – to at least two cognitively normal respondents (ties in matching are included). We matched with replacement, allowing each unit in the reference group to be used as a match more than once if necessary. The underlying assumption in this estimation was that after conditioning on a set of observed covariates, the effective incidence of cognitive impairment was independent of potential outcomes. We chose observable characteristics that are known to jointly influence incidence and progression of dementia and AD, as well as labor market outcomes. We required matched pairs to coincide exactly in the wave of the interview, age (in years), and indicators for non‐Hispanic white, college degree or more, married, and stroke, and we chose matches that minimized the Mahalanobis distance for the following survey variables: race, Hispanic ethnicity, other marital status, indicator for any children in the family, highest educational degree, quartile of maximum labor earnings observed, and indicators for obesity, smoking history, diabetes, and high blood pressure. The set of covariates included the risk factors that represent the highest population attributable fraction for dementia according to the Lancet Commission Report 23 and may also be associated with labor market outcomes.

In the estimation, which used all data waves, 431 (1.4%) (313, 0.8%) observations from 268 (179) male (female) respondents were excluded due to having fewer than two exact matches. Of the 7033 (8252) male (female) control patients, 231 (272) were matched more than once. Table A.1 presents the pre‐ and post‐matching differences.

Statistical analysis

The estimated average effect of cognitive impairment incidence on individuals’ outcomes was calculated as the difference between their observed average outcomes after onset and the estimated average counterfactual outcomes they would have experienced had they remained cognitively unimpaired. Statistically, this difference can be expressed as E[YMCI,i−YNormal,i|MCIi=1], where Y represents the outcomes of interest, and MCI i = 1 indicates individual i is in the incident cognitive impairment group (MCI i = 0 indicates reference group). The effect of cognitive impairment incidence, given the covariate values X, is δX=E[YMCI−YNormal|Xi,MCIi=1] . Since we could not observe the counterfactual E[YNormal,i|MCIi=1], the matching estimator imputed this missing potential outcome for each individual using an average of the outcomes of similar subjects that remained cognitively intact. Assuming unobservable characteristics that influence incidence do not affect potential outcomes conditional on observables X, the effect of cognitive impairment incidence was approximated by δX=E[YMCI|Xi,MCIi=1]−E[YNormal|Xi,MCIi=0]. 24 The matching estimator combined the sample counterpart of δX for each combination of covariates in a weighted average using the empirical distribution of covariates among the afflicted.

We performed separate matching and estimations by sex, as men's and women's labor outcome dynamics are different. We estimated the association of incidence of cognitive impairment with labor earnings using the subsample of respondents in our analytic sample who were in the labor force at the time of incident. Each estimation used all available observations for the incident subsample and the relevant observations from the matched subsample.

Estimation of overall economic loss

We calculated the average annual U.S. national economic loss due to progression from cognitively unimpaired to MCI or mild dementia by first scaling the incident cases identified in the HRS up to the national level. We apply the age‐ and sex‐specific proportions of the incident cases in the HRS as multipliers to the corresponding age and sex brackets of the 2024 U.S. Census population estimates. 25 The number of cases was divided by two to generate annual numbers, as the HRS survey is fielded every other year, and we identified incident cases from changes between consecutive waves.

To obtain the aggregate values of loss in labor earnings and reductions in labor force size, we multiplied the projected annual national number of incident cases who were in the labor force before the incident by the estimated individual level impacts of disease progression on average labor earnings and labor force participation, separately for men and women. We projected the additional participation in SSDI/SSI and additional SSDI/SSI benefit payments by multiplying the projected annual national number of incident cases by the estimated average impact on SSDI/SSI participation and by the average benefit amounts from SSDI/SSI for incident cases.

Attribution of economic loss to AD

We projected the subset of cases with incident cognitive impairment that was expected to be due to AD rather than other etiologies. For this, we formed the ratio of age‐specific prevalence estimates for MCI due to AD from Gustavsson et al. 26 to MCI of any cause from Petersen et al. 27 to approximate the proportions of early‐stage incident AD among all cases of incident cognitive impairment (see Table A.2 in the appendix for details). The age‐specific proportions ranged from 30.9% (50 to 54 years old) to 55.1% between 75 and 79 years old. These were then applied to our estimates of the national number of incident cognitive impairment cases due to any etiology to obtain the projected number of cases of incident cognitive impairment due to AD. The appendix reproduces the prevalence estimates used to compute the ratios and reports estimated total cases (Table A.3). The ratio of cases attributable to AD over all cases is then used as a multiplier to calculate the economic loss due to incident early‐stage AD.

Uncertainty analysis

We conducted an uncertainty analysis using a Monte Carlo simulation with 20,000 iterations. For the coefficients estimated in this study (regressions on labor earnings, labor force participation, and Social Security participation), we assumed normal distributions parameterized by the estimated means and standard deviations. For parameters obtained from external sources (the sex‐ and age‐specific fraction of MCI attributable to AD) and for the analytical sample averages (incidence, Social Security benefits), we assumed uniform distributions with a coefficient of variation of 25%.

For our main analysis, we identified 5232 incident cases of MCI and mild dementia. We identified 91 additional cases of seemingly newly developed moderate or severe dementia, which we included only in our sensitivity analysis. Table 1 also presents a comparison of statistics for the subsample without cognitive impairment with respect to the subsample with incident cognitive impairment. Before matching, the demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of the two subsamples differed significantly: The subsample with incident cognitive impairment was significantly older (67.5 vs 63.4 years old), more likely to be male (47% vs 43%), to have high school degree or less (87% vs 63%), to belong to a racial/ethnic minority (49% vs 24%), to have obesity (35% vs 33%), and to collect SSDI/SSI benefits (15% vs 7%). The incident cognitive impairment subsample is less likely to be in the labor force (37% vs 61%) and has lower levels of earnings and of SSDI/SSI benefits (US$1267 vs US$1452).

Labor market outcomes

Table 3 reports the estimation results for men (panel A) and women (panel B). Incident cognitive impairment is significantly associated with reductions in annual earnings and in labor force participation. Both women and men are around 5 percentage points (p.p.) (95% CI: ∼ −7 to −3) less likely to participate in the labor force after onset of cognitive impairment than matched controls if they were previously in the labor force (column 2), from baseline rates of 76% and 75% for men and women, respectively. Therefore, labor market participation would decline in subsequent periods by more than 6% in relative terms. Of the overall reduction, around 2.8 and 2.4 percentage points for men and women, respectively, occurred in the same wave as incidence (column 3). The estimated reduction in annual earnings for those who remained in the labor force was US$8233 (95% CI: −9703 to −6762) for men and US$5616 (95% CI: −6789 to −4443) for women (column 1). In relative terms, annual earnings would decline by 23% for men and 18% for women remaining in the labor force after developing cognitive impairment.

Participation in social assistance programs

Table 3 reports that incident cognitive impairment is significantly and positively associated with the receipt of benefits from SSDI/SSI of 3.5 p.p. (95% CI: 0.026 to 0.045) for men from a basis of 13% and 5.6 p.p. for women (95% CI: 0.048 to 0.065) from a basis of 15%. Put differently, the development of cognitive impairment is associated with a 36% increase of likelihood of receiving SSI/SSDI benefits in men and 69% in women. The average annual benefit amount from SSDI/SSI for new recipients was US$1550 and US$1109 for men and women, respectively.

Projected economic effects of incident cognitive impairment

The estimated average earnings loss across all incident cases not previously retired was US$4604 (95% CI: −5573 to −3635) for men and US$1873 (95% CI: −2583 to −1163) for women. These amounts are consistent with the estimated reductions in both extensive (labor force participation) and intensive (earnings conditional on participation) margins mentioned earlier.

Table 4 reports the expected aggregate annual economic loss due to progression from preclinical AD to MCI and mild dementia due to AD for ages 50 to 79 on labor earnings, labor force, and participation in SSDI/SSI, using the projected national number of incident cases due to AD. The simulated total loss of labor earnings is more than US$1.2 billion (2024 dollars; 95% simulation interval: US$0.83 billion to US$1.72 billion), and more than 18,000 workers (95% simulation interval: 12,000 to 25,000) would leave the labor force. SSDI/SSI rolls would grow by approximately 26,000 (95% simulation interval: 18,000 to 32,000), with a corresponding increase in benefit payments of about $42 million USD (2024 dollars; 95% simulation interval: US$24 million to US$63 million) annually.

Sensitivity analyses

We relaxed the definition of incidence to include all cases of incident MCI or dementia of any severity. This resulted in 91 additional respondents in the incident group, to a total of 20,823 instead of 20,732. The estimation results using this expanded sample were almost identical to the main results and are presented in Table A.4.

DISCUSSION

Our results show that incident cognitive impairment is strongly associated with negative labor market outcomes for the afflicted individuals. Using a matching estimation approach, we found that incidence of cognitive impairment was associated with substantially lower annual earnings of afflicted workers while active in the labor force (US$8233 and US$5616 for men and women, respectively) and a 5‐p.p. higher likelihood of exiting the labor force. Those losses are significantly larger than those estimated for individuals who have developed and are progressing from MCI due to AD, which Prados et al. estimated at US$1600 and US$876 for working men and women, respectively. This result is to be expected because the preclinical AD population is younger and less impaired than the MCI and mild dementia due to AD population.

According to our estimates, around half of the decrease in labor force participation of the individuals afflicted by cognitive impairment appeared in the same wave as the onset of cognitive impairment, and the remaining decrease occurred within a 2‐year span. This sequence of changes provides reassurance that the estimated effects are not due to reverse causation, that is, retirement in itself might have increases the risk of cognitive impairment, as has been postulated in the literature. 28

Quantitatively, our estimates for productivity loss due to incident cognitive impairment were of a similar order of magnitude as that calculated by the limited existing research on this topic. Using a published algorithm that associates time use data from the American Time Use Survey with health‐related quality‐of‐life scores, the authors approximated how many fewer hours patients with AD might spend on labor market activities and estimated annual productivity losses of US$4608 per case. 29

Qualitatively, our results complement research that found a rise in unemployment and in long‐term sick leave rates in Danish individuals younger than 65 years who were diagnosed with AD. 30 This result is consistent with cognitive challenges starting in the preclinical phase of AD, and it highlights the potential benefits from secondary prevention. Similar findings come from two recent working papers that explored the association of genetic risk for AD based on the apolipoprotein E ε4 (APOE ε4) carrier status and polygenic risk scores and labor market outcomes. The first 31 found female but not male APOE ε4 carriers and those with one standard deviation above average risk scores experienced significantly worse economic outcomes. The second also reported that higher genetic risk for AD was associated with worse economic outcomes on several dimensions, including work, income, and wealth. 32

Extrapolating our estimates of incident cognitive impairment cases due to AD to the U.S. national level, the aggregate annual monetary cost of progression from preclinical to MCI and mild dementia due to AD disease is around US$1.27 billion, 97% of which is due to lost earnings of previously non‐retired individuals and the remainder due to the annual increase of 26,000 additional social assistance programs (SSDI and SSI) beneficiaries, at an average yearly benefit level of US$1485. Put differently, a hypothetical treatment that reduces progression from preclinical to MCI and mild dementia due to AD by 50% administered to patients aged 50 to 79 would yield labor market gains of more than half a billion dollars in individual productivity per year. Moreover, as patients with AD progress to more severe stages, there are additional impacts on the labor market beyond patients’ participation in the labor force. The literature contains estimates that one in five of those caring for someone with Alzheimer's or other dementias had to leave their jobs, either temporarily or permanently, due to caregiving demands. 33 Subsequent societal costs of individuals progressing further from MCI and mild dementia due to AD are substantially higher, estimated at $49 billion per year, with formal social care amounting to the lion's share of about $9.9 billion. 34

By directly examining incident cognitive impairment in a U.S. nationally representative survey or adults 50 and older, this paper extends the literature in three important ways. First, it estimates associations across multiple economic outcomes – participation, earnings, and public transfer receipt – capturing both private and fiscal consequences. Second, it situates cognitive impairment within the broader U.S. institutional context, enhancing policy relevance. Third, by quantifying the potential national impact of treating pre‐clinical AD in terms of preserved human capital, it bridges epidemiological and economic evidence, translating clinical intervention into macroeconomic and public finance implications. In doing so, the paper advances understanding of the economic value of early detection and treatment beyond traditional cost‐of‐illness approaches.

Limitations

The results should be viewed in the context of the study's limitations. First, despite the robust research design using a longitudinal panel and statistically matched comparators, observational studies remain vulnerable to residual confounding. Diagnoses of cognitive impairment in the HRS are based on telephonic assessments rather than a full clinical evaluation, which may lead to false positive assignment. 35 However, false positive cases would bias our results downward. Self‐reported survey data on outcomes may be more prone to errors and, thus, noisier in cognitively impaired survey respondents. 36 We did not include the small component (1%) of proxy respondents on cognitive state, but that exclusion was not likely to change our results. Moreover, cognitively impaired survey respondents may have differential attrition rates than cognitively normal respondents. Estimates of the economic benefit of a treatment for preclinical AD use a hypothetical effect size that has not yet been confirmed with clinical trial results. Our estimation imputes counterfactual outcomes for incident respondents while they are alive and does not account for differential mortality or attrition – both of which are higher after development and subsequent progression of cognitive impairment. 37 Attribution of the economic impact to AD rather than other etiologies was based on population estimates rather than biomarker confirmation of the disease in included individuals, which may have misestimated the attributed amount.

Conclusions

Incident cognitive impairment has a statistically significant and meaningful association with labor force participation and earnings as well as participation in social assistance programs. The economic losses identified are comparatively higher than the ones found for MCI and mild AD. This reinforces the importance of considering these economic losses when analyzing the value of treatments for preclinical AD.
