A predictive model for evaluating the risk of latent tuberculosis relapse via machine learning
Authors: Chunze Wang, Gao Li, Heqiu Ruan, Jiaxing Lin, Xiaoyan Tang, Jiangtao Zhang, Jingting Li, Xiaoling Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReactivation of latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) is a major obstacle to tuberculosis eradication. Predicting LTBI relapse is crucial for effective disease management but remains underexplored.MethodsWe analysed gene expression data from the GSE54992 dataset via differential expression analysis and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to identify genes associated with LTBI relapse. A risk-predictive model was developed using three genes (ALG2, FARS2, and PGP) selected through a support vector machine with recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), and random forest algorithms. Model performance was assessed via the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was employed to predict correlations between gene activities and immune pathways in monocytes, and gene expression was validated in infected macrophages.ResultsWe identified 279 co-differentially expressed genes between LTBI and active tuberculosis infection (ATBI) patients. A final set of three predictive genes (ALG2, FARS2, and PGP) was selected, yielding AUC values of 0.781 and 0.725 for validation. GSEA revealed that ALG2 was associated with immune response pathways, whereas FARS2 and PGP were linked to energy and lipid metabolism in LTBI. The expression levels of these three genes were correlated with the BCG load.ConclusionThis integrative model provides a robust tool for assessing LTBI reactivation risk and supports more personalized tuberculosis management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11881-8.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) continues to be a major global public health crisis, with 98% of cases concentrated in low- and middle-income countries. In 2023, TB affected an estimated 10.8 million people and caused 1.25 million deaths. This resurgence highlights persistent challenges in funding and healthcare access, particularly in high-burden regions [1]. A key factor in the persistence of TB is LTBI, which is characterized by an asymptomatic and dormant state of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) within the host [2]. Although LTBI is neither contagious nor fatal, its potential to reactivate presents a significant threat by potentially leading to TB transmission [3].

The lifetime risk of LTBI reactivation ranges from 5% to 10%[4, 5]. Preventive treatment has been shown to reduce the risk of progression to active TB by 60% to 90%[6]. However, challenges remain in accurately identifying potential reactivated LTBI individuals and implementing early targeted interventions. Current diagnostic tools, including the tuberculin skin test, interferon-gamma release assays, and 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography, lack the necessary specificity and sensitivity to distinguish between potential reactivated LTBI and silent LTBI [7, 8]. This highlights the crucial need for ongoing research into predicting LTBI relapse, which is vital for effective TB control measures.

Recent advances in bioinformatics and gene expression profiling have opened new avenues for identifying and predicting therapeutic targets in various diseases, including TB. The use of blood transcriptomics for early TB diagnosis has shown significant potential and clinical relevance. Pioneering work by Berry et al. identified interferon-induced transcripts in blood samples from patients with active TB and LTBI, introducing novel biomarkers and emphasizing the value of transcriptomic applications [9]. Subsequent studies have identified key transcripts, such as the “Zak 16” and “RISK4” features, which strongly predict disease progression, although their effectiveness decreases with delayed diagnosis of active TB[10, 11].

The emergence of machine learning technologies offers new methods for distinguishing between LTBI and ATBI [12, 13]. Machine learning models excel at processing large datasets and continuously improve accuracy through ongoing learning [14]. These models autonomously make decisions, reducing their reliance on the subjective judgment of diagnosticians [15]. They are adaptable, adjusting to new data to increase prediction accuracy, and effectively handle complex multidimensional data [16, 17]. Research has demonstrated that machine learning algorithms can efficiently screen signature genes, yielding predictive models with superior performance compared with traditional methods based on histological biomarkers [18]. Algorithms such as SVM-RFE, LASSO, and random forest have shown the potential to mitigate the risk of overfitting in predictive models [19].

The application of high-throughput biotechnologies and advanced analytical methods has significantly increased the speed and accuracy of diagnostics [16], further facilitating the implementation of personalized prevention strategies and profoundly impacting patient care and public health [20]. Moreover, robust quantitative analysis has substantially improved the predictive accuracy of the model [21], making it a key tool in bridging clinical practice with public health initiatives.

In this study, a machine learning model was developed to predict the reactivation of LTBI, achieving an AUC of 0.778. This model underwent external validation using macrophage and whole blood samples, with AUC values of 0.781 and 0.725, respectively. Functional and phenotypic analyses revealed that the core genes in the model played significant roles in macrophage immune responses, as well as in lipid and energy metabolism. They are also significantly associated with macrophage phenotypes, which are critical in the early stages of LTBI reactivation [22, 23]. This predictive model aimed to identify asymptomatic LTBI patients at high risk of reactivation, enabling timely and effective intervention.

Training Dataset: GSE54992[24] was used as the primary training dataset, including baseline data from six healthy controls, six LTBI individuals, and nine TB patients, as well as longitudinal data from 18 TB patients at three and nine months of treatment.

Validation Datasets: GSE11199[25], GSE19439[9], and GSE19444[9] were used for external validation. GSE11199 comprises a total of 24 samples, including 12 samples from the H37Rv stimulation group (4 from LTBI and 8 from ATBI individuals) and 12 samples from the PBS control group. Our study focused on analyzing the 12 samples from the H37Rv stimulation group to evaluate the immune response related to latent tuberculosis reactivation. GSE19439 and GSE19444 feature profiles from 13 to 21 ATBI individuals, 17 and 21 LTBI individuals, and 12 healthy controls, respectively.

To ensure the quality and comparability of the microarray data, a uniform preprocessing workflow was applied to both the training dataset (GSE54992) and the validation datasets (GSE11199, GSE19439, and GSE19444). To address batch effects, the ComBat algorithm within the “SVA” package (R version 3.50.0) was applied [26]. These datasets served as independent validation sets to evaluate the model’s applicability across diverse populations and sample types.

Differential expression analysis of GSE54992 expression data from the GEO database

Differential expression analysis was conducted using the “limma” package to identify genes differentially expressed between healthy donors (HDs), LTBI, and ATBI groups, with a stringent significance threshold of adjusted p < 0.05.

Visualization included volcano plots highlighting the top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with adjusted p < 0.01 and logFC > 2, as well as hierarchical clustering of the top 10 DEGs to elucidate expression patterns. Comparative analysis identified co-DEGs across conditions, which were extracted for further biological analysis.

WGCNA and intersecting gene acquisition

Key modules and characteristic genes associated with TB were identified from the GSE54992 dataset using the WGCNA package in R[27]. Genes were selected based on the top 75% MAD to ensure robustness against outliers.

A scale-free network was constructed by systematically evaluating soft-thresholding powers to optimize scale independence and network connectivity. A minimum module size of 50 genes was set to avoid spurious clusters.

Gene expression profiles were correlated to compute a similarity matrix, transformed into a TOM to quantify dissimilarity. Hierarchical clustering grouped genes into modules based on expression profiles. Modules significantly associated with ATBI (p < 0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg corrected) were identified using Pearson correlation.

Genes within significant modules were filtered using MM and GS thresholds of 0.8. The Venn package identified the intersection of DEGs with WGCNA characteristic genes, enabling focused analysis of relevant gene sets.

Employing machine learning algorithms for predictive modelling of LTBI relapse

Key genes predictive of LTBI relapse were identified using three machine learning algorithms: LASSO (glmnet v4.1–8), SVM-RFE (e1071 v1.7–14), and random forest (Random Forest v4.7-1.1.1) in R. These algorithms were applied to a common gene set derived from prior intersection analysis to identify pivotal candidates, followed by cross-method intersection to consolidate robust predictors. Predictive accuracy of resulting gene combinations was assessed via AUC from ROC analysis (pROC v1.18.5). A logistic regression model, trained on hub gene expression data (GSE54992), was optimized using ROC and Nomo score analyses. The final model was validated on an independent dataset to ensure diagnostic robustness.

Development and validation of predictive models with hub gene expression analysis

To predict LTBI relapse, we employed three machine learning algorithms: LASSO, SVM-RFE, and Random Forest. These algorithms were implemented in R using the “glmnet” (version 4.1–8.1), “e1071” (version 1.7–14), and “RandomForest” (version 4.7–1.1) packages. Each algorithm was applied to a previously identified set of intersecting genes to select pivotal genes.

The key genes identified by each algorithm were further intersected to consolidate findings across methodologies, enhancing the robustness of gene selection. This analysis yielded a refined list of key genes critical for predicting LTBI relapse.

The predictive accuracy of these genes was validated by modelling different combinatorial patterns, with each model’s performance evaluated using the AUC via the “pROC” (version 1.18.5) package. A comprehensive predictive model was developed using logistic regression on a training set, with ROC analysis and Nomo scores optimizing model configuration based on the highest AUC values. The final model was rigorously validated using an independent dataset, ensuring its reliability as a diagnostic tool for TB management.

Integrated single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of TB reactivation

GSEA was performed using R software (version 4.3.3) and the “clusterProfiler” package (version 4.10.1), a tool for biological theme enrichment analysis and visualization developed by Wu et al.[28].

The macrophage gene expression dataset was pre-processed and normalized, including background correction, quantile normalization, and log transformation, tailored to the database’s characteristics.

The reference gene set was obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), incorporating canonical pathway gene sets from the KEGG MEDICUS pathway database, as supported by Liberzon et al. and Kanehisa and Goto [29, 30].

Enrichment significance was assessed via permutation tests with 1000 permutations. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to adjust p-values for multiple comparisons, controlling the false discovery rate [31].

A heatmap was generated using an online bioinformatics platform to visualize enriched pathways, facilitating comprehensive data interpretation.

Integrated GSEA to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of tuberculosis reactivation

To identify biological pathways associated with DEGs, GSEA was conducted. DEGs from the GSE54992 dataset were annotated and filtered using a significance level of p < 0.05 and a logFC criterion. Genes were classified as upregulated, downregulated, or stable based on these criteria.

Gene symbols were converted to ENTREZ IDs using the Human Gene Database (org.Hs.eg.db) to ensure accurate identification in GSEA. The converted data were merged with the original DEG dataset, and genes were ranked by logFC in descending order to create a ranked list for GSEA.

Using the “clusterProfiler” R package (version 4.10.1), GSEA was performed to identify Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways associated with DEGs. The analysis parameters included 10,000 permutations, a minimum gene set size of 15, a maximum gene set size of 200, a p-value cutoff of 0.05, and a multiple testing correction of 0.25.

A heatmap was generated using an online bioinformatics platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn) to visualize the biological functions and significance of DEGs, providing key insights into their roles in LTBI and ATBI.

Immune cell population analysis and correlation assessment methods to analyse differences between LTBI and ATBI

The LM22 gene signature set was obtained from the CIBERSORTx website. To assess immune cell proportions in LTBI and ATBI samples from GSE54992, gene expression data were normalized via Z-score standardization. CIBERSORT analysis was performed using an unbiased regression approach to deconvolute immune cell composition, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Data visualization and analysis were conducted in R. The ggplot2 package visualized immune cell proportions between LTBI and ATBI. Additional visualizations utilized “ggpubr” and “cowplot”. Correlations between key genes (ALG2, FARS2, PGP) and immune cell types were explored using “corrplot” and “ggstatsplot”, with Spearman correlation and multiple testing corrections.

THP-1 cell culture, BCG infection, and gene expression analysis

THP-1 human monocytic cells (SCSP, SCSP-567) were cultured in THP-1 culture medium (Procell, CM-0233) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Differentiation was induced by treating the cells with 100 ng/ml phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; HY-18739; MedChem Express, Shanghai, China) for 48 h. Following differentiation, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated in fresh medium for an additional 24 h. Differentiated macrophages were subsequently infected with live Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5, 10, or 20 for 6 h. After infection, the macrophages were washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular bacteria and cultured for 48 h.

Total RNA was extracted from infected cells via the AxyPrep Total RNA Small Volume Preparation Kit (Axygen, AP-RN50). The RNA quality was assessed via a Nano Drop spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized via the Power Up™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix Kit (Thermo Fisher, A25742). Quantitative RT‒PCR was performed via the Bio-Rad CFX Maestro system (Bio-Rad, CFX96), with β-actin used as the reference gene. Relative gene expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCT method with the primers listed in Table 2. Results

Key transcriptional changes associated with LTBI relapse revealed by differential expression analysis

To explore the genetic determinants influencing the progression from LTBI to ATBI, we analysed gene expression profiles comparing HD to individuals with LTBI and HD to those with ATBI from GSE54992. Our study revealed that 872 genes were differentially expressed between HD and LTBI, and a larger set of 6,381 genes were differentially expressed between HD and ATBI. The results indicated that the differences between LTBI and HD are less pronounced than those between ATBI and HD are, suggesting that the gene expression differences between LTBI and HD are subtle, partially explaining the asymptomatic nature of latent infections. Furthermore, these findings are consistent with the significant transcriptional changes accompanying disease progression.

To analyse these changes, we first normalized the gene expression data from the GSE54992 dataset, a crucial step for accurate differential analysis. The top 10 significantly upregulated and 10 downregulated genes between HD vs. LTBI (Fig. 1A), and HD vs. ATBI were identified (Fig. 1C). The genes that were most significantly upregulated in LTBI patients compared with HD patients, including TECRL, LINC02152, PRR5-ARHGAP8, and LOC105376139, and those that were significantly downregulated, including EAF2, PTPN18, and LINP1, were demonstrated in volcano plots to visualize the magnitude of the significant differences in gene expression (Fig. 1B). Similarly, Fig. 1D presents the most significantly upregulated and downregulated genes in ATBI patients compared with HD patients, such as HLA-DQB1, TAFA1, P2RY13; CCL7, CCL20 and GEM. Key gene modules associated with tuberculosis progression

To identify gene modules associated with the progression from LTBI to ATBI, we conducted WGCNA using the same dataset (GSE54992). A soft-thresholding power of β = 2 was selected, resulting in a scale-free topology fit index exceeding 0.9 and an average connectivity of 2000 to ensure a robust network architecture (Fig. 2A). A dynamic tree-cutting approach identified four distinct gene modules (Fig. 2B). We then correlated these modules with the clinical classifications of HD, LTBI, and ATBI, visualized through a heatmap (Fig. 2C). Notably, the turquoise module demonstrated a very strong positive correlation with ATBI (r = 0.98, p < 0.0001), and the blue module exhibited a significant negative correlation (r = −0.7, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2D). Deciphering genetic predictors of tuberculosis progression through machine learning models

To unravel the complex genetic underpinnings that facilitate the progression from LTBI to ATBI, we employed three distinct machine learning algorithms: SVM-RFE, LASSO regression, and random forest. These methods were selected for their specific strengths in handling large-scale genomic data and their ability to robustly identify significant predictors despite the high dimensionality and multicollinearity inherent in such datasets. Each algorithm provided unique insights into the genomic features most associated with disease progression.

SVM-RFE is particularly adept at managing high-dimensional data, making it ideal for genomic applications. It ranks features on the basis of their impact on model accuracy and eliminates the least significant features iteratively [32]. In this study, SVM-RFE identified 22 pivotal genes that potentially influence TB progression (Fig. 3A; Table 4). Known for its robustness against overfitting, random forest analyses multiple decision trees to improve predictive accuracy and control for bias. Its capacity to capture complex interactions among variables makes it indispensable [34]. This method helped identify 12 pivotal genes linked to TB progression (Fig. 3C), with criteria for gene selection detailed to ensure transparency in the methodological framework.

A comparative analysis of the results from these models revealed a significant overlap of three genes (Fig. 3D). This convergence across disparate models indicates a potential synergistic effect of these genes on the relapse of LTTB, warranting further exploration.

We constructed three predictive models on the basis of these genes and validated their efficacy through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (Fig. 3E). The models exhibited AUCs of 0.759, 0.778, and 0.778 for models 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Notably, the model incorporating the smallest gene set (3 genes) was deemed the most practical for clinical application because of its equivalent or superior predictive performance and enhanced feasibility. This suggests that a streamlined gene set may be sufficient for effective prediction, simplifying testing procedures and making them more accessible for clinical use.

To enhance the generalizability of the predictive model, the joint datasets GSE19399 and GSE19444 for whole blood cell samples, along with the GSE11199 dataset for mononuclear-derived macrophages, were selected for ROC analysis to predict disease progression. The model demonstrated robust predictive ability, achieving AUCs of 0.781 for the macrophage samples and 0.725 for the whole blood samples (Fig. 4B). The slightly lower AUC observed in whole blood samples may reflect the increased complexity of these datasets, as they encompass a broader spectrum of immune cell types. This inherent heterogeneity in whole blood-derived samples could dilute the specific contribution of macrophage-associated signals, potentially impacting model performance. Notably, the consistent performance of our model across these diverse datasets, which differ in both sample composition and size, underscores its remarkable robustness and generalizability. These findings provide strong evidence for the reliability and applicability of our predictive model in various experimental and clinical settings.

The feasibility of the model, which uses readily collected and processed whole blood, underscores its practical applicability in routine clinical settings. This advantage positions the predictive model as a valuable tool for early diagnosis and intervention in tuberculosis management.

The dynamic expression patterns of ALG2, FARS2, and PGP were further analysed during TB infection and after infection. While ALG2 expression gradually decreased with disease progression and increased with time after disease onset, FARS2 and PGP exhibited opposite expression patterns (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that ALG2 may function as a protective factor during TB infection, whereas FARS2 and PGP may serve as risk factors. The biological significance of these observations, potentially related to their involvement in specific metabolic pathways influencing the immune response to TB, warrants further investigation.

Single-gene enrichment analysis reveals the biological functions of the predicted genes in LTBI macrophages

To elucidate the impact of intracellular gene expression within macrophages on latent tuberculosis reactivation, a single-gene GSEA was performed on the GSE11199 dataset. A reference gene set of 658 genes, derived from the KEGG_MEDICUS subset of the classical pathway within the C2 signature gene set, was employed to investigate potential biological pathways influencing disease progression. As the primary host cells for Mtb during LTBI, macrophages play crucial roles in intracellular bacterial replication, proliferation, and immune evasion. Alterations in macrophage intracellular metabolic pathways have been shown to directly impact these Mtb mechanisms [35, 36].

The normalized enrichment score (NES) was employed to quantify the correlation between the expression of the predicted genes and the respective gene sets. These genes were found to be significantly enriched in multiple pathways associated with immune regulation and host defence responses, as indicated by NES values greater than 1 or less than − 1, with a false discovery rate (FDR) less than 0.05.

PGP was significantly enriched in 123 pathways, with 119 exhibiting NES > 1 and 4 pathways with NES<−1 (Suppl. Table 3). In conclusion, the distinct yet overlapping pathways enriched across ALG2, FARS2, and PGP suggest a complex interplay of metabolic and immune-related processes in LTBI relapse. These findings may inform further investigations into specific cellular mechanisms and therapeutic targets for preventing latent tuberculosis relapse, providing a foundation for developing predictive models for this patient population.

Discussion

The current study presents a predictive model for LTBI relapse, based on the expression of three genes-ALG2, FARS2, and PGP-which are functionally implicated in energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, and macrophage immune regulation. Notably, the model exhibited robust performance in consistent predictive accuracy across diverse sample types, AUC values of 0.781 in macrophage-derived samples, 0.778 in PBMC samples, and 0.725 in whole blood samples. Especially, the model’s ability to achieve acceptable accuracy (AUC > 0.7) in whole blood and PBMC highlights its clinical potential for practical application in LTBI relapse prediction.

The genes identified by three machine learning models are critical regulators of host cell metabolism and immune responses in individuals with LTBI, aligning with the established conclusion that host immune responses are central to LTBI reactivation [37, 38]. Of particular interest, ALG2 exerts a critical regulatory role in DNA-induced innate immune responses by controlling STING trafficking, thereby modulating the production of type I interferons and inflammatory cytokines in HSV-1-infected THP-1 monocytes [39]. Furthermore, under nutrient stress conditions such as mTOR inhibition or amino acid starvation, ALG2 has been demonstrated to regulate innate immune responses by mediating lysosome dependent endoplasmic reticulum exit sites micro-autophagy, a process essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis [40]. The role of ALG2 in immune signalling and autophagy in LTBI relapse needs further investigation. As established, FARS2 is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis that its deficiency leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, hyper-fragmentation, and disrupted mitophagy [41]. Whether the alterations in FARS2 expression reflects the shifts in cellular metabolic demands, potentially serving as a biomarker for Mtb reactivation from dormancy, is not clear. Additionally, the elevated expression of PGP in ATBI likely reflects the increased metabolic demands imposed by active bacterial replication. PGP, a highly conserved enzyme involved in energy and lipid metabolism, dephosphorylates key metabolic intermediates such as 2-phosphoglycolate and 2-phosphoglycerate, thereby preventing their accumulation and subsequent inhibition of glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway [42, 43]. Collectively, these findings underscore the intricate interplay between metabolic regulation and immune functions in the reactivation of LTBI, highlighting the potential utility of our machine learning model in predicting and understanding disease progression.

A novel 3-gene signature that was established in our study represents a unique advancement in the field of LTBI relapse predication. This signature not only demonstrates the potential molecular mechanisms but also expanding our knowledge beyond the current findings focused on interferon signalling and antiviral immunity. For instance, Zak et al. reported a 16-gene signature predominantly associated with interferon signalling, achieving a sensitivity of 66.1% and specificity of 80.6% in predicting TB progression in a South African adolescent cohort [10]. other studies have identified distinct gene signatures, such as a 3-gene signature (GBP5, DUSP3, KLF2) by Sweeney et al.[44] and Warsinke et al.[45], a 3-gene signature (BATF2, GBP5, SCARF1) by Roe et al.[46], and a 6-gene signature (SERPING1, TRMT2A, GBP2, SDR39U1, FCGR1B, TUBGCP6) by Penn-Nicholson et al.[47]. Notably, the gene signature in current study exhibits significant differences in terms of study design, population characteristics, and biological focus. We emphasis on the interplay between metabolic regulation and immune modulation within macrophages, which are the primary host cell and the initial site for Mtb replication, leading to disease relapse. All these findings highlight the multifaceted nature of tuberculosis pathogenesis and underscore the importance of adopting comprehensive and targeted approaches in the discovery of robust biomarkers for LTBI recurrence.

Our study has several limitations. First, the model was trained exclusively on a dataset derived from the Chinese population, which may limit its generalizability to populations with different genetic backgrounds or environmental exposures, such as variations in ALG2 and FARS2 alleles or differing Mtb strains and BCG vaccination programs. Second, the relatively small dataset size, despite the use of multiple machine learning algorithms, poses a risk of overfitting and potential bias, which could impact the reliability and robustness of the model’s performance. Finally, while we identified associations between ALG2, FARS2, PGP gene expression, and the number of BCG-carrying macrophages, we were unable to establish a causal relationship between these genetic factors and the reactivation of latent tuberculosis infection, highlighting the need for further experimental studies (CRISPR-Cas9 screening in human granuloma organoids as a priority for future validation) to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel predictive model for LTBI relapse based on a three-gene signature involved in key metabolic and immune pathways. While the model demonstrates strong performance across different sample types, further validation in larger cohorts and functional studies are necessary to confirm its clinical applicability. These efforts will be crucial for translating our findings into a practical tool for identifying individuals at high risk of TB reactivation, ultimately contributing to more effective TB control strategies.
