Active Eco-friendly Mucilage-Based Films Reinforced with Graphitic Carbon Nitride (g‑C3N4) for Sustainable Food Packaging Applications
Authors: Adeola F. Muyiwa, Jerry O. Adeyemi, Olaniyi A. Fawole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing global reliance on plastic poses serious environmental and health risks, highlighting the need for biodegradable packaging alternatives. Opuntia ficus-indica mucilage (OFIM) is a promising biopolymer due to its abundance and biodegradability, although its poor mechanical and barrier properties limit direct application. In this study, OFIM-based films reinforced with cellulose nanofibers (CNF) and graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) nanoparticles at 1%, 3%, and 5% were developed for food packaging applications. The g-C3N4 nanoparticles and OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 composite films were characterized using UV–vis spectroscopy, XRD, and FTIR, confirming successful nanoparticle synthesis and incorporation into the biopolymer matrix. The nanocomposite films exhibited significantly improved physicochemical, mechanical, optical, and biological properties (p < 0.05). Increasing g-C3N4 concentration reduced moisture content, solubility, and water vapor permeability while enhancing tensile strength, thickness, and UV-blocking capacity, with optimal overall performance at 5% g-C3N4. Antioxidant activity increased with nanoparticle concentration, as demonstrated by ABTS radical scavenging assays. Antimicrobial evaluation against Enterococcus faecalis, Escherichia coli, and Staphylococcus aureus revealed enhanced inhibition, particularly at 3% g-C3N4, attributed to improved nanoparticle dispersion and bioactive compounds in OFIM. Overall, the OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 nanocomposite films show strong potential as sustainable active food packaging materials, supporting reduced plastic use and postharvest losses in line with the Sustainable Development Goals.

Introduction

Plastic pollution has emerged as one of the most pressing environmental issues, with over 400 million tons produced annually and persistent accumulation in terrestrial and aquatic ecosystem. , Fragmentation into microplastics poses ecological and potential human health risks through bioaccumulation in food systems. , Despite these concerns, petroleum-based polymers such as PE, PP, and PET dominate packaging due to their low cost and favorable mechanical properties. However, they function mainly as passive barriers and lack intrinsic antimicrobial or antioxidant activity, contributing to food spoilage and postharvest losses. ,

These limitations have accelerated the development of sustainable, functional packaging materials. Biopolymer-based systems derived from renewable plant resources offer biodegradability, biocompatibility, and structural adaptability for food-contact applications. , Furthermore, their chemical functionality enables the incorporation of active agents to impart antimicrobial, antioxidant, and improved barrier properties, while supporting circular bioeconomy strategies through the valorization of agricultural byproducts. ,

Mucilage from Opuntia ficus-indica (OFIM) cladodes, a widely available plant in South Africa, represents a promising biopolymer due to its abundance, renewability, and inherent film-forming ability. The utilization of this plant-derived biomass contributes to the valorization of agricultural residues and promotes waste minimization strategies. However, films based solely on mucilage are often limited by their hydrophilic nature, resulting in poor mechanical strength, high moisture sensitivity, and elevated water vapor permeability. While plasticizers enhance film flexibility, their incorporation often compromises tensile strength and water resistance, thereby restricting the practical application of mucilage-based films in food packaging systems. To overcome these drawbacks, recent research has focused on reinforcing mucilage matrices with cellulose nanofibers (CNFs), a natural, high-aspect-ratio filler known to enhance structural integrity and flexibility. CNFs strengthen the polysaccharide network through extensive hydrogen bonding, thereby reducing brittleness and improving mechanical performance. Nonetheless, CNF-reinforced films exhibit moderate to low active functional properties, particularly antimicrobial activity, which is crucial for enhancing the microbial safety of perishable fresh produce. Given the growing global concern over antimicrobial resistance (AMR), highlighted by the World Health Organization as one of the leading threats to global health and food security, the integration of bioactive nanomaterials into biopolymer matrices offers an advanced and sustainable solution.

Among emerging nanomaterials, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) has gained remarkable attention due to its metal-free composition, environmental compatibility, visible-light activity, and chemical robustness. While CNF serves as the principal structural reinforcement, literature reports indicate that exfoliated g-C3N4 nanosheets possess appreciable intrinsic in-plane stiffness (Young’s modulus 120–160 GPa). Unlike many inorganic nanoparticles, g-C3N4 nanoparticles (NPs) exhibit low cytotoxicity and excellent stability, and have demonstrated cytocompatibility toward mammalian cells. Beyond its established photocatalytic applications, g-C3N4-NPs possess inherent antimicrobial and antioxidant functionalities, stemming from their ability to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and to scavenge free radicals, making them suitable for food-contact materials. , Hence, its incorporation into polymer matrices offers a significant pathway to improve oxygen barrier performance, free-radical quenching capacity, and microbial inhibition, which in turn results in enhanced product preservation. , Furthermore, when integrated with CNFs, g-C3N4-NPs, a synergistic nanohybrid structure material ensues, in which the CNF network provides mechanical reinforcement, while g-C3N4-NPs contribute active functional property enhancement. , This dual reinforcement, combining CNF for enhancing mechanical strength and water resistance with g-C3N4-NPs for antimicrobial and antioxidant functionality, provides a balanced improvement in both the structural integrity and active performance of food packaging films. ,

Therefore, this study aims to fabricate an eco-friendly active packaging film by embedding green-synthesized graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) nanoparticles within Opuntia ficus-indica mucilage biopolymer matrices to enhance their functional performance for postharvest preservation, while systematically evaluating the effect of varying g-C3N4 concentrations on the physicochemical, mechanical, optical, and antimicrobial properties of the resulting nanocomposite films.

Materials and Methods

The Opuntia ficus-indica cladodes were sourced from Ubali Pomegranate & Prickly Pear Farm in Pretoria, South Africa (25°38′31.9″ S, 28°27′33.0″ E) and transported to the Postharvest Research Centre at the University of Johannesburg for mucilage extraction. Foodborne bacterial strains, including Gram-negative (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922), Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212), were employed for the biological property evaluations.

Synthesis and Characterization of Graphitic Carbon Nitride

Graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) was synthesized following an established literature protocol. Briefly, 10 g of melamine (nitrogen-rich precursor) was placed in a crucible and thermally polymerized in a muffle furnace at 600 °C for 4 h under an air atmosphere (Figure ). The resulting yellow solid was subsequently ground into fine powder and stored in a desiccator until further use.

Schematic polymerization of nitrogen-rich precursor (melamine).

Extraction of Mucilage from Opuntia ficus-indica Cladode

The extraction of Opuntia ficus-indica mucilage (OFIM) followed a protocol reported in the literature. First, the Opuntia ficus-indica cladodes were peeled, chopped into small cubes, and then microwaved for 4 min at 800 W to facilitate the release of mucilage. The resulting liquid extract was centrifuged at 8000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to isolate the mucilage. This collected mucilage was then freeze-dried for 72 h to yield a fine, dry powder, which was stored in airtight containers for further analysis. The graphical approach is presented in Figure .

Schematic mucilage extraction from cladodes of Opuntia ficus-indica.

Fabrication of Biodegradable OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 Film

Following the literature report on the use of OFIM film formation with some modifications, the nanocomposite film was fabricated by first dissolving the OFIM powder in distilled water at a concentration of 3% (w/v). This was stirred continuously at 50 °C for 30 min. Afterward, 1 mL of Tween 80 and 1 mL of canola oil were added as a blend to improve the film’s flexibility. Glycerol (20% v/v) was incorporated to enhance flexibility. Subsequently, 4% (w/w) CNF was incorporated, and the solution was heated and stirred at 50 °C for 20 min. To incorporate the prepared g-C3N4-NPs (from 2.1), the synthesized g-C3N4-NPs were dispersed into the mucilage solution at different concentrations of 1%, 3%, and 5% (w/v). Thereafter, the solutions were homogenized for 5 min, followed by ultrasonication for 1 h to uniformly disperse g-C3N4-NPs in the homogenized mixture. The resulting mixture was transferred into sterile Petri dishes (30 mL film solution) and allowed to dry at 50 °C for 24 h. The summarized procedure is presented in Figure . The prepared films at 1%, 3%, and 5% (w/v) of g-C3N4 were designated as OFIM/CNF + 1% g-C3N4, OFIM/CNF + 3% g-C3N4, OFIM/CNF + 5% g-C3N4, respectively.

Characterization Approaches for the Synthesized g-C3N4-NPs

Structural and Surface Morphology of g-C3N4-NPs

The size, shape, and internal structure of the synthesized g-C3N4 were investigated utilizing Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) on a JEM-2100 electron microscope. To further analyze the surface morphology, microstructural features, and elemental composition, Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was employed at an operating voltage of 20 kV utilizing SEM, SU8010, in conjunction with Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) employing an AMETEK analyzer, which identifies elements based on the characteristic X-rays emitted during electron beam bombardment.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) of g-C3N4-NPs and the Films

The crystal structure and phase composition were examined using X-ray powder diffraction (XRD), conducted on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (X’Pert PRO-PAN Analytical, USA) equipped with Cu-Kα radiation (40 kV, 20 mA) and a diffracted beam monochromator. The film samples were tested by cutting them into circular shapes with a diameter of 3.5 cm, after which they were placed on the X-ray exposure stage. Data was collected at 2θ ranging from 10° to 50°.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) of g-C3N4-NPs and the Films

The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of g-C3N4-NPs were analyzed utilizing an infrared spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Smart iTR) functioning at a wavenumber 4000 - 600 cm–1. Similarly, nanocomposite films were analyzed by cutting them into strips and placing them on the exposure stage, and individual wavelength measurements were taken.

Ultraviolet Visible Spectroscopy (UV–Vis) of g-C3N4-NPs and the Films

The optical properties of g-C3N4 nanoparticles (NPs) were systematically investigated by introducing a uniformly dispersed sample into a quartz cuvette. Absorbance measurements were conducted utilizing a UV–visible diffuse reflectance spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2450, Kyoto, Japan). The resultant spectra were subsequently analyzed to assess the light absorption characteristics of the sample. Additionally, the absorbance of the films was measured individually by positioning film strips (1 cm × 4 cm) within a cuvette, with measurements taken over the wavelength range of 300 to 600 nm.

2,2′-Azino-bis­(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) Cation (ABTS+) Radical Scavenging Activity of g-C3N4-NPs

The antioxidant activity of the nanomaterials was evaluated using the ABTS+ radical scavenging assay, adapted from a previously reported method. Stock solutions of ABTS (7.0 mM) and potassium persulfate (2.4 mM) were prepared and mixed in a 1:1 ratio and incubated in the dark at 24 °C for 12 h to generate the ABTS+ radical. The working solution was adjusted with 100% methanol to an absorbance of 0.704 ± 0.001 at 734 nm. Nanomaterial samples at concentrations ranging from 0.031 to 0.50 mg/mL were reacted with the ABTS+ solution, vortexed, and incubated in the dark for 7 min. Absorbance was measured at 734 nm using methanol as the blank. Ascorbic acid was used as a positive control, and radical scavenging activity was expressed as percentage inhibition using eq ABTS+radicalscavengingactivity(%)=AbsABTS+−AbssampleAbsABTS+X100% 1 Where Abs ABTS+ signifies the absorbance of the control solution (composed of methanol and ABTS+, excluding the prepared nanomaterials), while Abs sample refers to the absorbance of the prepared nanomaterials.

Characterization of Fabricated Film

Color of the Films

The color characteristics of the nanocomposite films were assessed in accordance with the methodology outlined by Leta et al, employing a portable colorimeter (Konica Minolta Chroma Meter CR-400, Osaka, Japan) that had been precalibrated with a white reference standard to ensure measurement precision. The color parameters were recorded in the CIE Lab* color space, wherein L* denotes brightness, a* signifies red-green chromaticity, and b* represents yellow-blue chromaticity. Each film sample underwent analysis in triplicate to derive average color coordinates. The total color difference (ΔE), which quantifies the overall visual discrepancy between each film and the reference control, was computed using eq : ΔE=[(ΔL)*2+(Δa)*2+(Δb)*2]0.5 2 where ΔL*, Δa*, and Δb* denote the respective differences in L*, a*, and b* values between the sample and the control film.

Moisture Content Determination of the Films

The moisture content (MC) of the nanocomposite films was determined following a previously reported method with minor modifications. Film samples (2 cm × 2 cm) were analyzed in triplicate. The initial mass (M0) was accurately recorded using an analytical balance (RADWAG Electronic PS 4500.R2.M, Poland; accuracy ± 0.01 g). Afterward, the samples were oven-dried at 100 ± 1 °C for 24 h to remove residual moisture. The final mass (M1) was then measured, and the moisture content (%) was calculated based on the difference between the initial and dried weights. MC(%)=M0−M1M0X100 3

Water Solubility Determination of the Films

The water solubility (WS) of the nanocomposite films was assessed following a literature report with minor modifications. Predried film samples (2 cm × 2 cm) were analyzed in triplicate at 50 °C for 24 h. The initial dry weights (W0) of each sample were subsequently recorded using an analytical balance. Thereafter, the samples were immersed in 30 mL of deionized water and maintained at 50 °C overnight with intermittent stirring to promote solubilization. Following this period, the suspensions were filtered through Whatman filter paper to separate and recover the undissolved film residues. The film residues were then dried in an oven at 100 °C for 2 h, and their final weights (W1) were recorded. Water solubility (%) was calculated as the difference between the initial and final dried weights using the following eq : WS(%)=W0−W1W0X100 4

Water Vapor Permeability of the Films

The water vapor permeability (WVP) of the films was examined following the method in literature with minor modifications. Film samples (8 cm × 8 cm) were subjected to triplicate testing, where each specimen was carefully positioned over a cup containing 25 mL of deionized water, and the top was sealed with parafilm to prevent leakage and maintain a controlled environment. Initial weights will be recorded utilizing a precision electronic balance (RADWAG PS 4500.R2.M, Poland; ± 0.01 g accuracy). The prepared receptacles were subsequently placed in a hot air oven maintained at 50 °C with a regulated relative humidity of 50%. The weight loss of each receptacle due to water vapor permeation through the film was monitored hourly for a total of 8 h to assess vapor transmission. The water vapor permeability (WVP) of the film will be determined using the following eq : WVP=WVTR.XP(R1−R2) 5

WVTR (gm –224–1 h) represents the slope (g/h), which is divided by the total surface area (mm2); X (mm) denotes the thickness of the film; and (Δp = p (R2 – R1), R2 = 50% and R1 = 0%).

Mechanical Properties of the Films

Tensile Strength of the Films

The tensile strength (TS) of the fabricated films was analyzed utilizing a texture analyzer, in accordance with a previously reported approach with minor modifications. Film samples were cut into square strips measuring 5 cm × 5 cm and individually subjected to puncture utilizing an 8 mm diameter probe. The maximum force required to rupture each sample was recorded as the puncture resistance. The tensile strength (TS) of the films was subsequently determined using eq . TS=FmaxLxW 6 where Fmax denotes the maximum force (N) applied before film rupture, while L and W correspond to the length (cm) and width (cm) of the tested film samples, respectively.

Film Thickness

The thickness of each film sample was measured following the method by Nxumalo et al. with minor modifications. A digital micrometer, with readings taken at five distinct points per film to ensure precision and consistency. The average thickness of each film was recorded (n = 5).

Opacity of the Films

The opacity of the films was evaluated utilizing the method outlined by Othman et al. Each sample was sectioned into dimensions of 1 cm × 4 cm, and its transparency and opacity were assessed using a UV–visible spectrophotometer calibrated to a wavelength of 600 nm. The opacity of the films was then determined based on the absorbance readings obtained during the analysis and the film thickness (in mm), utilizing the following eq : Filmopacity=Abs600L 7

2,2′-Azino-bis­(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) Cation (ABTS+) Radical Scavenging Activity Assay of the Films

The antioxidant activity of the films was evaluated using the ABTS+ radical scavenging assay, adapted from a previously reported method. Stock solutions of ABTS (7.0 mM) and potassium persulfate (2.4 mM) were prepared and mixed in a 1:1 ratio and incubated in the dark at room temperature for a duration of 12 h to generate the ABTS+ radical. The working solution was adjusted with 100% methanol to an absorbance of 0.700 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. Following this, 50 mg of the film samples were placed in 10 mL of distilled water, vortexed for 2 min, and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min. Three milliliters of the supernatant were then mixed with 1 mL of the methanolic ABTS solution and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was recorded at 734 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Spectrum Instruments, SP-UV 300, Shanghai, China). The analysis of the ABTS radical scavenging activity was performed utilizing the following eq : ABTSradicalscavengingactivity(%)=AbsABTS−AbssampleAbsABTSX100 8

Antimicrobial Evaluation of g-C3N4 and Nanocomposite Film

Microdilution Assay for Determination of MIC and MBC of g-C3N4-NPs

The antimicrobial activity of g-C3N4 nanoparticles was evaluated using a broth microdilution technique to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC). Serial dilutions of g-C3N4 (0.5–0.004 mg mL–1) were prepared in Mueller-Hinton broth and inoculated with standardized microbial suspensions in 96-well microplates. Neomycin served as the positive control, while untreated wells were used as growth controls. Plates were incubated for 24 h at the appropriate temperatures (37 °C for bacteria and 30 °C for fungi). Resazurin at 0.02% v/v was added as a viability indicator, and MIC values were defined as the lowest concentration that inhibited visible growth. MBC values were determined by subculturing samples from MIC and higher concentrations onto agar plates and identifying the lowest concentration yielding no microbial growth.

Antimicrobial Activity of the Films

The antimicrobial activity of the nanocomposite films was assessed using the agar disc diffusion method, a standard technique for evaluating microbial inhibition on solid media. Three common foodborne bacterial pathogens were selected for testing: Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923), Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC 29212), and Gram-negative Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922), all of which are widely recognized reference strains in food safety research. Conducted under aseptic conditions, bacterial suspensions (100 μL each) were inoculated onto Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) plates, which were subsequently spread uniformly using a sterile L-shaped spreader. Film discs (10 mm in diameter) with varying concentrations of the nanocomposite were meticulously placed on the inoculated agar surfaces. A positive control disc containing 60 μL of neomycin solution was incorporated into each assay for comparative purposes. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which the zones of inhibition (ZOI) surrounding each film disc were measured in triplicate. This procedure was conducted in accordance with the modified methodology proposed by Mohammadi et al.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were carried out in triplicate. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the experimental data to determine statistical differences among treatment groups. Duncan’s multiple range test was applied to identify significant mean differences at a confidence level of p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using STATISTICA software (version 14.0; TIBCO Software Inc., Tulsa, OK, USA). Graphical representations and data visualizations were prepared utilizing Microsoft Excel (version 13.0; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Results and Discussion

XRD of g-C3N4 and the OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 Nanocomposite Films

The collected XRD diffractogram is presented in Figure (A), showing an intense diffraction peak at 2 theta = 27.5° and indexed to the (002) planes of g-C3N4, aligning with the already established pattern in literature. This peak corresponds to the stacking of the aromatics in the structure of graphitic carbon nitride. The same peak was also observed in nanocomposite films as a bump around 2 theta = 27.5° and progressively increased as the concentration of g-C3N4-NPs in the films increased, as seen in Figure (B). The gradual increase in peak intensity with higher g-C3N4 concentration indicated improved crystallinity and matrix-dense nature, a key feature for moisture resistance and mechanical integrity. Similar crystalline behavior has also been documented in the literature.

XRD patterns for (A) g-C3N4-NPs and (B) composite films; FT-IR spectra of (C) g-C3N4-NPs and (D) composite films.

FTIR of g-C3N4 and the OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 Nanocomposite Films

FT-IR spectra of both g-C3N4-NPs and OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 nanocomposite films were collected and are presented in Figures C and D, respectively. The spectra in Figure C show prominent bands in the range of 1200–1650 cm–1, corresponding to C-N and C = N stretching vibrations. Additionally, broad absorptions observed between 3100 and 3300 cm–1, representing terminal -NH and -OH groups, were identified for both g-C3N4 NPs and the OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 nanocomposite films. The broader band observed in the films suggests the formation of hydrogen bonding between OFIM, CNF, and g-C3N4. This observation is consistent with literature reports indicating that in CNF-composite films, the -OH stretching region becomes broader when hydrogen bonding is enhanced by the incorporation of nanomaterials. , Furthermore, the sharp peak near 810 cm–1, attributed to triazine ring vibrations, suggests the successful formation of polymeric carbon nitride. The intensity of this peak increased with the increase in concentration of g-C3N4 NPs in the nanocomposite film, suggesting a stronger interfacial interaction, which promotes mechanical stability and barrier performance.

UV–Visible Analysis of g-C3N4 and the OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 Nanocomposite Films

The UV–vis spectra presented for both the g-C3N4 and the films in Figure A and B, respectively, indicate strong absorption in the visible light region (300–460 nm). The UV–vis spectra revealed strong absorption in the 300–460 nm range, typical of g-C3N4’s semiconductor bandgap (=2.7 eV). Increasing g-C3N4 concentration-enhanced absorption intensity, signifying improved light-harvesting ability. These films thus possess the potential to act as UV-shielding materials, protecting food from photo-oxidative degradation and nutrient loss. Similar UV absorption enhancement was observed in PbO/ g-C3N4 nanocomposites by Kaliyaperumal et al.

Scanning and Transmission Electron Microscopy (SEM & TEM) for g-C3N4

The microscopic structure of g-C3N4 NPs has been observed via the collected micrographs of SEM and TEM presented in Figure (A & B). The micrographs of the synthesized g-C3N4-NPs revealed a wrinkled lamellar morphology typical of 2D polymeric carbon nitride at different magnifications, 100 and 50 μm, respectively, confirming the material’s exfoliated structure. On the other hand, the TEM micrograph showed thin sheets with visible stacking layers at different magnifications (100 and 50 μm), respectively, increasing the reactive surface area of g-C3N4. Such high-surface morphologies may enhance interfacial bonding with polysaccharides, such as OFIM and CNF, resulting in films with improved strength and water resistance. Similar morphologies were observed in nanocomposite films developed by exopolysccharides/potato starch integrated with g-C3N4 and AgNO 3 by Du et al.

SEM (A & B) and TEM (C & D) micrographs of the synthesized g-C3N4-NPs.

EDX for g-C3N4-NPs

The EDX spectrum presented in Figure for the synthesized g-C3N4-NPs exhibited pronounced and well-defined peaks corresponding to carbon (C) and nitrogen (N). The absence of peaks corresponding to other elements indicates a high-purity polymeric structure and minimal surface oxidation during thermal condensation at 600 °C. This also suggests the successful polymerization of melamine into g-C3N4. The observed elemental uniformity and lack of additional elemental peaks, which attest to the purity, are critical for facilitating electron transfer and enhancing antimicrobial properties. Morsy et al. reported identical C-N peaks without the presence of foreign elements, thereby confirming the purity and layered polymeric configuration of thermally polymerized g-C3N4-NPs. These observations validate the synthesis of g-C3N4 and its suitability for biopolymer integration, ensuring chemical stability, nontoxicity, and strong compatibility with polysaccharide-based matrices, specifically OFIM and CNF.

Physicochemical Characterization of the OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 Nanocomposite Films

Color and Visual Appearance of the OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 Nanocomposite Films

The color attributes of packaging materials significantly influence consumer acceptance and product appeal, making color an essential consideration in packaging design. Table presents the color attributes (L*, a*, and b*) and the total color difference (ΔE) of the fabricated film. The total color difference showed a significant increase (p < 0.05), indicating a visually perceptible change, as illustrated in Figure . An increase in g-C3N4-NPs concentration resulted in a significant decrease in L* (lightness) and an increase in a* (redness) and b* (yellowness), leading to films that appeared more yellow. This yellow color is due to the intrinsic properties of g-C3N4-NPs and enhanced light scattering. The total color difference (ΔE) rose from 8.77 for 1% g-C3N4 to 13.67 for 3% to 5% g-C3N4, indicating a noticeable visual change compared to the OFIM/CNF film. The moderate opacity and warm color of the OFIM/CNF + 3% g-C3N4 film may enhance its aesthetic appeal while limiting light penetration, which is beneficial for packaging, as controlled light blocking can help reduce postharvest deterioration.

Moisture Content, Water Solubility, and Water Vapor Permeability

Controlling the moisture content of packaging materials is critical to preventing moisture migration, which can compromise product stability and facilitate microbial proliferation. The data presented in Table indicate that moisture content (MC), water solubility (WS), and water vapor permeability (WVP) decreased significantly (p < 0.05) with increasing concentrations of g-C3N4. Specifically, MC decreased from 20.27% in the OFIM/CNF sample to 18.27% in the OFIM/CNF + 5% g-C3N4 sample, while WS decreased from 88.82% in the OFIM/CNF sample to 72.00% in the OFIM/CNF + 5% g-C3N4 sample. These findings suggest that the films exhibit enhanced water resistance and reduced hydrophilicity. This reduction can be attributed to the dense hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic surface characteristics of g-C3N4 nanoparticles, which limit moisture absorption and the mobility of polymer chains.

Thickness, Opacity, and Tensile Strength of the Film Samples

Mechanical properties are a crucial parameter for evaluating the strength, flexibility, and stiffness of films, which in turn reflect the capacity to preserve structural integrity throughout production and transportation. The observed data indicate a significant increase in film thickness with the concentration of g-C3N4 nanoparticles, attributed to the higher solid content and incorporation of the nanoparticles. Specifically, the film thickness exhibited a marked increase when compared to the control (OFIM/CNF), which can be explained by the elevated solid content. Conversely, opacity experienced a slight increase, likely due to light scattering effects. Similarly, the tensile strength increased significantly from 16.75 ± 26.64 MPa for the control to 197.4 ± 21.17 MPa at OFIM/CNF with 3% g-C3N4 (p < 0.05). This enhancement can be ascribed to the strong hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking interactions between g-C3N4 nanoparticles and the polysaccharide chains, which facilitate efficient stress transfer within the polymer network. Comparable mechanical reinforcement at moderate nanoparticle loading has been reported in g-C3N4-starch films by Du et al. However, a slight decrease in tensile strength was observed at OFIM/CNF with 5% g-C3N4, as indicated in Table , likely due to nanoparticle aggregation, which diminished the interfacial adhesion between the g-C3N4 and the OFIM/CNF matrix. , The results indicate that OFIM/CNF with 3% g-C3N4 exhibited the most favorable mechanical properties.

Antioxidant Activity of g-C3N4 and the Films

The intrinsic antioxidant activity of g-C3N4 nanoparticles (NPs) is primarily attributed to their conjugated π-electron system and the presence of nitrogen lone pairs within their tri-s-triazien structure, which facilitates electron donation and free radical neutralization. However, the efficacy of pristine g-C3N4 is frequently hindered by particle aggregation, which diminishes the reactive surface area and the accessibility of active sites. As the stacked nanosheets aggregate during experimentation, the available surface for redox interactions is reduced, leading to a moderate radical scavenging potential. This phenomenon has been extensively documented in related studies. , Consequently, the antioxidant capacity, evaluated through ABTS+ radical scavenging assays, revealed that g-C3N4 exhibits relatively weak activity compared to the control, ascorbic acid (AA), as shown in Figure (A). However, upon incorporation into the OFIM/CNF biopolymer matrix, the dispersion of g-C3N4-NPs was significantly enhanced (p < 0.05), as demonstrated in Figure (B). Specifically, the antioxidant activity of the film ranged from 41.7% for OFIM/CNF to 71.4% for OFIM/CNF combined with 5% g-C3N4. These findings indicate that increasing the concentration of g-C3N4 correlates with an enhancement in the antioxidant activity of the film. This enhancement may be attributed to the interactions between the polysaccharide chains of the OFIM matrix and the hydroxyl (-OH) groups of cellulose nanofibers (CNF) through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction, which stabilize g-C3N4 within the film network. This improved dispersion increases the availability of active nitrogen sites for radical scavenging reactions. According to Lobo et al. (2021), the presence of the -OH group in the polysaccharide is solely responsible for the free radical scavenging activity against ABTS. In addition, the percentage of ABTS+ radical inhibition in the nanocomposite films increased significantly with higher concentrations of g-C3N4, demonstrating a direct correlation between nanoparticle dispersion and antioxidant performance. The highest ABTS inhibition was recorded at OFIM/CNF combined with 5% g-C3N4, achieving 71.4% inhibition compared to 41.7% for OFIM/CNF (control). This enhancement may be attributed to the ability of g-C3N4-NPs in aqueous solution to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby improving the oxidation resistance of the film.

Antimicrobial Activity of g-C3N4 and the Film Samples

The assessment of antimicrobial activity in active food packaging systems is a key factor in ensuring food safety and shelf life extension by suppressing microbial proliferation. Hence, one of the rational for using g-C3N4 as an active ingredient for fortification of the film is the known antimicrobial properties. This was assessed, and the antibacterial data presented in Table of g-C3N4 demonstrated that this material possesses the intrinsic ability to inhibit the growth of gram-positive and gram-negative foodborne pathogens at relatively low concentrations. Precisely, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values for E. faecalis, S. aureus, and E. coli were 62.5 μg/mL, 125 μg/mL, and 125 μg/mL, respectively, indicating moderate antimicrobial potential compared to a known antibacterial like Neomycin. The lower MIC value observed for E. faecalis suggests that g-C3N4 interacts more effectively with the simpler cell wall of Gram-positive bacteria, allowing easier penetration of the nanoparticles. This trend aligns with findings where it was stated that g-C3N4 nanosheets can disrupt bacterial membranes by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and through electrostatic interactions, leading to inhibited bacterial proliferation. Nevertheless, although g-C3N4 effectively inhibited growth at the MIC level, visible bacterial colonies reappeared at the MBC stage (see Figure ), indicating that the tested concentrations suppressed bacterial growth but did not achieve 99% death. This partial inhibition occurs because g-C3N4 primarily exerts bacteriostatic rather than bactericidal effects, disrupting metabolic processes and cell integrity without complete cell death. Neomycin exhibited inhibition at the MIC for all bacterial strains, but showed no bactericidal activity against E. coli and E. faecalis.

General Summary of Findings: OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 Nanocomposite Film

The incorporation of g-C3N4 into the OFIM/CNF matrix progressively improved the overall functional performance of the films. Increasing nanoparticle concentration enhanced moisture resistance, reduced solubility, and strengthened barrier properties, while also improving UV-shielding and antioxidant activity. Mechanical performance was optimized at intermediate loading (3%), beyond which slight reductions were observed due to nanoparticle aggregation. Antibacterial activity followed a similar trend, with the most balanced multifunctional performance achieved at 3% g-C3N4, making the nanocomposite film suitable for eco-friendly active packaging applications.

Conclusion

This study developed and comprehensively characterized a sustainable nanocomposite film based onOpuntia ficus-indica mucilage (OFIM), cellulose nanofibers (CNF), and graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) nanoparticles (NPs) for active food packaging applications. The incorporation of g-C3N4 significantly enhanced the physicochemical, mechanical, optical, and biological properties of the biopolymer matrix. Among the formulations, the OFIM/CNF film containing 3% g-C3N4 demonstrated optimal functionality, characterized by enhanced tensile strength, reduced water permeability, and improved UV-blocking, antimicrobial, and antioxidant properties. These properties were attributed to uniform nanoparticle dispersion and robust polymer-filler interactions.

Antimicrobial assessments indicated that while g-C3N4 inhibited bacterial growth, its integration with the OFIM/CNF matrix enhanced the antibacterial activity of the film, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria such as Enterococcus faecalis and Staphylococcus aureus. This enhanced bioactivity underscores the synergistic contribution of OFIM-derived phenolics, CNF reinforcement, and g-C3N4 nanostructures, resulting in a multifunctional packaging material. Overall, the OFIM/CNF/g-C3N4 nanocomposite film represents a promising eco-friendly alternative to conventional petroleum-based plastics, with strong potential to mitigate food safety, extend shelf life, reduce postharvest losses, and alleviate environmental pollution. The outcomes of this work directly support the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 2, 9, 12, and 13, emphasizing food security, industrial innovation, responsible production, and climate action.
