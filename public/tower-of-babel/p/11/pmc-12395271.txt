Newly Diagnosed Celiac Disease in a Patient With a Body Mass Index of 50.8 kg/m2
Authors: Mehak Sachdeva, Abdul‐Rahman Diab, Thomas Brown
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCeliac disease (CD) was previously known to be seen in underweight individuals, with weight loss being one of the key symptoms; however, newer data today is starting to show that most adults with celiac disease are either normal weight, overweight or obese. Here we present a 52‐year‐old patient with a body mass index (BMI) of 50.8 kg/m2 who was diagnosed with biopsy‐proven CD and her tissue transglutaminase immunoglobulin A level was 45.5 U/mL (0–15 U/mL). CD is becoming increasingly prevalent in overweight and obese individuals, making it essential for clinicians to be aware of this atypical presentation to prevent any delay in diagnosis and improve their quality of life.Keywords: autoimmune, body mass index, celiac disease, gluten‐free diet, morbid obesity

Introduction

Celiac disease (CD) is one of the most common autoimmune disorders worldwide, with a continuously increasing prevalence [1]. It results from gluten intolerance in genetically susceptible individuals, causing gastrointestinal and extra‐intestinal manifestations. The classic gastrointestinal symptoms previously documented were diarrhea and weight loss due to malabsorption; however, these symptoms are more commonly seen in children. Adults present more often with milder gastrointestinal symptoms, including diarrhea, constipation, abdominal pain or bloating, and thus they tend to be normal weight, overweight, or even obese [2]. Here we present one such case of a 50‐year‐old woman with a BMI of 50.8 kg/m2 who was diagnosed with CD.

Case Report

A 52‐year‐old caucasian female with a past medical history of systemic lupus erythematosus not recently on any treatment or steroids, Sjogren's syndrome, Raynaud's syndrome, fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, hypertension, depression, and migraines presented to the clinic with complaints of abdominal pain and constipation alternating with diarrhea for several years. Her symptoms had been progressively worsening and were now more diarrhea‐predominant. She denied any rash, melena, weight loss, or anorexia. At the time of presentation, her body mass index was 50.8 kg/m2, with a height of 5 ft 4 in. and a weight of 296 lbs. A CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast was done prior to her clinic visit at our center, which was negative for any etiology explaining her symptoms. After evaluating her symptoms, an esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and colonoscopy were scheduled. Her lab results showed abnormal liver function tests with an AST of 32 U/L (10–35 U/L), ALT of 40 U/L (6–29 U/L), ALP of 56 U/L (37–153 U/L) and a fasting glucose of 102 mg/dL (65–99 mg/dL). Her EGD showed mild cobblestoning present throughout the duodenum. Duodenal biopsies showed mild villous blunting, increased lymphoblast cells in the lamina propria, and 27 intraepithelial lymphocytes per high‐power field. The tissue transglutaminase immunoglobulin A level was elevated at 45.5 U/mL, which confirmed the diagnosis of CD. The HLA typing which was done as an additional confirmatory test further showed that the patient was positive for the HLA‐DQ2 variant and negative for the DQ8 variant. The patient was educated to begin a strict gluten‐free diet. The patient was reevaluated 4 months later and at this time she had improved symptomatically. Her stool was more formed and she began losing weight. Her BMI decreased from 50.8 to 49.09 kg/m2 after being unable to lose any weight prior to presentation.

Discussion

Screening studies done in several countries have shown the prevalence of CD to be approximately 1% of the general population [3]. However, there is a disparity between that number and the number of clinically diagnosed cases. One reason for this disparity is a lack of diagnosis in those with subclinical or silent presentations. Some of these extra‐intestinal manifestations of subclinical CD include iron deficiency anemia, osteoporosis, alopecia, and dermatitis herpetiformis [4]. Clinicians are becoming increasingly aware of CD presenting in patients who do not have the typical symptoms of weight loss, diarrhea, and malabsorption.

Atypical symptoms seen commonly include constipation, abdominal pain, vomiting, neurological symptoms, elevated liver enzymes, aphthous ulcers, and peripheral neuropathy [5]. Another such atypical manifestation is the presence of CD in overweight and obese patients. Classically, CD patients are underweight. Our patient discussed here had a BMI of 50.8 kg/m2 at the time of diagnosis and denied any recent weight loss. We could not find any documented case of a patient with a BMI of 50 kg/m2 or higher who has been diagnosed with active CD. A study comparing BMI and age at diagnosis of CD showed that age of diagnosis rose with BMI, with those in the obese category being diagnosed almost a decade after the average age. This study showed that the overall age at which patients started getting diagnosed with CD was 18, however, it rose to 23 years in the overweight group and 28 years in the obese group [6]. There is no data yet showing whether obesity predisposes individuals to the development of CD. However, obesity being a pro‐inflammatory state with elevated serum levels of C‐reactive protein, interleukin6, tumor necrosis factor‐alpha, and leptin raises the question of whether it could increase the risk of inflammation and immune sensitivity [7]. Another study showed that 18% of overweight and 42% of obese individuals with CD who were started on a gluten‐free diet lost weight [8]. We, as clinicians, can reassure obese patients with CD that following a gluten‐free diet will not result in weight gain as seen in underweight CD patients [9].

As we continue to discover CD's polymorphic presentation, we continue to realize just how prevalent it is in today's population. Thus, it is important to look past the typical presentation of CD and be mindful of atypical presentations to facilitate an earlier diagnosis in symptomatic patients to improve their quality of life and prevent further complications.
