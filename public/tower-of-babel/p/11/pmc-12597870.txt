Physician assistant legalization and conflicts in South Korea: need for an approach based on lessons from three countries
Authors: Suhyun Oh, Kye-Hyun Kim, Seog-Kyun Mun
Published: 2025

South Korea’s medical system faces a crisis due to large-scale exodus of medical residents from the medical field to protest the government’s plan to drastically increase medical school admissions. This revealed the structural vulnerability of the Korean medical system, which depends heavily on residents to provide essential medical services at large hospitals, raising fundamental questions about medical personnel management’s efficiency and sustainability. Discussions on the physician assistant (PA) system and its legalization to fill medical care gaps caused by residents’ absence are gaining momentum.

As of 2022, the number of doctors in Korea is about 140 000 [1] and the number of PA is about 6000 [2]. However, PA has skilled nurses working mainly in large hospitals in that it has high professionalism and can work in a better working environment, but most of them have no official title and are active in a ‘legal gray area’, making it difficult to determine the exact number. The government enacted the Nursing Act, allowing nurses to work as medical support personnel performing some of doctors’ duties if they have certain qualifications, without any social consensus on their education system, qualification requirements, clear scope of work, or legal responsibility. However, medical groups strongly oppose PAs’ legalization, claiming that this could reduce medical care quality, intensify inter-professional conflicts, and collapse medical residents’ training environment. Even the nursing community has different opinions on PA qualifications, scope of education, increased workload, and legal responsibilities. Therefore, designing a desirable PA system in Korea requires closely examining various challenges and success factors of international cases such as Taiwan, Australia, and South Africa, and taking a systematic and cautious approach that fits Korea’s unique circumstances.

Taiwan attempted to legalize PAs in the early 2000s to address the medical personnel shortage, but it failed due to a lack of agreement with the medical community and the nursing community’s opposition. The government maintained a cautious attitude of not promoting the PA system without consulting the two organizations; PA legalization discussions eventually halted in 2005 after all PAs converted to nurse practitioners [3]. Australia’s attempt to institutionalize PAs to address physician shortage in rural areas was aborted due to the lack of clear role recognition, concerns about medical quality deterioration, and medical associations’ and nurse unions’ opposition [4]. South Africa’s government introduced a new ‘clinical associate’ job with a national licensing system and education programme, establishing a clear scope of work and supervision system to gain medical associations’ support and cooperation. Clinical associate successfully established the position by participating in medical treatment under doctors’ supervision after completing a three-year undergraduate course [5].

South Korea’s immediate motivation for institutionalizing PAs was the residents’ strike, but the fundamental motivation aligns with the international issues of increasing medical demand and essential medical personnel shortage. The crisis in South Korea requires swift action, but international cases show that PAs’ partial introduction or informal operation without a legal and institutional infrastructure is unlikely to be sustainable. PAs should not undermine doctors’ professionalism, and stakeholders should sufficiently agree with measures to minimize the nurse–PA role overlap.

Drawing on the three countries’ efforts, a multifaceted strategy can be considered for PA introduction in Korea. First, a clear legal and regulatory framework must be established. A law specifying PAs’ legal status, qualification requirements, scope of work, responsibilities, and supervision system is needed, and content delegated to lower-level laws should be minimized to increase legal stability [6]. Second, a standardized education system and qualification requirements are needed. A nationally accredited education curriculum should be developed, education should be provided by accredited institutions, qualification should be granted through a nationally accredited qualification examination to only those completing the education curriculum, and quality control should be promoted through a renewal system [7, 8]. Third, a clear scope of work ensuring patient safety should be established by prioritizing practical needs, PA capabilities, and patient safety, and adjusted to fit reality through periodic review and improvement [6]. Fourth, PA work should be performed under doctors’ guidance and supervision, and the supervision’s scope and method should be differentiated by stage according to the PA’s career, skill level, and difficulty and risk of work [9]. The supervising doctor’s scope of responsibility should be clearly defined to reduce potential legal disputes. Fifth, interprofessional cooperation and a supervision system are essential, premised on communication and participation among stakeholders. This is possible when PAs play complementary roles rather than replace doctors.

No one-size-fits-all model exists for institutionalizing PAs, and each country should design its unique system. A successful system adheres to the core principles of safety, quality of care, and clear regulations. This process takes considerable time and is repetitive. Continuous efforts to build trust among professionals are necessary, along with pilot projects, legislative adjustments, and the improvement of scope of work. Korea’s PA system may overcome the current medical crisis and help improve the medical system’s long-term efficiency and sustainability. However, successful PA system establishment is possible only when efforts are made to establish a solid legal and institutional foundation based on sufficient communication and social consensus among stakeholders, build a high-quality education and training system, and establish an effective cooperation model.
