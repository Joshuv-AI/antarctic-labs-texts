Assessing emotional intelligence of pharmacy, medicine, allied health, and dentistry students: a cross-sectional study from Kuwait
Authors: Israa K. Abdelghany, Asmaa Al-Haqan, Saja Alnahar, Salah Waheedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, healthcare providers and systems are encouraged to deliver and tailor healthcare interventions and programs that are patient-centered and responsive to different patients’ needs. This approach requires that healthcare professionals be attentive, empathetic, and sympathetic toward their patients. Accordingly, healthcare providers should be able to monitor and manage their feelings and understand others’ feelings and motives. This study aims to assess the emotional intelligence of health sciences’ students at Kuwait University.MethodsThis cross-sectional study is based on a self-administered questionnaire instrument developed based on Daniel Goleman’s theory. The instrument assessed five emotionally intelligence-related constructs: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, and social skills. Captured data were analyzed descriptively and inferentially using SPSS.ResultsA total of 393 students completed the questionnaire. The highest EI scores were observed among students in their fifth-year. There was no significant difference between the EI constructs’ scores among females and males except for motivation (p = 0.048). Furthermore, the gender differences in total EI scores were not statistically significant. Students choosing their faculty as their primary preference exhibited significantly higher EI scores of empathy compared to those who did not opt for their faculty (p = 0.037). Out of the five constructs of EI, most of the students had self-awareness, motivation, and empathy as their strengths. However, social skills emerged as a crucial development area. A positive correlation was also observed among the five EI constructs, with statistical significance noted (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe study highlighted areas necessitating improvement in emotional intelligence. The findings suggest integrating emotional intelligence training into curricula, focusing on developing interpersonal and communication skills. These insights contribute to enhancing the emotional intelligence of future healthcare professionals.

Introduction

In the evolving landscape of medical education, a significant transformation is taking place, aiming to shape future healthcare providers into not only knowledgeable individuals but also empathetic and compassionate professionals. This paradigm shift recognizes the growing importance of emotional intelligence (EI) among students pursuing careers in pharmacy, dentistry, medicine, and allied health. EI is acknowledged for its role in determining success, potentially surpassing the significance of intelligence quotient (IQ) as noted by Goleman in 1995 [1]. Initially introduced by Salovey and Mayer in 1989, EI is defined as a form of social intelligence encompassing the capacity to monitor and manage one’s own and others’ emotions, discriminate among them, and guide one’s thoughts and behaviors [2, 3].

Mayer and Salovey Four-Branch Model of EI dissects it into distinct yet interconnected abilities [3, 4]. The first branch, perceiving emotio

ns, focuses on recognizing and interpreting emotions in various contexts, including one’s own [4]. It serves as the foundation for processing emotional information [4]. The second branch, using emotions, involves leveraging emotions to enhance cognitive functions like problem-solving [4]. Understanding emotions, the third branch, encompasses comprehending and verbalizing emotional language, recognizing emotional transformations over time [4]. Managing emotions, the fourth branch, entails regulating emotions in oneself and others, including the ability to influence others’ emotions [4]. While Goleman did not establish the EI theory, he developed a framework of five key constructs: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, and social skills [5].

Various perspectives on EI have emerged, classifying it either as the ability to comprehend emotions theoretically or through self-report assessments aligning with the ability-based and trait-based models, respectively [3]. The mixed-model theory of EI expanded the emotional quotient (EQ) by incorporating positive qualities, including empathy and emotional self-awareness [3].

High EI has been associated with positive outcomes such as improved social skills, academic success, cooperative interpersonal relationships, and effective emotion management [6]. Several studies suggest a positive connection between EI and academic success among healthcare students [7–11]. In the medical field, high EI students exhibit strong social skills, fostering positive peer relationships and enabling effective communication with patients [12, 13]. They demonstrate a heightened capacity to navigate and manage stressful situations compared to their low EI counterparts, who often exhibit deviant behaviors and poor interpersonal relationships [14].

The literature emphasizes the role of EI in stress management, with a consistent negative relationship between EI and perceived stress [7, 10, 15]. Students with higher EI scores demonstrate enhanced abilities in managing various forms of stress, leading to improved academic performance [15]. Stress levels are notably higher among students in clinical years and those whose career choice was not their own [11].

To address the need for effective relational skills and emotional competence in healthcare practice, educational interventions are crucial. Incorporating EI competencies into healthcare training is essential, requiring active and personalized approaches that emphasize practical application and constructive feedback [16]. Fostering the development of EI in educational settings involves incorporating modules that focus on teaching emotional competencies, enhancing self-awareness, self-control, and empathy [9, 17]. Educational strategies such as inquiry-based learning, reflective activities, self-assessment, feedback, and modeling of EI skills have proven valuable in enhancing EI [16, 18]. The use of simulated patients in role plays has also demonstrated effectiveness as an intervention strategy [12, 19]. Adopting self-directed learning and improvement curricula, along with periodic assessments of emotional intelligence, can motivate students to proactively engage in their emotional development [7, 9]. Integrating activities focused on building trust and managing relationships is crucial for supplementing self-expression development [7].

Interestingly, the timing of interventions plays a crucial role, with improvements in EI being more pronounced when introduced later in the course, particularly in the students’ final year [12]. Regardless of the intervention duration, the most significant positive effects are observed when delivered within a short timeframe, typically less than a month [12]. Healthcare educational and training approaches should consider students’ current EI capabilities and strive to develop them further [19]. Traditionally, healthcare curricula prioritize academic knowledge and technical skills, neglecting the importance of soft skills like interpersonal abilities [14, 20].

Various demographic factors can affect emotional intelligence scores. Female students tend to have higher EI scores, potentially attributed to greater emotional expressiveness and sensitivity [9, 10, 14, 21]. On the other hand, male students might demonstrate higher scores in specific EI aspects, such as self-awareness [14, 21]. Moreover, the academic year represents a crucial demographic factor influencing EI, with later clinical years demonstrating a positive association with EI [10, 22]. Students who achieve outstanding grades often exhibit high levels of EI, while those with passing grades demonstrate lower levels [9].

A significant research gap was identified, particularly in the context of Kuwait and generally in the Middle East, where studies related to undergraduate students’ EI in the healthcare sciences have been notably limited. The decision to conduct the proposed study is further motivated by the fact that different EI theories have been studied but not comprehensively explored in this specific regional context. Additionally, there is a shortage of research that considers all healthcare professions collectively, making the study timely and essential to address these gaps.

At Kuwait University, the Health Sciences Center serves as the principal institution for educating healthcare professionals in the country. It encompasses four faculties: Medicine, Pharmacy, Dentistry, and Allied Health Sciences. The Faculty of Medicine delivers comprehensive undergraduate and postgraduate programs aimed at preparing physicians with both clinical and research competencies. The Faculty of Pharmacy provides training in pharmaceutical sciences and clinical practice, equipping graduates to contribute to medication management and healthcare delivery. The Faculty of Dentistry offers rigorous academic and clinical programs designed to prepare dental practitioners for professional practice. The Faculty of Allied Health Sciences delivers a range of programs, including Medical Laboratory Sciences, Physical Therapy, Radiologic Sciences, Occupational Therapy, Health Informatics, and Nursing, thereby addressing diverse healthcare workforce needs. Notably, Kuwait University is the only higher education institution in the country offering accredited degree programs in the health sciences, making its graduates the primary source of the national healthcare workforce. Although emotional intelligence is not explicitly offered as a stand-alone course within these curricula, its components are embedded within clinical education, communication skills training, and patient-centered care modules, underscoring its relevance to professional practice across disciplines.

This study aimed to assess the emotional intelligence of health science students at Kuwait University’s Health Science Center (HSC) using a self-reported instrument. It investigated the correlation between students’ demographics and their total emotional intelligence score, determining priority areas for training and development within emotional intelligence constructs.

Methods

Overall study design

The study adopts a cross-sectional, descriptive, and correlational research design. The survey tool used in this research was adopted from the emotional intelligence questionnaire published by the National Health service (NHS) Leadership Academy England in 2014 [23]. Data were collected using a self-administered electronic version of the EI scale, developed based on Daniel Goleman’s emotional intelligence theory. Goleman’s theory identifies five primary constructs that manifest emotional intelligence: self-awareness, self-regulation (managing emotions), motivation, empathy, and social skills [5].

Questionnaire design and validation

As this study aimed to assess a psychological concept which might be affected by cultural and contextual factors, it was essential to use a questionnaire instrument that is valid and reliable to assess emotional intelligence and that is context-specific and culturally sensitive. Therefore, the research team followed a step-wise approach in designing, validating, piloting and finalising the questionnaire instrument guided by Tsang et al. approach [24]. First, the research team sought the NHS Leadership Academy’s permission to use their emotional intelligence questionnaire developed based on Daniel Goleman’s theory. Second, two independent and qualified translators carried out a forward translation process. The research team then reviewed, amended and consolidated the translated versions into a unified Arabic version. Third, a multidisciplinary panel of psychology, behavioral science and business administration experts reviewed the consolidated Arabic version. The experts reviewed the accuracy of the translation, as well as its context and cultural appropriateness. Additionally, the experts assessed the questionnaire’s face and content validity regarding relevance, clarity, and presentation. Fourth, the research team updated and amended the instrument based on the experts’ recommendations and advice. Fifth, a backward translation by a third independent and qualified translator. The reverse translation was juxtaposed with the original English questionnaire, revealing no significant disparities and validating the correctness of the translation process. Sixth, a pilot study was carried out to assess the instrument’s flowability, comprehension and reliability. The pilot study included 67 third-year pharmacy students in Jordan. The internal consistency of each construct using Cronbach’s coefficient α analysis, Cronbach’s-alpha of (0.70) and above was considered acceptable. Finally, the necessary modifications and adjustments were considered based on the piloting and reliability assessment. As part of this process, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted, and three items were removed because their factor loadings were below the acceptable threshold (λ < 0.40). The final questionnaire instrument included forty-seven items measuring five emotional intelligence-related constructs: self-awareness, self-recognition, motivation, empathy and social skills.

Following this approach was crucial to guarantee the questionnaire’s validity, reliability, and cultural appropriateness. The translation procedure, which included independent qualified (English-Arabic) translators and expert evaluations, was essential for preserving the original instrument’s integrity while adapting it to an Arabic-speaking audience. The research used a multidisciplinary panel, a diverse group of experts from psychology, behavioral science, and business administration, to guarantee that the language was culturally relevant and that the questionnaire’s content was lucid and applicable to the targeted group. The reverse translation offered extra quality assurance, validating the correspondence between the translated and original texts. The pilot research and reliability analysis confirmed the instrument’s internal consistency, demonstrating its scientific validity and appropriateness for appropriately evaluating emotional intelligence in the study population. The survey gathered demographic and background information, including age, gender, year of study, and academic subject, alongside the emotional intelligence evaluation tool. Participants were also enquired if their current faculty/discipline was their primary preference at the time of university enrollment.

Study setting

The study’s target population consists of students enrolled at the Health Science Centre, Kuwait University. This includes pharmacy, medicine, dentistry, and allied health students.

Sample size and recruitment

The sample size was calculated using the Raosoft sample size calculator by choosing 5% as a margin of error, 95% as a confidence interval (CI), and a response distribution of 50% [25]. According to the Student Affairs Department of each college of the HSC, their student record for the year 2023 resulted in a total of 2803 students. A minimum sample of 338 is required to have statistically meaningful results. Students currently enrolled at the HSC were contacted and recruited through WhatsApp and other social media platforms.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected from October to November 2023. The survey tool used in this research consisted of two sections, with the first section involving a total of five questions to gather essential demographic characteristics of the participants. The second section of the questionnaire aimed to assess the five main constructs of EI (Supplementary File 1). To achieve this, a five-point Likert scale was utilized (ranging from 1 = does not apply to 5 = always applies), enabling participants to evaluate and assign scores to statements related to EI constructs. Students rated themselves in each of the forty-seven questionnaire items mapped to specific EI constructs.

Scores were computed individually for each construct, by which scores between the range of 35–50 demonstrated strength within that specific construct. While scores between 18 and 34 indicated the need to give attention to that construct. Lastly, if students’ scores fell within the range of 10–17, it was essential to prioritize the development of that specific EI construct. Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, ver. 28; SPSS, Chicago, IL). The descriptive analysis, including frequency, percentage, median, and means, was conducted to determine the students’ demographics as well as their EI levels. The study also uncovered correlations between the total EI levels and the demographic characteristics of the students, and between the diverse EI constructs themselves. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. The total Emotional Intelligence (EI) score demonstrated a normal distribution, whereas the individual EI constructs did not. Correlations between the EI constructs were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Joint Health Science Center and the Ministry of Health Research Ethical Committee, Kuwait (VDR/EC-2022).

Results

A total of 393 students completed our questionnaire. Table 1 shows the characteristics of the participating students. There were 351 (89%) females and 42 (11%) males, and the median age was 21 years. The response rates of pharmacy, medicine, allied health, and dentistry were 189 (48.1%), 135 (34.4%), 44 (11.2%), and 25 (6.5%), respectively. This distribution reflects the acceptance capacity limits set by Kuwait University, where Medicine and allied health have larger student groups, while pharmacy and dentistry accept fewer students. More than half of the students (63.1%) chose their faculty as their first choice.

Our findings demonstrated that self-awareness is the EI construct with the highest percentage of students (325, 82.7%) showing strength. Moreover, only 68 (17.3%) students indicated a need for further attention in this area, while none necessitated prioritization in developing self-awareness. Similarly, empathy emerged as another construct where all students scored above 10, indicating that it is not a development priority area. Conversely, 32 (8.1%) students identified a necessity to enhance their social skills, designating this construct as a priority for training and development, as shown in Fig. 1. Additionally, the EI construct with the lowest percentage in strength (46, 11.7%) and the highest percentage in the need to improve (345, 87.7%) was found to be self-regulation. This emphasizes the crucial requirement for training and education directed at nurturing emotional management among HSC students. Nearly 60% (234) of students demonstrated high EI levels in motivation, contrasting with approximately 40% (157) who identified a need for improvement, revealing a relative weakness in this construct.

The comparative analysis of student scores across various faculties reveals significant variations in construct strengths and areas needing development (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). In the faculty of pharmacy, a noticeable need for prioritizing the enhancement of social skills was identified, evidenced by 19 students. Additionally, the data revealed that students in dentistry (p = 0.008) and those enrolled in their first-choice faculty (p = 0.037) exhibited significantly higher levels of emotional intelligence in the construct of empathy. Lastly, it was observed that only fifth-year students displayed notably higher scores in the self-awareness construct compared to other academic levels (p = 0.028).

Discussion

Prior studies have underscored the vital role of demographic factors in the development of EI [22]. Our study’s results suggested no significant difference in mean EI between females and males, a finding consistent with prior studies [15, 26]. Nevertheless, a few studies have presented contrasting results, with some indicating higher emotional intelligence in females [9–11, 27], while others suggest the opposite, favoring males [28]. These discrepancies in findings across studies could be due to differences in sample size, target population, and/or other social factors. In our study, only motivation scores were significantly higher among females, while total EI scores did not differ. This outcome may be patly explained by the strong gender imbalance in our sample, with females comprising nearly 90% of participants. However, the gender and college distribution of the sample was consistent with the overall demographics of students enrolled in the Health Sciences Center at Kuwait University where female students form the majority across most faculties.

The highest EI scores were observed among senior students, in their fifth year, a critical juncture marking the transition from preclinical to clinical years. Furthermore, fifth-year students demonstrated a notable impact on the construct of self-awareness, indicating a heightened ability to recognize their emotions and guide their decision-making processes. Additionally, students choosing their faculty as their primary preference exhibited slightly higher mean EI scores compared to those who did not opt for their faculty. According to prior studies, those who are satisfied with their study choice have higher EI scores, aligning with our study [10]. Furthermore, our findings indicated that these students displayed significantly elevated scores in empathy, signifying their capability to understand and share the feelings of others.

Our study revealed positive correlations among diverse EI constructs, aligning with earlier findings in existing literature [26, 27]. Nevertheless, these associations were based on different emotional intelligence theories than ours, which may involve other constructs [26, 27].

A growing body of literature highlighted the increasing importance of emotional intelligence in students in different healthcare fields, emphasizing its role in academic success. The present study aimed to determine the priority areas for training and development within the emotional intelligence constructs. While different strengths and areas for improvement were identified among various faculties, it is apparent that integrating EI training into the curricula is anticipated to enhance the capabilities of future healthcare professionals, particularly in terms of interpersonal and intrapersonal competencies. HSC students’ predominant competency lies in self-awareness, with the highest proficiency in emotional awareness, self-assessment, and self-confidence. Conversely, the lowest construct is social skills, indicating challenges in communication, conflict management, leadership, and teamwork capabilities among the students. One of the essential interventions found in the literature to improve EI is developing a course that provides instruction on the definition and importance of EI, the five EI constructs, and the key competencies of each construct [17]. Another emotional intelligence development strategy in different medical fields involves incorporating role plays with simulated patients [12, 19, 20]. Engaging in simulated patient activities where students actively strive to influence patient decisions provides an opportunity for enhancing social-emotional competencies [12, 19, 20]. Lastly, Periodic assessments of emotional intelligence levels can be valuable in tailoring and refining those interventions [7].

Strengths and limitations

The study has several limitations that may have affected the results obtained. The findings reflect the cultural and educational context of Kuwait university, and their generalized ability to other settings, may therefore be limited. While the research aimed to encompass a diverse array of student demographics, certain faculties had relatively small sample sizes, potentially limiting their representativeness. Nonetheless, the deliberate inclusion of faculties like pharmacy, medicine, dentistry, and allied health is a strength. Such inclusivity across varied academic disciplines offers a better understanding of EI. Additionally, one major limitation is that the study followed a cross-sectional design, as the findings reflect only a single point in time rather than tracking students’ behaviors longitudinally. Another limitation is the reliance on a self-reported questionnaire, which may be subject to response bias, despite the steps taken to ensure validity and reliability. An additional limitation relates to the strong, gender imbalance in the sample, with nearly 90% of participants being female, which may have influenced the observed, lack of gender differences in total EI scores. The findings reflect the cultural and educational context of Kuwait university and their general ability to other settings may therefore be limited. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to assess EI among health discipline students in Kuwait. The insight of this study could be used in developing training courses and materials related to interpersonal skills and competencies. Furthermore, this study could be the starting point for several initiatives needed to implement fit-to-practice student recruitment and admission policies in Kuwait and other countries in the region.

Conclusion

Across diverse faculties, our study uncovers distinct patterns in construct strengths and areas for improvement related to emotional intelligence. Notably, most students exhibited self-awareness, motivation, and empathy as strengths, while social skills emerged as a crucial developmental area. Interestingly, gender differences in total EI scores were not statistically significant, although female students demonstrated superior motivation scores. Furthermore, we found that students prioritizing their first-choice faculty exhibited heightened EI scores in empathy. Our findings also underscored the importance of integrating EI training and developing courses into curricula, particularly focusing on enhancing interpersonal and intrapersonal skills.
