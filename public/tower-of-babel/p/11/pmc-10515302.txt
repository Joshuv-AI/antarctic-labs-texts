A rare case of submandibular mucocele extending into the oral cavity: A case report

Introduction

Mucoceles represent cystic lesions resulting from mucus accumulation within the salivary glands. Although they commonly manifest in the minor salivary glands, the involvement of major salivary glands, particularly the submandibular gland, is relatively uncommon [1,2]. There is a noticeable gap in the literature regarding mucoceles originating specifically from the submandibular gland, with only a limited number of reported cases serving as references [1–4].

The presence of mucoceles in the submandibular gland poses diagnostic complexities due to their infrequent occurrence and distinctive clinical characteristics. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the unique aspects of submandibular gland mucoceles to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

In this report, we present a surgical case involving a 9-year-old child with a right submandibular gland mucocele that extended into the oral cavity.

Our work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2020 criteria [5].

Case presentation

A 9-year-old male patient no relevant medical history presented to our ENT department with progressively enlarging submandibular swelling over the past year. Additionally, the patient recently noticed the onset of swelling in the oral cavity. The patient did not complain of episodes of hernia or salivary colic.

On physical examination, the right submandibular tumefaction was firm, painless, measuring 4 cm. It showed mobility concerning both deep and superficial planes (Fig. 1). There were no palpable cervical lymph nodes. The contralateral submandibular compartment was free. Upon examination of the oral cavity, a bluish bulge on the floor of the mouth won the same side as the tumefaction was observed. Cervicofacial computed tomography (CT) scan showed a distinct oval cystic lesion measuring 65 ∗ 23 ∗ 35 mm, originating from the right submandibular gland and extending to the right sublingual space. The submandibular gland was displaced laterally and posteriorly. Additionally, the CT scan ruled out the presence of cervical lymphadenopathy (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Axial CT scan of the cervical region showing a cystic mass (white arrow) originating in the left submandibular gland (white star) extending to the floor of the oral cavity (orange arrow).Fig. 2

The patient underwent surgery under general anesthesia after obtaining informed consent from his legal guardian. A cervical incision measuring 4 cm in length was created, running parallel to and positioned 3 cm below the basilar rim. This incision was performed to reveal the gland capsule after the separation of both subcutaneous and muscular planes.

Following a laborious dissection, we identified a cystic mass at the expense of the internal face of the submandibular gland, extending upwards towards the floor of the oral cavity. Subsequently, excision of the cystic mass, associated with submandibulectomy and excision of the sublingual gland, was performed (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Intraoperative view revealing a bluish cystic formation (white arrow) originating from the deep aspect of the right submandibular gland (white star).Fig. 3

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient experienced a smooth recovery.

Histopathological examination of the surgical specimens revealed a 5-centimeter retention salivary cyst, with the submandibular gland demonstrating normal characteristics.

Discussion

Salivary mucoceles are benign cysts that develop from both major and minor salivary glands. Mucoceles originating from the submandibular gland are uncommon, constituting only 0.6 % of all submandibular gland tumors and cysts [2,6,7].

To the best of our knowledge, based on the available scientific literature, a male predominance is evident, with 15 cases involving males and 3 cases involving females [[2], [3], [4],[6], [7], [8]].

Salivary mucoceles can arise from either the traumatic rupture of the salivary ducts, resulting in the leakage of mucus into the adjacent tissues, or from ductal obstructions caused by inflammation, calculi or strictures. These obstructions lead to the formation of mucous retention cysts, also called sialocysts [9].

In general, submandibular gland mucoceles tend to be unilateral, with only a single reported case of bilateral occurrence [4].

The clinical features of submandibular gland mucoceles are not very distinct and typically encompass a painless, gradually enlarging mass in the submandibular region and the floor of the mouth.

Theses submandibular mucoceles generally manifest as a soft, well-circumscribed and movable swelling.

The diagnosis remains difficult to be evoked on the basis of clinical signs. The differential diagnosis of a submandibular cystic-like mass includes congenital lesions such as branchial cleft cysts, dermoid cysts, cystic hygroma, and thyroglossal duct cysts, as well as acquired lesions such as ranula, abscesses, and cystic degeneration of neoplasms [2,7,10,11].

Consequently, the diagnosis of a submandibular cyst heavily relies on imaging techniques including ultrasound, CT scan or MRI.

These imaging modalities offer enhanced exploration of the salivary glands, precise lesion localization, and differentiation between benign and malignant lesions [1,7,12]. It's worth noting that distinguishing between a submandibular gland mucocele and a plunging ranula can be challenging based solely on imaging findings.

In cases of plunging ranulas, a characteristic “tail” sign is often visible on contrast-enhanced CT scans, extending from the sublingual gland through the mylohyoid muscle. Furthermore, ultrasound can reveal the presence of mylohyoid muscle dehiscence [8,11].

Although we did not perform it our case, fine needle aspiration cytology should be considered as it can provide insights into the presence of mucus and inflammatory cells. Additionally, it serves as a means to rule out other neoplastic lesions.

The management of submandibular mucoceles involves both conservative and surgical approaches [1,7,12,13]. Conservative treatments encompass procedures like aspiration, sclerotherapy, irradiation, marsupialization, and incision and drainage [[1], [2], [3]]. However, these methods are increasingly being replaced by surgical intervention. Surgical options range from simple cystectomy to the complete removal of both sublingual and submandibular glands affected by the mucocele. Advances in minimally invasive surgical techniques have significantly improved these procedures [13].

One notable advantage of this technique is the reduced risk of facial nerve damage, although potential injury to the lingual nerve remains a concern. Careful examination and preservation of the hypoglossal nerve during dissection are also imperative [3,13].

Comparatively, excising the mucocele in continuity with the submandibular gland, as opposed to other methods preserving the gland, presents a lower recurrence rate advantage. Recurrences of cysts are often attributed to incomplete excision with remnants of the cystic wall [[1], [2], [3]].

Left untreated, the cyst may continue to grow, potentially leading to issues with mastication, swallowing, and phonation. Respiratory complications like snoring and sleep apnea are particularly concerning. There is also the possibility of spontaneous regression of the cyst [1,3,8].

Conclusion

In summary, this case highlights the diagnostic intricacies of submandibular mucoceles, particularly in pediatric cases. The rarity of submandibular gland involvement, along with the challenges posed by its clinical presentation, underscores the significance of accurate diagnosis and appropriate management. This case underscores the importance of surgical intervention for submandibular mucoceles in pediatric patients, ensuring optimal outcomes and preventing potential recurrence.
