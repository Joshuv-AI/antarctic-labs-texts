Bioinformatics identification of candidate biomarkers associated with T cell proliferation in atherosclerosis via WGCNA and machine learning
Authors: Wen Xiong, Xiang Long, Feng Lu, Kun Lin, Zhigang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtherosclerosis (AS) is a complex chronic disease caused by the development of atherosclerotic plaques. T cell proliferation exerts a vital influence on development of the AS. This research aimed to conduct a comprehensive analysis to computationally screen candidate T cell proliferation-related biomarkers in AS and to explore their potential molecular mechanisms.MethodsThe transcriptional datasets of AS patients were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository. To identify differentially expressed-T cell proliferation-related genes (DE-TPRGs), we integrated differential gene expression analysis with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), taking the intersection of DEGs and WGCNA-derived T cell proliferation-related module genes (TRMGs). Biomarkers were selected and validated through machine learning algorithms and expression levels. Moreover, a nomogram for predicting AS risk was developed based on the biomarkers. Enrichment analysis was employed to examine relevant pathways, while immune cell infiltration analysis was conducted to investigate the connection between immune cells and biomarkers. Finally, the construction of molecular regulatory and compound prediction networks, as well as molecular docking and molecular dynamic simulations, further validated the key regulatory roles of biomarkers in AS.ResultsOverall, two candidate genes (CLU and GUCY1B3) were determined to be potentially associated with the progression of AS. The nomogram constructed from these biomarkers showed good performance in predicting AS risk. Reactome rRNA processing and reactome translation were notably enriched in the pathways related to two biomarkers. CLU and GUCY1B3 showed positive computational corrections with activated dendritic cells, suggesting a possible involvement of these immune processes in AS. Moreover, 50 miRNAs (like hsa-miR-4504) and 68 transcription factors (TFs) (like MYB and TAL1) were found to have relationships with biomarkers. Importantly, the results and molecular dynamics simulation analyses predicted potential binding interactions between these candidate genes and bisphenol A, with GUCY1B3 demonstrating relatively greater binding stability.ConclusionsThe bioinformatics findings suggested that CLU and GUCY1B3 may serve as candidate biomarkers warranting further experimental investigation in AS associated with T cell proliferation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04545-4.

Background

Atherosclerosis (AS) represents a chronic inflammatory disorder marked by lipid accumulation, endothelial impairment, immune cell invasion, vascular smooth muscle cell growth, and fibrous plaque development [1]. As the primary pathological basis for cardiovascular disorders (such as coronary heart disease, heart attack, and cerebrovascular accident), AS remains a major global health burden [2]. Although current therapeutic approaches, including statins and interventional therapies, can partially control the progression of AS, their limitations—such as drug side effects, limited efficacy, and risk of recurrence-have prompted researchers to delve deeper into the biological mechanisms underlying AS to develop more precise and optimized treatment strategies [3]. In recent years, the immune system, particularly chronic inflammation driven by immune cell infiltration, has been recognized as a central player in AS pathogenesis [4].

T cells, as a core component of adaptive immunity, contribute significantly to AS onset and evolution through clonal expansion, lineage commitment, and cytokine production, which are involved in arterial wall inflammation, immune regulation, and plaque stability modulation [5]. In AS, the proliferation of Th1 cells releases a large number of pro-inflammatory factors, exacerbating arterial wall inflammation and promoting plaque formation [6]. Conversely, the proliferation of Treg cells suppresses inflammatory responses, protects endothelial function, and delays AS progression. Additionally, T cell proliferation influences the functions of macrophages and smooth muscle cells, thereby modulating plaque formation and stability. Studies have shown that dietary supplementation with the amino acid arginine can reduce AS lesions in hyperlipidemic mice, a mechanism potentially linked to reduced T cell proliferation, migration, and inflammatory cytokine expression [7]. This finding suggests that regulating T cell proliferation may represent a novel strategy for AS intervention.

In this study, by analyzing the AS gene expression data in the public database, the key genes related to T cell proliferation were screened out. After verification by machine learning algorithms and cross-examination of experimental data, we identified reliable biomarkers. By studying the biological pathways engaged by these markers, the characteristics of the immune microenvironment and their regulatory networks, and establishing clinical prediction models, it is helpful to elucidate the contribution of T cell proliferation in the diagnosis of AS. Recent integrative bioinformatics studies show that combining DEG analysis, WGCNA, machine learning, and immune infiltration effectively identifies disease-relevant biomarkers and networks in AS [8, 9]. The identification of new therapeutic targets for AS is the ultimate aim of this study, which may facilitate the discovery of novel clinical treatments.

Methods

Data sources

The GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) was accessed to collect gene expression profiles generated by microarrays from the GSE27034 and GSE12288 cohorts related to AS. Among them, GSE27034 (GPL570) served as the training set, which contained a cohort of 19 AS patients (AS group) and 18 healthy subjects (normal group) providing peripheral blood samples.

GSE12288 (GPL96) served as the validation set, which consisted of 110 AS whole blood samples and 112 healthy controls. In addition, 37 T cell proliferation-related genes (TPRGs) (Additional Table 1) were obtained from the literature [10], which served as computational anchors for module and candidate gene identification, not as direct evidence of T cell involvement.

Acquisition of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between atherosclerosis and control cohorts, we conducted comprehensive differential expression analysis on the GSE27034 dataset. The limma software package (version 3.56.2) [11]was utilized for this purpose, with stringent screening criteria established as follows: absolute log2 fold change values exceeding 0.5 and a statistical significance threshold set at p-value less than 0.05. For effective data visualization and interpretation, we generated volcano plots to illustrate the distribution pattern of differentially regulated genes using the ggplot2 visualization package (version 3.5.1) [12]. Using nominal p-values without multiple testing correction may increase the risk of false positives in transcriptome-wide analyses; however, given the exploratory nature of this study, a lenient threshold was applied to avoid excluding potentially relevant genes during the initial screening. Additionally, to provide a more intuitive representation of gene expression patterns, heat maps were constructed to display the top 10 most significantly upregulated and downregulated DEGs, which were generated using the pheatmap visualization package (version 1.0.12) [13].

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

To explore the potential computational association between T cell proliferation signatures and AS, in GSE27034, the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm of the GSVA package (v 1.53.28) [14] was utilized to calculate the TPRGs scores. The disparities in TPRG scores between the two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05).

Subsequently, these TPRG scores were treated as phenotypic traits to establish a WGCNA network via the WGCNA package (v 1.73) [15]. Initially, the goodSamplesGenes was utilized to identify and eliminate outliers. Secondly, an optimal soft threshold was determined to guarantee that the network construction would adhere to a scale-free topology (R2 = 0.85). The minimum number of genes for each module was set to 50, while the mergeCutHeight was set to 0.25. Finally, the Spearman correlation coefficients (cor) between TPRG scores and key modules were computed using the stats package (v 4.3.3) [16] and then visualized by generating a heatmap (|cor| > 0.3 and p < 0.05). The two modules that had the most positive and the most negative correlations with the TPRG scores were picked out and designated as key modules. Genes included in the key modules were denoted as T cell proliferation-related module genes (TRMGs) and used in subsequent analysis.

Determination and function analysis of differentially expressed (DE)-TPRGs

The overlap of DEGs and TRMGs (T cell proliferation-related module genes derived from WGCNA key modules) was employed to acquire DE-TPRGs using the ggvenn package (v 0.1.10) [17]. Subsequently, to comprehensively elucidate the biological roles and functional pathways of the identified DE-TPRGs within cellular systems, we conducted functional enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.8.3) [18]. This analysis encompassed both Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. The GO enrichment analysis was categorized into three distinct functional domains: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF), providing a multidimensional view of gene functionality. To further investigate the molecular interactions among DE-TPRG-encoded proteins, we constructed a comprehensive protein-protein interaction (PPI) network utilizing the STRING database platform (http://www.string-db.org/) with a confidence threshold of 0.15. The resulting interaction network was subsequently visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.3) [19], facilitating computational examination of potential interconnected relationships between these differentially expressed proteins.

Determination of biomarkers

Within this research, feature genes of AS were identified using two machine learning strategies: support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) and Boruta. For SVM-RFE in GSE27034, redundant variables were removed by eliminating the feature vectors generated by the SVM. Then, 10-fold cross-validation was performed, and the caret package (v 6.0.94) [20] was employed to identify SVM-RFE-feature genes. The Boruta algorithm assessed the importance of each gene by creating shadow features and comparing them with the original features using the Boruta package (v 8.0.0) [21]. Through this process, Boruta-feature genes were screened out. Ultimately, the overlap of SVM-RFE-feature genes and Boruta-feature genes was utilized to acquire feature genes using the ggvenn package (v 0.1.10). Moreover, in GSE27034 and GSE12288, biomarkers with consistent expression differences between the normal and AS groups were determined through the Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To further investigate the potential biological pathways and functional mechanisms associated with T cell proliferation-related biomarkers, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.8.3) on the GSE27034 dataset. The analysis employed stringent statistical criteria, including p-value < 0.05 and absolute normalized enrichment score (NES) > 1, to ensure robust identification of significantly enriched pathways. The analytical workflow began with computation of Spearman correlation coefficients between each identified biomarker and all other genes within the dataset, establishing comprehensive co-expression relationships. Subsequently, these correlation values were arranged in descending order based on their association strength with the respective biomarkers, creating ranked gene lists for enrichment analysis. The GSEA procedure utilized the curated reference gene set “c2.cp.v7.2.symbols.gmt” obtained from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB) repository (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp), which contains well-annotated canonical pathways and molecular signatures for comprehensive functional interpretation.

Immune microenvironment analysis

The significant changes in immune cells might mirror the regulatory imbalance of the immune system under the AS condition, indicating the clinical and pathological significance of these changes in terms of diagnosis and treatment efficacy. In GSE27034, the relative abundance of 22 immune cell types [22]was obtained using the CIBERSORT package (v 1.03) [23], and differential immune cells (DICs) between normal and AS groups were identified (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05). Subsequently, Spearman correlation analyses were carried out between DICs and biomarkers to probe into their relationships via the psych package (v 2.4.6) (|cor| > 0.3 and p < 0.05). It should be noted that CIBERSORT provides relative, gene expression-based deconvolution estimates rather than direct measures of immune cell abundance or function.

Creation and assessment of the nomogram

To further appraise the overall predictive capability of biomarkers for AS, a nomogram was established by applying the rms package (v 6.8.1) [24] in GSE27034. Each biomarker was assigned a certain number of points. The total points were mapped onto the linear predictor, and then the probability of developing AS was obtained. Subsequently, to evaluate the reliability of the nomogram based on biomarkers, the regplot package (v 1.1) [25] was employed to conduct calibration curve analysis. The closer the calibration curve was to the dotted line with a slope of one, the more accurate the prediction of the nomogram. The ggDCA package (v 1.2) [26] was employed to perform decision curve analysis (DCA) to appraise the clinical usefulness of the nomogram. A net benefit value greater than zero indicates a good prediction effect. The ROC curves were employed to estimate the predictive effectiveness of the nomogram via the pROC package (v 1.18.5) [27]. When the AUC value > 0.7, it suggests that the nomogram exhibits strong predictive performance.

Correlation analysis and post-translational modification (PTM) analysis of biomarkers

To explore the synergistic effect of biomarkers on the progression of AS in the AS samples of GSE27034, the psych package (v 2.4.6) was used to conduct a Spearman correlation analysis among the biomarkers (|cor| > 0.3, p < 0.05).

The PhosphoSitePlus database (https://www.phosphosite.org/homeAction) was initially consulted for PTM annotation of the biomarkers; however, no confident PTM sites or standard modification types (e.g., phosphorylation, ubiquitination and acetylation) could be reliably assigned to CLU or GUCY1B3. Therefore, PTM analysis was excluded from the final results.

Tissue localization analysis and subcellular localization analysis of biomarkers

The expression levels of biomarkers in different tissues were examined within the Human Protein Atlas (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/) database. The top five tissues with the highest biomarker distribution were imported into the Cytoscape software (v 3.10.3) to draw a tissue-biomarker network diagram. Moreover, biomarkers were uploaded to the Genecards database (https://www.genecards.org/) to gain the subcellular distribution information (Confidence = 5). The top five subcellular compartments with the highest biomarker distribution were selected to draw a bar chart for information presentation.

Molecular regulatory network

Transcriptional level regulation is a crucial part of gene expression regulation. Transcription factors (TFs) achieve transcriptional regulation of a gene by binding to specific nucleotide sequences upstream of the gene. TFs that regulated these biomarkers were obtained through KnockTF (http://www.licpathway.net/KnockTF/index.html) and CHIPBase (http://rna./chipbase/index.php) databases. Then, the intersections of the two prediction results were identified to obtain the intersecting TFs. miRNAs represent a category of endogenous short non-coding RNA sequences that play crucial regulatory roles in post-transcriptional gene control. These regulatory molecules primarily function by binding to complementary sequences within messenger RNA (mRNA) transcripts, thereby either suppressing protein translation or facilitating mRNA degradation, ultimately modulating gene expression patterns at the molecular level. To identify potential miRNAs that specifically target the identified biomarkers, we conducted comprehensive database screening using the miRDB computational platform (http://mirdb.org/miRDB/), applying a stringent Target Score threshold of ≥ 85 to ensure high-confidence predictions. This scoring system reflects the probability and strength of miRNA-mRNA interactions based on sequence complementarity and thermodynamic stability. Subsequently, to visualize the complex regulatory relationships among transcription factors, mRNAs, and miRNAs, we constructed an integrated TF-mRNA-miRNA regulatory network using Cytoscape visualization software (version 3.10.3), enabling comprehensive analysis of multi-level gene regulation mechanisms.

Compound prediction and molecular docking

Potential compounds related to AS treatment were explored through the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, https://ctdbase.org/). Based on the interaction count, the top 20 compounds were selected for AS. Subsequently, the Cytoscape software (v 3.10.3) was employed to construct a compound-biomarker interaction network diagram.

Molecular docking represents a fundamental computational approach extensively utilized in drug discovery and development for evaluating the binding interactions between candidate compounds and their target proteins, while simultaneously identifying optimal binding conformations and interaction sites. In this study, the molecular structures of candidate compounds were retrieved from the PubChem chemical database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to serve as ligand molecules for docking analysis. Subsequently, the three-dimensional protein structures corresponding to our biomarkers were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) repository (http://www.rcsb.org) and designated as receptor molecules for the docking simulations. The molecular docking between biomarkers and potential compounds was executed by leveraging the AutoDock software (v 1.5.7) [28]. The values approaching or below − 5.0 kcal/mol are considered indicative of moderate-to-strong binding interactions [29].

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2024.4. For small molecule preprocessing, AmberTools 22 was utilized to assign the GAFF force field, while Gaussian 16 W was employed for molecular hydrogenation and RESP charge calculation. The generated potential data were incorporated into the system’s topology file. Simulations were conducted at a constant temperature of 300 K and atmospheric pressure (1 bar), utilizing the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field with the TIP3P water model as solvent. The total charge of the system was neutralized by adding appropriate Na+ ions. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent method, followed by NVT (isothermal-isochoric) and NPT (isothermal-isobaric) equilibration. The initial velocity seeds were set to -1 and 67,890, with a total simulation duration of 100 nanoseconds. Each simulation was independently repeated twice to ensure data reliability. Upon completion of the simulations, trajectory analysis was performed using the software’s built-in tools. The root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), total energy, number of hydrogen bonds of the protein, and the binding distance between amino acid residues (CLU and GUCY1B3) and small molecules were calculated.

Blood specimen collection and qRT-PCR analysis

Experimental subjects and control subjects were recruited from individuals who had undergone catheterization in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. According to the results of coronary angiography, they were divided into experimental subjects with coronary heart disease and control subjects without coronary heart disease. To further clarify the significance of hub genes in patients, 12 patients were enrolled in this study. After being approached and providing informed written consent, subjects had clinical and blood specimens collected. The present investigation received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Ethical Application Ref: IIT-O-2025-309). Among the identified hub genes, we selected two upregulated candidates (CLU and GUCY1B3) for validation and performed quantitative analysis of their expression levels in peripheral blood samples using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Total RNA extraction from peripheral blood specimens was accomplished using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), followed by complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis utilizing a reverse transcription kit obtained from Applied Biological Systems. Quantitative real-time PCR amplification was conducted on the BioRad CFX96 thermal cycler platform employing the KAPA SYBR Green FAST BioRad Cycler Kapa kit (PeqLab). Target gene expression quantification was performed using the 2−ΔΔCt method with β-actin serving as the internal reference control. The primer sequences were as follows: CLU: 5’- GATGAATGGTGACCGCATCG − 3’(forward), 5’- ACGGAGAGAAGGGCATCAAG − 3’(reverse). GUCY1B3: 5’- AGCTCCAACCACATCCAAG − 3’(forward), 5’- TTCTGGGAATACAAGGCCAAG − 3’(reverse).

Statistical analysis

All data were handled using R software (version 4.3.3). The Wilcoxon test was applied to conduct comparative analyses, with a p-value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. For DEG analysis, nominal p-values were used instead of FDR-adjusted values to facilitate exploratory gene discovery, followed by downstream validation using machine learning and an independent dataset.

Subsequently, TPRG scores were notably higher in the AS group in GSE27034 (p = 0.000151) (Fig. 1C). WGCNA analysis confirmed that there were no outliers, and all samples were retained for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1D). The optimal soft threshold value (power) was ascertained to be 12 (R² > 0.85), when mean connectivity tended to be zero (Fig. 1E). Using this threshold, genes were organized into a scale-free network, and 10 co-expression modules were identified (Fig. 1F). The two key modules most positively and negatively correlated with the TPRGs score were the MEblack (176 genes, cor = 0.48, p < 0.01) and the MEmagenta (84 genes, cor = -0.60, p < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 1G). Therefore, all module genes of the key modules were selected, and a total of 260 TRMGs were obtained.

Identification and functional evaluation of 15 DE-TPRGs

The intersection between 96 DEGs and 260 TRMGs was taken to obtain 15 DE-TPRGs (Fig. 2A, Additional Table 2). The molecular biological functions associated with the 15 DE-TPRGs were characterized through GO analysis, resulting in the identification of 55 enriched terms. Blood coagulation (GO:0007596), coagulation (GO:0050817), and hemostasis (GO:0007599) were significantly enriched in 16 BP entries. In the 16 entries of the CC category, platelet alpha granule (GO:0031091) and chromaffin granule (GO:0042583) were identified. And 23 entries of MF category indicated that DE-TPRGs were highly pertinent to cAMP-dependent protein kinase inhibitor activity (GO:0008603) and protein kinase A catalytic subunit binding (GO:0034236) (Fig. 2B, Additional Table 3). Moreover, KEGG analysis demonstrated that DE-TPRGs were concentrated in five KEGG pathways, such as efferocytosis (hsa04148), phagosome (hsa04145), PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (hsa04151), and bladder cancer (hsa05219) (Fig. 2C, Additional Table 4). A PPI network comprising 14 proteins and 36 interaction pairs was constructed for the 15 DE-TPRGs, with TUBB1, H2BC21, H2AC6, THBS1, and CNG11 exhibiting the highest number of interactions (Fig. 2D).

The identification and validation of biomarkers

In the SVM-RFE analysis of GSE27034, when the number of SVM-RFE-feature genes was nine (THBS1, CLU, HIST1H2AC, TRBV27, LGALSL, GUCY1B3, P2RY12, HIST2H2BE, SDPR), the prediction accuracy of the model reached the highest for the first time (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, the Boruta algorithm was iteratively executed, gradually removing unimportant features. Eventually, four Boruta-feature genes, namely CLU, GUCY1B3, THBS1, and TRBV27, were determined (Fig. 3B). The two machine learning methods were employed to intersect the feature genes of AS associated with T cell proliferation, resulting in the four feature genes, namely CLU, GUCY1B3, THBS1, and TRBV27 (Fig. 3C).

Next, the expression trends of the four feature genes were validated in the AS and normal groups of GSE27034 and GSE12288. It was found that CLU and GUCY1B3 exhibited notably up-regulated expression in the AS group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3D-E) (the TRBV27 gene was not present in GSE12288). Therefore, CLU and GUCY1B3 were selected as candidate biomarkers for subsequent bioinformatics analyses. To further assess the differential expression of these candidate hub genes (CLU and GUCY1B3) in clinical samples, we performed qRT-PCR expression analysis in peripheral blood specimens. Our results showed that these candidate hub genes displayed substantially upregulated expression patterns in the AS patient group relative to healthy individuals consistent with the bioinformatics predictions (Fig. 3F).

GSEA and infiltration analysis in AS

The strength of GSEA lies in its ability to reveal large-scale, consistent shifts in biological process activities. The GSEA pathways associated with CLU were closely related to reactome translation, reactome rRNA processing, reactome influenza infection, and WP cytoplasmic ribosomal proteins (Fig. 4A). GUCY1B3 was significantly enriched in pathways such as Reactome rRNA processing and Reactome translation, among others (Fig. 4B). Reactome rRNA processing, reactome translation, and reactome influenza infection were collectively enriched in the pathways associated with these two biomarkers. These enrichment results suggest that CLU and GUCY1B3 may be computationally associated with T cell proliferation-related functional pathways in AS, though experimental confirmation is needed.

To conduct a more in-depth exploration of the immune microenvironment in AS, immune cell infiltration analysis was conducted using the data from GSE27034. The analysis uncovered considerable disparities in four DICs between the normal and AS groups (p < 0.05). Specifically, the proportions of naive B cells and resting natural killer (NK) cells were notably lower in the AS samples, while the proportions of activated dendritic cells and eosinophils were notably higher in the AS samples (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C-D). Moreover, Spearman correlation analysis showed that CLU was computationally positively correlated with activated dendritic cells (cor = 0.74, p < 0.001) and eosinophils (cor = 0.65, p < 0.01), while GUCY1B3 was positively correlated with activated dendritic cells (cor = 0.57, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4E). These computational findings suggest that CLU and GUCY1B3 may be associated with immune cell composition changes in AS, though causal relationships require experimental validation.

Establishment and assessment of a nomogram for AS diagnosis

Based on CLU and GUCY1B3 data from GSE27034, a nomogram was constructed for AS diagnosis. An increased total score correlates with elevated AS risk probability (Fig. 5A). The calibration curves revealed that the Ideal curve and the Bias-corrected curve almost overlapped, indicating a strong consistency, indicating the nomogram’s strong predictive accuracy (Fig. 5B). DCA analysis demonstrated that the nomogram model (red line) exhibited substantially higher benefit rates compared to both the diagonal line (All) and horizontal line (None), indicating the model’s clinical utility (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, ROC curves offered additional demonstration of the nomogram’s reliable forecasting performance. With an AUC value of 0.766, it indicated that the nomogram model indicated a certain reference value for the diagnosis of AS (Fig. 5D). Overall, the nomogram incorporating these candidate biomarkers showed acceptable predictive performance in this bioinformatics cohort, suggesting its potential utility as a reference model for AS risk stratification.

The TF-mRNA-miRNA network was employed to investigate the molecular mechanism in AS (Fig. 7A). Through the KnockTF and CHIPBase databases, for CLU, a total of 65 TFs were predicted, while for GUCY1B3, five TFs were predicted as regulators. These computational network analyses generated hypotheses regarding potential upstream regulatory relationships in AS that may merit experimental investigation.

The biomarkers were input into the CTD to identify 37 molecular compounds. Among them, three common targeted molecular compounds were bisphenol A, cadmium chloride, and benzo(a)pyrene (Fig. 7B). Since cadmium chloride had no 3D structure and benzo(a)pyrene had no hydrogen bonds, they were not subjected to molecular docking with the biomarkers. Molecular docking analysis computationally predicted that CLU may interact with bisphenol A (a binding energy: -4.17 kcal/mol), suggesting a possible moderate binding affinity (Fig. 7C). GUCY1B3 also showed a moderate-to-strong in silico binding affinity toward bisphenol A, with a binding energy of -5.02 kcal/mol (Fig. 7D). To further confirm the binding between bisphenol A and biomarkers, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to calculate the RMSD, RMSF, energy changes, hydrogen bond count variations, and binding distances between amino acid residues and small molecules in the bisphenol A-biomarker complexes. As the simulation progressed, the RMSD of GUCY1B3-bisphenol A gradually stabilized, indicating that the complex was becoming increasingly stable. In contrast, the CLU-bisphenol A complex demonstrated “relative stabilization in the early stage and significant flexible fluctuations in the later stage”. These simulation results suggest that the predicted binding of GUCY1B3 with bisphenol A may be relatively stable (Fig. 7E), while the predicted binding of CLU with bisphenol A appears less stable, though in vitro validation is required. In the GUCY1B3-bisphenol A group, the C-terminal and N-terminal regions were identified as “flexible hotspots”, whereas other structural regions showed relative rigidity. For the CLU-bisphenol A group, the C-terminal, N-terminal, and middle regions all exhibited “flexible hotspots” (Fig. 7F). During the molecular dynamics simulation, the total energy of the complexes displayed stable fluctuations without significant deviations from energy equilibrium, indicating that the simulation system possessed thermodynamic stability (Fig. 7G). The number of hydrogen bonds was concentrated between 1 and 2, suggesting that the formation and breaking of hydrogen bonds within the system were relatively stable, maintaining a dynamic equilibrium with slight fluctuations (Fig. 7H). Under different random seeds of initial velocities, the complexes exhibited minimal fluctuations, indicating that hydrogen bonds confer high overall stability to the complexes (Fig. 7I-J). These computational predictions may provide a preliminary basis for future experimental studies on BPA-target interactions in the context of AS.

Discussion

AS is a vascular disease characterized by dyslipidemia, chronic inflammation, and immune imbalance, with its pathological progression involving dynamic regulation of multiple immune cells [30]. In recent years, the role of T cell proliferation in AS has emerged as a research focus. Through differentiation, proliferation, and cytokine secretion, T cells participate in shaping the inflammatory microenvironment and modulating plaque stability [6]. This study integrated bioinformatics analysis and machine learning algorithms to computationally identify CLU and GUCY1B3 as candidate T cell proliferation-associated genes in AS. Subsequent functional enrichment, immune microenvironment profiling, and molecular regulatory network analyses provided computational insights into their potential associations with AS pathogenesis. Below, we contextualize these findings within current scientific understanding. This approach aligns with recent AS frameworks integrating WGCNA, machine learning, and immune profiling to improve biomarker robustness [31].

CLU (Clusterin/Apolipoprotein J), a multifunctional chaperone protein, has been reported in the literature to exhibit dual roles in lipid metabolism and inflammatory regulation [32]. As a component of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), CLU facilitates cholesterol reverse transport by binding to lipoproteins, thereby reducing low-density lipoprotein (LDL) deposition in the arterial wall. Its antioxidant properties inhibit LDL oxidation, alleviating oxidative stress-induced endothelial damage and foam cell formation. Furthermore, CLU has been reported to be associated with macrophage polarization (e.g., suppressing the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype) and downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokine release (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) in prior studies [33, 34]. Prior clinical studies report an inverse correlation between serum CLU levels and plaque instability in AS patients, suggesting its potential to stabilize advanced lesions.

GUCY1B3 (Guanylate Cyclase 1 Soluble Subunit β-1) is an essential component of the NO-sensitive soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC), which mediates the NO-cGMP signaling pathway to regulate vascular tone [35]. In AS, endothelial dysfunction has been associated with reduced NO bioavailability, which correlates with diminished sGC activity and subsequent cGMP depletion [36]. This promotes vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) proliferation and vasoconstriction, while also impairing cGMP-dependent platelet inhibition, thereby increasing thrombotic risk [37]. Animal studies suggest that GUCY1B3 deficiency exacerbates plaque burden and inflammatory infiltration under hyperlipidemia, highlighting its critical role in vascular homeostasis [38].

Functional enrichment analysis highlighted the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, efferocytosis, and phagosome pathway as central to AS pathogenesis. The PI3K-Akt pathway exhibits dual roles: its hyperactivation in macrophages promotes pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion (e.g., IL-1β) and foam cell formation, while its regulation of T cell metabolism drives differentiation into pro-atherogenic Th1 subsets. Impaired efferocytosis in advanced plaques leads to necrotic core expansion via NLRP3 inflammasome activation and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) release. Bioinformatics analysis suggests CLU may enhance efferocytic efficiency by opsonizing apoptotic debris, offering a therapeutic strategy for plaque stabilization. Dysregulation of the phagosome pathway, linked to lysosomal dysfunction, fosters cholesterol crystal accumulation and inflammasome activation, accelerating plaque progression. GUCY1B3-mediated NO signaling may restore phagosomal acidification, improving lipid metabolism.

Immune microenvironment assessments identified marked variations in the infiltration of activated dendritic cells (DCs) and eosinophils between the AS and control groups. Activated DCs drive T cell immune responses by presenting oxidized lipid antigens, while their secretion of IL-12 and IL-23 promotes Th1/Th17 polarization, exacerbating vascular inflammation. CLU expression inversely correlated with DC activation markers (e.g., CD86), suggesting its role in suppressing adaptive immunity. Eosinophils exhibit context-dependent effects: anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-4, IL-13) may stabilize plaques, but excessive infiltration disrupts endothelial integrity via eosinophil cationic protein (ECP) release. GUCY1B3 may regulate eosinophil recruitment through cGMP-dependent chemokine modulation, a mechanism requiring further validation.

Analysis from our research showed that AS patients exhibited considerably lower proportions of infiltrating naive B cells and inactive NK cells in their peripheral circulation. Current evidence indicates that B cells have been reported to participate in immune responses through direct cell-to-cell contact, antigen presentation, and cytokine secretion, thereby participating in both systemic and local vascular immune responses in AS [39]. Notably, no direct association between resting NK cells and AS has been reported in existing studies. Previous research has demonstrated that regulatory T cells (Tregs), as the only T-cell subset with intrinsic negative regulatory functions in autoimmunity, play a pivotal role in the pathological progression of AS by suppressing excessive immune activation and inflammatory responses, including critical processes such as modulating foam cell formation and maintaining plaque stability [40]. These computational results provide preliminary insights into potential immune cell composition changes in AS, The associations involving naive B cells and resting NK cells may represent areas for future experimental and clinical investigation. Molecular regulatory network analysis identified c-MYB, as a computationally predicted transcription factor. Prior literature associates c-MYB with pro-atherogenic processes in B cells, including OxLDL-specific IgG production and plaque inflammation. c-MYB inhibition may be associated with expansion of protective IgM-secreting B1 cells and reduced lipid deposition. Additionally, molecular docking analysis predicted potential interactions between endocrine disruptor bisphenol A (BPA) and CLU/GUCY1B3, these computational predictions suggest a possible association between BPA exposure and disruption of NO signaling and lipid metabolism pathways, warranting experimental verification. Epidemiological studies have reported associations between BPA exposure and cardiovascular risk, potentially mediated by estrogen receptor interference and mitochondrial oxidative stress [41]. The simulation results suggest that the predicted BPA–candidate gene interactions showed relative stability in terms of overall protein structure and conformational dynamics, providing a computational basis for further exploration of their potential biological relevance. However, all docking and MD results are purely computational and hypothesis-generating, and require experimental validation.

In summary, this study computationally identifies CLU and GUCY1B3 as T cell proliferation-associated biomarkers in AS, with potential bioinformatics-inferred associations spanning lipid metabolism, inflammation, and vascular function. These candidate biomarkers may have exploratory value for AS risk stratification and warrant further clinical validation. However, limitations persist: (1) The bioinformatics predictions reported here require experimental validation via methods such as immunohistochemistry and Western blot in human AS tissues; (2) The association of CLU and GUCY1B3 with T cell proliferation is inferred from transcriptomic co-expression with a literature-curated TPRG set, representing indirect computational evidence; no primary T cell functional assays were performed, and their causal role in AS remains untested; (3) BPA–CLU/GUCY1B3 docking and MD results are purely in silico; no functional, pharmacological, or phenotypic assays were performed. Accordingly, causal or therapeutic inferences in AS cannot be drawn, and these hypothesis-generating findings require validation in cellular, animal, and population-based studies. In the future, we can conduct multicenter cohort studies to verify the diagnostic efficacy of biomarkers. In the tissues of AS patients, the expression characteristics of CLU/GUCY1B3 can be verified through techniques such as immunohistochemistry and Western blot, offering a theoretical foundation for the development of new immunomodulatory therapies and individualized diagnosis and treatment plans. Additionally, future studies employing primary T cell isolation from peripheral blood or atherosclerotic plaque tissues, flow cytometric subtype identification (e.g., CD4 + effector T cells, Tregs, Th1 subsets), and functional proliferation assays (e.g., CFSE dilution assay, Ki-67 staining) will be required to establish a direct functional link.

Conclusions

The bioinformatics findings suggested that CLU and GUCY1B3 are candidate genes potentially associated with T cell proliferation in AS, and may provide a computational basis for future experimental and translational investigations.
