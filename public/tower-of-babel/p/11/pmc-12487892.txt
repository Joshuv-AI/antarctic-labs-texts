Alloys with an exceptional combination of strength and ductility
Authors: En Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intelligently designed complex concentrated alloys achieve yield strength rivaling ultrahigh-strength steel and tensile ductility akin to unhardened elemental metal, synergistically reaching both coveted properties.

Metals are the workhorse material for the manufacturing industry and structural applications. High strength is required to withstand service loads without yielding. Ductility allows ample safety margins against failure. Particularly important is high uniform tensile ductility, which is indispensable for adequate processability when forming and shaping. A question naturally arises: Can metallic alloys ever be simultaneously high in both strength and ductility?

STRENGTH-DUCTILITY TRADE-OFF

The commonly heard answer to the question above is “No.” Unstrengthened elemental metals offer excellent uniform tensile elongation (εu). However, their yield strength (σy) is inadequate for most structural applications. Over the centuries, a number of mechanisms raised the yield strength to gigapascal (GPa) levels to satisfy increasingly demanding load-bearing needs. Unfortunately, such an effective gain in strength is accompanied by a drastic sacrifice in tensile ductility and especially εu, seriously hampering formability. The area under the green banana-shaped curve in Fig. 1 shows this trade-off between strength and ductility (1). The strength-ductility space in this plot is the one currently accessible for bulk-sized alloys, bounded by yield strength σy ~ 2.3 GPa (upper limit from special ultrahigh-strength steels) and uniform elongation ~30% (as large as unstrengthened elemental metals). A synergy in σy and εu approaching the upper right corner of Fig. 1 was dismissed as a “mission impossible” by the metallurgy community up to a few years ago. A commonly held rationale refuting this proposition is the following: High strength means severe difficulty against the motion of plasticity carriers (defects called dislocation), whereas easy movements of dislocations correspond to high ductility. Demanding both at the same time frustrates the dislocations, such that they would not be able to comply.

However, this seemingly logical reasoning is flawed: While easy dislocation movement translates into large ductility, it does not mean that difficult dislocation motion must necessarily preclude extensive flow. Strengthening metallic materials to GPa strength should not be conflated with a near-total suppression of plastic flow capability. This is qualitatively different from the case of most ceramics and brittle intermetallics, in which dislocation motion is suffocated altogether such that ductility is intrinsically minimal due to the lack of operative plasticity mechanism. Lending support to this perspective is the observation that the compressive ductility of alloys is mostly uncompromised even when strengthened to 2 GPa. Barring premature damage, the loss of elongation in uniaxial tensile deformation results from an increased propensity for unstable plastic flow at higher flow stresses. This is because, when the yield strength is raised, uniform elongation is stable only when the strain hardening rate Θ, i.e., the slope of the stress-strain curve, stays sufficiently high to avoid the onset of instability (1, 2). Unfortunately, the strain hardening rate often fails to keep up proportionally with the yield strength elevated via strengthening. In the absence of adequate strain hardening to ensure uniform strains across the sample volume, plastic instability (necking, or another form of severe strain localization such as shear banding) sets in (1, 2), which truncates uniform tensile elongation. The excessively concentrated strain in the necked region eventually instigates damage. As a result, strengthening to above ultrahigh-strength steel while keeping a ductility similar to pure metal becomes a formidable challenge.

PRIOR ART

Nonetheless, the origin for the strength-ductility trade-off suggests that the two properties are not mutually exclusive. For a strengthened alloy, enough dislocations can be activated by the imposed ultrahigh stresses, which force tenacious dislocation motion to permeate plastic flow to the deforming sample volume. If mechanisms are present to sustain adequate strain hardening and/or strain rate hardening, flow localization instability can be prevented to preserve large uniform elongation. Progress in this direction has been gradual but steady over the past decades. The strategy for simple metals and alloys made use of heterogeneous nanostructures (deployed in forms of nonuniform distribution of different-sized grains or lamella) to induce strain gradient inside the material (1). To accommodate the mismatch between softer plastically deforming regions and the harder domains often remaining elastic, high densities of geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) are created (2), which generate back stresses that impose additional nonlocal hardening. This extends the well-known effect of nonhomogeneous plastic deformation (including strain gradient plasticity) (1). However, the best successes relying on such structural inhomogeneities remained just above the interpolation line (red dashed rule-of-mixtures in Fig. 1), connecting the high yield strength (but low εu) and the high εu (but low yield strength) end points (3). Thus, the prior art was, for the most part, a compromise not much above a rule-of-mixtures average.

ACCOMPLISHING THE IMPOSSIBLE

For the strength regime below ~1.2 GPa, earlier high-entropy alloys successfully marched into new territory, turning the banana curve upside down (4). Most recent progress has pushed the envelope to ~2 GPa, as highlighted in Fig. 1. This panoramic view shows the state-of-the-art reached by the expanding, gray-shaded region [new complex concentrated alloys (CCAs) including advanced steels (5–12)]. Looking at the current frontier, alloys can now be strengthened to ~2 GPa, exceeding the yield strength of ultrahigh-strength steels, while retaining a εu (~30%) similar to unstrengthened metals. Alternatively, looking from a different angle, ultrastrong alloys with yield strength as high as ~2 GPa can now retain most of the εu known for pure metals. This synergy upends the ancient Chinese proverb, “It is impossible to have fish and bear’s paw at the same time.” In practical applications, having it both ways (rather than the painful pick of “either strength or ductility”) means the availability of all-around metallic alloys, which, even when engineered to reach the highest strength currently possible for bulk materials, can still possess a ductility on par with elemental metals to allow easy shaping and forming.

MECHANISTIC UNDERSTANDING

The breakthrough in Fig. 1 demonstrates the power of materials science. The repertoire of plasticity mechanisms is bolstered by the advent of the CCAs containing multiple principal elements. To strengthen an alloy with accompanying strain hardening, the underlying materials science principle is to set up abundant effective barriers along the path of dislocations. These “speed bumps” make the movement of dislocations difficult, raising the strength (the stress needed to drive massive dislocations). With regard to strain hardening, they render dislocation traffic sluggish and tortuous and hence generate more and more jams and immobile dislocations via their interactions, hardening dislocation pathways with increasing strain.

Three specific strengthening and strain-hardening mechanisms are heightened by the abundant structural and chemical inhomogeneities inherent to CCAs (4). Specifically, (i) traditional mechanisms such as deformation twinning and phase transformations have been pushed to their extreme, because the CCA is enriched in alloying elements that depress stacking fault energy to abnormally low levels. (ii) The compositional, lattice-strain, and potential-energy landscapes in CCA are all so rugged, spatially varying from location to location, that frequent detrapping is necessary for the dislocations traversing across. (iii) The unusually high degree of freedom for composition tuning in CCAs has enabled an exceptionally high-volume fraction (of multiple types) of intermetallic particles densely populated in the matrix, effectively impeding and regulating dislocation motion. Chemical ordering of the multiple constituent elements on the sublattices in the intermetallic compounds can tailor the antiphase boundary (APB) energy to either very high or very low levels rarely reachable in the past (10, 12): These second-phase precipitates can be tuned to be either exceptionally strong or abnormally ductile and strain-hardenable, different from their base binary intermetallic compounds.

OUTLOOK

All of the above, particularly the multitude of strain-hardening mechanisms simultaneously at play, regulate plastic flow while imparting strengthening. In Fig. 1, unprecedented strength-ductility combinations are therefore being realized one after another. We are on the verge of combining ~2.3 GPa strength, more powerful than all steels, with ~30% uniform ductility, a processing flexibility normally belonging to pure metals. These advances call for metallurgists to reassess what previously irreconcilable targets are now possible using carefully crafted CCAs, what property combinations are ultimately possible with metallic alloys, and what is in store with regard to new discoveries and materials selection options.

The next challenge is to reach for super-high yield strength (well above 2.3 GPa) alloys without sacrificing much ductility. This is uncharted territory and would require yet another level of insightful alloy design to fight off escalating instability threats. In this regard, basic materials science questions abound. For example, to what extent can we still elevate strength before uniform elongation becomes invariably terminated by premature cracking? Have we exploited the rich potential of complex alloys to the fullest, in creating innovative microstructures? Are there new strategies/mechanisms yet to be discovered while meeting new property goals? Can we exploit these inspiring opportunities, collaborating with the community targeting ductile ceramics? From the perspective of engineering applications, research emphasis can now shift from ultrastrong yet ductile alloys to other roadblock properties. In particular, bottleneck issues associated with complex loading conditions, such as fatigue endurance, fracture toughness, and processability under multiaxial stresses, need to be high on the priority list.
