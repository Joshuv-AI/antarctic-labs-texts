The Impact of Preexisting Diabetes Mellitus on Chronic Lung Allograft Dysfunction After Lung Transplantation
Authors: Ye In Christopher Kwon, Michael Keller, Holly Caboti-Jones, Matthew Ambrosio, Barry C. Gibney, Luca Paoletti, Rachit D. Shah, Vipul Patel, Vigneshwar Kasirajan, Zubair A. Hashmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) remains a major cause of mortality after lung transplantation. Diabetes mellitus (DM) may contribute to CLAD through metabolic dysregulation. We evaluated post–lung transplant outcomes in recipients with DM.MethodsAdults undergoing first-time, isolated lung transplant (November 2017 to December 2024) with at least 6 months of follow-up were identified in the United Network for Organ Sharing registry and stratified by DM diagnosis at waitlist registration. CLAD was defined as ≥20% forced expiratory volume in 1 second and/or forced vital capacity decline after >3 months follow-up. Kaplan-Meier and multivariable Cox models assessed survival and CLAD risk, with subgroup analysis by race and ethnicity.ResultsAmong 18,199 analyzed patients, 3479 (18.3%) had DM. CLAD rates were higher among recipients with DM (21.3% vs 17.4%, P = .003). Grade 3 primary graft dysfunction at 72 hours was more frequent among recipients with DM (20.3% vs 17.1%, P = .03). Additionally, 5-year overall survival was lower in DM patients (55.2% vs 58.7%, P = .003). On multivariate analysis, DM was associated with increased risk of 5-year mortality (hazard ratio, 2.58, P = .004) and CLAD (odds ratio, 1.46, P = .03). Notably, in patients with DM, 5-year survival was significantly decreased among Asian patients (P = .024) compared with other races. Among patients with DM, Asian status was independently associated with increased risk of mortality (hazard ratio, 1.72, P = .002).ConclusionsRecipient DM is associated with reduced long-term survival and CLAD risk after lung transplant. Asian American recipients with DM appear to face disproportionately worse survival, suggesting a potential interaction between race, ethnicity, and metabolic status.

Lung transplantation (LT) remains the definitive treatment for patients with end-stage pulmonary disease, yet long-term survival continues to lag behind other solid-organ transplants.1 Chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) is the principal driver of mortality.2 In 2019, the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) formally recognized CLAD as a multifactorial complication encompassing both bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS) and restrictive allograft syndrome.2,3 This framework underscores the importance of identifying risk factors for CLAD and late graft failure. Among these, diabetes mellitus (DM) merits particular attention, as its prevalence among lung transplant candidates has risen alongside national metabolic trends.4 In LT, recipient DM has been linked to higher risks of infection, impaired healing, organ rejection, and long-term mortality.4, 5, 6 Prior studies suggest that preexisting DM confers higher 10-year mortality risk,4,7 yet its specific relationship to CLAD and airway complications such as bronchial stricture and dehiscence remains poorly understood. Thus, this study provides a contemporary, national analysis of the association between preexisting DM and post-transplant outcomes, including recipient and graft survival, CLAD, and airway complications, and explores whether these relationships vary in demographic subgroup or by donor type.

Patients and Methods

Study Population

Adults (age ≥18 years) listed in the United Network for Organ Sharing standard database for first-time, isolated LT between November 24, 2017, and December 31, 2024, with ≥6 months of follow-up were included. Supplemental data were obtained for post-LT pulmonary function test results and dates. Patients undergoing multiorgan transplant, retransplant, or lost to follow-up were excluded. Recipients were stratified by preexisting DM diagnosis at waitlist registration. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by race/ethnicity, sex, and donor type (donation after circulatory vs brain death [DCD vs DBD]). As all data were deidentified, this study was exempt from the Virginia Commonwealth University institutional review board.

Outcomes

Post-LT recipient and graft survival were estimated at 30 days, and 1, 3, and 5 years by the Kaplan-Meier method. Rates of grade 3 primary graft dysfunction (PGD), acute rejection, airway complications (stent for bronchial stricture, dehiscence), ventilator dependence, length of hospital stay, dialysis, treated rejection within 1 year, infection or rejection-related hospitalizations, and retransplantation were also compared. CLAD was defined per 2019 ISHLT criteria as ≥20% decline in forced expiratory volume in 1 second and/or forced vital capacity after 3 months of follow-up.2 Grade 3 PGD was defined per 2017 ISHLT criteria as PaO2/FiO2 <200 or on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support at 72 hours post-LT.8

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± SD and categorical variables as counts (%). Between-group comparisons used χ2, Wilcoxon rank-sum, or Fisher’s exact tests. Temporal trends were assessed by Mann-Kendall test. Survival differences were assessed with Kaplan-Meier and log-rank tests. An exploratory joint analysis assessed overlap between grade 3 PGD and CLAD using crosstabulation, overall and stratified by diabetes status. Variables with P < .20 in univariable analysis and clinically relevant covariates selected a priori6,7 were entered into multivariable Cox models. Multivariable logistic regression estimated associations between DM, CLAD, and airway complications. Results were reported as adjusted hazard ratio (HR) or odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI. All analyses were performed in R version 3.6.2 (R Core Team), with significance set at P < .05.

Results

Among 18,199 LT recipients, 3479 (19%) had preexisting DM (Figure 1). The proportion of LTs in recipients with DM rose significantly over time among DCD donors (P = .004), male individuals (P = .034), and Hispanic/Latino (P = .006) and Asian (P = .004) patients (Figure 2). DM recipients were more often male (66% vs 59%), had higher body mass index (26.8 vs 26.0 kg/m2), chronic steroid use (40% vs 36%), and shorter waitlist days (81 vs 115) (all P < .001) (Supplemental Table 1). Survival and Perioperative Outcomes

Preexisting DM did not impact early post-LT survival, with comparable 30-day survival between groups (97.8% vs 97.8%, P = .98). However, recipients with DM demonstrated lower survival at 1 year (88.2% vs 89.3%, P = .02), 3 years (70.0% vs 72.7%, P < .001), and 5 years (55.2% vs 58.7%, P = .003; Supplemental Table 2, Figure 3A). Recipients with DM experienced longer length of hospital stay (20 vs 19 days, P < .001), higher rates of dialysis (9.9% vs 8.1%, P < .001), acute rejection (24% vs 22%, P = .033), infection-related hospitalization (59% vs 53%, P < .001), and ventilator dependence ≥5 days (26% vs 23%, P < .001; Table). Among recipients with DM, Asian patients had the lowest 5-year survival (P = .024; HR, 1.84, P < .001; Figure 3B). Male recipients had worse 5-year survival than female recipients (P = .034; HR 0.94, P = .04; Figure 3C). Survival did not differ between DM recipients receiving DCD vs DBD lungs (P = .71; HR, 0.89, P = .75; Figure 3D). Risk of PGD, CLAD, and Airway Complications

On multivariable analysis, recipient DM independently predicted higher 5-year mortality (HR, 2.58, P = .004), along with pre–lung transplant extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support (HR, 1.42, P < .001), ventilator dependence at transplant (HR, 1.10, P = .004), and chronic smoking (HR, 2.05, P = .001; Supplemental Table 3). Grade 3 PGD at 72 hours posttransplant occurred in 17.7% of the overall cohort and was more frequent among recipients with diabetes (20.3% vs 17.1%, P = .03). The overall incidence of CLAD was 18.1% and was higher among diabetic recipients (21.3% vs 17.4%, P = .003). Among 3221 recipients with PGD, 640 (19.9%) developed CLAD, while the majority of CLAD cases occurred in recipients without PGD (2661 of 3301 [80.6%]). A similar pattern was observed among recipients with diabetes, in whom 170/707 (24.0%) of PGD recipients developed CLAD, whereas most CLAD cases occurred in the absence of PGD (570 of 740 [77.0%]). Recipient diabetes was associated with increased odds of CLAD (OR, 1.46, P = .03) and bronchial stent placement (13.8% vs 7.7%, P = .02; OR, 1.39, P = .045), but not airway dehiscence (1.6% vs 1.6%, P > .9; OR, 0.99, P = .72). Elevated risk of CLAD and bronchial stenting was also associated with Asian ethnicity, pretransplant extracorporeal membrane oxygenation use, ventilator dependence, chronic smoking, and higher body mass index (all P ≤ .01).

Comment

Approximately 19% of LT recipients have preexisting DM, yet its impact on long-term outcomes remains poorly understood.9 In this contemporary analysis, we report that preexisting DM independently predicted higher post-LT mortality, aligning with annual ISHLT registry analyses3 and single-center studies identifying DM7 and poor glycemic control as key predictors of survival.6 Beyond mortality, preexisting DM was associated with increased risk of CLAD and greater need for airway interventions, including bronchial stenting. Prior studies likewise demonstrate that both preexisting and new-onset post-LT DM increase risks of infection, acute kidney injury, CLAD, and death.5,7 As vascular and metabolic complications of DM evolve over decades, these associations likely reflect more immediate mechanisms, possibly through accelerated BOS, characterized by submucosal lymphocytic inflammation and obstruction of small airways.7 This may explain the comparable long-term graft survival among patients with pre-LT DM, despite the increased burden of CLAD, airway complications, and hospitalizations for infections. Biologic parallels between DM and fibrotic lung injury offer plausible mechanistic links. Chronic hyperglycemia promotes accumulation of advanced glycation end products and oxidative stress, promoting fibrotic lung tissue remodeling.5 These pathways may accelerate small airway fibroobliteration or impair anastomotic repair similar to those seen in BOS, thereby contributing to the increased odds of bronchial stenting in our DM cohort.

PGD represents an important marker of early lung injury. However, emerging evidence suggests that among recipients who survive index hospitalization, PGD does not appear to be independently associated with adverse long-term survival.10 This distinction is clinically important, as increasing PGD severity has been proposed as a contributor to CLAD raising concern that metabolic dysfunction in DM and early ischemia–reperfusion damage may synergistically accelerate late graft failure. In this study, although recipients with DM experienced higher rates of severe PGD, this did not translate into worse early mortality. Importantly, exploratory analyses demonstrated limited overlap between PGD and CLAD, with only a minority of recipients with grade 3 PGD subsequently developing CLAD and most CLAD occurring in recipients without prior severe PGD. These findings suggest that while PGD may reflect early graft vulnerability in metabolically high-risk recipients, it is unlikely to be the dominant pathway through which DM drives chronic allograft dysfunction. Rather, diabetes may predispose recipients to CLAD through mechanisms independent of early severe graft injury, including chronic microvascular dysfunction, impaired tissue repair, and sustained inflammatory signaling.6,7,9

Notably, we found disproportionately worse outcomes among Asian recipients with DM. Existing literature suggests that Asians develop DM at lower body mass indexes, with higher visceral adiposity and insulin resistance, conferring heightened vulnerability to metabolic injury and bronchial fibrosis.5 Socioeconomic and access disparities throughout the transplant continuum may further compound the adverse impact of concurrent DM. Our preliminary finding should be interpreted cautiously but argues for targeted investigation of metabolic control, nutritional status, and care access within Asian subgroups. Clinically, there is a critical need for systematic glycemic surveillance and aggressive perioperative metabolic management. Our institutional approach to DM management in LT candidates has evolved substantially since program inception. Previously, DM was often detected incidentally or after symptom onset, and management intensity was limited. Patients with DM are co-managed by endocrinology and diabetes educators for individualized care.

This analysis carries the inherent limitations of any retrospective administrative registry analyses. Data accuracy and completeness within the United Network for Organ Sharing database are center-dependent and may be subject to reporting variability over time. Importantly, key DM-specific variables, including glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), duration of disease, oral antiglycemic agent use, and insulin dependence, are not captured, precluding assessment of glycemic control or treatment intensity as modifiers of outcome. Thus, our findings underscore the need for future studies evaluating whether optimization of perioperative metabolic control may mitigate long-term allograft injury. Similarly, the United Network for Organ Sharing does not provide standardized grading of CLAD severity, imaging correlates, or delineation of its phenotypes between BOS and restrictive allograft syndrome.

In conclusion, preexisting DM was independently associated with higher mortality, greater risk of CLAD, increased airway complications, and infection-related hospitalizations following LT. These findings highlight DM as a critical determinant of long-term graft and recipient outcomes. Notably, Asian recipients with DM experienced disproportionately poorer survival, suggesting that metabolic and sociodemographic factors may interact to amplify risk.
