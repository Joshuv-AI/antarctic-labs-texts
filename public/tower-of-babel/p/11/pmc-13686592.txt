Long‐Term Outcomes of Sacral Neuromodulation Explantation: A 12‐Year Experience at a High‐Volume Tertiary Center
Authors: Carly Fiorido, Mario Henrique Bitar Siqueira, Mohammed Aldoukhi, Nojoud Alamri, Eviatar Fields, Naeem Bhojani, Bilal Chughtai, Omar Buksh, Sagi Shprits, Katherine Lajkosz, Dean Elterman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSacral neuromodulation (SNM) is a therapeutic option offered to patients with idiopathic overactive bladder (OAB) or non‐obstructive urinary retention, as an alternative treatment for refractory medical management. It allows for electrical stimulation of inhibitory circuits that modulate the micturition reflex and improve lower urinary tract symptoms. Despite high efficacy and safety, explantation remains a challenge clinically as it is unknown which factors predict explantation. Herein, we sought to understand the factors influencing SNM explantation.MethodsWe conducted a long‐term retrospective cohort study of 587 patients receiving SNM at one academic tertiary care center in Toronto, Ontario, performed by one surgeon.ResultsThe Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated explantation‐free survival probabilities of 97% (95% CI 96%–99%) at 1 year, 92% (90%–95%) at 3 years, and 81% (77%–86%) at 5 years. We did not determine any variables independently associated with time nor risk of explantation. The most common indication for explantation was loss of efficacy (57.7%), followed by patient preference (16.7%) and pain (16.7%).ConclusionExplantation after SNM occurs in a minority of patients, and is largely attributable to a loss of sustained therapeutic efficacy over time.

Introduction

Sacral neuromodulation (SNM) was introduced as a therapeutic strategy to modulate detrusor activity, establishing the foundation for current concepts and technologies [1]. SNM offers an effective therapeutic option for patients with idiopathic overactive bladder (OAB), with or without urinary incontinence, as well as for those with non‐obstructive urinary retention (NOR), particularly in individuals who do not respond to or cannot tolerate conservative or pharmacological therapies [2].

Although voiding is largely under voluntary control, it is regulated by complex interactions between somatic and visceral afferent pathways. Electrical neuromodulation is thought to activate primitive inhibitory mechanisms within the spinal cord that modulate the micturition reflex, thereby improving lower urinary tract function. However, the precise mechanisms underlying the effectiveness of neuromodulation across these comorbid conditions remain incompletely understood [3]. Current clinical practice therefore recommends SNM as a non‐pharmacological treatment option for OAB and chronic urinary retention when conventional therapies fail or are poorly tolerated [4].

Sacral neuromodulation has demonstrated strong efficacy and durability in large randomized controlled trials and long‐term prospective studies for the treatment of refractory OAB [5]. Additionally, SNM has been shown to be effective in other conditions, such as fecal incontinence, serving as an alternative to conservative management and more invasive surgical interventions [6]. In patients with chronic non‐obstructive urinary retention (CNOUR), defined as the inability to adequately empty the bladder in the absence of a mechanical obstruction, SNM has also demonstrated favorable outcomes and represents a promising therapeutic pathway [7].

Following approval by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), sacral neuromodulation has been widely adopted, with more than 325,000 implantations performed worldwide [8]. Despite its widespread use and overall success, explantation of sacral nerve stimulation systems remains a clinical challenge. Attempts to identify demographic, medical, social, or behavioral factors predictive of explantation have yielded inconsistent results [9]. This study presents long‐term data aimed at evaluating the epidemiology of SNM explantation and identifying factors associated with device removal.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study reviewed the medical records of patients who underwent sacral neuromodulation (SNM) implantation by a single high‐volume urologist at an academic tertiary‐care center in Toronto, Canada, between December 2013 and December 2024. The study was approved by the institutional research ethics board as a medical quality review, and the requirement for informed patient consent was waived.

Patient Population

All patients in the database underwent percutaneous nerve evaluation (PNE) or stage 1 SNM for the treatment of bladder or bowel disorders—including overactive bladder (OAB), chronic non‐obstructive urinary retention, fecal incontinence, and constipation—of at least 6 months’ duration and refractory to conservative therapies. The study population of interest included patients who were successfully implanted with an InterStim device (Medtronic, Minnespollis, MN) and subsequently underwent device explantation. Explantation was defined as complete device removal for any indication without replacement. Explantation did not include lead revision, lead or battery replacement, or lead or battery repositioning.

Data Collection

Collected variables included patient demographics (age and sex), indication for referral, prior treatments before SNM, the need for additional stage 1 SNM testing following PNE, procedural complications, and clinical outcomes (treatment success, postoperative complications, and use of adjunct therapies).

Successful lead placement was defined as the presence of pelvic floor bellows or toe dorsiflexion at a stimulation threshold of < 2 mA in at least three electrodes. Patient satisfaction was assessed during follow‐up visits using a patient‐reported satisfaction rating. Improvement was defined as a sustained > 50% improvement from baseline in one or more bothersome symptoms, including urinary frequency, urinary incontinence episodes, bowel seepage, or bowel urgency, as documented in bladder and/or bowel diaries. Patients who reported satisfaction and met the improvement criteria were classified as having achieved success.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort. Baseline variables were compared between patients with and without SNM explantation. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate, while continuous variables were compared using the Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test based on data distribution.

Time to explantation was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, with patients without explantation censored at the date of last follow‐up. Explantation‐free survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years were calculated, and differences between groups were assessed using the log‐rank test.

Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify predictors of time to explantation. Variables included age, sex, and clinical indications such as overactive bladder, fecal incontinence, urinary retention, and bladder/pelvic pain. A separate model evaluated the impact of single versus multiple diagnoses. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

All tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17BE (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

The cohort had a mean age of 58.6 ± 14.9 years (range 23–90) and included a predominantly female population (90.0%, 529/587). The median follow‐up for the overall cohort, which was 37.9 months (IQR 13.1–69.5). Baseline indications for SNM included overactive bladder (OAB) in 358 patients (61.0%), fecal incontinence (FI) in 150 (26%), urinary retention in 61 (10%), bladder or pelvic pain in 55 (9%), and less frequent indications such as interstitial cystitis and constipation (Table 1).

Comparison by Explantation Status

Comparison of baseline characteristics between patients with and without explantation demonstrated no statistically significant differences in age, sex, or underlying diagnoses, including OAB, FI, urinary retention, or bladder/pelvic pain (all p > 0.05; Table 1).

Explantation‐Free Survival Analysis

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated high rates of explantation‐free survival, with probabilities of 97% (95% CI 96%–99%) at 1 year, 92% (90%–95%) at 3 years, and 81% (77%–86%) at 5 years (Figure 1) These findings were consistent across subgroups stratified by sex, diagnosis, and number of indications (Table 2). Patients with bladder or pelvic pain showed a trend toward lower explantation‐free survival at 5 years (73% vs. 82%), although this did not reach statistical significance.

Predictors of Explantation

In Cox proportional hazards modeling, no variables were independently associated with time to explantation. Age (adjusted HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.98–1.01), male sex (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.31–1.65), OAB (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.27–1.55), urinary retention (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.22–1.94), and FI (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.27–1.69) were not significant predictors. Bladder or pelvic pain demonstrated a non‐significant trend in unadjusted analysis (HR 1.89, p = 0.051) but was not significant after adjustment (HR 1.29, 95% CI 0.48–3.47) (Table 3).

Indications for Explantation

The most common indication for SNM explantation was loss of efficacy, observed in 45 patients (57.7%). Patient preference accounted for 13 explantations (16.7%), while pain at the implantable pulse generator (IPG) site was also reported in 13 patients (16.7%). A smaller proportion of patients underwent explantation due to resolution of symptoms or no longer requiring therapy (5 patients, 6.4%). Infection was an uncommon cause, occurring in 2 patients (2.6%) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this 12‐year retrospective cohort study, 13.3% of patients required SNM explantation, falling well within the reported explantation rates within the literature. A comprehensive literature review encompassing 161 studies reported explantation rates ranging from 3.2% to 26%, with substantial heterogeneity largely attributable to differences in follow‐up duration, which varied from a mean of 10 to 124 months [10]. One study of 248 women found that 25% pursued explantation [11]. In a separate single‐center study including 219 patients, the explantation rate was 16%, with the most common indications for device removal being refractory pain at the implantation site (20%) and infection (20%) [12]. Another study evaluating 96 patients reported explantation in 12 individuals, primarily due to poor efficacy, further highlighting the variability in reported indications for device removal across the literature [13].

In this study one of the largest cohorts reported to date—lack of efficacy was the predominant indication for device explantation, accounting for 57.7% of cases. Explantation‐free survival at 5 years was 81% for the overall cohort, indicating good long‐term device durability, with lack of efficacy remaining the most common reason for device removal. In contrast, explantation due to infection was uncommon (2.6%) and comparable to rates reported in large long‐term series. In an analysis of 1930 sacral neuromodulation procedures with follow‐up extending beyond 10 years, the incidence of infection‐related explantation was 1.97%, with methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus identified as the most frequent pathogen (38%) [14].

Overactive bladder (OAB) is the most common indication for sacral neuromodulation implantation and, consequently, accounts for a large proportion of device removals reported in the literature [15, 16]. Despite this, studies specifically evaluating explantation have not consistently demonstrated a direct association between underlying diagnosis and explantation rates. In one such study, age was the only variable identified as a significant predictor of explantation in univariate analysis, with younger patients exhibiting a higher risk of device removal [17]. Another study, in contrast, reported that younger age may act as a risk factor for explantation in prior cohorts [12]. Here, we did not identify a statistically significant association between age and explantation on univariate analysis, similar to others who report a lack of association [18].

Diagnosis emerged as an important determinant of explantation. In a Medicare‐based cohort with a mean follow‐up of 60.5 months, 63 of 558 implanted batteries (11.3%) were explanted [19]. Among the 19 patients with a diagnosis of interstitial cystitis (IC), all underwent device removal. IC was the only variable independently associated with early explantation. No other evaluated variables reached statistical significance [19]. Similar to the present study one group found that symptom recurrence and battery depletion were key indications for explantation [20], at similar rates to those presented here. Other reasons for explantation, such as trauma, patient preference, or the need for MRI, accounted for a small proportion of cases and were predominantly observed in patients with underlying neurological or orthopedic conditions [21, 22].

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective, single‐center design may limit generalizability, as practice patterns, patient selection, and follow‐up intensity at a high‐volume tertiary referral center may differ from those in community or lower‐volume settings. Second, although the cohort spans more than a decade, the analysis is subject to inherent limitations of retrospective data, including incomplete documentation, potential misclassification of indications for explantation, and reliance on chart‐recorded symptom improvement rather than standardized, validated patient‐reported outcome measures. The median follow‐up for the overall cohort was 37.9 months (IQR 13.1–69.5). Although Kaplan–Meier analysis appropriately accounts for censoring, the decreasing number of patients remaining under follow‐up over time may reduce the precision of the 5‐year explantation‐free survival estimates. Therefore, these long‐term results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Third, the absence of a non‐explanted comparator group precludes estimation of overall explantation risk factors across the entire implanted population and limits causal inference regarding predictors of device removal.

Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured factors—such as psychosocial variables, patient expectations, programming strategies, and adherence to follow‐up—may have affected explantation decisions and could not be evaluated within the available dataset.

Conclusion

In this large cohort of patients undergoing sacral neuromodulation, explantation occurred in a substantial minority and was most commonly attributable to loss or lack of sustained therapeutic efficacy over time.
