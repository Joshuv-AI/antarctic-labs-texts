Risk of eating disorders among university students and its association with dieting, weight control behavior and non-substance addictions
Authors: Maria Antònia Amengual-Llofriu, Pedro Tauler, Antoni Aguiló
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEating disorders (ED) most often develop during adolescence and young adulthood. Therefore, university is deemed a period of risk due to increased academic demands and uncertainty about the future. Despite the high prevalence of ED among young adults, there is limited evidence regarding their association with non-substance behavioral addictions—such as mobile phone use and exercise addiction—in Mediterranean university populations. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of risk for ED and its association with risk factors among university undergraduate students. The association between ED risk and dieting, weight control behavior and the problematic use of mobile phones was also analyzed.MethodsTwo thousand six undergraduates aged between 18–30 at the University of the Balearic Islands participated in a cross-sectional survey. This survey included assessments of ED risk (Eating Attitudes Test—26 items, EAT-26), exercise addiction (Exercise Addiction Inventory, EAI), mobile phone addiction (Questionnaire on Mobile Phone-Related Experiences, CERM), body shape satisfaction (Stunkard Figure Rating Scale), and personality (Ten Item Personality Measure, TIPI). Additionally, questions regarding binge eating, dieting and other weight control behaviors, as well as sociodemographic and anthropometric data were included.ResultsAmong participants, 6.1% were found to be at risk for ED, with a higher prevalence in women than men (7.9% vs. 2.9%, p < 0.001). The logistic regression analysis showed that participants at risk were more responsible (OR = 1.410; p = 0.001), with lower emotional stability (OR = 0.810; p = 0.011), they had more frequent problems related to mobile phone (OR = 5.523; p < 0.001) and more risk of exercise addiction (OR = 8.965; p < 0.001). Furthermore, participants with ED risk performed more weight control behavior (OR = 2.808; p < 0.001), binge eating (OR = 1.887; p = 0.007) and dieting (OR = 7.465; p < 0.001 for successful diets; OR = 2.670; p = 0.011 for unsuccessful diets), and reported a desire for a lower body mass (OR = 4.411; p < 0.001).ConclusionsED risk was found to be associated with more frequent problems of mobile phone use and more risk of exercise addiction. The risk for eating disorders was associated with following a diet but also with the intention of following a diet. Therefore, measuring addictive exercise, problematic phone use or the continuous intention to follow diets could contribute to early determine ED risk.Keywords: Eating disorders, Problematic mobile phone use, Exercise addiction, Dieting, University students, Weigh control, Body shape satisfaction

Introduction

Eating disorders (ED) are multifactorial diseases where genetic, biological, psychological, family, and sociocultural factors play important roles [1]. ED exhibit the highest morbidity and mortality rates among mental health disorders, impacting physical, psychological, and social well-being [2]. Additionally, ED are associated with high healthcare costs [3, 4] and diminished quality of life [4]. In this light, ED are considered a major public health problem [5].

Body dissatisfaction has been recognized as a significant contributing factor to the development and perpetuation of ED [6, 7]. Furthermore, it has been observed that personality traits such as perfectionism, responsibility or emotional instability are among the most significant psychological risk factors involved in the development and maintenance of eating disorders [8–10]. Particularly, perfectionism can lead an individual to rigidly adhere to the thin ideal and increased body dissatisfaction [9]. This dissatisfaction often prompts various behavioral patterns aiming to improve one’s body image, particularly in women, leading to attempts to lose weight. Consequently, weight control behaviors such as binge eating, purging through vomiting or laxative misuse, as well as dieting, and compulsive or addictive exercise are commonly associated with ED [11, 12].

Nowadays, the increased and addictive use of electronic devices, primarily smartphones, among adolescents and young adults should also be considered. These devices provide easy and continuous access to social media and online information about eating disorders, as well as diets and ideal body shapes [13, 14]. In this regard, social media usage contributes to body image concerns and eating disorders (ED), and has been identified as a plausible risk factor for the development of ED [15]. Notably, ED most often develop during adolescence and young adulthood, representing the third most prevalent chronic disease among adolescent and young women [16]. Consequently, university stage is deemed a period of risk due to morphological changes, challenges related to independence, increased academic demands, and uncertainty about the future [17].

Despite the high prevalence of ED among young adults, there is limited evidence regarding their association with non-substance behavioral addictions—such as mobile phone use and exercise addiction—in Mediterranean university populations. The present study aimed to determine the prevalence of risk for ED and its association with risk factors among undergraduate students at the University of the Balearic Islands. The association between ED risk and dieting, weight control behavior and the problematic use of mobile phones were also analyzed. Based on previous findings, it was anticipated that female students would exhibit a higher prevalence of ED risk, dieting, and weight control behavior [7, 13, 18–21]. Additionally, it was expected that participants at risk for ED would demonstrate a higher prevalence of mobile phone addiction and excessive exercise.

Method

Study design and participants

A descriptive cross-sectional study was performed on a convenience sample of undergraduate university students from all subject areas between February and May 2018. Participants qualified for the study if they were currently enrolled on any undergraduate course at the University of the Balearic Islands (Palma, Balearic Islands, Spain, https://www.uib.eu/) and aged 18–30 years old. The previous sample size calculation (GRANMO 7.12) [22] determined that 1,692 participants would be required to extrapolate the results to all undergraduate students at the University of the Balearic Islands (95% confidence, 2% precision, and a replacement rate of 20%). Furthermore, a prevalence rate of 17% was considered, based on the average prevalence reported in previous studies conducted at Spanish universities [23–25].

Undergraduate students attending their lectures were verbally informed about the study and invited to participate. Subsequently, the survey —a pen-and-pencil questionnaire—was distributed to those students who consented to participate. Initially, 2,155 students voluntarily and anonymously completed the survey. However, 149 respondents (6.9%) were discarded due to incomplete records (n = 48), or because they were outside the age range (n = 101). This led to a final number of 2,006 participants (19.4% of all potential participants, n = 10,353), comprising 1,294 women (64.5%) and 712 men (35.5%).

All participants were informed of the purpose and requirements of the study. Consent to participate in the study was granted by agreeing to complete the survey. The protocol aligned with the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects and was approved by the University of the Balearic Islands Research Ethics Committee (21CER18).

Data collection

Self-reported data were collected from participants using the survey. Sociodemographic variables collected were gender, age and course year. Self-reported mass and stature were recorded as anthropometrical measurements, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated. Participants were categorized depending on their BMI in accordance with the following WHO criteria: underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (BMI = 18.5 to 24.9 kg/m2), overweight (BMI = 25.0 to 29.9 kg/m2) and obesity (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2) [26]. Personality traits (extraversion, affability, responsibility, emotional stability and openness to experience) were determined by using the validated Spanish version of the Ten Item Personality Measure (TIPI) questionnaire [27], initially developed and validated by Gosling et al. [28].

The validated Spanish version of the Eating Attitudes Test—26 Item (EAT-26) was used to determine the ED risk [29]. The questionnaire comprises three subscales: dieting, bulimia, and food preoccupation (eating and weight control). Questions were presented with a 6-point Likert scale (0: never, 5: always) and the cut-off score was set at 20 points, with higher scores revealing a risk for ED [30].

Tobacco consumption was assessed using four standardized questions, which enabled the classification of participants into one of four categories: non-smokers, former smokers, occasional smokers, or daily smokers. This classification was based on the criteria established by the 2020 European Health Survey in Spain [31].

Questions focused on ascertaining binge eating behavior were included in the survey. To verify this behavior, a general question was formulated: "in the last three months, have you eaten a large amount of food in a short period of time with a feeling of loss of control?" If the respondent gave a positive answer, they had to indicate the weekly or monthly frequency. This question reflects the diagnostic criteria for Binge Eating Disorder as defined in the DSM-5 [32]. Furthermore, participants were asked whether they engaged in behaviors commonly associated with weight and body shape control. The specific behavior patterns included both extreme practices—such as skipping main meals, inducing vomiting, using diuretics and laxatives, and engaging in compensatory or excessive exercise—and non-extreme practices, such as taking diet pills, avoiding family and social gatherings, controlling food quality and quantity, smoking to suppress appetite, and engaging in compensatory exercise. These behaviors were selected because they are the most frequently reported in the literature [33, 34]. An open option (“other”) was also included. The frequency and duration of the behavior was also ascertained. Furthermore, dieting behavior was determined by asking participants whether they had followed any type of diet in the last year (YES/I tried but I did not achieve it/NO). The option “I tried but I did not achieve it” was defined by a duration under one week.

Regarding non-substance addictions, the validated Spanish version of the Exercise Addiction Inventory EAI [35] was used to determine participants’ addiction to exercise. This questionnaire comprised six items that assess each component in behavioral addiction: relevance, mood change, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict and relapse [36]. Possible answers were presented on a 5-point Likert scale, leading to total scores ranging from 5 to 30. Participants could be categorized as asymptomatic (6–12), symptomatic (13–23) and at risk of an exercise addiction (a score of 24 or higher). Furthermore, addictive behavior related to mobile phones was also assessed using the questionnaire on mobile phone-related experiences (CERM) [37]. This questionnaire comprised ten items on mobile phone use on a 4-point Likert scale. The score was calculated by adding up all the answers, with a maximum of 40 points. Previously established cut-off points were applied [38]: no problems (10–15), occasional problems (16–23) and frequent problems (24–40 points).

Finally, body shape satisfaction was measured using the Stunkard Figure Rating Scale [39]. This test has been commonly used in similar studies [40, 41]. The questions referring to one’s own body image included illustrations of nine male and nine female bodies. The body shapes range from underweight to obesity and participants were asked to indicate the one they considered best matched their own body image (perceived body image) and the one they would like to have (desired body image). Body satisfaction level was calculated through the discrepancy between perceived and desired body image using the Stunkard figure rating scale, which presents nine gender-specific silhouettes ranging from underweight to obesity [39]. Participants were then classified as satisfied (same perceived and desired figures), as having a desire to lower their body mass (selecting a desired thinner figure) or as having a desire to increase their body mass (selecting a larger figure).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 software (SPSS/IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical significance was accepted at a p-value below 0.05. All the data were tested for normal distribution (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Descriptive analysis was used to report the frequencies and percentages of category variables, and the mean and standard deviation (SD), or the median and the interquartile range (IR, Q25-Q75) were reported for quantitative variables. Either Student’s t-test for unpaired data, the U-Mann Whitney test or Pearson’s chi-square (χ2) test was used to evaluate differences between groups. All analyses were stratified by gender. Logistic regression analysis was used to determine the association between eating disorder risk (YES/NO) and independent variables in the entire sample. The following independent variables showing significant differences between participants with and without an ED risk in the binary analysis were considered: gender, responsibility, emotional stability, BMI, CERM, EAI, weight control behavior types, binge eating, dieting and satisfaction with body mass. Logistic regression models were also conducted separately for women and men, to examine gender-specific associations with ED risk.

Results

General characteristics and risk factors of study participants

Table 1 shows the characteristics of study participants and the values for measured variables. The median age of participants (64.5% women, 35.5% men) was 20 years. The overall prevalence of overweight and obesity was 14.5% and 3.3%, respectively. These values were significantly higher in men (17.5% overweight; 3.7% obesity) than in women (12.9% and 3.1%, p < 0.001 for gender differences). A higher percentage of women reported occasional or frequent problems with mobile phone use (p = 0.021). Nonetheless, a higher prevalence of symptomatic exercise addiction or risk thereof was found in men (p < 0.001). Among personal traits, higher values in women (p < 0.001) were observed for extraversion, responsibility, and openness to experience, whereas men showed higher values for emotional stability (p < 0.001).

The prevalence of binge eating (p = 0.032), and body shape dissatisfaction (p < 0.001) was higher in women. General characteristics and risk factors of study participants stratified per ED risk

Table 2 and Figs. 1, 2 and 3 show the characteristics of study participants stratified by ED risk. Among personal traits, higher values for responsibility (p = 0.001) but lower values for emotional stability (p < 0.001) were observed in participants at risk of an ED. A higher prevalence of both problems related to mobile phone use (p < 0.001) and the risk of exercise addiction (p < 0.001) was found in participants at risk for an ED. These differences in non-substance addictions can be also observed in Figs. 1 and 2.

Weight control behaviors among study participants

The most prevalent weight behavior types were avoiding family and social gatherings and skipping meals (Table 3). All weight control behavior deemed extreme was seen in higher percentages of women. Most students who showed weight control behavior reported performing one (69.7%) or two types (21.7%), with lower figures for three (5.6%), four (1.4%), five (1.1%), six (0.3%) and seven (0.3%). No differences were observed between men and women in the number of control behaviors performed (p = 0.378), with 96.2% of men and 89.5% of women showing one or two control behaviors.

Table 5 shows the results of the logistic regression analysis for ED risk (YES/NO). Discussion

ED represent a major public health concern due to their complex etiology, high prevalence among youth, and potential for severe health consequences [1, 2]. The aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of risk for ED and its association with risk factors among undergraduate students. The association between ED risk and variables such as dieting, weight control behaviors, and behavioral addictions—such as mobile phone use and exercise addiction—was also analyzed. The results of this study revealed a 6.1% prevalence of ED risk among university students, with a higher prevalence in women compared to men. Interestingly, ED risk was found to be associated with frequent problems with mobile phone use and a risk of exercise addiction. Furthermore, ED risk was also associated with certain personal traits, including higher levels of responsibility and lower emotional stability, as well as body dissatisfaction, weight control behaviors, binge eating, and dieting. Notably, even the intention of following a diet was associated with an increased risk of ED.

The prevalence of eating disorder (ED) risk in this study was similar to or slightly lower than previously reported rates for university students, which range from 4.5% to 14.3% [13, 18, 42–44]. It is important to note that studies using different tools for ED risk screening or relying on completely self-selected participant recruitment have reported prevalence rates as high as 40% [19–21, 40, 45–47]. A sole focus on women in such studies may explain the higher figures, as this study also demonstrates that ED risk prevalence is higher in female university students compared to males [18–21, 23, 41, 42, 46]. This higher prevalence in women has been attributed to biological factors such as differences in the neuroendocrine system, as well as to sociocultural factors, including different culturally formed beliefs and social pressures [6, 7]. However, it has also been pointed out that ED in men are underdiagnosed, as screening tools are more sensitive to behaviors more commonly exhibited by women [48].

Dieting has been commonly and strongly associated with ED in university populations [21, 49]. Indeed, it has even been considered the most important predictor for ED among university students [49]. This association has been attributed to an increased preoccupation with weight or body shape [2, 50]. In the present study, participants at risk for ED reported a higher prevalence of dieting. Notably, merely attempting to follow a diet, even without successfully adhering to it for at least one week, was also found to be associated with ED risk. This suggests that the intention to diet could characterize individuals at the ED onset. However, further research is needed to fully elucidate this relationship, considering that experimenting with various diets is common among university students in the context of all changes arising from the transition to university life [49]. In fact, and consistent with previous findings [18, 46, 51, 52], about one in five participants without ED risk also reported following a diet. Additionally, around 16% of participants without risk exhibited weight control behaviors. As could be expected, this figure is lower than that for participants with ED risk, reinforcing the commonly reported association between ED risk and weight control behaviors among university students [42, 53].

The most prevalent weight control behavior among participants with ED risk was avoiding family and social gatherings. This behavior may arise from challenges in social adaptation and a fear of judgment based on physical appearance [54] Additionally, since such gatherings often involve food intake, avoidance may also serve to prevent weight gain. This could also explain why skipping main meals, particularly dinner (results not shown) [21], was the second most common weight control behavior observed [42, 55].

In the present study, a significant association between ED risk and problematic mobile phone use was also observed. It is worth noting, however, that previous studies focusing on university students have reported ED risk as being more strongly associated with internet addiction than with problematic mobile device use [56], showing significant associations between ED and addiction to both the internet [14, 56, 57] and social networks [40]. Given that young people primarily access the internet through smartphones, these results could indicate that the risk is not so much associated with the mobile device itself, but with internet usage in general. The continuous development of these devices and the emergence of new social networks expose individuals with ED risk to a greater risk of addiction and, consequently, increased ED risk [14]. The results of this study may validate problematic phone use as a marker of ED risk, since the association was only found among participants who reported “frequent problems”, and no association was found among participants who reported “occasional problems” with mobile phone use.

The increased risk associated to the rise of internet usage could mainly be explained by sociocultural [58] and self-objectification [59] theories. These theories are based on how the media broadcasts beauty ideals, particularly thinness in women, which are generally far from reality. Indeed, it has been shown that media-idealized images have the most harmful and significant impact on vulnerable individuals [60]. With repeated exposure to these ideals, individuals begin to self-objectify, seeing themselves as objects rather than people. Specifically, they objectify their bodies, comparing them to the unrealistic beauty standards they have internalized. This comparison leads to body dissatisfaction, which may drive susceptible individuals to engage in disordered behaviors to achieve their ideal [61]. Results from the present study also suggest that personal traits, such as a higher responsibility, characterize individuals susceptible to ED. Responsibility can result in heightened internal pressure, contributing to disordered eating behaviors as individuals strive to meet unattainable standards [62].

Body dissatisfaction is considered as one of the most important predictors for developing ED [6, 7]. Results of the present study also suggest a strong association between ED and body dissatisfaction in women, who commonly report higher levels of body dissatisfaction [40, 47, 52, 63]. Furthermore, body dissatisfaction in women was also found to be completely associated to a desire to lose weight, which could be explained by the societal ideal of a thin body [64]. The desire to lose weight has been reported as one of the main predictors for developing ED and, consequently, as a primarily reason for the higher prevalence of ED in women [6, 7]. However, in line with previous results [40, 52], it is noteworthy that half of the participants without an ED risk -out of a population where around 73% of individuals had a normal weight- also reported a desire to lose body weight. These results characterize a university student population with a high prevalence of characteristics and behaviors associated with ED risk, such as body shape dissatisfaction, weight control behaviors and dieting, even among students without risk of ED.

The body dissatisfaction of participants with an ED risk may also be reflected in an addiction to physical activity. Addiction to physical activity in patients with an ED has been linked to concerns over body image or weight, and an attempt to increase energy expenditure to achieve a desired thin ideal, rather than deriving satisfaction from the activity itself [44, 54]. In fact, it has been reported that addictive physical activity could play an essential role in ED risk development [11]. In line with these observations, a significant association between ED risk and exercise addiction was found in the present study. Furthermore, excessive exercise as a weight control behavior has been revealed as a potential differential factor between participants with and without ED risk when combinations of behaviors were analyzed (Fig. 4). However, while the aforementioned association with problematic mobile phone use was significant in both men and women, the association of ED risk with addictive exercise was significant only in women. The lack of a significant association in men aligns with results from an earlier study [44] and could be attributed to higher overall levels of physical activity commonly found in men.

Interestingly, similar to the findings of the present study regarding mobile phone use, it is interesting to note that no association with ED risk was found in participants exhibiting symptoms of exercise addiction (categorized as the intermediate level according to the questionnaire scoring protocol). Hence, concerning "non-substance" addictions, ED risk may solely be linked to extreme behaviors. These results could suggest that assessing addictive exercise and problematic phone use may offer some discriminatory capacity in predicting the development of ED.

This study presents some limitations that should be acknowledged. In addition to the limitations inherent to its observational design, the current study was limited to cross-sectional, self-reported data from students at a single university. The different questionnaires used in this and previous studies to record ED risk and other study variables may have impacted comparisons with previous results. The relatively low number of male participants could be also considered as a limitation of the study. In this regard, and given the low prevalence of ED risk, particularly among men, some subgroup analyses may not have achieved a proper statistical power.

Conclusions

ED risk was found to be associated, among other variables, with problematic mobile phone use and addictive exercise. To our knowledge, this is the first study reporting a significant association between ED risk and problematic mobile phone use. Nonetheless, these associations were only found in the most extreme categories: frequent problems with phone use and the risk of exercise addiction. Therefore, measuring addictive exercise, problematic phone use or the continuous intention to follow diets could contribute to early determine ED risk. Regarding dieting, ED risk was found to be associated with the mere intention of doing so. Attention should be paid to young university adults without an ED risk since the prevalence of patterns commonly associated with ED risk, such as dieting or weight control behavior is high. Taking into account these findings, it would be advisable to design preventive interventions that specifically focus on digital behaviors and body image perception. Furthermore, future research should consider longitudinal study designs to explore causal relationships between non-substance behavioral addictions, impulsive and addictive physical exercise practices, and the risk of ED.
