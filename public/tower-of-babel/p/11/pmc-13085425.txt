Buffering the effects of work stress on anxiety and depression: a moderated mediation model of physical activity enjoyment and sleep disturbance in China
Authors: Kunpeng Li, Chih-Hung Pai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the pathways linking work stress to anxiety and depression among a sample of full-time white-collar professionals (spanning various industries and organizational hierarchies) in China’s first-tier cities and to test the buffering role of physical activity enjoyment in this adverse process.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 1,546 professionals in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou. A moderated mediation model was tested using the PROCESS macro (Models 4 and 59) to examine the mediating role of sleep disturbance and the moderating role of physical activity enjoyment.ResultsThe results indicated that work stress was significantly and positively associated with both anxiety and depression (β = 0.184, p < 0.001) and sleep disturbance (β = 0.431, p < 0.001). Sleep disturbance, in turn, was positively associated with anxiety and depression (β = 0.126, p < 0.001). Sleep disturbance partially mediated the relationship between work stress and anxiety and depression (indirect effect β = 0.071, 95% CI [0.049, 0.094]). Crucially, physical activity enjoyment significantly moderated the associations between work stress and anxiety and depression (β = -0.166, p < 0.001), between work stress and sleep disturbance (β = -0.081, p < 0.001), and between sleep disturbance and anxiety and depression (β = -0.031, p < 0.05). This moderation ultimately attenuated the strength of the overall indirect effect.ConclusionsThe findings demonstrate that physical activity enjoyment is a key protective resource for mitigating the adverse mental health associations of work stress. This study extends the Effort–Reward Imbalance (ERI) and Stress-Buffering Models (SBM) and provides critical empirical evidence for workplace interventions, suggesting a strategic shift from emphasizing quantity of exercise to enhancing the quality of the exercise experience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04320-z.

Introduction

Data from the World Health Organization (WHO) underscore the global scale of mental health challenges stemming from work stress. Workplace adversity is a primary contributor to 70% of cases involving anxiety and depression, and work-related stress is linked to 120 million cases of depression annually [1]. Furthermore, a significant association has been established between long working hours and the prevalence of depressive symptoms, with this relationship being more pronounced in Asia than in Europe [2, 3]. Against this global backdrop, the issue of work-related stress is particularly acute in China. Recent socioeconomic transformations have cultivated a hypercompetitive professional environment, positioning the country as a compelling case for in-depth investigation. According to 2022 national monitoring data on occupational health literacy from the National Health Commission of China, 15% of the workforce reported experiencing negative emotions such as depression, tension, or anxiety during the previous year. This figure ranks as the third most prevalent health issue monitored, highlighting the problem’s severity and pervasiveness [4]. In an era of intensifying involution (a cycle of relentless internal competition), the demands of work and social comparison with peers have emerged as the foremost stressors for professionals [5, 6]. These unique psychosocial pressures collectively contribute to the growing prominence of mental health issues such as anxiety and burnout among China’s working population, underscoring the urgent need for both rigorous scholarly inquiry and effective intervention strategies.

Evidently, the modern professional landscape has become a significant psychosocial stressor. Work-related stress is evolving into a formidable global challenge and is a primary driver of mental health issues among employees, including anxiety and depression [7]. A body of research confirms that psychosocial factors—such as effort–reward imbalance, a negative workplace ethical climate, and high job demands—are significantly associated with adverse outcomes, including depression, anxiety, and psychological distress [8–11]. While the connection between work stress and anxiety and depression is well established, the intricate mechanisms underlying this relationship, particularly the mediating role of insomnia, remain underexplored [11, 12]. Long working hours frequently come at the expense of sleep, and the deprivation of this critical resource for biopsychosocial recovery has been shown to directly exacerbate the vicious cycle between work-related stressors and insomnia [13].

In this context, physical activity has emerged as a valuable intervention, with its role in alleviating the burden of mental health issues becoming increasingly recognized [14]. Evidence indicates that different types of exercise offer distinct psychological benefits; aerobic exercise has been shown to significantly mitigate anxiety symptoms, whereas resistance training appears to be more effective at reducing depressive symptoms [15]. Furthermore, combined training programs that incorporate both aerobic and resistance elements have also proven effective at lowering depression levels, presenting a comprehensive intervention strategy for improving employee well-being [16]. Despite the well-documented benefits of physical activity, a paradoxical reality persists: many individuals who exercise regularly still suffer from work-related mental health problems. This suggests that the quantitative dimensions of exercise, such as frequency and duration, may not fully account for its protective effects [14]. Emerging research has begun to emphasize that the psychological attributes of exercise may be more critical than its physical parameters. Specifically, exercise motivation stemming from intrinsic interest has been found to be a more powerful predictor of positive mental health outcomes than exercise duration itself is [17]. This enjoyable exercise experience can serve as a vital psychological resource. It may not only replenish the positive affect and energy depleted by work stress but also proactively reshape an individual’s cognitive appraisal of that stress [18, 19], thereby more effectively interrupting the negative cascade from stress to psychological distress. Accordingly, this study posits that the qualitative experience of exercise—specifically, the enjoyment derived from it, or physical activity enjoyment—represents a critical missing piece in the current literature. The present research aims to address this paradox through empirical investigation.

Currently, few studies have incorporated physical activity enjoyment as a moderating variable within a comprehensive model examining the interplay of work stress, sleep, and mental health, with a particular dearth of validation using longitudinal data [20]. Therefore, this study aims to construct and test a moderated mediation model to elucidate the underlying mechanisms through which work stress is associated with anxiety and depression. The analysis specifically investigates the mediating role of sleep disturbance in this relationship and the moderating effect of physical activity enjoyment on the pathways involved. This research not only promises to deepen the theoretical understanding of the stress‒health nexus but also has significant practical implications. By clarifying the protective role of physical activity enjoyment, the findings provide more precise, evidence-based guidance for mental health interventions targeting urban populations. This could help foster a strategic shift from merely prescribing exercise to actively promoting enjoyable physical activity. Accordingly, this study seeks to address the following research questions (RQs):

Literature review

Work stress and anxiety and depression

In the field of occupational health psychology, the Effort–Reward Imbalance (ERI) model provides a core theoretical framework for understanding how work stress affects mental health [21]. This model posits that psychological distress arises when an individual’s high level of effort at work is not met with adequate rewards. A consistent body of research indicates that adverse organizational environments, negative workplace ethical climates, and specific work-related stressors are significant factors associated with anxiety and depression among employees [22–24]. This relationship has been widely validated across diverse occupational groups [25, 26], with numerous empirical studies providing robust evidence for the negative association between work stress and mental well-being [10, 27]. The central mechanism of the ERI model is that a sustained state of imbalance between effort and reward can trigger negative emotions and feelings of frustration in individuals, in turn degrading their psychological health [28–30]. Grounded in ERI theory and supported by extensive empirical findings, work stress has been identified as a key factor related to anxiety and depression. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

Sleep disturbance mediates the relationship between work stress and anxiety and depression

Prolonged work stress is known to significantly increase the risk of insomnia [31–33]. Stress not only depletes an individual’s psychological capital, making it more difficult to cope with sleep disruptions [33], but it can also lead to excessive work engagement, a state in which individuals are unable to mentally detach from work-related thoughts during nonwork hours, thereby impeding the sleep onset process [34]. Conversely, sleep deprivation or poor sleep quality is not merely a passive consequence of stress but also an active risk factor that can significantly amplify symptoms of anxiety and depression. Across various occupational populations, sleep disturbance has been identified as one of the most potent factors related to anxiety and depression [35, 36]. Evidence from intervention studies further demonstrates that effectively alleviating insomnia symptoms can lead to significant improvements in depression, stress, and burnout, confirming that enhancing sleep is a viable pathway for promoting mental health [37, 38]. Sleep disturbance is a partial mediator of the relationship between occupational stress and depression, anxiety and emotional exhaustion. Research has also suggested that insomnia is an important mediator between job burnout and depression, accounting for approximately 69.2% of the total effect [39]. Research on interventions that target sleep to improve mental health and that work through sleep also lend some indirect support to this pathway [37].

Collectively, these results indicate that sleep disturbance is an important linking mechanism in the negative cascade between work stress and anxiety and depression [40, 41]. On the basis of this body of evidence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Physical activity enjoyment as a moderator of the relationship between work stress and psychological distress

Within the theoretical framework of this study, physical activity enjoyment may function as a critical psychological resource. The Stress-Buffering Model (SBM) offers a theoretical explanation for the affronted protective mechanism [42]. This model suggests that psychosocial resources can effectively protect individuals against the harmful effects of stressors on physical and mental health. On the basis of this model, it is suggested that the subjective experience of physical activity enjoyment, which goes beyond the instrumental properties of the exercise, may exert its effects through different psychological routes [43].

First, physical activity enjoyment may buffer the positive association between work stress and anxiety and depression. When people experience work stress, it is likely that their positive psychological resources become depleted and that negative cognitive activity worsens their negative emotions. Physical activity enjoyment refers to an experience consisting of pleasure and intrinsic satisfaction, which is opposed to any work-related experience. Exercise can therefore be an effective tool for renewing these psychological resources [44, 45]. This positive experience may facilitate a cognitive reappraisal of stress and enhance self-compassion, potentially mitigating the cognitive pathways through which stress translates into negative emotional outcomes [44, 45]. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H5a: Physical activity enjoyment significantly moderates the association between work stress and anxiety and depression. Specifically, the positive association between work stress and anxiety and depression is weaker for individuals with higher levels of physical activity enjoyment.

Second, physical activity enjoyment may similarly attenuate the association between work stress and sleep disturbance. One of the primary psychological mechanisms through which work stress leads to sleep disturbance is believed to be hyperarousal. When physical activity is experienced as enjoyable, individuals may find it easier to become fully engaged in the activity itself. This positive engagement is thought to enhance emotional regulation skills, enabling individuals to better manage stress-related emotions. This, in turn, may lower their arousal levels, creating more favorable conditions for sleep onset [46, 47]. Indirect support for this inference comes from studies on mind-body exercises; these forms of activity, characterized by their intrinsic enjoyment and focus, have been found to be more effective than conventional exercise in alleviating stress and improving sleep [48, 49]. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H5b: Physical activity enjoyment significantly moderates the association between work stress and sleep disturbance. Specifically, the positive association between work stress and sleep disturbance is weaker for individuals with higher levels of physical activity enjoyment.

Finally, we further postulate that, even when sleep has already been disrupted, physical activity enjoyment may still weaken the association between sleep disturbance and anxiety and depression. Sleep disturbance typically exacerbates states of anxiety and depression by depleting an individual’s cognitive and emotional resources, making them more vulnerable to negative moods the following day. However, physical activity itself is regarded as an independent antianxiety and antidepressant intervention [45, 50, 51]. It is therefore plausible that individuals who find enjoyment in exercise are more likely to employ it as a regular and proactive coping strategy. This type of enjoyable activity is theorized to improve mood through various psychological mechanisms. This mood enhancement could, in turn, alleviate the sleep difficulties that stem from emotional distress, thereby indirectly promoting sleep recovery [52–54]. On the basis of this reasoning, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H5c: Physical activity enjoyment significantly moderates the association between sleep disturbance and anxiety and depression. Specifically, the positive association between sleep disturbance and anxiety and depression is weaker for individuals with higher levels of physical activity enjoyment.

In summary, drawing upon the theoretical logic of SBM and insights from related empirical research, it is reasonable to conclude that physical activity enjoyment may play a critical moderating and mitigating role at multiple points along the pathway from work stress to anxiety and depression. It may not only weaken the initial impact of the stressor but also slow the deterioration of intermediate links and help counteract the final adverse health outcomes. Accordingly, this study proposes a moderated mediation hypothesis:

Methodology

Data collection and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design with purposive sampling to obtain a representative sample, rather than a specific restricted cohort. This approach was selected to ensure that the findings could be cautiously generalized to the broader population of urban professionals sharing similar sociodemographic and environmental characteristics. Data were collected through the professional services of Wenjuanxing (WJX.cn), China’s leading online survey platform, which has been recognized as a preferred tool for researchers because of its reliable responses and participant impartiality [55, 56]. The survey was administered over a 36-day period, from June 15 to July 20, 2025.

The sampling frame was carefully constructed with specific inclusion criteria to obtain the required population. The participants must have been (1) full-time white-collar professionals (defined in this study as individuals engaged in mental or administrative labor across various sectors, such as technology, finance, education, healthcare, and enterprise management, encompassing organizational hierarchies ranging from entry-level staff to middle and senior management), (2) currently residing in one of China’s three first-tier cities (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou), or (3) residents of their respective cities for at least six months to be familiar with the working landscape. The WJX.cn platform was responsible for managing the recruitment process; this platform automatically sends out survey invitations to sample pool members who met our requirements. The quality of the data and validity of the responses were controlled using several measures. First, the system of the platform did not allow a person to submit a form twice using the same IP address. Additionally, the platform flagged responses that were submitted for a very short completion time. The beginning of the survey contained a screening question to reconfirm eligibility (e.g., city of residence). Finally, an attention check item was included in the questionnaire to identify and exclude inattentive respondents. This multifaceted approach was designed to maximize the relevance and reliability of the collected data.

The selection of China’s three first-tier cities was based on several key considerations. Geographically, Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou serve as the respective cores of northern, eastern coastal, and southern coastal China, representing three major economic circles and encompassing regional and cultural diversity. Economically, their status as premier metropolitan centers reflects a high level of modernization, and their professional environments are indicative of leading-edge workplace trends. The survey was conducted in two phases. The first phase consisted of a pilot survey in which 314 questionnaires were distributed across the three cities. After invalid responses were excluded, 296 valid questionnaires remained, yielding a validity rate of 94.3%. The reliability and validity of the measurement instruments were assessed, with all the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients exceeding 0.80. On the basis of these promising results, we proceeded to the second phase: the formal survey. To ensure the statistical validity of the study and the robustness of the moderated mediation model, an a priori sample size calculation was conducted using two complementary approaches. First, utilizing G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) [57], a power analysis for a multiple linear regression model was performed. People with active engagement were the focus of this study. Given that more professionals in first-tier cities in China (Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou) participate in different types of sports, we categorized the types of major exercise of the participants into two categories: aerobic exercise and resistance exercise. The American College of Sports and Medicine (ACSM) [60] and relevant recent literature [15, 61, 62] were used for classification to accurately capture the potential effects of different modalities of exercise. This strategy improved the relevance of the survey to the participants and generated higher interest levels from the target audience in the survey. It was reasoned that the subjects’ exercises involved would be likely to share their thoughts, therefore increasing the value of the data collected. The survey instrument consisted of three distinct sections. Survey respondents were asked for their demographic information, including their city, sex and age. The second section aimed to gather more data related to their exercise attributes, including the exercise they undertook and the exercise intensity they felt. In the last section, the researcher has the measurement items of the study core constructs.

Scales

The study’s measurement instruments assessed four core constructs: work stress, sleep disturbance, anxiety and depression, and physical activity enjoyment. All the scales underwent a rigorous, standardized “translation, back-translation, and expert panel review” procedure to ensure both linguistic and cultural validity. On the basis of this process, all the items were administered in Chinese.

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) [63] was used to measure work stress. This scale moves beyond a simple inventory of objective stressors to assess an individual’s subjective perception of their life as unpredictable, uncontrollable, and overloaded over the previous month. The psychological core of the stress experience is captured exactly; it serves as a link between external demands and internal feelings. The PSS-10 is a suitable instrument for assessing the primary independent variable of the current study, given its excellent reliability and validity and extensive use and validation in occupational health [64]. We tailored the scale’s items to specifically ask about stress perceived in the work setting. An example item is “How often in the past month have you been upset because of something that happened unexpectedly at work?” Responses were given on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree); this scale was found to have good internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.93).

Sleep disturbance was assessed using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) [65]. The ISI is a brief, 7-item self-report questionnaire designed to evaluate the nature, severity, and impact of insomnia symptoms. It is widely used in both clinical practice and research to screen for and quantify the severity of insomnia [66]. A sample item is “To what extent do you consider your sleep problem to interfere with your daily functioning (e.g., daytime fatigue, ability to function at work/daily chores, concentration, memory, mood)?” Items were rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (not at all severe) to 5 (very severe). The internal consistency of the scale was excellent in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.92).

Anxiety and depression were measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [67]. The HADS is a widely validated instrument for assessing the severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms in various populations, including working adults [9, 68]. The questionnaire comprises 14 items divided into two subscales: a 7-item scale for anxiety and a 7-item scale for depression. A sample item from the anxiety subscale is “I feel tense or ‘wound up’.” and the scores for each subscale range from 0 to 21. The established clinical cutoffs categorize scores of 8–10 as mild, 11–14 as moderate, and 15–21 as severe for both anxiety and depression. The total HADS scale demonstrated outstanding internal consistency in the present study (Cronbach’s α = 0.96).

Physical activity enjoyment was measured using the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PACES) [69]. While originally developed for assessing enjoyment in younger populations, the scale has been widely adapted and validated for use with adults [70, 71]. For this study, a validated 4-item short version of the scale was utilized. A sample item is “I find physical activity to be pleasurable”. Items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The internal consistency of the scale was excellent in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.94).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0. To test the hypothesized mediation and moderated mediation effects, the PROCESS macro for SPSS (version 4.2) was employed. Following the recommendations of Hayes [72], PROCESS Model 4 was used to test the simple mediation model, and Model 59 was used to test the moderated mediation model. All the inferential tests were based on 5,000 samples. The 95% bias corrected confidence interval (CI), which is a measure of statistical significance, must not contain zero for an effect to be considered statistically significant [73, 74]. To probe conditional effects, the moderator was tested at three levels: one standard deviation below the mean (M – 1 SD), the mean, and one standard deviation above the mean (M + 1 SD). To summarize the data, descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages and means, were calculated. To analyze the effects of sociodemographic factors and exercise modalities, mean comparison tests were performed. Confirmatory factor analysis was adopted to assess the goodness of fit of the proposed models, using AMOS version 29,0 [75]. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to examine the bivariate relationships between the scale dimensions. Finally, the internal consistency reliability of the scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Furthermore, to ensure the robustness of our findings and explicitly account for the conceptual distinctions between anxiety and depression, sensitivity analyses were conducted by testing the moderated mediation models separately for the anxiety and depression subscales.

Results

Sample characteristics and descriptive statistics

The sample consisted of 809 male (52.3%) and 737 female (47.7%) participants. The age distribution was concentrated among respondents aged 31–40 years (41.0%), followed by those aged 41–55 years (37.6%). A majority of the participants had completed a college or undergraduate degree (52.1%). In terms of monthly income, 50.6% reported earning between 5,000 and 10,000 RMB, while 27.5% reported earning between 10,000 and 20,000 RMB. Notably, there was considerable diversity in both the choice and intensity of physical activity. Among those engaging in aerobic exercise, spinning was the most popular activity in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, particularly among younger participants (63.0%). This was followed by walking (34.1%) and yoga (33.8%). Cycling was also a preference of a significant portion (23.4%), reflecting the growing trend of nighttime riding. With respect to resistance training, bodyweight exercises were the most common (85.3%), followed by machine-based training (63.0%). Free weights (57.9%) and elastic resistance bands (46.9%) were also popular choices. In terms of exercise patterns, a weekly frequency of 2–3 sessions was the most reported (40.0%), and the majority of participants exercised for a duration of 30 to 60 min per session (49.2%). These diverse and realistic engagement patterns suggest that this sample was well suited for this investigation and possesses a high degree of ecological validity (see Table 1).

Additionally, Table 1 presents a comparison of mean scores for the core study variables across participant demographic characteristics. For work stress, the highest mean scores were observed among participants who exercised for more than two hours per session (M = 3.59, SD = 0.95), followed by those who exercised for one to two hours (M = 3.48, SD = 0.97). Compared with males, females reported higher levels of work stress (M = 3.45, SD = 0.96) (M = 3.39, SD = 0.98), and individuals over the age of 40 reported higher levels of stress than their younger counterparts. High stress scores were also concentrated among those with a college degree or undergraduate degree. With respect to exercise choice, the highest levels of work stress were reported by those who engaged in swimming (M = 3.44, SD = 0.98) and machine-based resistance training (M = 3.44, SD = 0.96). In terms of sleep disturbance, the highest scores were reported for the group exercising for more than two hours (M = 3.48, SD = 1.04), followed by individuals with a monthly income exceeding 20,000 RMB (M = 3.44, SD = 1.03). Among specific activities, participants who chose dancing reported notably higher sleep disturbance scores (M = 3.40, SD = 1.01) than those who used machine-based training did (M = 3.37, SD = 1.03). With respect to anxiety and depression, the highest scores were reported by participants who exercised for more than two hours per session (M = 3.05, SD = 0.81), followed by those in the 41–55 age group (M = 2.95, SD = 0.84). The scores were largely consistent across different exercise types, with the highest levels concentrated among participants in dancing, spinning, and machine-based training (mean scores approximately 2.92). Finally, for physical activity enjoyment, the highest levels were reported by individuals who exercised six or more times per week (M = 5.05, SD = 1.67), followed by those whose sessions lasted less than 30 min (M = 5.03, SD = 1.63). Among aerobic activities, spinning was associated with markedly greater enjoyment (M = 4.99, SD = 1.65) than other options. In contrast, the enjoyment levels for resistance training were highly consistent, with mean scores ranging between 4.93 and 4.95. Furthermore, a cross-variable comparison revealed several overarching patterns. A consistent trend emerged for the adverse outcome variables: females, individuals aged 41–55 years, those with a college/undergraduate degree, and participants who exercised for more than two hours per session consistently reported the highest mean scores on work stress, sleep disturbance, and anxiety and depression. Interestingly, the patterns for two of these groups—females and individuals with a college/undergraduate degree—were consistent across all four constructs, as they reported high scores not only for the three negative outcomes but also for physical activity enjoyment. In contrast, the other demographic groups exhibited divergent trends. For instance, participants aged 31–40 years and those with a monthly income between 10,000 and 20,000 RMB reported higher levels of physical activity enjoyment than their respective counterparts in other age brackets and income brackets did.

The descriptive statistical analysis in this study provides a realistic portrait of the mental health and exercise behaviors of professionals in China’s first-tier cities. Through preliminary mean comparisons across multiple dimensions, our findings reveal the complex distribution of the core variables among different sociodemographic and behavioral subgroups. Notably, the data reveal a compelling issue: individuals who might be considered highly active—specifically, females, those in the 41–55 age range, and those exercising for more than two hours per session—also reported higher average scores for work stress, sleep disturbance, and anxiety and depression. These findings suggest that the benefits of exercise duration do not follow a simple “more is better” paradigm; rather, the relationship appears to be more nuanced. This implies that the protective effects of physical activity may not be determined solely by quantitative metrics. Instead, they may be significantly influenced by qualitative dimensions, thereby providing preliminary, contextual evidence for the subsequent examination of physical activity enjoyment as a key moderator.

Second, the results of the analysis revealed that work stress was significantly positively associated with sleep disturbance (β = 0.431, p < 0.001), and sleep disturbance, in turn, was positively associated with anxiety and depression (β = 0.126, p < 0.001). From a psychological perspective, excessive occupational stress can trap individuals in a ruminative thought pattern focused on work-related problems, leading to a prolonged state of cognitive and physiological hyperarousal. This persistent state of arousal conflicts with the biopsychosocial relaxation necessary for sleep onset, thus acting as a primary trigger for sleep disturbance. In turn, sleep disturbance impairs emotional regulation capabilities, and such dysregulation is a key factor in the development of anxiety and depressive symptoms. Therefore, H2 and H3 were supported. More importantly, the bootstrap analysis confirmed that sleep disturbance played a significant mediating role in the relationships between work stress and anxiety and depression. The indirect effect was statistically significant (β = 0.071, 95% CI [0.049, 0.094]), thus supporting H4. These results indicate that a portion of the adverse association between work stress and negative emotional outcomes is not direct; rather, it is indirectly channeled through the fundamental pathway of compromised sleep quality. In this model, sleep disturbance emerges as a core pathway through which stress is linked to anxiety and depression.

The core objective of this study was to test whether physical activity enjoyment could effectively buffer the adverse associations of work stress with anxiety and depression and with sleep disturbance, thereby revealing its multipathway protective mechanism within the stress–health model. First, the results revealed that physical activity enjoyment significantly moderated the relationship between work stress and anxiety and depression (β = -0.166, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.197, -0.135]). An analysis of the interaction effect (Fig. 2) revealed that for individuals with low physical activity enjoyment, the positive association between work stress and anxiety and depression was particularly strong (βsimple = 0.464, p < 0.001; 95% CI [0.386, 0.541]). In contrast, for individuals with high physical activity enjoyment, this association, while still significant, was substantially attenuated (βsimple = -0.095, p < 0.01, 95% CI [-0.156, -0.035]). These findings indicate that the enjoyment of physical activity is more than mere physical exertion; it acts as a form of psychological immunization. Providing positive emotional experiences and a sense of accomplishment effectively counteracts the psychological depletion caused by work stress, helping individuals maintain emotional stability. Therefore, H5a was supported.

Second, physical activity enjoyment significantly moderated the association between work stress and sleep disturbance (β = -0.081, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.114, -0.047]). The interaction plot (Fig. 3) indicated that while sleep may be compromised for everyone under high-stress conditions, the strength of the association between work stress and sleep disturbance was significantly weaker for individuals with high physical activity enjoyment (βsimple = 0.295, p < 0.001; 95% CI [0.223, 0.366]). In contrast, for those with low physical activity enjoyment, the association was much stronger (βsimple = 0.567, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.485, 0.648]). This may be because enjoying physical activity facilitates psychological detachment from work, allowing individuals to fully immerse themselves in the pleasant sensations of exercise. This process could interrupt the persistent cognitive and emotional impact of work stress, effectively lowering hyperarousal and creating the preconditions for quality sleep. These findings support H5b.

Finally, even when an individual’s sleep was already compromised, physical activity enjoyment significantly attenuated the association between sleep disturbance and anxiety and depression (β = -0.031, p < 0.05). As illustrated in the interaction plot (Fig. 4), as the severity of sleep disturbance increased, the corresponding increase in anxiety and depression was significantly smaller for individuals with high physical activity enjoyment (βsimple = 0.074, p < 0.05, 95% CI [0.018, 0.130]) than for those with low physical activity enjoyment (βsimple = 0.179, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.121, 0.236]). This is a particularly crucial finding. It suggests that physical activity enjoyment functions as an effective emotion regulation strategy, counteracting the negative mood states induced by sleep deprivation through mechanisms such as boosting positive affect and enhancing self-efficacy. Doing so helps to disrupt the vicious cycle from sleep problems to anxiety and depression. Therefore, H5c was supported.

Having established support for the individual moderation effects, a subsequent analysis was conducted to test the overall moderated mediation model. The objective was to determine whether physical activity enjoyment moderates the entire indirect effect of work stress on anxiety and depression through the mechanism of sleep disturbance. In other words, this analysis examined whether physical activity enjoyment could weaken the negative pathway linking work stress, sleep disturbance, anxiety and depression as a whole. As shown in Table 4, the strength of this indirect effect varied significantly across levels of physical activity enjoyment. Specifically, for the low physical activity enjoyment group, the indirect association of work stress with anxiety and depression due to sleep disturbance was the strongest (indirect effect β = 0.101, 95% CI [0.079, 0.126]). In contrast, for the high physical activity enjoyment group, the magnitude of this indirect effect was reduced to its lowest point (indirect effect β = 0.022, 95% CI [0.002, 0.044]). Table 4 also presents pairwise comparisons of these conditional indirect effects. The results indicated that the indirect effect at a moderate level of physical activity enjoyment was significantly weaker than the effect at a low level (contrast = -0.047, 95% CI [-0.067, -0.028]). Furthermore, the indirect effect at a high level of physical activity enjoyment was not only significantly weaker than the effect at a low level (contrast = -0.079, 95% CI [-0.108, -0.051]) but also significantly weaker than the effect at a moderate level (contrast = -0.033, 95% CI [-0.042, -0.023]). Therefore, H6 was supported.

Sensitivity Analyses: Given the distinct conceptual and clinical characteristics of anxiety and depression, we conducted sensitivity analyses by re-estimating the moderated mediation models with anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D) as separate outcome variables. The results demonstrated that the overarching pattern of significance remained highly robust and consistent with the combined model. Specifically, physical activity enjoyment significantly moderated the pathways linking work stress, sleep disturbance, to both anxiety and depression individually, with only marginal variations in the magnitude of the coefficients. To maintain model parsimony and focus on the overarching mechanism of general psychological distress in the main text, the detailed results of these separate models are provided in Supplementary Appendix.

Taken together, the findings of this study move beyond an analysis of discrete pathways to reveal the value of physical activity enjoyment from a more holistic and systematic perspective. Such enjoyment not only functions as a buffer at individual nodes within the model but also mitigates the entire core negative cascade from work stress to anxiety and depression. These findings suggest that enjoyable physical activity is not merely a simple coping strategy but rather a protective resource capable of reshaping the dynamic relationship between stress and health. When individuals derive intrinsic satisfaction from exercise, this experience can effectively buffer the adverse impact of work stress on emotional well-being, particularly the portion that is channeled through sleep-related problems.

Discussion

This study was designed to address the increasingly severe mental health challenges faced by professionals in China’s first-tier cities. The central objective was to elucidate the complex psychological pathways through which work stress is linked to anxiety and depression and to explore whether physical activity enjoyment can function as a protective resource for this process. The results provide clear empirical support for the proposed moderated mediation model. Work stress was not only directly positively associated with anxiety and depression but also indirectly associated with these outcomes via the mediating role of sleep disturbance. Crucially, the core contribution of this research lies in demonstrating the key moderating role that physical activity enjoyment plays in this adverse pathway.

Work stress and the cascade of emotional distress

The initial findings of this study align with the established literature in occupational health psychology. The positive association between work stress and anxiety and depression corroborates conclusions from cross-cultural and cross-occupational research, confirming that adverse work environments are a significant contributor to psychological distress among professionals [7–9, 26]. This relationship, however, presents additional complexity within the context of China’s hypercompetitive professional landscape. Our study revealed that individuals who engaged in excessively long exercise sessions reported higher, not lower, levels of stress. This suggests that when the nature of exercise shifts from leisure to being task oriented, its positive psychological effects may be diminished. In high-pressure work environments, individuals may alienate physical activity from a form of self-care and leisure, transforming it into another obligatory, performance-driven task that must be completed. This phenomenon, which can be described as a compensatory‒compulsive exercise [80, 81], extends the traditional boundaries of the ERI model. While use of the ERI model has historically focused on the occupational domain, our findings imply that this imbalanced psychological pattern can spill over into an individual’s nonwork domains, particularly their exercise habits.

Similarly, our finding of a positive association between work stress and sleep disturbance is well supported by a broad body of research demonstrating that chronic occupational stress can significantly contribute to insomnia through mechanisms such as hyperarousal [13, 31, 34]. These results prompt a reexamination of the high levels of sleep disturbance reported by the subgroup engaging in prolonged single-session exercise. This observation suggests that when physical activity is undertaken in a high-stress context and lacks inherent relaxing or restorative qualities, it may paradoxically become a new source of stress. In these cases, exercise may fail to counteract the hyperarousal induced by work pressure and could thereby negatively impact sleep [20]. In conclusion, our findings showed that sleep disturbance was significantly related to anxiety and depression, thus demonstrating the basic importance of sleep as a resource for emotions. Consistent findings across multiple studies suggest that sleep quality deterioration is a significant factor in mood disorders [11, 12, 36, 40]. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of exercise in managing emotions might be limited by sleep quality. When significant sleep disturbance was present, even long-duration exercise failed to effectively inhibit the pathway to anxiety and depression in our sample. This, once again, hints at the critical role of the subjective experience of exercise.

Sleep disturbance as a core mechanism in the work stress pathway

One of the central findings of this study is the significant mediating role of sleep disturbance in the relationship between work stress and anxiety and depression. This result is consistent with discoveries from previous research across diverse occupational groups [11, 12, 35], collectively revealing that the adverse impact of work stress is not always direct. Instead, its negative effects are frequently channeled indirectly through the deterioration of sleep. The core mechanism underlying this pathway is that chronic work stress prevents the body’s biopsychosocial systems from effectively calming down and relaxing. This state allows work-related thoughts to remain persistently active during nonwork hours, thereby interfering with normal sleep onset [34]. However, our findings, situated within the context of the current Chinese professional landscape, add a new layer of complexity to this established pathway. We found that this negative mediating chain persists even among individuals who are actively engaged in specific types of physical activity. This suggests that while different exercise modalities (e.g., aerobic vs. resistance) may offer distinct benefits for emotional well-being [15], mere participation in physical activity, in and of itself, appears insufficient to completely buffer the detrimental association between work stress and sleep disturbance.

Furthermore, a significant advancement of the present study, compared with prior research that often focused on validating this single mediational pathway in more narrowly defined populations [34, 39], lies in its application to a more representative sample. By extending the analysis from specific occupational groups to a broad cross-section of professionals in China’s first-tier cities, our research confirms the model’s applicability across diverse sociocultural and professional contexts. By replicating the pathway through which high-effort, low-reward stress patterns are associated with adverse mental health outcomes in this wider and more diverse sample, our study enhances the external validity and theoretical generalizability of the ERI model.

Physical activity enjoyment: a theoretical extension of the stress-buffering model

By examining the moderating effects of physical activity enjoyment across multiple pathways, this study expands the theoretical boundaries and explanatory power of the SBM.

According to the first study, physical activity enjoyment was found to buffer the relationship between work stress and anxiety and depression. Thus, an enjoyable exercise process can effectively refill the psychological reserves that are emptied when dealing with occupational stress, as it provides intrinsically motivated feelings of pleasure and accomplishment [18]. With such a positive experience, an individual may experience a fundamental shift in mindset, changing from a passive stress sufferer to an active, effective stress cope [19]. These findings provide important information for exercise interventions in stressful working environments in China. People may not benefit much from aerobic exercise that alleviates anxiety and resistance training that reduces depression. For this reason, when sufferers from anxiety and depression does attempt such exercises, they should have a good experience if they are to benefit. That is, we suggest that the pleasantness of the experience of exercising may explain its effect on stress relief. This understanding fine-tunes what can be seen as a no-pain, no-gain task into an enjoyable leisure activity. Exercising, therefore, is a powerful psychological resource.

In addition, physical activity enjoyment may promote exercise adherence and sustainability by weakening the relationship between work stress and sleep disturbance [20]. Notably, mind–body exercises such as yoga and tai chi, which are known for their capacity to bring individuals to focus on the task at hand and are enjoyable in themselves, have already been shown to reduce stress and improve sleep [48, 49]. These findings provide collateral support for our results. This moderating effect is particularly significant in the context of China’s professional landscape, where long working hours and high job demands are prevalent. The state of persistent biopsychosocial tension induced by work stress is a key driver of insomnia [34]. Enjoyable physical activity can help mitigate this tension and promote relaxation, thereby effectively alleviating work-related sleep disturbances. Therefore, our study introduces a crucial boundary condition to the conventional wisdom that exercise improves sleep. While prior research has often emphasized the benefits of long-term, regular exercise [20], our findings reveal that the degree of enjoyment an individual derives from physical activity may be directly related to his or her ability to achieve a state of psychophysiological tranquility conducive to sleep.

Finally, this study is the first to demonstrate that physical activity enjoyment can buffer the subsequent association between sleep disturbance and anxiety and depression. This implies that even when an individual’s sleep is already compromised, an enjoyable exercise experience still appears to exert a psychological buffering effect, helping to attenuate the spillover from sleep problems to negative emotional states. The underlying mechanism may be that physical activity itself is an independent antianxiety and antidepressant intervention [45, 50]. Individuals who enjoy exercise are likely more inclined to use it as a proactive coping strategy to counteract the negative moods and energy depletion that result from insufficient sleep [82]. In the Chinese professional context, when employees face exhaustion and low spirits due to poor sleep, enjoyable physical activity can provide immediate positive affective feedback and a sense of accomplishment, which can effectively combat the negative mood states exacerbated by sleep problems. This discovery offers a new perspective on SBMs. This reveals a moderating mechanism that operates at a late stage of the stress process. Whereas previous models have often focused on how resources help individuals cope with an initial stressor, our research indicates that high-quality psychological experience can effectively mitigate the adverse consequences stemming from an intermediary factor, such as sleep disturbance.

This study is the first to include physical activity enjoyment, viewed as a dynamic, actively cultivable subjective experience, within this framework. Thus, in creating the model, we have moved our theory from a static conception of having to understanding the process of experiencing. The model we developed has more rich and encompassing content. This extension is consistent with emerging ideas in exercise science, through the empirical validation of the relationship between a stressor (work stress), a mediator (sleep disturbance), and a moderator (physical activity enjoyment) on a health outcome (anxiety and depression), this study not only contributes to the theoretical richness of the model but also helps pave the way toward more humanistic workplace mental health interventions.

Conclusion

Drawing upon the theoretical foundations of the Effort–Reward Imbalance (ERI) and the Stress-Buffering Models (SBM), this study investigated the associations of work stress with sleep disturbance and anxiety and depression. We further examined the moderated mediating role of physical activity enjoyment in the relationship between work stress and anxiety and depression via sleep disturbance. The results demonstrated that this moderating effect significantly attenuated the strength of the entire indirect pathway, confirming that the critical protective value of physical activity enjoyment in mitigating the process through which work stress is linked to anxiety and depression. Consequently, this research makes important contributions to both theory and practice.

Theoretical contributions

First, this study moves beyond the generalized approach often used in the social science of sport literature concerning the mental health benefits of exercise. By focusing specifically on aerobic and resistance training in a more representative sample of general professionals in China’s first-tier cities, we dissected the adverse cascade linking work stress, sleep disturbance, and anxiety and depression. The core innovation of this research lies in its shift beyond the physical attributes of exercise to a deeper investigation of its qualitative dimensions. We demonstrated that the positive psychological experience derived from exercise—conceptualized as physical activity enjoyment—is a critical variable that can effectively intervene in the aforementioned stress process. This not only offers a more granular understanding of the psychological protection mechanisms of exercise but also fills a theoretical void in the literature concerning the role of the psychological attributes of exercise within specific stress–health models.

Second, in terms of theory-building, this study innovatively extends two foundational models in occupational health psychology: the Effort–Reward Imbalance (ERI) model and the Stress-Buffering Model (SBM). Within a unified empirical framework, our results demonstrate that the adverse effects of stress, as articulated by the ERI model, can be effectively mitigated by the protective resources described in the SBM. More groundbreaking is our study’s introduction of physical activity enjoyment—a dynamic, actively cultivable subjective experience—as a core moderating variable within the SBM framework. This is the first study to incorporate this. This conceptual move propels the focus of the theory beyond relatively static resources, such as social support, toward an exploration of dynamic, process-oriented positive psychological experiences, thereby greatly enriching its theoretical substance and practical applicability. These findings open a new perspective for future interventions, suggesting a shift from merely identifying external risks to enhancing the internal experiences that build individual psychological resilience. In doing so, it lays a solid foundation for constructing more agentic and empowering workplace mental health support systems.

Practical implications

The practical implications of this study are clearly targeted, aiming to provide evidence-based intervention strategies for urban professionals facing high-stress work environments, as well as for the health service and management systems that support them. First, for individuals, the key takeaway is the need to redefine the goal of exercise: shifting the focus from completing a health-related task to discovering a source of intrinsic enjoyment. We encourage professionals to proactively explore a variety of physical activities. Whether it is a team-based sport or a spinning or dance class that combines music and social interaction, the primary criterion for selection should be “Do I enjoy this process?” rather than simply “How many calories am I burning?” Correspondingly, this places new demands on physical activity service providers, such as gyms, personal trainers, and online platforms. Their product design and instructional strategies must move beyond performance metrics to focus more on cultivating a positive and supportive social atmosphere, enhancing the enjoyability of their programs, and helping users discover and sustain their passion for movement through personalized plans. By doing so, they can guide users in making a profound shift from passive fitness to proactive well-being.

Second, for organizations and public health bodies, our findings highlight the need to develop a broader, synergistic support system. For corporations, employee wellness programs should evolve beyond traditional offerings such as gym memberships. Instead, they should focus on organizing more diverse and inclusive team-based physical activities, such as outdoor hikes or recreational sports days. Moreover, companies should actively cultivate a workplace culture that encourages employees to take restorative breaks and psychologically detach themselves from work. Promoting employees’ enjoyment of physical activity should be viewed not as a perk but as a strategic investment in organizational psychological capital and productivity. For municipal public health and sports authorities, this study offers a new direction for policy-making. Public health campaigns should shift their messaging from a singular emphasis on the functional benefits of exercise to a dual focus that also champions its enjoyable aspects, thereby shaping a more positive societal conception of physical activity. More importantly, urban planning and development initiatives should prioritize the creation of highly accessible, aesthetically pleasing, and multifunctional recreational spaces. Doing so would provide citizens with both the robust physical infrastructure and the social support network necessary to truly enjoy being active.

Limitations and avenues for future research

Several limitations of the current study must be acknowledged. First, our use of a cross-sectional design inherently constrains our ability to draw causal inferences among the variables, as all the data were collected at a single point in time. Although our model is grounded in established theory, the correlational nature of the data cannot rule out the possibility of reverse causality or bidirectional relationships. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as significant associations rather than confirmed causal links. The pathways specified in our model represent a theoretically plausible relational structure that requires further validation through more rigorous research designs. Second, the study sample was limited to professionals in China’s first-tier cities, and all the data were based on self-report measures. These factors may limit the generalizability of our conclusions and could introduce reporting biases. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extending these findings to broader or different populations. Finally, our measurement of the core construct of physical activity enjoyment was relatively broad. This approach may not have fully captured the nuanced effects of its specific subdimensions, such as social enjoyment versus immersive flow experiences. It is possible that these distinct facets of enjoyment play different buffering roles, a possibility that warrants further investigation.

Indeed, a key finding from our descriptive statistics, namely, that longer exercise durations were paradoxically associated with poorer mental health outcomes serves as the primary impetus for both the present study and future explorations. This observation highlights the limitations of assessing psychological benefits based solely on physical metrics and provides a strong theoretical rationale for a more nuanced future research agenda. Therefore, we recommend that future studies employ longitudinal designs or randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to more clearly delineate the temporal sequencing and causal pathways among work stress, sleep, physical activity enjoyment, and mental health. To enhance external validity, subsequent research should broaden its sampling to include different city tiers and occupational groups while also incorporating objective measurement tools, such as data from wearable devices or physiological indicators, to validate and supplement self-report information. Furthermore, future inquiries could deconstruct the core construct of enjoyment to examine the differential effects of its subdimensions in buffering the associations between work stress and the outcome variables (sleep disturbance and anxiety and depression). Finally, to bridge the gap between research and practice, we advocate for action research or field experiments conducted in real-world workplace settings to evaluate the efficacy and challenges of corporate wellness programs designed to promote physical activity enjoyment. Such research must include careful ethical consideration: when enjoyable exercise is institutionally promoted, could it be unintentionally alienated into a new form of performance pressure? Attending to these real-world complexities will significantly increase the societal value and applied depth of future research in this area.
