Changes in body composition and low-grade inflammation status in boys during a basketball afterschool program for three years
Authors: Cristina Castro-Collado, Jose Manuel Jurado-Castro, Mercedes Gil-Campos, María Cruz Rico, Belén Pastor-Villaescusa, Gracia María Quintana-Navarro, Francisco Jesús Llorente-Cantarero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Regular exercise during childhood is crucial for optimal growth. However, evidence on its long-term effects on physical fitness, inflammation and metabolic status remains limited for this period of life. To assess the longitudinal effects of a three-year basketball program on physical fitness, body composition, metabolic and inflammatory markers in prepubertal boys. Seventeen boys (8–13 years) participated in an after-school basketball program with 96 weeks of training across three academic years. At baseline and follow-up, assessments included anthropometry, body composition measured by bioimpedance, physical fitness using cardiorespiratory and strength tests, and fasting blood biomarkers. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT07007624; 06/06/2025). At the end of the program, body fat remained stable, while fat free mass increased in upper and lower limbs (e.g., + 1.5 kg in lower limbs, p < 0.05). Physical fitness improved in endurance (Course Navette: +4.2 min, p < 0.01) and strength tests. Inflammatory markers showed elevations in PAI-1 (+ 86%, p < 0.05), MCP-1 (+ 23%, p < 0.01), IL-8 (+ 400%, p < 0.001), and TNF-α (+ 98%, p < 0.05), possibly reflecting increased training intensity. Long-term basketball training improved physical fitness and lean mass in prepubertal boys. However, the small sample size and absence of a control group warrant cautious interpretation and underscore the need for further research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22628-5.

Introduction

Engagement in exercise during childhood is crucial for both children’s physical and mental development throughout life. In this context, regular participation in sports have been proposed as an effective approach1. However, the effects of exercise on specific health outcomes in children, particularly concerning inflammation and metabolism, remains unclear.

During childhood, important somato-functional changes occur that shape both the physical performance and the response to training2. This developmental stage is characterized by continuous growth in height and body mass, progressive increases in muscle mass and strength, and improvements in neuromuscular coordination and motor skills. Furthermore, the maturation of metabolic and hormonal systems is still ongoing, affecting recovery capacity and susceptibility to fatigue or overtraining3. These particularities make this age range especially relevant for studying the effects of structured exercise interventions such as basketball, where training loads must be adapted to ensure both safety and optimal development.

Children’s participation in vigorous physical activities significantly improves metabolic health. This type of exercise helps regulate blood glucose levels, enhances insulin sensitivity4, and improves the lipid profile5. These benefits may persist into adulthood, potentially reducing the risk of cardiovascular diseases, strokes6, type 2 diabetes7, and certain cancers8. Conversely, vigorous activity can temporarily elevate inflammatory markers, and when excessive, may lead to overtraining and contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation. Low-grade inflammatory status may be particularly harmful in children9. It can interfere with normal growth and development and alter immune function8. Furthermore, chronic inflammation may disrupt metabolism, predisposing children to conditions such as obesity and insulin resistance10.

Few studies have analyzed the effects of physical activity (PA) on inflammation, with some results suggesting health benefits. Overall, PA contributes to reduce the low-grade inflammatory status, and to enhance immune and metabolic functions10. Moreover, activities that involve intermittent bouts of vigorous intensity, such as running and jumping, have positive effects on children’s health5. In particular, basketball stands out as a highly comprehensive sport for achieving these goals, as it provides a vigorous cardiovascular activity11. Incorporating basketball into children’s routines can therefore represent an effective strategy for preventing inflammation and promoting overall health from an early age12. Regular exercise with well-controlled intensity levels have been linked to a reduction of inflammatory parameters, through the release of cytokines1,13. The relationship between some inflammatory parameters, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and the effects of overtraining has gained increasing attention in sports performance and health14. This link has been also studied in the context of basketball training8. Children who engage in vigorous exercise may experience temporary elevations in inflammatory markers as part of the body’s adaptation to training1. Likewise, overtraining can result in a low-grade chronic inflammatory response, which adversely impacts athletic performance and increases the risk of injuries15. This chronic inflammation may stem from the prolonged physical and emotional stress experienced during intense training sessions. This state is coupled with repetitive musculoskeletal injuries commonly associated with overtraining16. Skeletal muscle, as a metabolically active tissue, plays a central role in this interplay17.

Therefore, it is crucial to address and prevent inflammation from an early age through interventions that promote regular PA with healthy lifestyle habits10. Preventing inflammation in children serves as a key investment in their future well-being, reducing the risk of diseases and enhancing their quality of life8. In this context, basketball may be a valuable sport for supporting health during childhood. This study aims to assess the effects of basketball on body composition, physical fitness, metabolism, and inflammatory markers in a cohort of healthy children.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A prospective, non-randomised controlled intervention study with repeated measures was conducted. The protocol was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07007624; 06/06/2025). The study was designed and reported following the TREND statement guidelines18.

An after-school basketball program was offered to scholars with a middle socio-educational class in Córdoba (Spain). Boys participated in the youth basketball category and were evaluated twice a year: in September, when the program started, and in June, at the end of the school year. These evaluations took place in the first and third year of the intervention.

The inclusion criteria were boys aged 8 to 13 years, at Tanner stage I (prepuberal)19, and in good health. All participants were registered in a federation, belonging to the same category established by the Spanish federation for playing basketball. Exclusion criteria included the presence of any clinical or analytical signs of puberty, disease, or the use of medication that alters blood pressure (BP), glucose or lipid metabolism. Seventeen boys met these criteria and were therefore included in the study.

The study was designed following the ethical principles for human research of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by the local ethics and research committee in the Reina Sofia University Hospital (Code: 1866). All participants and their families were informed about the study and voluntarily agreed to participate. Informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardian of each child.

Intervention

The intervention design was planned for three school years, with the same structure applied to each academic year. During the first two years, the basketball-based program consisted of two phases: a six-week pre-season stage at the beginning of the academic year, followed by a 32-week in-season phase that continued until the end of the school year. During the pre-season, participants engaged in five training sessions per week on weekdays, each lasting two hours, with an emphasis on developing dribbling, defensive, and shooting techniques. The in-season phase comprised three two-hour training sessions per week, scheduled after school hours, along with a competitive game each weekend. Each session was divided into two periods, separated by a 10-min break: the first period focused on individual skills with moderate to high-intensity (60–85% of HR) exercises, and the second period focused on game tactics and strategy. The trainers had high coaching qualifications, ensuring the quality of the sessions. In the third year, participants trained twice a week, with each session extended to three hours. They spent the first two hours on their regular training, as in previous years. However, for the last hour, they trained with teammates from a higher category, resulting in higher training intensity compared to the previous years. The subjects also played a match each weekend. A total of 32 weeks of intervention were conducted each year, amounting to 96 weeks of training in total.

Outcomes

Related to evaluations, baseline measurements were conducted at the beginning of the first academic year in September (B1), with a follow-up measurement at the end of the academic year in June (B2). A final evaluation was conducted in the third academic year, following the same schedule as in the first course in September (F1) and June (F2). No measurements were conducted in the second year. This decision allowed us to focus on long-term effects, reduce participant burden, and minimize variability associated with repeated testing, thereby strengthening the validity of comparisons between baseline and follow-up. In addition, this approach enabled us to specifically capture the impact of the intensified training implemented in the third year. Consequently, assessments were carried out only in the first and third years, beginning and the end of each academic period. (Fig. 1).

Clinical examination

A comprehensive clinical evaluation was conducted by trained healthcare professionals. The assessment included a structured anamnesis to gather relevant medical history, family history of metabolic or cardiovascular diseases, current health status and medication use. Anamnesis and physical examination, including the evaluation of sexual maturity according to Tanner’s five-stage scale, were assessed. In all cases, this evaluation was conducted by a paediatric endocrinologist or trained clinician, in a private setting to ensure confidentiality and minimize discomfort. For boys, genital development and pubic hair distribution were assessed, following the guidelines established by Tanner20.

Body composition

Body weight and height were measured using standard techniques, a beam balance and a precision stadiometer SECA 213 (Scale 20–205 cm; SECA), with the participants lightly dressed and barefoot. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m2) and compared to growth charts for Spanish children21. Body composition components such as fat free mass and fat mass (kg and %) were obtained by bioimpedance analysis using a Tanita BC 418 MA Segmental Body Composition Analyser® (Tokyo, Japan).

Blood pressure

Systolic and diastolic blood pressure (BP, mmHg), along with heart rate (HR, lpm), were recorded from the right arm while participants were seated. Measurements were taken using a random-zero sphygmomanometer (Dinamap V-100) after the individual had remained at rest in the same position for a minimum of five minutes.

Blood sampling and inflammatory biomarkers

Blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein between 08:00 and 09:30 h, following a minimum 12-hour overnight fast. The samples were drawn into EDTA-treated tubes, centrifuged shortly after collection, and plasma aliquots were promptly stored at − 80 °C. Hematological and biochemical assessments were conducted in duplicate at the hospital’s clinical laboratory. Blood glucose was assessed using the glucose oxidase method with an automated analyzer (Roche-Hitachi Modular P and D; Roche Laboratory Systems, Mannheim, Germany), achieving a coefficient of variation (CV) of 1.0%. Serum insulin concentrations were measured through radioimmunoassay (RIA) using an automated microparticle analyzer (AxSYM; Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA), with a CV of 2.6%. The homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was used to estimate insulin resistance.

Serum lipid profile components, including triglycerides (CV = 1.5%), total cholesterol (CV = 0.9%), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c) (CV = 0.8%), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) (CV = 1.5%), as well as additional parameters such as leukocyte count, hemoglobin, ferritin, iron, apolipoprotein A and B, and liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT)), were analyzed using the same automated platform. Plasma C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations were determined by turbidimetric latex-enhanced immunoassay on the Architect c16000 autoanalyzer (Abbott Diagnostics®, Abbott Laboratories, Madrid, Spain).

Additional inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers in plasma, including interleukins IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), nerve growth factor (NGF), plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), and adipokines such as leptin, adiponectin, and resistin, were quantified in duplicate using the Luminex 200 system (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA) with XMap technology and monoclonal antibody-based Milliplex assays (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Quality control procedures ensured the reliability of measurements, maintaining intra- and inter-assay CVs below 10%.

Evaluation of cardiorespiratory fitness (course navette test)

Cardiorespiratory fitness was assessed using the 20 m shuttle run (Course Navette) test, which is part of the standardized ALPHA fitness test battery22. In this test, participants ran repeatedly between two lines s20 meters apart, synchronized with audio signals that progressively increased in speed. The test measured the time (minutes) participants were able to maintain the required pace, providing an indicator of their cardiorespiratory endurance.

Evaluation of upper body strength (flexed arm hang test)

Upper body strength was assessed using the Flexed Arm Hang test23. Participants were instructed to maintain an isometric flexed-arm position for as long as possible while hanging from a bar. The total time (seconds) holding this position was recorded as a measure of muscular endurance and strength in the upper body.

Lower body explosive strength (horizontal jump test)

Lower body muscular strength is evaluated through the horizontal jump test24. This assessment required participants to perform a forward jump with feet together while maintaining an upright posture. Participants were instructed to perform three jumps recording the greatest distance achieved. A mark is placed at the take-off line to ensure correct foot positioning (referred to as the standing long jump test). This procedure was maintained throughout subsequent assessments to prevent participants from crossing the starting line, thereby invalidating their attempts and necessitating repetition. Participants were encouraged to exert maximal effort.

Core strength (abdominal test)

Core strength and endurance were assessed using a 30-second sit-up test. Participants assumed a supine position with knees flexed to 90°, feet flat on the floor, and arms crossed over the chest. Upon the examiner’s signal, they executed as many correct sit-ups as possible within 30 s, ensuring that elbows touched the thighs and shoulders returned fully to the mat on each repetition. Improper form or incomplete movements were not counted.

Evaluation of anaerobic capacity (40-meter speed test)

Anaerobic capacity was assessed with the 40-meter speed test25. Participants were required to sprint 40 m as quickly as possible, and the time taken to complete the distance was recorded in seconds. This test provided a measure of short-term, high-intensity performance capacity.

For all physical fitness assessments, a qualified evaluator supervised the tests and provided standardized instructions and encouragement.

Sample size

Since horizontal jump performance was a primary outcome in this study, the required sample size was calculated through a statistical power analysis based on previous findings11 in a comparable male population. Assuming a statistical power of 0.80 and a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, a minimum of 17 participants was estimated to detect an 8% difference in horizontal jump outcomes. A 10% dropout rate was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the data distribution of variablesTo compare physical fitness, anthropometric, biochemical and inflammation variables at different times during the intervention, general linear models for repeated measures were conducted, adjusting for age. The normality, homogeneity and sphericity of the residues were controlled for all analyses. All statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS statistics 22. In all analyses statistical significance was set at a 2-tailed P value < 0.05.

In the upper limbs (Fig. 2C), there was an increase in fat free mass from basal time measurements to the final evaluation, without significant changes between B1 and B2; and F1 compared with F2. In the lower limbs (Fig. 2D), an increase in fat free mass was noted between the B2 and F1, both on the right and left sides maintained at F2 on the right sides compared with basal time (B1) and F2 on the left sides compared with B2.

Significant differences were observed in certain inflammation parameters (Table S3, supplementary material). Total PAI-1 levels showed a significant increase only between baseline and final time point (Fig. 4C). HGF, IL8, NGF and TNF-α showed higher levels in F2 compared with the other time points (Fig. 4). In the variables mentioned, no significant differences were found in intermediate time. IL-6 concentrations was below the limit of detection in over half of the B2 samples, and were therefore excluded from the analysis. At F1, IL-6 levels were significantly higher compared with basal time (Fig. 4A). MCP-1 concentrations increased from F1 to F2, and both values were elevated compared with B2 (Fig. 4B). In resistin, there was a global difference (p = 0.024), although only a significant result was observed between B2 and F2 (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this basketball three-year training intervention in prepubertal boys, participants had an appropriate growth without changes in BMI, maintaining a normal weight status. There was a progressive improvement in body composition and physical fitness. However, an increase in some inflammation biomarkers was observed, especially in the third year.

The changes in fat free mass observed in this study, particularly the increases in upper and lower limbs, are consistent with previous reports showing that systematic PA during childhood favors lean mass accretion, especially in weight-bearing and skill-based sports26. For example, studies on youth soccer and swimming conducted on male children aged between 10 and 17 years have reported similar improvements in appendicular lean mass without significant reductions in overall body fat percentage, aligning with the present results27. Regarding physical fitness, the improvements in cardiorespiratory capacity and muscular endurance are in line with previous team-sport interventions, where regular participation enhanced aerobic performance and motor skills in prepubertal populations on a study conducted on male children aged between 11 and 17 years28.

It is known that exercise increases total energy expenditure and resting metabolic rate, which may favor lipid oxidation and reduce body fat29. A systematic review demonstrated that both reductions in visceral fat mass and the anti-inflammatory environment induced by exercise produce long-term anti-inflammatory health benefits30. The excess of adiposity is a potential driver of cytokines and adipokines secretion, thereby activating the inflammatory system31. The increase in fat free mass observed during the intervention can improve body composition by reducing fat mass. In these boys, specific compartments, especially the trunk and limbs, showed increases in fat free mass during the basketball training from baseline to the third year.

Furthermore, physical fitness improved throughout the training, and participants maintained a good fitness level even after spending the summer with an expected lower intensity and frequency of PA. Consistent enhancements were observed in the Course Navette test and in anaerobic assessments, such as the abdominal test and the speed run, indicating the effectiveness of basketball training. The best results emerged in the final year, with a higher training intensity. However, performance in the flexed arm hang test remained stable, with no differences between the evaluation periods. Likewise, although horizontal jump performance improved, the gains in the second year were not significant. This suggest that the increased training intensity had a minimal effect on this specific measure.

Fitness has been associated with favorable changes in body composition, including reduced adiposity and enhanced aerobic capacity through cardiovascular training32. During exercise, the body releases hormones and signaling molecules that regulate the inflammatory response33. High-intensity training33, particularly through regular exercise, yields beneficial effects on inflammation and immune function34. These benefits include modulation of the inflammatory cascade, contributing to improving vascular health by proper blood circulation, and mitigating blood clot formation35.

In the current study, general biochemical parameters remained within normal ranges during training, with no significant changes. This excluded the presence of relevant pathologies. However, it has been reported that vigorous physical exercise can trigger an inflammatory response in blood, as evidenced by the delayed increase in acute-phase proteins, including CRP. This inflammatory response is attributed to micro-injuries and local inflammation in the muscles induced by vigorous exercise. This phenomenon is particularly prominent in sports disciplines involving significant eccentric forces and muscular microtrauma, such as basketball36. In a study conducted among adult athletes with a high PA level, elevated values of circulating markers of vascular inflammation were detected, suggesting a higher risk to develop cardiovascular diseases35. In the present study, an increase in some inflammatory molecules was observed in the third year, maybe due to the higher intensity and duration of training in the last period.

When overtraining occurs, skeletal muscles release a wide array of molecules known as myokines in response to contraction14,37. These myokines exert effects both locally, within the muscle itself, and distally, affecting organs such as the liver, pancreas, and adipose tissue15. This complex signaling network orchestrated by myokines serves to modulate various physiological processes, including inflammation17. However, when the balance between exercise intensity, duration, and recovery is disrupted due to overtraining, it leads to a dysregulation of the inflammatory process17. Prolonged overtraining stressed the body, triggering chronic inflammation that can compromise tissue repair and immune function, and increase susceptibility to injuries15.

It has been suggested that systemic inflammation could be the central factor in overtraining syndrome, similarly to burnout. According to this hypothesis, musculoskeletal trauma triggers local inflammatory responses. Prolonged intense training might generate chronic and systemic inflammation, contributing to the performance deterioration, inadequate rest and recovery associated with overtraining38,39. This status may be associated with the activation of circulating monocytes which have the capacity to produce significant amounts of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α38.

Furthermore, recent studies highlight the close relationship between biomarkers such as IL-6 and TNF-α with PA40. During acute exercise, muscular contraction results in increases in circulating IL-6 levels, up to more than 100 times during intense and prolonged exercises14. This notable elevation of IL-6 can shift inflammatory pathways and induce IL-10, a potent anti-inflammatory molecule. IL-10 may inhibit the synthesis of various proinflammatory mediators, such as IL-1α, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-8, and macrophage inflammatory protein-1α (MIP-1α)41. In a study conducted on healthy children aged 9 to 11 years, comprising both a control group and a training group, an enhancement in physical fitness was noted among the training group. However, an increase of TNF-α levels was observed, associated with the elevation of proinflammatory cytokines (such as IL-&) and linked to an increased exercise42. In two studies conducted in youths, an increase in IL-6 was observed following intense exercise15,43. However, if this practice is pursued excessively, it can have the opposite effect on inflammatory markers15.

Moreover, in children, the exercise-induced inflammatory response is lower than in adults due to adaptive responses to exercise resulting from childhood growth. However, there is scarce literature regarding the inflammatory response after exercise in this population17. Children exhibit more pronounced changes in immune mediators after exercise compared to adults under similar exercise conditions. While TNF-α may play a critical role in regulating the inflammatory response, its excessive activity could detrimentally impact growth44. Conversely, the reduced IL-6 response to exercise in children correlates with decreased neutrophil mobilizations44,45. Moreover, several studies have reported higher post-exercise levels of growth hormone in children compared to adults46,47. Additionally, elevated IL-6 levels following exercise in children compared to adults have been reported44. This indicates distinct immune and endocrine responses that could influence developmental outcomes and overall health in young individuals.

To achieve benefits of some sports as basketball in inflammation status, the intensity of the exercise needs to be in a moderate-to-vigorous level, aiming for at least 70% of maximum aerobic capacity14. Basketball inherently involves intermittent bursts of high-intensity activities like sprinting, jumping, and quick direction changes. Therefore, it is expected to achieve health benefits such as the decrease in inflammatory markers with a regular practice48.

Currently, normal reference ranges of cytokines are not available for these age groups. Therefore, we compared the time periods, considering that at baseline, the intervention group had anthropometry and basic metabolic data similar to those of a healthy reference group49. In this sense, clinical relevance cannot be determined through these results, as they have not been associated with any other metabolic or clinical data indicating inflammatory or cardiovascular damage. They could only provide an approximation to a higher risk compared to previous data.

The fact that the sample was small limited the evaluation of potential differences in inflammation considering sex or age groups. Moreover, the participation was voluntary, introducing the possibility of selection bias. Furthermore, the absence of a control group makes difficult to isolate the effects of the intervention from other factors. Nevertheless, the intervention group was compared at baseline with a reference healthy group in terms of anthropometry and basic metabolic parameters to confirm overall comparability49. However, due to ethical considerations, blood sampling could not be performed at every timepoint, and therefore a direct comparison of inflammatory biomarkers with the healthy reference group was not feasible. Although a strength is that In this study, a federated basketball team was necessary to implement a targeted and personalized intervention, ensuring homogeneous training conditions for all participants. Consequently, the intervention was conducted by experienced coaches with this specific cohort, conformed by a relatively small sample of prepubertal boys.

Conclusion

Long-term basketball training in prepubertal boys appears to promote favorable changes in body composition and fitness. Nevertheless, the rise in inflammatory markers observed during the third year highlights the importance of balancing training intensity with adequate recovery. Coaches and parents should carefully monitor load to maximize benefits while minimizing the risk of maladaptation. Future studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are needed to confirm these findings and to examine sex- and maturation-related differences, as well as the potential health consequences of overtraining and low-grade inflammation.
