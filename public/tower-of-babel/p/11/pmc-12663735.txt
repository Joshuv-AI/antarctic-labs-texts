Phenotype specific nuclear lamina remodeling in hiPSC derived cardiomyocytes bearing TNNT2 sarcomeric variants
Authors: Isabella Leite Coscarella, Olalekan H Usman, Lili Wang, Maicon Landim-Vieira, Lillian Wirstiuk, Amber N Brown, Diego AR Zorio, Brian K Washburn, Cynthia Vied, Bjorn C Knollmann, Christopher Solís, Vitold E Galkin, P Bryant Chase, J Travis Hinson, Jerome Irianto, Jose Renato Pinto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCardiomyocytes endure physical stress from the myocardium environment while generating their own mechanical strains. The force generated by sarcomeres is transmitted both longitudinally to adjacent sarcomeres and laterally to the cytoskeleton via intermediate filaments. This mechanical stimulus impacts other organelles, including the nucleus, thus playing a vital role in sensing and signaling nuclear adaptations. However, there is limited understanding of how changes in cardiac contractility affect nuclear mechanics. Here, we sought to investigate the effects of hyper- and hypo-contractility in nuclei of human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) bearing TNNT2 pathogenic variants associated with hypertrophic (HCM) or dilated (DCM) cardiomyopathies. Transcriptomics analyses of these variant bearing hiPSC-CMs confirmed that differential gene expression occurs and is associated with maladaptive and compensatory responses in HCM and DCM. Our findings show a cause-and-effect link between impaired contractility and nuclear lamina remodeling in cardiomyopathic phenotypes. Disease-induced dysfunctional contractile transients alter the expression of nucleoskeleton protein lamin A/C, influencing nuclear stiffness. These changes in stiffness were rescued by treatment with myosin modulators Mavacamten or Omecamtiv Mecarbil. This study shows that nuclear mechanics is influenced by the interaction between the sarcomere and the cytoskeletal network. Exploring the relationship between contractile dysfunction and nuclear lamina remodeling may reveal new therapeutic targets for cardiomyopathies.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Cell biology, Natural sciences, Stem cells research

Introduction

The sarcomere is the functional unit of striated muscle and thus has a fundamental role in the mechanical pumping of the heart. Sarcomere proteins are responsible for both passive tension and the generation of Ca2+-activated forces. Each sarcomere contains a highly organized arrangement of myofilament proteins, and its contractility is controlled by regulatory proteins of the thin and thick filaments. The thin filament consists of two strands of actin monomers, troponin complexes (troponin C, troponin I, and troponin T), and tropomyosin.1,2,3,4 In contrast, the bipolar thick filaments consist of myosin and associated proteins such as myosin-binding protein C and the giant, elastic protein titin. A significant proportion of diagnosed cardiomyopathies are strongly associated with variants of sarcomere protein genes.5,6,7,8 The structural and functional protein variants contribute to cardiomyocyte dysfunction that induces maladaptive responses. Abnormal calcium handling,9 fibrosis,10, and arrhythmias are commonly observed in the disease progression.11 From the clinical perspective, there is a variance in cardiomyopathic phenotypes and disease severity, even among patients with the same pathogenic variant. Most variants associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) or dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) are β-myosin heavy chain (MYH7), cardiac troponin T (TNNT2), cardiac troponin I (TNNI3), cardiac troponin C (TNNC1), α-actin (ACTC1), α-tropomyosin (TPM1), titin (TTN), α-actinin (ACTN2), and cardiac myosin binding protein-C (MYBPC3).5,6,12 Where these cardiomyopathy-associated variants have been investigated in detail, identification of primary abnormalities of biomechanical and energetic dysfunction provides insight into altered cardiovascular function but has not yet led to a complete understanding of the pathways that result in downstream clinical manifestations of the disease. For example, the TnT1 region of cardiac troponin T, which stabilizes the so-called head-to-tail overlap between adjacent tropomyosin molecules (i.e., the junction region),1,2,13 is a hot spot for HCM/DCM associated mutations14 with a high incidence of sudden cardiac death (SCD)15 and atrial fibrillation (AF).16 Its interactions with actin and tropomyosin stabilize the relaxed state of the cardiac thin filament.17,18

Structural coordination is essential to maintain homeostasis of the heart since the sarcomeric apparatus repetitively undergoes contraction and relaxation during systole (ejection phase) and diastole (filling phase), respectively, in cardiomyocytes. Beyond that, the forces generated during thin and thick filament dynamics not only propagate longitudinally through the sarcomeres connected in series within the myofibrils but also through lateral interactions across the width of a cardiomyocyte.19 From the Z-disc, force radiates to costameres and the extracellular matrix (ECM), and runs along intermediate filaments throughout the cytoskeleton. The longitudinal communication running through serially connected sarcomeres promotes the contraction of the muscle fiber. In contrast, lateral force transmission modulated by Z-disc proteins is transmitted to intermediate filaments,20 adding mechanistic signaling transduction to the cytoskeleton within cardiomyocytes.21 The cytoskeleton is a network of filamentous proteins such as actin, microtubules, and intermediate filaments. It provides a cellular frame that is responsible for mechanical integrity, cellular shape, and stabilization of the myofibril structure.22 In eukaryotic cells, the filamentous actin lattice assists tension,23 migration,24 and promotes architectural support.22,23,24 Microtubules are present in regions neighboring myofibrils, mitochondria, and wrapped around the cardiomyocyte nucleus.25 The most abundant intermediate filament expressed in cardiomyocytes consists of desmin proteins and forms an intricate intracellular meshwork that propagates mechanical signals through Z-discs, sarcolemma, the nucleus (or nuclei in multinucleated cardiomyocytes), and other organelles.20

Desmin filaments, which are anchored to the Z-discs, sense and transmit biomechanical signals from forces being generated by the sarcomeres to myocyte nuclei26,27,28 in addition to longitudinally compressive forces during systole that are experienced by cardiomyocyte nuclei that, unlike nuclei in normal skeletal muscle, are embedded centrally, between myofibrils.29 Forming a cage-like structure around the nucleus, filamentous desmin provides a mechanotransduction bridge from sarcomeres to the nucleus.30 The nucleus is a mechanoresponsive organelle, which senses and responds to physical and biochemical signals detected by proteins of the nuclear envelope (NE). Close to the nucleus, the interactions between the cytoskeleton and proteins anchored in the nuclear envelope are largely mediated by the linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton complex (LINC), nesprins, plectins, and kinesins. Proteins of the nuclear envelope form direct interactions with the nucleoplasmic interior and nuclear lamina. Nuclear lamina is a filamentous meshwork that provides structural support and chromatin organization via lamina-associated domains (LADs). The type-A lamins (lamin A and C) can be regulated to endure different microenvironment stiffnesses and mechanical stresses,31 and the transmission of mechanical load is associated with nuclear remodeling.32 Moreover, degradation of lamin A/C is a downstream event following its phosphorylation. Consequently, lamin A/C phosphorylation contributes to the regulation of nuclear stiffness.33 The contractile anomalies in cardiomyopathy can be severe, and additional nuclear irregularities could be the foundation for further pathological consequences in an already unhealthy heart. Although mechanotransduction is not new to the scientific community, little is known about nuclear mechanotransduction in cardiomyocytes.

Therefore, we investigated nuclear mechanics in human induced pluripotent stem cell derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) bearing TNNT2 pathogenic variants. The human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) were induced from fibroblast cultures, and pathogenic variants were engineered via CRISPR-Cas9 technology as described previously.34,35 The introduced heterozygous variants were associated with HCM or DCM derived from two different donors: the variants I79N (HCM) and R141W (DCM) were derived from one donor (isogenic control), and R92Q (HCM) and R134G (DCM) originated from a second donor (isogenic control). We characterized the transcriptomic profile of regulated genes in each pathogenic variant, aiming to correlate disease development and maladaptive response pathways in the hiPSC-CMs model. The hiPSC-CMs revealed altered contractility and exhibited significant nuclear alterations between HCM and DCM phenotypes and their respective controls. Abnormal sarcomeric contractility was associated with changes in nuclear stiffness and nuclear lamin A/C expression. To scrutinize the observed relationship between altered contractility and nuclear properties, we introduced myosin modulators Mavacamten or Omecamtiv Mecarbil into the HCM and DCM phenotype cell cultures to decrease or increase contractility, respectively. Mavacamten (MyoKardia – Bristol Myers Squibb) is a clinically approved, first-in-class treatment for HCM that reduces contraction and relaxation rates.36 In contrast, Omecamtiv Mecarbil (formerly known as CK-1827452, Cytokinetics) enhances contractility rates.37 Contractility rates were normalized by treatment for 24 h. Remarkably, the treatments with myosin modulators prevented nuclear lamina remodeling. Beyond examining the expression levels of the nuclear lamina, we further evaluated the phosphorylation status of lamin A/C, which is required for protein turnover.33 The alterations in contractility dynamics were associated with the regulation of lamin A/C phosphorylation levels, which suggests that the nuclear lamina is modulated by mechanical stress generated by cardiomyocyte contraction.

This study demonstrates that nuclear lamina remodeling occurs not only in response to the abnormal contractile mechanics of the sarcomeric variants investigated but also following treatment with myosin modulators that effectively restore nuclear lamina dynamics, improving the capacity of the nucleus to withstand mechanical stress. Furthermore, we raise the question of whether remodeling of the nuclear lamina in the context of cardiomyopathies may influence gene regulation, in light of the established role of lamin A/C in chromatin organization.38,39 These results draw attention to the mechanobiology and maladaptive response under physical stress in cardiomyocyte nuclei. The transmission of forces through physical connections between the sarcomere, the cytoskeleton, and nuclei is associated with nucleus lamina remodeling, although the mechanistic details are still unclear. Ultimately, the insights from this study will assist in the understanding of mechanotransduction pathways in cardiac diseases.

Results

Selection of cardiac troponin T pathogenic variants

To select TNNT2 variants associated with cardiomyopathies, we used an atomic model of the junction region of the cardiac thin filament (Figure S1A) obtained by high resolution cryo-electron microscopy.1 Four mutations located in the troponin T1 region of cardiac troponin T (cTnT) were selected to represent different mechanisms of alteration of the thin filament structure that lead to either HCM or DCM. The interaction of troponin T1 with actin and tropomyosin stabilizes the inhibited state of the thin filament.17,18 Therefore, I79N,4,40 which disrupts the interaction of troponin T1 with actin,1,4 and R92Q41 that presumably destabilizes the actin binding loop of cTnT11 are both linked to HCM (Figure S1B). On the other hand, both R134G42 and R141W,43,44 which are predicted to enhance troponin T1 interactions with tropomyosin by either reduction of repulsive forces between troponin T1 and tropomyosin or formation of novel bonds1 (Figure S1C), are linked to DCM.

Hypertrophic and dilated cardiomyopathy phenotypes in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes display cardiac markers

To study TNNT2 variants associated with cardiomyopathies, we established four hiPSC lines bearing either one of the two HCM-associated TNNT2 variants (I79N or R92Q) or one of the two DCM-associated TNNT2 variants (R141W or R134G) (Figure 1A). The pathogenic heterozygous variants were introduced through CRISPR-Cas9 knock-in technology (Figure S2) as described previously.34,35 The hiPSC lines were differentiated to hiPSC-CMs and, to enhance maturation levels, we applied a hormone-based maturation method45 with thyroid and glucocorticoid hormones (triiodothyronine and dexamethasone, respectively), which are critical for cardiac maturation. After the hormone treatment, several cardiac markers were observed within the hiPSC-CMs, including the myofibrillar striation pattern of the filamentous actin and the cardiac troponin T (Figures 1B and S3), and the expression of ventricular-specific myosin light chain-2 (MLC2V, Figure 1C). These markers suggest the successful differentiation of the hiPSCs into hiPSC-CMs. Additionally, through immunostaining against the ventricular marker MLC2V and the atrial cardiomyocyte marker MLC7, we observed that the hiPSC-CMs population was comprised mostly of ventricular cardiomyocytes (Figure 1D). We also observed visual differences in morphology between DCM and HCM cell lines, with HCM cells having a larger area than DCM cardiomyocytes (Figure S3).

To assess the global transcriptomics of hiPSC-CMs bearing TNNT2 pathogenic variants, we performed RNA-seq analysis on the hiPSC-CM cell lines. We identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the hiPSC-CMs bearing TNNT2 variants and their corresponding isogenic controls (Figures 2A and 2B). Next, we identified the top ten DEGs of each variant included in this study (Table 1). HCM hiPSC-CMs exhibited regulated genes such as PENK, ATP1B4, RGL3, SHISA3, and MMP-9 that are involved in cardiac hypertrophy, remodeling, atrial fibrillation risk, and heart failure (HF).46,47,48,49,50 In contrast, DCM hiPSC-CMs revealed regulated genes associated with maladaptive myocardium response, infarction, oxidative stress, and HF, including WNT3A, CHAT, TRH, and CTSF.51,52,53,54 To identify signaling pathways that are impacted by the TNNT2 variants, enrichment analysis was performed using DEGs against the KEGG pathway database55 (Figures 2C–2F).

We observed the enrichment of multiple pathways correlated to cardiomyopathy disease, such as Ca2+ signaling pathways, cardiac muscle contraction, HCM, DCM, and ACM (arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy), and also to cardio-protective pathways, such as cGMP/PKG and PI3K/Akt signaling. Overall, enrichment analysis of the top ten DEGs indicates cardiomyopathic pathophysiological and maladaptive regulation by TNNT2 pathogenic variants. An additional analysis comparing expression levels of MYH6 (myosin heavy chain-6) and MYH7 (myosin heavy chain-7) from RNA transcripts was performed to further validate the maturation status of the hiPSC-CM cell lines (Figures S4A and S4B). MYH6 is the faster of the two cardiac myosin heavy chain isoforms and is typically upregulated in various cardiac disease states, whereas MYH7 is the predominant isoform expressed in normal adult human ventricular cardiomyocytes. Thus, the relative expression levels of these isoforms can provide evidence regarding the developmental stage of the hiPSC-CMs.

To correlate the identified DEGs with HCM or DCM, we compared the regulated genes grouped by phenotype (Figures 2G and 2H; Table S2), where most of the top overlapping DEGs were associated with cardiomyopathy progression. In HCM hiPSC-CMs, we observed the regulation of genes correlated with increased heart rate and hypercontractility (Table S2). In DCM hiPSC-CMs, the top ten overlapping DEGs were related to contractile dysfunction, cytoskeleton disarray, and myocardial recovery (Table S2). We also analyzed regulated genes for hiPSC-CMs that originated from the same donor, but were regulated in opposite directions to pinpoint genes that are inversely regulated in HCM and DCM (Figures S5A and S5B). Global transcriptomics analyses revealed the regulation of pathways associated with cardiac contraction and cardio-protective mechanisms.

Differential force and contractility in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes bearing hypertrophic and dilated cardiomyopathies-associated to cardiac troponin T variants

HCM and DCM cardiomyopathies are typically associated with altered contractility associated with the abnormal development of steady-state, isometric force at saturating and sub-saturating Ca2+(the latter reflected in Ca2+ sensitivity) and crossbridge kinetics. We measured spontaneous contractions of the TNNT2 variant hiPSC-CM cell lines and their corresponding isogenic controls by analyzing contraction and relaxation period transients in kymograph plots (Figures 3A and S6). Both HCM hiPSC-CMs displayed shorter contractile periods (higher frequency of contractions) when compared to their respective isogenic controls (Figures 3B and 3C). In contrast, both DCM hiPSC-CMs showed the opposite effect, with longer periods of contraction and relaxation (slower frequency of contractions) when compared to their respective isogenic controls (Figures 3B and 3C). From kymograph plots, we also obtained time-to-peak (TTP, time for contraction) and time-to-relax (TTR, time from full contraction to relaxation). TTPs for I79N and R141W were significantly different, with the DCM variant R141W displaying longer periods when compared to HCM variant I79N and its isogenic control (Figure 3D). The DCM variant R134G variant displayed significantly longer (slower) TTP when compared to its isogenic control, while the HCM variant R92Q showed shorter (faster) TTP (Figure 3E). TTRs were similar for DCM variant R141W and its isogenic control, while HCM variant I79N exhibited shorter (faster) TTR (Figure 3F). No significant differences were present in TTRs for HCM variant R92Q, DCM variant R134G, and their isogenic control (Figure 3G). These results suggest these sarcomeric variants in cardiac TnT – I79N, R141W, R92Q, and R134G – alter contractility rates in hiPSC-CMs, with the main difference in contractile transients originating from the time required for contraction (TTP) rather than for relaxation (TTR) (Figures 3D–3G). Moreover, when TTP and TTR were normalized to the respective contractile period, both DCM cell lines (R141W and R134G) showed longer activation rates and shorter relaxation rates in comparison with their respective isogenic controls, while the HCM cell lines (I79N and R92Q) did not show significant differences in comparison with their respective isogenic controls for either parameter (Figures 3H–3K).

Next, to measure the force generation of our hiPSC-CMs contractility, we performed traction force microscopy (TFM) by culturing the hiPSC-CMs on polyacrylamide gels of varying stiffness (0.3 kPa, 1 kPa, 10 kPa, and 40 kPa, values that bracket most of the range of what is physiologically and pathophysiologically relevant for the human heart) (Table S1), embedded with fluorescent microspheres, and followed by imaging of the spheres during hiPSC-CM contraction (Figure 3L). Forces were calculated from the displacement vectors of the spheres and substrate stiffness and cellular area by using iterative Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) analysis, and the corresponding force generation field was derived by using Fourier Transform Traction Cytometry (FTTC) (Figure 3L).56 Within each genotype, force increased with increasing substrate stiffness (Figure S7). All force development results showed statistically significant differences between the pathogenic variants in lower stiffness substrates (0.3 and 1 kPa), with the I79N and R92Q HCM variants showing greater force and the R141W and R134G DCM variants showing lower force development relative to the respective isogenic controls at the same substrate stiffness (Figure S7). At higher stiffness (10 and 40 kPa), we similarly observed higher forces in HCM variants relative to the respective isogenic controls at the same substrate stiffness, but there were no significant differences between DCM variants and isogenic controls. The overall observed forces at 0.3 kPa and 1 kPa substrate stiffnesses were lower than those at 10 kPa substrate stiffness (Figures 3M and 3N), which is similar to the stiffness of a healthy heart,57,58 and 40 kPa substrate stiffness, which is beyond the physiological condition.

Lamin A/C levels and nuclear stiffness are altered in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes bearing hypertrophic or dilated cardiomyopathy sarcomeric variants

The NE serves as a protection and communication network between the nucleus interior and the cytoplasm. Its plasticity allows for nuclear lamina remodeling during cell growth, differentiation, disease, and aging, while playing a critical role in mechanotransduction signaling pathways.59 Lamin A/C makes up the nucleoskeleton that is immediately inside the NE and is particularly important for heart function. Several variants in LMNA are known to be associated with cardiomyopathies,60 while the depletion of lamin A/C in beating cardiomyocytes has been shown to be followed by DNA damage,61 suggesting its role in protecting the nucleus from the mechanically demanding environment. Interestingly, cellular contractility has been shown to regulate lamin A levels through access to phosphorylation and degradation,61 which suggests the possibility that the alterations in sarcomeric contractility that we observed could alter lamin A/C expression.21,22 Indeed, we observed significant differences in the immunoblotting of lamin A/C protein levels between HCM and DCM hiPSC-CMs lines, as both of the highly contractile HCM hiPSC-CMs demonstrated significantly higher levels of lamin A/C expression, while the less contractile DCM hiPSC-CMs had diminished expression levels (Figures 4A and 4B); this suggests a positive correlation between contractility level (Figures 3M, 3N, and S7) and nuclear lamin A/C (Figures 4A and 4B). The nucleus aspect ratio was similar across all cell lines, and immunofluorescence imaging revealed an increase in lamin A/C intensity per nuclear area in the HCM hiPSC-CMs and a decrease in the DCM cells (Figure S8).

Lamin A/C levels are known to dictate nuclear stiffness.62 Hence, to assess the functionality of the observed protein expression level differences, we measured nucleus stiffness by performing micropipette aspirations on nuclei from mononucleated hiPSC-CMs cells (Figure 4C). Cells were treated with latrunculin A/colchicine to disassemble the structural cytoskeleton, and nuclei were aspirated by capillary glass micropipettes with a negative pressure of ∼6 kPa for 5 min. The Young’s modulus was quantified considering the length of nucleus displacement inside the micropipette, the total force applied, and the diameter of the micropipette.63 Indeed, measurements of single nuclei from the HCM hiPSC-CMs demonstrated significantly higher stiffness when compared to their respective controls (Figures 4D and 4E), while the DCM variants have softer nuclei (Figures 4D and 4E). Taken together, these results suggest a relationship between impaired sarcomeric contractility and nuclear lamina remodeling.

Altered nucleus stiffness and impaired contractility are ameliorated upon the treatment of human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes with a myosin modulator

To confirm the relationship between sarcomeric contractility and nuclear lamina remodeling, we treated the hiPSC-CMs with myosin modulators that were developed for the treatment of sarcomeric dysfunction. The small molecule Mavacamten (MAVA) is a β-cardiac myosin ATPase inhibitor that directly affects the crossbridge cycle.36 MAVA reduces the number of myosin molecules available for binding to actin—i.e., ON disordered-relaxed state (DRX) myosin heads—by stabilizing myosin molecules in the biochemical OFF super-relaxed state (SRX). SRX may be associated with compact myosin forms with a closer proximity to the thick filament backbone that are unable to attach to actin.18,64,65 Consequently, with slowed crossbridge kinetics, MAVA normalizes contraction and relaxation rates linked to HCM phenotypes. Omecamtiv Mecarbil (OM) is another small molecule β-cardiac myosin modulator that, in contrast to MAVA, increases the crossbridge duty ratio by slowing transitions of myosin heads attached to thin filaments, thus increasing the number of attached crossbridges.37,66

MAVA or OM (in DMSO) was diluted in culture medium at 0.1 μM concentration, and we performed the treatment of our hiPSC-CM cell lines for 24 h. We observed overall rescue of contractile periods (contraction and relaxation) by applying MAVA to HCM or OM to DCM phenotypes, with values after treatment that were comparable to isogenic controls in the absence of drug. The HCM hiPSC-CMs treated with MAVA had significantly longer contractile periods (Figure 5A). Meanwhile, the DCM hiPSC-CMs treated with OM had the opposite effect, where we observed significantly shorter contractile periods (Figure 5B). These findings demonstrate that the myosin modulators are capable of rescuing contractility in the hiPSC-CMs model: treatment with MAVA decreased hyper-contractility of HCM-bearing variants, while OM treatment increased hypo-contractility in the DCM-bearing variants (Figures 5A and 5B).

Mavacamten (MAVA) and Omecamtiv Mecarbil (OM) drug treatments ameliorate overall nuclear stiffness in hiPSC-CMs

(A) Contractility was measured through kymograph analysis: both HCM-variants I79N and R92Q showed enhanced contractile periods after 24-h treatment of 0.1 μM MAVA. The treatment also had a similar effect in the respective isogenic controls.

(B) Kymograph analysis showed a decrease in contractile periods in DCM-variants R141W and R134G after 24-h treatment with 0.1 μM OM. Their respective isogenic controls demonstrated similar effects.

(C) Nuclear stiffness of HCM-variants I79N and R92Q showed a significant decrease after 24-h 0.1 μM MAVA treatment. The HCM associated phenotype nuclei demonstrated values closer to their respective isogenic controls.

(D) Increased nuclear stiffness was revealed in DCM-variants R141W and R134G after 24-h 0.1 μM OM treatment. One-way ANOVA was conducted to assess differences within groups; the results indicated statistically significant effects (p < 0.0001). Data are represented as mean ± SD, with N = 10 per group.

The next question to be answered was if the rescue of the contractility phenotype by these myosin modulators would also normalize nuclear stiffness. To test this, we performed the micropipette aspiration of latrunculin A/colchicine-treated hiPSC-CMs nuclei after 24-h treatments with MAVA or OM. Consistent with our hypothesis, both HCM-associated variants, I79N and R92Q, exhibited lower nuclear stiffness after MAVA treatment (Figure 5C), while the DCM-associated variants, R141W and R134G, exhibited elevated nuclear stiffness after OM treatment (Figure 5D). The observed results demonstrate that drug treatments ameliorated nucleus stiffness, reverting nuclear stiffness to levels similar to respective isogenic controls in the absence of the drugs (Figures 5C and 5D). These findings confirm the causative relationship between sarcomeric variants, dysfunctional contractility, and nuclear lamina remodeling in cardiomyocytes.

Contractile force influences lamin A/C and phosphorylated lamin A/C dynamics in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes

Our findings indicate a strong association between cardiomyocyte contractility and nuclear stiffness by the modulation of the nuclear lamina. To investigate how altered mechanical forces arising from cardiomyocyte contraction contribute to nuclear lamina remodeling, we performed immunofluorescence analysis of nuclei from hiPSC-CM cell lines before and after treatment with myosin modulators (Figure S9). Given that lamin A/C phosphorylation is essential for both protein turnover and nuclear softening,33 we sought to determine whether the observed changes in nuclear stiffness were driven by alterations in lamin A/C turnover dynamics. This analysis shows that hiPSC-CM cell lines carrying sarcomeric variants associated with HCM-I79N and R92Q – exhibit elevated levels of lamin A/C and reduced levels of phosphorylated lamin A/C (Ser22) (Figures S10A and S10B). Following treatment with MAVA, both cell lines displayed decreased lamin A/C expression and increased phosphorylated lamin A/C (Ser22) levels compared to their pre-treatment state (Figures S10A and S10B). Moreover, the hiPSC-CM lines harboring DCM-associated variants – R141W and R134G – initially exhibited reduced levels of lamin A/C and elevated levels of phosphorylated lamin A/C (Ser22) (Figures S10C and S10D). Following OM treatment, these expression patterns were reversed (Figures S10C and S10D). Isogenic control lines from both groups were also treated with myosin modulators and exhibited significant changes in response to the treatments, further supporting the role of contractile force in regulating nuclear lamina mechanics (Figures S9 and S10). These findings corroborate the existing literature on nuclear lamina dynamics in other cell types and provide novel evidence that cardiomyocyte contractile force directly influences nuclear lamina remodeling and mechanical properties to preserve nuclear integrity under abnormal mechanical load.

Discussion

The innovation of reprogramming fibroblasts into hiPSCs and differentiating hiPSCs into hiPSC-CMs provides a powerful method for recreating disease-relevant, humanized models that can be applied to various cardiac disease scenarios. This has made it possible to model human diseases in vitro and could serve as a valuable tool for patient diagnostics and treatment. Moreover, CRISPR-Cas9 facilitated genetic editing and generation of isogenic controls, allowing for the precise investigation of genetic variants. In this study, we chose two TNNT2 variants associated with HCM—I79N and R92Q—as well as two variants linked to DCM—R141W and R134G—and generated these four hiPSC-CMs models of disease via CRISPR-Cas9 (Figures 1A and S2). Despite different mechanisms of the thin filament structure alterations by the selected HCM or DCM mutations (Figure S1), the effects of either pair of pathogenic variants on the nucleus lamina remodeling were similar. This suggests that the mechanosensing mechanism in cardiomyocytes that links the nucleus and contractile apparatus may sense any pathogenic alteration in the sarcomere structure that leads to changes in the sarcomere mechanical output.

This study focused on the effects of hyper- and hypo-contractility on nuclear plasticity of hiPSC-CMs and the preventive effect of MAVA and OM on nucleus lamina remodeling under impaired mechanical contractile load. We show that abnormal sarcomeric contraction associated with pathogenic variants causes a mechanical burden in the nucleus, resulting in nuclear lamina remodeling in order to withstand/adapt to changes in mechanical loading. Here, we revealed the non-canonical downstream effects of myosin modulators MAVA and OM on cardiac nuclei mechanical properties. Moreover, our findings identify the nuclear lamina—and specifically the phosphorylation of lamin A/C—as a potential contributor to nuclear remodeling processes in cardiomyocytes. It will be important to further investigate strategies focusing on nucleus remodeling and its pathological effects in disease. We propose that future research should pay close attention to cytoskeletal intermediate filaments and nuclear envelope protein functions in cardiac muscle. We conclude that our study provides evidence that nuclear remodeling is present in cardiomyocytes bearing TNNT2 sarcomeric variants, and the treatment of impaired contractility is sufficient to prevent nuclear alterations.

Transcriptomics reveals concordantly regulated genes in troponin T2 variants

It is fundamentally important to understand regulatory alterations triggered by cardiomyopathy disorders because multiple transcriptional pathways are intrinsically part of cardiac remodeling in disease, and pathogenic variants localized in the same gene can induce opposite phenotypes. Thus, we sought to characterize the transcriptomic profiles of HCM and DCM hiPSC-CMs bearing TNNT2 variants by performing RNA-sequencing. After a comprehensive analysis of expression profiles, we noticed that a portion of the DEGs is concordantly regulated in both HCM and DCM TNNT2 variants of a given donor. This can be observed in the DEG heatmaps (Figures 2A and 2B), which may reflect the overall cardiac dysfunction and pathophysiological disturbances in molecular function. The identified regulated genes (Table 1) were mostly closely related to cardiac disorder and HF (e.g., genes PENK, MMP-9, ATP1B4, CTSF, and RGL3), and myocardial remodeling response (e.g., genes CHAT, SHISA3, and WNT3A).

Similarly, we also observed concordant enrichment of multiple signaling pathways (Figures 2C–2F) such as Ca2+ signaling pathways, cardiac contractility, and cardiomyopathies, including HCM and DCM. Moreover, KEGG exhibited regulated endogenous cardio-protective pathways such as cGMP/PKG, which has been suggested to modulate Ca2+ oscillations and mitochondrial homeostasis in myocardial reperfusion,67 and PI3K/Akt signaling that has a central role in cardiomyocyte survival, contraction, and cardiac fibrosis by participating in processes such as cardiac hypertrophy, myocardial injury, and HF.68 The differential regulation in ECM receptors and focal adhesion pathways may correlate to the mechanical stresses of cardiac remodeling. Cell-cell adhesion of cardiomyocytes, in costameres and intercalated disc regions, is critical for cytoskeletal function and electrical signaling. The abnormal expression of these pathways is related to compensatory responses to cardiac disease, and associated with the consequences of TNNT2 pathogenic variants.

The expression profile analyses indicate that TNNT2 variants present the differential regulation of genes related to cardiomyopathy disorder, compensatory cardiac response, and cardioprotective effects. These similarities in the profile changes found in diverse cardiomyopathies are expected due to the dysfunction of the biomechanical structure and at the molecular pathophysiological level. Nevertheless, some DEGs are unique to each TNNT2 variant and are likely to be responsible for the distinct phenotypes of HCM and DCM. In addition, the identification of protein expression levels from RNA transcripts indicates the differential expression of MYH6 and MYH7 in these hiPSC-CMs. The ratio of MYH6/MYH7 is modulated during cardiac development, pathophysiological stress, and HF. In healthy adult hearts, the expression of the MYH7 isoform is predominantly higher, but postnatal cardiomyocytes undergoing the final stages of maturation mainly express MYH6 in rodents.69 Our findings on the gene expression and ratio of MYH6/MYH7 (Figures S4A and S4B) indicate that the genetic background may be an important player in the cardiac differentiation and maturation processes. The cell lines derived from one donor were capable of achieving higher levels of MYH7 expression, indicating that their maturation was more successful than that of the secondary donor, even though they were exposed to the same maturation protocol. We speculate the possibility that key transcriptional regulators may be associated with the ability to reach higher levels of maturation based on the diversity of genetic backgrounds. Regardless, measurements of contractile kinetics and nuclear stiffness revealed no significant differences between both donor-derived isogenic controls, suggesting that variations in MYH6/MYH7 expression did not contribute to the abnormal contractile parameters observed in the HCM and DCM phenotypes of the cell lines derived from the second donor.

Hyper- and hypo-contractile profiles in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes bearing cardiac troponin T variants match literature reports

Cardiomyopathy-associated sarcomeric variants are closely associated with abnormal contractility.6,70,71 Thus, we sought to investigate if hiPSC-CMs in culture would translate relevant HCM and DCM contractile phenotypes at the cellular level. Variants I79N and R92Q are associated with increased myofilament Ca2+ sensitivity in hiPSC-CMs and mouse models.18,34,41,72,73,74 Thin filament variant I79N in mice destabilizes the resting state of the thin filament4,18 and variant R92Q in mice leads to enhanced systolic function, a hyper-contractile heart, and significantly smaller cardiomyocytes.72 On the other hand, thin filament variants R141W and R134G are associated with Ca2+ desensitization.42,44,75 The R141W variant causes contractile dysfunction by strengthening the interaction between cTnT and tropomyosin, which inhibits the thin filament and, at the macroscopic level, decreases heart rate.42,44 The affinity between tropomyosin and cTnT is also increased by the R134G variant,76 accompanying a lower ejection fraction at the macroscopic level.77 Our contractility measurements from spontaneously beating cells evaluated by kymograph analysis demonstrated hyper-contractility in I79N and R92Q, and hypo-contractility in R141W and R134G (Figures 3A–3K, S6A, and S6B), which are consistent with previous studies.18,34,41,42,44,72,76,77 The troponin complex exists in multiple conformation states in high and low Ca2+ concentrations.3 Thus, troponin variants could disrupt the distribution of troponin conformational states during muscle activation, as demonstrated for the I79N variant.4

Force development by human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes increases with substrate stiffness

In addition to contractility measurements, we measured the force development of hiPSC-CMs containing TNNT2 pathogenic variants grown on substrates with different matrix stiffnesses (Figures 3L–3N and S7). The microenvironment of the myocardium includes biomechanical loads that cardiomyocytes work against, in addition to the blood within the chambers of the heart. As cardiac muscle matures from the embryonic stage to adult, the heart undergoes morphological changes and consequent stiffening with the expression of contractile and cytoskeletal proteins, isoform switches, and extracellular matrix proteins.57,58

The tissue stiffness begins to change from approximately 0.3 kPa–1 kPa within the first 4 days of embryonic heart development, reaching elastic Young’s modulus E ∼ 10 kPa at birth and for the rest of an individual’s life unless disease and/or aging alter that stiffness.57,58 In diseased hearts, cardiac tissue stiffness can be markedly increased, ranging from 40 to 100 kPa,78,79 where HCM phenotypes are characterized by increased myocardial stiffness, reaching E ∼ 40 kPa,80,81 and cardiomyocyte death can lead to myocardial inflammation and fibrosis with E ∼ 100 kPa.79 To investigate the influence of stiffness on contractility, we performed TFM assays on hiPSC-CMs seeded onto hydrogels of varying matrix stiffness ranging from 0.3 kPa to 40 kPa, a range that covers all but the most extreme values that might be encountered by cardiomyocytes in the human heart.

The displacement of fluorescent beads embedded in the hydrogels was used to quantify the force of contraction of beating cardiomyocytes. In substrates with a stiffness of 0.3–1 kPa, the force measurements for each genotype were lower than on higher stiffness substrates (Figures S7A and S7B); we suggest that this may be due to the feedback between the higher force that is required to the effect displacement of the stiffer substrates and both short- and long-term adaptations of the hiPSC-CMs. An analogous effect that may reflect the well-established Frank-Starling mechanism observed in the intact heart, whereby increased myocardial stretch leads to enhanced contractile force.82 Force development at 10 kPa and 40 kPa is consistent with literature studies where HCM variants show greater force of contraction, but DCM variants did not show significant differences from isogenic controls in these higher stiffness substrates (Figures S7C and S7D).83 Thus, our results provide an interesting perspective on the contractile force development from spontaneous beating of DCM variants under physiological conditions, suggesting possible changes not seen in animal model studies. Since DCM hiPSC-CMs could only show lower force on softer substrates, these changes would not likely be observed in healthy adult hearts.

Divergent nuclear lamina remodeling in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes bearing cardiac troponin T variants

We performed immunoblotting for lamin A/C expression levels to evaluate whether impaired contractility due to the expression of HCM or DCM pathogenic variants leads to nuclear remodeling, presumably through mechanotransduction pathways. There are established relationships between LMNA variants and clinical DCM phenotype (one form of laminopathy) and other cardiomyopathies such as arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM), left ventricle noncompaction cardiomyopathy (LVNC), and restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM).60 However, little has been previously reported about the effect of contractile dysfunction on lamin A/C expression in cardiomyocytes. Our findings report increased lamin A/C expression levels in both I79N and R92Q (HCM) hiPSC-CMs, and diminished levels in R141W and R134G (DCM) hiPSC-CMs (Figures 4A and 4B). These results indicate that sarcomeric pathogenic variants can lead to alterations in the nuclear lamin A/C protein levels.

To evaluate the modulation of lamin A/C dynamics, we investigated the phosphorylated lamin A/C (Ser22) levels before and after 24-h treatment with myosin modulators. Our phosphorylated lamin A/C (Ser22) findings are consistent with previously reported observations in the literature.33,62 These results further support the evidence of nuclear lamina remodeling in this study, in which increased cellular contractility (Figures 3M, 3N, and S7) is associated with reduced lamin A/C protein turnover (Figures S9 and S10). To further test the functional significance of altered nuclear lamin A/C levels and mechanical contractile load, we sought to verify if the nuclear stiffness would also be altered by hyper- and hypo-contractility associated with TNNT2 variants. The nuclear lamins meshwork provides structural support to the nuclear envelope and chromatin organization through the lamina-associated domains (LADs), and can be regulated to endure different microenvironment stiffness and mechanical stresses.31 The aspiration of nuclei from hiPSC-CMs showed increased nuclear stiffness in I79N and R92Q hiPSC-CMs, and reduced nuclear stiffness in R141W and R134G hiPSC-CMs when compared to isogenic controls (Figures 4D and 4E). Together, these findings indicate a close interrelationship between altered contractility and nuclear mechanosensing, with nuclear lamina expression and nuclear envelope stiffness altered to match changes in mechanical load. We propose that altered contractility, originating from TNNT2 variants, is translated into mechanical stress, and transmitted through the cytoskeleton to the nucleus or directly by the compression of the nucleus upon each contraction, resulting in alterations to the nuclear lamina. The nuclear lamin A/C plasticity changes are a potential mechanoresponsive attempt to endure and maintain nuclear homeostasis.

Non-canonical roles for myosin modulators Mavacamten and Omecamtiv Mecarbil in preventing nuclear lamina remodeling

If nuclear lamina remodeling is indeed an effect originating from sarcomere dysfunction caused by the presence of TNNT2 variants, the treatment of contractility dysfunction with myosin modulators MAVA and OM would suffice to ameliorate nuclear compliance. To test this hypothesis, 24-h treatments were performed in hiPSC-CMs, where contractility periods and nuclear stiffness were measured. First, the drug treatments effectively normalized hiPSC-CMs contractility. The I79N and R92Q hiPSC-CMs showed longer contraction periods after MAVA treatments, i.e., durations closer to isogenic controls before treatment (Figure 5A). R141W and R134G hiPSC-CMs displayed shorter contraction periods after OM administration, again, durations closer to isogenic controls before treatment (Figure 5B). The commonly present hyper-contractility of HCM is reduced in studies of HCM variants upon MAVA administration: myosin ATPase activity is reduced, and there are preventive effects on left ventricle wall thickening and myocardial fibrosis.84 Other reports show that MAVA induces the neutralization of peak amplitude and slower relaxation rates, tending to a normalization of overall cardiac function in R92Q variants of cardiomyocytes isolated from guinea pig hearts.36,85 On the other hand, the contractility phenotype in DCM ameliorated upon OM treatment86 and clinical data suggest that OM increased contractile function, stroke volume, and augmentation of systolic function in human failing hearts,87 consistent with our results showing increased contractility in hypo-contractile hiPSC-CMs upon OM administration.

Second, after 24-h treatments with MAVA or OM, we showed amelioration of nuclear stiffness in all hiPSC-CM cell lines, where nuclear stiffness of HCM phenotypes decreased and DCM increased after MAVA or OM treatments, respectively (Figures 5C and 5D). Since these two myosin modulators act directly on the ATPase activity of β-cardiac myosin, offering target selectivity, we suggest that mechanotransduction pathways may transduce mechanical stimuli directly from sarcomeres to the nucleus. The nucleus is a mechanosensing organelle, and the mechanism by which cardiomyocytes can transduce sarcomeric forces to the nucleus is still unclear, but we suggest that intermediate filaments are a crucial link between the cytoskeleton and nucleus lamina remodeling. Furthermore, this study also demonstrated that the phosphorylation of lamin A/C (Ser22) plays a pivotal role in the remodeling of the nuclear lamina and is therefore integral to the regulation of mechanosensing dynamics. These findings demonstrate that it is possible that HCM- and DCM-associated impaired contractility can initiate mechanosensitive responses in cardiomyocyte nuclei.

Limitations of the study

The hiPSC-CM model enables researchers to conduct in-depth molecular studies of cardiomyopathies, as illustrated by our investigation of TNNT2 variant-mediated HCM and DCM. However, it is important to recognize the model’s limitations when interpreting these findings. For instance, hiPSC-CMs are generally considered to be an immature version of adult cardiomyocytes, and their maturation is fundamentally restricted. Consequently, they do not fully mirror the characteristics of adult cardiomyocytes derived from in vivo models. In addition, the inherent heterogeneity of the hiPSC-CM population—comprising cells at various maturation levels—is another technical challenge, as evidenced by the differences in the expression of cardiac markers (Figures 1D and S4).

Experimental models and study participant details

Cell line models

Detailed descriptions of the materials and experimental procedures can be found in the STAR Methods section including materials and methods. The cell lines designated as Group 1 (WT, I79N and R141W) in this study were developed by the Vanderbilt University Medical Center and have been successfully validated for pluripotency. The design methodologies and experimental protocols are detailed in previously published research.34 The cell lines used in this study (referred to as Group 2, including WT, R92Q and R134G) were derived from the GM23338 induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) line, originally established from fibroblasts and obtained from the NIGMS Human Genetic Cell Repository at the Coriell Institute for Medical Research. These cell lines are both derived from male patients and are classified as Highly Unique Resources. Using CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing technology, heterozygous cells were genetically engineered to express mutant forms of the TNNT2, specifically the I79N, R92Q, R141W, and R134G variants.

Cell culture

Culture plates were coated with Matrigel (Corning, #354230) at 5 mg/mL concentration. The hiPSC vials received from collaborators were thawed and seeded in pre-coated Matrigel plates and cultured in mTESR1 media (Stemcell, #85857) supplemented with 10 μM Y27632 ROCK inhibitor (Tocris, #1254). After 24 hours, fresh media was changed, and cell islets were passaged every 4 days. Once islets reach 80% confluency and are ready for cardiac differentiation, fresh RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with B-27 minus insulin and 6 μM CHIR99021 (Tocris, #4423) was added. This was called Day 0 of cardiac differentiation (CD). Medium was changed with RPMI 1640 (Gibco, #11875-119) medium supplemented with B-27 minus insulin (Gibco, #A1895601) after 24 hours (Day 1 CD). Post 72 hours of CHIR99021 addition (Day 3 CD), a combined medium was prepared. The combined medium contained 1:2 ratio of the previous medium and new RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with B-27 minus insulin, were 5 μM of IWP2 (Tocris, #3533) was added to the final volume. On Day 5, medium was changed for new RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with B-27 minus insulin. And on Day 7, the medium is changed for RPMI 1640 medium with B-27 supplement (Gibco, #17504044). After the metabolic selection (Day 7 CD), the medium was changed every other day, and hiPSC-cardiomyocytes were passaged until experiments were performed (Day 30 post-CD). Media was supplemented with hormonal treatment mix of Triiodo-L-thyronine hormone (Sigma Aldrich, #T2877), and Dexamethasone (Cayman, #11015) from Day 16 to Day 30 post-CD at concentrations 0.01 μM and 1 μM, respectively, as previously described.45 The cells lines were not tested for mycoplasma under cardiomyocyte differentiation conditions.

Method details

Immunofluorescence

Coverslips were placed at the bottom of culture plates before Matrigel coating and cell culture were performed. hiPSC-cardiomyocytes were washed with 1X Phosphate Buffered Saline solution (PBS) before fixation buffer (4% formaldehyde) was added to the wells and incubated for 15 minutes under agitation. Wells were then washed 3X with 0.1% BSA, followed by 10 minutes incubation of 0.5% TRITON X-100 under agitation. After removal of TRITON X-100, wells were washed 3X with 0.1% BSA, followed by a 30-minute incubation of 5% BSA under agitation. Once 5% BSA is removed, primary antibodies were prepared and added to the wells for overnight incubation under agitation at 4°C. On the next day, wells were washed with 0.1% BSA one time, and secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 dye donkey anti-rabbit, and Alexa Fluor 647 dye donkey anti-mouse, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A-21206 and #A-31571, respectively) were prepared at 2 μL/mL concentration and incubated for 90 minutes under agitation in room temperature. The wells were washed 3X with 0.1% BSA for 5 minutes under agitation, followed by addition of Phalloidin dye at 1 μL/mL for 45 minutes under agitation. Once Phalloidin was removed, the wells were washed 3X with 0.1% BSA for 5 minutes, and HOESCHT (Thermo Fischer Scientific, #H1398) dye at 0.5 μL/mL concentration was added to the wells for 15 minutes under agitation. Removal and 3X washes with 0.1% BSA were followed subsequently. The coverslips were detached from the culture plates and mounted in glass slides with anti-fading solution. Imaging was performed on the next day in inverted microscope (Olympus IX71) and in confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 880 Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope). Fluorescence intensities were acquired using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA, https://imagej.net/ij/), and graphs were plotted in GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0 GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA), where groups were compared using one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Representative immunofluorescence images were imported into Adobe Illustrator (version CC 28.5; https://adobe.com/products/illustrator) for organization and layout purposes.

RNA-sequencing

Cells were detached and collected from culture plates post 30 days of cardiac differentiation. RNA isolation (Qiagen RNAeasy) was prepared following manufacturer’s instructions. A standard NEBNEXT RNA Ultra II Library was prepared using 400ng as sample input of total RNA (determined by Qubit RNA HS reagents, Thermo) with a RIN >7 (TapeStation 4200 High Sensitivity RNA ScreenTape, Agilent). Libraries were generated using the NEBNEXT Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module and NEBNEXT Ultra II RNA Library Prep kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) according to manufacturer’s instructions, with an RNA fragmentation time of 15 minutes, a 1/10th dilution of NEB adaptor and 11 cycles of PCR amplification with NEB dual-indexing primers. Amplified libraries were initially quantified by Qubit DNA HS reagents, checked for size and artifacts using TapeStation DNA High Sensitivity ScreenTape reagents and KAPA qPCR (KAPA Biosystems) was used to determine molar quantities of each library. Individual libraries were diluted and pooled equimolar, and the pool was again checked by TapeStation and KAPA qPCR before submission for sequencing. A 50 bp paired-end reads sequencing run was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 at the Florida State University Translational Science Laboratory. Individual sample was sequenced to about 10 million reads. After quality control with fastqQC (FastQC, bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk), the sequenced reads were aligned to the hg38 human reference using the STAR (version=2.7.9a).88 Differential gene expression analysis was done using the DESeq2 package (version 1.42.0).89 To identify the genes that are statistically significant, results from DESeq2 was filtered based on adjusted p-value < 0.05 and fold change > 2 when compared to isogenic control samples. Gene ontology and pathway analysis for these genes were done using Enrichr.90

Western blots

Cells cultured for 30 days were collected, centrifuged at 200 G for 5 minutes and resuspended in 1X PBS. This process was repeated twice. Once the last PBS wash was removed, cell pellets were frozen at -20°C overnight. On the next day, appropriate amount of RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific, #89900) (50 μL for 1 million cells) and protein inhibitor cocktail (PIC) at 1:100 concentration were used for resuspension of cell pellets. Samples were sonicated for 15 seconds, then remained on ice for 30 minutes. Following centrifugation was performed, at 13 rpm for 20 minutes in 4°C, and supernatant was transferred to new tubes. An appropriate volume of LDS/2-Mercaptoethanol (Gibco, #21985023) buffer was added to samples (at ratio of 36 μL LDS/2-Mercaptoethanol buffer for 100 μL supernatant), and tubes were heated for 10 minutes at 90°C. Samples were frozen at -20°C overnight. On next day, samples were centrifuged at 13 rpm for 20 minutes in 4°C, and loaded into pre-cast gels (Invitrogen, Bolt Bis-Tris Mini Gel 4-12%, #NW04120BOX) with a blue standard ladder (Invitrogen, SeeBlue Plus 2 Protein Standard, #LC5925). Gels were run at 160 V for 50 minutes, and transferred in iBlot equipment (iBlot 2 Transfer Device, Thermo Fischer Scientific) with iBlot Transfer stacks (Invitrogen, iBlot 2NC Regular Stacks, #IB23001). After transfer was completed, membranes were washed with 1X Tris Buffered Saline (TBS) and blocked with 1% BSA in TBS for 15 minutes under agitation. Membranes were incubated overnight under agitation at 4°C, with primary antibody cocktail against Lamin A/C (Santa Cruz #7292, at concentration 1:100) and GAPDH (Santa Cruz #25778, at concentration 1:500), diluted in 1X Tween-Tris Buffered Saline (TTBS). On the following day, primary antibodies were removed, and membranes washed 3X with TTBS. Secondary antibody cocktail (LICORbio, IRDye® 800CW Donkey anti-Rabbit and IRDye® 680RD Donkey anti-Mouse, at concentration 1:10000) was incubated on membranes for 1 hour under agitation at room temperature. Succeeding 3X washes with TTBS were performed, and membranes were scanned in Odyssey CLx imaging system equipment (LICORbio). Band intensities were quantified using Image Studio software (LICORbio) with background subtraction and standardized band area selection. The resulting values were imported into GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0 GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA), where groups were compared using one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Drug testing

Omecamtiv Mecarbil (Adooq Bioscience, #A11206) and Mavacamten (MedChemExpress, #HY-109037) were initially dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 10 mM. For drug treatment, the compounds were diluted to a final concentration of 0.1 μM in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with B27. The serum-free medium was then replaced with the drug-containing medium for treatment of hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes. Cells were incubated with the compounds for 24 hours at 37°C prior to experimentation.

Micropipette aspiration

Micropipettes were prepared by horizontal pulling and heating the middle of the capillary glass, generating two micropipettes’ halves. The micropipettes were heated by a heated glass dome to cut approximate 3 μm tips. Cells were collected from culture plates, transferred to 15 mL conical tubes, and centrifuged at 200 G for 5 minutes. After removal of supernatant, pellet was resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with B27 and 1 μM latrunculin A/HOESCHT. Samples were incubated in water bath and protected from light for 30 minutes. Once incubation was finished, samples were centrifuged at 200 G for 5 minutes, and pellet resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with B27 and 0.5 μM latrunculin A/colchicine. Samples were protected from light until experiment start. Micropipettes were filled with 1X PBS to get rid of possible air bubbles. Micromanipulator, pressure controller and 60X objective lens were used for live imaging. The cells were maintained in suspension, as a drop of 50 μL from the sample placed on coverslip. Micropipettes were immersed in cell suspension and single cells were captured by approximating micropipette to cells at 0 kPa pressure. Once single cells were stable, negative pressure (∼ 6 kPa) was applied manually by pressure controller, and recordings of 5 minutes were performed. During this time, nuclei were deformed inside micropipettes until stabilized at certain length. Pressure, tip diameter and length parameters were recorded for each sample. Analysis was done using elastic Young’s modulus,63 and graphs were made in GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0 GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA), where groups were compared using one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The length of aspirated nucleus inside of the micropipette during experiments revealed visible variations of the nucleus stiffness.

Kymographs

Kymographs were generated using the Kymograph plugin in ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA, https://imagej.net/ij/). A line was drawn along the region of interest using the Line Tool, followed by application of the plugin to produce a spatiotemporal representation of contractile movement along the selected path over time. The resulting kymographs were analyzed to assess dynamic movement of hiPSC-CMs contractility and plotted in GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0 GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA), where groups were compared using one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Traction Force Microscopy

Traction force microscopy (TFM) assay was performed following standard preparation methods.33 LabTeK plates’ glass (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Nunc™ Lab-Tek™ II Chambered Coverglass, #155382PK) was firstly cleaned with 3 washes of methanol, and silanized by ATCS (Allyltrichlorosilane 95%, Alfa Aesar) in methanol at concentration 1:500 for 30 minutes. Chambers were washed 3 times with methanol and dried in vacuum desiccator overnight. On the next day, precursors of polyacrylamide (PA) gel recipes ranging from 0.3 kPa to 40 kPa were prepared as indicated in Table S1, where indicated quantities of distilled water, 0.02 μm microspheres (Polystyrene latex microspheres, Thermo Fischer Scientific, #C37231), 40% Acrylamide solution, and N,N’-Methylenebisacrylamide (Sigma Aldrich, #146072). From PA gel precursors, 88 μL were transferred to new vials, and 10 μL of 10% APS (Ammonium Persulfate, Sigma Aldrich, #248614), 1 μL of 0.1% v/v TEMED (tetramethylethylenediamine, VWR, #110-18-9) and 1 μL of Fluorescent Beads (0.2 μm FluoSpheres Carboxylate Modified Microspheres, Invitrogen, #F8810) were added. Rapidly, 10 μL of completed PA gel were transferred to the center of chambered LabTek silanized glasses, and covered with a squared pre-cut coverslip. Small weights were placed on the top, allowing to polymerize and completely crosslink for 45 minutes. Once gels were crosslinked, the weights were removed and 1X PBS was added into the chambers. Plates were placed on rocker for 1 hour. After 1X PBS rinse, coverslips were removed using sharp needles and tweezers, and a second 1-hour wash with 1X PBS on rocker was performed. Following removal of 1X PBS and a wash with distilled water, gels were immersed in Sulfo-Sanpah (Thermo Fischer Scientific, #A35395) diluted in 50 mM HEPES at concentration 1:500, and chambers were exposed to U.V. lamp (without lids) for 10 minutes. After removal of Sulfo-Sanpah solution, 2 washes of distilled water were performed and pre-chilled Collagen Type I (Corning, #354236) solution in 0.1 M Acetic Acid (VWR, #64-19-7) and 50 mM of HEPES (Gibco, #15630080) at final 0.2mg/mL concentration was added to chambers under agitation at 37°C overnight. On the following day, chambers were washed with distilled water, 1X PBS was added to chambers and plate was placed on rocker for 1 hour. Then, 1X PBS was replaced for sterile 1X PBS and chambers were exposed to U.V. lamp (without lids) for 2 hours in cell culture hood. Once sterilization was complete, 1X PBS was replaced for RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with B27, and hiPSC-cardiomyocytes were seeded into chambers. The cardiomyocytes demonstrated spontaneous contractility within 3 days after passaging, and live imaging was performed afterwards. LabTek plates were recorded for 10 seconds at 30 fps (frames per second) in spinning confocal microscope (Nikon Eclipe Ti) using Andor iQ3 System software. Analyses were performed using an encoding algorithm in ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA, https://imagej.net/ij/) with the “PIV” and “FTTC” plug-ins for evaluation of bead displacement and force fields (https://sites.google.com/site/qingzongtseng/tfm), as described.56,91 The generated force fields were mapped by cardiomyocyte cell area. For each TNNT2 variant, the mean force development by area was plotted for our results. Our findings were plotted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0 GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA), where groups were compared using one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data in this study are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), with individual data points shown for each group. The sample size (n) ranged from 5 to 50, depending on the specific assay conducted. P-values indicating statistical significance are displayed above the bars corresponding to the compared groups (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001, p < 0.0001. All datasets were evaluated for homogeneity of variances using the Brown-Forsythe and Bartlett’s tests, followed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparisons test.
