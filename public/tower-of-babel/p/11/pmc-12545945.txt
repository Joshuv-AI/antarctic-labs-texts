Clinical dental privileges in Saudi Arabia: awareness, perceived competence, and practice among general dentists
Authors: Suliman Alsaeed, Batool Alhabib, Amal Albarrak, Farah Alhamdan, Leqaa Alowaidi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to assess the awareness of general dentists in Saudi Arabia on clinical dental privileges, evaluate the practicality of these privileges in clinical practice, and to explore dentists perceived competence in performing procedures that are considered beyond their authorized scope. A questionnaire was distributed randomly to general dentists across Saudi Arabia through the research offices of the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties and the Saudi Dental Society. The questionnaire included demographic data, self-reported awareness of MOH dental privileges, and responses to six clinical scenarios to assess privilege awareness, perceived competence, and clinical practice. Chi-square tests were used to analyze associations between these variables, with significance set at p < 0.05. Out of 238 respondents, 72% reported awareness of MOH dental privileges. However, significant mismatches were observed between awareness and actual practice across most scenarios. Procedures most commonly performed outside authorized privileges included in-office vital tooth bleaching, placement of single-unit crowns, and molar root canal treatment (RCT). A strong association was found between lack of awareness of the privileges or perceived competence and performing these procedures. Despite moderate levels of awareness, significant gaps remain between general dentists' awareness, perceived competence, and actual clinical practice. The majority of the sample perform procedures beyond their authorized scope of care, often due to misunderstandings about privilege boundaries or confidence in their skills. These findings highlight the need for enhanced communication of privilege regulations, integration of privilege education into undergraduate curricula, and alignment between training and policy. Such reforms are essential to ensure safe, standardized dental practice in line with the goals of Saudi Vision 2030.Keywords: Dental clinical privileges, General dentists, Scope of practice, Perceived competence, Patient safety

Introduction

The Ministry of Health (MOH) in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia serves as the primary governmental authority responsible for providing and financing healthcare services nationwide. It oversees preventive, curative, and rehabilitative care through an extensive network of facilities and programs (Ministry of Health 2025). As the leading healthcare body, the MOH plays a major role in achieving an integrated and sustainable health system to promote public health, prevent diseases, enable competencies and support innovation. Additionally, the MOH regulates licensing for medical facilities and formulating health policies for both public and private sectors, ensuring compliance with standards and regulations across the country (Ministry of Health 2025).

The government of Saudi Arabia has introduced several initiatives to improve the quality of healthcare services, including the Saudi Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions (CBAHI). CBAHI is the official agency authorized to grant accreditation certificates to all governmental and private healthcare facilities operating in Saudi Arabia (Saudi Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare 2025). The institution supports healthcare system through introducing quality and safety performance standards for all healthcare facilities operating in the Kingdom to provide excellent and effective healthcare services known as the safest services at the highest quality and best value (Ministry of Health 2023, Saudi Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare 2025). The MOH has also implemented various programs, such as the National Transformation Program (NTP) in line with the goals Saudi Vision 2030, aimed at improving the quality and efficiency of healthcare services in the country (Saudi Vision 2030 2025).

One of MOH’s key quality initiatives is the implementation of a credentialing and privileging system for medical and dental providers (Credentialing and Privileging Regulations 2025). This is to ensure competence and promote patient safety, regulatory compliance, risk management, quality standards, and good institutional governance. According to the National Committee for Quality Assurance (NCQA), credentialing can be defined as the process by which an organization reviews and evaluates qualifications of licensed independent practitioners to provide service to its members, to ensure that all patients receive quality care by competent and qualified practitioners (Credentialing and Privileging Regulations 2025). On the other hand, privileging can be defined as the process of reviewing an individual’s credentials to determine the authority and responsibility to be granted to a practitioner for making independent decisions to diagnose, initiate, alter, or terminate a regimen of medical or dental care. Privileging determines the physician’s scope of practice in the organization determined by his or her competencies (Credentialing and Privileging Regulations 2025). A recent study reported that a contemporary credentialing and privileging program identified expired licenses, malpractice histories, and other critical issues before granting privileges, underscoring its role in risk management and patient safety (Robinson et al. 2023). Similarly, a study from Harvard School of Dental Medicine described a faculty group practice–driven credentialing and privileging program that balanced clinical, educational, and accreditation needs, emphasizing that privileges must evolve with technology and provider competencies (Kalenderian et al. 2010).

Clinical dental privileges for dental practitioners fall into three categories: core privileges, special non-core privileges, and additional privileges (Dental License Requirements 2025). The dental core privileges are granted to a fully trained, entry-level health care provider who is qualified to do particular dental procedures on completing an approved training program. Dentistry core privileges include examination, diagnosis and treatment plan, caries control, preventive measures (fluoride application and fissure sealant), direct operative restorations, simple extractions, incision and drainage for fluctuate intra oral swelling, management of acute periodontal lesion, non-surgical periodontal therapy, pulp extirpation, endodontic treatment for anterior teeth and premolars (non-complicated canal teeth) and pulpotomy (Dental License Requirements 2025). Procedures that go beyond the core that would require additional training and/or experience are covered by additional specific privileges. To be eligible to apply for the special non-core privileges, the provider must demonstrate successful completion of an approved, recognized course when such exists, or acceptable supervised training in residency, fellowship or other acceptable experience, and provide documentation of competence in performing the requested procedure. For extra privileges to be granted, the practitioner will need to submit documentation of successful completion of qualifying education and training certificates, courses or workshops (Dental License Requirements 2025).

The aim of this study was to evaluate general dentists’ awareness of clinical dental privileges in Saudi Arabia, assess their practicality in clinical practice, and explore dentists’ perceived competence regarding procedures considered beyond their authorized scope.

Material and methods

This was a descriptive, cross-sectional study conducted among general dentists currently practicing in Saudi Arabia. The study received ethical approval from King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (Study # NRC22R/418/08).

Questionnaire content and structure

The questionnaire was divided into three main sections:

Demographic information

This section collected variables such as gender, age, nationality, region of practice, type of practice (governmental or private), and years of experience as a general practitioner.

MOH dental privileges

Q: Are you aware of the Ministry of Health's dental clinical privileges and regulations in Saudi Arabia? (Yes/No)

Q: Do you have any additional or unspecified privilege? If yes, please mention it.

The six clinical scenarios included:

Of the six scenarios, only scenario #4 (Class III restoration on tooth #11) is officially included within the MOH’s defined core privileges for general dental practitioners. To ensure that all participating dentists had no additional clinical privileges, the questionnaire began by asking participants whether they held any additional or unspecified privileges.

The questionnaire underwent expert review by two professionals in clinical governance and dental regulation prior to pilot testing with a small sample (n = 15) of general dentists. Revisions were made to enhance clarity and contextual relevance, including clarifying that “impaction” referred specifically to soft-tissue impaction and adding photos and X-rays to the relevant clinical scenarios to improve understanding.

Based on a moderate effect size and statistical power of 80%, a sample size of approximately 230–240 participants were targeted. The questionnaire was submitted to the research offices of the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS) and the Saudi Dental Society (SDS), which used their internal random distribution systems to disseminate the survey to registered general dentists. While the exact randomization algorithm was managed by SCFHS and SDS, participation was voluntary and therefore the final sample represents a randomly invited but self-selected group. All participants provided informed consent prior to accessing the survey and no identifying personal data were collected.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Responses were collected using Google Forms and exported to IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to summarize demographic variables and response patterns. Associations between privilege awareness, perceived competence, and self-reported clinical practice were analyzed using chi-square (χ2) tests across each of the six clinical scenarios. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Scenario 2: placement of a single unit crown on tooth #36 after successful RCT

For this scenario, 73% of respondents reported they would perform the placement of a single unit crown after root canal therapy in their clinical practice, whereas 27% would refrain from doing so (Table 2). Interestingly, more than half (54%) assumed they were privileged to carry out this procedure, despite it not being within the authorized scope for general practitioners. Additionally, 21% were unsure about their privilege status. When it comes to perceived clinical capability, a large majority (85%) believed they had the necessary skills, while 15% did not share that confidence (Table 2).

A statistically significant association was observed between awareness of privileges and real practice (χ2 = 82.88, p = 0.001), as almost 90% of those who perform this treatment were not fully aware of the correct privileges (Table 3).

A strong association was found between perceived competence and clinical practice (χ2 = 25.27, p = 0.001) (Table 4). Most of those who perform the treatment felt that they had the required skills, however, interestingly 66% of those who don’t perform this treatment believed that they had the required skills to do it (Table 4).

Scenario 3: In-office vital tooth bleaching

In this scenario, 76% of participants stated they would perform in-office vital tooth bleaching in their clinic, while 24% said they would not (Table 2). Although general practitioners are not officially privileged to perform this procedure, 60% believed they were, and 18% were unsure of their privilege status. From a competency perspective, 83% of respondents felt the procedure was within their acquired skills, whereas 17% did not (Table 2).

A statistically significant association was observed between awareness of privileges and real practice (χ2 = 93.19, p = 0.001) (Table 3). Among those who perform this procedure, 75% mistakenly believed they were privileged to do this treatment.

There was a strong and statistically significant association between perceived competence and clinical practice (χ2 = 70.79, p = 0.001) (Table 4). Of those who perform this procedure in their clinic, 95% believed that they had the required skills to do it. Although only one quarter of the sample don’t perform this procedure in their clinic, almost half of them believed that they are competent to do it.

Scenario 4: class III restoration on tooth #11

In this scenario, 98% of participants stated they would perform a Class III restoration on tooth #11 in their clinic, and 95% believed it falls within their acquired skills (Table 2). Notably, 86% correctly reported being privileged to perform this procedure, which falls within the authorized scope of general practitioners (Table 2).

Scenario 5: root canal treatment on upper right first molar (tooth #16)

In this scenario, 49% of participants reported they would perform root canal treatment on the upper right first molar (tooth #16) in their clinic, while 51% said they would not (Table 2). Although general practitioners are not authorized to perform this procedure, 35% mistakenly believed they were privileged to do so, and 17% were uncertain about their privilege status. In terms of perceived competence, 67% felt the procedure fell within their acquired skills, while 33% did not (Table 2).

A statistically significant association was found between awareness of privileges and clinical practice (χ2 = 80.53, p = 0.001) (Table 3). Among those who perform this treatment in the clinic, 21% were aware of the fact this treatment is beyond their privileges, while 61% incorrectly believed they were privileged to do this procedure.

Perceived competence was significantly associated with actual practice (χ2 = 63.04, p = 0.001) (Table 4). As most of the sampled dentists felt the procedure falls within their required skills, 91% of those who perform this treatment in their clinic, believed that they have the required skills to do it, while 43% of those who don’t perform this procedure in the clinic had the same perception of self competence.

Scenario 6: clear aligner treatment for diastema and spacing

In this scenario, only 9% of participants indicated they would provide clear aligner treatment for diastema and spacing in their clinic, whereas 91% would not (Table 2). Despite not being authorized to perform this procedure, 9% of general practitioners believed they were privileged to do so, and 11% were uncertain about their status. Regarding perceived competence, 19% felt the procedure was within their acquired skills, while 81% disagreed (Table 2).

There was a statistically significant association between awareness of privileges and clinical practice (χ2 = 103.16, p = 0.001) (Table 3). Among those who perform this treatment, 64% incorrectly assumed that they were privileged to do this treatment.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to shed the light on the area of clinical dental privileges in Saudi Arabia, especially the awareness, understanding and application among general dentists. Although the majority of respondents (72%) indicated awareness of MOH clinical dental privileges, the responses on the detailed scenario-based questions showed a significant mismatch between actual practice and authorized scopes of care. There was a consistent theme across multiple scenarios reflecting that many dentists perform procedures outside their core privileges, often due to lack of awareness on the privileges or believing in their clinical competency to perform that specific procedure. This is an important takeaway from this study, that can help tackle the issues of practicing beyond the dental privileges, which can lead to several consequences, including, but not limited to, legal liability for the dentist and potential risks to patient safety (Alsaeed et al. 2022).

This study assessed six clinical scenarios involving different dental procedures, of which only one procedure was within the core privileges of general dentists in SA (Scenario 4: Class III Restoration on Tooth #11). The remaining procedures lie outside the authorized scope for general practitioners (Credentialing and Privileging Regulations 2025, Dental License Requirements 2025). Among these, the most common procedures that were performed beyond the approved privilege set were:1. in-office vital tooth bleaching, 2. placement of a single unit crown on tooth #36 after successful root canal treatment (RCT) and 3. RCT on upper right first molar (tooth #16). Interestingly, a previous study on the prevalence of dental malpractice lawsuit cases in SA found that most of the cases involved general dentists, with the majority linked to procedures in prosthodontics or endodontics—areas closely aligned with the treatments reported in this study (Alsaeed et al. 2022).

On the other hand, many general dentists argue that procedures such as in-office vital tooth bleaching, single-unit crowns, root canal therapy on molars were taught in their undergraduate dental education. Indeed, many dental curricula worldwide—including in Saudi Arabia—include clinical exposure to such treatments during the 4–5 years of formal training. For endodontics, previous studies in Saudi Arabia have shown that all participating universities in the study included clinical training on RCT for molars, with approximately 90% of dental schools setting a minimum requirement for completing RCTs on molars (Alobaid et al. 2022) (Algahtani et al. 2022). As for prosthodontics, although there is no evidence documenting the minimum requirement for single unit crowns, it is widely recognized that this procedure is considered a fundamental component of dental education. As such, many general dentists may feel justifiably competent to perform these procedures in their private clinics.

That said, the findings of this study highlight key gaps in the awareness, understanding, and application of MOH dental clinical privileges among general dentists in Saudi Arabia. There is an obvious need to improve communication strategies with dentists regarding clinical privileges and to enhance the accessibility of MOH privileges guidelines. Although almost three quarters of the participated dentists were aware of the dental privileges, the scenarios reflected a misunderstanding on the authorized scope of care. It is also needed to integrate privilege education into undergraduate dental curricula. Moreover, it might be advisable that MOH review the current core privileges, involving policy makers, accredited agencies and dental colleges in coordinated dialogue to determine whether current privilege classifications remain valid. If a significant proportion of general dentists had training in certain procedures during dental school but are later restricted from performing independently after graduation, this might lead to a potential misalignment between standards of education and regulatory scope of care.

In countries such as the United States and the United Kingdom, all the clinical scenarios discussed in this study generally fall within the scope of practice for general practitioners, provided they have received adequate training and are competent to perform the procedures (GDC Scope of Practice 2013; Gilbert et al. 2015; ADA Commission on Dental Accreditation 2022). In contrast, within the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, the scope of treatment varies considerably, with Saudi Arabia having one of the most restrictive systems for general practitioners. For example, in the United Arab Emirates (UAE), general dentists are permitted to perform single crowns and in-office vital bleaching (UAE Department of Health 2023). Regarding root canal treatment for first molars, UAE regulations define a “simple endodontic case” as one that meets the American Association of Endodontists’ criteria for low or moderate difficulty, which theoretically may include first molars (American Association of Endodontists 2022). However, the regulations do not explicitly state whether general practitioners are permitted to perform such treatments (UAE Department of Health 2023). In Qatar, general practitioners are allowed to perform bleaching and single-unit crowns but are generally not permitted to treat first molars according to national guidelines (Qatar Ministry of Public Health 2023). It is important to emphasize that the aim of this study is not to advocate for an expansion of the scope of treatment for general dentists in Saudi Arabia, but rather to assess their awareness, perceived competence, and current practice related to clinical dental privileges, and to call for a comprehensive review of the existing clinical privileges system.

The limitation of this study is mainly on the cross-sectional design and the limited ability to establish causal relationships between awareness, attitude, and practice domains. Although the questionnaire was randomly distributed through the research offices of SCFHS and SDS, most of the respondents had less than 5 years of experience. This limits the ability to thoroughly assess the impact of professional experience on awareness of dental privileges. However, since most of the samples were performing beyond the scope of dental privileges, it is plausible that a more experienced sample, potentially more confident, might exhibit even higher rates of practicing beyond defined privilege limits. Also, the study relied on self-reported data, which can lead participants to overestimate their competency or underreport unauthorized clinical practices. Despite these limitations, the study provides a valuable foundation for policy refinement, educational interventions, and future research on clinical privileging in the dental profession in Saudi Arabia that align with the goals of Saudi Vision 2030.

Conclusion

In summary, while most of the sample reported awareness of MOH dental clinical privileges, significant gaps remain between their awareness, perceived competence, and actual clinical practice. The majority of the sample perform procedures beyond their authorized scope of care, often due to misunderstandings about privileges’ boundaries or confidence in their skills. These findings urge the need for better communication between MOH and dentists and dental clinics, updated guidelines with involvement of stakeholders from different institutions and integration of privilege education into dental curricula to ensure safe and high-quality dental care aligned with national standards and patient safety goals.
