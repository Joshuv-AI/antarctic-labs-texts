Burden of opioid use disorder in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA): findings from Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study
Authors: Mohammad Effatpanah, Homa Seyedmirzaei, Hossein Karami, Pegah Rasoulian, Reza Mehrizi, Khashayar Danandeh, Leila Ghamkhar, Zeynab Efatpanah, Amin Nakhostin-Ansari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpioid Use Disorders (OUDs) present critical health challenges across the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. The primary objective of this study is to assess the prevalence and burden of OUDs within the MENA region.MethodsIn the current study, we used the Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) 2019 study data. In the GBD study, OUDs were defined as the ICD-10 codes of F11.0-F11.9, P96.1, and R78.1 and the DSM-IV (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition) code of 304.00. We retrieved the prevalence, death, and disability-adjusted life year (DALY) crude rates and age-standardized rates for OUDs throughout MENA countries, the MENA area, and globally, across years (from 1990 to 2019), genders, and age groups.ResultsAge-standardized DALY rate of OUDs in MENA increased by 40.6%, from 104.4 (95% UI: 78.2–136.0) in 1990 to 147.0 (95% UI: 111.8-187.7) per 100,000 people in 2019. In 2019, the highest DALY rate of OUDs for both males (411.3 per 100000) and females (330.2 per 100000) and in total (372.3 per 100000) was in the 25–29 years age group. In 2019, the highest age-standardized DALY rates of OUDs were UAE (373.9 per 100000, 95% UI: 278.2-481.7), Libya (341.1 per 100000, 95% UI: 248.9-435.8), and Iran (281.5 per 100000, 95% UI: 221.1–349.0). Age-standardized DALY rates decreased in Qatar (-10.2%) and Iraq (-1.9%) from 1990 to 2019. While, age-standardized DALY rates increased in other countries, with the highest increases being in Libya (167.3%), Lebanon (121.1%), and UAE (113.31%).ConclusionsThe burden of OUDs has increased from 1990 to 2019 in the MENA region. These results necessitate new governmental policies and global collaborations to effectively control opioid production and trafficking and manage their negative health-related outcomes.Keywords: Burden, Epidemiology, Middle east, North africa, Opioid use disorders

Introduction

Opioids, derived from the opium poppy, have historically been utilized for their medicinal properties, primarily in the management of acute and chronic pain [1, 2]. However, the non-medical use of opiates, whether from natural sources like the opium poppy, pharmacological agents such as hydrocodone, or illicitly manufactured substances like heroin and fentanyl, can lead to dependency and addiction. These disorders pose substantial health and socio-economic challenges [3–5]. The incidence of Opioid Use Disorders (OUDs) has increased from 1990 to 2017, and in 2017, they had the highest incidence rate compared to other drug use disorders [6]. The high prevalence and incidence of OUDs impose an economic burden on societies. A 2017 study found that OUDs and fatal opioid overdoses were associated with costs amounting to $1.02 trillion in the US [7].

OUDs present a significant health challenge, particularly in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region [8, 9]. A recent study on the epidemiology and burden of substance use disorders in the MENA region found that OUDs had the highest age-standardized disability-adjusted life year (DALY) rates across all MENA countries compared to other types of substance use disorders. Similarly, among individuals aged 15 years and older, OUDs had higher DALY rates compared to other substance use disorders [10]. On the other hand, the rising abuse of opiates in the MENA region is not only worsening public health issues but also impacting the social cohesion and economic stability of these nations [11–13]. These findings highlight the substantial burden and public health importance of OUDs in the region and emphasize the need for further studies to identify high-risk groups in the specific MENA region, which is essential for designing and implementing targeted interventions. This is particularly important as some treatment options, such as opioid agonist treatment, are only available in a few countries in the region, and there is a need to identify the countries and groups that may benefit the most from the allocation of resources [9]. Despite the well-documented harmful effects of OUDs, comprehensive solutions to address this issue remain insufficient. This is partly due to the lack of regional and national data on the prevalence and nature of opiate use and its associated impacts [8, 14–16]. While some studies have examined the epidemiology of OUDs in the MENA region as a whole or in individual MENA countries, there is no comprehensive comparative study on the burden of OUDs in the region and across different demographic groups and countries [17–19]. This knowledge gap makes it difficult to develop effective policies and put interventions into place that are specific to the cultural and socioeconomic conditions of the MENA region and the novel ones adapted from more developed countries [20].

To summarize, as the global burden of OUDs grows, accurate and comprehensive epidemiological data are essential for understanding its evolving impact in the MENA region. However, there is no comprehensive study to compare the burden of OUDs across different MENA countries, different demographic groups, and across years to guide future research and practice. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the epidemiology and burden of OUDs in the MENA region from 1990 to 2019. The primary objectives were to identify trends in prevalence, mortality, and associated burden of OUDs over this 30-year period.

Methods

Source

In the current study, we used the Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) 2019 study data to examine the burden of OUDs in the MENA region from 1990 to 2019. The methodology for the GBD study has been reported previously [21, 22]. In brief, the Institute of Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) performed the GBD project, which assessed the burden and epidemiology of 87 risk factors as well as 369 diseases and injuries and 87 risk factors across 204 countries and territories [21, 22].

Definition of condition

In the GBD 2019 study, OUD was defined as a substance use disorder characterized by a dysfunctional pattern of opioid use, with the ICD-10 codes of F11.0-F11.9, P96.1, and R78.1 and DSM-IV (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition) code of 304.00. The input data was based on a systematic review of studies evaluating the epidemiological aspects of OUD, including incidence and prevalence, in representative population-based studies, as well as drug service data and data from surveys. After applying age and sex splitting for prevalence estimation, data adjustments were made to account for differences in measurement methods. The GBD study recognizes two direct and an indirect method for assessing opioid dependence, with the latter considered the gold standard, as the direct method tends to underestimate the true prevalence of OUDs. Therefore, to account for these differences, an adjustment factor (beta coefficient = − 1.05) derived from MR-BRT (meta-regression—Bayesian, regularized, trimmed) was applied to prevalence estimates obtained through direct methods. It should also be noted that in the GBD study, no incidence of OUDs was assumed in the ages less than 15 or over 64. In the DisMod meta-regression model for the estimation of the prevalence of OUDs, intravenous drug use prevalence, excess mortality rate due to intravenous drug use prevalence, and opioid consumption per inhabitant per day were included as covariates [21].

For the estimation of the deaths attributable to OUDs, ICD-10 codes of F11.0-F11.9 and T40.0-T40.6 were used, with the input data being from vital registries, surveillance, and verbal autopsies. Cause of death ensemble modelling was used to model the deaths attributable to OUDs, Covariates included in the model were intravenous drug use prevalence, opioid consumption per inhabitants per day, prevalence of smoking, cumulative cigarettes, opium cultivation bin, access to health care and its quality, education, sociodemographic index, and lag-distributed income [21].

Finally, to estimate the years lived with disability (YLD) rates, the prevalence of OUDs at each severity level was multiplied by its corresponding disability weight (0.335 for mild severity and 0.697 for moderate to severe). DALYs were then calculated by summing YLD and YLL (years of life lost) [21].

Indexes

We used the GBD results tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results) to extract the burden of OUDs in the MENA region and countries from 1990 to 2019. MENA countries include Afghanistan, Algeria, Bahrain, Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Sudan, Syria, Tunisia, Turkey, United Arab Emirates (UAE), and Yemen. We retrieved the prevalence, death, and DALY crude and age-standardized rates for OUDs throughout MENA countries, the MENA region , and globally, across years (from 1990 to 2019), genders, and age groups. Furthermore, we retrieved the proportion of all-cause DALY rates that were attributable to OUDs. All of these measures were accompanied by 95% uncertainty intervals (UI). In the GBD study, 1,000 iterations were conducted for each measure to estimate the 95% UIs. The mean of these estimates was used as the main value, with the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles defining the bounds of the 95% UIs [21].

In the present research, we also employed the socio-demographic index (SDI), which quantifies a country’s development status based on its distributed income per capita, fertility rate of persons under 25 years old, and mean education years for adolescents and adults aged 15 and above [21]. We examined the link between DALY and SDI across nations throughout time. Furthermore, locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) regression was utilized to evaluate the predicted relationship between SDI and age-standardized DALY rates across MENA countries. We also assessed the trends in prevalence, death, and DALY rates using linear regression models with these variables as the dependent variable and year as the independent one.

To produce all of the figures and maps, we utilized Python versio 3.8 and libraried, including Matplotlib 3.4.3, Pandas 1.3.3, Numpy 1.21.2, Scipy.stats, Geopandas, and Folium 0.12.0.

Results

The age-standardized prevalence rate of OUDs in MENA increased by 37.9%, from 188.5 (95% UI: 149.3-143.7) in 1990 to 259.9 per 100,000 people (95% UI: 210.9-325.1) in 2019. Similarly, the age-standardized DALY rate of OUDs in MENA increased by 40.6%, from 104.4 (95% UI: 78.2–136.0) in 1990 to 147.0 (95% UI: 111.8-187.7) per 100,000 people in 2019. There was an increasing trend in both age-standardized prevalence (beta = 3.176, p < 0.001) and age-standardized DALY (beta = 1.829, p < 0.001) rates of OUDs in MENA from 1990 to 2019. These increases in age-standardized DALY and prevalence rates of OUDs in MENA were similar to the global trends, where there were also increasing trends. In both 1990 and 2019, the age-standardized DALY and age-standardized prevalence rates of OUDs were lower in MENA compared to the world (Table 1).

In 2019, the highest DALY rate of OUDs for both males (411.3 per 100000) and females (330.2 per 100000) and in total (372.3 per 100000) was in the 25–29 years age group, which was similar to 1990. Across all age groups, the DALY rate was higher in males than females in 2019.

The highest prevalence rate of OUDs in 2019 was in the 25–29 years age group for males (687.8 per 100000), females (718.9 per 100000), and in total (702.7 per 100000). In the 15–29 years and 65–74 years age groups, the prevalence rate was higher in females compared to males in 2019, but in other age groups, the prevalence rate was higher in males.

In 2019, the death rate was higher in males compared to females across all age groups. Furthermore, in males (8.5 per 100000), females (3.0 per 100000), and in total (4.7 per 100000), the highest death rates were in the + 95 years age group, which was similar to 1990 (Fig. 2).

In 2019, the highest age-standardized prevalence rates of OUDs were in Libya (639.9 per 100000, 95% UI: 525.8-772.6), UAE (540.3 per 100000, 95% UI: 459.4-657.1), and Lebanon (506.6 per 100000, 95% UI: 424.4- 608.7). On the other hand, the lowest age-standardized prevalence rates were in Yemen (125.5 per 100000, 95% UI: 103.0-153.9), Sudan (142.2 per 100000, 95% UI: 116.2-178.7), and Iraq (161.7 per 100000, 95% UI: 133.8-196.3). The age-standardized prevalence rate rose across all countries from 1990 to 2019, with the largest increases observed in Libya (167.1%), Lebanon (123.4%), and Tunisia (110.5%). The only exceptions were Qatar (-10.6%) and Iraq (-18.6%).

In 2019, the highest age-standardized DALY rates of OUDs were UAE (373.9 per 100000, 95% UI: 278.2-481.7), Libya (341.1 per 100000, 95% UI: 248.9-435.8), and Iran (281.5 per 100000, 95% UI: 221.1–349.0). On the other hand, the lowest age-standardized DALY rates were in Egypt (75.0 per 100000, 95% UI: 49.3–106.0), Oman (82.3 per 100000, 95% UI: 56.1-114.3), and Yemen (88.0 per 10000, 95% UI: 66.7-111.6). Age-standardized DALY rates decreased in Qatar (-10.2%) and Iraq (1.9%) from 1990 to 2019. While, age-standardized DALY rates increased in other countries, with highest increases being in Libya (167.3%), Lebanon (121.1%), and UAE (113.31%) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, the present study is the first GBD study to assess the burden of OUDs in MENA. We reported age-standardized prevalence, DALY, and death rates in this region and showed their increased burden in 2019 compared to 1990. We revealed that the burden of OUDs was higher in males, except for 1990–1993, when females had higher rates of age-standardized prevalence. Moreover, the 25–29 age group had the highest age-standardized DALY and prevalence rates in 1990 and 2019, while + 95 years of age accounted for the highest age-standardized death rates. We also presented the burden trends in individual countries and showed that the SDI and age-standardized DALY rates had negative associations.

The MENA region has had a key role in worldwide opium use for more than 50 years, and, therefore, various factors should be considered when interpreting our results. As previously explained, Afghanistan and Iran, along with Pakistan, make up the Golden Crescent region, a principal global site for opium production and distribution [23]. In 2019, the overall income from domestic use, production, and exports of opioids was estimated at 1.2–2.1 billion dollars in Afghanistan [24]. This country produced more than 9000 tons of opium from the poppy plant in 2017, which drastically decreased to less than 8000 tons in the next year [25]. This change was in line with alterations in age-standardized prevalence rates of MENA in our study; this rate’s increasing trend stopped in 2017 and decreased afterward. This alignment might imply the direct effects of domestic production on the opioid administration in the MENA region. Furthermore, the establishment of UNODC (United Nations Office of Drug and Crime) strategies to reduce the accessibility and harms associated with opium use may be another factor that contributed to the decrease in the prevalence of OUDs in MENA from 2018 onwards [26].

There are various gender differences in the health outcomes of OUDs. Women show higher blood concentrations after equivalent doses of the drug and, subsequently, get intoxicated faster [27]. Moreover, women with OUDs experience higher rates of opioid adverse effects, such as sedation, dry mouth, nausea, dizziness, vomiting, and constipation [28]. Diagnosed women with OUDs have more psychiatric comorbidities, such as mood disorders, while men show more aberrant behaviors [29]. However, still, men still showed higher rates of OUD-induced death in our study, due to their significantly higher OUDs prevalence. The 15–29 and 65–74 age groups were the only groups in which the female prevalence exceeded the male in 2019. The reasons behind this observation are unknown. These age groups should be of focus in females when preventing and managing OUDs, especially since women are more likely to participate in economically driven risky sexual activities, share needles, and experience subsequent drug abuse in their early reproductive age, i.e., 15–29 years of age [30].

We also showed the trends of OUD burden changes in individual countries of the MENA region. We reported that Iraq and Qatar were the only countries having decreased age-standardized prevalence and DALY rates when comparing 1990 and 2019 data. The specific reasons behind the trends in countries need thorough investigation; however, the establishment of the first drug law and commission in Iraq in 2017 and the shift of Qatar policies defining drug use disorders as diseases rather than crime in recent years might have affected the burden [31, 32]. In addition to government policies against drug use disorders, wars, medical approaches to prescribing opioids, and the unique sociodemographic and cultural characteristics of each MENA country can contribute to the overall burden profile of OUDs [33, 34].

Finally, we also evaluated the SDI of MENA countries and the age-standardized DALY rates and showed that the expected line has a downward slope. It is in line with previous findings, as a systematic review by Draanen et al. in 2020 reported various sociodemographic factors associated with opioid-related overdose, namely criminal justice system involvement, income, employment, social support, health insurance, housing/homelessness, education, and composite measures of socio-economic status [35]. Although the overall analysis showed a negative association between SDI and the burden of OUDs in MENA, Fig. 4 of the study implied an upward overall trend for each country. The fact that a better socioeconomic status in a country might lead to better accessibility of the people to drugs might explain this phenomenon.

The results of this study should be interpreted considering some limitations. We acquired the study data from the GBD database, which relies on the estimations of the burden of diseases instead of precise evaluations in countries. This limitation is especially prominent in developing countries, including the MENA region, as they have limited epidemiological data available [36]. This is, in particular, mentioned in the GBD study that the majority of the input data for the estimation of the burden of OUDs comes from developed countries, such as European and North American countries. Moreover, while by making adjustments, efforts have been made in the GBD study to reduce the effects of underreporting, opium and overall drug use disorders are considered stigmatized disorders, making the GBD estimates prone to reporting bias [37]. Therefore, while the current study offers a foundation to inform research, policy, and practice, further studies are still needed to more accurately assess the burden of OUDs in the MENA region. Finally, we were not able to separate the burden of smoked, eaten, and injected forms of opioids in our results as GBD did not provide such data.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we presented the first GBD study to assess the burden of OUDs in the MENA region from 1990 to 2019. According to our findings, the age-standardized prevalence, DALY, and death rates increased when comparing 1990 and 2019, but remained less than the worldwide burden of OUDs. These results imply that current policies against opium production, distribution, and trade are insufficient in MENA, and global and regional collaborations are needed to effectively reduce the burden of OUDs.
