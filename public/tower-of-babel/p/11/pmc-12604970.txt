Introduction

In recent years, phytomedicine has garnered increasing attention due to its multi-component synergistic effects and growing evidence-based validation [1,2]. Among the various factors affecting therapeutic efficacy, the efficiency of active component extraction plays a pivotal role [[3], [4], [5]]. To enhance this efficiency, modern extraction technologies have been continuously developed, among which ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) is the most representative, along with other methods such as microwave-assisted extraction and supercritical carbon dioxide extraction [6,7]. UAE acts on plant cells through cavitation, mechanical, and thermal effects, generating specific shear forces that disrupt the cell wall and thereby significantly enhance the extraction efficiency of target compounds [8]. However, the extraction process remains complex [9], influenced by multiple interrelated parameters such as time, temperature, pressure, and solvent ratio [7,10]. To rationally optimize these conditions, research on extraction kinetics is essential, as it reveals the underlying mass transfer and reaction mechanisms, providing a theoretical foundation for process optimization [[11], [12], [13]].

Indeed, extraction kinetics aims to study the dynamic process and patterns of the migration of active components from solid or liquid matrices to solvents. Its core lies in revealing the dissolution rate, mass transfer behavior, and influencing factors through mechanism analysis, thereby providing a theoretical basis for process optimization [14,15]. In this field, constructing a scientifically reasonable mathematical model is considered an important step in revealing mass transfer mechanisms and achieving process parameter optimization. Several common models have been widely applied in extraction kinetics modeling, including Fick’s second law model [8,16,17], second-order kinetic model [[18], [19], [20]], two-path kinetic model [[21], [22], [23]], Peleg’s model [[24], [25], [26]], first-order model [[27], [28], [29]], Weibull model [25,30], Patricelli model [31,32], Elovich’s model [33,34], power law model [25,35], film theory model [36,37], etc. These models can offer reliable references for optimizing experimental conditions [38].

However, the theoretical foundations for constructing and applying extraction kinetics models remain insufficiently developed, as evidenced in some of the existing literature [39]. The analytical expressions of some models are unclear, and the formulas are inconsistent across different studies [25]. Many researchers regard these models as independent systems, thus neglecting the intrinsic connections among them. Some of these models can be extended from other models by introducing additional parameters or adjusting the basic assumptions, while others are mathematically equivalent and can be converted through parameter substitution. Moreover, the absence of a technical platform results in researchers relying on only a few models without conducting comparative analyses. This can lead to the selection of inappropriate models, and manual parameter adjustments further complicate the process and reduce accessibility [40,41].

To address these issues, the relevant literatures on kinetics modeling for herbal extraction in phytomedicine are systematically analyzed. Rigorous and detailed mathematical derivations of the analytical solutions for each model are provided, and the intrinsic hierarchical relationships among the models are uncovered. Furthermore, a comprehensive multi-criteria evaluation of model performance across various datasets is conducted, based on which guidelines for selecting appropriate extraction kinetics models are proposed. To gain a deeper understanding of the relationship between ultrasound power—a key factor in the UAE process—and the intrinsic parameters of extraction kinetics, the selected optimal model was applied to experimental UAE data. The analysis systematically investigated the inherent correlation between the extraction rate constant and ultrasonic power. Finally, all models are integrated into a web-based platform with a user-friendly interface to facilitate convenient access and use by researchers.

Literature search and data extraction

The search strategy for the evidence

To obtain experimental data for extraction kinetics modeling, this study conducted a literature search in the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and the thesis database ProQuest. In ProQuest, the search was restricted to the Dissertations & Theses Global and Research Library collections to match the characteristics of the research topic. During the search process, multiple keyword combinations were used to ensure the highest possible accuracy. The final search query in CNKI was: “SU=('Extraction kinetics' + 'Kinetic study' + 'Mass transfer kinetics') AND SU =('Extraction' + 'Process optimization') NOT SU=('Fault diagnosis' + 'Adsorption' + 'Pharmacokinetics')”, where SU indicates the subject field. The search query used in ProQuest was: “(Extraction kinetics OR Kinetics OR Kinetic modeling) AND (Extraction) AND (Active compound OR Bioactive compound)”. Given that journal articles often lack complete kinetic experimental data, this study limited the search to master's and doctoral theses published before July 2025.

A total of 3450 documents were retrieved from the CNKI (895 documents) and ProQuest (2555 documents) databases. Although these documents satisfied the keyword search criteria, their content was deemed irrelevant to the present study. An initial screening based on titles and abstracts was performed, resulting in the exclusion of 2869 documents. Subsequently, the remaining documents were subjected to a systematic evaluation according to the PICOS criteria, leading to the exclusion of an additional 344 documents that failed to meet the inclusion requirements. Furthermore, 190 documents were excluded due to the absence of experimental data suitable for kinetic modeling. Ultimately, 46 studies fulfilling all criteria were included in the analysis. The document screening procedure is depicted in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of literature screening.

The screening was conducted independently by two authors. The first author reviewed the titles and abstracts of all the literature, while the second author randomly selected 10 % of the excluded reports for re-review to conduct a sensitivity check [40,41]. Each exclusion decision was verified by both authors, and no discrepancies were found. Finally, a total of 419 experimental data sets were compiled from these 46 studies, forming the basis for the subsequent analysis of model performance. Among them, the 16 datasets obtained from 4 studies were closely associated with variations in ultrasound power.

Analytical derivations and Interconnections

As summarized in Section 3, a total of 13 commonly used extraction kinetics models were identified, including Fick’s second law model, pseudo-first order model, the second-order kinetic model, the two-path kinetic model, Peleg’s model, the first-order model, the compartment model, the Weibull model, the Patricelli model, the Elovich’s model, the parabolic diffusion model, the power law model, and the film theory model. From a mathematical modeling perspective, these models can be categorized into three types: ODE models, PDE models, and empirical fitting models.

PDE model

In the process of extracting kinetic modeling, the most common PDE model is derived from Fick's second law, expressed as ∂C∂t=D∂2C∂x2+∂2C∂y2+∂2C∂z2, where D is the diffusion coefficient, ∂2C/∂x2, ∂2C/∂y2, and ∂2C/∂z2 represent the second spatial derivatives of concentration along the x ,y, and z axes, respectively. In the actual solution process, the above spatiotemporal model is typically simplified in the literature based on the following assumptions [47] (1) The distribution of components within the herbal particles is uniform, considering only radial diffusion and neglecting axial diffusion; (2) The herbal particles are regarded as symmetrical and homogeneous spherical structures; (3) The liquid within the reactor is fully turbulent and completely mixed, with mass transfer resistance negligible; (4) The active components diffuse simultaneously with other compounds, and there is no interaction between them.

Empirical fitting models

Besides ODE and PDE models, empirical models based on the shape of the extraction yield curve have also been adopted in some studies. These models are typically derived through fitting experimental data and aim to simplify complex extraction processes. By observing and modeling the relationship between extraction yield and time, empirical models offer a convenient and effective tool for practical prediction. Unlike mechanistic models, they do not rely on specific physical or chemical assumptions, but rather characterize system behavior in a data-driven manner.

(1) The power law model.

This is a commonly used empirical model, often applied to describe the relationship between concentration or other variables and time in various physical and chemical processes. Its expression is given by Eq. (18)

Multi-criteria comprehensive evaluation

In practical applications, uncertainty often exists regarding the suitability of various extraction kinetics models, primarily due to limited prior knowledge and insufficient systematic experimental validation. For example, multiple model assumptions are typically applied to interpret and describe the extraction process for a given experimental dataset. To address this issue, a data-driven modeling approach is employed in this section to systematically evaluate the comprehensive performance of 13 representative models across 419 experimental datasets. Through this comprehensive data mining analysis, this study aims to provide a robust and reliable basis for researchers to support model selection and mechanism inference.

Evaluation metrics

(1) The coefficient of determination (R2).

This coefficient describes the fit between experimental and theoretical values and is defined by the following formula

where Ci and C^i represent the observed (actual) and predicted values, respectively; C¯ and C^¯ denote the means of the actual and predicted values, respectively.

As a commonly used goodness-of-fit indicator, a higher R2 value—closer to 1—indicates better model performance. However, since R2 does not account for model complexity, it may lead to overfitting in the case of overly complex models. Therefore, it should be used in conjunction with other metrics to comprehensively evaluate model performance.

(2) The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).

This metric accounts for both the goodness of fit and the complexity of the model, and its formula is

where RSS denotes the residual sum of squares as RSS=∑i=1n(Ci-C^i)2, φ1 is the number of experimental data points, and φ2 is the number of model parameters.

AIC integrates the value of the likelihood function with the number of model parameters, incorporating a penalty term to effectively suppress overfitting[48]. Therefore, AIC is more suitable for comparing models with different structures and varying numbers of parameters. When comparing multiple models, a smaller AIC value indicates a better balance between goodness of fit and model simplicity.

(3) The residual normality significance test (pn).

In addition to AIC, the distribution of model residuals also plays an important role in model evaluation. In most statistical inferences and model assumptions, the model residuals should follow a normal distribution N(0,σ2). In this work, the Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of the residuals. If the p-value of the significance test is less than 0.05, this may indicate potential model misspecification or systematic bias [49]. Otherwise, the null hypothesis is not rejected, suggesting that the model assumptions are reasonable and that the results can be considered reliable. Therefore, the p-value obtained from the residual normality test is used as the third metric for model performance evaluation and is denoted as pn.

(4) The residual mean significance test (pm).

In addition to the normality assumption of the residuals, it is also necessary to perform a hypothesis test to determine whether their mean significantly differs from zero. Depending on the residual distribution, either a t-test or a nonparametric test (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank test) is performed to assess whether the mean significantly differs from zero[50]. Moreover, a p-value ≤ 0.05 indicates a significant deviation of the residual mean from zero, while a p-value > 0.05 suggests no significant deviation. Accordingly, the p-value from the significance test of the residual mean is used as the fourth model performance evaluation metric, denoted as pm.

The film theory model achieves the highest R2 value

As shown in Fig. 4(A) and Table S1, the film theory model has the highest average R2 of 0.9569 (95 % CI: [0.9476, 0.9652]). It follows from Table 2 that the R2 distributions of all models significantly deviate from the normal distribution (p<4.4057×10-34). Further analysis using the Friedman test yielded p<3.0982×10-102, indicating that at least two models differ significantly in terms of R2.

Subsequently, post-hoc comparisons were then conducted, as shown in Fig. 5(A). It follows that no significant differences were observed among the film theory model, the two-path kinetic model, and the Patricelli model (p> 0.5952), suggesting comparable fitting performance. In contrast, significantly better performance was observed for the film theory model compared to the remaining models (p<0.0004). In summary, the film theory model, the two-path kinetic model, and the Patricelli model were evaluated as the best-performing kinetic models based on R2.

The film theory model is the optimal model in terms of AIC

From Fig. 4(B) and Table S2, the film theory model has the lowest average AIC value of –69.01 (95 % CI: [–84.57, –55.17]). As in the previous subsection, the AIC distributions of all models show significant deviations from normality (p<1.9979×10-34), and the AIC differs significantly among at least two models (p<6.9197×10-13). Subsequently, as shown in Fig. 5(B), multiple pairwise post hoc tests indicated that no statistically significant differences (p>0.1673) were observed among the film theory model and the two-path kinetic model, the Patricelli model, and the Weibull model. Meanwhile, the significant differences between the film theory model and the remaining models (p<0.0275) indicate its markedly superior fitting performance. Therefore, based on the AIC, film theory model, two-path kinetic model, Patricelli model, and Weibull model can be considered the four best-performing models.

The residuals of the Weibull model are closest to a normal distribution

It is evident from Fig. 4(C) and Table S3 that the mean value of pn in the Weibull model is the highest at 0.4879 (95 % CI: [0.4593, 0.5167]). All models’ residual normality tests show significant deviation (p<3.4923×10-15). The Friedman test indicates significant differences between models (p<1.7279×10-24). Post-hoc tests indicate that the Weibull model and the film theory model, as well as the Elovich model and the Power law model, show no statistically significant differences between each other (p>0.0627), whereas the Weibull model exhibits significant differences compared with the other models (p<0.0406), as shown in Fig. 5(C). Although from a purely statistical perspective of residual normality, the Weibull model performs best, the criterion for the normality test considers a model to satisfy the normality assumption whenever p>0.05. As shown in Fig. 4, only the Fick’s second law model displays residual normality test results clearly clustered below 0.05, while the residual p-values for all other models—including the film theory model—are generally distributed above 0.05. This suggests that, according to this criterion, these models can be regarded as meeting the normality assumption.

The film theory model yields residuals with a mean (median) closest to zero

Similar to the analysis of the normality test pn, the film theory model shows the highest pm values in Fig. 4(D) and Table S4, with an average of 0.9752 (95 % CI: [0.9653, 0.9842]). Multiple comparisons reveal a significant difference between the film theory model and the other models (p≤1.214×10-300). If the threshold for pm is set at 0.05, then all models except the Fick’s second law model can be considered acceptable.

Comprehensive evaluation highlights the film theory model as the top choice

Based on multiple evaluation metrics, the film theory model demonstrated the best overall fitting performance in this study. In terms of R2, it showed high fitting accuracy, with mean values above 0.95 and narrow confidence intervals, indicating both precision and stability. It also produced the lowest mean AIC, suggesting a favorable balance between goodness-of-fit and model complexity. Although the Weibull model performed best in the residual normality tests (pn), the film theory model remains a practical and feasible option. Further residual analysis showed that its residual mean was closest to zero, and the corresponding pm values differed significantly from those of other models, indicating the least fitting bias. Overall, the film theory model consistently exhibited stable, accurate, and interpretable performance across all datasets and is recommended as the preferred model for kinetic modeling applications.

Stability analysis

To further explore the reliability and practicality of the film theory model in real-world applications, this section conducts a stability analysis. Experimental results are often affected by unavoidable factors such as operational errors, instrument inaccuracies, and environmental disturbances. As a result, the data inevitably contain noise, making it difficult to obtain completely accurate or ideal values. In such cases, the model's ability to resist interference directly affects the reliability of its predictive results, with model stability becoming a critical consideration. If a model is highly sensitive to input values, even minor errors can lead to significant deviations in prediction results, thereby reducing the model's practicality and robustness.

Specifically, noise was added to the datasets with varying intensities ranging from 1 % to 10 %, denoted by Δ. For each noise amplitude and each dataset, 10,000 samples were randomly generated. Using these perturbed datasets, the stability of the film theory model across four evaluation metrics was evaluated, as presented in Fig. 6.

Stability analysis results of the film theory model: (A) SR2, (B) SAIC, (C) Spn, (D) Spm.

As shown in Fig. 6(A), SR2 exhibits a gradual decline with increasing noise amplitude, indicating that bias has an adverse effect on the goodness of fit. When the noise amplitude is within 5 %, the error bars of SR2 remain relatively small, whereas beyond 5 %, the error bars increase noticeably, suggesting a more pronounced impact on R2. Fig. 6(B) shows that SAIC also decreases with increasing noise amplitude. However, when the noise amplitude exceeds approximately 7 %, its standard deviation no longer increases significantly, implying that the fluctuation of this metric tends to stabilize under high-noise conditions. In contrast, Spn in Fig. 6(C) is the most sensitive to noise, with its mean value exhibiting large variations and its standard deviation changing most markedly. It should be noted that the theoretical maximum value of the stability index is 1, and the relatively large error bars observed in the figure are mainly due to some metrics having very small values. As shown in Fig. 6(D), both the mean and standard deviation of Spm change only slightly across different noise amplitudes, indicating that this metric is relatively insensitive to noise. Overall, R2 demonstrates high stability under low-noise conditions, whereas AIC and pn are more sensitive to noise. These results suggest that the film theory model exhibits strong robustness under small noise perturbations, although certain evaluation metrics may experience significant fluctuations under high-noise conditions.

Ultrasound power and extraction kinetics

To further elucidate the influence of ultrasonic conditions on extraction kinetics, the film theory model, identified as the optimal model, was applied to experimental data from UAE to obtain three key parameters: C∞, α and k. The parameters were fitted and analyzed at different ultrasound power levels. Both linear and quadratic functions were used to examine how ultrasound power affects the kinetic parameters.

The fitting results of the film theory model are shown in Fig. 7, where scatter plots and fitted curves are based on UAE data. As shown in Fig. 8(A–C), which are based on the kinetic parameters obtained from the film theory model fitting in Fig. 7(A), the relationships of C∞, α and k with ultrasound power were further analyzed. The results suggested that the rate constant k exhibited a possible upward trend with the increase of ultrasonic power, which may indicate a potential association between ultrasonic intensity and mass transfer efficiency. Both linear and quadratic fittings yielded relatively high R2 values. However, due to the small number of data points (p > 0.146), the statistical significance of this trend was somewhat limited. For the equilibrium concentration parameter C∞ and the structural parameter α, satisfactory fitting performance was also observed in most cases. Similarly, their differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.171) because of the limited amount of data. The analysis of this study preliminarily verified the applicability of the film theory model in explaining the kinetic behavior under ultrasonic conditions. In addition, the potential relationship between ultrasonic power and kinetic parameters was also explored, providing a kinetic reference for understanding the role of ultrasound in enhancing mass transfer and improving extraction efficiency. The results further indicated that this effect may primarily occur through modulation of the rate constant k. However, due to the limitation of data volume, this adjustment mechanism is still not clear enough at present.

Graphical user interface design

The development of extraction kinetic models requires solid domain expertise and modeling capabilities. Moreover, achieving robust parameter estimation for these models involves a certain level of algorithmic understanding and programming proficiency. To reduce the technical barrier associated with model application and enhance modeling efficiency, this study presents an integrated graphical user interface (GUI) platform developed in Python. The platform modularizes a range of commonly used kinetic models and aims to offer a convenient and efficient analytical tool for kinetic studies in the field of herbal extraction.

Designed with a single-window interface, the platform supports experimental data import, curve fitting of extraction profiles, multiple model selections, and evaluation metric computation. It also enables quick export of results. Currently, it includes several widely applied kinetic models, such as the film theory model and the Peleg model. Users can carry out the entire process—from data preprocessing to model fitting and performance evaluation—through a fully visual workflow. This platform substantially lowers the barrier to entry for kinetic modeling in medicinal plant extraction research, while significantly improving modeling efficiency, parameter optimization, and fitting processes.

The platform is composed of several functional modules. (1) Data Import Module: Allows users to upload experimental data in standard.xlsx format (Fig. 9A). (2) Model Selection Module: Enables customization of the number and types of kinetic models to be fitted (Fig. 9B). (3) Result Calculation Module: Automatically generates fitting outcomes, including R2, AIC, pn, pm, along with the corresponding model parameters (Fig. 9C). (4) Result Export Module: Supports the saving of fitting results and associated graphical outputs (Fig. 9D).

Discussion

(1) Model Framework and Theoretical Relationships.

With recent advances in research on extraction processes, an increasing number of studies have focused on developing kinetic models to explain the migration and transformation of components during extraction [55]. At present, there isn't a single standard or systematic theoretical framework for choosing models, setting evaluation criteria, or ensuring data accuracy. This study systematically derived the mathematical formulations of the kinetic models and clarified their intrinsic relationships. For instance, the Patricelli model and the two-path kinetic model were shown to be mathematically equivalent. This clarification helps reduce unnecessary model construction and supports a more coherent interpretation of extraction kinetics. To build on these insights, we conducted a comparative analysis of the structural characteristics and applicability of different models. The results are intended to inform practical implementation and to support subsequent evaluation and selection.

(2) Comparative Evaluation and Robustness of Kinetic Models.

Subsequently, a comprehensive evaluation framework was established based on the four factors: goodness-of-fit, model complexity, residual distribution characteristics, and residual bias. Using extraction kinetic data from academic theses in both Chinese and English, this framework was applied to conduct a systematic analysis of the performance of each model in real-case extraction data. The results show that the film theory model works better and more consistently across a range of evaluation metrics. The mean values for the film theory model in terms of goodness-of-fit R2, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), residual normality test pn, and bias stability test pm are: 0.9569 (95 % CI: [0.9476, 0.9652]), –69.01 (95 % CI: [–84.57, –55.17]), 0.4660 (95 % CI: [0.4365, 0.4942]), and 0.9752 (95 % CI: [0.9653, 0.9842]), respectively. In conclusion, the film theory model is the best at modeling the kinetics of the extraction of medicinal plants. They should be used as the main kinetic models for these kinds of studies.

Note that there is some inconsistency between the different evaluation dimensions. For instance, the Weibull model does the best on the residual normality test, but it doesn't fit as well as other models. Some models, such as the film theory model, fit the data well but show slight deviations from normality in their residuals. However, their residual means (or medians) are close to zero, and the corresponding pm values are high, indicating that the errors remain within an acceptable range. This means that using only one indicator is not enough to fully evaluate model performance, and that a multi-dimensional joint assessment is a better way to choose the best model. Additional stability analysis shows that the key indicator response values of the film theory model stay mostly stable when there are changes of 1 % to 10 %. This is especially true in the R2 and AIC dimensions, where the effect of unit changes gradually decreases, showing that the models are very resistant to interference.

(3) Ultrasound Power and Kinetic Parameters

In this study, the UAE data were fitted using the film theory model. The potential relationship between dynamic parameters and ultrasonic power was further explored. The results show that the rate constant k increases slightly with increasing ultrasonic power, which implies that ultrasonic power may enhance the mass transfer rate to some extent. This phenomenon may be related to particle collisions caused by ultrasonic cavitation, which can accelerate the penetration and diffusion of solvents through the cell wall [16]. The limited variations in C∞ and α may arise from their stronger dependence on the solute content and structural characteristics of the system. The influence of ultrasonic power on these factors seems relatively minor. It is worth noting that although both linear and quadratic fits yield relatively high R2, the limited number of samples may not provide sufficient statistical power. Due to the limited amount of available data, it is necessary to investigate further the intrinsic relationships between ultrasonic parameters and kinetic modeling parameters in future studies. In addition, it is recommended that subsequent research on extraction kinetics should make raw experimental data publicly available to facilitate model validation and academic reproducibility. In a word, the results of this study tentatively support the applicability of the film theory model under ultrasonic conditions. The findings suggest that ultrasonic power may influence the rate constant k. This provides indicative evidence that ultrasound can enhance mass transfer and extraction efficiency.

Recent research in ultrasound-assisted extraction (2023–2025) indicates that the field remains highly active. However, most kinetic studies treat ultrasonic power as a fixed variable rather than as a factor for systematic optimisation [56]. Few studies quantitatively analyze the relationship between ultrasonic power and kinetic parameters such as rate constants k or equilibrium concentrations C∞ [57]. In light of the present findings, this relationship warrants further investigation, as ultrasonic power may significantly influence mass transfer through cavitation effects. From a modeling perspective, recent studies mainly use PDE and ODE models, such as Fick's second law model and second-order kinetic model [58]. However, these models are typically used independently, clarifying their theoretical equivalence or hierarchical relationships. By contrast, this study systematically demonstrates that certain models can be transformed into one another through parameter substitutions or hypothesis adjustments, revealing deeper mathematical connections between kinetic models. Furthermore, most studies still rely solely on R2 as an indicator of model quality, failing to comprehensively reflect the balance between fitting accuracy and model complexity. This reliance on a single metric may lead to overfitting or model selection bias. This study introduces the AIC and residual-based significance tests (such as pm and pn) into the analysis, establishing a more systematic multidimensional evaluation framework. This provides a more reliable statistical basis for subsequent comparisons and validations of ultrasonic extraction kinetic models.

(4) GUI Platform Design and Application

Additionally, this paper developed an integrated herbal extraction kinetics modeling platform based on the model evaluation system built by the research institute. Along with functional modules for goodness-of-fit calculation, parameter estimation, and residual analysis, the platform incorporates several widely used extraction kinetics models, including film theory models, film theory transfer models, Peleg models, and others. Through a single interface, users can perform data import, model selection, parameter fitting, and multi-indicator comprehensive evaluation. This platform greatly reduces the barrier to entry for kinetic modeling by providing substantial benefits in terms of operational efficiency, visualization analysis, and result comparison when compared to conventional fitting methods. This platform provides researchers with an effective and standardized support tool that features excellent scalability and application potential. It is particularly useful for addressing real-world challenges such as large-scale experimental data from medicinal plant extraction, multi-model selection, and complex result interpretation. The platform can be further enhanced by expanding its model library and algorithm modules. In the future, it may be integrated with a database of medicinal plant extraction experiments to provide stronger data support and technical foundations for modeling and process optimization.

(5) Limitations and Outlook

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. Firstly, the data collected in this study mainly come from academic dissertations rather than peer-reviewed journal literature. During the literature search, journal articles were also included in the initial screening. However, since most studies did not provide complete experimental data, they were ultimately excluded from the systematic analysis. Relying solely on data from academic dissertations may introduce statistical bias, thereby affecting the robustness of model comparison and parameter estimation. Therefore, we encourage future researchers to make their original experimental data publicly available when publishing results related to extraction kinetics. Such data can be uploaded as supplementary materials or deposited in public databases to facilitate model validation, result reproducibility, and academic exchange. Secondly, in the analysis of the ultrasonic-assisted extraction, due to the relatively limited amount of available data, the statistical power is insufficient, and the stability of the results still needs to be further verified in a larger sample. The potential relationship between ultrasonic power and kinetic parameters remains an important direction worthy of in-depth investigation. Future studies with systematic experimental design and multi-source data integration may further elucidate the intrinsic mechanisms of mass transfer and kinetic behavior under ultrasonic conditions.

Conclusion

A four-dimensional evaluation framework to assess kinetic models of medicinal plant extraction was established. The four dimensions include fitting accuracy, model simplicity, residual distribution characteristics, and bias stability testing. Results show that the film theory model achieved high scores in all evaluation indicators and effectively characterized the kinetic behavior of the extraction system. The findings highlight differences in model performance under different evaluation criteria, emphasizing the necessity of multi-indicator joint assessment. Stability analysis further indicates that the film theory model still has robustness when dealing with small sample noise data. Furthermore, the analysis based on the ultrasonic-assisted extraction data indicates that ultrasonic power may affect the rate constant k, suggesting its potential role in enhancing mass transfer and extraction efficiency. Meanwhile, the developed GUI platform has achieved high-precision fitting across different datasets, demonstrating excellent generalization ability and application reliability. In summary, this study proposes a comprehensive method that combines theoretical derivation, practical verification and visual modeling, providing new insights and practical tools to support the systematic evaluation and process optimization of herbal extraction kinetics models.
