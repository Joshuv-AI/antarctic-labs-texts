Breast cancer radiotherapy in patients with autoimmune or cancer-associated myositis: balancing oncological benefit and immune-mediated risk
Authors: Sara Falivene, Simona Mercogliano, Nicoletta Bertolini, Luisa Costa, Mario Cascone, Emanuela Esposito, Piera Ferraioli, Rocco Mottareale, Vincenzo Ravo, Francesco Caso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer radiotherapy and autoimmune myositis intersect in a clinically complex setting in which paraneoplastic disease, pre-existing inflammatory myopathy, and radiation-related muscle injury may overlap. Dermatomyositis is the idiopathic inflammatory myopathy most strongly associated with malignancy, and breast cancer is one of the solid tumours most frequently reported in cancer-associated myositis, particularly in anti-TIF1γ- and anti-NXP2-positive subsets.MethodsA structured narrative review of PubMed and Scopus was performed from database inception to March 2026. Evidence was synthesised qualitatively owing to limited and heterogeneous literature.ResultsMuscle symptoms during breast cancer treatment may reflect autoimmune, paraneoplastic, radiation-induced, fibrosis-related, or recall myositis. MRI is the most informative modality for pattern-based assessment, with FDG PET-CT and ultrasound providing complementary value in selected cases. Although direct disease-specific evidence is sparse and derives largely from retrospective series, mixed connective tissue disease cohorts, radiobiological studies, and case reports, radiotherapy does not appear to represent an automatic contraindication when disease activity, multidisciplinary evaluation, and conformal planning are taken into account.ConclusionsBreast cancer radiotherapy in patients with autoimmune or cancer-associated myositis requires structured differential diagnosis rather than categorical avoidance. Diagnostic assessment should integrate timing, field distribution, imaging, serology, systemic features, and treatment sequence. Further studies should refine risk stratification, muscle-specific dose constraints, and biomarkers distinguishing radiation-related from autoimmune or paraneoplastic myositis.

Introduction

The association between idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs) and malignancy is well established, particularly in dermatomyositis (DM), in which cancer risk is consistently higher than in the general population [1–3]. Across cohort studies, registry analyses, and reviews, breast cancer is repeatedly reported among the solid tumours observed in women with DM or IIM, together with ovarian, lung, gastrointestinal, and haematological malignancies [2–7]. The temporal relationship is also clinically relevant, as malignancies are most often identified within the years immediately preceding or following myositis onset, with the highest concentration around the first year from diagnosis [8–10]. This temporal clustering underpins the concept of cancer-associated myositis (CAM) and is particularly important when breast cancer and inflammatory myopathy are diagnosed in close succession.

Among serologically defined subsets, anti-TIF1γ is the autoantibody most consistently associated with cancer-associated DM, and anti-NXP2 has also been linked to increased malignancy risk in selected cohorts [11–14]. Breast cancer is among the malignancies reported in these subsets, although much of the disease-specific literature remains descriptive and is based on retrospective analyses, small series, or case reports [11–17]. The clinical implication is not that a specific autoantibody establishes causality, but rather that serological profile may help identify patients in whom a paraneoplastic mechanism should be considered carefully.

In patients with breast cancer, the relationship with myositis becomes particularly relevant during treatment planning and follow-up. Radiotherapy is a standard component of treatment for many patients undergoing breast-conserving therapy and is also frequently delivered after mastectomy in selected risk groups. In this setting, muscle symptoms may reflect several biologically distinct processes: pre-existing autoimmune myositis, paraneoplastic or cancer-associated myositis, radiation-induced inflammatory injury or later fibroatrophy, and radiation recall myositis triggered by subsequent systemic therapy [18–31]. Because these entities may overlap in timing and presentation, their distinction is often challenging but clinically essential.

The diagnostic complexity is increased further by the fact that cancer treatment itself can be associated with immune-mediated muscle toxicity. In a recent case report, dermatomyositis and interstitial pneumonia developed during trastuzumab deruxtecan therapy in a patient with recurrent breast cancer, although a paraneoplastic contribution could not be excluded [18]. In a prospective real-world study of pembrolizumab combined with radiotherapy in early triple-negative breast cancer, grade 4 myositis was reported in one patient, leading to permanent treatment discontinuation [19]. These reports do not establish a uniform treatment effect, but they illustrate the complexity of interpreting muscle and immune phenomena in contemporary breast oncology.

For radiation oncologists, rheumatologists, medical oncologists, radiologists, and breast surgeons, the central clinical question is therefore not simply whether radiotherapy can be delivered in patients with myositis, but how risk should be stratified and how muscle symptoms arising during treatment should be interpreted. This requires integration of autoimmune phenotype, cancer context, treatment timing, imaging distribution, and radiotherapy exposure.

Aim of the review

This narrative review examines the interface between breast cancer radiotherapy and autoimmune or cancer-associated myositis. Specifically, we review: (i) the epidemiological and serological context linking breast cancer and inflammatory myopathy; (ii) the mechanistic overlap between myositis biology and radiation-induced tissue injury; (iii) the clinical spectrum of radiation-induced myopathy, radiation recall myositis, and paraneoplastic or autoimmune myositis; (iv) the available evidence on radiotherapy in patients with pre-existing myositis or related connective tissue disease; and (v) practical principles for differential diagnosis and multidisciplinary management during breast cancer treatment.

Methods

This narrative review was undertaken to synthesise the available evidence on the interplay between radiotherapy and myositis, with particular attention to idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, cancer-associated myositis, radiation-induced myopathy, radiation recall phenomena, and breast cancer. A structured literature search was performed in PubMed and Scopus from database inception to March 2026. The search strategy combined terms related to myositis and radiotherapy, including myositis, dermatomyositis, polymyositis, idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, connective tissue disease, radiotherapy, breast cancer, radiation-induced myopathy, and radiation recall.

Studies were considered eligible if they addressed one or more of the following domains: pathophysiological mechanisms relevant to inflammatory or radiation-associated muscle injury; epidemiological and clinical aspects of breast cancer-associated myositis; radiotherapy outcomes in patients with myositis or related connective tissue diseases; radiation-induced or radiation recall myositis; and radiological assessment of muscle injury in irradiated tissues. No restrictions were applied according to study design because the literature in this field is sparse and methodologically heterogeneous. Non-English publications and conference abstracts without accessible full text were excluded.

Because the available evidence consisted predominantly of observational studies, retrospective series, reviews, and case reports, a formal meta-analysis was not considered appropriate. The evidence was therefore synthesised qualitatively, with emphasis on biological plausibility, internal consistency, clinical relevance, and the distinction between direct evidence specific to inflammatory myopathies and indirect evidence derived from broader connective tissue disease populations.

Mechanisms of myositis and their intersection with radiotherapy

The interaction between myositis and radiotherapy is best understood as the convergence of two partially overlapping biological frameworks: the immune-mediated mechanisms that drive muscle injury in idiopathic and cancer-associated myositis, and the radiobiological processes through which ionising radiation induces inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and fibrotic remodelling. These mechanisms arise in different clinical contexts, but converge on shared domains of antigen exposure, cytokine activation, endothelial injury, interferon signalling, and aberrant tissue repair. This overlap provides a biologically plausible basis for the diagnostic uncertainty that arises when muscle symptoms develop during or after breast cancer treatment.

Pathophysiology of idiopathic and cancer-associated myositis

IIMs are a heterogeneous group of immune-mediated disorders characterised by skeletal muscle injury resulting from the interaction of cytotoxic T-cell responses, complement-mediated microangiopathy, innate immune activation, and autoantibody-associated pathogenic pathways [32]. Contemporary classification frameworks recognise myositis-specific autoantibodies as key markers of clinically and biologically distinct subsets, each associated with different patterns of muscular and extramuscular involvement, prognosis, and malignancy risk [32]. Autoantibodies such as anti-TIF1γ, anti-Mi-2, anti-MDA5, and anti-NXP2 are linked to discrete phenotypes, and anti-TIF1γ and anti-NXP2 are particularly relevant to cancer-associated disease [11–14, 32].

Within CAM, myositis is generally regarded as a paraneoplastic immune phenomenon arising from tumour-driven loss of self-tolerance. A plausible pathogenic model involves immune cross-reactivity between tumour antigens and structurally related muscle targets, with aberrant antigen presentation, sustained systemic inflammation, and tumour-derived immune signals amplifying both innate and adaptive immune activation [33, 34]. In this context, muscle inflammation is not simply coincidental to cancer but may represent an immunological consequence of tumour-host interaction. This framework also helps explain why CAM may present with rapid progression, prominent extramuscular manifestations, or incomplete separation from the oncological course [33, 34].

Radiobiological mechanisms relevant to muscle injury

Radiation-induced skeletal muscle injury arises from the combined effects of direct cellular damage, oxidative stress, endothelial injury, inflammatory activation, and dysregulated wound repair. Ionising radiation induces DNA double-strand breaks and generates reactive oxygen species, thereby activating cell death pathways and inflammatory mediators, including IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α [35, 36]. These early events alter the local microenvironment and affect endothelial cells, fibroblasts, stromal cells, resident progenitors, and infiltrating immune populations within the irradiated field [35, 37].

Microvascular injury is a central component of this process. Endothelial apoptosis, capillary rarefaction, and impaired perfusion promote tissue hypoxia, reduced regenerative capacity, and persistent inflammation [35, 37]. Over time, these changes favour activation of profibrotic pathways involving transforming growth factor-β, connective tissue growth factor, and downstream SMAD signalling, with consequent myofibroblast differentiation, extracellular matrix accumulation, and progressive fibrosis [38–41]. Although skeletal muscle is often considered relatively radioresistant compared with other tissues, radiation can impair satellite-cell function, alter myofibre composition, and reduce regenerative potential, thereby contributing to chronic weakness, stiffness, pain, and contractile dysfunction [20, 42, 43].

Radiotherapy also exerts important immunomodulatory effects. Cytosolic double-stranded DNA generated after irradiation activates the cGAS-STING pathway, promoting type I interferon production, dendritic-cell maturation, and antigen presentation [44]. This response is influenced by dose and fractionation. At higher single-fraction doses, generally above 12–18 Gy, upregulation of the DNA exonuclease Trex1 may degrade cytosolic DNA and attenuate STING-dependent signalling, thereby limiting radiation-induced immunogenicity [45]. By contrast, repeated irradiation below the threshold for Trex1 induction may sustain interferon-β production and promote CD8-positive T-cell priming, with implications for antitumour immunity and for the interaction between radiotherapy and immunotherapy [45–47].

Biological overlap between myositis and radiation-related injury

In patients with pre-existing or cancer-associated myositis, these radiobiological mechanisms are particularly relevant because radiation-induced antigen release, interferon signalling, and tissue remodelling may overlap with pathways already implicated in autoimmune muscle injury [21, 32, 44–47]. This does not mean that radiotherapy uniformly triggers autoimmune disease, nor that all muscle symptoms during treatment are immune mediated. Rather, it provides a biologically coherent explanation for why radiation-related injury, autoimmune flare, and paraneoplastic disease may be difficult to distinguish clinically. The mechanistic interface is therefore important not only for pathophysiological interpretation, but also for clinical attribution and therapeutic decision-making (Table 1).

Biological interface between myositis and radiation-related muscle injury

Effects of radiotherapy on muscle tissue

The muscular effects of radiotherapy form a continuum from acute inflammatory injury to chronic fibroatrophy and functional impairment. In breast cancer, this spectrum is clinically relevant because standard tangential fields, as well as chest-wall and regional nodal irradiation may expose portions of the pectoral and intercostal musculature to therapeutic doses. Although muscle-specific toxicity remains less well characterised than skin, pulmonary, cardiac, or brachial plexus effects, local pain, oedema, stiffness, weakness, and fibrotic restriction may all arise within irradiated tissues [20, 23, 42, 48, 49].

Acute and late radiation-induced myopathy

Acute radiation-induced muscle injury is initiated by DNA damage, oxidative stress, cytokine release, and microvascular disruption, leading to local inflammation and oedema within the irradiated field [35–37, 42]. Clinically, these early changes may manifest as pain, swelling, tenderness, or transient weakness affecting the treatment area. In some patients, these manifestations remain mild and self-limited; in others, they may evolve into more persistent symptoms.

Late injury is more closely associated with chronic hypoxia, impaired regeneration, fibroblast activation, and extracellular matrix deposition, culminating in radiation fibrosis syndrome and fibroatrophic change [20, 38–41]. Stubblefield described radiation fibrosis syndrome as a multisystem process involving not only muscle, but also fascia, soft tissue, and peripheral nerves, thereby contributing to pain, reduced range of motion, contracture, and functional decline in long-term cancer survivors [20]. In this framework, late radiation-induced myopathy is best understood not as an isolated muscular phenomenon, but as part of a broader chronic tissue-remodelling process.

Dose-volume considerations and contemporary techniques

In breast radiotherapy, substantial portions of the pectoralis major, pectoralis minor, and intercostal musculature may be included within low-to-intermediate and, in selected regions, higher dose areas, particularly when boost irradiation or regional nodal treatment is delivered [48, 50]. Although clinically validated muscle-specific dose-volume relationships have not been established, it is biologically plausible that larger irradiated muscle volumes and higher local doses may contribute to a greater burden of oedema, fibrosis, and functional impairment [35, 37, 42, 50, 51].

Contemporary treatment techniques may help reduce unnecessary exposure of adjacent normal tissues, including muscle, although the clinical significance of this specifically for myositis-related endpoints remains uncertain. Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) allow more conformal dose shaping in anatomically complex breast and nodal fields, and deep inspiration breath hold may alter thoracic geometry in a way that reduces exposure of selected chest-wall structures, especially in left-sided treatment [50]. In this setting, technical optimisation should be regarded as a reasonable strategy to limit avoidable normal-tissue exposure, while recognising that muscle-specific dosimetric evidence remains limited.

Distinguishing radiation-induced injury from autoimmune myositis

Accurate distinction between radiation-induced muscle injury and autoimmune myositis is critical because these entities differ in pathogenesis, prognosis, and treatment implications. Radiation-induced myopathy or myositis is typically restricted to the irradiated field and reflects local tissue injury driven by genotoxic stress, vascular dysfunction, and fibrotic remodelling [20, 38–42, 49]. By contrast, autoimmune myositis more often presents with symmetric proximal muscle weakness and may be accompanied by cutaneous disease, interstitial lung disease, arthritis, or other systemic manifestations depending on the clinicoserological subset [32].

Imaging is central to this distinction. MRI descriptions of radiation-related muscle injury generally show focal oedema, fatty change, or fibrosis confined to the treatment field, whereas autoimmune myositis more often demonstrates diffuse, multifocal, or symmetric involvement [22, 32, 49]. Serological assessment provides an additional layer of discrimination: myositis-specific autoantibodies support classification of idiopathic or cancer-associated myositis but are not features of radiation-induced disease [11, 14, 32–34]. In clinical practice, diagnosis therefore depends on integration of anatomical distribution, treatment timing, laboratory data, systemic features, and imaging pattern.

Development of myositis after radiotherapy

Myositis arising during or after radiotherapy encompasses several distinct clinical scenarios. These include direct radiation-induced inflammatory muscle injury, radiation recall myositis triggered by systemic agents given after radiotherapy, and coincidental or temporally overlapping autoimmune or paraneoplastic myositis. Although these entities may share clinical manifestations such as pain, weakness, oedema, and elevated muscle enzymes, they arise through different mechanisms and require different therapeutic responses (Table 2).

Radiation-induced inflammatory myopathy

Radiation-induced inflammatory myopathy represents the local clinical expression of tissue injury caused by inflammatory signalling, microvascular dysfunction, and remodelling within irradiated muscle [21, 35–37, 42]. Yahyapour and colleagues highlighted the capacity of ionising radiation to amplify cytokine signalling, alter vascular integrity, and promote inflammatory phenomena in susceptible tissues [21]. In muscle, this may manifest as a field-restricted syndrome ranging from acute inflammation to later fibrotic dysfunction. The condition is best conceptualised as lying on a continuum between early radiation-related tissue injury and later radiation fibrosis syndrome rather than as a completely separate nosological entity [20, 21, 38–41, 43].

Radiation recall myositis

Radiation recall myositis is a distinct inflammatory reaction confined to previously irradiated tissues and triggered by systemic agents administered after radiotherapy [28]. In breast oncology, this phenomenon is especially relevant because systemic treatment commonly follows radiotherapy and may include agents already implicated in recall reactions. Gemcitabine is among the most frequently reported triggers, and focal myositis within prior radiation fields has also been reported with paclitaxel, carboplatin, pazopanib, and sunitinib [22, 28–31]. The precise pathogenesis remains uncertain, but proposed mechanisms include persistent tissue sensitisation, altered vascular permeability, and immune reactivation within previously irradiated muscle.

The key distinguishing feature is the temporal relationship between prior radiotherapy and later systemic therapy, together with confinement of the inflammatory process to the earlier treatment field. Recognition of this entity is clinically important because it may mimic both direct radiation toxicity and autoimmune disease, yet management may require reconsideration of the implicated anticancer agent rather than escalation of immunosuppressive therapy alone.

Paraneoplastic or cancer-associated myositis

Breast cancer is a recognised trigger of paraneoplastic dermatomyositis and, less commonly, polymyositis or necrotising myopathy [12, 15–17, 24, 27, 33, 34, 52–55]. In this scenario, myositis reflects systemic tumour-associated autoimmunity rather than local treatment-related injury. The clinical challenge arises when myositis develops near the time of radiotherapy, because temporal proximity to radiation may encourage misattribution of symptoms to treatment. This distinction is particularly important in patients with high-risk serological subsets, including anti-TIF1γ-positive DM and selected anti-NXP2-associated phenotypes, in whom malignancy-related mechanisms should be considered carefully [11–14].

Paraneoplastic or cancer-associated myositis is generally suggested by systemic distribution, extramuscular involvement, autoantibody profile, and close relationship to cancer behaviour rather than by field-confined anatomy alone [27, 32–34]. Correct classification has direct implications because control of the underlying tumour is central to management, and delayed recognition may lead to both inadequate oncological assessment and inappropriate attribution of symptoms to local radiation effects.

Radiotherapy in patients with pre-existing myositis or connective tissue disease

Historically, radiotherapy in patients with collagen vascular disease (CVD) raised concern because early clinical reports suggested increased acute toxicity, more severe skin reactions, late fibrosis, necrosis, and impaired wound healing [43, 56, 57]. These concerns informed a longstanding perception that autoimmune connective tissue disease represented a major contraindication to radiotherapy, particularly in breast cancer.

Subsequent evidence has supported a more nuanced interpretation. Later retrospective series and reviews suggest that radiotherapy can often be delivered safely in selected patients with autoimmune or connective tissue disease, especially when disease activity is controlled and contemporary planning techniques are used [58–61]. In breast-conserving therapy cohorts, acceptable toxicity profiles have been reported in patients with CVD, challenging the earlier view that severe toxicity is inevitable [58]. Matched-control and case-controlled studies have likewise suggested that acute and late complications may be comparable to those seen in controls when underlying disease is quiescent and treatment is appropriately individualised [59, 61].

However, caution is required when extrapolating these findings to myositis specifically. Much of the published evidence concerns mixed connective tissue disease populations rather than inflammatory myopathy alone, and disease-specific data for dermatomyositis, polymyositis, or immune-mediated necrotising myopathy remain sparse [43, 56–61]. Accordingly, the available literature supports the view that pre-existing myositis should not be regarded as an automatic contraindication to radiotherapy, but it does not justify complacency. Risk likely varies according to disease subtype, baseline activity, organ involvement, concurrent immunosuppressive treatment, and the extent of planned normal-tissue irradiation.

From a practical perspective, treatment is best delivered during periods of stable or controlled autoimmune disease whenever feasible [57, 59–61]. Collaboration with rheumatology is important to assess disease activity, optimise immunomodulatory treatment, and interpret muscle enzymes or new symptoms arising during radiotherapy [26, 62]. Serial assessment of creatine kinase, aldolase, and muscle strength may assist in recognising disease flare or radiation-related injury, although these measures are not disease-specific in isolation [20, 23, 32, 62]. Technical planning should also aim to minimise avoidable dose to muscle and other vulnerable normal tissues through appropriate field design and conformal techniques, particularly in anatomically complex chest-wall and regional nodal treatments [50].

Radiological assessment

Imaging is central to the evaluation of myositis arising during or after breast cancer radiotherapy, particularly when symptoms, laboratory findings, and treatment timing do not clearly distinguish local radiation-related injury from systemic inflammatory myopathy. In this context, imaging should not be interpreted in isolation, but as part of an integrated assessment incorporating treatment history, clinical distribution, autoantibody status, and cancer behaviour.

MRI

MRI remains the most informative imaging modality for the assessment of muscle inflammation and for anatomical pattern recognition in the differential diagnosis of radiation-related and systemic myositis [22, 49]. Radiation-induced muscle injury typically appears as focal T2-weighted or short tau inversion recovery hyperintensity confined to the radiation field, reflecting oedema and inflammatory change within irradiated tissues [49]. T1 hyperintensity may indicate fatty infiltration or chronic degenerative change, while post-contrast enhancement may support the presence of active inflammation and increased vascular permeability rather than established fibrosis alone [20, 38, 49].

By contrast, idiopathic or cancer-associated inflammatory myopathies more often show diffuse, multifocal, or symmetric muscle involvement rather than a strictly field-confined pattern [32, 49]. MRI is therefore particularly useful for pattern-based discrimination, although overlap may occur and imaging findings must be interpreted in relation to the clinical setting.

FDG PET-CT

FDG PET-CT may demonstrate increased uptake in both radiation-related and autoimmune myositis because inflamed or regenerating muscle shows increased glucose utilisation [22, 49, 63, 64]. Its value therefore lies less in the mere presence of uptake than in the anatomical distribution and systemic context. Localised uptake confined to a prior radiation field supports radiation-induced injury or recall myositis, whereas diffuse or multifocal muscular uptake should raise suspicion for systemic autoimmune or paraneoplastic disease [25, 33, 49]. PET-CT may also be useful when malignancy progression or occult systemic inflammatory disease remains part of the differential diagnosis.

Ultrasound and elastography

Ultrasound is an accessible adjunctive modality for evaluating muscle architecture and focal oedema, although it is less sensitive than MRI for deep or complex muscle groups [49]. Elastography may provide additional information on tissue stiffness and therefore has potential value in assessing fibrotic evolution within previously irradiated muscle, consistent with the recognised progression toward fibroatrophy in chronic radiation-related injury [20, 38–41, 49]. At present, ultrasound-based techniques should be regarded as complementary rather than primary tools in this setting.

Therapeutic implications and multidisciplinary management

Management of myositis in the setting of breast cancer radiotherapy depends on accurate attribution of the underlying process. The central therapeutic task is to distinguish local radiation-induced injury, radiation recall myositis, paraneoplastic or cancer-associated myositis, and flare of pre-existing autoimmune disease, because treatment priorities differ substantially among these scenarios. In practice, this requires a multidisciplinary approach integrating oncology, rheumatology, radiology, rehabilitation, and, where appropriate, dermatology.

Management of radiation-induced inflammatory myopathy

Radiation-induced inflammatory myopathy should be approached primarily as a local treatment-related toxicity arising from inflammatory injury, vascular dysfunction, and progressive fibrotic remodelling within irradiated muscle [20, 21, 35–43]. Management begins with recognition of the syndrome, exclusion of systemic autoimmune disease, and assessment of functional impact. Supportive care, analgesia, rehabilitation, and longitudinal monitoring are central, particularly when symptoms remain confined to the radiation field and imaging supports a local process [20, 23, 49]. Because persistent inflammation may evolve into fibrosis and functional restriction, early recognition of pain, oedema, weakness, and reduced range of motion is important.

Management of radiation recall myositis

Radiation recall myositis should prompt careful review of the sequence of radiotherapy and systemic treatment, particularly when exposure to agents such as gemcitabine, paclitaxel, carboplatin, pazopanib, or sunitinib precedes symptom onset [22, 28–31]. Management is individualised and depends on severity, oncological priorities, and the importance of the implicated agent. The principal implication is that the inflammatory syndrome may require modification, interruption, or reassessment of the triggering systemic therapy rather than interpretation as primary autoimmune disease. Accurate recognition may therefore prevent inappropriate diagnostic escalation and facilitate better coordination between radiation oncology and medical oncology.

Management of paraneoplastic or autoimmune myositis during breast cancer treatment

When myositis reflects paraneoplastic disease or active autoimmune myopathy rather than local radiation injury, management priorities shift substantially. In paraneoplastic myositis, control of the underlying tumour is central because muscular and cutaneous manifestations may parallel tumour activity [27, 33, 34]. In patients with active autoimmune myositis, treatment generally follows established IIM management principles, including glucocorticoids as first-line therapy and the use of steroid-sparing immunosuppressive agents according to phenotype, severity, organ involvement, and treatment response [32, 62, 65]. Intravenous immunoglobulin and rituximab may be considered in selected severe or refractory cases within established IIM treatment frameworks [62, 65]. In the oncological setting, these decisions require careful balancing of disease control against infection risk, impaired wound healing, and the timing of systemic anticancer treatment (See Fig. 1).

Balancing immunosuppression and oncological control

Across all scenarios, the central management dilemma is the balance between immunosuppression and oncological control. Under-recognition of autoimmune or paraneoplastic myositis may lead to undertreatment of clinically significant systemic disease, whereas excessive attribution of local symptoms to autoimmunity may expose patients with radiation-related injury to unnecessary immunosuppression. For this reason, management should be based on integrated assessment of symptom distribution, timing relative to radiotherapy and systemic therapy, imaging pattern, autoantibody profile, disease activity, and tumour behaviour [18, 19, 23, 32, 60, 61, 65].

Whenever feasible, radiotherapy should be planned during periods of disease stability in patients with pre-existing autoimmune myositis, and treatment decisions should be made jointly with rheumatology [26, 60–62]. In parallel, radiotherapy planning should seek to minimise avoidable dose to muscle and other susceptible normal tissues while maintaining oncological adequacy, particularly in complex chest-wall and nodal treatments [50]. In this field, precision medicine should be understood not as reliance on a single biomarker, but as clinically integrated stratification based on autoimmune phenotype, malignancy context, radiotherapy exposure, systemic therapy sequence, and imaging-defined pattern of injury (Table 3).

Conclusion

Radiotherapy can be delivered to many patients with breast cancer and pre-existing autoimmune myositis or cancer-associated myositis, but only within a framework that recognises the biological and clinical complexity of this intersection. The available evidence does not support a simplistic view of radiotherapy as either uniformly hazardous or universally innocuous in this population. Rather, the balance of benefit and risk appears to depend on the interaction between autoimmune phenotype, disease activity, paraneoplastic context, systemic treatment exposure, and radiotherapy-related normal-tissue irradiation.

In clinical practice, the main task is not merely to identify muscle symptoms, but to determine whether they reflect radiation-induced inflammatory injury, radiation recall myositis, flare of pre-existing autoimmune disease, or paraneoplastic myositis. This distinction requires integrated interpretation of timing, field distribution, imaging pattern, autoantibody profile, systemic manifestations, and tumour behaviour [18, 19, 22, 23, 27, 32–34, 49]. MRI is central for anatomical and pattern-based discrimination, whereas FDG PET-CT and ultrasound-based techniques may provide complementary information in selected cases [22, 49, 63, 64].

The current literature suggests that pre-existing myositis should not be considered an automatic contraindication to breast radiotherapy, particularly when disease activity is controlled and treatment is planned with contemporary conformal techniques [50, 59–61]. However, this conclusion should be interpreted cautiously because disease-specific evidence remains sparse and much of the available data is indirect. For patients with active autoimmune disease or suspected paraneoplastic syndromes, close coordination between radiation oncology, medical oncology, rheumatology, radiology, and rehabilitation medicine is essential. When systemic autoimmune myositis is present, treatment should follow established IIM principles while remaining closely integrated with oncological priorities and treatment timing [62, 65].

The evidence base at the intersection of breast cancer radiotherapy and myositis remains limited. Much of the literature consists of retrospective series, mixed connective tissue disease cohorts, radiobiological studies, and case reports, with relatively little disease-specific evidence for inflammatory myopathies and even less for breast radiotherapy specifically [22, 28–31, 43, 49, 50, 56–61]. Outcome measures are heterogeneous, definitions of toxicity vary, and muscle-specific dose-volume relationships are poorly characterised. In addition, part of the available evidence is indirect, deriving from broader connective tissue disease populations rather than dermatomyositis, polymyositis, or immune-mediated necrotising myopathy specifically. These limitations should temper overly definitive conclusions and reinforce the need for cautious, individualised interpretation.

Future research should focus on prospective risk stratification, the integration of serological phenotype into oncological decision-making, the development of biomarkers capable of distinguishing radiation-related injury from autoimmune or paraneoplastic myositis, and the definition of clinically relevant muscle-specific dose-volume parameters in breast radiotherapy. Progress in this field will depend on multidisciplinary collaboration and on a more explicit effort to move from empirical caution to biologically informed, individualised care.
