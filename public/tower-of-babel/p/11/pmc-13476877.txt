Complete Versus Incomplete Revascularization in Patients With Severe Aortic Stenosis Undergoing Complex, High-Risk PCI: Insights From the ASCoP Registry

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComplex coronary artery disease frequently coexists with severe aortic stenosis. The prognostic impact of complete revascularization (CR) versus incomplete revascularization (IR) in patients undergoing complex and/or high-risk indicated percutaneous coronary intervention (CHIP) and transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) remains unclear.MethodsWe analyzed 550 patients who underwent CHIP and transfemoral TAVI between 2013 and 2023 at 14 international centers. Patients were stratified by completeness of revascularization. The primary endpoint was a composite of all-cause death and unplanned cardiovascular (CV) hospitalization at 1 year.ResultsCR was achieved in 299 patients (54.4%). Patients with IR more frequently had three-vessel disease, bifurcation lesions, left main disease, and need for mechanical support. Balloon-expandable valves were more frequently implanted in the CR cohort. Short-term outcomes were similar in the two groups. At 1 year, the incidence of the primary endpoint did not differ significantly between CR and IR groups (20.1 vs. 27.3%, hazard ratio [HR] 0.72, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.49-1.07). CV rehospitalizations were significantly reduced in the CR group (14.8 vs. 22.8%, HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.38-0.97) even after multivariable adjustment and excluding hospitalizations for repeat revascularization.ConclusionsIn patients undergoing CHIP and TAVI, CR was associated with a reduction in CV hospitalizations. Further studies are needed to confirm this finding.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is common in patients with aortic stenosis (AS) and is associated with worse clinical outcomes.1,2 When patients with AS are referred for transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), management of CAD poses several challenges in terms of indication, timing, and completeness of revascularization.3,4

In this population, the role of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) over medical therapy remains to be determined. Moreover, conflicting results have been reported on the impact of complete revascularization (CR) on clinical outcomes.5, 6, 7 In case of incomplete revascularization (IR), performing TAVI with residual coronary ischemia may jeopardize the procedure or undermine its effectiveness. On the other hand, in the context of severe AS, operators may prefer IR to reduce procedural length and ischemic time as compared to CR.

However, patients undergoing complex PCI have frequently been excluded from both observational and randomized studies, and multiple studies have shown how the prognostic impact of CAD in AS patients seems to be directly related to CAD complexity.2,8 Hence, current consensus on CAD revascularization cannot be generalized to patients undergoing complex and/or high-risk indicated PCI (CHIP). The Aortic Stenosis with COmplex PCI (ASCoP) registry has found that CHIP procedures are increasingly performed in patients undergoing TAVI and that a staged strategy is associated with less procedural risk and adverse events than a concomitant one.9

This retrospective analysis from the ASCoP registry aims to compare CR and IR in patients undergoing CHIP and TAVI.

Methods

Study Design

The design of the ASCoP registry (NCT05750927) has been described previously.9 Briefly, all consecutive patients that underwent complex PCI before, during, or after TAVI between January 2013 and June 2023 at 14 international centers were retrospectively evaluated for inclusion in the registry (Supplementary Table 1). The inclusion of patients in the study was approved at each institution by a local ethics committee or per local practice for the collection of retrospective data. Patients were eligible for inclusion in the ASCoP registry if they had severe symptomatic AS, were candidates for transfemoral TAVI by the Heart Team, and had concomitant complex CAD as defined by the presence of one or more of the following criteria.10,11(a)Left main (LM) CAD or proximal left anterior descending/left circumflex lesions requiring LM PCI;(b)Three-vessel disease PCI;(c)PCI of a last remaining vessel;(d)Bifurcation PCI (defined as “true bifurcation” including any proximal branch);(e)Severely calcified lesions requiring calcium debulking;(f)Lesion length ≥30 mm;(g)Revascularization in severely reduced left ventricular ejection function (LVEF) (≤30%);(h)Any condition requiring upfront mechanical circulatory support (MCS) during PCI.

Patients were excluded if emergent/urgent revascularization was deemed necessary (e.g., in the setting of ST-elevation myocardial infarction [MI]) if the time between PCI and TAVI was greater than 6 months or in cases of valve-in-valve TAVI.

For the present analysis, patients were stratified into two groups by completeness of revascularization. CR was defined as an intended treatment of all angiographically significant coronary stenoses (>70% stenosis in vessels more than ≥2.5 mm or >50% for LM) during PCI.12 Otherwise, patients were included in the IR group. Patients with successfully treated single-vessel disease were included in the CR cohort. The decision on patient treatment and valve model was solely clinical and at the physician’s discretion.

Endpoints

For the endpoints of the present study, the definitions used in the original ASCoP registry were used for consistency. The primary endpoint was the composite of all-cause death and first unplanned hospitalization for cardiovascular (CV) causes. The secondary endpoint was the composite of major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events that included all-cause death, stroke, acute MI, major bleeding, major vascular complication, and unplanned revascularization. The occurrence of both endpoints was evaluated at 30 days and at 1 year after the index procedure, defined as the last intervention performed (PCI and/or TAVI). We also investigated the occurrence of each component of the two study endpoints at 1 year and the rate of in-hospital events (all-cause death, bleeding, vascular complications, stroke or transient ischemic attack, and acute kidney injury [AKI]). For patients who underwent staged procedures during two different hospitalizations, in-hospital events occurring during both procedures were considered. All events were categorized according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3, Bleeding Academic Research Consortium, and Academic Research Consortium-2 consensuses.13, 14, 15

Statistical Analysis

Results are presented as mean ± SD or as median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and as absolute numbers (percentages) for categorical data, and all variables were tested for normality with the Shapiro–Wilk normality test and reported accordingly. One-way analysis of variance and the Student’s unpaired t-test were used to compare normally distributed continuous variables. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. The Chi-square and Fisher exact tests were used to compare categorical variables.

Cumulative incidence of the primary and secondary endpoints was assessed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between groups with the log-rank test for the time to the first event. For each endpoint, follow-up was censored at the date of the event or at the latest available contact date. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to evaluate the prognostic impact of completeness of revascularization on the primary and secondary endpoints. Results are reported as hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). The impact of completeness of revascularization on clinical adverse events was adjusted for relevant covariates in a multivariable Cox regression model, which included age, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and history of previous coronary artery bypass graft (CABG).

In addition, we conducted a propensity score (PS)–adjusted analysis using the PS as a covariate together with the treatment group.16 The PS predicting each patient’s probability of undergoing CR or IR was estimated with multivariable logistic regression including variables with a difference in their distribution between the two treatment groups or deemed to be clinically relevant. The following variables were included in the model used to estimate the PS: age, body mass index, diabetes, CKD, previous CABG, use of beta-blockers or ivabradine, LM PCI, and bifurcation PCI.

Prespecified subgroups analyses according to relevant covariates (namely, age with a cutoff of 75 years, Society of Thoracic Surgeons [STS] score with a cutoff of 4%, presence of angina, reduced LVEF, and timing of PCI) were performed.

As a sensitivity analysis, the impact of completeness of revascularization on CV hospitalizations was tested (using a Fine–Gray model with all-cause death as competing risk) after excluding patients who were hospitalized for a repeat revascularization (RR). In addition, the CR cohort was split to compare patients with single-vessel or multivessel disease, and clinical outcomes of CR versus IR were tested after excluding patients with single-vessel disease from the CR cohort.

The statistical analysis, the Kaplan–Meier mortality curves, and the graphs were performed with the use of Stata (version 18; StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).

In-Hospital Events

Around 99% of patients were discharged alive in both groups; medical therapy at discharge is shown in Supplementary Table 4. In-hospital events were similar in the two groups, with low rates of stroke (0.8% in IR and 2.3% in CR, p = 0.16) and relatively low rates of bleeding (10.8% in IR and 9.0% in CR, p = 0.50) and AKI (AKI, 6.8% in IR and 7.7% in CR, p = 0.68) (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to compare CR and IR in a complex cohort of patients undergoing CHIP and TAVI. The main findings of this study can be found in the Graphical Abstract.-Approximately 55% of patients underwent a CR. They were characterized by a lower incidence of multivessel and bifurcation lesions, a lower implantation of self-expandable valves, and a lower need of MCS as compared to the IR group.-In the overall cohort, no significant differences were found for the primary or secondary endpoints between the two groups. The concomitant treatment of CAD and AS is an increasingly common clinical issue, as TAVI indications expand toward a larger and younger population. Currently, PCI before TAVI is recommended in case of ≥90% stenosis in segments with a reference diameter ≥2.5 mm (class IIa) or >70% stenosis in proximal epicardial vessels (class IIb).3,17,18 Although the latter recommendation is based only on observational studies with conflicting results,19 the former is defined on the inclusion criteria of the recent Nordic Aortic Valve Intervention (NOTION) 3 trial—the only randomized controlled trial on the topic if we exclude the prematurely stopped Percutaneous Coronary Intervention Prior to Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation trial.20 NOTION 3 compared PCI to conservative treatment in patients undergoing TAVI and with functionally significant (fractional flow reserve ≤0.80) or subocclusive stenosis (percentage of stenosis ≥90%), and it showed a significant reduction in major adverse cardiac events in the PCI group. However, it did not address the issue of completeness of revascularization, as 89% of patients received the treatment of all the lesions. In the REVASC-TAVI registry, the largest study to date to address this issue, CR did not confer a benefit on clinical outcomes at 2 years.5 In the setting of complex CAD with multivessel disease and a large ischemic burden, whether or not the conservative approach proposed by current guidelines can be safely applied is questionable. Current evidence cannot be generalized to patients undergoing CHIP, as they were underrepresented (or did not represent) in available studies. Data on this population are relevant, as CHIP procedures are now performed with growing frequency thanks to greater experience and a wider range of available devices,21,22 and CAD complexity has been shown to impact outcomes in patients undergoing TAVI.6,8,23 In NOTION 3, patients with LM disease or severe CKD were excluded as well as those presenting with an acute coronary syndrome (ACS).7 Hence, the enrolled cohort presented a very low-risk population with limited coronary complexity. Similarly, the Percutaneous Coronary Intervention Prior to Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation trial excluded patients with a recent ACS, an unprotected LM stem disease, or a Canadian Cardiovascular Society angina class ≥III. In REVASC-TAVI, the included lesions were far less complex, as only 12.2% of patients presented with LM disease and 26.4% with bifurcation lesions. Multivessel PCI was performed in only 34.2% of patients in the IR group, and debulking was performed in only 4.9%.5 Moreover, relevant information including stent length and use of MCS was not reported.

By contrast, our population was characterized by extensive and complex CAD, not presented in previously available cohorts. Included patients had a relevant symptom burden (New York Heart Association class ≥III in more than half of patients and Canadian Cardiovascular Society ≥III in one of five) and multiple features of complexity, including LM disease (39.4% of the overall cohort), bifurcations (39.6%), and long lesions (39.4%). More than half of patients had more than one CHIP indication. As in previous studies, the IR cohort showed more diffuse disease.5 The fact that the number of treated lesions was comparable suggests that CR was obtained thanks to a lower disease extent rather than a more aggressive treatment. This is also concordant with the comparable rates of in-hospital complications, including AKI, and short-term adverse events. However, the benefit of CR on CV rehospitalization was confirmed even after excluding the patients presenting with a single-vessel disease from the CR cohort. Notably, the use of MCS was very low in both groups. This could be explained by severe AS being a contraindication to the use of microaxial flow pumps, the relatively low rate of patients with a severely reduced LVEF, and the overall stability of the included population (STS score: 4.4 ± 3.1 in the CR cohort and 5.2 ± 3.9 in the IR cohort; 30-day death rate: <2% in both groups).

A significant imbalance in the implanted transcatheter heart valve design was found between the two cohorts. Balloon-expandable valves allow for a higher rate of selective coronary cannulation as compared to self-expanding valves.24, 25, 26 However, the majority of our cohort underwent PCI as a staged procedure before TAVI, so the impact of valve choice on the PCI procedure is limited.

A relevant finding of our study is that CR was associated with a reduction in CV rehospitalizations. One could argue that baseline differences in lesion characteristics may have a role in the reduction of CV rehospitalization. However, our sensitivity analyses showed this was not linked to a reduction in MI or RR. Nevertheless, this finding remains hypothesis-generating. We may speculate that CR’s potential prevention of recurrent ischemia may lead to seeking medical attention and, sometimes, hospital admission. Congestion is frequently associated with myocardial injury,27 which may be more extensive in case of an IR.

Another relevant result is the beneficial effect of CR on the primary endpoint in older patients. Although PCI did not prove to be beneficial in an elderly population with non–ST-elevation ACS,28 a physiology-guided CR was found to be superior to culprit-only CR in elderly patients with any ACS.29 However, inclusion of CHIP patients was limited in randomized trials, and PCI was beneficial in elderly patients with complex lesions in an observational study.30 Given the context of multiple testing, our findings on the elderly subgroup should be considered exploratory. Notably, as is frequently reported in observational studies, the use of intravascular imaging was relatively low in the context of complex CAD.

Because of the design of the ASCoP registry, this analysis could not compare CR and IR to conservative treatment of complex lesions. This and other remaining areas of uncertainty regarding PCI in TAVI patients are currently being addressed in the Staged Complete Revascularization for Coronary Artery Disease vs. Medical Management Alone in Patients With AS Undergoing Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (NCT04634240) and the Functional Assessment in TAVI (NCT03360591) trials.

Study Limitations

First, our study is limited by its retrospective design, which did not include patients undergoing conservative medical treatment and entails the risk of selection bias. Second, retrospective definition of completeness of revascularization can be subject to errors in reporting significant untreated lesions.31 Third, operators’ choice in performing a CR or IR could have been influenced by local practice and/or factors not captured in our dataset. Fourth, PCI procedural length and contrast volume used were not reported in our dataset. The necessity to reduce contrast use in patients with CKD, who were more represented in the IR group, may have contributed to the choice of an IR. Fifth, specific causes of CV rehospitalization and data regarding antithrombotic therapy at follow-up were not collected. Sixth, since patients had to undergo both TAVI and PCI to be included in the registry, an underestimation of early fatal events is possible. In addition, the 1-year follow-up was relatively short despite its relevance in an old and comorbid population. Lastly, despite all those involved being high-volume centers, the long timeframe of observation (2013-2023) may include disparate learning curves, changing clinical practice, and evolving technologies.

Conclusions

In this retrospective international registry of patients with AS and complex CAD undergoing TAVI and CHIP, CR led to a similar rate of primary or secondary endpoint as compared to IR but was associated with a reduction in CV hospitalizations. Further studies are needed to confirm our preliminary findings.
