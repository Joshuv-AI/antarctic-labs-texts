Evaluation of a novel laser‐system operating at 1940 nm for weed control and determining the influence of biological and operational factors
Authors: Ran Nisim Lati, Neria Suliman, Rotem Nahear, Evgeny Smirnov, Avital Bechar, Gilad Marcus, Salman Noach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDThis study evaluates a novel laser‐based pulse‐system for weed control characterized by two primary innovations: a thulium‐doped yttrium aluminum perovskite (Tm:YAP) laser operating at a 1940 nm wavelength aligned with the peak water absorption band to maximize photothermal efficiency and the implementation of pulsed laser operation. The system's efficacy was examined across varying weed species (dicots and monocots), growth stages (2–3, 4–5, and 7–8 leaf stages), and target locations (growth meristem and stem), alongside technical parameters including beam diameter (0.1–4.7 mm) and exposure time (0.25–2.5 s) and compared against a continuous‐wave (CW) system.RESULTSTargeting the plant stem was significantly more effective than the meristem with mean biomass (proportion of control) values of approximately 0.0 and 0.25, respectively, following a treatment of 1.5 s with beam diameter of 1.6 mm. Across the majority of tested parameters, the pulsed configuration demonstrated no significant advantage over the CW system. Early treatment of the dicot Solanum nigrum at the 2–3 leaf stage resulted with 6% survival using energy density of only 1.5 J mm−2 (0.25 s exposure). However, sensitivity varied greatly by species; Abutilon theophrasti was significantly more susceptible than grass species (e.g., Sorghum halepense), and had a higher effective dose for a 50% response (ED50).CONCLUSIONWhile pulsed and CW modes yielded similar results, indicating that total energy delivery governs the thermal response, the 1940 nm wavelength demonstrated superior energy efficiency compared to previously reported systems. These findings provide a solid foundation for developing energy‐efficient, site‐specific robotic weeding technologies. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Weeds are one of the main biotic factors limiting crop yield, jeopardizing farmers' ability to ensure global food security. 1 While herbicides are the primary means to control weeds in most industrialized countries, interest in the development and adoption of alternative non‐chemical control methods has increased in recent years. This trend is driven by several factors, including growing concern over sustainability, an increase in herbicide‐resistant weed populations, reduced availability of active ingredients, and increasing demand for pesticide‐free food. Furthermore, currently available technologies offer site‐specific weed control, that is, precise application of control means on individual weeds or weed spots. 2 Such site‐specific methodologies can facilitate selective weed control using non‐selective tools without damaging the crop plants, thereby increasing available weed‐control options. 3

Laser‐based weed control has been suggested as an alternative tool, as it can accurately deliver high‐density electromagnetic energy to specific locations. The beam warms up the treated plant tissue and may result in complete death or growth inhibition. 4 Since laser treatments offer one of the highest levels of precision among targeted weed control methods, they can facilitate precise and selective treatment. 2 Furthermore, this weeding approach does not cause soil erosion and does not leave any chemical residue or environmental contamination; it is, therefore, regarded as an environmentally sustainable alternative. Laser treatments can be applied via either pyrolysis or stem cutting. The pyrolysis approach involves a laser beam irradiating a plant part, typically the growing meristem. Rapid heating results in cell rupture and plant tissue damage, similar to the effects of other thermal methods such as electrocution. 5 In stem cutting, the laser acts as a physical knife to cut through plant stems. Various types of laser systems have been evaluated, including carbon dioxide (CO2), fiber, diode, and frequency‐doubled solid‐state neodymium‐doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG) lasers. 6 , 7 , 8

The efficacy of laser treatment is determined by several biological (plant‐related) and technical (system‐related) factors. Regarding the treated plants, some species (e.g., Chenopodium album (goosefoot)) are more sensitive than others (e.g., mustard), and treatment at early growth stages yields superior control results compared to later stages. 9 Plant absorption pattern of electromagnetic energy vary among species and growth stages; in some scenarios, a low amount of energy absorbed into the plant tissue results in ineffective control. 10 The diameter of the laser beam plays a crucial role in outcome control. A smaller beam reduces energy demands but makes it more challenging to accurately target the developing meristem. Conversely, a larger beam requires less precision but may lead to inadequate damage or unintended energy dispersion beyond the target area. 4 Moreover, it requires a more energetic laser.

Among the numerous parameters affecting control results, laser wavelength appears to have a significant impact. Wöltjen et al., 5 demonstrated that a CO2 laser system with a central wavelength of approximately 10 600 nm was more effective and had lower energy requirements compared to diode systems operating at 940 nm. These authors showed that to achieve 90% control (effective dose for a 90% response (ED90)) of Nicotiana tabacum L. treated on the meristem at the two‐leaf stage, the CO2 and diode laser systems required approximately 1 and 3 J mm−2, respectively. However, despite their commercial deployment in the vegetable fields (mainly in California and Arizona, USA), CO2 laser systems exhibit clear physical limitations. These include structural fragility, high volumetric bulk, and the necessity for heavy power supplies and active cooling chillers, which complicate field integration. 11 Kaierle et al., 12 who compared three laser systems with wavelengths of 532, 940, and 1908 nm, showed that the 532 nm system was inferior to the other systems in terms of energy demands for effective weed control. The overall energy demand for the 532 nm system to kill two leaf stage Amaranthus retroflexus plants that were treated on the meristem was 1400 J per weed, compared to approximately 230 J for the two other systems. These findings suggest that water absorption has a greater impact on control results than pigment absorption. Motivated by these results, we tuned our laser system at 1940 nm, the prominent water absorption peak in the 780–2500 nm spectral range, 13 to improve performance and optimize energy demands.

Utilizing laser systems based on pulsed rather than continuous‐wave (CW) mode is another possible approach to improve control performance and energy consumption. In a pulsed system, a very high peak power, sufficient to initiate ionization of the plant tissues, is applied. 14 The initial ionized electrons are accelerated by the laser field and collide with the sample to further evaporate and ionize additional material (Fig. 1). This avalanche process, known as laser ablation, can completely evaporate sections of plant tissue and create a small crater on its surface, potentially within a shorter time span and with lower energy. 14 Pulsed‐ablation lasers are frequently used for medical procedures such as laser vision correction, dentistry, and dermatology. 15 However, in the context of laser‐based weeding, studies using pulsed lasers, either in ablation mode or the heating regime, are scarce. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, no previous study has tested a pulsed system operating at a wavelength of 1940 nm. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to: (1) evaluate the efficacy of a laser system tuned to the water absorption peak at 1940 nm for weed control; (2) compare the performance of pulsed versus CW systems; and (3) determine the impact of biological (weed species, growth stage, and target location) and technical factors (beam diameter and exposure time) on the performance of this system.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material

The seed‐bearing parts of the weeds (black nightshade (Solanum nigrum L.), Johnson grass (Sorghum halepense (L.) Pers.), velvetleaf (Abutilon theophrasti) and jungle rice (Echinochloa colonum (L.) Link)) were collected between 2016 and 2018, from a naturally infested field near the Newe Ya’ar Research Center in Israel (latitude 32°42′ N, longitude 35°10′ E) that had not been treated with herbicides for 6 years. Samples were collected in the field and air‐dried for 1 month in a dry‐environment glasshouse (~40 °C at noontime) until the seeds naturally separated from the plants. Cultivated black mustard (Brassica nigra (L.) Koch) seeds of the common cultivar in Israel were also used.

Growth conditions

Experiments were held at the Newe Ya’ar Research Center, and laser treatment were conducted at the Jerusalem College of Technology between 2022 and 2024. Pots (250 mm) were filled with clay soil (57% clay, 23% silt, 20% sand, on a dry‐weight basis, and 2% organic matter) and seeded with five seeds that were thinned to one plant per pot 7 days after emergence. Plants were placed in a net‐house and watered by an automated mini‐sprinkler irrigation system, as needed. Plants were fertilized once with 20–20–20 (nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P2O5), potassium (K2O), and microelements) to avoid fertilizer stress. Pots were taken from the net‐house to an indoor laboratory for the laser treatment. Following treatment, pots were returned to the net‐house and after 14 days, survival rate was documented, shoots were harvested, and their fresh biomass was documented. Experiments were conducted to determine the impact of various operational and biological factors on weed control performance.

Laser system

The laser system was based on a Q‐switched thulium‐doped yttrium aluminum perovskite (Tm:YAP) laser operating at 1940 nm, which corresponds to the prominent peak absorption wavelength of water in the 780–2500 nm range. The system was designed to operate in both CW and pulsed modes. 16 , 17

The laser cavity is depicted in Fig. 1 and consists of a linear resonator with a total length of 220 mm. The back resonator mirror was a plano mirror with high reflectivity (HR) coating in the 1850–2000 nm range and high transmission (HT) at the pump wavelength of 793 nm. The front output coupler was a plano‐concave mirror with a radius of curvature (R) of −200 mm and 55% reflectivity across the 1850–2000 nm spectral range. The gain medium comprised a Tm:YAP crystal with thulium atomic concentration of 3% and dimensions of 3 × 3 × 10 mm3. Optical pumping was achieved using a fiber‐coupled aluminum gallium arsenide (AlGaAs) diode delivering up to 30 W at 793 nm focused into the Tm:YAP crystal. Q‐switching functionality was implemented using an acousto‐optic modulator (AOM) (Gooch & Housego, Ilminster, UK), with CW mode achieved by switching off the AOM.

The system maintained an average output power of 6 W in both CW and pulsed operational modes. In pulsed mode, the laser pulse energy was up to 3 mJ at a repetition rate of 2 kHz, and approximately 30 ns pulse duration, corresponding to a peak power of approximately 100 kW. The output M‐square (M 2) beam quality was M 2 < 1.5, which indicates a near‐Gaussian beam with high focusing capability.

For alignment and targeting, a visible laser diode at 650 nm was co‐aligned with the infrared beam using a dichroic folding mirror. After the output coupler, a collimating lens produced a 4.7 mm diameter collimated beam. Various focusing lenses were used to focus the laser beam on the targeted plant with spot sizes of 4.7, 1.6, 0.8, 0.4, and 0.1 mm, enabling systematic investigation of spot size effects. These spot sizes were determined by the availability of optical components and to span a practical range of beam diameters relevant to the scale of the target plants. The laser operation and exposure times were controlled by a dedicated controller to deliver defined irradiation durations during the experiments.

Four experiments were conducted to evaluate the new system and compare it with conventional CW system where both systems operate at 1940 nm. Various combinations of beam diameters and exposure times were evaluated; the resulting energy densities (in J mm−2) are summarized in Supporting Information Table S1. All experiments were held in a full factorial complete randomized design, with 15 repetitions (n = 15) in Experiments 1 and 2, ten repetitions (n = 10) in Experiment 3 and seven repetitions (n = 7) in Experiment 4. Figure 2 summarizes the various biological and operational parameters that were tested and compared.

Experiment 1

The first experiment aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the pulse‐based laser system and compare it to the conventional CW system when applied on the plant stem as a ‘cutting knife’. For that purpose, Solanum nigrum plants were treated on the 3–4 leaf stage (stem diameter: ~3 mm). For both systems, plants were treated using three beam diameters: 0.4, 1.6 and 4.7 mm. Each diameter was tested at four exposure times: 0.25, 0.5, 1.5 and 2.5 s. Additionally, for the 0.25 s exposure time only, an extra beam diameter of 0.1 mm was evaluated under both the CW and pulse systems. The laser was located approximately 20 cm from the plant. The laser beam propagated horizontally and focused on the stem at height of 2–4 cm above the soil level (Fig. 2).

Experiment 2

The second experiment aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the pulse‐based laser system and compare it to the conventional CW system when applied on the plant meristem. The experiment was held at the same time as the first experiment, and shared similar non‐treated control plants. For that purpose, Solanum nigrum plants were treated on the 3–4 leaf stage. Since the plant meristem area is wider compared to its stem, only the two larger beam diameters were tested: 1.6 and 4.7 mm. Each diameter was tested at two exposure times: 0.25 and 1.5 s. The distance between the laser system and the plant was approximately 20 cm. The laser beam came vertically from above the plant and focused on the meristem. The spot was manually aligned until the red visible beam, co‐propagate with the laser beam, was clearly observed in the middle of the plant at the center of the growing meristem.

Experiment 3

The third experiment aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the pulse‐based laser system and compare it to the conventional CW system when applied on plants at different growth stages. For that purpose, Solanum nigrum plants were treated at three growth stages: 2–3, 4–5, and 7–8 leaf stages (stem diameters: ~2, 4 and 5 mm, respectively). For both systems, plants were treated using 1.6 mm beam in two exposure times: 0.5 and 2.5 s. Plants were treated on the stem as previously described. The distance between the laser system and the plant was approximately 20 cm. The laser beam was horizontal and the spot was manually aligned until the red visible beam, co‐propagate with the laser beam, was clearly observed on the stem.

Experiment 4

The fourth experiment aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the laser system on various weed species. Because the first three experiments revealed no distinct advantage for the pulse system, we restricted further testing exclusively to the CW system. For that purpose, B. nigra, Sorghum halepense, Abutilon theophrasti and E. colonum plants were treated at 3–4 leaf growth stages (stem diameters: ~4, 3 × 5 (minor × major axis) and 3, 3 × 6 (minor × major axis) mm, respectively). Plants were treated using two beam diameters: 0.4 and 0.8 mm. Each diameter was tested at three exposure times: 0.5, 1.5 and 2.5 s. Plants were treated on the stem as previously described. The combinations of beam diameters and exposure times were converted to energy density (in J mm−2), as detailed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and model fittings were conducted using R version 4.1.3 (R Core Team). Prior to modeling, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated for the raw biomass data residuals using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests, respectively. Both assumptions were significantly violated across all experiments (P < 0.05; Supporting Information Tables S2, S3 and Fig. S1). Consequently, biomass data were converted to proportions of the control and bounded between 0.0001 and 0.9999. Analyses were performed using generalized linear models (GLMs) with a quasibinomial distribution family and a logit link function, which is highly suitable for proportional data. 18 Depending on the specific experiment, the models evaluated the main effects and respective interactions of the experimental factors. Analysis of deviance (Type II Wald chi‐square tests) was conducted to assess the significance of the model effects. Post hoc pairwise comparisons of the estimated means (emmeans) were held using the response scale to evaluate significant differences between groups. The P‐values for these comparisons were adjusted using the Tukey method (α = 0.05) to control for Type I error rates. Based on these pairwise comparisons, letter groupings for significance were assigned using a compact letter display (CLD).

Specifically, in Experiments 1 and 2, the models initially evaluated the main effects and interactions of three experimental factors, laser type, beam diameter, and exposure time. Similarly, for the stem 0.25 s exposure treatment, a separate analysis was conducted to incorporate the 0.1 mm beam diameter. This model evaluated the main effects and interaction of laser type and beam diameter. Subsequently, as laser type was not a significant factor in the separate analyses of both the stem and meristem locations, the laser type data were pooled. The 1.5 s exposure time data were then analyzed by incorporating treatment location as a main factor, evaluating the main effects and interactions of treatment location and beam diameter (1.6 versus 4.7 mm). In Experiment 3, the models evaluated the main effects and interactions of two experimental factors: laser type and plant growth stage.

For Experiment 4, a dose–response analysis was conducted for the four weed species. To determine the optimal model for each species, linear and log‐logistic (two and three parameters) models were evaluated based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC), root mean square error (RMSE), and biological responsiveness. Based on these metrics, species were categorized into two distinct groups. While the tolerant species group (E. colonum and B. nigra) exhibited minimal response across the studied dose range, Abutilon theophrasti and Sorghum halepense demonstrated a pronounced, non‐linear decline, rapidly converging toward minimal biomass levels at higher doses. Consequently, to accurately capture these biological boundaries and maintain high goodness‐of‐fit (where AIC differences between competing models were statistically negligible, < 2; Table S4), B. nigra and E. colonum were fitted using a linear model, whereas Abutilon theophrasti and Sorghum halepense were analyzed using a two‐parameter log‐logistic model: (1) fx=11+expblog(x−loge) where f(x) is the weed biomass (proportion of control), x is the energy density (in J mm−2), b is the relative slope at the inflection point and e is the inflection point of the fitted line, which is equivalent to the effective dose (in J mm−2) required to cause 50% response compare to non‐treated control (ED50). Additionally, the ED90 was estimated by solving the fitted log‐logistic models, with their respective 95% confidence intervals determined via the delta method using the ED function in the drc package.

RESULTS

The laser system type (CW versus pulsed) had no significant impact on control results and both types exhibited a similar trend in biomass (proportion of control) reduction (Figs 3 and 4). These results were observed in both treatment locations, and the comparison between the system types resulted with P values of 0.11 and 0.75 in the stem and meristem, respectively, indicating that no distinct difference between these laser types was observed (Tables 1 and 2). For example, a mean biomass reduction of approximately 0.8 following the laser treatment, was observed in both laser types in the stem treatment (Fig. 3). However, the stem treatment was significantly more effective than the meristem treatment, resulting in mean biomass values of approximately 0.0 and 0.3, respectively, following a 1.6 mm treatment for 1.5 s (Fig. 5). Furthermore, the greater variability observed in the meristem treatment, ranging from 0 (complete control) to 1 (no effect), suggests lower consistency when targeting this location. At identical energy densities of 4.4 and 0.51 J mm −2, targeting the stem resulted in minimal survival rates (3% and 0%, respectively), whereas targeting the meristem allowed for much higher survival rates (54% and 81%, respectively; Tables S5 and S6).

The laser beam diameter had a significant impact on control results, however, only at the stem location (Fig. 3). For this location, the impact of the diameter size was negatively correlated with control results, and the weed mean biomass values ranged between approximately 0.1 (0.4 mm) to 0.3 (4.7 mm). Furthermore, the range of the control‐level results increased as the diameter increased, and in the 4.7 mm treatment the biomass values ranged between 0.0 to 1.0, suggesting the sensitivity of this treatment for biological and technical aspects. At the meristem location, no significant (P value = 0.20) differences were observed between the 1.6 mm and the 4.7 mm (Table 2).

As expected, exposure time had significant impact on control results, and weed biomass values were significantly (P value < 0.0001 and 0.003 for the stem and meristem, respectively) reduced as exposure time increased (Tables 1 and 2). As an example in the meristem, exposure times of 0.25 and 1.5 s resulted with biomass mean values of 0.55 and 0.30, respectively (Fig. 4). At the stem location, complete control was observed following treatments of ≥ 0.5 (Fig. 3), whereas the 0.25 s treatment was the only one that did not achieve complete control. These results further indicate the greater efficacy of the stem treatment. Furthermore, the smaller beam diameters (0.1 and 0.4 mm) achieved significantly greater control efficacy compared to the larger diameters (1.6 and 4.7 mm) within the 0.25 s treatment group (Fig. S2), regardless of laser type (Table S7).

The weed growth stage also had a significant impact on control results (P < 0.0001, Table 3 ) for both exposure times (0.5 and 2.5 s), and as the plants developed, they were less affected by the laser treatment (Fig. 6). At the first growth stage (2–3 leaf) both laser types provided complete control (0.0 biomass) of the plants, but had minor effect (~1.0 biomass) at the latest (7–8 leaf) stage. However, a significant difference between the laser systems was observed at the longer 2.5 s exposure time during the 4–5 leaf stage (Fig. 6 and Table 3 ). At this stage, the pulsed system outperformed the CW system, as reflected by mean biomass values of 0.4 and 0.6, respectively. In terms of survival, similar trends were revealed regarding the impact of growth stage when evaluated across energy densities. While an energy density of 7.46 J mm−2 achieved near‐complete mortality at the early 2–3 leaf stage (6% survival), plants at the 5–6 and 7–8 leaf stages exhibited total resilience, maintaining a 100% survival rate even at this higher energy density (Table S8).

Based on model selection, Abutilon theophrasti and Sorghum halepense followed a two‐parameter log‐logistic dose–response, indicating higher sensitivity to the laser. Conversely, E. colonum and B. nigra exhibited a linear response, reflecting greater tolerance across the tested energy densities (Table S4). Among the log‐logistic responder species, Abutilon theophrasti demonstrated substantially higher sensitivity to the laser treatment compared to Sorghum halepense (Fig. 7 and Table 4). This is highlighted by their ED50 parameters (e), where Abutilon theophrasti required an energy density of only 13.2 J mm−2 to achieve a 50% biomass reduction, whereas Sorghum halepense required a drastically higher dose of 98.2 J mm−2 (P < 0.0001 for both). Furthermore, Abutilon theophrasti displayed a steeper slope (b = 2.2, P < 0.01) than Sorghum halepense (b = 1.1, P < 0.01), indicating a more rapid rate of biomass decline once the effective threshold was reached. This disparity in sensitivity was further magnified at the 90% control level; the calculated ED90 for Abutilon theophrasti was 34.6 J mm−2, confidence interval: 12.7–56.4), while Sorghum halepense required an exceptionally high energy density of 639.7 J mm−2 However, the wide confidence interval for Sorghum halepense (confidence interval: −318.4–1597.8) indicates considerable variability in its high‐dose response (Table 4).

For the two tolerant species best described by linear models, a distinct contrast in laser sensitivity was observed based on their slope coefficients (a) (Fig. 7 and Table 5). Echinochloa colonum demonstrated a significant, dose‐dependent reduction in biomass with a negative slope of −0.0032 (P < 0.01), indicating that while tolerant, its growth was still actively suppressed as laser energy density increased. In contrast, the biomass reduction slope for B. nigra was slightly positive (a = 0.0011) and statistically non‐significant. This flat response demonstrates that B. nigra exhibited the highest resistance within the evaluated energy density range, showing no biomass reduction even at higher doses.

DISCUSSION

In this study, the efficacy of a novel laser system for weed control was evaluated. This system is unique due to two main innovations: the utilization of a 1940 nm wavelength peak and the implementation of a pulsed operational mode, rather than the CW mode commonly found in existing systems.

The main contribution of this new laser system is the employment of the 1940 mm wavelength, which minimizes the energy demands required for effective weed control. Previously reported systems operating at pigment‐absorbing or surface‐heating wavelengths required higher energy inputs for effective control of two‐leaf stage plants. Marx et al. 10 and Kaierle et al. 12 reported energy requirements ranging from 230 J for both near‐infrared (940 nm) and fiber (1908 nm) lasers up to an 1400 J for visible green (532 nm) lasers. In contrast, our system achieved 6% survival rate of Solanum nigrum at the 2–3 leaf stage using only 3 J. This input corresponds to an energy density of just 1.49 J mm−2, dropping to 0% survival using 7.4 J mm−2 (15 J total energy) when the exposure duration was raised to 2.5 s. Furthermore, our system effectively controlled weeds up to the 3–4 leaf stage with 0% survival using only 0.51 J mm−2 (9 J applied at 1.5 s exposure duration using 4.7 mm beam).

Our dose–response analysis revealed an ED90 value of 34.6 J mm−2 for Abutilon theophrasti at the 4–5 leaf stage. In contrast, previous studies employing CW CO2 laser systems (10 600 nm) reported a lower threshold of 10 J mm−2 for plants at a similar growth stage. 5 , 19 This discrepancy is merely a mathematical consequence of targeting strategies and beam geometries rather than the laser‐system inefficiency. While previous setups typically target the top‐facing meristem from above using a broad 6 mm spot profile and prolonged exposure times (1.0–4.0 s), our system utilizes a more effective stem‐cutting strategy compressed into a narrow focal point (0.4 and 0.8 mm diameters in the dose–response experiment). By localizing the beam directly to the thin stem, our system minimizes total energy input to a mere 0.5–15 J per plant, compared to the estimated 280 J expended by broad‐beam setups. More importantly, while CO2 architectures achieve a lower energy density on paper, they remain challenging for autonomous field robotics due to being bulky, expensive, fragile, and requiring massive power generators alongside heavy liquid chillers. 11 In contrast, our compact 1940 nm crystal architecture offers a cheap, small, and easily operated alternative that captures maximum photothermal efficiency without these operational constraints.

The advantage of the 1940 nm wavelength in our laser system is its correspondence to a major water absorption band in plant tissue. 20 , 21 Spectral studies indicate strong water absorption features around both approximately 1450 and 1940 nm, which are directly linked to vegetation water content. 22 Because water constitutes a large fraction of live plant tissue, irradiation at approximately 1940 nm likely results in more rapid internal tissue heating and, consequently, more efficient thermal damage compared with wavelengths used in prior studies (e.g., ~500–600 or ~1000–1100 nm) that rely more on pigment absorption or surface heating. 23 For future work, it would be valuable to evaluate wavelengths near the next water‐absorption peaks, such as the 2000–2500 nm range, 9 to investigate trade‐offs in absorption depth, selectivity, energy efficiency and operational optics complexities. By doing so, the potential of water‐targeted laser treatments can be more fully realized and optimized for field‐scale weed control systems.

In this study, the pulse laser system did not show clear improvement over the CW laser system. This lack of improvement was consistent across most experiments and tested parameters, including treatment location (stem and meristem), beam diameter, and exposure duration. The only significant improvement was observed in the growth stage experiment (for the 4–5 leaf stage) under the longer 2.5 s exposure time (Fig. 6), which is not preferable for field‐applicative reasons. Since thermal impact and resultant temperature increase are considered the primary control mechanisms, it can be inferred that for plant tissues, the differences in temperature elevation between the two systems are negligible under most tested scenarios.

Besides the laser type, all biological and technical aspects that were tested affected the control results. Applying more energy per area was more effective, as the higher energy applied to the plant caused more damage. Smaller beam diameter resulted in higher weed control. Here, reducing the beam diameters increases the energy density (J mm−2), and it can be assumed that the damage to the treated area is much higher. As an example, for the 0.5 s treatments the energy density of the 0.1, 0.4, 1.6 and 4.7 mm beams are 381, 23, 1.49 and 0.17 J mm−2, respectively. However, deploying a narrow 0.1 mm beam presents a major operational challenge due to the extreme targeting precision required to hit the plant stem. In contrast, a wider 4.7 cm beam simplifies alignment but suffers from energy waste, as a portion of the beam bypasses the stem. This geometric overfill lowers the effective energy density, meaning higher total energy levels must be deployed to ensure successful treatment. Thus, a balance between treatment efficacy and operational feasibility must be considered when selecting beam dimensions.

Younger plants exhibited higher sensitivity to the laser treatment, whereas plants at more advanced growth stages (7–8 leaf stage) were completely unaffected. At these later stages, even the smallest beam diameter (0.1 mm), with the largest dose of energy per area, failed to impact the plants. While laser irradiation was observed to cause physical damage in the stems, the plants recovered fully, and no significant biomass reduction was recorded. These findings agree with previous laser‐based studies and align with results from other thermal weed control methods, such as flaming and electrocution. 24 , 25 It is hypothesized that as plants mature, changes in stem cell composition (e.g., increased lignification) and reduced water content in the outer cell layers occur. Consequently, the peak temperatures reached during treatment are lower, resulting in diminished thermal damage. Consistent with previous studies, 5 , 19 variations in laser sensitivity were observed among weed species. These species‐specific differences are widely attributed to variations in plant morphology, stem anatomy, tissue moisture content, and the resulting wavelength‐dependent energy absorption profiles.

From a practical perspective, this approach targets young weeds using a portable laser system integrated with an autonomous robotic arm that navigates through the field. 26 The robotic system identifies individual weeds and directs the laser beam with high precision. This method facilitates weed treatment at early growth stages while minimizing total energy requirements. Furthermore, it can be implemented multiple times throughout the growing season, for instance, in watermelon crops, even after canopy closure when traditional control options become limited. During each session, emerging weeds can be effectively managed using low‐energy pulses, ensuring a strategy that is economical and environmentally sustainable. The widespread adoption of laser‐based weed control systems has the potential to enhance the sustainability of agricultural production and contribute to global food security, especially given the increasing constraints on herbicide development and application.

Despite its promising potential, several challenges must be addressed before laser‐based weed control can achieve widespread adoption. First, our findings indicate that directing the laser toward the weed stem is considerably more effective than targeting the growth meristem. However, accurate stem localization necessitates advanced vision‐based detection and targeting algorithms, particularly under variable field conditions where factors such as occlusion, inconsistent lighting, and plant overlap may interfere with precision. Further, targeting the weed stem at early growth stages from a peduncular viewpoint may be highly challenging and require alternative positioning of the laser beam. Second, enhancing system efficacy and throughput will likely depend on the development of parallel laser modules or higher‐output (in watts) systems to reduce treatment time while maintaining selectivity. Finally, operational safety is still a concern, as high‐power collimated beams pose severe risks of secondary reflections from field materials (e.g., stones) that can cause burns or blindness to humans and wildlife. Additionally, the risk of fire ignition must be mitigated. Addressing these technological and operational challenges will be crucial for translating this concept from experimental setups into robust, large‐scale field applications.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates the potential of a 1940 nm laser system as an efficient tool for selective weed control at early growth stages. The pronounced water absorption peak at this wavelength enables effective plant damage at low energy levels, significantly reducing power requirements compared to previously reported systems. However, weed sensitivity was heavily influenced by growth stage and species; young dicot plants exhibited the highest susceptibility, whereas older plants and monocots showed greater resistance. Under the tested conditions, the pulsed operational mode offered no distinct advantage over CW operation, suggesting that total energy delivery, rather than pulse structure, governs the plant's thermal response. While further improvements in detection accuracy and system throughput are required, these findings provide a solid foundation for the development of field‐ready, energy‐efficient laser‐weeding technologies.
