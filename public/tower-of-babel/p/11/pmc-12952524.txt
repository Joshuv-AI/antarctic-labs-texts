Drought Reduces Formation, but Enhances Persistence, of Mineral‐Associated Organic Matter in a Grassland Soil
Authors: Noah W Sokol, Megan M Foley, Steven J Blazewicz, Nicole DiDonato, Katerina Estera‐Molina, Mary Firestone, Alex Greenlon, Bruce A Hungate, William Kew, Ljiljana Paša‐Tolić, Eric Slessarev, Jennifer Pett‐Ridge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDrought effects are pervasive in terrestrial ecosystems, yet there is limited understanding of how drought impacts the transformation of plant carbon (C) inputs to mineral‐associated organic matter (MAOM)—the largest and slowest‐cycling pool of soil organic carbon (SOC). In a 12‐week 13C‐CO2 greenhouse labeling experiment, we tracked the formation of MAOM derived from the two dominant sources of plant C input to the mineral soil—living root inputs (13C‐rhizodeposits) and decaying root inputs (13C‐root detritus)—under normal moisture and droughted conditions in a semiarid grassland soil. At the end of the 12‐week period, we also measured the persistence of 13C‐MAOM formed from rhizodeposits versus root detritus via a subsequent persistence assay. Drought reduced the formation of MAOM derived from living roots by decreasing rhizodeposits, reducing microbial growth rates, and altering the composition of organic matter, lipids, and metabolites. Drought initially delayed the formation of MAOM derived from root detritus by slowing the early stages of root litter decomposition (week 4–8), but did not decrease total MAOM formation by the end of the 12‐week period. Notably, drought enhanced the persistence of MAOM derived from root detritus, but did not influence the persistence of MAOM derived from rhizodeposits. Our results provide some of the first direct evidence that drought can reduce the formation of MAOM in a grassland soil, but may enhance its persistence, based on the source of plant input from which MAOM is derived.Keywords: 13C‐labeling, drought, grasslands, microbial growth rate, mineral‐associated organic matter, rhizodeposition, root litter, soil carbon cycle

Introduction

Much of the Earth's land surface will experience increasing drought events under a changing climate, yet there is limited understanding of how altered precipitation will affect the transformation of plant C to soil organic carbon (SOC)—Earth's largest terrestrial C pool (Dai 2013; Canarini, Kiær, and Dijkstra 2017). In particular, it is unclear how changes in precipitation will affect the formation and persistence of mineral‐associated organic matter (MAOM)—the largest and slowest‐cycling pool of SOC (Schimel 2018; Heckman et al. 2022; Jilling et al. 2024). MAOM‐C is primarily derived from belowground plant C inputs entering the mineral soil, which are subsequently transformed to MAOM largely through the activities and bioproducts of soil microbes (Sokol et al. 2019; Sokol, Whalen, et al. 2022). The transformation of these inputs into MAOM draws atmospheric CO2 into soil, which can persist over a range of time scales—with a portion of MAOM turning over slowly and a portion cycling more rapidly (Lavallee et al. 2020; Neurath et al. 2021; Jilling et al. 2025). Soil moisture influences MAOM cycling through its effects on net primary productivity (NPP), soil microbial growth and activity, and the movement of solutes through the soil matrix (Churkina et al. 1999; Schimel 2018). The abundance and persistence of MAOM are strongly linked to moisture availability (Heckman et al. 2023), yet few manipulative studies have directly tested how drought affects the transformation of plant C inputs into MAOM and its subsequent persistence (Canarini and Dijkstra 2015; Canarini, Mariotte, et al. 2017; Jilling et al. 2024). Such data are critical to inform biogeochemical models predicting effects of drought on the terrestrial C cycle (Malik and Bouskill 2022).

A range of contrasting hypotheses describe how drought may affect SOC cycling via effects on soil microbes, plant C inputs, and OM‐mineral interactions (Schimel 2018). Drought may reduce microbial activity and growth, thus decreasing the microbial transformation of plant C compounds to MAOM (Hueso et al. 2012; Fuchslueger et al. 2019; Domeignoz‐Horta et al. 2020). Drought may decrease net primary productivity (NPP) and total plant C inputs to the soil, reducing total MAOM formation (Zhang and Xi 2021; Sokol, Whalen, et al. 2022). Notably, drought may have either positive or negative effects on belowground root inputs (Eziz et al. 2017; Guasconi et al. 2023). Since belowground plant inputs are the dominant source of plant C to the MAOM pool—they are retained in soil between 2 and 13 times more efficiently than aboveground inputs (Rasse et al. 2005; Jackson et al. 2017; Sokol et al. 2019; Sokol and Bradford 2019)—drought effects on root inputs could therefore have disproportionate positive or negative impacts on MAOM formation (Sokol, Whalen, et al. 2022). Moisture limitation in soil can also decrease diffusion of compounds through the pore space and water films of the mineral matrix (Manzoni et al. 2014), reducing the incidence of physical interactions between OM and minerals and decreasing MAOM formation. Alternatively, drought may increase MAOM formation by enhancing interactions between soluble organic compounds and mineral surfaces, via increased water tension during dry down events (Kemper et al. 1987; Kaiser et al. 2015), or by stimulating the synthesis of specific root and microbial products, such as extracellular polymeric substances (EPS)—which are thought to be key precursors of MAOM (Sher et al. 2020; Benard et al. 2023; Sokol et al. 2024).

Beyond influencing how much new MAOM is formed, drought may also impact how much MAOM persists over a defined timeframe. For instance, a recent observational field study of 34 field sites in the continental United States found that MAOM abundance (MAOM‐C %) and persistence (measured via the radiocarbon age of MAOM‐C, Δ14C (‰)) were negatively related in humid soils, though there was no relationship between MAOM abundance and persistence in arid soils (Heckman et al. 2023). This observation was posited to be the result of higher NPP in humid environments, and greater abundance and biochemical quality of root inputs into the soil (e.g., low molecular weight root exudates). These factors were theorized to contribute to a large standing stock of MAOM in humid environments, but a stock that is also more susceptible to priming and decomposition and thus cycled more quickly than MAOM in arid environments (Heckman et al. 2023). However, given the lack of mechanistic studies of drought effects on plant C inputs and MAOM formation, it is unclear how these hypotheses will play out, especially in different ecosystems experiencing drought (Sokol, Whalen, et al. 2022; Heckman et al. 2023).

Grasslands—which cover more than 40% of Earth's land surface—are particularly susceptible to increasing drought events (Wu et al. 2022), and more than 70% of SOC in grasslands exists as MAOM‐C (Sokol, Whalen, et al. 2022; Georgiou et al. 2022). In grasslands, MAOM is primarily derived from two sources of root input—living root inputs (rhizodeposits) versus decaying roots inputs (root litter)—which generate the two dominant microbial habitats in soil where SOC is formed (Pett‐Ridge et al. 2021). Rhizodeposits generate a distinct microbial habitat around the living root (the rhizosphere), which contains more labile C inputs (e.g., lower molecular weight root exudates), fast rates of microbial biomass growth and turnover, high rates of microbial predation, and rapid OM transformations (Kuzyakov and Blagodatskaya 2015; Sokol, Slessarev, et al. 2022). Root litter forms the microbial habitat known as the detritusphere, which contains more complex OM structural litter inputs, a greater abundance of microbes that secrete extracellular enzymes to degrade this litter, and slower microbial growth and biomass turnover (Kuzyakov and Blagodatskaya 2015; Sokol et al. 2024; Witzgall et al. 2024).

Key differences between rhizodeposits and root litter may lead to distinctive impacts of drought on their transformation to MAOM and subsequent persistence in soil (Sokol et al. 2024). Drought may reduce total root biomass—and thus the total amount of root detritus entering the soil (Guasconi et al. 2023). Since decomposition of root litter generally follows a predictable trajectory over time (Poll et al. 2008), the decomposition process may be delayed under drought conditions due to reduced microbial activity, though not substantially altered (Seres et al. 2022). As a result, a lower proportion of root detritus may be transformed to MAOM, or MAOM accrual may simply be delayed under drought. By affecting total living root biomass, drought can also reduce the absolute quantity of rhizodeposits across the entire soil profile (Hou et al. 2025), though drought may increase rhizodeposits per unit root area (de Vries et al. 2019; Williams and de Vries 2020). As a result, it is possible that drought could lead to lower rhizosphere SOC accrual across the soil profile but greater SOC accrual per gram of rhizosphere soil. Drought can also alter the composition of rhizodeposits—such as increasing the proportion of organic acids (Naylor and Coleman‐Derr 2018; Gargallo‐Garriga et al. 2018; Jiang et al. 2023), which are less efficiently transformed to MAOM relative to other compounds, like sugars or amino acids (Oldfield et al. 2018), and can also disrupt organo‐mineral associations (Keiluweit et al. 2015). Both of these factors should lead to lower accrual of MAOM. In terms of SOC durability, a high rate of labile OM input in the rhizosphere may lead to more rapid accrual of MAOM, but also faster “on–off” dynamics of OM on mineral surfaces (Neurath et al. 2021), and thus shorter relative persistence of rhizosphere SOC compared to detritusphere SOC. While there is clearly a range of possibilities for how drought may impact MAOM derived from living versus dead roots, few if any manipulative studies have directly examined these different pathways.

Here, in a manipulative 13C‐tracer greenhouse experiment, we directly tested how soil drought affected the formation and short‐term persistence of MAOM derived from living versus dead root inputs in a semiarid grassland soil. Over the 12‐week spring growing period of Avena barbata , an annual grass common to Mediterranean grasslands, we tracked 13C‐rhizodeposition from living plants versus decaying 13C‐root litter in soil organic matter (SOM) pools, under both normal moisture and moisture‐limited (“drought”) conditions. Since microbial community‐level growth rate is often strongly correlated with SOC formation (Sokol et al. 2024; Foley et al. 2024), we evaluated the effect of drought and microbial habitat on microbial growth rates (measured via 18O incorporation into DNA). We also assessed the relative short‐term persistence of 13C‐MAOM that was formed at the end of 12‐week period, via a subsequent 90‐day persistence assay (Kallenbach et al. 2016; Oldfield et al. 2018; Whalen et al. 2024). By combining 13C‐MAOM accrual data with microbial community‐level growth rates along with the chemical composition of SOM (measured via Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry, FTICR‐MS) and lipid and metabolite composition (via liquid chromatography mass spectrometry, LC‐MS) in the rhizosphere and detritusphere, we asked: (1) How does drought affect the accrual and chemical composition of MAOM derived from living root inputs versus decaying root litter inputs? (2) Does MAOM formation and MAOM persistence respond the same way to drought in different microbial habitats? (3) What plant and microbial dynamics help explain the responses of MAOM to drought? We hypothesized that drought would reduce MAOM formation in both the rhizosphere and detritusphere—by influencing the amount and composition of root inputs—though it would enhance the short‐term persistence of MAOM by enhancing interactions between mineral surfaces and organic compounds during dry down and by promoting root‐associated compounds known to persist as MAOM (Kemper et al. 1987; Kaiser et al. 2015; Sher et al. 2020; Sokol et al. 2024).

Methods

Experimental Design

To test how a spring growing season drought affects the formation and persistence of MAOM in a semiarid grassland soil, we conducted a 13CO2‐labeling greenhouse tracer and soil moisture manipulation study for a 12‐week period. The experimental design is described in detail in Sokol et al. (2024). Briefly, soil was collected from 0–10 cm depth (“A” mineral horizon) below a stand of Avena barbata at the University of California Hopland Research and Extension Center (39°00.106′N, 123°04.184′W)—a Mediterranean annual grassland ecosystem (MAT max/min = 23/7°C; MAP = 956 mm yr.−1), where Avena spp. is part of the dominant vegetation. The soil is classified as a Typic Haploxeralf, has a pH of ~5.6, contains 45% sand, 36% silt, 19% clay, and has an initial C and N content of 2.2% and 0.24%, respectively. After soil collection, roots were removed, and soil was passed through a 2‐mm sieve and packed to field bulk density (1.21 g cm−3) in rectangular acrylic microcosms (11.5 × 2.9 × 25.5 linear cm). Two types of microcosms were established: planted microcosms (“rhizodeposit only” treatment), where 13C‐CO2 was tracked through living A. barbata plants into the rhizosphere, and unplanted microcosms (the “root detritus only” treatment), where 13C‐labeled A. barbata root detritus was tracked into the detritusphere (Figure S1).

In planted microcosms (“rhizodeposit only” treatment), three germinated A. barbata seedlings were planted evenly apart in the soil. After a 2‐week seedling establishment phase, microcosms were then placed inside growth chambers (56 × 56 × 76 linear cm) at the Environmental Plant Isotope Chamber (EPIC) facility at the Oxford Tract Greenhouse at University of California Berkeley (Pett‐Ridge and Firestone 2017; Pett‐Ridge et al. 2021). A subset of the planted microcosms (n = 6 per timepoint) were grown inside 13CO2 growth chambers for 10 continuous weeks, filled with ~99 atom% 13CO2 (Sigma Aldrich) and maintained at a CO2 setpoint of 400 ppm. A control set of planted microcosms (n = 4) were grown in natural abundance (12CO2) growth chambers, and used for measurements that did not require an enriched 13C‐tracer, including FTICR‐MS, lipidomics, and metabolomics (discussed further below). Full details on chamber maintenance and monitoring (including temperature, moisture, light, CO2 concentration 13C enrichment, and other parameters) are fully described in Sokol et al. (2024).

In unplanted microcosms (“root detritus only” treatment), a 28‐μm mesh bag was buried in the microcosm center, which contained ~65 g of Hopland soil mixed with 1–5 mm fragments of either 13C‐labeled A. barbata root detritus (77 ± 1.7 atom% 13C‐labeled) or natural abundance A. barbata root detritus to a concentration of 0.013 g root detritus dry g soil−1 (Nuccio et al. 2020; Sokol et al. 2024). Avena barbata root detritus was previously generated using the same source of seeds used in the main experiment; plants were grown for 7 weeks in a mix of Hopland soil and sand immediately prior to the experimental period. After 2 weeks of plant establishment, half the plants were exposed for 5 weeks of continuous 13CO2 labeling (using the same protocol as described above), and the other half were grown in a natural abundance environment. After 7 weeks of growth, roots were harvested, washed, dried to constant temperature, cut into 1–5 mm fragments, mixed with Hopland surface soil, and then packed into detritusphere mesh bags. All unplanted microcosms were incubated in growth chambers with a natural abundance CO2 headspace throughout the experimental period. Since the concentration of root litter was standardized within all detritusphere microcosms, we only tested the effect of drought on root litter decay; our design did not capture how drought could reduce total root detrital inputs entering the detritusphere.

Planted and unplanted microcosms were maintained at one of two moisture treatments during the 12‐week experimental period, “normal moisture” (~16% ± 0.3 gravimetric soil moisture; mean ± standard error) or “drought” conditions (~8% ± 0.5 gravimetric soil moisture)—representative of the range of soil moisture conditions at the Hopland field site (Fossum et al. 2022; Sokol et al. 2024). To monitor soil moisture, all microcosms were weighed twice weekly and adjusted to the correct soil moisture by mass (Shi et al. 2015). At each harvest, gravimetric soil moisture was measured for all microcosms. As described in Sokol et al. (2024), it was confirmed that the hydrological conditions of the detritusphere mesh bags were not significantly different from those in the rhizosphere.

Soil and Plant Collection

Soil and plant collection was performed at three time intervals—4, 8, and 12 weeks—which coincide with key growth stages of A. barbata , and also capture different stages of root decomposition dynamics (Zhalnina et al. 2018; Pett‐Ridge et al. 2021). Avena barbata growth typically peaks between 8 and 12 weeks, followed by flowering and then senescence (Maherali et al. 2009; Zhalnina et al. 2018; Sokol et al. 2024).

In microcosms with living plants, aboveground A. barbata biomass was clipped at the base of the stems at each harvest, then dried at 65°C and weighed. Rhizosphere soil was collected by gently shaking the root systems to remove loosely attached soil; rhizosphere soil was defined as soil still clinging to the roots (Nuccio et al. 2020). A subset of roots with rhizosphere soil was immediately placed within a 15‐mL falcon tube on dry ice and stored at −80°C. The remaining rhizosphere soil was carefully separated from the roots by hand; a subset was kept fresh at room temperature for measuring microbial community‐level growth rate and other assays (described below), and a subset was air‐dried for SOC analysis. After analyses, all roots were washed, dried at 65°C, and weighed to give a value for total belowground biomass.

In unplanted microcosms, detritusphere soil was collected from inside the 28‐μm detritusphere mesh bag, avoiding any obvious fragments of decaying root material. Subsets of detritusphere soil were stored at −80°C, fresh and air‐dried for subsequent analyses.

SOC Pools

We used a combined density and physical fractionation protocol to measure three distinct SOC fractions—mineral‐associated organic matter (MAOM), particulate organic matter (POM), and sand‐ and coarse silt‐sized organic matter (coarse‐MAOM) (Soong and Cotrufo 2015; Leuthold et al. 2024; Sokol et al. 2024). First, 25 mL of 1.85 g cm−3 sodium polytungstate (SPT) was added to 5 g of air‐dried soil in a 50‐mL falcon tube and shaken on a reciprocal shaker (~200 oscillations/min) for 18 h with glass beads. The supernatant was filtered with a glass fiber filter to separate the light particulate fraction (< 1.85 g cm−3)—defined as POM. The pelletized heavy fraction at the base of the tube (< 1.85 g cm−3) was rinsed repeatedly to remove excess SPT, vortexed with 25‐mL deionized water and passed through a 53‐μm sieve. The < 53‐μm fraction (clay + fine silt) was defined as MAOM; the > 53‐μm fraction (coarse silt and sand)was defined as coarse‐MAOM (Soong and Cotrufo 2015; Samson et al. 2020; Leuthold et al. 2024). All samples were dried, ground, weighed, and analyzed for % total C and δ13C on an elemental analyzer coupled to an isotope ratio mass spectrometer (EA‐IRMS; Costech ECS 4010, Costech Analytical Technologies, Valencia, CA, USA).

The atom% 13C enrichment of each SOM pool was calculated by subtracting the atom% 13C at t = 0 from the atom% 13C in the enriched sample. The total μg of accumulated 13C in each SOM pool (i.e., MAOM, POM, and coarse‐MAOM) was then calculated using a standard mixing model (Kallenbach et al. 2016; Sokol et al. 2024). Since drought may have contrasting effects on rhizodeposits per unit root area versus on total rhizodeposition throughout the entire root system (see Introduction), we calculated effects of drought on SOM pools both per g of soil (μg 13C‐ g soil−1) as well as across the whole root system in each microcosm (μg 13C microcosm−1) (Sokol and Bradford 2019). Microcosm‐scale values were calculated by multiplying the μg 13C g rhizosphere soil−1 or g detritusphere soil−1 by the total grams of rhizosphere soil or detritusphere soil within the microcosm.

SOC Persistence

To measure the short‐term persistence of the 13C‐MAOM fraction, we subjected a ~10 g subsample of soil from each 13C‐labeled rhizosphere or detritusphere microcosm harvested at week 12 to a subsequent 90‐day persistence assay (Bradford et al. 2013; Kallenbach et al. 2016; Oldfield et al. 2018; Whalen et al. 2024). During this 90‐day period, soils were incubated at 30°C and 65% water holding capacity in 50‐mL falcon tubes, and frequently hand‐mixed to provide ideal conditions for microbial activity such that all easily available C could be potentially mineralized (Bradford et al. 2013). The concentration of 13C‐MAOM g soil−1 remaining at the end of the persistence assay relative to the concentration at the beginning was considered a proxy of more persistent 13C‐MAOM (Kallenbach et al. 2016; Oldfield et al. 2018; Whalen et al. 2024). The concentration of 13C‐MAOM (μg 13C g soil−1) at the end of the persistence assay was calculated using a standard isotope mixing model (Kallenbach et al. 2016; Whalen et al. 2024). Then, this ending 13C‐MAOM concentration was divided by the concentration of 13C‐MAOM in that sample at the beginning of the assay and multiplied by 100% to give the % of 13C‐MAOM that remained after 90 days, and may be considered more persistent over the short term.

Microbial Community‐Level Growth Rate

To measure microbial community‐level growth rate (hereon, microbial growth rate), we used the 18O‐H2O method, first described by Spohn et al. (2016) and described in detail in Sokol et al. (2024) as it relates to measuring microbial growth rate in different moisture conditions in this particular grassland soil. Briefly, a 1 g sample of fresh soil was weighed into a 20‐mL Wheaton glass serum vial, and a combination of 18O‐H2O (98 atom % H2 18O, Isoflex, San Francisco, CA, USA) and natural abundance H2O was added to the sample so the resulting soil water solution was ~20 atom% 18O. Vials were capped, incubated for 72‐h at room temperature, then 5‐mL of gas from the vial headspace was collected for CO2 analysis (and respiration rate) on a gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890B GC System). After sampling the headspace, the soil was removed and immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. DNA was extracted from frozen soil using a Qiagen DNEasy PowerSoil Pro kit following the manufacturer's instructions; the bead beating step was performed for 1 min at 5.5 m2/s with a FastPrep homogenizer. DNA was quantified using the Qubit DNA BR Assay Kit (ThermoFischer Scientific). A 50 μL aliquot of DNA was dried at 60°C in a pre‐weighed silver capsule spiked with 100 μL salmon sperm DNA (to achieve the oxygen detection limit) and analyzed for δ18O and total O content (μg) on a Thermochemical Element Analyzer (TC/EA) coupled to an IRMS (Yale Analytical Stable Isotope Laboratory, New, Haven, CT, USA).

Microbial community‐level growth rate was calculated from the amount of new DNA produced during the incubation period (tracked by 18O incorporation into microbial DNA during the 72‐h incubation). The amount of new DNA produced during the incubation period (DNAp) is the difference in DNA 18O abundance between the isotopically labeled and control incubations, times the proportion (by mass) of DNA that is oxygen (0.3121), divided by the length of the incubation and mass of soil incubated. Organic Matter Analysis, Metabolomics, and Lipidomics

Natural abundance samples were primarily used for water‐extractable organic matter analysis by Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FTICR‐MS), and metabolite and lipid analyses by liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC‐MS), all analyzed at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (Richland, WA, USA). A detailed description of the extractions, analysis and data processing are included in Text S1. Briefly, on each sample, a water extraction was first performed for FTICR‐MS analysis of water‐extractable SOM, followed by a modified Folch type extraction which yielded polar and non‐polar liquid fractions containing metabolite and lipid components (DiDonato et al. 2024). FTICR‐MS data were acquired with negative electrospray ionization (ESI) on a 7 Tesla Bruker scimaX instrument, with LC‐MS metabolomics and lipidomics data acquired on Orbitrap mass spectrometers in positive and negative ESI.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.2.1 (R Core Team 2024). We used two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to analyze the effect of soil moisture, time (week 4, 8, and 12), and their interaction (time × moisture) on 13C‐SOC pools (MAOM, POM, and coarse‐MAOM), aboveground and belowground plant biomass, microbial community‐level growth rate, water‐extractable SOM compounds (proteins, amino sugars, carbohydrates, and the total mass of OM compounds), as well as the total number of soil metabolites (as described below, unique metabolites in each habitat were visualized separately with volcano plots). For all two‐way ANOVAs, the time × moisture term was retained even if it was not significant at p < 0.05. All model output from the two‐way ANOVAs is shown in Table S1. ANOVA models were run separately for the rhizosphere and the detritusphere (Sokol et al. 2024). Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene's test. Variables failing Levene's test were subsequently log‐transformed (13C‐MAOM and 13C‐POM) to meet the requirements for parametric testing. We used simple linear models to test for the effect of soil moisture on MAOM persistence. We used t‐tests to test for the difference between normal moisture and drought treatments at each individual timepoint (e.g., 13C‐MAOM at week 4); as shown in Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.

Results

Soil Organic Matter

During the 12‐week experiment, drought reduced formation of 13C‐MAOM derived from living roots in the rhizosphere (Figure 1). In the rhizosphere, the effect of drought on 13C‐MAOM changed through time—both per gram of rhizosphere soil (Figure 1a; time × moisture interaction, p = 0.04) and at the whole microcosm scale (Figure 1b; time × moisture interaction, p = 0.0003). Drought had the strongest negative effect on rhizosphere 13C‐MAOM formation at week 12 (i.e., the end of A. barbata's growing season)—both the formation of 13C‐MAOM per g of rhizosphere soil (p < 0.001) and the formation of 13C‐MAOM at the whole microcosm scale (p = 0.0003).

There was no significant time × moisture interaction for 13C‐MAOM formed from decaying roots in the detritusphere—both per g of detritusphere soil or at the microcosm scale (p = 0.4 and p = 0.6, respectively). However, there were clear, contrasting temporal patterns in how drought affected new MAOM formation in the rhizosphere versus the detritusphere. Drought had the most pronounced negative effect on detritusphere 13C‐MAOM at week 4, during the early stages of root decomposition (Figure 1c,d). In droughted microcosms at week 4, there was less 13C‐MAOM per g of detritusphere soil (p = 0.02; Figure 1c) and less 13C‐MAOM across the entire microcosm (p = 0.01; Figure 1d) relative to the normal moisture treatment. While this pattern persisted at week 8, by week 12 there was no moisture treatment difference in the amount of detritusphere 13C‐MAOM per g of soil (p = 0.9; Figure 1c) or at the microcosm scale (p = 0.7; Figure 1d).

As with rhizosphere MAOM, the effect of drought on rhizosphere 13C‐POM changed through time (time × moisture interaction, p = 0.09). Drought had the strongest effect on the 13C‐POM pool at week 12 (p = 0.01; Figure S3a). While the rhizosphere 13C‐POM pool increased through time, the 13C‐POM pool in the detritusphere decreased over the 12‐week period (p = 0.04; Figure S3c), and drought conditions led to a relatively larger detritusphere 13C‐POM pool relative to normal moisture conditions (p = 0.08; Figure S3d). Drought also had the strongest effect on the rhizosphere 13C‐coarse MAOM pool at week 12 (p = 0.01; Figure S3c). In the detritusphere, there was no significant effect of time (p = 0.4), soil moisture (p = 0.97), or the interaction between time × moisture (p = 0.5) on the 13C‐coarse MAOM (Figure S3d).

Plant Biomass

In the microcosms with living plants where rhizosphere SOM pools were measured (i.e., 'rhizodeposit‐only' microcosms; Figure S1), the effect of drought on plant biomass changed through time—both with aboveground biomass (time × moisture interaction; p < 0.001; Figure S2a) and with total belowground root biomass (time × moisture interaction p < 0.001; Figure S2b). Drought had the strongest negative effect at week 12 on aboveground biomass (p = 0.008) and on belowground biomass (p = 0.0001).

13C‐MAOM Persistence Assay

In the rhizosphere, there was no significant difference in the short‐term persistence of rhizosphere 13C‐MAOM that formed under normal moisture versus drought conditions (i.e., the % of 13C‐MAOM remaining after a 90‐day persistence assay) (p = 0.2; Figure 2a). In the detritusphere, 13C‐MAOM that formed under drought conditions was more persistent than 13C‐MAOM that formed under normal moisture conditions (p = 0.0006; Figure 2b).

Microbial Community‐Level Growth Rate

Drought reduced microbial growth rate in both the rhizosphere and detritusphere (Figure 3). Microbial growth rate followed contrasting temporal patterns in the rhizosphere versus detritusphere—it increased through time in the rhizosphere (Figure 3a; p = 0.001) and decreased through time in the detritusphere (Figure 3b; p = 0.049). In the rhizosphere, drought had the strongest negative effect at week 12, when microbial growth rate was at its highest (time × moisture interaction, p = 0.01). There was no significant time × moisture interaction in the detritusphere (p = 0.4).

Chemical Composition of Organic Matter (FTICR‐MS)

The chemical composition of water‐extractable soil organic matter (OM), measured via FTICR‐MS, changed through time in some what contrasting ways in the rhizosphere and detritusphere (Figure 4). In the rhizosphere, protein‐like compounds increased through time (p = 0.008; Figure 4a), as did amino sugar‐like compounds (p = 0.0001; Figure 4b) and carbohydrate‐like compounds (p = 0.02; Figure 4c). The average mass of OM compounds decreased through time in the rhizosphere (p = 0.001; Figure 4d). In contrast with the rhizosphere, protein‐like compounds in the detritusphere decreased through time (p = 0.04; Figure 4e), as did carbohydrate‐like compounds (p = 0.08; Figure 4g). The average mass of OM compounds increased through time in the detritusphere (p = 0.04; Figure 4h).

In the rhizosphere, the effect of drought on protein‐like compounds changed through time (time × moisture interaction; p = 0.01), as did the effect of drought on amino sugar‐like compounds (time*moisture interaction; p = 0.02), and on carbohydrate‐like compounds (p = 0.006), and on the average mass of OM compounds (time × moisture interaction; p = 0.001). For all three compound classes, values were lower under drought conditions earlier in the growing season (week 4), and by week 12, values were higher under drought conditions in the rhizosphere. The inverse was observed for the effect of drought on the average mass of OM compounds in the rhizosphere, where values were higher under drought at week 4, and similar between the two moisture conditions by week 12 (time × moisture interaction; p = 0.001; Figure 4d).

In the detritusphere, there were no significant time × moisture interactions for protein‐like compounds (p = 0.5), amino sugar‐like compounds (p = 0.6), or carbohydrate‐like compounds (p = 0.3). All three compound classes were more abundant under drought relative to normal moisture conditions in the detritusphere (Figure 4e–g). The average mass of all water‐extractable OM compounds was also higher under drought conditions (p = 0.08; Figure 4h).

Metabolites and Lipids (LC‐MS)

In the droughted rhizosphere, the total number of metabolites increased over time and peaked at week 12, whereas under normal moisture conditions the total number of metabolites in the rhizosphere peaked around 8 weeks and then declined at 12 weeks (time × moisture interaction, p < 0.001; Figure 5a). In the detritusphere, the total number of metabolites stayed relatively constant under normal moisture conditions, but declined by week 12 in the drought treatment (time × moisture interaction, p = 0.04). Somewhat similar patterns were observed through direct comparisons of unique metabolites in the rhizosphere, though not in the detritusphere (Figure 5b). Under normal moisture conditions, the number of unique metabolites increased through time in the rhizosphere, though they decreased through time in the detritusphere (Figure 5b). Under drought conditions, the number of unique metabolites in the rhizosphere increased through time, but stayed relatively consistent in the detritusphere.

Under normal moisture conditions, the total number of unique soil lipids increased slightly with time in the rhizosphere, but did not change in the detritusphere (Figure S4). Under drought, the number of unique lipids increased in the detritusphere, but did not change in the rhizosphere (Figure S4). There were notable differences between the top 25 most abundant lipids in the rhizosphere versus the detritusphere at week 4 and at week 12 (Figure 6). At week 4, several diacylglycerol lipids and a range of triacylglycerols were more abundant in the detritusphere compared to the rhizosphere (Figure 6, left panel). At week 12, a range of triacylglycerols and a diacylglycerol were more abundant in the rhizosphere versus the detritusphere, and several glycerolipids, glycerophosphocholines, and glycerophosphoethanolamines were more abundant in the detritusphere (Figure 6, right panel).

Heatmaps show the top 25 most significantly different lipids at week 4 (left) and week 12 (right) between the two habitats, with log‐normalized, mean‐centered relative abundances (red indicates more abundant, and blue indicates less abundant, as shown in the log‐normalized scale bar on the right). Each column represents an individual replicate in each habitat and moisture treatment. N = 4.

Discussion

Drought exerts substantial effects on plant growth and microbial activity—altering aboveground and belowground plant biomass, affecting the composition and quantity of rhizodeposits, inducing the production of certain microbial compounds like extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and osmolytes, and influencing the mobility of OC compounds and microbes through the soil matrix (Schimel 2018; Williams and de Vries 2020; Malik and Bouskill 2022). However, the subsequent effects on SOC have rarely been tested in manipulative studies—especially the transformation of plant inputs to MAOM, the largest and slowest‐cycling pool of SOC (Sokol, Whalen, et al. 2022; Heckman et al. 2023). Here, in a 12‐week 13CO2‐labeling experiment in a semiarid annual grassland soil, we found that drought reduced formation of MAOM derived from living root inputs by the end of the 12‐week growing season in the rhizosphere (Figure 1a,b). Drought delayed formation of 13C‐MAOM derived from root detritus during the early stages of root litter decay (weeks 4–8), but it did not significantly affect new MAOM formation in the detritusphere by the end of the 12‐week root decomposition period (Figure 1c,d). In contrast, drought enhanced the persistence of MAOM formed from root detritus (i.e., the proportion of 13C‐MAOM that persisted in a subsequent 90‐day persistence assay), but drought had no impact on the persistence of MAOM formed from living root inputs (Figure 2). Below, we first discuss SOC dynamics in the rhizosphere, followed by SOC dynamics in the detritusphere, and conclude by addressing effects of drought on soil C persistence.

Drought had the strongest negative effect on MAOM formation in the rhizosphere at week 12 (i.e., the end of the growing season)—both on a per‐gram‐of soil basis and across the entire microcosm (Figure 1a,b). Drought is known to impact rhizosphere SOC cycling by affecting the amount and chemical composition of rhizodeposits and microbial uptake—and these distinct effects are known to evolve throughout the plant growing season (Shi et al. 2015; Zhalnina et al. 2018). Plants often release the greatest quantity of rhizodeposits during periods of fast vegetative growth (Eisenhauer et al. 2017; Landl et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2024). Indeed, prior studies with Avena barbata have found that its peak growth coincides with the highest rate of root exudation, and metabolite profiles shift dramatically between early (3 weeks) to mid (6–9 weeks) to late (12 weeks) of plant development stages (Zhalnina et al. 2018). In our experiment, A. barbata's aboveground and belowground biomass growth peaked at week 12 (Figure S2)—during this same period, drought most strongly reduced both aboveground and belowground root biomass (Figure S2). Lower belowground biomass under drought decreased total rhizodeposit‐C inputs throughout the soil profile, and decreased rhizosphere 13C‐MAOM formation throughout the entire microcosm (μg 13C‐MAOM microcosm−1; Figure 1b). The lower concentration of rhizosphere MAOM per g of soil (μg 13C‐MAOM g soil−1; Figure 1a) may have been due to a lower rate of rhizodeposition per unit root surface area – although other recent studies have found increased rhizodeposition per unit root area even while total rhizodeposition has decreased (de Vries et al. 2019; Williams and de Vries 2020). Alternatively, drought may have reduced MAOM per gram of rhizosphere soil by inducing a greater proportion of organic acids in rhizodeposits (Naylor and Coleman‐Derr 2018). Organic acids are less efficiently transformed to MAOM (Oldfield et al. 2018) and can also directly disrupt organo‐mineral associations and lead to losses of MAOM (Keiluweit et al. 2015).

During the 12‐week experiment, the composition of SOM changed through time in the rhizosphere, in ways that may suggest greater microbial transformation of rhizodeposits to MAOM. Amino sugar‐like compounds, protein‐like compounds, and carbohydrate‐like compounds all increased through time in the rhizosphere (Figure 4)—which may reflect a greater microbial‐derived signature of SOM (Angst et al. 2021; Whalen et al. 2022; Sokol et al. 2024). All three compound classes were initially higher under normal moisture conditions at week 4, though all were higher under drought conditions by week 12 (Figure 4). Prior work shows that the composition of rhizodeposits changes during plant development: relatively simple, labile compounds are more predominant early in plant development, and this shifts towards more complex compounds as plants reach maturity and senescence (Zhalnina et al. 2018; Tian et al. 2020). Drought could have delayed this trajectory, such that more labile compounds were released much later during plant development; this would explain their more pronounced increase over time under drought and greater abundance under drought by week 12 (Figure 4). The average mass of water‐extractable OM compounds in the rhizosphere decreased through time (Figure 4b)—potentially indicating fewer higher molecular weight inputs, and a greater proportion of lower molecular weight compounds (and hence, higher biochemical quality and potentially more microbially assimilable compounds) throughout the growing season (Lehmann and Kleber 2015). Indeed, microbial growth rate also increased in the rhizosphere over the growing season (Figure 3a)—which could be also due to a greater abundance of assimilable, lower molecular weight inputs that promote fast microbial growth (Malik et al. 2020). Rhizosphere microbial growth rate was also most reduced by drought at week 12 (Figure 3a)—mirroring the pattern observed with drought effects on the rhizosphere 13C‐MAOM pool (Figure 1). Put together, under normal moisture conditions, both the quantity and biochemical quality of 13C‐rhizodeposits stimulated a greater microbial growth rate and greater microbial transformation of rhizodeposits to MAOM as the growing season progressed (Teixeira et al. 2024; Sokol et al. 2024). Drought impacted this rhizodeposit‐to‐MAOM pipeline most substantially at week 12, overlapping with the period of peak plant growth (Figure 1).

In contrast with rhizosphere MAOM, detritusphere MAOM was most negatively impacted by drought during the early stages of root decomposition (week 4), and had no significant effect on detritusphere MAOM formation by week 12 (Figure 1c,d). Thus, while drought delayed formation of MAOM, it did not influence total accrual of new MAOM over the 12‐week experimental period. Importantly, we found that drought decreased belowground root biomass in microcosms with living plants (Figure S2). In a field setting, this would have reduced the total amount of root litter C entering the soil profile (Guasconi et al. 2023), and thus reduced the amount of root detrital‐C that could form new MAOM. Since we standardized the amount of 13C‐root detritus in our detritusphere microcosms, our observation of reduced detritusphere 13C‐MAOM formation at week 4 and 8 under drought was not the result of reduced 13C‐root detritus inputs. Instead, our findings likely reflect the delayed microbial decomposition of 13C‐root detritus and reduced movement of dissolved OM compounds through the soil matrix under moisture‐limited conditions (Manzoni et al. 2012; Cruz‐Paredes et al. 2021; Seres et al. 2022). It will be important for future manipulative studies to capture the effects of reduced root biomass on MAOM formation, since drought likely reduces root litter‐derived MAOM formation across the soil profile.

Root detritus contains chemically complex compounds that require decomposition by extracellular enzymes—which are largely produced by soil fungi and certain bacteria (Shi et al. 2018; Zhou et al. 2023). Indeed, at week 4, we found that triacylglycerols—storage lipids that are abundant in fungi and the bacterial genus Actinomyces (Harwood and Russell 1984; Alvarez and Steinbüchel 2002; Neurath et al. 2021)—were more abundant in the detritusphere compared to the rhizosphere. As root litter decayed over the 12‐week period—observed as a decreasing pool of detritusphere 13C‐POM over time (Figure S3)—the average mass of OM compounds increased through time in the detritusphere (Figure 4h). The 13C‐POM pool (i.e., the 13C‐root litter remaining in soil) and the average mass of OM compounds were both higher under drought, suggesting slower decomposition and a greater proportion of more complex C compounds remaining with lower soil moisture (Figure 4). Protein‐like compounds and carbohydrate‐like compounds—two compounds classes that are representative of simpler, more soluble compounds—decreased through time in the detritusphere, and were also more abundant under drought conditions (i.e., due to slower decomposition under drought) (Figure 4). Soil metabolites decreased through time in the detritusphere—both the total number of metabolites (Figure 5a) and the number of metabolites unique to the detritusphere compared to the rhizosphere (Figure 5b). These decreases may suggest that certain metabolites—perhaps smaller, more easily assimilable compounds—preferentially form MAOM early during root decomposition either through the microbial pathway or the direct sorption pathway, and so fewer metabolites remain after 12 weeks of decomposition. Moreover, microbial growth rate—which is influenced both by the amount and quality of C input—decreased through time in the detritusphere and was reduced by drought (Figure 3b). This confirms prior work that shows that low molecular weight and more soluble root compounds are typically released first during root litter decomposition, followed by more complex polymeric C compounds in later decomposition stages (Poll et al. 2008). These smaller, more simple compounds are thought to form MAOM most efficiently since they do not require extensive microbial decomposition before associating with soil minerals, and are associated with higher microbial growth rates (Cotrufo et al. 2015; Haddix et al. 2016). Put together, slower decomposition of root litter under drought likely delayed the release and transport of simpler and more soluble compounds, which in turn reduced microbial growth rates, and delayed MAOM formation during the first 8 weeks of root litter decay.

While drought did not affect the formation of MAOM in the detritusphere by the end of the 12‐week experimental period, drought did enhance the persistence of 13C‐MAOM formed from root litter (Figure 2). Drought may have increased the persistence of root litter‐derived MAOM by selecting for certain compounds that form strong associations with mineral surfaces (e.g., EPS),reducing microbial activity, enhancing interactions between complex OM compounds and mineral surfaces, and/or by drawing mineral particles and soluble compounds more closely together during soil drying (Kemper et al. 1987; Kaiser et al. 2015; Witzgall et al. 2021). An important limitation of our experimental design, as discussed above, was that we standardized the amount of 13C‐root litter in all detritusphere microcosms, so we could not detect the effect of reduced belowground litter inputs under drought on MAOM formation. Even if drought reduced total root biomass and total root litter‐derived MAOM in a field setting, the greater persistence of MAOM formed under drought could partially compensate for lower total MAOM accrual. In Mediterranean annual grasslands, root detritus is largely decomposed in the following growing season—and about 27% of that root‐derived C will become MAOM over the year following senescence and root decay (Shi et al. 2018; Fossum et al. 2022; Witzgall et al. 2024). Future work will be necessary to determine how, over multiple seasons, the greater persistence of MAOM in the detritusphere may be counteracted by drought impacts on the formation of new root detritus‐derived MAOM.

In contrast with the detritusphere, drought did not influence the persistence of rhizosphere MAOM (Figure 2a). Rhizosphere MAOM is known to form and turn over more rapidly, driven by dynamic interactions of OM with mineral surfaces (Neurath et al. 2021). These rapid “on–off” dynamics of organic matter on mineral surfaces in the rhizosphere may override the effects of drought on the strength of organo‐mineral interactions and soil C persistence (Neurath et al. 2021; Heckman et al. 2023). Prior work on MAOM abundance and organic compound turnover times suggest that in more arid regions, higher molecular weight and more aromatic compounds (i.e., more representative of root litter compounds) tended to persist longer, perhaps because microbes preferentially use smaller, more soluble compounds (i.e., compounds more representative of low molecular compounds in rhizodeposits) (Heckman et al. 2023). Indeed, we found that detritusphere MAOM was overall more persistent than rhizosphere MAOM in the semiarid grassland soil we studied (Figure 2). In Mediterranean annual grasslands, it is important to recognize the strong link between living and dead roots—living roots transfer C to the rhizosphere during the spring growing season; those living roots then die during the dry season to form a detritusphere (Shi et al. 2018; Fossum et al. 2022; Witzgall et al. 2024). Our evidence suggests that drought may reduce the amount of total new MAOM formed from living roots during the growing season, though not the relative durability of that MAOM. During root litter decomposition, drought may not affect the amount of new MAOM formed from root litter (particularly per g of detritusphere soil), but can boost the durability of this MAOM in the mineral soil.

In conclusion, we provide some of the first direct evidence that drought reduces the formation of 13C‐MAOM from living roots in a semiarid grassland soil—both per gram of rhizosphere soil and across the soil profile. This may be due to decreased total rhizodeposits under drought, as well as decreased rhizodeposit‐C per unit root area and/or increased production of compounds (like organic acids) that are less efficiently transformed to MAOM and can disrupt existing organo‐mineral associations. We found that drought delayed formation of MAOM in the detritusphere, but did not reduce overall MAOM formation by the end of 12 weeks—though our experimental design did not account for reduced total root biomass inputs under drought, which would likely lead to lower MAOM formation throughout the soil profile. Drought had the most pronounced effects on rhizosphere MAOM formation and microbial growth at the end of the growing season, and on detritusphere MAOM and microbial growth during the early stages of root decomposition. Notably, we found that drought enhanced the persistence of MAOM formed in the detritusphere, though not in the rhizosphere. Overall, MAOM derived from root detritus was more persistent than MAOM derived from rhizodeposits. Future research will be necessary to determine how these drought responses affect SOM dynamics over multiple growing seasons in the field, the mechanisms underpinning the effect of drought on MAOM persistence, how the timing and severity of drought events may differentially impact MAOM cycling, and how MAOM in other ecosystem types responds in similar or different ways to drought. Such questions are crucial to understand as increasingly dramatic and unpredictable precipitation patterns pose uncertain consequences for the global carbon cycle.
