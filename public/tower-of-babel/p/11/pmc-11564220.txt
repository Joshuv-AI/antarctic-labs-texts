Scale for the assessment of mucosal wave dynamics of the free edges during stroboscopic examination: clinical validation study and analysis of results
Authors: Walter Tenesaca Pintado, Roberto Fernández Baillo, Isabel Cardoso Lopez, Ángel Rodriguez Paramas, Alfredo Martínez Martínez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIdentifying the mucosal wave (MW) is essential when studying the voice; however, its characterization and perceived measurement during laryngeal stroboscopy (LS) are not well defined or standardized because of the subjectivity of its interpretation. This article proposed and validated a scale that characterized and approximated MW measurement during LS, applied it to participants divided into a healthy group and groups with free edge conditions, and identified differences between them.MethodsThis is a descriptive and clinical validation study of the “VASQ (Vertical axis, Anteroposterior axis, Symmetry and Quantity) mucosal wave score” scale based on stroboscopy images of 137 adult men and women divided into a control group and functional and organic pathology groups. The images were analyzed by three evaluators according to an established protocol. Measurements dictating the reproducibility and validity criteria as well as the MW score in each group were obtained.ResultsThe reliability of the scale was α = 0.90, internal consistency success rate was 91%, intra-observer reliability was 0.83, inter-observer reliability was 0.83, content validity coefficient was 0.92, and factor loading was 0.37–0.53. The MW total score values between 5 and 6 were established as a reference for normality (P < 0.05). Organic pathology showed lower MW score values (P < 0.05), and functional pathology to a lesser extent (P > 0.05).ConclusionsThe proposed scale is a consistent, valid, and reliable tool. Its widespread application would favor commonly used terminology and facilitate quantitative comparisons in future studies.Keywords: Dysphonia, Free edges, Mucosal wave, Stroboscopy, Vocal fold vibration, Voice, Voice disorders

Introduction

Aerodynamically driven vibration of the free edges (FEs) of the membranous regions of the vocal folds (VFs) occurs during normal phonation, and involves a complex three-dimensional pattern of oscillatory movements of the covers of the FEs known as “mucosal wave” (MW) [1–6]. The MW involves the inferior, medial, and upper surfaces of both FEs. It usually exhibits synchrony and symmetry, and its trajectory has vertical, horizontal, and anteroposterior vectors [7].

In the functional study of the voice, understanding the MW and its characteristics is considered fundamental since they are indicators of tension and flexibility of the underlying tissues, and are essential to produce effective phonation [8–10]. In addition, this knowledge provides information on how different normal or pathological conditions impact its execution, in order to develop actions aimed at improving prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and monitoring activities [10, 11].

Several methods can be used in clinical voice evaluation [7, 12–27], however, laryngeal stroboscopy (LS) is the most widely used technique because its costs and time optimization, and it plays a fundamental role in diagnostic and therapeutic decisions by enabling a real-time approach to FE dynamics and vowel cycle characteristics [28–31]. The stroboscopic effect creates the optical illusion of slowing down and reveals a pattern of periodic rapid movement, turning MW into an observable phenomenon and creating a visual reconstruction from different capture points [5, 10, 26, 32].

The vertical path of the MW involves the dynamics of the upper and lower margins of the FEs. As the wave movement travels in an upper direction, the margins move laterally with a phase difference, the lower ones first and the upper ones shortly thereafter. The presence of this phase difference is a very important variable for normal phonation [7, 8, 33–36].

As this phenomenon is reduced and is no longer observable in one or both FEs, the MW will be decreased or absent.

Under normal conditions, the MW is distributed over the entire length of the FEs. The presence of segments that have lost this property may be caused by glottic closure defects, or increases in cover tension due to histological changes [7, 37]. Likewise, the predominance of MW in a certain segment may suggest an area of greater impact.

The similarity or coordination between the joint dynamics of both FEs in relation to the midline during the vocal cycle is also an important property of the MW; an asymmetric dynamic may be present in organic or functional disorders [31, 37–39].

The degree of lateral displacement of the FEs in relation to the midline (Amplitude) and the wave that propagates laterally on the upper faces of the FEs are of great relevance because they are correlated with alterations in the underlying tissues [31, 36, 39, 40].

All of the features described above can be visualized using LS. However, despite its clinical generalization, there are important limitations because LS data alone have not been calibrated sufficiently to produce quantitative measurements of MW and currently their interpretation is subjective and descriptive. There is little published information on widely accepted scales or forms that characterize the MW during LS. This implies difficulties in comparing results between studies, with negative impacts on clinical practice and research progress [10, 41, 42].

This study provided a practical scale, that allowed approximation of the measurement of perceived MW during LS. Moreover, it allows for the unification of terms among voice professionals and shows how MW is perceived under normal conditions and conditions of organic and functional alteration.

Material and methods

Scale design

A review of the scientific literature was performed using questionnaires or forms previously designed in an attempt to assess MW during LS [8, 29, 31, 33–50]. From these studies, some stroboscopic parameters were selected specifically aimed at approximating the three-dimensional trajectory of the MW independently of the dynamics of other adjacent structures such as the supraglottic and glottic gap structures A form was developed and submitted for discussion by a group of seven otorhinolaryngologists specializing in voice pathology. The scale form (Fig. 1) called “VASQ Mucosa Wave Score” (vertical axis, anteroposterior axis, symmetry and quantity) was obtained considering the proposed suggestions after several reviews, tests, and modifications. Of the stroboscopic parameters included, one evaluates the vertical path of the MW, another evaluates its distribution along the entire length of the FEs (anteroposterior axis), one parameter evaluates the horizontal path of the MW (amplitude), one parameter evaluates both the vertical and horizontal paths of the MW (Propagation), and another parameter evaluates the joint dynamics of both FEs (symmetry). Figure 1 shows the descriptions and scores for each parameter included in the scale. Figures 2 and 3 show the graphic examples that are attached to the “VASQ Mucosal Wave Scoring” form. Participation of the MW in the vertical axis (V). It identifies the phase difference between the upper and lower margins during MW generation, regardless of the contact with the contralateral free edge and symmetry.

Participation of the MW in the anteroposterior axis (A). It determines whether the MW covers the entire length of the FEs or is focused on, considering three regions: the posterior third, middle third, and anterior third. Non-vibratory segments were identified, without considering symmetry or contact with the contralateral free edge. However, this was not a glottal gap assessment.

Symmetry (S). It determines whether the dynamics of both FEs correspond to or differ from each other and their relationship with the midline regardless of the presence of MW dynamics.

Quantity (Q). It considers the relationship between MW amplitude and propagation, regardless of the contact and symmetry with the opposite edge. Amplitude is the distancing of the FEs in the lateral direction in relation to the midline, and during stroboscopy, it is evaluated considering the upper margins. Propagation is an undulatory movement, similar to a wave, that moves from the lower margins and can be visualized when it reaches and extends along the upper surfaces of the FEs. The segments established to qualify the amplitude and propagation ranges are shown in Fig. 3A and B.

Study design and scale validation

This was a descriptive and clinical validation study. The form was applied to a sample of subjects with the help of three evaluators specializing in voice pathology who previously received instructions and a user manual. The sample comprised 137 participants, with an average age of 39.8 years, who were part of the database of patients who attended an otolaryngology consultation at a third-level hospital between October 2022 and September 2023. Men and women participated, which consisted of healthy subjects and subjects with the pathologies shown in Table 1. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and approval was obtained from an ethics committee. All participants were questioned about their case history and asked to complete the VHI-10 questionnaire. [51] In addition, the image of the stroboscopic examination in the phonation of the vowels /a/ and /i/ held at a natural pitch and volume was recorded in a video using a flexible nasopharyngolaryngoscopy video tower with a tip chip and a strobe light source. The recording segments showing the entire length of the VFs and their sustained vibration for at least 5 s were selected and edited. The recordings were coded and delivered to evaluators who, after viewing them carefully, rated them according to the proposed scale. As part of the established sample collection protocol, a 4-s voice recording of the vowel /a/ held at normal pitch and volume was also obtained the Online Lab® Voice Clinical Systems® application, version 1.4.0, by connecting a BOYA BY-M1 unidirectional microphone to an Android device [19, 52]. Information from biomechanical voice analysis was used, through the R1 Screening Test [53], provided by the tool as support for the creation of the study groups. Therefore, the sample population was divided into three groups. First, a control group made up of 12 women and 16 men, under the following criteria: (a) no history of voice pathology and not attending the consultation for dysphonia, (b) obtaining a VHI-10 score of less than 10, and (c) no findings of FE alteration in the imaging study. Second, the functional pathology group comprised eight men and eight women, with the following criteria: (a) reporting voice problems, (b) obtaining a VHI-10 result greater than 10, (c) obtaining a functional alteration value greater than 50%, according to the R1 Screening Test of biomechanical analysis [54], and (d) no alteration during the imaging examination. Third, an organic pathology group formed of 56 women and 37 men was defined by presenting findings of altered FEs regardless of the VHI-10 result and biomechanical analysis.

Measures establishing reproducibility and validity criteria were obtained [12, 54–57]. Measures establishing reproducibility and validity criteria were obtained [9, 44–47]. The reliability of the scale was determined by calculating the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, and Spearman’s correlation coefficient between the items of each parameter was used to obtain internal consistency [44, 47, 48]. To obtain intra-observer reliability, all samples were rated twice by the same evaluators. After completing the first evaluation, a period of three weeks was allowed, the coding of the samples was changed to avoid bias, and the samples were rated again. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated between the test and retest values. For inter-observer reliability, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was also used for all evaluators. Content validity was obtained through agreement by expert judgment with the determination of the content validity coefficient as described by Hernández-Nieto in 2002 [58, 59], and through exploratory factor analysis [12, 54].

The distribution values of the findings regarding MW in the different groups were also described, using the Student’s t-test as a hypothesis contrast test. A statistical significance level < 0.05 was established a priori. Python 3.11.5 and SPSS V.29 software were used for statistical analysis.

Results

The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient [54, 60] reached a high reliability value, (α = 0.90). The internal consistency determined from the range of Spearman’s correlation coefficients between the items and their respective domains ranged 0.49–0.91 (Table 2). The accepted ranges were those with values equal to or greater than 0.4. Based on these values, a success rate of internal consistency of 0.91 was obtained. Regarding intra-observer reliability (test–retest), the Pearson correlation coefficient between the different parameters of the scale in the test and retest phases ranged 0.71–0.94, with an average of 0.83 in the total score (Table 2). The degree of agreement between evaluators determined by the Pearson correlation coefficient indicated that the total sum of the scale scores obtained an average value of 0.83, which according to the definition of Landis and Koch [61], indicated almost perfect reliability. Parameters (V), (A), and (Q) demonstrated substantial reliability (0.67–0.80). (S) had the lowest value (0.54), and none had values below 0.50 (Table 2). The agreement of the seven specialists obtained an excellent content validity coefficient (0.92) according to the definition described by Hernández-Nieto. The exploratory factor analysis obtained a global variance value between 0.37–0.53. The recommended values should be equal to or greater than 0.3.

Results in the control group

Considering all evaluators and the two evaluations conducted, the total sum of the VASQ form obtained a mean value of 5.46 (Table 3, Fig. 4A), and 76.5% of the control group population was between the range of total scores of 5 and 6 (P < 0.05). An analysis of the differences by gender allowed us to determine that there were significant differences (P < 0.05), with the MW scores observed in men being higher than in women with mean values of 5.75 and 5.08, respectively (Fig. 4B).

Results in the pathology group

When comparing the total score results obtained between the control group and the pathology group, divided into organic and functional pathology, we observed that the pathology in general presented with lower mean MW score values (Table 3 and Fig. 4A). In the case of organic pathology, with an average value of 4.7, significant differences were observed (P < 0.05) compared to the control group, without significant differences in the functional pathology group with a mean value of 5.2 (P > 0.05). Considering the mean average of all evaluators in the two observations, only 56.25% of the subjects with FE alterations achieved MW scores within the normal range (Table 4), compared to 76.5% in the control group, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). Decreased values in the MW score were present in 33.45% in the population of the organic pathology group, and increased values were present in only 10.3%.

Discussion

This study aims to contribute to the field where the most used tool for the study of vocal function lacks a standardized language among professionals and it is difficult to quantify findings; thus, limiting the ability of professionals to compare their results with those reported in the literature. In a systematic review conducted by Bonilha et al. [42] to determine the scope and consistency of the parameters and scales used in studies that used stroboscopy as an outcome measure, it was reported that there was a notable lack of standardization and incompatibility regarding terminology, with a lack of practical and clear definitions of the different parameters and their associated scales. The few published investigations have not been accepted, and many have often been associated with poor reliability and validity values. The creation of the “VASQ MUCOSAL WAVE SCORE” Scale focused on clearly defining the concepts and references of each parameter based mainly on the trajectory analysis of the MW from a three-dimensional point of view, taking into account its vertical, horizontal, and anteroposterior path vectors, thus allowing us to focus exclusively on the dynamics of the FEs independently of other adjacent structures. This enabled us to focus exclusively on the dynamics of the FEs independently, and aimed to give a value to the perceived MW and approximate its measurement as an organized, simplified, and effective process without overloading professionals with complex scales or questionnaires, which may cause uncertainty or delay healthcare activities.

One of the most notable investigations is that of Rosen et al. [41], who developed a questionnaire for the perceptual evaluation of stroboscopy based on a series of 27 cases. Symmetry, amplitude, periodicity, non-vibratory segments, duration, and glottal closure pattern were included, and intratester reliability yielded values greater than 80%. In another study conducted by Poburka et al. [45] a form for evaluating the dynamics of the VFs during phonation with videostroboscopy and high-speed videoendoscopic (HSV) was created based on a sample of 66 participants; amplitude, MW, non-vibratory segments, supraglottic activity, closing phase, symmetry, periodicity, glottal closure, vertical level, and free edge contour were included. Intertester reliability ranged 0.57–0.96 for stroboscopy and 0.81–0.94 for HSV. Intratester reliability ranged 0.19–0.87 for stroboscopy and 0.28–0.85 for HSV. However, despite their firm and consistent methodological structure, these study questionnaires are not widely accepted due to the complexity of their structure and difficult practical application, and they are not specifically focused on the evaluation and measurement of the MW.

The need for objective measurement instruments in healthcare is increasing. Scales are tools designed to evaluate observations that cannot be directly measured and are accepted when they are designed and validated according to established scientific methods [12, 54]; The VASQ Mucosal Wave Score scale yielded values within the recommended ranges of reliability, internal consistency, intra- and inter-observer reliability, content, and criterion validity. Based on this, it can be established that the scale is a valid, reliable, and consistent tool.

The total score of the scale ranges from − 2 to 8, and the results of this study establish normality values between 5 and 6 as a reference. Total values less than five indicate a decreased MW score in the range, and values greater than six indicate an increased score (Fig. 5, sequences A–H). Regarding the results differentiated by gender, higher MW scores were obtained in men than in women (P < 0.05). Adult male population has a greater VFs length and thickness than women. Vibration at higher frequencies because of the shorter length of the VFs or a smaller thickness, requires less time and less amount of mass or mobile tissue available, causing the MW to decrease. The opposite occurs with low-tone phonation because of the greater VF length or thickness, resulting in an increase in the mass of tissue with vibratory capabilities [30].

In general, the results of the group analysis showed that the organic pathology group was more frequently associated with a decrease in MW. Although it did not have a consolidated organic lesion, the functional pathology group was characterized by an altered MW. On an average, the examiner perceived it as decreased although the differences were not statistically significant.

During an organic or functional alteration, there are variations in the MW that may produce dysphonia, triggered as a compensatory mechanism for a more effective glottic closure that acoustically corresponds to better phonation through hyperfunctional behavior, increasing tension and strength, and causing the cover to reduce its vibration capacity. It is uncommon to find an increase in MW even when an increase in MW is determined by stroboscopy, which decreases because of this mechanism [62–64]. The MW may increase in the inflammatory phases of organic pathologies due to local edema of the lesions; however, when a lesion has already been established (e.g. fibrous nodules), the MW decreases. This was demonstrated by cases of Reinke’s edema in mild and moderate stages, where normal (Grade I) or increased (Grade II) MW values can be obtained. However, in the severe stage, where edema acquires a large size and there is even overlapping of the FEs, MW is difficult or impossible.

Conclusions

The proposed scale is consistent and is a valid and reliable tool. In addition to helping to unify the qualification and approximation of the measurement of FEs dynamics during stroboscopic examination, it makes it possible to achieve a highly demanded common language, allowing for comparisons in quantitative studies.

Pathology with FEs alterations was more frequently associated with a decrease in perceived MW score. The effectiveness of this scale is greater when it comes to the evaluation of organic disorders of the FEs. However, it is limited for the evaluation of functional disorders. Pathology with FEs alterations was more frequently associated with a decrease in perceived MW score. In the case of Reinke’s edema, there is variation in MW depending on its severity.
