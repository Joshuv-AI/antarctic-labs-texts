Invited commentary on ‘Population health, health equity and health system impacts of light vehicle electrification: a modelling study in Aotearoa/New Zealand’
Authors: Quynh C Nguyen, Pankaj Dipankar, Diego Salazar, Amrutha Alibilli, Yulin Hswen
Published: 2025

Electrification replaces internal combustion engine vehicles with battery electric vehicles, leading to reduced tailpipe emissions, cleaner air, less noise and more sustainable environments, while also providing health benefits for the public.1 However, more recently, evidence has begun to emerge that suggests potential negative health and environmental side effects of electric vehicles.2 This raises key questions about whether fleet electrification has an overall net positive effect on population health and health equity, especially when considering impacts beyond climate change mitigation.

The study by Shaw et al sought to answer this question and investigate the complex relationship of fleet vehicle electrification on a broader spectrum of public health and equity outcomes that include cost and environmental factors in Aotearoa/New Zealand. To do so, Shaw et al leveraged transport-health-environment multistate life table models to examine changes in the vehicle fleet and travel behaviours from 2018 to 2050 because of electrification. Interestingly, their results showed that under this electrification scenario, although health impacts related to air pollution decreased, negative consequences included an increase in road injury, contributing to net health loss. Such that there would be a 21% increase in per capita kilometres travelled by car compared with the 2018 baseline, a negative net loss of 270 health-adjusted life years and an increase in healthcare costs by $44 million 2018 New Zelanad Dollar (NZD). This study contributes to the existing literature by exploring various pathways affected by light vehicle fleet electrification, rather than concentrating solely on air pollution. Notably, it challenges common assumptions by suggesting that electrification might lead to negative population health outcomes.3

However, these findings should be taken with the understanding that, like many modelling-based works, it relies on assumptions and data availability, which may lead to underestimating or overestimating effects. Errors can occur in calculating the operating costs and particle emissions of electric vehicles2 which, due to their heavier weight, can generate particle emissions from tire wear, brake wear and resuspended road dust. The model assumes an increase in car travel due to lower driving costs of electric vehicles and a concurrent decrease in active travel practices like physical activity such as walking and cycling. The model assumes no other vehicle electrification policies were in place during the simulated period and does not consider factors like commercial or transport noise. The study did not examine other health impact pathways, such as how electric vehicle manufacturing involves lithium, which is linked to environmental and health risks from mining and air and water contamination. Thus, these unaccounted factors may affect the applicability of the findings to real-world contexts, highlighting the need for integration of additional variables and model refinement.

Moreover, while the health benefits of light fleet electrification remain under debate, there is strong evidence of the environmental advantages of reducing transport emissions. As it has been shown, using renewable energy for charging significantly reduces greenhouse gas emissions.4 Transport contributes 21% of global CO2 emissions, with road transport responsible for 75% of this.5 Electric vehicles have been reported to have, on average, 49% lower well-to-wheel greenhouse gas emissions than conventional internal combustion engine vehicles.6 Nonetheless, greenhouse gas emissions from electric vehicles vary based on their charging sources. If charging comes from high-emission electricity generation, emissions may be worse.

In terms of equity, emerging research suggests that light fleet electrification does not automatically ensure equitable benefits, and transport policies should explicitly incorporate spatial and social justice objectives.7 Previous research has documented that electric vehicle drivers are more likely to be involved in at-fault collisions compared with drivers of internal combustion engine vehicles.8 Furthermore, electric or hybrid electric vehicles may pose a greater risk to pedestrians in urban environments because these drivers have been shown to exhibit greater unsafe driving behaviours compared with traditional vehicle drivers.8 9 This study sheds important light on different pathways that link electrification and human safety and health beyond air pollution alone.

As Shaw and colleagues raise concerns that although vehicle fleet electrification can promote decarbonisation, electrification could result in a net health loss. If it is correct that vehicle electrification increases the total number of cars on the road, elevates traffic-related injuries and decreases physical activity, then it is important that city planners address these risks and begin to invest in road safety and infrastructure. Investing in traffic calming measures (eg, speed bumps, roundabouts) and creating protected spaces for cyclists and pedestrians can enhance safety while promoting active transportation and should be considered for the future if we are to adopt widespread electrification.

This study underscores the importance of developing models that forecast not only the intended benefits of fleet electrification but also its unintended consequences. To guide effective policy and planning, we need more research that moves beyond a narrow focus on the impact of electric vehicles on reduced emissions and considers the broader environmental, health and social impacts. A holistic perspective ensures that optimism about new technologies does not blind us to their risks. Comprehensive research is needed to explore both positive and negative effects, enabling us to anticipate challenges and design interventions that maximise public health and environmental benefits.
