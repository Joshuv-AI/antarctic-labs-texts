Geospatial mapping of gender-sensitive malaria vulnerability and ITN usage barriers in Zamfara State, Nigeria
Authors: Abimbola Atijosan, Ayobami Atijosan, Ayodeji Olalekan Salau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria continues to pose a serious public health challenge in Zamfara State, Nigeria, disproportionately affecting women and children due to socio-economic barriers. This study aimed to develop a Composite Gender-Sensitive Malaria Vulnerability Index (CGMVI) to identify areas of heightened malaria vulnerability and support informed targeted interventions.MethodsMalaria incidence and mortality data from the Malaria Atlas Project, and population data from WorldPop, were integrated with indicators of Insecticide-Treated Net (ITN) access and usage, maternal education, and household wealth. A Pentagonal Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process was used to compute the CGMVI, while Getis-Ord Gi* hotspot analysis identified spatial patterns of vulnerability across Zamfara State.ResultsThe CGMVI revealed a west-to-east gradient of malaria vulnerability across Zamfara State, with hotspots in Gummi (3.86) and Bukkuyum (3.62), characterized by low ITN usage (82–85%), high maternal education deficits (89–91%), and household poverty (85–90%). Moderate vulnerability was observed in central Local Government Areas (LGAs) such as Anka and Bungudu, while northern and eastern LGAs, including Shinkafi, Zurmi, and Tsafe, exhibited low vulnerability with higher ITN coverage (≈92%) and better socio-economic profiles. Hotspot analysis using Getis-Ord Gi* confirmed statistically significant clustering in western LGAs (z-scores 2.11–4.89 at 99% confidence), while coldspots in the north and east had z-scores from − 1.69 to − 3.39. These patterns reflect intersections of socio-economic barriers, ITN access, and maternal education, highlighting areas where women and children face elevated malaria risk.ConclusionThe study offers a spatial framework for understanding how epidemiological, socio-economic, and gender-related factors shape malaria vulnerability in Zamfara State. By identifying areas where women and children may face elevated risk, the findings can inform gender-sensitive ITN distribution, community malaria literacy initiatives, and socio-economic support strategies. The CGMVI provides a tool to guide evidence-informed planning and better integrate gender considerations into malaria control efforts in the state.Keywords: Malaria, Malaria vulnerability, Insecticide treated net, Gender inequality, GIS, Pentagonal FuzzyAHP

Introduction

Malaria is preventable and treatable, yet it remains a leading cause of illness and death in sub-Saharan Africa, with Nigeria carrying the heaviest burden worldwide [1–3]. In 2022, according to a World Health Organization report, Nigeria accounted for one in every four malaria cases worldwide and nearly a third of all malaria-related deaths [4–6]. These figures underscore the urgent need to strengthen malaria prevention, tailored to Nigeria’s unique social and health system context.

Zamfara State, located in the Northwestern part of Nigeria, is one of the worst-affected areas, with year-round transmission that peaks during the rainy season [7–9]. Poverty, rural livelihoods, a mainly agrarian economy, and limited healthcare access increase vulnerability, particularly for women of reproductive age and children under five [10–12].

Insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) are a key malaria prevention tool. Evidence suggests that consistent household use can significantly reduce malaria transmission and lower child mortality [13–15]. Over the past two decades, Nigeria has scaled up ITN distribution nationwide through campaigns led by Roll Back Malaria (RBM) and the National Malaria Elimination Programme (NMEP) [6, 16]. Unfortunately, Nigeria still carries the heaviest share of the malaria burden, alongside its devastating consequences. While ownership of ITNs has improved, actual use remains uneven [17, 18]. The reasons are complex, linked to social, economic, cultural, and educational factors that current interventions may not fully address.

One vital challenge is that most interventions overlook how barriers to ITN use vary across locations and between genders [14, 19]. In northern Nigeria, where patriarchal norms are strong, household power dynamics can significantly shape decisions about health practices [20]. Women, often the primary caregivers and managers of child health [21, 22], may lack the autonomy or resources to acquire, install, or consistently use ITNs. Male-controlled household budgets, cultural expectations, and intergenerational educational deficits, where children are born to mothers with no formal schooling, further perpetuate low malaria awareness and suboptimal prevention behaviours [23, 24]. Without precise identification of where these gendered vulnerabilities are most acute, interventions risk being poorly targeted and less effective.

To address this gap, this study develops a Composite Gender-Sensitive Malaria Vulnerability Index (CGMVI) for Zamfara State by integrating socio-economic, educational, health, and ITN data from WorldPop and the Malaria Atlas Project [25, 26]. The index is constructed using the Pentagonal Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (PFAHP), a Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM), approach that applies pentagonal fuzzy numbers to capture the uncertainty and ambiguity often present in expert judgments [27]. Through this process, indicators are weighted and aggregated to generate the CGMVI, which provides a clear and reliable measure of malaria vulnerability across the study area.

The CGMVI highlights geographic hotspots where malaria risks are most acute, enabling prioritization of interventions, particularly in areas where socio-economic and health data are imprecise. Building on these findings, the study examines gender-sensitive differences in ITN use and explores the socio-economic and educational factors that shape usage patterns. Results are presented through thematic maps and hotspot analyses, offering nuanced and actionable insights to guide targeted, culturally appropriate, and gender-sensitive malaria control efforts.

The objectives of the study are to map and classify malaria vulnerability into hotspots and cold spots across Zamfara State; assess gendered disparities in ITN usage, focusing on these hotspots; and generate actionable evidence to inform policymakers, health planners, and community stakeholders in designing interventions that dismantle socio-cultural and economic barriers to ITN use.

This research makes methodological and practical contributions to spatial epidemiology and gender-responsive public health planning. By integrating gender-sensitive indicators into a composite spatial vulnerability model, the study addresses a critical gap posed by the scarcity of gender-disaggregated malaria data. The resulting high-resolution maps and analyses provide useful guidance for malaria program implementers, policymakers, and gender equity advocates seeking to optimize resources and implement effective, targeted interventions. Furthermore, the study aligns with global health priorities articulated in the Sustainable Development Goals, specifically SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 5 (Gender Equality), which emphasise equitable access to healthcare and the elimination of gender-based disparities. To operationalize these objectives, we developed a CGMVI, guided by the conceptual framework in Sect. "Conceptual Framework for Gender-Sensitive Malaria Vulnerability".

Conceptual framework for gender-sensitive malaria vulnerability

The Composite Gender-Sensitive Malaria Vulnerability Index (CGMVI) integrates epidemiological, socio-economic, and gender-sensitive dimensions to capture spatial patterns of malaria vulnerability in Zamfara State. In this framework, maternal education and household wealth are used as proxies for gendered barriers, reflecting women’s knowledge, decision-making capacity, and access to resources that influence malaria prevention behaviors, such as ITN use. Malaria incidence and mortality represent exposure and health outcomes, while ITN access and usage reflect the availability and adoption of preventive interventions. By combining these indicators within a weighted, geospatial model, the CGMVI aims to highlight how structural socio-economic constraints intersect with gendered roles, particularly the caregiving responsibilities of women, to create localized hotspots of vulnerability. This conceptual model illustrates the multidimensional and spatially heterogeneous nature of malaria vulnerability, supporting targeted, gender-sensitive interventions.

Methodology

Study area

The study area is Zamfara State and it is located in the northwestern part of Nigeria, between latitudes 11°N, 13°N and longitudes 5°E, 7°E as shown in Fig. 1. Zamfara State has an estimated landmass of approximately 38,000 square kilometres. It is a well-known hotspot for malaria in Nigeria [28, 29].

Study area map

Data sources and indicators

Using datasets from [25, 26], this study sought to capture the intersection of malaria transmission, preventive practices, and socio-economic inequalities that carry gender-sensitive dimensions. The six indicators were selected based on the conceptual framework in Sect. "Conceptual Framework for Gender-Sensitive Malaria Vulnerability", as they capture key dimensions of malaria exposure, prevention, and socioeconomic disadvantage [3, 14, 18, 19, 21]. Maternal education and household wealth were used as proxies for gender-sensitive vulnerability. While they do not directly measure women’s agency or intra-household decision-making, they are relevant and sufficient for identifying spatial patterns of gendered malaria vulnerability. Dataset details are in Table 1, and indicator definitions, including numerators, denominators, and target populations, are provided in Table 4. For comparability, all raster layers were standardized using min–max normalization, which placed the indicators on a common scale and allowed them to be integrated into a single geospatial framework. Malaria incidence, mortality, and ITN data are from 2022, while maternal education and household wealth are from 2021. This one-year difference was due to data availability and is not expected to substantially alter spatial patterns, although minor temporal variations (e.g., seasonal malaria fluctuations or ITN campaign timing) may not be captured. Malaria Atlas Project (MAP) data are modelled annual gridded estimates at 5-km spatial resolution. Small gaps in the reclassified rasters were smoothed using ArcGIS focal statistics with a 3 × 3 kernel and a single iteration. All raster layers were analyzed in their native GCS WGS 1984 projection, with no resampling applied. For the Getis-Ord Gi* analysis, data were temporarily reprojected to UTM Zone 32N, again without resampling.

Weight assignment using a pentagonal FuzzyAHP

To assess the relative importance of the factors shaping malaria vulnerability, this study employed the Pentagonal Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process. This technique allows expert judgments to be combined in a systematic manner. Furthermore, it enables the effective capturing of uncertainty through the use of pentagonal fuzzy numbers. A Consistency Ratio of 0.0189 was obtained for the pairwise comparison matrix. This is well below the 0.1 threshold recommended in literature [30], indicating that the pairwise judgments were highly consistent and the derived weights are logically coherent. Thereafter, the weights were applied to the spatial input layers, and the different factors and constraints were combined using the Weighted Linear Combination (WLC) method [31].

Vulnerability classification

The CGMVI raster was reclassified into five categories of vulnerability through Jenks’ natural breaks, a method chosen for its ability to minimize within-class variation. The resulting classes, Very Low, Low, Moderate, High, and Very High, provide an intuitive scale for comparing levels of malaria vulnerability across Zamfara State. This approach helps to identify priority areas where malaria risk and gendered vulnerabilities overlap, guiding focused interventions.

Hotspot analysis and gender inference

Hotspot analysis using the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic [32] was applied to detect statistically significant clusters of high malaria vulnerability. Vulnerability hotspots coinciding with elevated maternal education deficits (MEDUCATION) and low ITN access were identified as gender-sensitive priority zones, reflecting socio-cultural and economic barriers to malaria prevention faced by women.

Visualization and mapping

All spatial analyses and cartographic outputs were generated using ArcGIS Pro. Outputs include thematic maps of each input indicator, the composite vulnerability map and hotspot cluster maps. Summary statistics aggregated by Local Government Areas (LGAs) were produced to aid malaria program implementers, policymakers, and community stakeholders in identifying and prioritizing intervention zones.

Validation

Only state-level malaria indicators were available for Zamfara, including prevalence, incidence, ITN use and treatment-seeking [38]. While these data did not permit direct LGA-level validation of the modelled hotspots, they provided an important ecological reference. The high overall malaria prevalence (36.6% by microscopy) and persistently elevated incidence (317 per 1000) indicate a broadly high-transmission environment where spatial clustering is expected [38]. Declining ITN use and suboptimal care-seeking patterns further support the plausibility of heterogenous transmission within the state. However, because no subnational or facility-level data were available, hotspot accuracy could not be confirmed directly.

Discussion

Spatial distribution of the composite gender-sensitive malaria vulnerability index

The spatial distribution of composite gender-sensitive malaria vulnerability across Zamfara State, shown in Fig. 14, demonstrates considerable variation among Local Government Areas (LGAs). The CGMVI combines key metrics, including malaria incidence and mortality, ITN access and utilization, the proportion of children aged 12–23 months from the poorest households, and those born to mothers without formal education, into a multidimensional index ranging from very low to very high vulnerability. The index incorporates weights derived through the Pentagonal Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (PFAHP), ensuring that each factor’s relative significance is systematically reflected in the vulnerability assessment.

Areas of high and very high vulnerability (orange and red) are largely concentrated in the western LGAs, notably Gummi, Bukkuyum, and parts of Maru and Bakura. These patterns correspond to challenges such as a low ITN usage rate, limited ITN access, and high proportions of children from impoverished households or mothers lacking formal education (Figs. 4, 5, 6, and 7). These conditions likely reflect socio-economic and gender-sensitive constraints, which prior studies [33–35] suggest may restrict women’s decision-making power and access to malaria prevention.

Importantly, these results align with findings from the 2021 Nigeria Malaria Indicator Survey (NMIS) study [33, 34], which showed that community poverty and maternal education significantly influenced ITN use among women of reproductive age, with lower use in poorer communities and among mothers with limited education. This highlights the impact of socio-economic and community-level factors on shaping malaria prevention outcomes.

In contrast, northern LGAs such as Shinkafi, Zurmi, and Maradun display low to very low vulnerability (green), reflecting comparatively lower malaria mortality, higher ITN coverage, and use (Figs. 3, 4, and 5). Shinkafi, in particular, records very low to low vulnerability, corresponding with relatively reduced malaria incidence and mortality, as well as widespread ITN ownership and use (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5). Tsafe LGA shows low to moderate vulnerability, which is consistent with comparatively lower household poverty rates and fewer maternal education deficits (Figs. 6 and 7). Similar associations were highlighted by [35], who found that urban clusters with stronger socio-economic status, better housing, and greater ITN access experienced reduced malaria prevalence, underscoring the consistent link between socio-economic improvements and lower vulnerability across both rural and urban contexts.

Central LGAs such as Anka and Bungudu demonstrate moderate vulnerability (yellow), reflecting mixed performance across indicators and transitional socio-economic conditions. In a similar study across urban Nigeria, [35] emphasizes the importance of using socio-demographic predictors to prioritize interventions, reinforcing that targeted strategies based on education, wealth, and housing can optimize ITN distribution and malaria control efforts. By integrating gender-sensitive indicators such as maternal education and household wealth, the CGMVI offers a comprehensive framework for policymakers to develop gender sensitive and geographically targeted strategies to reduce malaria disparities in Zamfara State.

Hot and cold spots

The spatial distribution of malaria vulnerability hot and cold spots across Zamfara State, as illustrated in Fig. 15, highlights significant spatial variability based on the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic, to identify areas of statistically significant clustering. Hotspots, indicating high vulnerability, are prominently located in the western and central-western LGAs, including Gummi, Bukkuyum, Bakura, and parts of Maru, with z-scores ranging from 2.11 to 4.89 at 99% confidence (red dots) and 0.79 to 2.11 at 95% confidence (orange dots). These areas likely reflect the intersection of low ITN usage, low ITN access and gender-specific barriers such as limited maternal education and household resources control. For instance, the intense clustering in Gummi and Bukkuyum suggests considerable structural challenges that may be exacerbated by poverty and patriarchal norms. Within these hotspots, some variation is evident, with Bakura showing clusters significant at both the 95% and 99% confidence levels, confirming localized differences in vulnerability intensity. These hotspots are consistent with findings by [35], who reported that lower household wealth and limited post-primary education were associated with higher malaria risk in children under five. Suggesting that socio-economic deprivation may contribute to elevated malaria vulnerability in these regions. At the state level, ITN access is 43.7% and ITN usage is 40.2% among all ages, with 46.1% of children under five sleeping under an ITN; malaria prevalence by microscopy is 36.6%, and estimated incidence is 317.3 per 1000 population [36].

In contrast, coldspots, representing low vulnerability, are concentrated in the eastern and northern LGAs, including Shinkafi, Zurmi, Tsafe, and Gusau, with z-scores from − 1.69 to − 3.39 at 99% confidence (dark blue) and − 0.39 to − 1.69 at 95% confidence (light blue). These regions may reflect better ITN access or lower socio-economic deprivation, aligning with the map’s spatial gradient of declining vulnerability eastward. Non-significant areas (green dots, z-scores − 0.39 to 0.79) are scattered across central LGAs like Bungudu and Anka, suggesting mixed or transitional vulnerability profiles. Together, the hotspot and coldspot patterns underscore the spatial heterogeneity of malaria vulnerability across Zamfara State. The localized clustering observed in this study aligns with broader patterns of spatial clustering documented at the national level [37], reinforcing the relevance of hotspot mapping in identifying priority areas. These patterns suggest that malaria risk may be influenced by both spatial clustering and gendered factors, such as differential access to resources and caregiving roles, supporting the need for geographically and gender-tailored interventions [38].

Gender-sensitive and socio-economic dimensions of malaria vulnerability

Table 3 highlights the gender-sensitive and socio-economic dimensions of malaria vulnerability across Zamfara State, with CGMVI values identifying hotspots such as Gummi and Bukkuyum. These LGAs are characterized by low ITN usage (82% and 85%), high proportions of children born to mothers without formal education (89% and 91%), and limited household wealth (85% and 90%), signaling compounded risk for women and children. These local patterns align with national trends indicating that women, children under five, and adolescent girls face disproportionate malaria impacts due to low maternal education, limited resources, and gendered caregiving responsibilities [39, 40].

The high CGMVI in Gummi and Bukkuyum reflects both extreme vulnerability and moderate intra-LGA variability, suggesting that some communities are particularly exposed while others are less so. Conversely, LGAs with lower CGMVI, such as Zurmi (2.44) and Tsafe (2.64), display better socio-economic profiles, indicating that lower maternal education and household poverty are key drivers of vulnerability. This reinforces the role of maternal education and household wealth as widely accepted gender-sensitive proxies in malaria research [38].

Beyond descriptive patterns, the findings demonstrate that low maternal education and poverty intersect to hinder effective malaria prevention. Women in these high-risk areas are less likely to use ITNs and have limited knowledge of malaria prevention, reducing household-level protective behaviors. These structural disadvantages underscore the importance of integrating gender and socio-economic indicators into malaria vulnerability mapping. Interventions in Gummi and Bukkuyum should prioritize ITN distribution, maternal education programs, and poverty alleviation strategies, explicitly targeting women as primary caregivers.

Comparative evidence further supports these findings. In northern Nigeria, gendered norms limit women’s decision-making power and access to resources, heightening vulnerability. Similar patterns are observed in Migori County, Kenya, where cultural beliefs and gender roles influenced malaria perceptions and prevention practices [19]. Globally, socio-economic and gendered factors shape malaria vulnerability in context-specific ways, emphasizing the need for interventions that are both gender-sensitive and geographically targeted [41].

Overall, this multidimensional analysis underscores that malaria risk is strongly gendered: maternal education, household wealth, and ITN usage collectively shape vulnerability, with women and children disproportionately affected. Addressing these intersecting factors is critical for designing effective, equitable malaria control strategies in Zamfara State and similar contexts.

Pentagonal fuzzy analytic hierarchy process (PFAHP) methodology

Expert participation

Three experts participated in the pairwise comparison process. Their domains included GIS, health geography/spatial epidemiology, gender and health. Experts were selected based on relevant experience and expertise in malaria intervention planning and spatial health analysis. Judgments were elicited on the 10th of May, 2025.

Fuzzy set theory and FAHP

Fuzzy set theory, introduced by Zadeh, handles uncertainty and imprecision in real-world problems by allowing elements to have partial membership values between 0 and 1. Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP) extends traditional AHP by incorporating fuzzy numbers to represent expert judgments in pairwise comparisons, effectively capturing uncertainty in decision-making [45]. Pentagonal FAHP (PFAHP) was chosen over other MCDM techniques, such as TOPSIS or ELECTRE, because it models expert judgment ambiguity more precisely and provides robust, consistent priority weights for complex, multi-criteria spatial health assessments.

Pentagonal fuzzy numbers (PFN)

A pentagonal Fuzzy Number (PFN) Fp=(a1,a2,a3,a4,a5:r) is defined by a membership function as shown in Eq. 2, describing the degree of membership of values across five parameters, where a1,a2 represent the lower bounds, a3 the peak (most plausible) value, and a4,a5 the upper bounds. PFNs provide superior modeling of ambiguity compared to triangular or trapezoidal fuzzy numbers, improving uncertainty management in expert evaluations [27, 46].

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis methodology

To assess the robustness of Fuzzy AHP-derived criterion weights, each weight was randomly perturbed ± 20% from its baseline value while ensuring the total sum remained 1. A total of 1,000 perturbed weight scenarios were generated, and descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum) were computed for each criterion, with boxplots used to visualize the distribution. The scenario with the largest deviation from baseline, as measured by symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error (sMAPE), was selected to compute the CGMVI and verify hotspot stability (Table 8, Figs.16, 17,18).

Policy implications for targeted interventions

The spatial patterns identified in this study, particularly the west-to-east vulnerability gradient with hotspots in Gummi, Bukkuyum, Bakura, and parts of Maru, provide a foundation for targeted malaria control in Zamfara State. Derived from the CGMVI and hotspot analysis using the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic, these high-vulnerability clusters intersect with gendered socio-economic barriers, including low maternal education (proportions > 0.89 in Gummi and Bukkuyum) and household poverty (85–90%). This highlights the need for geographically precise, gender-sensitive interventions [19].

Prioritizing resource allocation to vulnerability hotspots is critical. Very high vulnerability areas spanning 2192 km2 in Gummi and 1628 km2 in Bukkuyum, with elevated malaria incidence (> 500 cases/1000), mortality (> 280 deaths per 100,000), and suboptimal ITN usage (82–85%), require intensified, targeted ITN distribution. Geospatial targeting using CGMVI maps provides an evidence-informed approach to optimize ITN delivery, helping ensure coverage reaches the most vulnerable populations. State Ministry of Health and LGA PHC teams should lead ITN distribution, with immediate implementation before the rainy season, targeting households in Gummi and Bukkuyum. This approach, aligned with spatial overlays, maximizes ITN penetration and has been shown to reduce malaria incidence and morbidity in similar contexts [17, 33, 42].

Gendered dimensions embedded in the CGMVI, proxied by maternal education and household wealth, indicate that interventions must address cultural barriers in patriarchal settings. In high-vulnerability LGAs, where maternal education deficits correlate with low ITN usage (SD = 0.056–0.065), community-based education programs for women, partnering with local women’s groups and religious leaders, may enhance malaria literacy and ITN usage. These programs should be coordinated by State Ministry of Health in collaboration with NGOs and community-based women’s associations, with medium-term implementation over 6–12 months in priority hotspots. Targeted cash transfers or subsidies for poor households (HWEALTH > 0.85) would empower women to prioritize ITN procurement, reinforcing broader socio-economic and gender-focused interventions aligned with SDG 5 on gender equality. LGA social welfare offices and NGO partners should manage cash transfers, prioritizing between 500 and 1000 households per hotspot cluster in the first year. These integrated approaches combining education, engagement, and economic support are supported by evidence to improve ITN usage and reduce malaria risk [23, 43, 44].

Culturally sensitive strategies are essential for effectiveness. Engaging male household heads in awareness campaigns addresses low ITN usage driven by male-controlled budgets. Seasonal targeting during the rainy peak (June–September) should prioritize enhanced ITN distribution to remote areas, using mobile units or community-based networks to overcome access barriers, while integrating CGMVI data into digital dashboards enables adaptive, real-time monitoring (e.g., standard deviations in CGMVI of 0.20–0.22 in hotspots). Coordinated action with the Roll Back Malaria Partnership and local NGOs can enhance feasibility and equitable coverage.

In summary, linking CGMVI hotspots to malaria control allows optimized resource allocation: concentrating ITN distribution, education, and poverty alleviation in western LGAs. Immediate actions focus on ITN distribution in hotspot clusters, medium-term actions on women’s education and cash transfer programs, targeting defined numbers of households per cluster. This evidence-based, gender-sensitive strategy is consistent with both the spatial and socio-economic evidence presented and may enhance malaria control outcomes in high-burden settings.

Limitations

While the integration of WorldPop and Malaria Atlas Project data enabled construction of the CGMVI, reliance on secondary datasets limits the precision of local-level representation, particularly in areas affected by insecurity or underreporting. Validation of vulnerability hotspots was constrained by the absence of LGA-level, gender-disaggregated malaria data for Zamfara, precluding direct ground-truthing and highlighting the need for future field-based verification. The use of proxy measures such as maternal education and household wealth, though analytically robust, cannot fully capture socio-cultural dynamics, including intra-household bargaining, gender norms, and caregiving roles that shape ITN utilization. Future research could combine qualitative, longitudinal, and quantitative data to better evaluate the drivers of gender-sensitive malaria vulnerability. Despite these limitations, the CGMVI offers a novel, scalable, and policy-relevant tool for identifying vulnerability hotspots and informing equity-driven malaria interventions in high-burden settings.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study has illuminated the spatially uneven and gendered nature of malaria vulnerability in Zamfara State, Nigeria, through the development and application of a Composite Gender-Sensitive Malaria Vulnerability Index that integrates epidemiological, socio-economic, and educational indicators. The findings reveal a clear west-to-east gradient of declining vulnerability, with significant hotspots in western LGAs such as Gummi and Bukkuyum, where high malaria incidence and mortality intersect with low ITN usage, maternal education deficits, and household poverty. These patterns underscore the critical role of gender norms in perpetuating barriers to ITN utilization. By identifying statistically significant clusters via hotspot mapping and linking them to gendered proxies, the research fills a key gap in gender-disaggregated malaria data, providing high-resolution insights that advance spatial epidemiology and support SDGs 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and 5 (Gender Equality).

The results demonstrate that while ITN access has improved in northern and eastern LGAs (e.g., Shinkafi and Zurmi, with low CGMVI scores of 2.37–2.44), persistent mismatches between access and usage in hotspots highlight the need for culturally tailored interventions. These findings help policymakers direct resources to the most vulnerable areas through geospatial targeting.

Based on these findings, the following recommendations are proposed:

Geospatial targeting of ITN distribution should utilize CGMVI and hotspot maps to direct NMEP campaigns toward very high vulnerability zones in Gummi, Bukkuyum, and Bakura, aiming for universal ITN coverage (> 95%) through community-led distributions, while incorporating mobile units to address access gaps in rural peripheries.
