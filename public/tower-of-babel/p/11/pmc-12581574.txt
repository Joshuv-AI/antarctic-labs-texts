Magnitude and factors associated with virological failure among adults on first-line antiretroviral therapy in Dire Dawa, eastern Ethiopia: a retrospective chart review
Authors: Kenesa Tesema Dibaba, Dumessa Edessa, Abera Cheru, Melkamu Merid Mengesha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFirst-line antiretroviral therapy failure reduces the benefits of the antiretroviral therapy (ART) program and could lead to regimen change to a more expensive second-line ART. To identify ART failure, routine viral load monitoring is recommended as the gold standard and has been implemented in Ethiopia since 2017. However, evidence regarding the virological failure of the first-line ART is limited in the study setting, Dire Dawa Ethiopia.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the magnitude and factors associated with virological treatment failure among adults on first-line ART follow-up in Dire Dawa between January 2017 and December 2019.MethodsA retrospective chart review was conducted among 272 randomly selected adults on first-line ART follow-up, between January 2017 to December 2019. Data were collected from patients’ medical records, entered into epi data version 3.02 and exported to Stata version 15 software for analysis. A bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with virological first-line ART failure. The goodness of fit of the model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. All statistical tests are declared significant at a P-value of < 0.05.ResultsA total of 257 ART patients’ records were included for final analysis. The magnitude of first-line ART virological failure was 11.28% (95% CI 7.69, 15.80). Baseline undernutritional status (AOR = 3.72: 1.05, 13.14), serostatus nondisclosure (AOR = 4.45: 1.34, 14.79), early (≤ 30 days) ART initiation (AOR = 0.235: 0.064, 0.859), history of missed any daily ART dose (AOR = 3.16: 1.01, 9.89) and dolutegravir-based regimen (AOR = 0.28: 0. 09, 0.90) were significantly associated with virological failure of first-line ART.ConclusionThe magnitude of virological first-line ART failure in this study was relatively high. Virological first-line ART failure was significantly associated with baseline BMI, HIV serostatus disclosure, duration of ART initiation after HIV diagnosis, history of missed ART doses, and substitution of DTG-based ART first-line regimens. To avoid treatment failure, it is crucial to disclose one's serostatus, encourage adherence initiatives, and early initiation of ART preferably a dolutegravir-based regimen.Keywords: HIV/AIDS, Virological failure, First-line ART, Ethiopia

Introduction

The primary goal of antiretroviral therapy (ART) is to suppress virus replication and increase patient survival rates [1]. ART reduces the probability of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) transmission, prevents HIV-related illness, averts acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS)-related deaths, helps patients live a normal lifespan, and enhances both health and economic gains [1–4]. Even though ART has made HIV a manageable chronic illness, it is still not a curative treatment. It is a lifelong treatment with side effects and adverse reactions, requiring sustained adherence and high rates of retention in care [1, 5, 6]. Periodic monitoring is advised for people living with HIV (PLHIV) to ensure effective and durable treatment outcomes [7, 8].

The United Nations Programme on HIV and AIDS (UNAIDS) report for 2023 indicates that HIV/AIDS is Ethiopia's major public health concern, with 610,000 PLHIV and 9,200 deaths evident from the virus in 2022 [9]. More than 94% of the PLHIV in 2023 were adults, with 20,000 new infections. Despite evidence that 65% of PLHIV were enrolled in ART in 2019, no reports of viral suppression were reported [10]. However, a recent population-based survey among urban adults (i.e., 15–64 years of age) in Ethiopia revealed a viral suppression of 70.1% with 48.2% among young adults (i.e., 15–24 years), which was notably low. Despite ranking second among urban areas with an HIV prevalence of 4.1% in Ethiopia, a similar study revealed an inconsistent viral suppression report (71%, 95% CI 49.3–94.1) for Dire Dawa because of an insufficient number of participants enrolled in the study [11].

Before 2016, virological ART monitoring was rarely conducted in Ethiopia because of the limited access to viral load testing facilities. As a result, several earlier studies conducted in Ethiopia focused on survival outcomes, and clinical and immunological assessments were the main means of detecting treatment failure [12–15]. Specifically, a previous study conducted in Dire Dawa reported 22.7% clinical and immunological failure without evaluation of virological failure status [16]. Nonetheless, the use of virological methods is the preferred and confirmed way of assessing ART failure [17]. This superiority was evident from more than 14% of incorrect switches to second-line ART as a result of clinical and immunological approaches for ART monitoring [18].

Studies conducted in Ethiopia [12–14, 19] and elsewhere [20, 21] have reported different ART failure rates (ranging from 1 to 9 per 100 person-years) and proportions (ranging from 11 to 28%) [16, 22–25]. Owing to the lack of access to viral load determination in Ethiopia before 2017, previous studies have evaluated the ART failure status by using clinical and immunological methods. Accordingly, studies that have evaluated the rate and predictors of virological treatment failure in Ethiopia are scarce. Previous studies have reported that age, sex, income and educational status are sociodemographic factors associated with virological treatment failure [26–28]. Similarly, clinical and medication-related factors such as drug adherence, drug toxicity, cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) count, tuberculosis (TB) coinfection, duration of stay on ART, World Health Organization (WHO) clinical stages, inappropriate prescribing and anaemia are mentioned as predictors of ART failure [26, 27, 29–32].

On the basis of the national and relevant WHO recommendations, the Dire Dawa City Administration Health Bureau (DDAHB) promptly scaled up routine viral load monitoring for all patients on first-line ART in 2017 [1, 33]. To design measures that can minimize the emergence of additional ART failure cases, it is crucial to know the magnitude of virological failure. Our knowledge of factors that predict virological failure also helps to identify patients who are at greater risk. While some research has been conducted on related subjects across the nation, it is difficult to apply their results to Dire Dawa because of the city's distinct socioeconomic background and comparatively elevated HIV prevalence. Hence, this study aimed to determine the magnitude and factors associated with virological failure among adults on first-line ART follow-up in Dire Dawa City, eastern Ethiopia.

Main text

Methods

Study design and setting

A facility-based retrospective chart review was conducted among adults on first-line ART in the Dire Dawa city administration, which is located in eastern Ethiopia and is 515 km away from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. The study was conducted at all the ART sites in the city. According to the central statistical agency report of 2021, the population of the Dire Dawa city administration was estimated to be 537,345, with a nearly 1:1 sex ratio [34]. According to the Dire Dawa Administration District Health Information System (DHIS2) annual report in 2018, approximately 6648 adults aged 15 years and older were on ART, with 1632 starting during a 3-year data retrieval period. Currently, 14 health facilities provide ART services. For this study, medical records of adults who initiated first-line ART between 01 January 2017 and 31 December 2019 were extracted from December 15 to 31, 2020.

Study participants

All adults who initiated first-line ART at all the ART service sites in Dire Dawa city were the source population. Adults who initiated first-line ART between 01 January 2017 and 31 December 2019 at all the ART service sites composed the study population. Adult PLHIV who completed follow-up for at least 12 months after beginning first-line ART and who had viral load evaluation results were included in this study. However, adult PLHIV who had incomplete viral load data and those who started ART at another site (i.e., with incomplete records of baseline data) were excluded.

Sample size

A sample of 272 patients was determined via a double population proportion formula, taking CD4 status into account from a previous study in Northeast Ethiopia [35], the proportion of ART failure among patients with CD4 counts of 250–500 cells/ml (22.45%), the proportion of ART failure among patients with CD4 counts of > 500 cells/ml (8.16%), the 95% confidence level, 80% power, the 1:1 ratio of exposed to nonexposed groups, and a 20% contingency for incomplete charts. Next, we allocated samples proportionally on the basis of the year of ART initiation and health facility. Finally, we used a simple random sampling method to select participants from a sampling frame constructed from patient registers and electronic databases.

Data collection methods

A structured data abstraction checklist was developed and used to collect data from the ART register, patient chart, and ART intake forms. The checklist was prepared using data from nationally standardized HIV patient intake and follow-up forms, which are based on the WHO patient monitoring recommendations. A peer-reviewed published study [13, 15, 36] was also considered to further modify the checklist and finalize it for data collection. The data abstraction format had two sections. The first part captured background information, including age, sex, marital status, education, occupation, availability of a caregiver, type of health facility, and time of ART initiation. The second part included baseline and follow-up clinical characteristics (body mass index (BMI), functional status, WHO stage, ART adherence, CD4 count, opportunistic infections, HIV disclosure status, comorbidity, duration of ART, presence of reported side effects, drug toxicity, substitution of first-line ART, missed ART doses and treatment adherence).

The data were collected by three data collectors recruited from health facilities. They were healthcare professionals who were trained in comprehensive HIV care and had experience with ART data management..

Virological first-line antiretroviral therapy (ART) failure was measured. First-line ART failure was considered to have occurred when the plasma viral load exceeded 1000 copies/ml in two consecutive measurements within a 3-month interval with adherence support after at least 6 months of initiating ART [37]. Thus, patients with viral load test results greater than 1000 copies/ml on two successive evaluations were categorized as having virological ART failure, whereas those with less than 1000 copies/ml were categorized as having virological treatment suppression. Adherence to ART is defined as the percentage of missed doses within a month and was measured by the remaining pill counts or self-reports. Clinicians consider good, fair, and poor adherence to ART if the percentage of doses taken is > 95%, 85–94%, and < 85%, respectively [38], and functional status includes working (capable of going out of home and doing routine activities), ambulatory (capable of self-care and going to the toilet unsupported), and bedridden (a patient who cannot go even to the toilet unsupported) [33]. Dropout was considered for a client who has missed appointments for more than 3 months despite attempts that have been made to contact this patient [33].

Data quality control

To maintain the quality of the data, a two-day training, which focused on the objective of the study and data extraction approach, was given to the data collectors for two days before actual data collection began. A pretest was carried out on 20 patient charts before the actual data collection to check the adequacy and consistency of the tool and the time required to complete each checklist. A necessary adjustment was made to the data abstraction format after the pretest. Misclassification bias was minimized by using uniform ascertainment criteria for those clinical variables with more than one diagnostic criterion. The collected data were checked for completeness, consistency, accuracy, and clarity daily by the investigators, and feedback was given to the data collectors throughout the process of data collection.

Data processing and analysis

After the data were checked and cleaned for completeness, they were entered into Epidata version 3.02 and then exported into STATA version 15 for further data cleaning and analysis. Descriptive analysis was performed in terms of frequency distributions and percentages to describe sociodemographic, baseline, and follow-up characteristics. The findings were presented via texts, tables, charts and graphs. The prevalence of virological ART failure was calculated by dividing the frequency of failure by the total number of participants. Both bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. In the bivariable logistic regression analysis, factors with a p value less than 0.25 were considered to be associated with virological first-line ART failure. All variables that showed associations in binary logistic regression analysis at p < 0.25 were considered for multivariable analysis to control for possible confounders.

Multicollinearity was checked with variance inflation factors (VIFs). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, a p value of less than 0.05 was used to declare statistical significance. The adjusted odds ratio along with the 95% CI was used to determine the strength of the association. The goodness of fit (gof) of the model was assessed via the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and the model was well fitted (Pearson chi-square = 10.68, p value = 0.38).

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 272 patient records were reviewed, with a 94.5% rate of record completeness. After excluding 15 patient record with incomplete information, 257 patient records were included in the final analysis. Among the total participants included, 133 (51.75%) were following ART at hospitals, and 162 (63.14%) were females. The mean age of the participants at the start of ART was 35.7 ± 10 SD years, with a range of 15–87 years. Most participants, 243 (95.29%), were from urban areas and attended formal education (71.55%), with 74 (31.9%) participants being jobless (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics

The median BMI at enrollment was 20.57 (IQR: 18.31–23.51). At baseline, the majority of participants had working functional status (81.66%), CD4 counts < 500 cells/ml (77.71%), disclosed their HIV seropositive status to family members (66.94%), had contact persons (70.51%), and initiated ART within 30 days of HIV diagnosis (62.03%), mainly with a first-line ART regimen involving tenofovir (TDF), lamivudine (3TC) and efavirenz (EFV) (63.37%). Similarly, 67 (32.84%) participants had advanced WHO clinical stages of AIDS (i.e., 21.18% at stage III and 11.82% at stage IV). During the follow-up period, the median BMI at the 6th, 12th and 24th months was 21.50 (IQR: 18.91–24.21), 22.10 (IQR: 19.36–24.75) and 22.23 (IQR: 19.33–25.28), respectively. The majority of participants had working functional status at the 6th month (87.01%). The proportion of patients with advanced WHO clinical stage disease (stage III or IV) at the 6th month of follow-up was 30.10%. The mean duration of ART was approximately 30 months (29.66 ± 10.03), with approximately 10% of patients having poor ART adherence in the 6th month. The majority of patients had completed isoniazid (INH) (64.73%), with approximately one-third of them completing cotrimoxazole preventive therapy (CPT) (37.97%). Approximately 30 (11.95%) participants were diagnosed with tuberculosis (TB) comorbidity during the follow-up period. Among the 257 participants included in this study, 29 (11.28%) (95% CI 7.69, 15.80) experienced virological first-line ART failure (Table 2).

Factors associated with virological first-line ART failure

In the bivariable logistic regression analysis, sex, age, baseline BMI, HIV seropositive status disclosure, baseline WHO clinical stage, duration before ART initiation after HIV diagnosis, history of missed ART doses and substitution of the dolutegravir (DTG)-based ART first-line regimen were associated with virological first-line ART failure, with p values < 0.25. The variables that had a potential association with virological failure in the bivariable analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to control for confounding variables and identify factors associated with virological first-line ART failure.

According to the multivariable logistic regression model, baseline BMI, HIV seropositive status disclosure, duration from HIV diagnosis to ART initiation, history of missed ART doses, and substitution of the DTG-based ART regimen were significantly associated with virological first-line ART failure (p value < 0.05). Accordingly, the odds of virological first-line ART failure were 4.45 times greater among patients who did not disclose their HIV seropositive status than among patients who reported their HIV seropositive status (AOR: 4.45; 95% CI 1.34–14.79). The likelihood of virological first-line ART failure was 3.71 times greater among undernourished patients (baseline BMI ≤ 18.5) than among patients with normal nutritional status (BMI = 18.5–24.99) (AOR: 3.71; 95% CI 1.05–13.14). Patients who lately (> 30 days) initiated ART after HIV diagnosis were 4.25 times more likely to experience virological failure (AOR: 4.25; 95% CI 1.16–15.51) than those who initiated ART earlier (≤ 30 days). Similarly, the odds of virological first-line ART failure were decreased by 72.5% among patients who initiated the newly recommended DTG-based regimen compared with patients on other regimens (AOR: 0.275; 95% CI 0. 09 -0.89). Moreover, virological first-line ART failure was 3.16 times more likely among patients who had a recorded history of missed ART (AOR: 3.16; 95% CI 1.01–9.89) than among their counterparts (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the magnitude and factors associated with virological treatment failure among adults initiated on first-line ART between January 2017 and December 2019 in Dire Dawa city administration, eastern Ethiopia. In this study, the magnitude of first-line ART failure was 11.28% (95% CI 7.69–15.80). This study also revealed factors associated with virological failure, including baseline BMI, HIV serostatus disclosure, duration of ART initiation after HIV diagnosis, history of missed ART doses, and substitution of DTG-based ART first-line regimens.

In this study, the magnitude of first-line ART failure was 11.28% (95% CI 7.69, 15.80). This magnitude highlights a significant public health problem and a substantial challenge in effectively managing HIV treatment, necessitating focused attention and intervention to optimize treatment outcomes. This finding was in line with previous studies from different parts of Ethiopia (Tigray region of northern Ethiopia (11.5%), North Shoa Zone, Amhara Region, Ethiopia (10.24%) and Weliso town, Oromia region, Ethiopia (13%)), Uganda (11%) and four African countries (9%) [31, 32, 39–41]. However, this finding was lower than the findings of previous studies performed in Ethiopia [22, 35, 42], Nigeria [43], India [44], China [45], and Swaziland [46]. This discrepancy might be explained by methodological differences in the evaluation of failure, sample size, socioeconomic status, and medical services, all of which have a significant influence on the identification of treatment failure. This variation may also result from variations in the study setting and the expertise levels of healthcare professionals providing medication adherence and counselling.

This study also revealed that HIV serostatus disclosure decreases the odds of virological failure. The nondisclosure of HIV serostatus negatively influences ART adherence [47, 48], with consequent virological failure [49, 50]. Unlike this study, a previous study in Ethiopia reported an increase in the incidence of virological failure among adults with disclosed HIV-positive status, perhaps due to stigma and discrimination [51]. If it is not well handled, disclosure to friends and families may lead to a loss of support [48] and HIV serostatus-related discrimination [52]. Further studies are needed to reach a consensus. In this study, missing ART doses increased the odds of virological failure. This finding is congruent with those of the Nekemte Specialized Hospital, Western Ethiopia, African Cohort Study and Myanmar [21, 41, 53]. The increase in treatment failure among patients who miss the ART dose (inadequate ART adherence) may be due to the development of acquired resistance [54, 55]. ART interruption might result in a loss of opportunity to suppress viral replication, leading to virological failure [56, 57].

In this study, undernourished patients (with a baseline BMI < 18.5 kg/m2) had greater odds of virological failure than did patients with a normal BMI (with a baseline BMI = 18.5–24.99 kg/m2). This finding was consistent with those of studies performed in Northwest Ethiopia, Mettu Karl Specialized Hospital, Southwest Ethiopia and Borno State, Nigeria [58–60]. The possible reason for this might be that patients with a low BMI have a low nutritional status, which leads to weakened immunity, a blunted immune response and a poor virological response [61–63]. Late ART initiation (initiation after 30 days of HIV diagnosis) was significantly associated with increased odds of virological failure. This finding was consistent with reports from other studies that early ART may benefit HIV patients by enhancing viral suppression and survival [64–66]. Despite the lack of sufficient data for a similar duration of ART, the findings of this study suggest that rapid ART initiation generally leads to improved virological outcomes.

Moreover, this study revealed that the rate of first-line ART failure decreases among patients on the recently recommended DTG-based ART regimen compared with those on other regimens. This finding was in line with previous studies, which reported evidence of treatment success while on DTG [67, 68]. On the basis of the WHO recommendation [1], Ethiopia’s ART guidelines have been revised to introduce first-line use of integrase inhibitors (e.g., DTG) in preference to EFV since August 2018 [69]. Resistances to EFV are important drivers to change it to DTG [70], while DTG-based regimens are potent treatment options [68, 71]. Poor adherence is one way by which drug resistance develops [72].

The findings of this study could contribute to our understanding of the magnitude and factors associated with virological failure in adults receiving first-line antiretroviral therapy, which may have implications for both clinical and public health practice. The clinical importance of this study was to guide healthcare providers in tailoring adherence support interventions, addressing barriers to treatment adherence, and implementing strategies to improve treatment outcomes among adults on first-line ART. The public health importance of this study was to provide valuable information for public health planners in Dire Dawa and similar settings. This study can also help with resource allocation and optimization by identifying the factors associated with virological therapy failure, ultimately leading to improved virological outcomes.

Conclusion

Virological first-line treatment failure among adult ART users was significantly high in Dire Dawa. Underweight nutritional status, nondisclosure of HIV serostatus, and interruption of ART doses by patients increased the magnitude of virological failure. The study findings also emphasize early initiation of ART (preferably a DTG-based regimen) with frequent virological monitoring to sustain the ART treatment program. All the stakeholders in the Dire Dawa HIV control program need to check the recommended standard of care and intervention to address the specified contributing factors. Therefore, disclosure of HIV serostatus, reinforcing adherence interventions and early initiation of ART, preferably with a DTG-based regimen, is important to minimize treatment failure.

Strength and limitations

The strength of this study was the consideration of all the ART centres in Dire Dawa through proportional allocation, which increased the external validity of the study. The limitation of this study was the use of secondary data, which results in incomplete information for some parameters, and it could underestimate the findings and reduce the statistical power of the study. The measures of some variables, such as adherence status, are subjective and susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias. Furthermore, in this study, the odds ratio estimated with logistic regression may tend to overestimate the risk ratio (showing a stronger effect than in reality).
