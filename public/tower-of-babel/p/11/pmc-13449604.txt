Ileocecal resection for primary Crohn’s disease: a 17-year experience from a tertiary care hospital in North Africa
Authors: Souhaib Atri, Mahdi Hammami, Ahmed Ben Mahmoud, Amine Sebai, Houcine Maghrebi, Wael Rebai, Youssef Chaker, Amine Makni, Amine Daghfous, Rachid Ksantini, Mohamed Jouini, Anis Haddad, Montassar Kacem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIleocecal resection (ICR) is the most common procedure for primary Crohn’s disease. While outcomes of ICR have been extensively studied in Europe and North America, data from North Africa remains scarce. This study reports a 17-year Tunisian experience, evaluating the feasibility of laparoscopic ICR in a low-income country in comparison to open surgery.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center study reviewed patients who underwent ICR for histologically confirmed Crohn’s disease between 2004 and 2020. Postoperative outcomes of laparoscopic and open approaches were compared. The endpoint was postoperative morbidity and mortality, intraoperative parameters, and recovery metrics.ResultsA total of 336 patients underwent surgery for non-recurrent ileocecal Crohn’s disease, with laparoscopic resection performed in 80% of cases. Conversion to open surgery occurred in 14.9%, mainly due to hemorrhagic dissection. Postoperative complications occurred in 9.2% of patients, with anastomotic leakage being the most frequent surgical issue. Medical complications were rare (1.2%). Laparoscopy was associated with faster recovery, shorter hospital stays, and lower analgesic requirements, although complication rates were not significantly different. The only significant predictor of anastomotic fistula was low body mass index (BMI) (P < .001). Postoperative mortality was 0.5%.ConclusionIn low-income countries, including North Africa, access to laparoscopy has expanded, with outcomes such as conversion rates and operative times approaching international standards. In our 17-year Tunisian experience, laparoscopic ICR proved feasible, reproducible, and safe. These findings contribute to the limited regional evidence and support the broader adoption of laparoscopy as the preferred surgical approach for Crohn’s disease.Keywords: Crohn’s disease, surgery, laparoscopy, open, postoperative complications, retrospective

Introduction

First described in 1932 in the United States by gastroenterologist Burrill Bernard Crohn, Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic inflammatory condition of the intestine characterized by relapsing and remitting episodes.1 This inflammation is segmental and transmural, most commonly affecting the terminal ileum but potentially involving any part of the gastrointestinal tract.2

Despite recent advances in medical treatments, more than 60%-70% of patients with CD will require surgery within 10 years of diagnosis.3 Surgery remains a key component of the therapeutic arsenal for CD. The primary indications for resection include symptomatic strictures, inflammatory masses, and fistulas, particularly when medical therapy has failed. In the absence of postoperative complications, surgical intervention leads to significant improvements in quality of life as early as 1 month after the procedure.4

The most commonly performed surgical procedure is ileocecal resection (ICR).

Most of the available data on surgical outcomes in CD come from high-income countries in Europe and North America, while reports from low- and middle-income countries, particularly in North Africa, remain scarce. This underrepresentation limits the understanding of regional disease patterns, access to care, and long-term outcomes.

In this context, the present study reports a 17-year experience with ICR for primary CD in a Tunisian tertiary care hospital. Our objective is to evaluate the feasibility of laparoscopic ICR, assess its impact on short- and medium-term postoperative outcomes, and compare it with open surgical approaches in a region where data remain limited. The study is based on the assumption that laparoscopic ICR is feasible and advantageous in a resource-limited environment, offering recovery, morbidity, and anastomotic leak outcomes equivalent to international standards.

This work was reported in line with the STROBE guidelines.5

Methods

We conducted a retrospective, single-center study that reviewed the records of patients who underwent surgery for ileocecal CD at the General Surgery Department “A” of Rabta Hospital in Tunis over a 17-year period, from January 1, 2004, to December 31, 2020. Patients were identified via the institutional surgical registry and cross-checked with electronic and paper records. Data were extracted independently by 2 investigators, with discrepancies resolved by consensus with a senior surgeon.

The Ethics Committee approved the study and waived informed consent due to its retrospective nature.

The study included a descriptive analysis to detail the epidemiological, clinical, therapeutic, and evolutionary characteristics of the patients. Additionally, an analytical study was performed to evaluate the outcomes of the laparoscopic approach and compare them with those of laparotomy.

Disease assessment

A detailed lesion assessment specifying the nature (stenosis, fistula, or inflammatory form) and topography of the lesions was mandatory. This evaluation was clinical and required a thorough examination of the anal margin to identify potential ano-perineal lesions, along with documentation of any extraintestinal manifestations.

The choice of investigations depended on the symptomatology and the location of the disease:

Ileo-colonoscopy: In cases of terminal ileitis (the most common lesion site), this investigation was used to determine the presence or absence of sigmoid colon involvement (diseased or affected segment). These findings influenced the surgical strategy.

Contrast-enhanced enterography (CT enterography)/magnetic resonance enterography (MR enterography): This imaging study was performed to assess the extent of lesions in the small bowel and to evaluate the characteristics of any stenosis. In cases of Crohn’s abscess, broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy combined with percutaneous drainage were indicated if the abscess size exceeds 4 cm. A follow-up CT scan was performed 5-7 days after the initial intervention. If the drainage were successful, an ICR was subsequently undertaken. In cases of failure, a resection with a closed approach was performed.

Perioperative protocol

Perioperative management adhered to standardized institutional practices. Antibiotic prophylaxis was administered in accordance with ASHP guidelines (American Society of Health-System Pharmacists), and venous thromboembolism prevention was ensured through low-molecular-weight heparin. Early mobilization was routinely encouraged, and oral intake was advanced based on patient tolerance. Notably, a formal Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) program was not available during the study period.

Surgical technique

Two operative approaches were used: laparoscopy and open surgery. The laparoscopic technique followed a standardized protocol used consistently throughout the study period. After a four-trocar setup, the ileocecal region was mobilized using a retro-mesenteric dissection, which provided a reproducible and safe exposure of the terminal ileum while preserving the mesenteric vasculature. Once mobilization was completed, the specimen was exteriorized through a small right iliac fossa incision, where bowel division and anastomosis were performed extracorporeally. The mesentery was divided close to the bowel to preserve intestinal length, and resection was limited to macroscopically diseased tissue with disease-free margins.

For patients undergoing open surgery, a conventional midline approach with lateral-to-medial mobilization of the right colon was used. The same principles of limited mesenteric resection, bowel preservation, and extracorporeal anastomosis were applied. Anastomotic reconstruction (end-to-end, end-to-side, or side-to-side; hand-sewn or stapled) was chosen based on intraoperative findings and surgeon preference.

The choice between laparoscopic and open surgery depended on disease severity, prior abdominal operations, surgeon experience, and equipment availability. Conversion to open surgery was performed when dense inflammation, bleeding, or inadequate visualization prevented safe laparoscopic progress.

Results

Demographics

Over the 17-year period from January 2004 to December 2020, 336 patients underwent surgery for non-recurrent ileocecal CD. The median age at operation was 31 years (range: 11-73 years), with the 20-30-year age group being the most represented (34.90%). In the preoperative phase, 22 patients (6.55%) were receiving corticosteroids, while no patient was on immunosuppressive agents or biologic therapy. Nutritional and biological assessments showed malnutrition in 50 patients (14.88%), anemia in 66 (19.64%), hypoalbuminemia in 82 (24.40%), and elevated inflammatory markers in 187 (55.65%). Preoperative optimization frequently involved RBC transfusion (8.33%), intravenous iron therapy (11.31%), and albumin infusion (21.43%).

Ileocecal disease complicated with abdominal abscesses was present in 125 patients (37.20%). Small abscesses (<4 cm) were managed medically (69.6%), whereas larger or drainable collections were treated with radiological drainage in 34 cases (27.2%), and 4 patients (3.20%) required surgical drainage.

Baseline characteristics including sex, age, ASA score, BMI, and smoking status were comparable between the laparoscopic and open groups (Table 1).

The majority of procedures were performed electively, with 302 patients (89.9%) undergoing scheduled surgery. Thirty-four patients (10.1%) required urgent intervention, primarily for subacute bowel obstruction or persistent intra-abdominal sepsis despite medical or radiological management.

Postoperative outcome

Three hundred and five patients had an uncomplicated postoperative course (90.8%), while 31 patients experienced postoperative complications (9.2%).

Medical complications

Four patients experienced a medical complication (1.2%): 1 patient with pulmonary embolism requiring anticoagulant therapy at a curative dose, 1 patient with thrombosis of a dialysis arteriovenous fistula requiring the placement of a new dialysis catheter, 1 patient with an epileptic seizure requiring anticonvulsant treatment, and 1 patient with an acute postoperative hepatitis.

Discussion

This study represents one of the largest series reported in Tunisia addressing the laparoscopic approach for ileocecal CD. Additionally, the study duration was sufficiently long to analyze the outcomes of laparoscopy in ICR for CD. The surgical technique used in our department was standardized and reproducible.6

The era when surgery was believed to cure CD is long gone. Surgery can provide patients with a symptom-free interval, but recurrence remains inevitable. Our study included patients with non-recurrent ileal or ileocecal involvement. Today, aside from complicated cases, several therapeutic strategies can be considered for the same patient (step-up, top-down approaches).7 Therefore, timely indicated surgery does not contradict medical treatment but rather helps achieve remission and improves quality of life. For surgical indications, isolated stenosis was the most frequent reason for surgery (45%).

Preoperatively, nutritional status is assessed based on the severity of malnutrition, albumin, and hemoglobin levels. Enteral and parenteral nutrition is necessary in cases of severe malnutrition and hypoalbuminemia (strictly below 30 g/L), as these are risk factors for anastomotic fistula formation.8–10 According to Adamina et al., preoperative nutritional optimization significantly reduces postoperative morbidity (21.9% vs. 73.2%, Odds Ratio (OR) 0.09, 95% CI, 0.06-0.13, P < .001). Preoperative corticosteroid dose tapering may reduce postoperative complications and the need for a stoma. However, discontinuation of anti-TNF alpha is not necessary.11 Preoperative sepsis control helps decrease postoperative septic complications.

Our study demonstrated that laparoscopic surgery for ileocecal CD is feasible and reproducible using a standardized technique that ensures retro-mesenteric, pre-duodenal dissection from bottom to top. We observed an acceptable conversion rate (14.9%), with difficult and hemorrhagic dissection being the primary reason for conversion (32.5% of cases, P < .001). A 2018 study by Medge et al., involving 427 patients, reported a conversion rate of 21.6%, with difficult dissection as the main cause (34% of cases).12

Laparoscopy is now considered the standard surgical approach, even for complex ileocecal CD.13 A 2022 study by Connelly et al. demonstrated the feasibility of laparoscopy for complex cases.14 The reproducibility of a surgical intervention is crucial for its validation, with blood loss and operative time serving as key indicators.15 In our study, laparoscopy and laparotomy were statistically comparable in terms of intraoperative bleeding (P = .1) and operative time (P = .75). Both groups also had similar intraoperative transfusion rates. Studies comparing laparoscopic and open approaches for ileocecal CD have generally found comparable intraoperative bleeding, though laparoscopic procedures are often reported to take longer.16–22 However, our study showed comparable operative times for both approaches, likely due to the retro-mesenteric approach, which is straightforward and well-mastered by our team, preventing prolonged operative duration. Studies showed also that laparoscopy is comparable to robotic-assisted approach in terms of complications and long-term outcome.23

Postoperative morbidity for CD is estimated at 25%, primarily due to the patients’ overall condition and immunosuppressive treatment with corticosteroids.24 Following ICR, nonspecific complications are rare, while specific complications, such as wound abscesses and anastomotic fistulas, are more common. The study by Ponsioen et al. reported an anastomotic leak rate of 4% in 70 patients,25 while Celentano et al. found a 3.7% rate in 427 patients,26 which was independent of the anastomotic technique but associated with ASA scores ≥3, penetrating disease phenotype, and ileocolic involvement. In our study, the postoperative morbidity rate was 9.2%, with an anastomotic leak rate of 5.7%, which aligns with literature findings. Two cases of postoperative acute peritonitis (0.5%) required reoperation. The postoperative mortality rate was 0.5%. Univariate analysis identified malnutrition as the only significant risk factor for anastomotic leakage (P < .001).

In our cohort, patient recovery was faster after laparoscopic surgery compared to open surgery: bowel function recovery (2 days vs. 3 days, P < .001) and oral intake resumption (3 days vs. 4 days, P < .001). The total length of hospital stay was also shorter (5 days vs. 7.9 days, P < .001). A meta-analysis including 2 randomized controlled trials confirmed the benefits of laparoscopy in postoperative recovery.21,27,28 Advantages of laparoscopy include faster bowel function recovery, shorter hospital stays, and lower morbidity rates. This has been confirmed by an American national registry study analyzing 49 609 resections for CD.29

With laparoscopy, we also observed a significant reduction in postoperative use of level 2 and level 3 analgesics (P < .001 and P = .03, respectively). Pak et al. reported lower postoperative pain scores in laparoscopic patients compared to open surgery (1.4 vs. 2.3, P < .001).30

Postoperative complications were lower in the laparoscopic group (8.9%) compared to the laparotomy group (10.4%), though this difference was not statistically significant (P = .6). However, nonspecific complications were significantly lower in the laparoscopic approach (P = .03), while surgical complications were comparable between groups (8.92% vs. 8.95%, P = .6).

Studies by Polle et al. and Tilney et al. found no significant differences in postoperative outcomes.20,21 However, a more recent meta-analysis by Patel et al. analyzing 30 studies with 1079 laparoscopic and 1221 open surgery patients demonstrated that laparoscopy significantly reduced postoperative morbidity risk (Risk Ratio 0.71, 95% CI, 0.58-0.86, P = .001).31 Pak et al. reported a significantly lower overall postoperative complication rate for laparoscopic resection compared to laparotomy (13.7% vs. 32.4%, P = .003).30 Intra-abdominal abscesses and anastomotic fistulas were significantly less frequent in the laparoscopic group (P = .035 and P = .014, respectively). Laparoscopy also showed advantages in terms of reduced blood transfusion rates (P = .021) and reoperation rates (P = .029).

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Its retrospective design inherently limits the control of confounding variables, introduces potential selection bias, and depends on the completeness and accuracy of medical records, particularly during earlier years of the study period. Although all data were extracted independently by 2 investigators, some variables—such as comorbidities, nutritional metrics, and preoperative medical therapy—were occasionally incomplete, and no multivariate analysis was performed, restricting the ability to adjust for interacting risk factors. Surgeon-related variability, including different levels of laparoscopic experience, may also have influenced operative and postoperative outcomes despite our sensitivity analysis showing no significant differences. The inclusion of a small number of younger patients (≥10 years) managed within an adult surgical unit may limit comparability with purely adult cohorts. Additionally, the absence of biologic therapy in this North African setting reflects local resource constraints and limits the generalizability of our findings to countries where biologics are widely used before surgery. Preoperative imaging interpretation and intraoperative assessment were not standardized across radiologists and surgeons, potentially introducing classification variability, particularly in differentiating inflammatory from fibrotic stenosis. Although postoperative outcomes up to 30 days were consistently available, the study lacked long-term follow-up, preventing assessment of recurrence, quality of life, or late complications such as stenosis or reoperation rates. Finally, the availability and use of enhanced recovery programs were limited, and their absence may influence the comparability of postoperative recovery metrics with centers that routinely implement ERAS protocols. These limitations reflect both the retrospective nature of the study and the realities of a lower-middle-income healthcare system, and they should be considered when interpreting our findings.

Conclusion

The surgical management of CD has undergone substantial evolution over the past 2 decades, with ICR remaining the most frequently performed procedure. Despite therapeutic advances, surgery continues to play a pivotal role in disease management. The advent and progressive adoption of laparoscopy have further consolidated its importance, particularly among younger patients. While laparotomy remains necessary in complex cases, laparoscopy is increasingly regarded as the standard approach for ICR, provided that it demonstrates safety, feasibility, and reproducibility, with acceptable conversion rates and outcomes equivalent to or superior to open surgery.

In recent years, access to laparoscopy has steadily expanded in low-income countries, including North Africa, with improved results reflected in decreasing conversion rates and operative times comparable to international series. In our experience, the laparoscopic approach to ICR proved both feasible and reproducible, particularly when combined with adherence to the principle of intestinal conservation. On this basis, we strongly recommend laparoscopy as the preferred technique and specifically advocate the total retro-mesenteric approach for its consistency and reliability.

This 17-year Tunisian experience contributes to the limited body of evidence from North Africa and underscores that favorable outcomes can be achieved in low-resource settings. By providing robust regional data, our study enriches the global literature on the surgical management of CD and supports the broader adoption of laparoscopic strategies.
