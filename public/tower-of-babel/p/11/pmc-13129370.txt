Advances and challenges of hydrogel electrolytes for rechargeable zinc-iodine batteries
Authors: Meiqi Zhao, Yalan Yao, Zixuan Yang, Yingkui Yang, Yunhai Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryZinc-iodine batteries (ZIBs) show great promise for safe, economical energy storage, benefiting from their high theoretical capacity and inherent safety. However, their practical deployment is severely limited by rapid capacity decay and premature cell failure, primarily originating from the polyiodide shuttling effect at the iodine cathode and interfacial instabilities at the zinc (Zn) anode, including dendrite growth and parasitic side reactions. Recently, functionalized hydrogel electrolytes (HEs) have attracted increasing attention as an effective strategy to address these challenges. This review comprehensively summarizes the design principles and working mechanisms of HEs in ZIBs, focusing specifically on their ability to suppress polyiodide shuttling through electrostatic regulation, chemical anchoring, and synergistic confinement effects, while simultaneously stabilizing Zn anodes by modulating ion transport, interfacial chemistry, and mechanical constraints. The underlying physicochemical mechanisms and representative implementation strategies are critically discussed. Finally, the current limitations of HEs in ZIBs are analyzed, and future research directions are suggested, including the enhancement of multi-electron iodine chemistries, wide-temperature operation, and multifunctional hydrogel systems. This review focuses on providing mechanistic insights and rational design guidelines for advancing high-performance ZIBs.

Introduction

Driven by the rising demand for high-performance and sustainable energy storage solutions,1 lithium-ion batteries have undergone rapid development over the past three decades.2 They are widely used in electric vehicles, aerospace applications, and portable electronics, and currently dominate the global energy storage market.3 However, issues such as scarce lithium resources, high costs, and the toxic and flammable nature of organic electrolytes present a major barrier to their widespread adoption.4,5 Consequently, safe, environmentally friendly, and low-cost zinc-iodine batteries (ZIBs) have emerged and continue to evolve through ongoing research and application.6,7 Zinc (Zn) metal offers advantages such as abundant reserves, low production costs, high theoretical capacity, and suitable electrochemical potential. Meanwhile, aqueous electrolyte systems not only eliminate the safety hazards of traditional organic systems but also feature high ionic conductivity, environmental friendliness, and simplified assembly processes.8,9 These characteristics make them an ideal candidate to replace lithium-ion batteries for both grid-scale storage and flexible electronics.

Among numerous aqueous Zn-based battery systems, the ZIBs are now at the forefront of research owing to their outstanding comprehensive performance. Its core advantages are as follows: (1) iodine cathode materials are naturally abundant and inexpensive.; (2) reaction mechanism is simple and highly reversible, avoiding structural collapse issues caused by ion insertion/extraction in metal oxide cathodes; (3) it possesses high theoretical energy density and the potential for multi-electron high-valency transformations, enabling further performance enhancement.10,11,12 Nevertheless, the path to commercialization is hindered by two critical issues. A major challenge on the iodine cathode side is the severe “polyiodide shuttle effect,” which is manifested by the diffusion of soluble polyiodide intermediates (such as I3−and I5−) through the electrolyte to the Zn anode. These intermediates undergo irreversible side reactions with Zn, leading to active material loss, significant self-discharge, reduced coulombic efficiency, and degradation of the anode interface. On the Zn anode side, issues such as Zn dendrite growth, hydrogen corrosion, and passivation collectively lead to reduced battery cycle life and diminished reversibility.13,14,15

The performance enhancement of ZIBs critically depends on effectively suppressing the polyiodide shuttle effect at the cathode and the dendrite growth at the anode. The field has investigated a range of approaches to tackle these issues, including high-concentration electrolytes,16 functional electrolyte additives,17 and deep-eutectic electrolytes.18 Among them, hydrogel electrolytes (HEs) stand out owing to their unique solid-liquid composite structure and designable functionality.19 Its three-dimensional polymer network can suppress the shuttle effect of polyiodide through size exclusion, while the introduced functional groups further promote their conversion via electrostatic interactions, chemical anchoring, and catalysis, thereby inhibiting the shuttle effect involving both physicochemical processes. Simultaneously, the robust mechanical strength of the hydrogel effectively restrains the penetration of Zn dendrites, and its abundant ion-transport channels, together with good interfacial compatibility, ensure efficient ion conduction and a stable electrode-electrolyte interface.20,21 These integrated features collectively improve the cycle stability, coulombic efficiency, and safety of ZIBs, offering a multifunctional viable pathway toward advanced aqueous energy-storage systems.

Although recent reviews have summarized the application of HEs in ZIBs, their focus has largely centered on challenges associated with Zn anodes and corresponding countermeasures.22,23 However, dedicated reviews specifically addressing ZIBs-systematically outlining their core challenges (particularly the shuttle effect at the iodine cathode) and multifunctional HEs solutions-remain relatively scarce. To address this gap, the current review centers on the functional design and working mechanisms of HEs in ZIBs. First, we will outline the underlying principles and critical issues of ZIBs, along with the fundamental properties of HEs. Subsequently, the core content will systematically discuss the regulation mechanisms of functionalized HEs: For single HEs, it will delve into how they suppress the shuttle effect and stabilize the Zn anode through charge regulation and chemical interaction regulation; for composite HEs (including doped, dual-network (DN), and Janus structures), it will emphasize how they achieve dual stabilization of both electrode interfaces through the synergy of composition and structure. Finally, this view will explore future development directions for HEs in ZIBs, including potential avenues such as promoting four-electron transfer, expanding wide-temperature adaptability, and developing flexible devices. Mechanism and analysis of key issues in aqueous ZIBs

Polyiodide shuttling

The two-electron reaction in ZIBs is based on the reversible conversion between iodide ions (I−) and iodine (I2) with an operating voltage around 1.3 V.24,25,26 This redox process enables energy storage and release through the valence state changes of iodine species.27 However, the self-discharge phenomenon has always been a key issue restricting the performance of this type of battery, and its occurrence is intimately related to the inherent characteristics of iodine and the side reactions during the reaction process.28,29 Due to iodine’s inherently poor conductivity and low utilization efficiency during the reaction, only a portion of I− successfully converts to I2; the remaining I− spontaneously combines with the generated I2 to form polyiodides (such as I3− and I5−).30 These polyiodides will be driven by the distributions of electric potential and ion concentration to break through the membrane barrier and migrate toward the Zn negative electrode (Figure 2B). This is the typical polyiodide shuttle phenomenon in ZIBs.31 The polyiodide that migrates to the negative electrode will undergo parasitic reactions with the Zn anode, causing the corrosion and passivation of the Zn anode, thereby significantly shortening the battery cycle life.32,33,34(Equation 1)I−+I2↔I3−(Equation 2)I3−+I2↔I5−Figure 2Schematic diagram of key mechanisms in ZIBs(A–C) Schematic diagram of ZIBs charging and discharging (A). Zn anode instability

Although ZIBs offer safety and environmental friendliness due to their use of aqueous electrolytes, water acts as a double-edged sword during battery cycling: Its presence directly or indirectly triggers unavoidable parasitic reactions such as Zn dendrite growth, hydrogen corrosion, and electrode passivation. Dendrite growth stems from the synergistic effects of the tip effect and accumulation effect: Zn ions preferentially deposit at surface protrusions where charge is concentrated, forming persistent “charge centers” that continuously attract further Zn ion deposition.37 From a fundamental electrokinetic perspective, Sand’s model of time-dependent concentration polarization provides critical insight: When the local current density exceeds the limiting diffusion current, the interfacial ion concentration depletes to zero at a characteristic transition time (the Sand time), triggering severe concentration polarization and local electric field amplification. This depletion-driven polarization, coupled with kinetic mismatch, leads to excessive accumulation of ions/electrons at the interface, creating strong local electric fields that severely exacerbate the non-uniformity of Zn deposition.38,39,40

To experimentally probe the origin of these kinetic and polarization phenomena, it is essential to characterize the solvation structure of Zn2+ in the electrolyte. Advanced spectroscopic techniques provide complementary insights into the local coordination environment. Raman spectroscopy can identify the presence of contact ion pairs (CIPs) and solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs) by tracking changes in the Zn2+-O (water) and anion-related vibrational modes. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), particularly 1H, 17O, and 67Zn NMR, reveals variations in electron density around Zn2+ and its ligands, thus reflecting alterations in the primary solvation sheath. Meanwhile, X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectroscopy, including XANES and EXAFS, delivers quantitative information on the coordination number and bond distance between Zn2+ and surrounding oxygen atoms. A well-defined solvation structure, characterized by reduced water activity and enhanced anion participation or functional additive incorporation, can effectively mitigate concentration polarization, thereby suppressing dendrite nucleation and growth from the very beginning.

Hydrogen evolution is thermodynamically almost unavoidable, as the reduction potential of Zn lies below that of hydrogen evolution across most pH conditions. This reaction proceeds through hydrogen adsorption and recombination steps, not only consuming active material and reducing coulombic efficiency but also potentially causing battery swelling and safety hazards due to hydrogen gas generation.41 Simultaneously, in weakly acidic to neutral electrolytes, the dissolving Zn anode reacts with anions to form porous, non-protective passivation byproducts, such as Zn hydroxide sulfate (ZnSO4(OH)6·xH2O, ZSH).42 These byproducts fail to form effective passivation layers, instead increasing interfacial polarization, hindering ion transport, and promoting dendrite nucleation. These failure mechanisms are not isolated but interact to form a vicious cycle: Dendrite growth expands the electrode/electrolyte contact area, intensifying surface side reactions such as hydrogen evolution and corrosion; hydrogen evolution consumes H+, raising local pH and promoting passivation product formation; while the passivation layer impedes Zn ion transport, exacerbating local polarization and further driving dendrite growth. Therefore, promoting reversible cycling and service life of Zn anodes requires a synergistic strategy that simultaneously suppresses dendrite growth, hydrogen corrosion, and surface passivation.

The two major challenges, polyiodide shuttling and Zn anode failure (dendrite growth, corrosion, and passivation), are not isolated phenomena. Instead, they are interrelated and mutually reinforcing, collectively accelerating the overall battery degradation process. On one hand, polyiodides shuttled to the anode directly undergo parasitic reactions with Zn, intensifying corrosion and destabilizing the interface. On the other hand, uneven deposition and byproducts (such as passivation layers) on the Zn anode surface further promote polyiodide formation and migration, creating a vicious cycle of “shuttle-induced anode degradation.”

Therefore, traditional strategies relying solely on single-function modifications are insufficient for achieving long-term stability. There is a critical requirement for the development of a synergistic electrolyte system capable of “simultaneously participating in and regulating reactions at both electrode interfaces.” This system must suppress I2 hydrolysis and polyiodide leaching at the cathode while guiding homogeneous Zn deposition and inhibiting side reactions at the anode. Through molecular-level bidirectional design and functional coupling, it fundamentally breaks the aforementioned failure cycle, achieving simultaneous enhancement of the overall performance of ZIBs.

The aforementioned principles governing the design of HEs, covering structural design, functional group regulation, mechanical properties, and interface optimization, lay the theoretical and material foundations for their functional applications in specific battery systems. In particular, for ZIBs, which face key challenges such as polyiodide shuttling and Zn anode corrosion, the design flexibility of HEs offers unique advantages in terms of adaptability.

Foundational principles for constructing hydrogel electrolytes

Based on the aforementioned design principles, the application of HEs in ZIBs is attracting widespread attention. ZIBs offer excellent prospects for development due to their high safety, low cost, and suitable redox potential. HEs serve as an essential element in ZIBs, playing a crucial part in ion conduction, interface stability, and functional regulation through their unique three-dimensional polymer network structure. This quasi-solid electrolyte is formed by hydrophilic polymers via physical entanglement, electrostatic interactions, or chemical crosslinking, with water and Zn salts filling its interior to provide fundamental pathways for ion transport. During battery operation, ions migrate directionally at the electrode-electrolyte interface, driving charge-discharge reactions. However, ZIBs are still grappling with long-standing challenges such as Zn dendrite growth, hydrogen evolution reactions, and byproduct formation. These factors severely limit their coulombic efficiency, cycling stability, and overall safety.

The nanoscale porous structure of HEs not only provides high specific surface area and tunable liquid-to-solid ratios but also enables precise control over mechanical properties43 and ion transport behavior44 through structural design. Their ionic conductivity typically ranges between that of solid-state polymer electrolytes and traditional liquid electrolytes. Zn ions migrate primarily through two mechanisms: structural diffusion and carrier diffusion. Water molecules in hydrogels exist in three forms: free water, intermediate water, and bound water.45 The high proportion of bound water significantly reduces water’s chemical reactivity, thereby suppressing side reactions such as hydrogen evolution and Zn corrosion. Material-wise, HEs are primarily based on two systems: synthetic polymers and natural polymers (Figure 3). Synthetic polymers such as polyacrylamide (PAM),46 polyvinyl alcohol (PVA),47,48 and polyacrylic acid (PAA)49,50 are rich in hydrophilic groups such as amino (-NH2), hydroxyl (-OH), and carboxyl (-COOH). These groups maintain high water retention through hydrogen bonding and can regulate Zn ion solvation structures and desolvation kinetics via chemical anchoring effects while inhibiting polyiodide shuttling.51,52 Natural polymers such as alginates,53 cellulose,54 and chitosa55 leverage their abundant hydrophilic groups and superior biocompatibility to suppress side reactions through electrostatic interactions or coordination bonds, thereby guiding uniform Zn ion deposition. Furthermore, these negatively charged or highly polar groups significantly inhibit the migration of soluble intermediates, such as polyiodides, through electrostatic repulsion, size exclusion, and selective adsorption. This provides crucial support for addressing shuttle effects in ZIBs systems, enhancing cycle stability and coulombic efficiency. Regarding mechanical properties, HEs can serve as integrated electrolyte-separator systems, with their strength tunable through different crosslinking approaches: Physical crosslinking provides flexibility, chemical crosslinking imparts strength, while DN structures achieve a balance of strength and elasticity. Some HEs exhibit high tensile strengths and elongation at break, enabling them to effectively counteract issues such as electrode fragmentation caused by volume expansion in ZIBs and facilitate application in flexible batteries, maintaining good electrochemical stability even under mechanical deformation.Figure 3Typical structures of HEs derived from synthetic and natural materials

Typical structures of HEs derived from synthetic and natural materials

Regarding interfacial performance, HEs exhibit excellent compatibility with Zn anodes. Interfacial adhesion can be further enhanced through in situ polymerization or the introduction of strong Zn-affinity groups, thereby reducing interfacial impedance and promoting uniform Zn deposition. The electrochemical stability window is typically narrow, but design strategies such as “water-salt HEs” and “low-water HEs” can extend it by reducing free water content and employing zwitterionic polymers. Regarding preparation techniques, HEs primarily employ sol-gel methods, involving steps such as precursor solution preparation, initiator and crosslinker addition, and gelation reactions. In situ polymerization enables direct formation of tight interfaces on electrode surfaces. Blending methods achieve rapid shaping through simple mixing, while photoinitiated polymerization is suitable for controlled crosslinking and precision fabrication. To enhance mechanical properties, DN structures are commonly employed, or salt-out effects are utilized to promote polymer chain aggregation. Despite the significant advantages of HEs in suppressing dendrites and side reactions, their practical application still faces challenges such as excessive thickness, limited electrochemical windows, and insufficient controllability during polymerization. Future efforts must focus on continuous breakthroughs in thin-film preparation, high-voltage compatibility design, interfacial optimization engineering, and large-scale production to propel Zn-ion batteries toward higher performance and enhanced safety and reliability.

Based on the above design principles, the following section focuses specifically on how these HE strategies are implemented to tackle the two core challenges of Zn-Iodine Batteries: polyiodide shuttling at the cathode and dendrite/corrosion issues at the Zn anode.

The application of hydrogel electrolytes in ZIBS

Single hydrogel electrolyte

Single-component HEs consist of a single polymer component, whose functionality can be customized through functional group modification. Depending on the introduced functional groups, these HEs are primarily categorized into polyionic and polycationic types. They leverage distinct intermolecular forces-such as charge regulation and chemical interactions-to specifically address challenges encountered by the anode and cathode in ZIBs. The following sections will focus on how functional groups utilize different intermolecular forces to regulate battery behavior, detailing the current research status and control strategies for single-component HEs.

Charge-controlled hydrogel electrolyte

The path to the practical deployment of ZIBs is fundamentally hindered by two intertwined challenges: the polyiodide shuttle effect and Zn anode instability (dendrite growth and corrosion). Notably, both issues are intrinsically linked to unregulated anion dynamics. During cycling, iodine (I2) at the cathode reacts with iodide (I−) to form soluble polyiodides (e.g., I3− and I5−), which are driven by concentration and electric field gradients toward the Zn anode—a process termed the shuttle effect. This causes depletion of active material, anode corrosion, capacity fade, and low coulombic efficiency. Simultaneously, during Zn plating, the migration of anions away from the interface depletes the local ion concentration. According to the Sand’s time model, this concentration polarization promotes dendritic Zn growth and aggravates parasitic water-splitting reactions. Therefore, strategic regulation of anion behavior through limiting their migration and diffusion is critical to suppress dendrites, mitigate corrosion, and alleviate the driving forces behind polyiodide shuttling.

Given this rationale, polyionic hydrogels have emerged as promising functional electrolytes. Their polymer backbones incorporate fixed anionic groups, forming a stable, charged network that acts as a “negative charge barrier.” This charge-regulation strategy enables dual functionality: electrostatically blocking polyiodide anions while modulating the anode interface to guide uniform Zn2+ deposition. Thus, it opens a novel avenue for synergistically enhancing battery performance. Yang et al.56 pioneered the design of polycationic HEs (Figure 4A) to validate this concept. Surface sulfonate groups (-SO3-) exert persistent coulombic repulsion on soluble polyiodides (e.g., I3−), elevating their diffusion energy barrier to 0.7 eV (Figures 4B and 4C) and significantly suppressing the shuttle effect. Concurrently, this electrostatic environment enhances Zn ion desolvation and deposition kinetics, achieving a Zn2+ migration coefficient as high as 0.81 (Figure 4D). In summary, these studies demonstrate that through precise molecular design of charge distribution, HEs can function as integrated “ion-selective sieves” and “interface-modulating layers.” This innovative paradigm provides a compelling solution to the core challenges facing ZIBs.

Hydrogel electrolyte based on chemical interactions

In addition to suppressing the polyiodide shuttle through charge regulation, HEs can also be designed to leverage specific chemical interactions for enhanced inhibition. These systems go beyond simple electrostatic repulsion by incorporating functional groups that engage in multi-level intermolecular forces, including Coulombic attraction, coordination, and dipole-dipole interactions, to achieve selective anchoring, localized enrichment, and controlled conversion of polyiodides, thereby fundamentally blocking their diffusion.

A prevalent strategy involves introducing positively charged groups (e.g., quaternary ammonium -NR3+, imidazolium, or pyridinium salts) into the hydrogel network. These cations exert stable electrostatic attraction toward polyiodide anions (I3− and I5−), anchoring them within the gel or near the cathode interface. This adsorption imposes an additional energy barrier for polyiodide escape, significantly retarding their diffusion kinetics and reducing the concentration gradient that drives shuttle behavior. However, as this approach primarily targets the cathode, its effect on anode stability is indirect.

To achieve concurrent regulation of both electrodes, zwitterionic HEs have been developed. For instance, Zhang et al.57 designed three hydrogel types: a poly-anionic gel (PAG, with -SO3-), a poly-cationic gel (PCG, with -N+(CH3)3), and a poly-ampholytic gel (PZG, containing both -SO3- and -N+(CH3)3CH3 groups) (Figure 5A). Their functions diverge markedly: PAG mainly regulates Zn2+ solvation and deposition via -SO3-; PCG adsorbs polyiodides through cationic groups; while PZG synergizes both mechanisms to provide “dual-function” protection (Figure 5B). DFT simulations quantified these interactions: PCG and PZG showed strong binding energies with I3− (3.03 eV and 1.22 eV, respectively), confirming electrostatic anchoring, whereas PAG exhibited a repulsive interaction (0.25 eV) (Figure 5C). H-cell experiments visually corroborated this: Significant I3− diffusion occurred with PAG, while PCG and PZG effectively blocked migration (Figure 5D). On the anode side, DFT revealed that -SO3- groups possess high Zn2+ affinity, promoting uniform deposition, while cationic groups have minimal influence (Figure 5E). Beyond electrostatic anchoring, coordination interactions and dipole-dipole forces can be engineered into hydrogel networks for multilevel suppression. Incorporating lone-pair-rich atoms (O, N, and S) or specific moieties (carboxyl, amino, and crown ether) creates Lewis acid-base or weak coordinate bonds with polyiodides, altering their local environment and raising migration barriers. Simultaneously, polar groups (e.g., C=O and sulfonyl) generate directional dipole-dipole attractions with polyiodide molecules, introducing steric hindrance along diffusion pathways. In summary, the synergistic combination of electrostatic attraction, coordination bonding, and dipole-dipole interactions within tailored hydrogel networks establishes a robust, multi-mechanism barrier that effectively confines polyiodides. This integrated approach more comprehensively inhibits shuttle behavior, contributing significantly to the cycling durability and coulombic efficiency of ZIBs.

Several key trends emerge from this analysis. For polyiodide anchoring, the -NR3+ group exhibits the strongest binding affinity (3.36 eV), aligning with the cationic anchoring mechanism previously discussed in zwitterionic systems. In contrast, -SO3- shows negligible binding (0.25 eV) or even a repulsive interaction, confirming that its suppression of the shuttle effect operates primarily through electrostatic repulsion rather than chemical adsorption. Regarding Zn2+ regulation, carboxylate (-COO-) demonstrates a remarkably high binding energy (24.93 eV), substantially exceeding that of sulfonate (5.00 eV) and neutral groups. This quantitative disparity explains why carboxylate-rich hydrogels, such as alginate and poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), are particularly effective in guiding uniform Zn deposition. As for water binding, -COO- again shows the strongest affinity (1.25 eV), which correlates with its capacity to reduce free water activity and suppress hydrogen evolution.

Together, these comparative data establish a clear design hierarchy: For polyiodide confinement, cationic groups (-NR3+) are preferred when chemical anchoring is desired, whereas anionic groups (-SO3-) serve as electrostatic barriers; for Zn anode stabilization, carboxylate groups offer the most potent regulation of both Zn2+ flux and water activity. This quantitative framework moves beyond conceptual descriptions and provides actionable guidelines for functional group selection in future HE designs.

Critical assessment of Zn2+ transference number measurements in hydrogel electrolytes

Building upon the quantitative analysis of functional group affinities, it is equally important to critically examine how key performance metrics, particularly the Zn2+ transference number (tZn2+), are assessed. This parameter governs concentration polarization and dendrite propensity, yet its reported values in the HE literature, often exceeding 0.7 or even 0.8, warrant scrutiny regarding measurement reliability.

The most commonly employed method is the Bruce-Vincent-Evans (BVE) technique, which combines chronoamperometry and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) on symmetric Zn||Zn cells. However, several caveats specific to HEs must be acknowledged. First, the BVE method assumes a binary electrolyte with a single mobile cation-anion pair. In contrast, HEs involve multiple species, including Zn2+, counter-anions, protons, and polymer-bound charges, thereby violating this fundamental assumption. Second, the presence of fixed anionic groups (e.g., -SO3-) generates a Donnan potential that complicates the interpretation of the steady-state current. Third, interfacial impedance changes during polarization, arising from Zn dendrite formation or byproduct accumulation, introduce systematic errors.

To enhance measurement reliability, we recommend the following practices: (1) concurrent measurement of tZn2+ using orthogonal validation methods such as concentration-cell or electrophoretic NMR; (2) rigorous control of electrode surface conditions and the use of symmetric cells with minimal initial asymmetry; (3) reporting of the full current-time transient and impedance spectra both before and after polarization, rather than solely the final tZn2+ value; (4) cross-checking tZn2+ against ionic conductivity and Zn2+ diffusivity for internal consistency via the Nernst-Einstein relation. In the literature, high tZn2+ values (e.g., 0.81 in CCH, 0.76 in LM-AP) are often attributed to fixed anionic groups that immobilize counter-anions, thereby rendering Zn2+ the primary mobile charge carrier. While this interpretation is plausible, quantitative validation via alternative methods remains limited. Future studies should prioritize cross-method validation to establish a more accurate and reproducible database of ionic transport properties in HEs, ensuring that performance claims are grounded in robust measurement practices.

Composite hydrogel electrolyte

In addressing the critical challenges of polyiodide shuttling, Zn dendrite growth, and interfacial side reactions in ZIBs, composite HEs systems have pioneered new avenues for designing high-performance ZIBs by integrating multiple functional components. Composite HEs consist of two or more polymeric materials with complementary functions, forming a multi-phase structure with multifunctional properties. Compared to traditional single-component HEs (such as alginate or chitosan), these materials demonstrate significant advantages in component regulation, structural design, and functional integration. In ZIB’s applications, composite HEs can simultaneously address critical challenges-including ion conduction, multi-iodide anchoring, interfacial stability, and mechanical adaptability-through carefully engineered synergistic interactions between their components.58

Current research primarily focuses on three representative composite HEs systems: Doped HEs achieve physical/chemical confinement of polyiodides and regulate Zn ion flow by introducing functional components into the polymer matrix, thereby promoting uniform Zn deposition; DN HEs establish an interpenetrating network structure combining rigidity and flexibility. This confers excellent mechanical toughness and high ionic conductivity while its robust network effectively suppresses polyiodide diffusion and accommodates volume changes in Zn anodes, synergistically enhancing both electrode stability. Janus HEs achieve dual-sided differentiated regulation by constructing asymmetric functional structures: managing polyiodides on the cathode side while optimizing Zn deposition behavior on the anode side. This paper provides a systematic overview of recent advancements in these three composite HEs for ZIBs, examining their design principles, working mechanisms, and approaches to performance enhancement. It explores the influence patterns of different composite strategies on overall battery performance, to offer conceptual understanding and actionable strategies for developing advanced high-energy-density, long-cycle-life ZIBs. This work also advances the innovative design of flexible, high-performance ZIB systems for practical applications.

Doped composite hydrogel electrolyte

Advancing beyond HEs engineered through specific chemical interactions (e.g., electrostatic forces and coordination bonds), researchers have recently pioneered a class of “functionally doped” HEs. This strategy aims to achieve deep synergistic regulation and performance enhancement across the entire anode-cathode interface in ZIBs. These HEs incorporate specific functional groups, nanostructures, or electrochemically active components into conventional polymer networks (e.g., PAM, PVA, and sodium alginate) through physical blending, in situ polymerization, or chemical grafting. This approach constructs composite HEs systems with clearly defined functional orientations. The core design principle lies in precisely selecting and structuring the dopant phase to endow the electrolyte with the ability to simultaneously regulate the microenvironments at both electrode interfaces, thereby achieving synergistic suppression of multi-iodide ion shuttling and Zn dendrite growth.

In specific material systems, the selection of dopant components exhibits diverse strategies: For instance, Tang et al.59 introduced liquid metal nanodroplets as an active dopant phase into sulfonate-functionalized polyionic aquagels. The liquid metal not only chemically anchors polyiodide ions via its surface Ga3+ ions but also forms electrostatic synergies with -SO3- groups on polymer chains, creating a dual barrier of “chemical adsorption-electrostatic repulsion” (Figure 6A). Guo et al.54 selected carboxylated cellulose nanofibers as a biomass-derived dopant. Its plentiful polar functional groups—including hydroxyl and carboxyl groups —not only strongly bind water molecules and promote Zn2+ desolvation, but also form a dense nano-network that acts as a physical barrier restricting free diffusion of polyiodide ions (Figure 6C). Zhao et al.60 innovated by introducing imidazooxime-functionalized intrinsically microporous polymers as the dopant phase (Figure 6E). This material combines a rigid microporous framework with protonatable amine groups. These dopant components synergistically optimize battery performance through multiple physicochemical mechanisms. On the cathode side, dopant phases carrying negatively charged groups (e.g., -SO3- and -COO-) or components providing positively charged adsorption sites (e.g., Ga3+) significantly mitigate the dissolution and shuttling of polyiodide ions. On the anode side, polar sites on the dopant phase coordinate with Zn2+, reconstructing the solvation shell and lowering the desolvation barrier. It often synergizes with the polymer matrix to guide uniform Zn deposition along thermodynamically stable crystal planes such as (002), thereby inhibiting dendrite growth. Simultaneously, the introduction of the dopant phase often constructs faster ion transport pathways inside the gel matrix, enhancing the migration number of Zn2+ and overall ionic conductivity while alleviating concentration polarization at the electrode surface.

Thanks to this multifunctional integrated design, ZIBs based on the aforementioned doped HEs exhibit significantly enhanced electrochemical performance. The LM-AP electrolyte enabled ultra-long cycling of 85,000 cycles at a high rate of 20 A g−1 (Figure 6B), attributed to the synergistic action of its sulfonic acid groups and Ga3+ chemical sites, which suppressed polyiodide ion shuttling while maintaining dynamic stability at the Zn anode interface. The PACH electrolyte, leveraging the tight coupling between carboxylated cellulose nanofibers and the PAM network, not only suppresses polyiodide ion migration through electrostatic repulsion and physical barriers but also constructs a dual pathway facilitating rapid Zn2+ transport and uniform deposition. This achieves outstanding rate performance and high reversible capacity (Figure 6D). The AO-PIM-1/Alg system synergistically utilizes a rigid microporous framework and an “egg-crate” structure to effectively block polyiodide ion diffusion while guiding preferential Zn growth along the (002) crystal plane. This enables the full cell to maintain an average coulombic efficiency of 99.4% after 8,700 cycles (Figure 6F). This flexible pouch battery combines practicality with flexibility: it successfully powers a toy airplane (Figure 6G) while maintaining stable charging and discharging performance across varying bending angles (Figure 6H). This suggests that the AO-PIM-1/Alg electrolyte holds significant potential for fabricating stable and durable quasi-solid-state ZIBs.

In summary, functionally doped HEs achieve “integrated” regulation of the ZIBs’ anode-cathode interface by introducing active components with complementary functions into the polymer matrix. This “doping modification” strategy not only provides an efficient material solution to address dendrite and shuttling issues but also, through its rich compositional and structural tunability, opens up broad exploration possibilities for electrolyte design in next-generation high-performance, high-safety aqueous Zn-based batteries.

Dual-network composite hydrogel electrolyte

To simultaneously tackle the asymmetric interfacial challenges at the Zn anode (dendrites/corrosion) and the iodine cathode (polyiodide shuttling) in ZIBs, DN HEs have emerged as a compelling integrated materials platform. Their core advantage lies in the synergistic combination of rigidity and flexibility61: A covalently cross-linked primary network provides mechanical integrity and stable ion channels, while a dynamically cross-linked secondary network (via ionic or hydrogen bonds) confers flexibility, self-healing, and stress dissipation, enabling adaptation to electrode volume changes.62,63,64 By further incorporating functional groups with specific affinities (e.g., for Zn2+ or polyiodides) into these networks, DN electrolytes transition from passive physical confinement to active chemical regulation of interfacial processes, addressing the fundamental limitations of single-network systems.

Research based on the aforementioned design principles continues to advance, with a clear trajectory toward increasingly precise and multifunctional regulation. For instance, inspired by the guiding effect of a tennis racket’s mesh on the ball, Yang et al.65 proposed a “tennis racket”-inspired DN HEs (Figure 7A). This structure features a rigid primary network formed by PAM and sodium lignosulfonate, while a flexible secondary network is created by sodium alginate crosslinked via Zn ions. This design leverages abundant oxygen-containing functional groups to direct uniform Zn-ion deposition, while its anionic groups (e.g., sulfonate) suppress polyiodide shuttling via electrostatic repulsion. It simultaneously addresses issues such as Zn anode dendrite growth, hydrogen evolution reaction, and polyiodide shuttling in the iodine cathode, demonstrating the fundamental mechanisms of DNs in ion flux regulation and electrostatic repulsion. Experimental results demonstrate that the Zn-iodine full cell assembled with this electrolyte exhibits long-term cycling stability for 15,000 cycles at 5 A g−1 (Figure 7B). Building upon the DN architecture, subsequent studies introduced synergistic chemical interactions beyond electrostatic effects. Xiong et al.66 demonstrated the synergistic effects of functional groups in a DN HEs based on polycarboxylic acid superplasticizer (PC) and PVA (Figure 7D). Constructed via chemical crosslinking and Zn-ion coordination, this DN system employs intermolecular hydrogen bonding to restrict the mobility of free water molecules and direct homogeneous Zn2+ deposition, effectively inhibiting dendrite formation and corrosion. Additionally, the strong chemical adsorption of polyiodide ions (e.g., I3−) by carboxyl groups mitigates the shuttle effect. This synergistic effect enables the Zn||I2 full cell to achieve a capacity retention of 92.6% after 5000 cycles at 1 A g−1 (Figure 7E). Flexible pouch batteries assembled with this electrolyte (Figure 7F) also demonstrate superior cycling stability and practical viability, outstanding long-term cyclability and application potential, indicating that the DN structure achieves high-performance flexible energy storage not only through electrostatic repulsion but also via the synergy of hydrogen bond locking and specific chemical adsorption.

To achieve more precise ion regulation by spatially decoupling the distinct chemical requirements at the two electrodes, Huang et al.67 employed a functional separation strategy to design a DN HEs (DCHE) with a “dual confinement effect” (Figure 7G). Unlike previous systems where anionic and cationic groups were integrated in a mixed manner, this work deliberately integrates anionic and cationic groups into distinct domains to establish a dual confinement mechanism combining electrostatic repulsion and chemical adsorption. This electrolyte integrates anionic and cationic groups to establish a dual confinement mechanism combining electrostatic repulsion and chemical adsorption, effectively suppressing polyiodide ion shuttling. Utilizing Zn-affinity and hydrophilic groups to regulate the interfacial environment, it suppresses side reactions and guides uniform Zn deposition. The DCHE-based Zn||ZnI2 full cell demonstrates outstanding cycling stability (Figure 7H) and excellent flexibility. Its flexible single-layer pouch cell retains 80% capacity after 100 cycles of 90-degree bending (Figure 7I), showcasing practical potential for wearable energy storage. This work achieves a leap from single functional groups to the functional separation and synergistic design of anionic and cationic groups.

Moving beyond functional-group engineering, further advances have achieved regulation at the molecular level by reconstructing ion solvation structures. Xu et al.68 achieved ternary synergistic regulation using a DN HEs based on sulfonated bacterial cellulose (VBC). This system employs a three-dimensional bacterial cellulose nanofiber network as a rigid scaffold (first network), upon which a polymer functional network rich in sulfonate groups (–SO3-) is chemically grafted (second network) (Figure 7J). This design fundamentally alters the thermodynamics and kinetics of ion transport by restructuring the solvation shell of Zn2+ through sulfonate groups, while enhancing electrostatic repulsion to suppress polyiodide ions. After cycling over 9,000 cycles at 1.06 A g−1(5 C), the VBC-based Zn-iodine full cell maintained a capacity of approximately 0.135 Ah g−1 with a stable coulombic efficiency exceeding 99% (Figure 7K). As shown in Figure 7L, this electrolyte outperforms most advanced systems in key metrics, including cycle life, rate performance, and capacity retention, highlighting the comprehensive advantages of pushing DN performance to its limits through precise molecular engineering.

Despite their significant advantages in mechanical robustness and functional integration, DN hydrogels face a key challenge in balancing mechanical strength and ionic conductivity. The introduction of a second network, particularly one with high crosslinking density or strong ionic interactions, may restrict the mobility of polymer chains and reduce the free volume, thereby hindering ion transport. If this trade-off is not addressed, it may limit the rate performance of ZIBs, particularly at high current densities. To mitigate this issue, researchers have explored various strategies. One effective strategy is to introduce a hierarchical or weakly cross-linked secondary network, such as one formed via hydrogen bonding or metal-ligand coordination, which preserves structural integrity while maintaining chain flexibility. Furthermore, introducing ion-conductive nanofillers, such as cellulose nanofibers, MXene, or metal-organic frameworks, into the DN matrix can create additional ion transport pathways without compromising mechanical stability. At the same time, optimizing the ratio of the two networks is crucial, as an excess of the rigid network may inhibit ionic mobility, while insufficient cross-linking may lead to poor mechanical properties. For example, the VBC electrolyte reported by Xu et al. achieved a good balance between high mechanical strength (18–24 kPa) and ionic conductivity (22.6 mS cm−1) through the rational design of the sulfonated bacterial cellulose and polymer network architecture. Furthermore, molecular engineering of the polymer backbone, such as introducing flexible spacer segments or hydrophilic side chains, can enhance segmental mobility and water retention, thereby facilitating ion diffusion. In summary, these strategies demonstrate that when network structure and component interactions are fully considered during design, DN HEs can achieve a favorable balance between mechanical robustness and ionic conductivity.

In summary, the DN HEs achieve comprehensive performance surpassing single-network systems through multi-level, multifunctional synergistic design. This evolutionary trajectory, progressing from initial ion flux guidance and electrostatic repulsion to synergistic hydrogen-bond locking and chemical adsorption, followed by dual-confined regulation with functional separation, and ultimately molecular-level reconstruction of solvation structures, highlights the continuous refinement of the design philosophy. The DN architecture not only physically suppresses dendrite growth and accommodates electrode deformation but also actively guides ion transport and inhibits polyiodide shuttling at the chemical level through the introduction of functional groups. These properties enable ZIBs based on DN HEs to achieve significant breakthroughs in cycle life, rate performance, and interfacial stability, laying a robust material foundation for developing next-generation aqueous safe and long-lived energy storage devices.

Janus composite hydrogel electrolyte

Addressing the intrinsically asymmetric interfacial challenges in ZIBs, spatially heterogeneous Janus HEs have been proposed. Inspired by biological compartmentalization, these systems integrate distinct functional domains within a single matrix to achieve precise and decoupled interfacial regulation at both electrodes. This “zonal design, synergistic optimization” strategy transcends the limitations of homogeneous electrolytes, advancing battery performance across multiple metrics. It is worth clarifying that Janus hydrogel and functional partitioning are related but distinct concepts. Functional partitioning refers broadly to the spatial separation of different functions within an electrolyte system, such as cathode-side polyiodide suppression and anode-side Zn stabilization. Janus hydrogel, in contrast, specifically denotes an asymmetric bilayer structure with distinct physicochemical properties on each side, representing one embodiment of functional partitioning. This distinction helps to better categorize the diverse design strategies reported in the literature.

The heterogeneous polyionic hydrogel proposed by Yang et al.69 (Figure 8A) represents an intuitive charge-driven interfacial regulation strategy based on the electrostatic complementarity between cationic and anionic functional groups. This hydrogel consists of a polycationic layer rich in ammonium groups tightly bound to a polyanionic layer rich in sulfonate groups. Cross-sectional and top SEM images (Figures 8B and 8C) clearly confirm its stable bilayer heterogeneous structure. The polycationic layer chemically traps polyiodide ions and catalyzes their conversion, while the polyanionic layer blocks polyiodide migration to the Zn anode via electrostatic repulsion and guides uniform Zn ion deposition. This charge-regulated synergistic mechanism not only effectively suppresses polyiodide shuttling but also significantly enhances reaction kinetics. Building upon charge regulation, Cao et al.70 further incorporated biomimetic principles to design a skin-like electrolyte with structure-function biomimicry, enhancing the systematic and refined nature of interfacial regulation (Figure 8E). Inspired by the epidermis-dermis bilayer structure of human skin, digital photography and atomic force microscopy images (Figure 8F) clearly confirm its dual-layer heterogeneous architecture: A negatively charged covalent organic framework nanolayer serves as the “epidermis,” effectively blocking the transmembrane migration of polyiodide ions via electrostatic repulsion; while the amide-rich aramid nanofiber hydrogel serves as the “dermis layer.” This layer induces the spontaneous dehydration of hydrated Zn ions through a hydrogen-bond network, significantly lowering the desolvation energy barrier to suppress Zn corrosion and dendrite formation. This biomimetic structure maintains high ionic mobility while achieving deep regulation of interfacial chemistry, enabling the ZIBs to exhibit outstanding reversibility across a wide rate range from 0.106 A g−1 to 5.275 A g−1 (0.5 C–25 C) (Figure 8G). Notably, it achieves an ultra-long cycle life of 45,000 cycles at the high rate of 2.11A g−1(10 C), with a capacity decay rate as low as 0.0018% per cycle (Figure 8H), demonstrating the unique advantage of the biomimetic strategy in enhancing battery cycling stability.

To further advance the practical application of ZIBs, Liu et al.71 developed a functionally integrated asymmetric hydrogel that synergistically incorporates an electron-conductive network and an ion-regulating network within a compartmentalized structure, achieving a leap from fundamental interfacial regulation to multifunctional system integration. The design employs a highly electron-conductive poly(3,4-ethynyl-2,3-thiophenediyl) ether-based hydrogel on the cathode side, which simultaneously enhances multi-iodide ion adsorption and reaction kinetics. On the anode side, a sodium alginate-carrageenan DN hydrogel coordinating Zn ions was constructed, guiding uniform Zn deposition through functional groups (Figure 8I). Its distinct heterogeneous bilayer structure is confirmed by SEM imaging (Figure 8J). This “cathode electron conduction, anode ion conduction” division of labor endows the battery with outstanding electrochemical performance: After 100 cycles at 0.1 A g−1, the battery exhibits a coulombic efficiency of 99.84% and a capacity retention rate of 95.94%, significantly outperforming the liquid electrolyte system (CE 97.25%, capacity retention 83.78%) (Figure 8K); It maintained stable operation for over 10,000 cycles at 5 A g−1 with a capacity retention rate >85% and an average CE of 99.84% (Figure 8L). The pouch cell based on this electrolyte also demonstrated excellent flexibility and safety, showcasing the application potential of Janus hydrogels in practical devices.

In summary, Janus HEs have progressively enhanced their capability for synergistic optimization at the electrode interfaces of ZIBs through diversified design strategies, evolving from charge regulation to structural biomimicry and functional integration. Future research should prioritize clarifying their coupling mechanisms between ion/electron transport and reaction kinetics at multiscale interfaces, developing low-cost, scalable fabrication processes, exploring the incorporation of intelligent features such as self-healing and stimulus responsiveness, and optimizing their performance under lean-electrolyte conditions. These efforts will propel high-performance, long-lifetime aqueous ZIBs from fundamental innovation toward practical application.

Conclusion and outlook

Summary of key advances

HEs have emerged as a promising platform for addressing the two intertwined challenges in ZIBs: polyiodide shuttling at the cathode and Zn dendrite growth/corrosion at the anode. This review has systematically summarized the design principles and working mechanisms of HEs in ZIBs. For single-component HEs, charge-regulation strategies (e.g., sulfonate-functionalized networks) electrostatically repel polyiodides while guiding uniform Zn2+ deposition, whereas chemically interactive hydrogels (e.g., zwitterionic networks) anchor polyiodides via cationic groups and regulate Zn2+ flux through anionic groups. For composite HEs, doped systems introduce functional additives (e.g., liquid metal, cellulose nanofibers, and microporous polymers) to create dual physical-chemical barriers; DN architectures synergize rigidity and flexibility to simultaneously suppress dendrites and accommodate volume changes; and Janus HEs achieve spatially resolved interfacial regulation through asymmetric functional layer design. Collectively, these advances have enabled ZIBs with extended cycle lives exceeding tens of thousands of cycles, high Coulombic efficiencies, and enhanced rate capabilities.

Challenges and future perspectives

Practical limitations

Despite the remarkable laboratory-scale achievements, a critical examination of the reported literature reveals several practical constraints that are often overlooked. First, the majority of ultra-long cycling demonstrations (>10,000 cycles) are achieved under low iodine loadings (<2 mg cm−2) and low areal capacities (<0.5 mAh cm−2), which are far below the requirements for practical applications (>5–10 mg cm−2). Second, HE thicknesses typically range from 0.5 to 1.0 mm, which is 10–50 times thicker than conventional separators (e.g., 20–40 μm for glass fiber or polyolefin membranes), leading to substantial increases in internal resistance and reductions in volumetric energy density. Third, there exists an inherent trade-off between mechanical strength and ionic conductivity: Increasing crosslinking density or incorporating rigid fillers suppresses dendrites but often compromises ion transport. Fourth, when the mass of the HEs is included in energy density calculations, the gravimetric energy density drops dramatically—in some cases to below 20 Wh kg−1. These observations highlight the urgent need to shift research focus from merely extending cycle life to achieving balanced performance metrics under practically relevant conditions that prioritize high mass loadings and thin electrolyte architectures.

Key scientific direction: Multi-electron iodine chemistry

The two-electron I−/I2 redox couple offers a theoretical capacity of 211 mAh g−1, while the four-electron I−/I+ pathway—involving I+ stabilized by halide complexation (e.g., ICl or IBr)—could theoretically deliver 422 mAh g−1, representing a transformative leap in energy density. However, realizing this potential requires overcoming substantial scientific hurdles.

Thermodynamic feasibility. The standard reduction potential of the I+/I− couple is approximately 1.6 V vs. SHE (I++2e− →I−), which is substantially higher than that of I2/I− (0.62 V vs. SHE). This implies that I+ is a much stronger oxidant and is thermodynamically unstable in aqueous media, readily undergoing disproportionation (3 I++3H2O→IO3−+2I−+6H+) or reduction by water. Consequently, accessing I+ requires either highly concentrated halide media to form stable polyhalide complexes (e.g., ICl2− and IBr2−) that shift the effective redox potential, or non-aqueous or water-in-salt environments that suppress water activity. HEs, with their ability to confine concentrated salt solutions and immobilize anions, offer a unique platform to stabilize these reactive intermediates.

Kinetic barriers. The I−/I+ conversion involves a two-electron transfer step that is kinetically sluggish compared to the sequential I−/I2/I+ pathway. The activation energy for direct I− to I+ oxidation is significantly higher due to the large reorganization energy associated with the formation of highly charged intermediates. Furthermore, the competing oxygen evolution reaction (OER, 1.23 V vs. SHE) becomes thermodynamically favorable before the I+/I− potential is reached, meaning that any practical four-electron iodine battery must operate in a kinetic regime where OER is suppressed.

Electrolyte stability under high-potential conditions. At the high potentials required for I+ formation (>1.6 V vs. SHE), most polymer backbones, particularly those containing ether, hydroxyl, or amide groups, are susceptible to electrochemical oxidation. Systematic anodic stability studies using linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) on inert electrodes are urgently needed to establish the electrochemical stability windows of candidate hydrogels in iodine-containing electrolytes. Toward practical applications

To bridge the gap between laboratory innovation and practical application, breakthroughs are urgently needed in the following areas.1)High-load electrode design. Future research should adopt an iodine loading of >5 mg cm−2 and an areal capacity of >2 mAh cm−2 as benchmark indicators for practical application potential.2)Thin hydrogel fabrication. Technologies such as solution casting with thickness control, in-situ polymerization on electrode surfaces, and electrospinning need to be further developed to reduce gel thickness to below 100 μm while ensuring mechanical integrity.3)Wide-temperature-range antifreeze HEs. Developing HEs with wide-temperature adaptability is crucial for the practical application of ZIBs. The core strategy involves introducing biomimetic antifreeze components or constructing high-concentration “hydrated eutectic” networks to disrupt the ordered hydrogen-bond network of water at the molecular level, thereby inhibiting ice crystal formation and maintaining liquid-like ionic conductivity even at temperatures tens of degrees below zero.4)HEs for flexible wearable devices. To meet the demands of next-generation flexible electronic devices, HEs must integrate mechanical adaptability, electrochemical stability, and structural intelligence. By constructing cross-linked networks based on dynamic reversible bonds (such as multiple hydrogen bonds and coordination bonds), tissue-like gels with high elasticity, tear resistance, and self-healing capabilities can be obtained, while maintaining the integrity of ion transport channels under deformation.5)Comprehensive performance balancing and optimization. The design of HEs must strike a reasonable balance between mechanical strength and ionic conductivity, with multiple performance metrics synergistically optimized for specific application scenarios. For example, while ensuring sufficient mechanical strength to suppress dendrite growth (e.g., >100 kPa), a high level of ionic conductivity (e.g., >15 mS cm−1) should be maintained to avoid one-sided optimization that sacrifices one property for another. Additionally, key parameters such as iodine loading, gel thickness, electrolyte-to-capacitance ratio, anode-to-cathode capacitance ratio, and energy density calculated based on total device mass should be standardized in reporting to enable fair cross-study comparisons.

In summary, research on HEs for ZIBs has entered a critical turning point. Although significant progress has been made in suppressing polyiodide shuttling and stabilizing the Zn anode, further advancement toward practical applications still requires addressing interrelated challenges such as limited energy density, insufficient temperature range adaptability, and poor mechanochemical synergy. Through the precise design of polymer frameworks, functional groups, cross-linking networks, and composite interfaces, next-generation HEs are expected to evolve from laboratory proof-of-concept materials into high-performance, device-level enabling components for ZIBs that offer high safety, low cost, and strong adaptability.
