Challenges and Future Directives of Synthetic Biology in Engineering Plant–Microbe Partnerships for Sustainable Agriculture
Authors: Ben Jesuorsemwen Enagbonma, Rebaona Reaobaka Molefe‐Madlaliso, Alaba Adewole Adebayo, Oghoye Priscilla Oyedoh, Godfrey Okainemen Oribhabor, Oluwaseun Emmanuel Shittu, Olubukola Oluranti Babalola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSynthetic biology has recently proven to be a valuable tool for enhancing agriculture even in the face of environmental and biological stresses. Understanding the challenges that militate synthetic biology will assist in ensuring safe, stable, and scalable crop production. Thus, we examined the challenges limiting synthetic biology applications in engineering plant–microbe partnerships and highlighted future research directions. Ecological, biological, technical, and regulatory barriers to synthetic biology‐driven plant–microbe engineering are the challenges examined in this review. Profound insight into these challenges will lead to a shift toward systems‐level approaches that integrate multiomics analyses, predictive modeling, and framework‐responsive genetic designs. To completely translate synthetic biology from the laboratory to the field, improved delivery methods, monitoring strategies, and harmonized regulatory frameworks should be encouraged. In addition, the development of robust and controllable microbial chassis should be emphasized.Keywords: compliance, ecosafety, host–microbe compatibility, microbial consortia, multiomics

Introduction

The focus of new‐age agriculture is to promote the resilience and productivity of crops even in the face of environmental stressors (Enagbonma and Babalola 2019). This new era of agriculture is also aimed at increasing yields without undermining soil fertility, the ecological balance, or biodiversity, even though the global needs for food, feed, and bioenergy continue to rise (Chauhan et al. 2025). Conventional practices involving the heavy application of fertilizers and pesticides may have resulted in immediate yield gains but have also led to soil degradation, nutrient imbalances, greenhouse gas emissions, and disruption of microbial diversity (Babalola et al. 2021; Md Mukhtar et al. 2025; Enagbonma et al. 2024). Because of the side effects linked to conventional practices in the environment, the use of plant–microbe partnerships in promoting plant development has now been favored.

Plant–microbe partnerships refer to the dynamic and intricate interactions that plants have with the microorganisms living around and within them either in the rhizosphere, phyllosphere, or endosphere (Molefe et al. 2023; Bibek et al. 2025; Enagbonma et al. 2023). Such interactions form the basis of sustainable agriculture by contributing to fertility in soils, the availability of nutrients for nutrient uptake, tolerance to stresses, and stability for whole ecosystems (Wankhade et al. 2025). These partnerships, when viewed from a holistic perspective, mean that plant health is not an isolated phenomenon but rather one intimately connected with environmental health. In fact, one health framework can be extended from human and animal health to cover the plant–environment interface by asserting that equitable interactions between plants, microbes, and their environments are prerequisites for agricultural productivity as well as ecological resilience (Proctor et al. 2025). Despite their importance, many natural plant–microbe interactions are inefficient, inconsistent, or unpredictable. For this reason, the need to use synthetic biology to design, optimize, and strengthen plant–microbe partnerships has increased. This strategy will improve the sustainability of resilient agricultural systems (C. Zhao et al. 2021). Synthetic biology is useful in redesigning microbial genomes at an unprecedented scale and precision (McCarty and Ledesma‐Amaro 2019). This can enhance microbial functions that are valuable to plants through the use of synthetic biosynthetic pathways or synthetic gene regulatory networks (Ye et al. 2025). To ensure that these engineered microbial functions (nitrogen fixation, tolerance to environmental stresses, or pathogen suppression) are sustainable in the agricultural field, computational and system‐level approaches are needed. These approaches are used to predict and optimize microbial behavior within plant‐associated communities (Kwan et al. 2023). This was supported by Gonçalves et al. (2023), who predicted the functional interactions between plants and microbes when they used genome‐scale metabolic modeling to reconstruct metabolic networks from metagenome‐assembled genomes. According to studies in grasslands, genome‐scale metabolic modeling revealed a smaller synthetic microbial community that retained key growth‐promoting traits, such as nitrogen fixation, iron acquisition, and phytohormone production (Karkaria et al. 2021). Complementary approaches that utilize Bayesian modeling for integrating genetic traits and intercellular communication predict the establishment of stable microbial consortia (Karkaria et al. 2021). Collectively, these computational tools allow the rational design of resilient plant–microbe partnerships. The integration of these approaches with synthetic biology would allow for the development of stable and highly beneficial plant–microbe consortia, a significant step toward precisely engineered sustainable agriculture (McNaughton et al. 2021).

Nevertheless, despite these advances, converting synthetic biology‐engineered plant–microbe partnerships into real‐world agriculture is difficult (H. Li et al. 2025). The performance of engineered microorganisms is often constrained by factors such as ecological rivalry and limited persistence in natural soils (Hamza et al. 2023). Additionally, the lack of target effects on endogenous microbial communities, biosafety and regulatory unknowns remain constraints on mass field implementation (Avery et al. 2026). These problems indicate that a better grasp of microbial ecology, host specificity, and system‐wide interactions is necessary to ensure that engineered solutions are effective and environmentally friendly (Hamza et al. 2023). This has led to an increasing demand to assess the barriers and limitations linked to synthetic biology‐based design of plant–microbe associations critically and to formulate leading strategies that can potentially be used to address these constraints (Avery et al. 2026). The future of microbial chassis selection, context‐responsive genetic circuits, and synthetic microbial consortia design points to promising paths on the way to stable, predictable, and sustainable relationships between plants and microbes.

Thus, this review aims to critically examine the ecological, biological, technical, and regulatory constraints of synthetic biology‐engineered plant–microbe partnerships while highlighting emerging strategies for achieving stable, safe, and scalable applications in sustainable agriculture. Furthermore, integrating synthetic biology with systems‐level computational modeling and ecological principles may enable the rational design of resilient plant–microbe consortia that outperform conventional microbial inoculants while maintaining environmental safety and ecosystem stability.

Mechanism of Plant–Microbe Partnerships Through Synthetic Biology

In the natural environment, plant–microbe partnerships are engineered through the root–soil interphase, which is driven by the diffusion and transport of thousands of compounds (physical and chemical changes) released by the root system (mucilage and other metabolites). These rhizodeposition exudates are affected by environmental factors. For example, research has shown that moderate drought increases mucilage release and that severe drought reduces its release (Bandopadhyay et al. 2024). In addition, these deposits have been reported to affect the physicochemical and microbial environments of roots. For example, in Zea mays, the mucilage secretions of aerial brace roots promoted colonization by diazotrophs for nitrogen fixation (Pankievicz et al. 2022). Drifting from the natural plant–microbe partnership to plant–microbe synthetic biology approaches, a designed circuitry with a host chassis (model plant and microbe) and a synthetic microbial community are commonly applied approaches.

Plant and Microbe Circuitry Factory in Engineering

In plants, the paradigm shift from conventional crossbreeding to genetic manipulation requires an understanding of specific molecular kits and endogenous control procedures to control the expression of genes across locations, magnitudes, and times. Through synthetic engineering, root forms and functions can be modified to produce novel crops that are well fitted for specialized and adverse conditions. This synthetic modification can be performed on the basis of the gravity setpoint angle and root branches, which can reprogram the root systems to be more suitable for nutrient and water acquisition, insulating roots against nutrient loss and modifying suberin (or lignin) deposition against toxic sodium and metal ion uptake (Ragland et al. 2024). These circuits can be easily designed by recombinase genetic engineering systems, which allows steady modifications in gene expression, allowing the circuits to be sensitive to recombinase expression levels. To trigger or suppress the expression of target genes in plants, many sequences encoding DNA‐binding domains obtained from species of bacteria are employed to produce artificial transcription factors (Alamos and Shih 2022). Synthetic transcription factor (TF) activity is responsive to synthetic promoter regions, which are successfully constructed to characterize efficiently DNA‐binding specific domains of interest (Brophy et al. 2022). However, the recent screening of a collection of TFs led to the identification of cross‐reactive subsets in synthetic promoters that contain cognate recognition sequences differing from those of the other synthetic TFs tested (this is called the orthogonal attribute of synthetic TF–promoter pairs). To trigger a permanent modification in synthetic genetic circuit architecture, sequence‐specific recombinases were employed as an alternative strategy to allow or prevent downstream reporter gene expression (J. P. Lloyd et al. 2022). This allows the irreversible modification of circuits, with steady alterations in gene expression by the recombinase. Hence, circuits are specifically responsive to the expression threshold of recombinase when conventional reporters prove insufficiently sensitive (J. P. Lloyd et al. 2022). The promoter element used to drive serine integrase into the cell subpopulation in the root (pericycle) contributes to starter cells for the development of the lateral root, as demonstrated in an experiment by Guiziou et al. (2023), which is a typical example of gene circuitry in the plant chassis. This promoter element was flipped from one state that promotes the expression of mTurquoise to another that supports mScarlet expression exclusively in lateral roots. Therefore, the combination of circuit modalities may increase the engineering of gene regulatory patterns. These advances will provide researchers with transcription factor‐based circuit opportunities to engineer the synthetic architecture of genes and develop specifications of the desired pathway. TF‐based circuits allow analog (or graded patterns of input promoter activity) to be converted into graded patterns of circuit output, whereas recombinase systems produce stable digital (on/off) circuits over time (Zhong et al. 2023). For example, in the engineering of root architecture for water and nutrient absorption during abiotic stress, the genetic basis for the root system branching rate and gravity setpoint angle has been precisely determined and applied for synthetic gene circuits (Brophy et al. (2022). In line with plant chassis modulation for selective soil solute adsorption, modifying the pathway associated with solute and water transport in the tissue spaces outside of cells will help enhance limited nutrient uptake or limit the uptake of toxic substances (e.g., sodium and metal ions). For example, suberin (an apoplastic barrier component) biosynthesis gene regulation in endodermal cells can help limit solute movement outside of cells (Shukla and Barberon 2021). This mechanism can be leveraged to facilitate interaction and resource trade with microbes and their rhizosphere. To understand specialized mechanisms of stress, plant synthetic circuit systems may be applied to design model plants for synthetic ecological studies. Conversely, to improve SynCom member stability, genetic circuits can be deployed to modulate members for better functional outputs.

An ideal rhizobacteria for synthetic circuitry can be developed through heterologous or metabolite synthesis to increase robust root colonization. These root colonizers can be efficiently transferred into the roots or have useful traits that can be tracked genetically and mined for engineering. In addition, poor colonizers of other qualities could be engineered for improved rhizosphere competence (Björklöf et al. 2003). The greatest challenge for synthetic circuit system introduction in these bacteria is the ability of the natural soil microbiome to swiftly outcompete model species, especially those accommodating metabolically expensive technology (Björklöf et al. 2003; Ragland et al. 2024). To circumvent this problem, antibiotics producing chassis or biocontrol traits can be introduced into the model strain to eliminate other ecological niches while removing keystone species. Hence, engineering plant‐associated microbes is not a trivial task because of the complexity of the community and complex polygenic traits that control the mechanism of competitive colonization (E. Li, Zhang, et al. 2021). The genetic circuitry of root bacteria is better designed outside the original host context to understand the intensity and expression of diverse ribosome binding sites or promoters (i.e., the functionality of their constituent genetic parts) (Tas et al. 2021). Circuitry design begins with the assembly of circuits into self‐replicating plasmid backbones. Confirmation of circuits integrated into the genome of the target chassis through long‐term functionality checks. The activity of the genetic circuit in the chosen chassis is empirically verified after design and optimized through iterative design‐build‐test‐optimized cycles. To improve engineering precision, CRISPR/Cas9 and related variants can be applied for defined editing of any location with a PAM sequence in the genome. Alternative tools used include modified ICEBs1 conjugative and integrative element 89 or recombineering tool 90. These technologies promote rapid genome integration into circuitry in wide strains (Ragland et al. 2024).

For example, engineering for improved abiotic stress resilience enhancement is quite complex because of the numerous direct and indirect mechanisms involved in stress resilience modulation by microbes. The strong polygenic and pleiotropic characteristics of these resilient traits make pinpointing specific systems for engineering, but genetic determinants can be found in rare instances (Ragland et al. 2024). For example, circuits for bacterial overexpression and heterologous expression (Rodríguez‐Salazar et al. 2009) of trehalose, cytokinin (J. Xu et al. 2012), and ACC deaminase (Y. Liu et al. 2017) successfully improved drought tolerance in colonized plants. However, these circuitry applications in plant–microbe partnerships can be improved by the production of multiple synergistic tolerance effector elements, such as trehalose and ACC deaminase (Orozco‐Mosqueda et al. 2019).

Efforts have been made to fix nitrogenase (nif) assembly in some chassis during nutrient fertilization enhancement. However, nitrogen fixation clusters are quite complex, with an oxygen‐sensitive catalytic center and a high fitness cost of hosting the pathway, making nif transfer into modified chassis challenging (Ryu et al. 2020). Therefore, researchers have focused on reducing sensitivity to oxygen, modifying downstream ammonia activation or suppression (Ryu et al. 2020; Schnabel and Sattely 2021a) and optimizing protein stoichiometry (Smanski et al. 2014) without reconstituting nif activity. In addition to nitrogen fixation, beneficial bacteria can improve nonbioavailable iron and phosphate in the soil through organic acid release, which mobilizes their ions from mineral complexes for absorption in plants (Oteino et al. 2015). Research efforts have revealed organic acid biosynthesis operons in strains, improved phosphate assimilation and enhanced rice development (Wagh et al. 2016). Another plant‐derived phosphate storage compound is phytate, which is solubilized in minerals in the soil, hence decreasing phosphate bioavailability. Through phosphate release from phytate, Arabidopsis growth was improved via the expression of 12 diverse phytases in 3 rhizobacterial chassis (Shulse et al. 2019).

Approaches to community or consortia engineering can be divided into top‐down strategies and bottom‐up strategies (Y. Liang et al. 2022). Conjugated elements or vectors can be applied to increase the range of hosts engineered for the transformation of diverse species with engineered payloads. Conjugation‐based tools include XPORT (Brophy et al. 2018) and MAGIC (Smanski et al. 2014), while transposases and phages can be used for stable genomic incorporation of engineered circuits (Ando et al. 2015). The combination of these tools with CRISPR‐based payloads allows for gene‐level editing of metagenomic functions and potentially the deletion of specific traits from an entire community or consortia (Rubin et al. 2022).

Synthetic Microbial Community (SynCom) Assembly

Microbes can be transferred naturally from the surrounding soil and rhizosphere environment through the roots into the endosphere and phyllosphere to execute abiotic and biotic stress tolerance and nutrient acquisition. This is how microbiota assembly promotes beneficial functionality, which opens the way for a deeper mechanistic approach to assemble and reconstitute synthetic microbial communities (SynCom). The communities are members of naturally occurring beneficial plant microbiomes that are culturable (i.e., > 50% at ASV level resolution) (Fitzpatrick et al. 2018) and synthetically engineered for specialized ecological services. Proper assembly or recruitment of members (which could be natural strains or engineered strains) of SynCom can provide adequate knowledge of the mechanism controlling microbial community building in plant–microbe partnerships for ecological service. Scientists achieve the SynCom assemblage by mimicking similar complexities of the natural microbial community (interspecific interactions) and simulating artificially imposed factors synonymous with associated community changes (Y. Liang et al. 2022). The recruitment or assembly of members of the SynCom is based on diverse strategies, as highlighted below.

Bottom‐Up Recruitment

This method of recruiting SynCom microbial members gives rise to simple SynCom involving single‐strain inoculation or multiple SynCom involving multiple‐strain inoculation. This strategy involves the optimization of physical and chemical parameters via natural microbial communities in a closed controlled system. The members are assembled on the basis of maximum community function from the complex ecosystem and are recruited under specific culture conditions with temporal stability. The random mode of selection of strains and nutrient enrichment strategies of the enclosed medium makes ecological balancing difficult. Hence, determining the direction of evolution is difficult (Gilmore et al. 2019). Although two approaches (serial dilution and continuous enrichment approaches) are used, scientists have tried to balance this system of SynCom assembly with no success. The signs of ecological imbalance in a SynCom system include species elimination and invasion and random small displacement of nutrients (Gilmore et al. 2019; Díaz‐García et al. 2021).

Top‐Down Recruitment

Top‐down recruitment involves the assembly of several stable cocultivations of bacterial groups to perform desired traits. This process involves long‐term cultivation and regulation of the community, resulting in severe competition for the same resources, and one strain may tend to be dominant. In this strategy, the metagenome of the entire native community is modified, capturing abundant natural diversity while promoting or eliminating target functions (Zuroff and Curtis 2012; Xie et al. 2021).

Multiassembly Strategy

A combination of the first two concepts led to the assembly of multiple members into a community on the basis of metabolic network principles. To regulate the community and enhance its function, the changing environmental variables (e.g., temperature cycling) are modified (Krieger et al. 2021).

Current Methods for Constructing the SynCom Microbial System

The construction of the SynCom microbial system helps to better understand the reciprocal interplay between microbial consortia and their environment, revealing the consequences for ecosystem functioning. The common principle of construction, which contributes to the diversity and stability maintenance of natural microbial communities, is the cross‐feeding‐based approach. During microbial growth in a SynCom system, substrates are utilized for byproduct release. A significant byproduct of increased microbial stability through the microbial reductionist principle is metabolite production. Additionally, when a metabolite is found in an environment, microbes lose some functionality due to mutation and auxotroph formation (D'Souza and Kost 2016). This metabolite must also be transferred to a receiver that will build an interactive network with the producer in a simple cross‐feeding manner. The energy conversion‐associated cross‐feeding mechanism can improve the adaptability of both feeders because of the acquired metabolite. This construction method can be one‐directional, two‐directional, or multiple‐directional and involves diverse species and groups of different genotypes (Fritts et al. 2021; Smith et al. 2019). Although, in nature, more complex cross‐feeding can occur, for example, the degradation of lignocellulose, some microbial community members are involved in releasing intermediate products through degradation pathways that are used by subsequent members, whereas other members breakdown toxic intermediate byproducts. Another method of cross‐feeding based on exchange involves microbe–microbe surface interactions. Through a microbial community that can transfer electrons, the methanogenesis process can be driven (Rotaru et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2021). When all members gain from cross‐feeding exchange, it is known as functional specialization (Giri et al. 2019). Through this specialized division of labor interactions, SynCom retains the capacity to promote ecosystem functions, developing tolerance to environmental turbulence and resource utilization. Competition can arise between crossfeeding members, irrespective of their cooperative interspecific relationship (Becker et al. 2019). Despite the multiple types of stability present during cross‐feeding, maintaining stability throughout the process is a considerable challenge. The major evolutionary–genetic instability that occurs results from cheaters that occur due to genetic mutations resulting in functional gene deletions (Pande et al. 2014; E. T. Miller and Klausmeier 2017). Instability could result from the presence of toxic or inhibiting intermediate metabolites and external interference (such as antibiotic resistance of most intolerant members, short‐board effects, a multidirectional cross‐feeding system, and disturbance or death of keystone community members, which turn the system into a competitive system, leading to system collapse) (Adamowicz et al. 2018; Hammarlund et al. 2019). The preference of SynCom members for similar substrates leads to growth competition, stress in metabolism, and selective substrate utilization. In SynCom, competition for substrates weakens stability. In addition, SynCom substrate utilization leads to the formation of byproducts. However, these byproducts can be utilized by communities to establish stable populations or can be excessively released in other synthetic cultures as inhibitors. The release of byproducts as inhibitors limits the growth of SynCom members and their metabolism to establish an unstable population. A well‐organized interaction between engineered microbes for balanced SynCom population establishment must be mutualistic to achieve cooperation in the microbial community (Sedlmayer et al. 2018).

Optimization of the Cross‐Feeding Approach

Ecological Model

This cross‐feeding approach can be optimized by relying on model building on ecological parameters. These parameters must be substantial for model fitting, as seen in the generalized Lotka–Volterra (gLV) and consumer‐resource (C‐R) models (Coyte et al. 2015; Niehaus et al. 2019). Recently, Liao et al. (2020) developed a coarse‐grained metabolic model that stimulated one‐directional, two‐directional, and multidirectional cross‐feeding for the optimization of more complex communities.

Quorum Sensing

Quorum sensing (QS) is a microbial mechanism that is density dependent and can promote metabolite tuning for cross‐feeding. QS regulates the exudation of extracellular digestive enzymes that produce significant metabolites to a threshold level such that the activities of some microbes are activated and their phenotype changes (Guo et al. 2021).

Metabolic Engineering

This method exploits the strengths and interactions of diverse species to optimize productivity. In diverse species, cross‐feeding with metabolically engineered species can reconstitute heterologous metabolic pathways, and a variety of strategies can be used to increase the metabolic flux of desired products (Park et al. 2018). Hence, rapid product detection technology (other technology applications for detection are shown in Table 1) and the operating efficiency of SynCom should be optimized (Zhalnina et al. 2018).

Construction on the Basis of the Quorum‐Sensing Circuit

This strategy helps to design, control, and stabilize the microbial population in SynCom by releasing a small chemical signaling molecule that controls desired gene expression (also known as an autoinducer) (Marchand and Collins 2016; C. Xu and Yu 2021). This principle operates through a cascade of signaling molecules and quorum‐sensing cell regulation, which activate or repress members of the synthetic microbial community to target gene expression and chemical production and regulate population levels (Marchand and Collins 2016; Wu et al. 2020). Most of these regulatory chemicals are produced by gram‐negative bacteria; for example, Escherichia coli can reprogram glycolytic fluxes by reducing the oversecretion of deleterious intermediates and increasing myo‐inositol production (a product of interest) (Gupta et al. 2017). Through synthetic QS‐mediated genetic circuits, E. coli (having reached the desired cell density) releases beta‐glucosidase to convert cellobiose to glucose and then to isobutanol in a circuit directed to express enzymes related to isopropanol generation. In addition, acylated homoserine lactone (AHL) is one of the most conserved quorum sensing signals regulated by gram‐negative bacteria. The LuxR–AHL complex can be initiated through this circuit to promote the transcription of target genes, which control specific biochemical products (Joint et al. 2002; Jiang et al. 2023). Therefore, through gene circuits, the population of the microbial community can be controlled. Consequently, the pathogenic nature of most quorum‐sensing strains raises concerns for synthetic communities established for plant–microbe partnerships. However, a promising QS circuit that can be applied for this partnership without pathogen concerns is the AI‐2 quorum circuit, which is recognized by a wide variety of bacterial strains (Sedlmayer et al. 2018). Phosphorylated from AI‐1, the AI‐2 circuit activates the expression of T7 RNA polymerase, followed by LasI gene synthesis of AI‐1, which binds the LasR gene. The latter gene activates the las promoter, resulting in HPr expression, which increases the cell growth rate (Jiang et al. 2023). Therefore, cooperative coordination of cell density and metabolism in SynCom population‐level dynamics can be programmed on the basis of the mutual coexistence of strains and corresponding QS circuits.

Trait‐Based Approach

This method is based on assessing the phenotypic properties of individual species in a community, in addition to their anatomical structures, organ functions, behavioral characteristics, cell morphology, dynamic processes and biochemical characteristics. It is challenging to quantify and characterize traits expressed by individual species for ecological functions. However, for microbes, the key traits that determine environmental adaptability are vague, and understanding traits at the community level is difficult, as information from individual traits may conceal local functionalities (Krause et al. 2014; Triadó‐Margarit et al. 2019). A trait‐based approach provides a basis for microbial structure manipulation to understand alterations in community functionality and their ripple effect on stability. By applying a trait‐based model, an environmental limit at which communities attain stability can be discovered, with side indications of functional redundancy. Trait‐based analysis of functional genes and protein sequence analysis are susceptible to the plasticity of traits, leading to trait (genotype and phenotype) diversity (Coutinho et al. 2016). Consequently, one can predict the complementary impacts of traits relative to taxa or gene or protein sequences via a model. For example, on the basis of three traits (moderate leaf nitrogen concentration, thick back and dense wood), and postdisaster mountain forest restoration in western North America, the complementary effects of the traits were predicted by regulating species number and introducing new species (Wanapaisan et al. 2018). With knowledge of the functionality of species, a library or culture collection bank with appropriate taxa and traits can be established.

Spatial‐Based Construction

Two basic community characteristics are species composition (species interaction) and structure (species diversity) (H. J. Kim et al. 2008). The spatial structure concept is related to the distribution of individual species and the functionality of a species within space. This structure is a combined effect of population biological characteristics, environmental conditions and intra‐ and interspecific interactions. The commonest manner of community spatial structure construction can be divided into two parts. The first mechanism involves cell movement and morphological changes and the self‐assembly of microorganisms through the differential adhesion hypothesis (Steinberg 2007; Druckenbrod et al. 2005). The spatial structure of multiple species is stronger than that of single or double species, as observed in biofilm communities. This structure is applied for ecosystem restoration and maintenance in a multikingdom structure. The community spatial structure helps understand species diversity and interactions in the community and dynamic population processes and reveals the mechanism underlying species maintenance in the natural community (Y. Liang et al. 2022). The major challenges associated with spatial structure include the inability to cultivate microbial systems in the laboratory, the stability of many microbial communities due to unbalanced competition between species for nutrients, the complexity associated with microbial community studies, and the large scale of analysis (Pandin et al. 2017). In the natural environment, bacteria are affected by several physical, biological, and chemical factors at the microscale. At the community scale, they affect several traits by impacting bacteria and nutrient diffusion (König et al. 2020). For example, in an association of two or three species, the synthetic microbial community is affected by species competition, the transmission of signaling molecules and the uniformity of nutrients in a homogenized environment. These factors may contribute to their lack of coexistence in such environments but coexist in structured or separated environments when microfluidic devices are used (Vallespir Lowery and Ursell 2019; H. J. Kim et al. 2008). This implies that for adequate species coexistence and a synthetic microbial community, a microscale spatial structure must be employed.

In addition, studies have revealed that cells maintain a targeted spatial structure on the basis of the abundance of local metabolites. For example, exopolysaccharide release promotes aggregation in biofilms, and the spatial structure of biofilms changes with changes in exopolysaccharide concentration. Furthermore, bacteria with locomotive behavioral responses (chemotaxis), which are controlled by chemical substance concentration dynamics, can directly alter community spatial structure (Ziemba et al. 2016; Y. Liang et al. 2022).

SynCom in Ecological Concept

In addition to functionality validation, this engineering approach is linked to the consequences for ecosystem health and the function of applying microbial swarmbots at ecosystem‐level resolution to comprehend the reciprocal interplay between the environment, host plant, and microbiota. The engineering of synthetic communities in plant–microbe partnerships (realistic field applications) has led to the design of a synthetic ecological framework otherwise known as SynEco. This engineered and reconstituted ecological system mimics the natural ecosystem in a programmed manner that promotes beneficial ecological services. There are diverse forms of synthetic ecology that arise from SynCom, such as the simple plant SynEco with a bacterial strain, a defined growth medium, a plant in a growth chamber, multiple plant SynEco with bacterial strains or fungal strains, plants and defined growth media in a chamber, and finally, the next‐generation SynEcos or multikingdom SynCom. The latter is a completely programmed gnotobiotic growth chamber that reconstitutes ecosystem complexity with balanced system control, bridging the gap between laboratory observations and natural ecosystem processes via a reductionist approach (Tecon et al. 2019; Chesneau et al. 2025). A SynEco must recapitulate a nutritional/chemical sphere—a metabolic system that monitors the interplay between the plant and microbial environment (Novak et al. 2024)—an environmental and climatic sphere—an environmental perturbation representing a sphere that, in some cases, includes abiotic or biotic stress. Capturing seasonal changes in temperature, day length and light intensity. Using low‐cost, programmable and miniaturized growth chambers or expensive ecotron‐like chambers, ecosystem functions can be continuously measured on a SynCom continuum (Chesneau et al. 2025; Yee et al. 2021), and a SynCom sphere, which includes single, multiple, or multiple organismal spheres, allows inter‐ and intraspecific interactions. The applicable SynEco in agriculture (Table 2) is the next‐generation SynEco and has been the focus of recent research, which relies on the physical and chemical composition, plant organ composition, organismal diversity, and temporal dynamics of the climatic conditions of an ecosystem (Montoya et al. 2025). Notably, the SynCom community applied for SynEco construction must include genomically defined culture collections and a well‐engineered system that efficiently mimics ecosystem parameters and links parameters to phenotypic traits (Salvatori et al. 2021).

Ecological and Biological Constraints Limiting the Field Deployment of Engineered Plant–Microbe Systems

Despite the revolutionary potential of this technology, the large‐scale use of engineered microbes continues to be limited by obstacles (Sharma and Bora 2025). It is significantly difficult to translate the positive results of engineered microbe–plant partnerships to actual field applications from laboratory studies. Field ecosystems are variable and complex, leading to performance anomalies in engineered microorganisms compared with laboratory conditions (Wankhade et al. 2025). Plants form a holobiont with their associated microbiota, and the interactions among members of the microbiome affect the performance of the crop as well as the proper functioning of the holobiont. Additionally, ecosystem functioning is influenced by the depth of interaction between various plant species and organisms (Tripathi et al. 2025). For example, the traits that are engineered into plants and microbes are not always stable, and it is difficult for these microbes to survive in different environments. Additionally, as depicted in Figure 1, some of these microbes do not always work well with the plants they are supposed to help, and there are also problems, such as how to deliver these microbes to the plants and find the right microbes to use (Schnabel and Sattely 2021b). Another key limitation is regulatory issues and concerns about safety, as it takes time to obtain approval, which makes it difficult to sell these products. In addition, it is expensive to develop these products, and farmers are not always sure about how to use them. Some people are also skeptical about this type of engineering (Ploll et al. 2022; English et al. 2026). All these issues mean that synthetic biology‐driven plant–microbe engineering is not being used much as it could be. This is a problem because synthetic biology‐driven plant–microbe engineering could be helpful for agriculture and could be used more if these issues were solved.

Obstacles to the successful adoption of synthetic biology‐driven engineering of plant–microbe partnerships.

Environmental Variability

The success of using engineered microbes within the soil is strongly affected by variations in the physical and chemical parameters of the soil (Krieger et al. 2021). For example, soil pH and large‐scale environmental gradients are consistently identified as key determinants of soil microbial community structure (Wankhade et al. 2025). This was clearly observed in controlled pH‐manipulation experiments in contrasting agricultural soils from Dezhou (alkaline; pH 8.43) and Wuxi (slightly acidic; pH 6.17). These findings indicate that fungal communities respond to pH variation but far less strongly than bacterial communities do, whose diversity and composition show pronounced pH sensitivity, particularly in initially acidic soils. These findings highlight the importance of initial soil conditions in mediating the effects of pH on microbial assembly. Adeniji et al. (2025) also examined how soil texture and nutrient availability shape root exudation patterns and their downstream effects on rhizosphere microbial communities, disease suppression, and plant resilience. Clay soils, characterized by dense structure and poor aeration, constrain root growth and stimulate enhanced exudate release to improve nutrient acquisition and recruit beneficial microbes, whereas sandy soils generally promote easier root penetration and reduced exudation. At broader spatial scales, field surveys and microcosm experiments along latitudinal gradients have demonstrated that microbial diversity patterns are kingdom dependent, with bacterial diversity decreasing, protist diversity exhibiting clumped distributions, and T4‐like viral diversity increasing with latitude. Climatic and edaphic factors dominate bacterial community structuring, with climatic influences intensifying sharply between 30° N and 32° N, whereas edaphic controls, including soil pH, remain relatively stable across the gradient. Collectively, these studies position soil pH as a strong but context‐dependent filter operating within a hierarchical framework of climatic, edaphic, and biotic controls on soil microbial biogeography (Xiong et al. 2024; X. Huang et al. 2024). Even changes in soil temperature influence key enzymes in nitrogen‐fixing bacteria. For example, vanadium nitrogenase at a temperature near 5°C was effective as the most reliable enzyme used during the nitrogen fixation process. Nonetheless, at temperatures as high as 30°C, molybdenum nitrogenase, owing to its improved affinity for nitrogen compared with vanadium nitrogenase, demonstrated better efficacy (Goicoechea 2020). These changes in environmental factors (pH, climate, and temperature) have the potential to inhibit engineered microbes and silence the expression of some characteristics, including phytohormone synthesis, nitrogen fixation, and siderophore production (Sharma and Bora 2025), posing major obstacles to the widespread use of this technology (Enagbonma et al. 2025).

Microbial Community Dynamics

Another challenge that works against the effective collaboration between genetically engineered microbes and plants is the dynamics of microbial communities (Babalola et al. 2020). The interaction between engineered microbes and the host plant in a natural habitat is challenging because of the activities of the indigenous microbiome, competition with native microbiota, and the stability and persistence of engineered microbes or consortia (Enagbonma et al. 2023). For example, population regulation by engineered E. coli was examined in soil microcosms by Recorbet et al. (1992). Their study revealed that sterile soils supported growth to stable densities, whereas natural soils caused a decline driven by predators and competitors. Protozoan predation and microbial competition limited persistence, and engineered strains presented reduced fitness relative to that of the wild type. Maintaining the prolonged stability of engineered microbes is a feat that must be pursued vigorously, as they compete fiercely with native rhizosphere microorganisms (Shayanthan et al. 2022).

A key factor in microbial community dynamics is the difficulty of allowing engineered microbes to outcompete the native microbiome for space and nutrients and colonize the rhizosphere (Sharma and Bora 2025). A study by Haskett et al. (2022) demonstrated that engineering rhizosphere microbes has the potential to alter their capacity to compete with indigenous soil microbes. Researchers modified barley to synthesize scyllo‐inosamine, which increased the sensitivity of barley to the nitrogen‐fixing bacterium Azorhizobium caulinodans. Despite improved colonization by A. caulinodans on the mutant barley, the engineered bacterium showed little nitrogenase activity, leading to decreased plant biomass. Therefore, there is a need for microbial consortia. Haskett et al. (2022) revealed that despite the strong emphasis on SynComs as predictable tools for enhancing plant performance, several experimentally validated studies (Table 3) have demonstrated that successful microbial colonization does not always translate into improved plant growth and, in some cases, can produce neutral or even negative outcomes (Yurgel et al. 2022; Schmitz et al. 2022). Microbial consortia function as complex adaptive systems whose emergent dynamics cannot be inferred from individual species alone. Kinetic modeling of multispecies interactions by P. Xu (2021) revealed that stable coexistence arises mainly through parasitism, commensalism, or cooperation. Notably, commensal systems optimize intermediate‐to‐product conversion and metabolic stability, offering valuable insights for ecological theory, bioprocess engineering, and therapeutic microbiome applications.

Furthermore, manipulation of the exudate composition affects microbiomes, but outcomes vary (Dundas and Dinneny 2022). For example, Kawasaki et al. (2021) investigated how modifying the expression of aluminum‐activated malate transporters and multidrug and toxic compound extrusion transporters in rice and wheat alters the release of organic anions from root apices and shapes root‐associated microbiomes. These changes in exudate composition (including malate, citrate, and γ‐aminobutyric acid contents) influence microbial community structure, root type, and root position. These findings highlight that targeted manipulation of root exudates can modulate cereal microbiomes and anatomical factors, which impose important constraints on microbiome engineering for crop improvement.

Host–Microbe Specificity

Deciphering complex communication between host genotypes and associated microbiota is also a major challenge limiting the field deployment of engineered plant–microbe systems (Fadiji et al. 2025). This is because the plant microbiota community structure is predominantly determined by the host genotype (Tabassum et al. 2024), and the compatibility of engineered microbes with different plant cultivars varies from plant to plant (Durán et al. 2022). This finding was supported by Schenk et al. (2024), who reported that plant growth‐promoting rhizobacteria (PGPRs) varied in effectiveness across different cultivars of industrial hemp, with each cultivar showing a preference for different microbial strains, demonstrating cultivar‐specific compatibility. Rotoni et al. (2024) reported that inoculation effects (on growth and the rhizosphere microbiome) differed markedly among five chrysanthemum cultivars, with some cultivars responding positively and others responding negatively to the same microbial treatments. A field study by Kurt (2025) further revealed significant cultivar × dose interactions, showing that different maize cultivars responded differently to the same microbial inoculant doses, highlighting the genotype‐dependent nature of microbial responsiveness.

Notably, host preference for engineered microbes is a community trait and is undetectable via monoassociation. Microbes can modulate host factors and preferentially choose one kind of host over another within the same plant community. Similarly, microbes from the original soil from which a plant was taken preferentially colonized the same plants even when they were sown in sterile soil rather than microbes from a different soil source (Copeland et al. 2025; Carlström et al. 2019). Hence, to benefit maximally from engineered microbes, both the plant and microbes must align for efficient persistence of mutant microbes within the microbiome. This will require a deep knowledge of the mechanisms behind the host preference of microorganisms (Copeland et al. 2025).

Complex Interactions

The successful establishment of a stable plant–microbe partnership may permanently alter the microbiome, while its lack may confer temporary, limited effects with subsequent disappearance that prevent permanent changes in the environment (X. Xu et al. 2025). This was observed in the Hannula et al. (2021) experiment, where fungal legacies were more persistent in the soil, whereas bacterial effects faded more quickly but could be maintained internally within the roots. These findings support the idea that a stable plant–microbe association can drive longer‐term microbiome alterations. This finding was further supported by C. Chen et al. (2022), a study that showed that even after beneficial bacterial inoculum is eliminated from soil, modifications in plant roots (epigenetic changes) persist and continue influencing plant growth. While not directly about microbiome permanence, it shows that lasting plant responses can occur even when the microbial partner does not persist.

The complex nature of plant–microbe interactions, involving several signaling pathways and regulatory networks, makes understanding their dynamics challenging (Jain et al. 2024). This finding was supported by Bao et al. (2025), a study that revealed how a bacterial effector can manipulate plant signaling pathways by interacting with receptor proteins (e.g., NFR5), indicating a sophisticated interplay between microbial effectors and plant immune and symbiotic signaling networks. Gao et al. (2021) demonstrated that soybean Nod factor receptors (NFR1 and NFR5) form a complex with signaling proteins (such as GmGEF2 and GTPases) during rhizobium symbiosis, revealing how multiple proteins and pathways interact to regulate symbiotic signaling.

In addition, determining the kinetics and mechanism of signal recognition at the molecular level and responses in different plant–microbe systems requires complex analytical techniques and experimental methods. For example, Y. Chen et al. (2024) identified a specific microbial metabolite (anthranilic acid) that influences plant root architecture through auxin signaling and ROS‐mediated pathways. The methods involve metabolite identification, signaling assays, and cell wall remodeling analyses, illustrating the technical complexity of dissecting microbial signal perception and downstream pathways in plant tissues. Another investigation by Palmer et al. (2014) revealed how plants perceive bacterial quorum‐sensing molecules and how enzymatic degradation affects signaling responses. This work uses chemical biology and network analysis techniques to separate recognition and response, highlighting the experimental challenges in quantifying microbial signal recognition and plant perception kinetics at the molecular level. The diverse nature and adaptation strategies of microbes make their understanding more complex, and detailed studies across several ecological niches and microbial taxa are needed (Jain et al. 2024).

Technical and Regulatory Barriers to Synthetic Biology‐Driven Plant–Microbe Engineering

Although synthetic biology holds promise for redesigning plant–microbe interactions to increase soil and plant nutrients while ensuring that plants are resilient to environmental stresses, several technical and regulatory barriers currently limit its translation from laboratory research to field applications (Clauer et al. 2026; Mandel and Marchant 2014).

Technical Limitations to Synthetic Biology‐Driven Plant–Microbe Engineering

First, there is high microbial diversity in the environment, and many microbes cannot be easily cultivated or manipulated (K. G. Lloyd et al. 2018). An investigation involving both cultivation and molecular surveys by Chaudhary et al. (2019) revealed that many bacterial taxa present in soil do not grow on traditional media and remain uncultured. Furthermore, metagenomic analysis of the wheat rhizosphere performed by Youseif et al. (2021) revealed that only ~1%–2.5% of the total bacterial diversity could be grown via standard laboratory culture methods, demonstrating that the vast majority of bacteria are not cultured easily. The effect of this is that it limits the organisms available for engineering and assembly of synthetic consortia.

Another barrier to synthetic biology‐driven plant–microbe engineering lies in the deployment of synthetic biology tools themselves (Enagbonma et al. 2025). Techniques such as CRISPR/Cas genome editing, modular design, and computational design require sophisticated expertise, specialized infrastructure, and integration with computational tools, which may be scarce in many research settings (Karimi et al. 2026; Ruffolo et al. 2025). Furthermore, the delivery of genetic constructs into plant genomes is technically difficult because of the rigid cell walls and complex tissue structures and may require advanced transformation methods. For example, Thorpe et al. (2025), a recent study on biolistic (particle bombardment) transformation, revealed that despite decades of use, delivery efficiency and consistency remain low. This is due to physical barriers such as tissue damage caused by microprojectiles and inefficient penetration of tissues. This highlights the technical difficulty of delivering DNA/RNP constructs into diverse plant tissues. Additionally, K. Miller et al. (2021) developed an improved method to evaluate DNA and CRISPR–Cas delivery in plant tissues. However, the authors reported that the biolistic transformation efficiency varies widely and is inconsistent across samples, partly because plant tissue barriers and cell walls make reproducible delivery difficult.

In addition, the cost of acquiring and operating these synthetic biology tools remains substantial (Somaly et al. 2026). DNA synthesis, gene assembly, and high‐throughput design platforms can cost thousands to tens of thousands of dollars per project, and advanced software or sequencing systems often demand significant investment, which limits participation by smaller research groups and in developing regions (Vegh et al. 2024).

Regulatory Issues Limiting Synthetic Biology‐Driven Plant–Microbe Engineering

On the regulatory side, there are challenges of regulatory uncertainty and long approval timelines for synthetic biology products (Fatehi and Hall 2015). This is because synthetic biology products often fall under existing GMO frameworks, which vary widely between nations, leading to regulatory uncertainty and long approval timelines (Marden et al. 2023). For example, Anyshchenko and Yarnold (2021) revealed that EU GMO regulation, shaped by bovine spongiform encephalopathy‐driven precaution, lags behind modern biotechnology, diverges from US approaches, fuels trade disputes, constrains genome editing and synthetic biology, and risks future food security globally. H. Kim et al. (2025) highlight divergent and inconsistent interpretations of synthetic biology under existing GMO regulatory systems across countries. This inconsistency affects innovation, commercialization, and cross‐border regulation. A recent industry survey revealed that regulatory complexity and uncertainty are the top barriers to synthetic biology commercialization, with companies citing average approval timelines of 24–36 months under GMO‐based frameworks (Eureka 2025). Additionally, in some countries, living‐modified organism authorization processes can take years and account for a large proportion of compliance costs (Faure and Jiang 2022).

Furthermore, regulatory systems are often not yet tailored to novel synthetic constructs, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, which trails E. coli in synthetic circuits despite their long human use and industrial promise. Thus, regulations are complicated, creating ambiguity in risk assessment and biosafety evaluation (Blount et al. 2012). Groff‐Vindman et al. (2025) noted that governance and regulatory frameworks, such as AI‐enabled genome design, were not designed for rapid technology convergence, leading to regulatory gaps and oversight challenges. Carter et al. (2014) identify specific regulatory gaps where US agencies may not have premarket authority over many synthetic biology techniques or constructs, highlighting that existing frameworks do not align with current synthetic biology capabilities.

Another ongoing challenge is how to harmonize frameworks internationally and develop clear guidelines that consider the novel traits of engineered microbiomes (Duhan et al. 2025). For example, Chemla et al. (2025) review how modern regulatory systems struggle to address engineered microbes released into the environment and propose new regulatory frameworks informed by recent global experience, explicitly pointing to the challenge of harmonizing regulations internationally. An assessment by Rodriguez et al. (2025) also described the evolving regulatory landscape for microbiome‐based therapies and highlighted differences in regulatory status across jurisdictions in Europe and the United States, noting the need for harmonized frameworks and clearer regulatory science to support global development and patient access.

Biosafety Issues Limiting Synthetic Biology‐Driven Plant–Microbe Engineering

The deployment of synthetic biology‐driven plant–microbe engineering in agriculture is also limited by biosafety concerns, and the main issue is linked with environmental risk (Parker and Kunjapur 2020). Many studies have questioned how native soil microbiota respond to unintended horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from engineered microbes, which can potentially alter ecosystem functions and resistomes (Gillett et al. 2025; Von Wintersdorff et al. 2016). Macedo et al. (2022) reported that plasmid transfer to native soil bacteria following manure amendment in soil microcosms involved the detection of transconjugants via 16S sequencing and the quantification of conjugation events. Rare events occurred shortly after amendment, and the plasmid did not persist long‐term in the soil community. Furthermore, genomic research has revealed recent HGT of antibiotic resistance genes across species within soil‐derived Listeria, revealing how genomic elements circulate in natural soil microbiota under environmental influence.

The persistence and spread of engineered organisms beyond target fields also raise concerns about ecological imbalance and loss of controllability (Then et al. 2020).

There is also uncertainty around off‐target effects, where engineered traits may interact unpredictably with plant hosts, nontarget organisms, or environmental stressors.

Additionally, public perception and ethical considerations, especially regarding environmental release, influence precautionary regulatory decisions. Together, these biosafety challenges necessitate robust risk assessment models, long‐term ecological monitoring, and updated governance structures before large‐scale agricultural adoption becomes feasible.

Emerging Directions and Prospects for Advancement

Although the scope of engineering plant–microbe partnerships still faces challenges, ranging from incomplete mechanistic understanding to translation and field stability constraints, emerging research directions offer fundamental concepts for overcoming these limitations (Fujiwara et al. 2023). Findings from Francioli et al. (2025), Hernández‐García et al. (2025), and (Mazumder et al. 2026) demonstrated that advances in multiomics technologies, host–target engineering, computational modeling, and synthetic microbial consortium (SynCom) design are improving the prediction, assembly, and ecological stabilization of beneficial plant–microbe interactions under both greenhouse and field conditions. Rather than relying solely on theoretical frameworks, Yamazaki et al. (2025) and Y. Song et al. (2026) increasingly integrate field‐scale validation with mechanistic data sets to evaluate SynCom persistence, colonization efficiency, nutrient mobilization, and crop yield performance across environmental gradients. However, these technologies are not universally successful, and several studies have reported inconsistencies in engineered trait expression, poor reproducibility across soil environments, and reduced microbial persistence under field conditions, as reported in Table 3. These scientific developments are expanding into engineered agroecosystems through precision farming, crop improvement programs, and sustainable agriculture, in which engineered microbiomes are plausibly used to augment low‐input agroecosystems through measurable improvements in nutrient‐use efficiency, drought resilience, disease suppression, and soil functional stability (C. Wang, Zhang, et al. 2025). Consequently, emerging technologies must be evaluated not only for their innovative potential but also for their scalability, ecological reliability, and long‐term field validation (Zhan 2024). The following sections underscore the most promising emerging directions and how these innovations can be used to compensate for the existing barriers while creating new avenues through which synthetic biology can improve plant–microbe systems.

Multiomics and Systems Biology Approaches: Using Genomics, Transcriptomics, Metabolomics, and Proteomics to Guide Design

Multiomics technologies have taken center stage in overcoming the historical hurdle of a limited mechanistic understanding of plant–microbe interactions (Jain et al. 2024). Since engineered functions frequently fail after being transferred from a controlled environment to heterogeneous soils, a better approach now depends on studying which microbial genes, metabolic pathways, and regulatory pathways promote positive effects in the natural environment under realistic agronomic conditions (Carlström et al. 2019; Mavrodi et al. 2021). To inform synthetic biology strategies, integrated multiomics systems, which combine genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, and, in certain cases, fluxomics, offer the multidimensional resolution necessary to identify these functional determinants. Experimental studies by Yadav et al. (2025) and Ma et al. (2025) have demonstrated that integrating metagenomics with metabolomic profiling can accurately predict microbial recruitment patterns and nutrient exchange networks within the rhizosphere, thereby improving SynCom assembly and stability under environmental stress.

Despite these advantages, multiomics integration still faces major limitations. Data sets generated from different omics platforms are often difficult to standardize because of variations in sampling procedures, sequencing depth, temporal resolution, and bioinformatic workflows. These inconsistencies can lead to contradictory interpretations of microbial function and community dynamics across studies. Moreover, many data sets are derived from controlled laboratory or greenhouse experiments, limiting their ecological representativeness under field conditions. The high cost of large‐scale multiomics experiments and the computational expertise required for integration further restrict their widespread adoption, particularly in low‐resource agricultural systems (Mallick et al. 2017).

Genomics provides a fundamental schematic for engineering by revealing biosynthesis‐related gene clusters, stress–response modules, effector repertoires, and metabolic capabilities that determine rhizosphere fitness (Carlström et al. 2019). High‐resolution comparative genomics has also revealed genetic traits linked to plant growth promotion, including siderophore production, phytohormone regulation, antimicrobial pathways, and root colonization factors (Kumar et al. 2022; Levy et al. 2018). For example, comparative genomic analyses of drought‐adapted rhizobacteria isolated from maize and sorghum rhizospheres revealed osmoprotectant synthesis pathways and exopolysaccharide‐associated genes that significantly enhanced root colonization and water‐use efficiency during field drought trials (Amer and Hamed 2025; Khaledi et al. 2025; Magaisa et al. 2025). These genomic markers are now being incorporated into rational SynCom selection pipelines to improve their ecological compatibility and persistence in semiarid agricultural systems (Shipar 2025). Transcriptomics provides the requisite background to understand how plant and microbial genes are expressed in response to environmental signals, plant exudates, and synthetic pathways. Studies have shown that root‐associated microbes respond to host chemical cues through dynamic transcriptional programs, allowing the discovery of stress‐regulated modules and inducible promoters suitable for engineered systems (Mavrodi et al. 2021; Bulgarelli et al. 2013). Dual RNA‐seq technology further revealed that sequencing of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens and Sclerotinia sclerotiorum during infection of soybean seedlings inhibits the expression of genes encoding the ribosomal subunit of S. sclerotiorum, resulting in reduced S. sclerotiorum susceptibility in soybean (Cheng et al. 2022). Proteomics and metabolomics associate gene expression patterns with real biochemical activity, which is a functional readout of engineered pathways. Root exudate metabolite‐based approaches have played crucial roles in informing host engineering approaches and forecasting the outcome of microbiome assembly (Zhalnina et al. 2018). Similarly, rhizosphere proteomics profiling has captured the presence of nitrogen‐cycling enzymes, quorum‐sensing proteins, and carbohydrate‐degrading pathways without revealing conspicuous microbes, which consequently reveals activities that impact the stability and activity of the consortium in situ (Keiblinger et al. 2016). Recent metabolomic‐guided studies across multiple phosphorus gradients in soybean further demonstrated that phosphorus starvation induced strong organic acid exudation and the recruitment of beneficial phosphorus‐acquiring microbes, including phosphate‐solubilizing organisms and arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM)‐associated functional groups (G. Zhao et al. 2025). These findings provide a basis for formulating phosphorus‐efficient soybean cultivars and for precision phosphorus management in sustainable farming.

The power of these multiomics methods lies in their ability to map connections among genes, proteins, metabolites, and phenotypes when such data sets are incorporated into systems biology frameworks. Integrative models, such as coexpression networks, metabolic reconstructions, and causal interaction models, reveal emergent properties of plant–microbe interactions, including cross‐feeding, metabolic complementarities, and host‐imposed restrictions. Importantly, recent systems‐level analyses have moved beyond descriptive profiling toward predictive modeling of SynCom performance. For example, genome‐scale metabolic modeling combined with computational prediction frameworks successfully predicted cooperative interactions among rhizosphere bacteria and identified consortium architectures that improved asparagine, vitamin B12, isoleucine, and their precursors or derivatives use efficiency and biomass accumulation in tomato rhizosphere trials (W. Wang, Xia, et al. 2025). Comparative analyses across recent experimental studies indicate that SynComs designed using integrated multiomics data sets and predictive computational frameworks generally outperform randomly assembled consortia in terms of colonization stability, stress resilience, and crop productivity metrics. For example, Yamazaki et al. (2025) combined microbial genomics, metabolomics, and machine‐learning approaches to design predictive SynComs that enhanced tomato growth and heat‐stress tolerance under both laboratory and field conditions, outperforming nonoptimized community assemblies. These findings suggest that data‐driven systems biology approaches are becoming essential for rational microbiome engineering.

A growing number of field‐oriented studies now provide quantitative evidence supporting the superiority of rationally designed SynComs. For example, Agoussar et al. (2025) reported that a 25‐member drought‐selected consortium significantly increased fresh wheat biomass under drought stress. The consortium also reshaped rhizosphere microbial communities toward drought‐resilient taxa, while Mulaudzi et al. (2025) observed from their field‐based maize study that AI‐assisted metabolomics was used to characterize microbial biostimulant consortia and demonstrated metabolic reprogramming linked to nutrient acquisition, stress adaptation, and improved crop performance. This work provides strong support for the use of metabolomics‐guided consortium design in sustainable nutrient management.

New technologies, such as single‐cell genomics, mass‐spectrometry imaging, and spatial transcriptomics, have now enabled researchers to map microbial activity and host–microbe exchange with unparalleled spatial resolution. These approaches have revealed microscale nutrient hotspots and localized signaling networks within the rhizosphere that were previously undetectable via bulk analyses, thereby improving the spatial precision of engineered microbial deployment strategies (Serrano et al. 2024; Veličković et al. 2024; Garcia Arredondo et al. 2024). Such developments are driving the broad field toward a more specific rhizosphere‐wide systems biology platform with an extensive ability to link biofunctions to genotype across scales. The mechanistic clarity offered by this new platform is necessary to support the truly rational and predictable engineering of plant–microbe partnerships.

With the increase in multiomics data sets, their core value essentially relies on computational frameworks that can predict how engineered microbes and host traits respond to changing environmental conditions. The broadening use of computational tools to predict and design models for plant–microbe interactions naturally enhances advances in synthetic biology. Recent artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) frameworks trained on multiomics data sets now enable the prediction of microbial compatibility, ecological persistence, and functional redundancy before field deployment, thereby reducing experimental uncertainty and accelerating SynCom optimization pipelines (Mulaudzi et al. 2025; De la Vega‐Camarillo et al. 2023).

Predictive Computational Modeling: AI and Network Models for Predicting Microbial Dynamics and Plant Responses

The increasing accessibility of multiomics data sets has led to an urgent need for computational tools that can predict and provide useful insight from translated raw data. The engineering of plant–microbe interactions via synthetic biology requires the ability to predict how engineered traits, microbial interactions, and environmental cues affect system behavior in the field (Ginatt et al. 2024; Deng et al. 2021). Predictive computational modeling, including ML, ecological network models, AI‐assisted design platforms, and genome‐scale metabolic models, currently serves as an analytical foundation needed to predict and inform choices on rational engineering (Deng et al. 2021).

ML and AI solutions are being applied to discover plant and microbial characteristics that drive positive impacts, including nutrient use efficiency, stress resilience, or disease suppression (Te Molder et al. 2021). For example, recent ML models that integrate multiomics data enable the prediction of specialized metabolic biosynthetic genes in Arabidopsis and further validate the genetic function of these genes in maize and tomato, highlighting the cross‐species prediction capacity of AI (W. Bai, Li, et al. 2024). Similarly, another study exploited ML to classify plant‐related bacterial phenotypes, where Pfam domain features were used on diverse Xanthomonas genomes to detect pathogenic strains and decipher genome‐encoded predictors for plant associations (Te Molder et al. 2021). Such ML‐based predictions can specify engineering targets, speed up hypothesis generation, and reduce reliance on the empirical trial‐and‐error approach. Nevertheless, several limitations constrain the reliability and generalizability of AI‐ and ML‐based approaches in plant–microbe engineering (Busato et al. 2023). Many predictive models are trained on relatively small, highly curated data sets generated under laboratory or greenhouse conditions, increasing the risk of overfitting and reducing predictive robustness under heterogeneous field environments (Aghdam et al. 2024). The complexity and context dependency of rhizosphere interactions further complicate model transferability across crops, soils, climatic zones, and developmental stages (Y. Liu et al. 2017). Additionally, discrepancies in metadata quality, sequencing protocols, and environmental measurements across studies can introduce biases into training data sets, thereby affecting model reproducibility (Forry et al. 2024). Some studies have also reported that predictive accuracies decline substantially when ML models are externally validated via independent field data sets rather than internally partitioned training data sets (Aghdam et al. 2024; Habibi et al. 2024). Consequently, although AI‐assisted modeling remains highly promising, substantial improvements in data set standardization, ecological validation, and longitudinal field experimentation are still needed before these models can reliably guide large‐scale agricultural deployment.

Complementary insights are offered by network‐based modeling, which represents metabolic exchanges, competition, and cooperation among microbial communities as an interconnected system. The metabolic potential of individual microbes can be simulated via genome‐scale metabolic models (GEMs) to predict their interaction networks under specific conditions. In one study, the GEMs of 193 Arabidopsis root‐associated bacteria were used to predict metabolic complementarity, cross‐feeding, and dependencies associated with simulated root exudate conditions (Mataigne et al. 2022). An in silico platform was also used to construct trophic‐dependency networks for a rhizosphere community by integrating metagenome‐resolved genomes with constraint‐based modeling to identify keystone taxa and potential resource flow (Carlström et al. 2019; Ginatt et al. 2024). These models provide a mechanistic understanding of species coexistence and stability, providing insights into which metabolites mediate cooperative interactions.

To model dynamics across space and time, computational platforms such as Computation of Microbial Ecosystems in Time and Space (COMETS) bring constraint‐based modeling to spatially structured environments. COMETS enables metabolic interaction simulation, combining cell growth with diffusion in a physical space, to predict how microbial consortia develop across gradients, how resource sharing evolves, and how engineered strains persist or dominate in a system (Dukovski et al. 2020). However, many spatial and metabolic simulations still rely on simplified assumptions regarding nutrient diffusion, microbial behavior, and environmental homogeneity, which may not accurately represent the complexity of natural rhizosphere systems (Endress et al. 2024). These spatiotemporal models enable the design of synthetic communities with potential high resilience under natural soil conditions. Emerging AI‐aided design systems now integrate multiomics, ecological networks, metabolic models, and environmental parameters into unified pipelines for in silico evaluations. A recent field experiment with maize involving AI‐integrated metabolomics in which ML was applied to LC–MS/MS system data revealed the effects of various microbial biostimulant consortia on plant metabolism under more realistic agronomic conditions (Mulaudzi et al. 2025). By integrating predictive modeling with experimental metabolomics, researchers can predict how engineered microbes or treatments influence metabolic pathways, stress responses, and growth dynamics in plants.

Furthermore, promising approaches based on hybrid modeling exist, where in one proof‐of‐concept, ML regressors are fed with metabolic network descriptors to predict cross‐feeding and the production of specific metabolites by microbial consortia (Silva‐Andrade et al. 2025). This coupling, in which ML complements GEM simulations, can be used to dramatically accelerate the prioritization and selection of strain combinations or metabolic designs for experimental validation (Mataigne et al. 2022). Despite this progress, contradictory findings regarding microbial interaction predictions and consortium stability remain common in the literature (Momeni et al. 2017; Meroz et al. 2021), emphasizing that computational predictions must still be validated experimentally across multiple environments and growing seasons. These predictive models are becoming increasingly powerful and integrative, enhancing precision in host engineering technology, which allows rational engineering of host traits, such as exudates or immune signaling, to align with designed microbial partners on the basis of predictions.

Precision Host Engineering: Modifying Plant Exudates, Immune Pathways, or Signaling to Favor Beneficial Microbes

A significant concern in engineering plant–microbe partnerships is the influence that the plant host itself has on microbiomes in terms of selective recruitment driven by root exudation, immune surveillance, and various signaling pathways (Y. Bai, Liu, et al. 2024). These processes serve as efficient ecological filters, determining which microbes colonize, survive, or function in the rhizosphere (Harcombe et al. 2014). As a result, even engineered strains of microorganisms can fail to implement or express engineered phenotypes owing to incompatibility with host control factors. However, precision host engineering tools, such as CRISPR/Cas systems, promoter editing, and metabolic or signaling pathway refactoring, offer avenues to modify plant traits to promote beneficial microbes and stabilize engineered interactions (Ginatt et al. 2024; Shekhawat et al. 2022).

An experimentally validated predictor of this process is root exudates, which act as a chemical filtration system for microbial recruitment (Y. Bai, Liu, et al. 2024). The root exudates of Arabidopsis thaliana contain specialized coumarins, particularly scopoletin, which strongly influence the rhizosphere microbial community structure. Scopoletin increases under certain conditions, and its presence can determine the abundance or shortage of specific microbial taxa, indicating a selective mechanism (Stringlis et al. 2019). Benzoxazinoids (BXs), a category of defense‐related metabolites, also play crucial roles in determining the rhizosphere microbiota in maize. Field research on various genetic lines and sites revealed that BX exudation strongly influences bacterial and fungal community structures (Cadot et al. 2021). Certain maize rhizobacteria can metabolize BXs through special enzymes, such as a bxdA gene‐encoded lactonase, suggesting that host‐secreted metabolites can select for metabolically specialized bacteria (Thoenen et al. 2024). With precision host engineering, it is possible to engineer or select for plants that release certain metabolites through their roots to shape the rhizosphere microbiome (Chesneau et al. 2025). This is evident in a study where A. thaliana was genetically altered to overexpress glucose and sucrose transporters in its root epidermal cells. This change increased sugar release into the soil, which consequently altered the structure of the microbial community such that certain bacterial species, such as Rhodanobacter, became prevalent, whereas certain fungal species, such as Candida, were reduced (M. Song et al. 2022).

Another promising direction in precision host engineering is to rewire plant immune pathways to enhance adaptation to beneficial microbes. This is exemplified in a study on A. thaliana, in which Van der Ent et al. (2008) employed loss‐of‐function genetic methods to demonstrate that the root‐specific transcription factor MYB72 is required during the initial stages of rhizobacterium‐induced systemic resistance (ISR). Mutations of myb72 result in failure to mount ISR in response to beneficial rhizobacteria, and subsequent studies have shown that myb72 regulates components of its downstream pathway, including BGLU42 and coumarin biosynthesis. These are experimentally validated host immune/regulatory node engineering approaches involving alterations that change the recruitment and activity of the microbiome (Van der Ent et al. 2008). Another effective pathway for host engineering is the precise manipulation of signaling pathways. Although certain previous claims from synthetic biology, such as synthetic Nod‐factor receptors in nonlegumes, are speculative, naturally, plants are able to perceive bacterial signals. For example, tomato cells are capable of perceiving rhizobial Nod factors but also degrade them via chitinases (Staehelin et al. 1994), highlighting both their capacity and their constraints in synthetic biology engineering. Additionally, although direct evidence that CRISPR‐edited EIN2 (ethylene signaling) plants prefer to associate with beneficial microbes under salt stress conditions is limited, ethylene signaling has long been known to govern plant–microbe interactions, particularly during abiotic stress (Shekhawat et al. 2022). These plant‐specific properties can be exploited with precision host engineering to enhance beneficial plant–microbe interactions. Plant hormones sometimes serve as components of larger signaling pathways (Y. Bai, Liu, et al. 2024). Gibberellin (GA) signaling, for example, regulates strigolactone production under controlled conditions, a pattern that is relevant to exudate reprogramming in engineered contexts. Kobae et al. (2018) investigated rice strigolactone (SL) biosynthesis mutants (D10, D17, D27) and reported that the genes involved in SL biosynthesis are required to initiate hyphopodium formation and entry of AM fungi into the roots. The SL‐deficient rice plants presented a slow rate of hyphopodium formation coupled with a decrease in intraradical colonization, indicating that the manipulation of host signaling has a causal effect on the recruitment and establishment of beneficial symbionts (Chesneau et al. 2025).

There is a growing concept that plants and microbes need to be engineered together rather than separately. Although some synthetic biology experiments are still at an early or conceptual stage, natural systems support the idea of metabolic codesign (Mavrodi et al. 2021). Natural plant secretions, such as BXs or phenolics, have the potential to select for specific microbial metabolizers, and synthetic strains could be engineered to align with host traits, establishing a stable, beneficial partnership (Harcombe et al. 2014). Such stable, functionally complementary communities designed for specific host conditions, synthetic microbial consortia, have become more promising, as the host filtering mechanisms are now subjected to engineering (Chesneau et al. 2025).

Synthetic Microbial Consortia: Designing Robust, Complementary Microbial Communities for Stable Field Performance

The emergence of synthetic microbial consortia (SynComs) signifies a paradigm shift in the application of synthetic biology toward plant–microbe engineering. SynComs are deliberately constructed assemblages of microbial taxa engineered to replicate or augment the beneficial functions inherent to natural plant microbiomes, including nutrient acquisition, stress resilience, and disease suppression (Table 4) (L. In contrast to single‐strain inoculants, synthetic communities (SynComs) exploit synergistic interactions among community constituents, thereby offering enhanced functional stability and adaptability in response to environmental fluctuations (Shayanthan et al. 2022). Recent advancements in high‐throughput sequencing technologies, multiomics approaches, and computational modeling have facilitated the identification of keystone taxa and the prediction of functional interactions. These developments enable the rational design of microbial consortia tailored to specific crops and agroecosystems (Tariq et al. 2025). Furthermore, ML and artificial intelligence have been increasingly employed to analyze intricate data sets, optimize community composition, and forecast field performance outcomes (L. Han 2024).

A fundamental principle in the design of synthetic microbial communities (SynComs) involves the selection of microbial strains that exhibit complementary and redundant functional capabilities, such as nitrogen fixation, phosphate solubilization, hormone production, and pathogen suppression (Sharma and Bora 2025).

The selection process of synthetic microbial consortia necessitates meticulous evaluation of several critical factors, including the imperative selection of strains that exhibit robust colonization capabilities within plant systems, maintain prevalence throughout various stages of plant development, and possess specific beneficial functions such as nutrient acquisition, disease suppression, and stress tolerance (L. Han 2024). Ensuring compatibility among strains, such that they can coexist without antagonistic interactions, is essential for the stability of the microbial community (Tariq et al. 2025). Functional complementarity, wherein each member of the consortium contributes distinct traits, significantly enhances the overall efficacy of the consortium. Additionally, environmental adaptability, metabolic interactions, and the capacity to form stable associations under field conditions are of paramount importance. The employment of computational modeling, high‐throughput screening, and omics analyses can further refine the selection process by predicting microbial interactions and functional outcomes (Timofeeva et al. 2023).

Empirical evidence from recent investigations has revealed that consortia characterized by moderate phylogenetic relatedness combined with functional diversity surpass those with high phylogenetic relatedness or monofunctional groups in terms of increasing plant growth, root colonization, and resilience under stress conditions (Yusuf et al. 2025). Technological advancements in the encapsulation and precision delivery of SynComs, including seed coatings and biofilm carriers, are being pursued to increase their persistence and efficacy within the rhizosphere (Tariq et al. 2025). Nevertheless, significant challenges persist, particularly concerning the stability and competitive viability of introduced consortia vis‐à‐vis native soil microbiota, as well as the scalability of production processes for commercial application (Sharma and Bora 2025).

In a study conducted by Y. Liu et al. (2025), a Bacillus synthetic consortium was developed with the objective of increasing cucumber growth. The selection of Bacillus strains was predicated on their phylogenetic relatedness as well as their plant growth‐promoting (PGP) characteristics, including the production of indole‐3‐acetic acid and siderophores. The consortia were constructed with varying levels of richness, comprising 1, 2, 4, and 8 strains, derived from an initial pool of 60 isolates, with assessments conducted on both highly related and moderately related groups. The consortia with moderate phylogenetic relatedness presented superior effects on plant growth, root colonization, and metabolic activity. The design of these consortia prioritized compatibility, functional complementarity, and ecological stability, thereby offering a framework for the assembly of robust consortia suitable for agricultural applications.

The establishment of standardized protocols for the assembly, field testing, and monitoring of synthetic communities (SynComs) is imperative to increase reproducibility and facilitate the process of regulatory approval (Shayanthan et al. 2022). The incorporation of ecological principles, such as priority effects, keystone species, and functional redundancy, into the design of SynComs is essential for achieving robust and predictable outcomes across diverse field conditions (Cui et al. 2025). Future investigations should prioritize the customization of SynComs to accommodate local environmental and crop‐specific contexts, exploiting advancements in computational modeling, multiomics techniques, and synthetic biology tools (Dubey et al. 2025). As these technologies advance, SynComs are anticipated to become a fundamental component of sustainable, resilient, and productive agricultural systems.

Another angle that biologists should develop in the future is the emphasis on “SynEco” (synthetic ecology) as a strategic framework for advancing synthetic biology‐driven plant–microbe engineering from controlled laboratory systems to realistic field applications (Coker et al. 2022). This emerging concept integrates the principles of microbial ecology, systems biology, and synthetic biology to design stable and functional microbial consortia that can adapt to dynamic environmental conditions while maintaining desired functions (Ishizawa et al. 2020). Unlike single‐strain approaches, SynEco considers microbial interactions, community resilience, and ecosystem compatibility. Consequently, it improves the predictability and persistence of engineered microbiomes under field conditions (Kong et al. 2018). This framework may serve as a critical bridge between laboratory experimentation and large‐scale agricultural implementation, which will support the development of more robust, sustainable and ecologically compatible bioengineered solutions for crop improvement (Singla 2021).

Integration With Sustainable Agriculture

The integration of synthetic biology‐driven microbiome engineering with sustainable agricultural practices is experiencing rapid progress (Figure 2). This integration offers a promising avenue to mitigate the use of chemical inputs, enhance soil health, and develop resilient agroecosystems (Yusuf et al. 2025). Engineered synthetic communities (SynComs) and plant growth‐promoting microbes (PGPMs) have the potential to improve nutrient cycling (Mažylytė et al. 2024), suppress plant diseases (Emmenegger et al. 2023), and increase plant tolerance to abiotic stresses, such as drought and salinity (Wankhade et al. 2025), thereby diminishing reliance on agrochemicals. As mentioned previously, host–microbe specificity and the instability of engineered traits under diverse field conditions influence the success of these microbiome‐based interventions. Therefore, when crop breeding programs and microbiome engineering are both integrated, they could provide a practical solution that selects crop genotypes with enhanced microbiome recruitment competence, root exudate compatibility, and the ability to support stable beneficial microbial associations under varying environmental conditions. Sustainable agricultural practices, including crop rotation, reduced tillage, organic amendments, and intercropping, help establish favorable conditions for beneficial microbial communities (Ullah et al. 2025). These practices can help make the agro‐environment more stable and reduce competition from microorganisms that are already in the soil. This is challenging because the resident microbiota is a major problem when new microbes are introduced into the field. This process can help new microbes survive and work well in the soil where crops are cultivated. This phenomenon is important for microbes and for the soil because microbes can then persist and carry out their functions in agricultural soils (S.‐W. Han and Yoshikuni 2022).

Integrated pipeline for SynCom design and deployment in agriculture.

Current research underscores the criticality of devising context‐specific interventions, as the efficacy of microbial inoculants is contingent upon local edaphic characteristics, climatic conditions, microbial community dynamics, and crop genotype (Yusuf et al. 2025). This is linked to challenges that always cause discrepancies between laboratory performance and field outcomes. The implementation of data‐driven methodologies, such as genomic profiling and comprehensive environmental evaluations, facilitates the selection and optimization of microbial strains tailored to particular agroecosystems (Gonçalves et al. 2023). Such approaches can also make engineered microbial consortia more compatible with the environment and stronger. This is because they consider how microbes interact with each other and the pressures they face in ecosystems.

Furthermore, participatory research paradigms that involve farmers and stakeholders in the codesign and assessment of sustainable practices are increasingly being adopted. This ensures microbiome‐based technologies. These methods address issues related to farmer reluctance, public skepticism, and the limited adoption of microbiome‐based technologies. These methods ensure that interventions are adapted to local agricultural realities and socioeconomic constraints (Afridi et al. 2024; De la Vega‐Camarillo et al. 2023). Despite these advancements, the translation of engineered microbiomes to field applications remains arduous because of factors such as the limited persistence of introduced strains and the requirement for robust delivery systems (S.‐W. Han and Yoshikuni 2022; Ge and Wang 2025). Research on encapsulation technologies, precision delivery systems, and real‐time monitoring tools designed to improve microbial survival, colonization efficiency, and trait stability under field conditions will be helpful in addressing these challenges (X. Xu et al. 2025). Regulatory frameworks are concurrently evolving to address the biosafety, efficacy, and public acceptance concerns previously identified as major obstacles to commercialization and large‐scale implementation of synthetic biology applications in agriculture (Yusuf et al. 2025; Lau et al. 2022). Ultimately, the integration of engineered microbiomes with sustainable agricultural practices holds significant potential for increasing food security, mitigating environmental impact, and fostering resilient agroecosystems amid climate change pressures (Hanif et al. 2024; Ullah et al. 2025).

Combining Engineered Microbiomes With Crop Breeding, Precision Agriculture, and Low‐Input Farming Practices

A transformative paradigm in the domain of plant–microbe engineering is the integration of synthetic microbiome technologies with advanced crop breeding methodologies, precision agriculture, and low‐input agricultural practices (Cernava 2024; Fagorzi et al. 2023). Modern breeding initiatives are progressively concentrating on the development of crop varieties capable of more efficacious recruitment or interaction with advantageous microbial communities. This is achieved by leveraging cutting‐edge advancements in genomics and gene‐editing technologies, such as CRISPR, to bolster plant–microbe compatibility and resilience (Portal‐Gonzalez et al. 2025; Tan et al. 2022). Precision agriculture technologies, encompassing remote sensing, soil mapping, and variable‐rate application, facilitate the precise delivery of microbial inoculants and real‐time monitoring of plant–microbe interactions, thereby optimizing resource utilization and mitigating environmental impacts (Getahun et al. 2024; Ambaru et al. 2025). Additionally, low‐input farming practices, which include the reduction of fertilizers and pesticides, conservation tillage, and the application of organic amendments, foster environments conducive to the establishment and persistence of beneficial microbiomes (Shittu et al. 2025; Oliveira et al. 2024).

The integration of these methodological approaches necessitates interdisciplinary collaboration alongside the establishment of standardized protocols for inoculation, monitoring, and data dissemination (X. Liang et al. 2025). Recent studies have advocated for a systems‐level perspective, conceptualizing the plant holobiont as a programmable ecosystem wherein plant genetics, microbial communities, and management practices are synergistically optimized for increased sustainability and productivity (Portal‐Gonzalez et al. 2025; X. Liang et al. 2025). Engaging in participatory research and cocreation with agricultural practitioners is critical for devising solutions that are tailored to local environmental conditions, thereby facilitating their practical implementation (Prajapati et al. 2025). As these technologies advance, the amalgamation of engineered microbiomes, cutting‐edge breeding techniques, and precision management strategies is anticipated to yield agricultural systems that are resilient, high yield, and environmentally sustainable (Singh et al. 2024; Thingujam et al. 2025).

Recent studies have elucidated the effective integration of engineered microbiomes with crop breeding, precision agriculture, and low‐input farming methodologies across several major crop species. In particular, maize cultivation in Mexico has achieved advantageous outcomes through precision biofertilization employing a teosinte‐derived synthetic microbial consortium, which was administered via drone technology. This approach has resulted in increased growth, increased yield, and improved soil functionality while concurrently supporting complex beneficial microbial networks (Hernández‐García et al. 2025). The SIMBA project has further demonstrated the efficacy of microbial consortia as biofertilizers in maize, wheat, and tomato under both organic and conventional farming conditions (Cangioli et al. 2024). Furthermore, breeding initiatives for crops such as sugarcane and grapevine incorporate microbiome selection strategies to increase stress tolerance and productivity, thereby underscoring the extensive applicability of these methodologies (Hanif et al. 2024).

Conclusion

In this review, we make bold steps in examining challenges such as the complexity of plant–microbiome interactions, genetic instability of engineered microbes, environmental variability, technical limitations, and regulatory and ethical constraints that mitigate synthetic biology, which offers transformative potential for engineering plant–microbe partnerships to increase crop productivity, resilience, and sustainability. Integrating multiomics analyses, computational modeling, and context‐responsive genetic designs will help overcome these hurdles. However, the development of robust microbial chassis, precise delivery mechanisms, and monitoring strategies should provide future prospects for ensuring that synthetic biology can provide scalable, sustainable, and socially responsible solutions in agricultural systems.
