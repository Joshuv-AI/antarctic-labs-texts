Short-term outcomes of selective laser trabeculoplasty in pseudophakic primary angle-closure glaucoma after iridotomy: a retrospective pilot comparison with primary open-angle glaucoma
Authors: Yun-Chen Hsieh, Wei-Yang Lu, Yi-Ling Chen, Shin-Lin Chiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT) is a widely accepted treatment for primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), but its role in primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) remains less defined. This study aimed to evaluate the short-term outcomes of SLT in pseudophakic PACG patients who had undergone prior laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI), and to compare outcomes with those in POAG patients.MethodsThis retrospective study included pseudophakic patients diagnosed with PACG after LPI or POAG who underwent SLT. Intraocular pressure (IOP) and topical antiglaucoma medication use were assessed at baseline and at 1, 2, and 3 months after SLT. Changes in IOP over time were analyzed, and multivariable linear regression was performed to evaluate factors associated with IOP reduction at 1 month.ResultsA total of 123 patients were included, comprising 89 with POAG and 34 with PACG. The PACG group was older and had a higher proportion of female patients. The mean IOP reduction was numerically comparable between groups at each follow-up visit. At 1 month, a statistically significant reduction in IOP was observed in the POAG group. The PACG group also demonstrated a numerical reduction in IOP, although this did not reach statistical significance. However, the between-group comparison at 1 month should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and multiple comparisons. The number of glaucoma medications remained unchanged during the follow-up period in both groups. In multivariable analysis, baseline IOP was associated with greater IOP reduction at 1 month, whereas SLT energy parameters were not significantly associated with treatment response. No major complications were recorded during the study period.ConclusionsSLT demonstrated a short-term IOP-lowering effect in pseudophakic PACG after LPI, with outcomes numerically similar to those in pseudophakic POAG. These findings should be considered preliminary and warrant confirmation in larger prospective studies with longer follow-up.Keywords: Selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT), Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG), Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), Intraocular pressure (IOP)

Background

Glaucoma remains one of the leading causes of irreversible vision loss worldwide, with its presentation and management varying on the basis of factors such as the type of glaucoma and individual patient characteristics [1]. It can be divided into two main types based on the status of the iridocorneal angle: primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) and primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) [2].

Lowering intraocular pressure(IOP) is the only widely accepted and proven method to prevent glaucoma progression. Treatment options include medications, laser procedures, and surgeries, with selective laser trabeculoplasty(SLT) often considered a safe and effective option for managing POAG [3]. SLT works through a unique mechanism, utilizing a specific wavelength to target only the pigmented epithelium within the trabecular meshwork. This approach preserves the anatomical structure, making the procedure repeatable if necessary [4]. PACG is more prevalent among Asians than among Europeans and Africans, with Asians accounting for more than 80% of all PACG cases [5]. Among individual countries, Japan (1.19%) and China (1.10%) reported the highest prevalence rates of PACG [6]. Laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI) is the first-line treatment for PACG, but long-term medical therapy is often necessary to maintain adequate IOP control. However, its effectiveness may be limited by poor patient compliance [7, 8]. SLT may be beneficial for PACG patients who experience angle widening after LPI but continue to have elevated IOP.

A previous randomized clinical trial demonstrated the efficacy of SLT in PACG patients who had undergone LPI, with a statistically significant 17% reduction in IOP [7]. Additionally, multiple studies have shown that lens extraction is an effective therapeutic strategy for lowering IOP in PACG patients [9, 10].

However, to our knowledge, limited research has been conducted on the effects of SLT in PACG patients. This study aimed to evaluate the short-term outcomes of SLT in PACG patients who have undergone both lens extraction and LPI, and to compare the results of SLT in POAG patients.

Materials and methods

We retrospectively reviewed the medical charts of all glaucoma patients who underwent SLT between January 2015 and May 2023 at Changhua Christian Hospital. This was a retrospective comparative cohort study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Changhua Christian Hospital.

The extracted data included a comprehensive range of parameters, baseline demographics, glaucoma types, and key measurements, such as best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), IOP, and the number of medications, which were collected before SLT and at 1, 2, and 3 months post-treatment. Additionally, specific details of the SLT procedure, including the laser energy and the number of spots applied, were also recorded.

The inclusion criteria required patients to meet the 5th edition of the European Glaucoma Society’s diagnostic criteria for POAG and PACG [11]. All enrolled patients were pseudophakic. Eyes in the PACG group had undergone prior LPI before cataract surgery and later received SLT, whereas eyes in the POAG group underwent SLT only. Most PACG eyes represented chronic narrow-angle disease rather than acute angle-closure. In most cases, cataract surgery was performed with the dual intent of visual rehabilitation and IOP-related management, depending on the individual clinical context. All patients were followed for a minimum of 3 months after SLT treatment. The exclusion criteria included patients who underwent glaucoma surgery within three months after SLT, those with phakic eyes, and individuals with secondary causes of angle closure, such as a subluxed lens, uveitis, trauma, or neovascular glaucoma.

In PACG eyes, SLT was applied only to visible, non-synechial trabecular meshwork on gonioscopy after laser peripheral iridotomy and cataract extraction, and areas with peripheral anterior synechiae(PAS) were avoided. Quantitative grading of PAS extent was not consistently documented in the medical records; therefore, PAS extent could not be analyzed.

The power of SLT was initially set between 0.6 and 0.8 mJ, with adjustments in 0.1 mJ increments or decrements until champagne bubbles were observed, at which point the procedure was completed using that energy level. All patients received a one-week course of topical corticosteroids following laser therapy.

Antiglaucoma medications were adjusted at the discretion of the treating physicians during follow-up. Medication reduction was not routinely attempted during the short observation period, particularly in patients with moderate to severe glaucoma.

The primary efficacy outcome was prespecified as the change in IOP from baseline to 1 month after SLT. Secondary outcomes included IOP change at 2 and 3 months, medication counts, and short-term safety events. An IOP spike was defined as an increase of more than 5 mmHg from baseline at the 1-week follow-up visit after SLT. Statistical analysis was performed via the SPSS software (v.25.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data are presented as percentages, and continuous data are expressed as the means ± standard deviation. Data comparisons between the POAG and PACG groups were performed via an independent t-test, whereas changes within each group before and after SLT were analyzed via paired t-tests and chi-square tests. Associations between SLT parameters and IOP reduction were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. Multivariable linear regression was used to identify factors associated with IOP reduction at 1 month, adjusting for baseline IOP, age, sex, and SLT spot count. Secondary timepoint analyses were considered exploratory and interpreted cautiously given multiple comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Figure 1 presents the changes in the mean IOP following SLT. Overall, the magnitude of IOP reduction was numerically comparable between groups across follow-up visits, with a statistically significant between-group difference observed only at 1 month. During this period, the mean IOP decreased from 19.92 mmHg at baseline to 18.35 mmHg in the POAG group and from 23.42 mmHg to 21.10 mmHg in the PACG group. A statistically significant IOP reduction at 1 month was observed in the POAG group, whereas the PACG group showed a numerically greater reduction that did not reach statistical significance. The between-group difference observed at 1 month should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size of the PACG group and the short follow-up duration. This isolated finding may reflect multiple comparisons and limited power rather than a true biological difference.

In multivariable linear regression analysis of change in IOP at 1 month, baseline IOP was independently associated with greater IOP reduction after SLT (R² = 0.45). After adjustment for baseline IOP, age, and sex, the number of SLT spots was not independently associated with change in IOP at 1 month. An IOP spike, defined as an increase of more than 5 mmHg from baseline, was observed one week following SLT. In the POAG group, 5 patients (5.6%) experienced an IOP spike, whereas in the PACG group, 3 patients (8.8%) were affected. No other major complications were recorded post-SLT within the 3 months follow-up period.

Discussion

The effectiveness of SLT in managing POAG and ocular hypertension (OHT) has been well established through numerous studies. SLT has therapeutic value both as a primary treatment and as an adjunct to medical therapy. Kadasi et al. reported that SLT offers efficacy comparable to topical medications and may be favored due to its advantages in avoiding systemic and local side effects, as well as long-term adherence issues commonly associated with pharmacologic treatment [12].

The effectiveness of SLT in PACG eyes compared with POAG eyes remains less well defined, with limited evidence in PACG. Our findings suggest that SLT may provide a short-term IOP-lowering effect in both POAG and pseudophakic PACG after LPI, with similar outcomes observed across most time points during the 3-month follow-up. This is consistent with prior studies demonstrating comparable IOP reductions between these groups following SLT. Wongwuticomjon et al. reported an IOP reduction of 22.8% in POAG and 30.7% in PACG with peripheral iridotomy (PACG-PI), although the difference was not statistically significant [13]. Similarly, Chang et al. reported that SLT significantly reduced IOP in both POAG and pseudophakic PACG patients receiving maximal medical therapy, with no significant difference in overall efficacy at 6 and 12 months [14]. Sihota et al. reported minimal pathological differences in the trabecular meshwork (TM) within non-synechial regions of chronic PACG eyes compared with POAG eyes [15]. While these histologic findings provide supportive background, they do not constitute a direct mechanistic explanation for our clinical outcomes. In PACG, the response to SLT is strongly influenced by the extent of accessible, non-synechial trabecular meshwork after LPI and cataract extraction. Because quantitative documentation of peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) extent and post-LPI angle status was not consistently available in this retrospective cohort, we could not evaluate how angle openness or PAS burden modified SLT response, which limits the mechanistic interpretation and generalizability of our findings.

Over the 3-month follow-up period, IOP reduction after SLT was observed without a significant change in medication counts. Narayanaswamy et al. reported that PACG patients may require additional medications over longer follow-up to maintain IOP control [7]. Given the short observation period and retrospective design of the present study, longer-term medication needs could not be assessed. Chang et al. also noted that the amount of ocular hypotensive drugs used before and after SLT did not significantly differ, as SLT was employed primarily as an adjunctive therapy rather than as a replacement for medication [14]. Importantly, the lack of medication reduction in our study should not be interpreted as a medication-sparing benefit of SLT. Because this was a retrospective real-world study, medication adjustments were made at the discretion of the treating physicians rather than being protocol-driven. In our cohort, most patients had moderate-to-severe glaucoma, and clinicians generally maintained baseline medications during the short follow-up period even when IOP decreased after SLT to reduce the risk of IOP fluctuation and disease progression. Therefore, the absence of medication reduction likely reflects clinical decision-making in a higher-risk population rather than a lack of therapeutic effect.

Several adverse effects associated with SLT have been reported, including conjunctival redness, ocular discomfort or pain, photophobia, transient IOP elevation, and peripheral anterior synechiae formation. Less frequently, hyphema, anterior uveitis, choroidal effusion, and cystoid macular edema have also been documented [16]. IOP spikes exceeding 5 mmHg have been reported in approximately 2.0% to 25.0% of cases [7, 17–19]. In the present study, no serious complications were observed during the 3-month follow-up period after SLT. However, given the short duration of follow-up, these safety findings should be interpreted as short-term outcomes only. Potential late-onset complications, such as sustained IOP elevation or progressive peripheral anterior synechiae formation, may not have been captured within the current observation period.

The relative effectiveness of 90°, 180°, and 360° SLT treatment remains debated. Chen et al. reported that SLT applied to 90° of the trabecular meshwork achieved a pressure-lowering effect comparable to that of 180° SLT [20]. Similarly, Nagar et al. found no statistically significant difference in outcomes between 180° and 360° SLT [21]. In contrast, Shibata et al. demonstrated that 360° SLT produced a greater reduction in IOP than 180° SLT [22]. In our study, the number of laser spots differed significantly between groups, whereas the energy levels used were comparable. The number of SLT spots reflects the extent of visible angle treated. Although spot count appeared to correlate with short-term IOP reduction in unadjusted analyses, this association was attenuated after adjustment for baseline IOP. This finding suggests that baseline IOP appeared to be a major determinant of absolute IOP reduction after SLT and may partially explain the spot–IOP relationship observed in univariate models. Therefore, spot count alone should not be considered a clinically decisive predictor of SLT response in this cohort. Furthermore, since SLT energy was titrated to a biological endpoint (champagne bubble formation), variability in energy settings was limited in our study population, which may have reduced our ability to detect a meaningful association between energy level and IOP response. The role of treatment extent (spot count) requires further investigation in adequately powered prospective studies.

This study has several important limitations. First, the retrospective and non-randomized design introduces the potential for confounding by indication, despite adjustment for major baseline variables. Second, the relatively small sample size of the PACG group limits statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses and safety outcomes. Third, the short follow-up duration restricts interpretation to short-term IOP-lowering effects and does not allow assessment of the durability of SLT efficacy or late-onset complications. Fourth, detailed quantitative assessment of PAS extent was not consistently available due to the retrospective nature of the study, which limits evaluation of the relationship between angle status and SLT response; however, SLT was performed only in eyes with visible, non-synechial trabecular meshwork. Finally, because medication adjustments were not standardized and most patients had moderate-to-severe glaucoma, medication regimens were often maintained during short-term follow-up, limiting assessment of SLT’s medication-sparing effect and confounding interpretation of its standalone efficacy. These limitations underscore the preliminary nature of the findings and highlight the need for prospective studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, and standardized medication adjustment protocols.

Conclusion

In conclusion, SLT demonstrated a short-term IOP-lowering effect in pseudophakic PACG patients after LPI, with outcomes numerically similar to those observed in POAG. In multivariable analysis, baseline IOP was the main factor associated with the magnitude of IOP reduction, while the association between spot count and change in IOP was not independent after adjustment. These findings should be interpreted as preliminary and limited to short-term follow-up, and warrant confirmation in larger prospective studies with longer follow-up.
