A novel anterior occipitocervical fixation instrument improves stability and minimizes bone-implant stress: a comparative finite element analysis
Authors: Jian Xiong, Hang Xiao, Panjie Xu, Jianying Zheng, Xuanhang Zhang, Yu Wang, Wei Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose Posterior fixation is commonly the preferred surgical method for occipitocervical instability. In certain cases, posterior surgery no longer holds an advantage, and it is more advisable to choose the anterior approach. Furthermore, some internal fixation failures are stress-related. Therefore, we designed three types of anterior fixation instruments and evaluated them via finite element analysis, aiming to identify a design that enhances stability while minimizing stress at the bone–implant interface.Methods We performed a finite element analysis using a validated C0-C2 model with simulated instability. Three distinct instruments were virtually implanted: Occipital Condylar Plate (OCP); Atlanto-Occipital Transarticular Plate (AOP); and Atlantoaxial-Occipital Plate (AAOP). We applied a 40-N axial load and 1.5-Nm moments to simulate flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation. Segmental range of motion (ROM) and von Mises stress at the implant and bone were analyzed.Results All three instruments increased stability. However, the OCP design was markedly superior, reducing flexion ROM by 50–61.54% compared to the AOP and AAOP, respectively. Most critically, the OCP demonstrated the lowest von Mises stress at the bone across all motion states. Its stress distribution was also the most uniform, in stark contrast to the high stress concentrations observed with the other designs.Conclusions Among the three instruments, the OCP provides the most robust stability and, crucially, the most favorable stress environment, which may reduce the clinical risk of stress-related internal fixation failure.Keywords: Occipitocervical fixation, Occipitocervical instability, Biomechanical, Internal fixation failure, Finite element analysis

Introduction

Instability of the occipitocervical region can lead to spinal cord compression and even pose a direct threat to life, making early surgical intervention essential. Posterior surgery has been the preferred approach for managing occipitocervical instability due to its safety, technical feasibility, and favorable clinical outcomes [1, 2]. In certain cases, the posterior approach is no longer advantageous—such as when there is severe destruction of anatomical structures, extensive fibrotic tissue from prior surgeries, pathological lesions located anteriorly or anterolaterally, or congenital or acquired posterior structural defects [2–6].

To date, numerous researchers, including our team, have demonstrated the feasibility of anterior occipitocervical fixation [3, 4, 6–9]. Dvorak et al. and Wu et al. have successfully applied this technique in clinical practice, achieving favorable postoperative stability with satisfactory long-term follow-up outcomes [3, 8]. These findings suggest that anterior fixation may serve as a viable alternative approach.

Internal fixation failure remains a significant challenge for orthopedic surgeons and can result from multiple factors [10–12]. Insufficient load-bearing capacity and uneven stress distribution are among the contributing factors. Our team proposes optimizing the stress distribution between bone and implant to reduce the risk of fixation failure. Finite element analysis is a widely used scientific method in spinal biomechanics research [7]. Through computer simulation, it allows for the analysis of spinal biomechanics under various physiological and pathological conditions. In this study, we designed three anterior occipitocervical fixation instruments and used finite element analysis to identify a design that enhances stability while minimizing stress at the bone–implant interface.

Materials and methods

Finite element model of the upper cervical spine

We recruited a healthy male volunteer of 36 years old who had no history of cervical spondylosis or surgery. High-resolution CT imaging of the upper cervical spine was performed using a Siemens 64-slice spiral CT scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Germany). The acquired DICOM data were processed using specialized software to construct a preliminary three-dimensional geometric model (Fig. 1-a), which was then imported into ANSYS (Workbench 2021) for finite element meshing and ligamentous tissue reconstruction.

Three-dimensional geometric model and intact finite element model of the upper cervical spine. (a) Three-dimensional geometric model of the upper cervical spine (constructed from CT data); (b) Intact finite element model of the upper cervical spine

Material properties were assigned according to established biomechanical parameters: cortical bone was modeled with a Youngs modulus of 12,000 MPa (Poisson ratio = 0.3), while cancellous bone was assigned a Youngs modulus of 450 MPa (Poisson ratio = 0.3). Articular cartilage surfaces were reconstructed with a Youngs modulus of 0.5 MPa (Poisson ratio = 0.3) and configured with frictionless sliding contact (coefficient of friction = 0.1). The transverse ligament, composed of low-elasticity fibrous tissue, was specifically modeled using tetrahedral elements (Young modulus = 20 MPa, Poisson ratio = 0.3). The cross-section areas were used to calculate the effective spring stiffness for each ligament. The data were taken from previous finite-element studies [13]. Complete material property specifications are provided in Table 1. The final intact upper cervical spine finite element model (FEM) comprised 130,611 elements and 208,505 nodes (Fig. 1-b). To simulate occipitocervical instability, the transverse ligament, atlanto-occipital membranes, and atlanto-occipital and atlantoaxial joint capsules were resected from the intact FEM to generate the unstable condition. The anterior and posterior atlanto-occipital membranes as well as the atlanto-occipital joint capsules were resected to disrupt the stability of the C0-C1 segment, and the transverse ligament and atlantoaxial joint capsules were resected to disrupt the stability of the C1-C2 segment [1].

Design of the three anterior fixation instruments

In the preliminary study [4, 6, 14], we performed radiographic evaluation of the anatomy of the upper cervical spine. Based on the anatomical characteristics of the upper cervical spine, our team developed an anterior Occipital Condylar Plate (OCP) and have been granted an invention patent (CN216167777U). The OCP is an integrated three-segment plate: the upper occipital-condyle portion, 40 mm wide and 10 mm high, closely fits the condyle; the horizontal atlas segment bifurcates from 40 mm to 35 mm (10 mm high) and continues posteriorly into the axis segment, where an inverted-T-shaped inclined upper zone tapers from 35 mm to 30 mm (5 mm high) and a lower horizontal zone narrows from 30 mm to 20 mm (10 mm high); the entire atlas-to-axis segment is angled at 20°–30°. Building on previous work, we designed two additional anterior fixation devices: the Atlanto-Occipital Transarticular Plate (AOP) and the Atlantoaxial-Occipital Plate (AAOP). The AOP was derived by removing the occipital-condyle fixation component from the OCP, whereas the AAOP consists of two components: occiput-to-axis screws and axis screws (Fig. 2). Screw trajectories and entry points were drawn from the literature: OCP screws follow Xu et al. (2020), AOP screws are based on our imaging study (2019), and AAOP screws adopt the values reported by Dvorak et al. (2003) [3, 9, 15]. All three devices were fabricated from titanium alloy (Ti-6Al-4 V). Based on existing reports, the plate thickness was set at 1.6 mm and the screw diameter at 3.5 mm [7, 13]. Geometries were reconstructed in Mimics 21.0 (Materialise, Belgium) and integrated into the unstable FEM through ANSYS.

Unstable upper cervical spine models implanted with three anterior occipitocervical fixation instruments. (a) Occipital Condylar Plate (OCP): The first set of screws was inserted with 84.1° posterior angulation in the sagittal plane and 26.7° lateral angulation in the coronal plane (to achieve bicortical purchase in the posterolateral occipital condyle), and the second set of screws was inserted with 15° lateral angulation; (b) Atlanto-Occipital Transarticular Plate (AOP): The first set of screws was inserted with 41.7° posterior angulation in the sagittal plane and 11.6° lateral angulation in the coronal plane, and this set of screws passes through the atlanto-occipital joint; (c) Atlantoaxial-Occipital Plate (AAOP): The top set of screws was inserted with 25° posterior angulation in the sagittal plane and 15° lateral angulation in the coronal plane, passing through the atlantoaxial joint and atlanto-occipital joint. All screws not specified in the above descriptions were inserted perpendicular to the vertebral body

Boundary and loading conditions

The inferior surface of the C2 vertebral body was completely fixed in all six degrees of freedom. A 40 N vertical follower load was applied to the occipital condyles to simulate physiological head weight, followed by pure 1.5 N·m moments applied to the occiput in flexion–extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation within the non-destructive range. C0–C2 range of motion (ROM) and peak von Mises stresses in bone and implant were recorded throughout.

Results

Validation of intact and unstable models

To validate the model, we calculated the ROM of both the C0–C1 and C1–C2 segments in the intact configuration and compared them with published in vitro data of Panjabi et al. and finite-element data of Cai et al. [7, 16]. Relative to the intact model, the unstable model showed significant increases in segmental ROM: at C0–C1, increases of 96.88% in flexion, 29.90% in extension, 12.70% in lateral bending, and 2.63% in axial rotation; at C1–C2, increases of 28.57%, 35.96%, 41.03%, and 42.66% in the same directions, respectively (Table 2).

ROM of the three models at C0-C2

Compared with the unstable model, all three fixation instruments significantly reduced ROM, demonstrating favorable immediate postoperative stability. The ROM of the OCP in flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation was 0.15°, 0.9°, 0.37°, and 0.4°, respectively. When compared to the AOP, the OCP reduced ROM by 50%, 30.23%, 22.92%, and 21.57% in flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation, respectively. Compared with the AAOP, the OCP reduced ROM by 61.54% (flexion) and 15.89% (extension), but increased ROM by 12.12% (lateral bending) and 11.11% (axial rotation) (Fig. 3-a).

The maximum von mises stress at the instrument and bone

Regarding both the maximum von Mises stress (MVMs) at the implant and bone, the OCP demonstrated lower stress values. For implant stress, compared to the AOP, the OCP reduced MVMs in flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation by 54.17%, 11.40%, 11.60%, and 20.44%, respectively; compared to the AAOP, reductions were by 53.97%, 6.15%, 4.90%, and 13.44%. For bone stress, compared to the AOP, the OCP decreased MVMs by 58.94%, 27.46%, 6.25%, and 19.95% in these motions, while compared to the AAOP, reductions reached by 77.46%, 63.44%, 46.95%, and 54.04% (Table 3; Fig. 3-b, c).

Discussion

Compared with the intact model, the ROM of the three fixation instruments in four movements was significantly reduced, which indicates that the postoperative stability is satisfactory. In flexion, the ROM of OCP decreased by 50% and 61.54% compared with that of AOP and AAOP, respectively. In extension, the ROM of all instruments was higher than that in other movements, and only the ROM of OCP remained below 1°. Since all three instruments achieve occipital condyle anchoring, this provides a good fixation effect for the occipital-C1 segment—which mainly contributes to the flexion and extension of the occipitocervical region. In lateral bending and axial rotation, the ROM of the three instruments were relatively small, which is due to the fact that they all provide excellent fixation for the atlantoaxial joint: OCP and AOP achieve fixation through upper and lower screws of the atlantoaxial joint and plates that fit the atlantoaxial joint; while AAOP achieves fixation by passing screws through the atlantoaxial joint [1].

In most spinal finite element studies, internal fixation stress is an important observation index, but few researchers have paid attention to bone stress. From the authors’ perspective, after the introduction of implant, it is necessary to simultaneously analyze the stress of the implant and bone, which could enable a more comprehensive analysis of the fixation effect. In this experiment, we analyzed both implant stress and bone stress, and presented the results in the form of stress distribution nephograms. In the implant stress nephogram, the stresses of the OCP and AOP are concentrated on the plates at the joints, while the stress of the AAOP is distributed on the occipito-atlantoaxial screws (Fig. 4). The screw-plate junctions are common sites for screw pull-out or fracture, and excessive stress concentration is the most important factor for this issue; all three fixation instruments have avoided it [16]. In the bone stress nephogram, the bone stress under the three instruments is relatively low, and the OCP results in the lowest stress. Additionally, it can be observed that the bone stress distribution of the unstable model loaded with OCP is uniform, while that of the AOP and AAOP is concentrated at the atlantoaxial joint (Fig. 5). The OCP features an anatomically more conforming plate design for the upper cervical spine, and possesses excellent stress-bearing and stress-dispersing capacities. This result is consistent with the view proposed by McCullen: increasing the direct bone-implant contact area can reduce the risk of implant weakening and failure [17].

Stress analysis is of great significance for internal-fixation surgery. Screw loosening, displacement, pull-out, and even fracture, together with implant displacement and subsidence, are all associated with excessive or uneven stress distribution [18]. Goel et al. demonstrated that non-uniform stress distribution induces persistent instability of internal fixation, thereby elevating the risk of non-union; the probability of fixation failure rises markedly, and radiographic surveillance frequently reveals screw loosening, pull-out, or implant migration even in the absence of clinical symptoms [10]. Christian et al. reported that in osteoporotic patients insufficient bone–implant interaction markedly increases the risk of internal-fixation failure [19]. Moreover, stress influences bone remodelling and implant longevity. Cheong et al. stated that, because of differences in rigidity and strength, initial stress is concentrated mainly on the implant. After bone remodeling is achieved the bone carries a greater share of the load, reducing implant stress by approximately 20% and significantly alleviating implant fatigue, thus extending service life [20]. Therefore, in the initial post-operative period the construct must possess adequate stress-bearing capacity to maintain anatomical stability until bone remodeling occurs. This perspective aligns with Goel et al.‘s clinical experience with 30 cervical-spine revision cases.

Anterior occipitocervical fixation surgery is a highly challenging procedure. First, regarding the selection of surgical approaches, the retropharyngeal approach and the transoral approach are available options [21, 22]. Given that the transoral approach has been reported to have relatively high complications and mortality rates, the anterolateral retropharyngeal approach is recommended in this study [23]. This approach can reduce tissue damage, lower the risk of infection, minimize the possibility of intracranial tissue injury, and at the same time provide adequate exposure of the anterior structures of the upper cervical spine. Second, damage to the hypoglossal nerve by occipital condyle screws is possible; although the risk of such injury is low based on the anatomical relationship between the hypoglossal canals and the occipital condyles described in our previous studies, the possibility of anatomical variations in the hypoglossal canals should still be taken into account [3, 6, 9]. Additionally, although there are differences in the performance of the three devices, the discrepancies are actually not significant. This reminds surgeons that when surgical outcomes are comparable, they should prioritize fixation devices that minimize surgical trauma, reduce operative difficulty, and shorten the duration of surgery. Finally, it should be recognized that there are relatively few reports on anterior plate-screw system fixation at present, and the available experience for reference is also insufficient. The optimized fixation instruments designed in this study still requires further improvements before proceeding with the next in vitro study.

Our study has several limitations that require improvement. First, the current FEM was based on a healthy young male specimen; future studies should incorporate pathological conditions (e.g., osteoporosis, post-traumatic deformities) to evaluate plate performance across diverse patient populations. Second, due to inherent limitations of the finite element method, we were unable to assess the influence of bone grafting and fusion on stability in actual surgical scenarios. Third, our finite element analysis employed simplified elastic and homogeneous material properties to simulate vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and ligaments, which cannot fully capture the nonlinear biomechanical behavior of human tissues. Finally, the investigation of implant–bone contact frictional behavior and fatigue or cyclic loading was not implemented in this study, which renders the long-term efficacy of the three devices unclear. In future research, relevant improvements will be made to conduct a more comprehensive evaluation of the instruments’ performance.

Conclusion

Among the three anterior occipitocervical fixation instruments, the OCP afforded the greatest stability and, most importantly, the most favorable stress environment. This biomechanically optimized load transfer unique to the OCP may reduce the clinical risk of stress-related internal fixation failure and offer a promising strategy for improving outcomes in this challenging surgical field.
