Unveiling the Nurses' Intentionality in the Transpersonal Caring Relationship: A Technical Action Research
Authors: Fatma Dursun Ergezen, Emine Kol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntentionality is a transformative antecedent of the transpersonal caring relationship (TCR). This study aimed to unveil the nurses' intentionality in the TCR after the Mindful Caring Education Program.MethodsThis was a technical action research. The study was conducted in three phases: planning, implementation, and evaluation. The researchers developed a four‐session education program (EP) based on the Theory of Unitary Caring Science. The clinical observations, individual interviews, and vignettes were used for evaluation. The study was completed with nine female nurses. The data were analysed using content analysis.FindingsIn the study, 4 themes, 11 categories and 8 sub‐categories were obtained. Nurses described intentionality as holistic and purposeful actions. Interviews and observations have shown that authentic presence, a helping‐trusting caring relationship, and a positive working environment facilitate intentionality. The caring relationship boundaries, heavy working conditions, fatigue, and the dominance of relatives hinder intentionality. Nurses stated significant personal and professional growth after the EP.ConclusionThe study identified various factors that either facilitated or hindered nurses' intentionality in the TCR. By participating in the mindful caring EP, nurses were able to cultivate a deeper sense of self‐awareness in both their personal lives and professional relationships.Keywords: action research, caring, human caring theory, intentionality, mindfulness, nurse

Introduction

Intentionality is defined as the property of being about or directed toward a subject, as inherent in conscious states, beliefs, or creations of the mind [1]. In nursing, intentionality is understood as a dynamic and evolving precursor to the transpersonal caring relationship (TCR), providing clarity and purpose to the nurse's actions, expectations, and beliefs, and serving as a meaningful source of insight in the healing process [2, 3]. In TCR, intentionality develops through a continuous feedback process shaped by the nurse's ongoing interaction with the patient [4]. Its formation and expression are influenced by multiple factors, including the nurse's cultural and ethnic background, personal beliefs, life experiences, reflective capacity, and level of self‐awareness. Additionally, mentorship and the broader work environment serve as important contextual elements that shape and sustain intentionality in nursing practice [3, 4]. The literature indicates that nurses must cultivate intentionality to achieve heightened awareness within the TCR. To support this development, it is recommended that nurses enhance their self‐care practices, seek guidance from a mentor, and incorporate mindfulness into their daily lives [3, 5, 6].

Mindfulness is defined as moment‐to‐moment awareness cultivated by intentionally paying attention to the present without judgement [7]. Its primary purpose is to loosen rigid attachments to beliefs, thoughts, and emotions, thereby fostering greater emotional balance, a sense of well‐being, and inner calm [8, 9]. Mindfulness also involves taking greater responsibility for one's life choices, approaching experiences with openness and curiosity, engaging all five senses, and responding thoughtfully rather than reacting automatically [8, 10]. By cultivating mindfulness, nurses may develop a deeper understanding of the needs of their patients and respond to them with greater compassion [11]. Taking a moment to focus on their breath before entering a patient's room or performing a procedure may help nurses cultivate a sense of well‐being and equanimity [12]. By pausing and fully attending to one's breath before reacting, nurses can enhance their caring comportment and decision‐making abilities, and become more focused on delivering procedures with authentic caring. This state of consciousness allows the nurse to become the healing environment itself [11, 12].

Mindfulness‐based interventions have been shown to enhance self‐care, emotion regulation, empathy satisfaction, and responsiveness to others among nurses; however, no research has examined the relationship between mindfulness and intentionality within the context of TCR [13, 14, 15]. Developing intentionality may lead to greater self‐awareness, purpose, and satisfaction in one's work [3]. In this context, a 4‐week education program (EP) was developed and implemented in this study. It is aimed to unveil the nurses' intentionality in the TCR after the Mindful Caring Education Program (MindCare‐EP). The research questions were as follows:

Methods

Design

This study utilises a qualitative technical action design that tests a specific intervention within a predetermined conceptual framework to improve nursing practice more effectively [16, 17]. In this design, the researcher serves as a professional expert who plans, conducts, and provides guidance using analytical and reflective techniques [16, 17].

Theoretical Framework

The Theory of Unitary Caring Science has been chosen as the theoretical framework for this study. Watson's theory focuses on human caring in a wide range of human experiences and guides the practice of intellectual, physical, spiritual, and emotional caring for oneself and others in daily life [11]. The caring relationship serves as a core concept of the theory. The caring relationship consists of caring moments and connections that embody the nurse's consciousness, intentionality, and unique authenticity [11]. The nurse engages with the other's mind–body–spirit within a shared moment of presence, which she refers to as the ‘caring moment’ [18]. The nurse brings their authentic self to the interaction and strives to create a space of trust, safety, and comfort for the person [19]. The relationship between the nurse and the person transcends illness and physical appearance. The nurse strives to recognise the soul‐filled being of the other. This connection allows the nurse to move beyond the confines of the ego, opening the heart to authentic presence. The healing energy radiated through this openness generates a healing environment [20].

In the theory, intentionality is intrinsically connected to consciousness and awareness directed toward a mental object. Within the context of the caring relationship, intentionality functions as a guiding force that gives the nurse purpose, direction, and motivation [2]. Watson emphasises the nurse's conscious creation of a caring‐healing field. The nurse helps the person to access their inner healing resources, including their own capacity for self‐care and self‐awareness. By being fully present and attentive, the nurse creates a transformative environment that supports the person's growth, development, and healing [19]. The Caritas Processes provide a theoretical guide for fostering TCR. The practice of Caritas Processes encourages nurses to prioritise self‐care and develop their consciousness to find greater fulfilment in both their personal and professional lives [21]. However, applying these processes in the TCR requires evolved knowledge, skills, and a depth of awareness [19]. Mindfulness can aid in developing this personal awareness, supporting growth and development in Caritas practice by fostering understanding, kindness, wisdom, and sensitivity [11]. Sample and Participants

The study was conducted in the Akdeniz University hospital located in the Mediterranean region of Türkiye. For action research to be successful, it is essential that the individuals or communities involved recognise and accept the need for change. If people do not perceive a need for change in their own lives, meaningful transformation may not occur [22]. For this reason, the researcher used purposive sampling to select participants who could provide rich and detailed data [23]. Accordingly, two clinics in which the researchers had previously conducted projects and established familiarity with the nursing teams were selected. Training nurses within the same clinic as a group was considered essential for strengthening the culture of care and fostering a shared professional language. The inclusion criteria for the study were: (a) at least 1 year working experience in the clinic, and (b) willingness to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were: participants who wished to withdraw at any point during the study, or who did not complete the EP. Initially, 14 nurses were invited to participate in the study. In the selected clinics, there were 12 female and 2 male nurses. Of these, 10 agreed to participate in the study, while four declined, including both male nurses. One nurse was unable to complete the study due to health issues. Accordingly, the study is limited to the results of the nurses who agreed to participate and completed the EP. Data Collection Tools

Data triangulation is a crucial method to enhance multiple sources of information from different perspectives [17]. For this purpose, five data collection tools were used in the study: ‘Individual information form’, ‘Semi‐structured interview form (Supporting Information S1)’, ‘Question form for vignettes (Supporting Information S2)’, ‘Evaluation form of education sessions’ and ‘Observation notes’.

Supporting Information S1 presents the semi‐structured interview guide used to obtain in‐depth qualitative data on participants' experiences and perceptions. Supporting Information S2 includes the vignette‐based question form designed to assess participants' applied judgements and decision‐making within standardised scenarios. The data collection tools were finalised after their development and subsequent review by experts.

Data Collection

Action research progresses in a spiral rather than following traditional, linear research designs. In its basic form, it involves a cyclical process that includes identifying and planning (pre‐action), implementing the action (action phase), and evaluating the outcomes (post‐action) to systematically improve practice. In line with the nature of action research, the present study was structured into three phases: planning, implementation, and evaluation. Figure 2 summarises the study process.

The study process.

Planning

Firstly, the study problem was identified and participants were selected. To support this process, observations were conducted over a 6‐month period to gain insight into nurse–patient interactions. Secondly, data collection methods and data collection tools were determined.

Implementation

Secondly, the 4‐week EP was developed, piloted, and implemented. Supporting Information S3 presents the detailed content and structure of EP, which was implemented to address the themes of consciousness, mindfulness, authentic caring, nurses' self‐care, and peer caring. Before the EP began, booklets containing the ‘Individual Information Form’, ‘Question Form for vignettes’, and practice forms were created. Additionally, a WhatsApp group called ‘Mindfulness’ was established to organise education days and times, as well as to communicate with each participant. Next, a face‐to‐face pilot study was conducted with six nurses. It was decided to continue the EP using an online platform. The pilot study was conducted from 29 December 2021 to 7 January 2022. The researchers recorded meditation practices that would be used in post‐education practice and shared them with the participants. Education sessions were scheduled twice a week, based on the nurses' shifts. Reminders were sent via WhatsApp the day before each session.

The education sessions incorporated Watson's 10 Caritas Processes and the caring–healing modalities recommended within the theory. The education sessions began with non‐verbal and natural music, followed by a review of the previous week's practices. The nurses were asked about their intentions for that day's session using the Mentimeter application. Each session ended with a meditation practice. Then, the practices and forms for the following week were shared. The EP was implemented from 21 January to 13 February 2022, with each session lasting approximately 2.5–3 h.

Evaluation

Intentionality is an abstract term that cannot be readily observed and quantified. It can only be analysed by one's discourse and actions [24, 25]. Accordingly, nurses' intentionality after the education was explored through clinical observations, individual interviews, and vignettes. The participatory observation method was used in an unstructured format to conduct the observation process. The observation process was carried out in a single shift for each nurse, lasting 8 h. At least 16 h of observation were conducted for each nurse. The researchers conducted 216 h of observation between 16 February and 17 March 2022. The entire observation process was conducted by a single researcher. The observation data started to reach saturation from the second observation period.

After completing the observation of each nurse, individual interviews were conducted in a designated room that was familiar to the participants. The room was set up with a table and chairs. The interviews were conducted according to the semi‐structured interview form. Both verbal and nonverbal responses were noted during the interview. To ensure consistency, the same researcher conducted all interviews. The interviews lasted between 1 h and 5 min to 1 h and 30 min. After completing the interview, vignettes were given to participants.

Data Analysis

The researchers used the qualitative content analysis method of Graneheim and Lundman to analyse the data [26]. This method is useful for analysing individual and group experiences, reflections, and attitudes. This analysis focuses on the subject and context, dealing with explicit or hidden content in the text. The content analysis consisted of five stages: familiarisation with the text, identification of meaning units, condensation and abstraction, coding and categorization, and theme development. At the initial stage, all interviews, field notes, and responses to vignettes were transcribed verbatim. All data were read several times to gain a comprehensive understanding of the text as a whole. During this phase, the analyses were conducted independently by two researchers. Subsequently, meaning units—words, sentences, or paragraphs—were identified. The text was then condensed while preserving its core meaning. During the abstraction phase, codes, categories, and themes were developed at different levels. Codes were used to label the meaning units, which were then compared for similarities and differences. Content sharing common characteristics was grouped first into subcategories, and then into broader categories, in response to the guiding question, ‘What?’ The preliminary categories were discussed and re‐evaluated by both researchers. Relationships among the categories were then explored to form themes, addressing the question, ‘How?’ Finally, the underlying meaning—that is, the latent content—beneath the categories was identified and formulated as a theme. The analysis process was carried out through a continuous back‐and‐forth interaction between the whole and the parts of the text.

Ethical Considerations

This study adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and received approval from the Clinical Study Ethics Committee of the Akdeniz University (Date: 9 October 2020, No: 668). Written and verbal consent was obtained from the nurses after explaining the study's purpose, and their anonymity was ensured by coding their quotes without using their names. During the observation period, the researcher joined nurses during handovers, accompanied them into patient rooms, and observed interactions throughout eight‐hour shifts, which introduced several situations requiring careful management. At the beginning of the process, nurses had difficulty behaving naturally due to the researcher's presence; however, as the long‐term observations continued, these reservations gradually diminished and interactions became more natural. When patients noticed the researcher in the room, brief pauses in communication occasionally occurred, necessitating a delicate balance between maintaining the natural flow of care and protecting privacy. Given that many nurse–patient interactions involved emotional and sensitive content, the researcher positioned herself in the background and took care not to interfere with the process. Details that needed to be remembered were noted immediately and later reorganised in a quiet and secure environment to ensure confidentiality. All field notes were stored in a locked cabinet accessible only to the researcher. At times, patients appeared emotionally vulnerable during care, creating a noticeable tension between the researcher's professional identity as a nurse and her role as a non‐intervening observer. Although the researcher felt a natural inclination to comfort the patient, she refrained from intervening to avoid influencing the interaction or disrupting the authenticity of the observation. After the observation was completed, however, the researcher engaged in a brief conversation with the patient at an appropriate time and offered emotional support, thereby upholding both professional boundaries and ethical principles while contributing to the patient's well‐being.

Rigour and Trustworthiness

Lincoln and Guba's criteria for rigour and trustworthiness, including credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [27]. The primary researcher (FDE) was solely responsible for conducting all observations and interviews during the research process. Recognising that such close involvement could potentially introduce subjectivity, several strategies were employed to enhance the study's reliability and credibility. To ensure credibility, the study employed long‐term participation, continuous observation, and data triangulation. For transferability, the contexts and events were explained in detail. To critically reflect on assumptions, detailed field notes were maintained throughout the process, documenting nurses' actions verbatim and without interpretation. The purposive sampling method was used to reveal the events, facts, and their varying features. Nursing experts were consulted during data collection tools and data analysis to ensure dependability. The entire transcript was provided without comment. Researchers independently read the transcript in detail and compared meaningful passages from context‐related words, sentences, and paragraphs. Finally, for confirmability, the study reached a consensus by comparing and discussing the codes, sub‐categories, and categories. The participant verification was utilised to minimise personal bias and ensure analytical rigour.

Findings

The findings are organised into interconnected themes that reflect intentional caring as an evolving and relational process. The themes and categories are interpreted in close connection with one another, highlighting how nurses' intentionality takes shape within the TCR. Within this framework, four themes encompassing 11 categories and 8 sub‐categories were identified.

Theme 1. Purposeful Actions

This theme highlights how nurses' intentionality emerges as a dynamic and meaning‐oriented process within the TCR. Purposeful actions are shaped through nurses' ongoing interpretation of patients' explicit and implicit calls for help. The findings indicate that intentionality is not a static attribute but develops through nurses' conscious engagement with situational cues and their orientation toward both immediate clinical needs and deeper human concerns. In this context, purposeful actions represent the initial manifestation of intentionality, grounded in nurses' recognition of directing situations and their ability to define a focal point for care.

Category 1. The Directing Situations

Nurses stated that each caring relationship established with a patient is unique and shaped by various situations. Participants reported that they perceived a call for help through patients' verbal and nonverbal communication and behaviours. This perception was particularly strong when caring for patients who were lonely, helpless, in critical clinical condition, or had high care needs. The observation process showed that nurses established closer communication with these patients, became more voluntarily involved in the caring process, and devoted more time to addressing additional problems.

The nurse entered the patient's room. While caring for one patient, she noticed that the other was suffering. The nurse observed the distressed person and took her hand. ‘How are you? Do you feel pain?’ she asked the patient. (Field note, Participant 3)

When I encounter a patient who is lonely, it affects me deeply and leads me to interact with them on a more emotional level. This, in turn, prompts me to ask many questions about their care needs. (Interview, Participant 1)

The moment I see a distressed patient my brain automatically switches to their needs. I am very attentive and careful not to miss anything or make any mistakes in their care. (Interview, Participant 9)

Category 2. Focal Point

Nurses stated that there was a distinct intentional purpose in the caring relationship they established with each patient. First, they defined responding to each patient's basic human needs and contributing to the healing process as the primary purpose of the TCR. In addition, some nurses reported a desire to establish a mutual and meaningful relationship with patients. In this regard, nurses emphasised that they oriented toward higher‐level purposes, including supporting patients' active participation in the care process, developing a trust‐based relationship that enables patients to express themselves more comfortably, and making a positive difference in patients' lives.

I believe that the primary goal is to address the patient's needs and alleviate any obstacles that may impede their well‐being. (Interview, Participant 4)

I feel the patient is tired. “Pity her heart, too,” I thought when she experienced persistent tachycardia. Long‐term tachycardia can tire the heart out, but short‐term tachycardia may have little effect. Her lungs get tired, and she can't breathe. She's out of air. I want this process to end. I want her to heal soon. (Interview, Participant 3)

I felt that it was important to gain the patient's trust and help her understand that she was not alone. I wanted her to have faith in us and know that we could work together to overcome her challenges. (Vignette, Participant 9)

Theme 2. The Facilitators of Intentionality

While Theme 1 demonstrates how intentionality becomes evident in purposeful actions, Theme 2 reveals the conditions that sustain and enhance intentional and compassionate care. This theme shows that intentionality is facilitated through an interplay between the nurse's inner availability, the reciprocal nature of the nurse–patient relationship, and the relational climate of the work environment. Rather than functioning independently, these facilitators collectively shape nurses' capacity to remain present, responsive, and meaning‐oriented in the caring encounter. Accordingly, the nurse's authentic presence, the helping–trusting–caring relationship, and a positive work environment emerge as interconnected dimensions that enable purposeful actions to evolve into deeper, more coherent caring practices.

Category 1. Nurses' Authentic Presence

This category reflects how nurses' authentic presence facilitates intentionality within the TCR. Nurses' authentic presence reflects a form of inner readiness that allows intentionality to unfold within the TCR. Empathy, professional experience, and mood collectively contribute to nurses' ability to notice subtle cues, remain emotionally attuned, and orient their actions toward higher‐level caring purposes. These findings suggest that intentionality is closely tied to nurses' psychological and experiential availability, which enables them to move beyond task‐focused care toward relationally grounded and consciously guided actions.

Sub‐Category 1. Empathy

Most nurses reported that empathy helped them understand patients, feel concern, and become more aware during care.

When I empathized with the patient, I wasn't sure how to feel. It was a mix of emotions, but mostly I felt bad for them. (Vignette, Participant 7)

When you wake up in the morning, you feel so uncomfortable… your mouth is dry, your eyes are crusted over. As soon as you get up, you want to wash your face. You feel distressed. I put myself in the patient's place. In that moment, I felt just as uncomfortable…. (Interview, Participant 3)

Sub‐Category 2. Professional Experience

Most nurses noted that professional experience helped them focus more on patients; while novice nurses were more concerned with technical tasks, experienced nurses reported a more holistic, person‐centred approach.

When I was a novice nurse, I used to worry about not administering treatments on time, and this made me work in a task‐oriented way. However, as I gained more knowledge and experience, I realized the importance of being patient‐centered. Now, my working style has changed, and I focus more on the needs of the patient. With increased knowledge and experience, my self‐confidence has also improved. (Interview, Participant 5)

Sub‐Category 3. Good Mood

Some nurses stated that working in a positive mood enhanced their awareness of patients, led to more positive interactions, and increased their desire to help.

I find that when I am in a positive mood, I can engage with my patients more positively. It makes me feel happier and more fulfilled at work, and I am motivated to do more. (Interview, Participant 6)

When my mood is good—when all my chakras feel open—I experience this surge of energy. If I'm going to enter three patient rooms that day, I find myself walking up and down the corridor, checking in: Do they need anything? Is there something I can do? If there's nothing specific, I go in and chat. I release that energy through the patient. (Interview, Participant 7)

Category 2. The Helping–Trusting–Caring Relationship

Most nurses stated that one of the key factors guiding the caring relationship and caring behaviours is the helping–trusting–caring relationship. Participants emphasised that this relationship is a reciprocal and interaction‐based process. In caring relationships characterised by patients' awareness of and respect for the nurse's professional role, cues relevant to the caring process were more readily perceived. Furthermore, in such relationships, the focus of caring behaviours was emphasised to shift from technical procedures toward higher‐level, meaning‐centred purposes.

I establish a distinct relationship with patients when they recognize me. When they come back to the clinic, I feel relieved and can greet them, talk with them, and experience a sense of worth. (Interview, Participant 8)

A trusting relationship with a patient leads to them being more open and transparent with us. (Interview, Participant 7)

Category 3. Positive Working Environment

The interviews and observations have demonstrated that positive relationships and effective communication between peers and team members play a crucial role in fostering nurses' intentionality.

When I work with a peer who is a loved one, I find that I spend more time with the patient and can communicate with them more effectively. (Interview, Participant 8)

When a physician trusts me, I feel more confident in caring for the patient and can gain a holistic understanding of their needs. (Interview, Participant 2)

Theme 3. Impediments to Intentionality

This theme highlights the factors that disrupt the continuity and depth of intentional and compassionate care. The findings indicate that impediments to intentionality arise not only from structural working conditions but also from relational constraints within the caring encounter. When nurses are unable to establish reciprocal engagement with patients or when external demands dominate the care context, intentionality becomes narrowed and care practices shift toward more superficial and technical forms. Taken together, these barriers demonstrate that intentionality is a fragile and context‐dependent process that requires relational openness and supportive conditions to be sustained.

Category 1. Boundaries in the Caring Relationship

Nurses emphasised that the caring relationship is inherently a process characterised by reciprocity and interaction. When patients do not engage in the caring relationship, nurses' intentionality is constrained from orienting toward the subtle and deeper dimensions of care. In this context, patients' failure to open their phenomenal field to the nurse and the continuation of the relationship through negative communication patterns were identified as the main factors preventing the caring relationship from reaching a depth of meaning.

I've encountered situations where patients keep their distance, even when we communicate verbally. For example, the other day, a patient refused to let me enter their personal space in any way. Despite my attempts to establish a connection, they set clear boundaries. Ultimately, I respected their wishes and kept my distance. (Interview, Participant 3)

When a patient is rude, I find it challenging to maintain a deep level of caring. I still attend to their needs and administer treatments, but it's difficult to connect with them emotionally. In these situations, I tend to provide more superficial care, keeping my interactions with the patient polite but brief. While I still aim to provide quality care, I don't feel as motivated to go above and beyond for a patient who is being rude or disrespectful. (Interview, Participant 8)

Category 2. Heavy Working Conditions

Nurse interviews and observational notes indicate that situations limiting intentionality largely stem from working conditions. High nurse‐to‐patient ratios limit the time nurses can spend with patients, thereby hindering the deepening of the caring relationship. In addition, it was observed that non‐nursing responsibilities within clinical settings weaken intentionality. Furthermore, delays in responding to patients' needs due to physicians' limited or intermittent presence on the unit negatively affect the sustainability of trust within the caring relationship.

Currently, I'm responsible for caring for 12 to 18 patients. However, I believe that if I had fewer patients, I could provide more focused attention and care to each patient. This would not only benefit the patients but also allow me to feel more fulfilled in my role as a nurse. With my current workload, I find that I have less time for both the patients and myself. (Interview, Participant 6)

During emergencies, my role description changes. I am no longer just a nurse, but I also work as a staff member. (Interview, Participant 4)

When a patient needs immediate intervention and I am unable to provide it, they often ask me what I am doing, which can make me feel helpless and unable to respond. This can lead to a breakdown in trust between the patient and myself. (Interview, Participant 1)

Category 3. Fatigue

Nurses described physical and mental fatigue as a condition that directly shaped how they engaged in the caring relationship. When they felt fatigued, they reported thinking more slowly and having difficulty maintaining focus, which made it harder to notice and respond to patients' needs and calls for care. Fatigue was also described as disrupting nurses' motivation to remain engaged in the relationship, gradually reducing their sense of readiness and willingness to be fully present with patients.

When I'm feeling tired, I tend to have less energy and find it difficult to communicate. (Interview, Participant 5)

Category 4. The Dominance of Relatives in the Caring Relationship

Interviews and observations indicated that the strong presence and frequent involvement of family caregivers altered the nurse–patient relational space. Nurses described that this involvement often limited their opportunities to engage directly with patients, resulting in caring interactions that remained mostly task‐focused and technical, rather than developing into a more meaningful and relational form of care.

When I have 22 relatives to communicate with for 22 patients, it becomes challenging to provide proper care. It becomes difficult for me to communicate with the patient when there are too many relatives involved. (Interview, Participant 7)

Theme 4. The Impact of MindCare‐EP on Nurses

This theme synthesises the findings by demonstrating how MindCare‐EP facilitated a transformative reorientation of nurses' intentionality at both personal and professional levels. Beyond enhancing skills, the education influenced how nurses understood themselves and their caring relationships. Increased self‐awareness and self‐compassion supported a more deliberate and conscious approach to care, while reflective engagement with caring experiences strengthened the alignment between nurses' intentions and their actions. Overall, MindCare‐EP functioned as an integrative process that enhanced nurses' capacity to sustain intentional and compassionate care across diverse and challenging clinical contexts.

Category 1. Personal Growth

Nurses reported that following the educational process, they integrated mindfulness practices into their daily lives and that this experience strengthened their perceptions of self‐compassion, self‐care, and self‐awareness. In this context, the category included two subcategories.

Sub‐Category 1. Enhanced Self‐Compassion and Self‐Care

Most nurses noted that the EP helped them value themselves more, make time for self‐care, and act with greater kindness.

When I went through the education program, I was able to recognize my worth and gain a better understanding of who I am as a person. (Interview, Participant 8)

We never questioned ourselves about what made us happy and what didn't. I've never questioned myself, “What made me happy in the last month?” I asked this question because of the training. I realized I was oblivious to my happy moments. (Interview, Participant 7)

Sub‐Category 2. Enhanced Self‐Awareness

Most nurses reported heightened self‐awareness and greater self‐reflection after the EP.

As for me, the education program had a big impact on my personal growth by helping me become more aware of my actions. I realized that even when I wasn't fully conscious of what I was doing I was still doing it. This program also encouraged me to question how much I knew and how conscious I was. (Interview, Participant 3)

Category 2. Professional Transformation

Nurses stated that sharing experiences during the caring sessions following the educational process affected them deeply, prompting greater reflective inquiry into the caring relationship and initiating a process of rethinking and reinterpreting the purpose of care. This category comprised three subcategories.

Sub‐Category 1. Making Sense of the Caring Moment

Most nurses reported that the EP helped them understand their emotions during care, which made them feel happier.

After completing the program, I started to name my experiences. I think that everything I feel or do should have a name, as it's hard to describe a feeling without one. (Interview, Participant 7)

Sub‐Category 2. Enhanced Intentionality

Some nurses reported that the EP prompted them to reflect more on their intentions and perceptions in the caring relationship and contributed to the development of their intentionality.

Since completing the educational program, I've noticed an increased awareness when I enter a patient's room. I find myself questioning my intentions—am I here for the patient or myself? (Interview, Participant 7)

Before EP: Solve their oral ulcerations and breathing problem.

After EP: I would first try to talk to him briefly and determine the best way to approach him. I try to understand the importance of a familiar person of him. I would bring him down and call his family once his vital signs had stabilized. (Vignette, Participant 1)

Sub‐Category 3. Listening to Someone Else's Story Authentically

Most nurses noted increased patience, calmer interactions, and deeper listening toward patients and colleagues after the EP.

I feel like I have a deeper understanding of other people now. Sometimes, I even sense that I have an inner source of motivation. (Interview, Participant 2)

Discussion

In this study, nurses conceptualised intentionality as purposeful actions within the TCR. Situations such as patients' calls for help, loneliness, helplessness, and urgent or high‐level care needs directed their attention toward the patient. They aimed to meet patients' physical, emotional, and spiritual needs, establish a helping–trusting–caring relationship, support healing, and leave a meaningful impact. Overall, nurses' statements and observational data showed that intentionality guided their care‐focused behaviours. Similar to our study, previous studies have revealed that nurses have taken into account patients' requests and closely observed nonverbal cues such as their physical appearance, facial expressions, moans, demeanour, and body language [28]. Also, nurses have expressed goals of meeting patients' needs, actively listening, establishing a trusting relationship, and promoting healing [29, 30]. In this regard, the findings of the study align with the existing literature on intentionality. The findings particularly support the views of Zahourek [31] and Watson [2], who argue that intentionality guides nurses' actions, expectations, beliefs, and will. Beyond these two scholars, the findings also resonate with Katie Eriksson's Caritative Caring Theory. Although Eriksson does not explicitly define intentionality, she asserts that the nurse engages in a conscious and intentional choice within the caring relationship—one grounded in the intention to do good and to uphold the dignity and sacredness of the human being [32, 33].

The study has revealed that the nurse's authentic presence, the establishment of a trusting relationship with the patient, and a positive work environment facilitated the nurses' intentionality. Previous studies have reported a relationship between intentionality and authentic presence which provides deeper focus and strength in every interaction [6, 34]. When nurses develop authentic presence, they experience a deeper connection with their thoughts, emotions, and experiences, as well as those of others. This heightened awareness helps them identify their true intentions and goals and approach them with greater clarity and purpose [3, 18]. Furthermore, it has been reported that a favourable working environment has promoted favourable interactions between nurses and patients, effective collaboration, and personal motivation [30, 35]. Within a positive work environment, nurses can confidently fulfil their roles, engage in mindful care practices, and remain attuned to their presence and actions in providing care [36]. The study demonstrated that, when supported by factors that facilitate intentionality, nurses enacted compassionate care. Observational data revealed increased use of intentional smiling, therapeutic touch, active listening, and efforts to provide comfort. Within the context of compassionate care, the nurse approaches the patient with a deliberate motivation to offer help and to mitigate suffering [37, 38]. The nurse engages in verbal, nonverbal, or physical actions aimed at eliminating, reducing, or alleviating the patient's distress [39].

This study has found that certain factors such as the boundaries between nurses and patients, challenging working conditions, fatigue, and family member dominance can hinder the development of intentionality. These factors led to a more task‐oriented approach to care, decreased motivation, and impaired cognitive processes and concentration. Studies have indicated that barriers can negatively impact the nurse–patient relationship and create challenges such as relational difficulties, systemic issues, and insufficient communication skills in TCR [40, 41]. It is thought that working in unfavourable conditions can negatively impact nurses, leading to lower levels of engagement, motivation, and increased stress, ultimately affecting their ability to deliver patient‐centred and compassionate care. Studies have indicated that nurses experience frustration, lower motivation, poor job satisfaction, forced emotional labor, and less time for caring when they encounter such barriers [42, 43]. A study demonstrated that when nurses encounter such obstacles, they prefer to create an emotional distance from patients [44]. It is recommended that healthcare organisations plan interventions to improve working conditions, such as promoting work‐life balance, fostering a culture of teamwork and respect, and recognising and valuing nurses' contributions to the healthcare team.

This study has indicated that MindCare‐EP is effective in developing nurses' personal and professional perspectives. Nurses reported improvements in their self‐compassion, self‐care, and self‐awareness following the program. Previous studies have reported that mindfulness‐based EPs are effective in increasing nurses' self‐compassion, compassion satisfaction, awareness, self‐acceptance, authentic presence, and authentic listening [45, 46, 47]. Consistent with these findings, other studies have shown that caring education helps nurses recognise previously unnoticed aspects of themselves and gain deeper insight [47, 48]. The study also showed that nurses' intentionality toward individuals and caring processes improved. After the education, they reported seeing different aspects of the person, questioning their purpose in the caring relationship, and engaging in deeper reflection. Consistent with the Theory of Unitary Caring Science, caring education has been shown to enhance nurses' intentionality and strengthen their confidence in sustaining caring practices [49, 50]. Similarly, previous studies have shown that such education leads to changes in nurses' perspectives toward individuals, greater reflection on the person, improved ability to focus on them, enhanced compassionate care skills, better communication, and increased sensitivity to patients' experiences [47, 48, 51]. In summary, EPs that help nurses increase their awareness of themselves, their caring practices, and their work environment can have meaningful and positive effects, as shown in this study. However, because this pilot study included only nine female nurses from two clinics, the findings need to be tested with larger and more diverse samples, including different genders and care settings. As Zahourek [25] highlights, intentionality is shaped by factors such as culture, religion, life experiences, and meaningful relationships. Therefore, evaluating this EP in varied contexts is essential to better understand its broader impact.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has some limitations. Firstly, the findings of this study are limited to nurses who participated in the study. Secondly, this study involved nine nurses, all female, from a single public hospital in Türkiye. As participation in action research inherently involves individuals who are willing to engage in change, the inclusion of such participants may have resulted in a relatively homogeneous and context‐specific sample. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings to other genders, institutions, and cultural contexts is limited. Moreover, as the culture of caring varies across clinics, institutions, and countries, the findings of this study cannot be generalised beyond the specific context in which the research was conducted. Additionally, the study has some strengths. The study addressed intentionality, a little‐known concept in nursing. Obtaining expert opinions at every stage of the research may be considered a strength. The use of multiple data collection methods is another strength.

Recommendations for Further Research

In line with the nature of action research, the aim of this study was not statistical generalisation but rather analytic generalisation, seeking to generate in‐depth insights. Within this framework, the study aimed to enhance participants' intentionality through mindfulness. Therefore, a single cycle of action research was conducted. The findings revealed both the fostering and inhibiting factors underlying nurses' actions. These results may serve as a model for researchers working in different gender, institutional, and cultural contexts. In this regard, future studies using the MindCare‐EP approach may focus on developing nurse leaders. At this stage, since awareness among nurses has already been fostered, a participatory action research design could be employed. Through participatory action research, nurses could plan interventions aimed at strengthening TCR and positioning healing at the centre of intentional practice, with long‐term outcomes systematically evaluated.

Implications for Policy and Practice

This study recommends that healthcare policies and practices prioritise creating a supportive work environment that fosters nurse–patient relationships. Additionally, programs such as MindCare‐EP can be disseminated to increase nurses' awareness. Interventions that enhance nurses' self‐awareness, self‐compassion, and self‐care can be planned.

Conclusion

In this study, it was determined that nurses perceived intentionality as a concept that directs the patient and enables determining the goal in the TCR. There are many factors that facilitate and hinder the intentionality of nurses. The results of the observation and interviews showed that MindCare‐EP is effective in developing the nurse's perspective.
