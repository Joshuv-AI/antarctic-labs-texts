Bypass of LANA-DNA crosslinks by Polη ensures KSHV genome maintenance and tumor growth
Authors: Jayaraju Dheekollu, Coltin J. Albitz, Anjali Pathania, Frederick Keeney, Samantha S. Soldan, Paul M. Lieberman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYEpisome maintenance of Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV) requires LANA protein binding to the viral terminal repeat (TR) DNA. Here, we show that LANA forms cell cycle-dependent DNA-protein crosslinks (DPCs) within the TR. Targeted shRNA screening identified translesional repair DNA polymerase Polη as necessary for the stability of KSHV TR DNA and viral episome maintenance. We found that Polη binds LANA and colocalizes with LANA nuclear bodies. Inhibiting the enzymatic activity of Polη by small molecule PNR-07–02 provided selective cell killing of KSHV+ B cells and inhibited tumor progression in mice xenografts. Mechanistically, Polη inhibition results in both single and double-strand breaks at the KSHV TR due to unresolved LANA-DNA crosslinks and replication fork collapse. These findings reveal a new mechanism of viral episome maintenance by DPC coupled to translesion DNA synthesis (TLS) DNA polymerases and identify Polη as a potential target for inhibition of KSHV latent infection in proliferating tumor cells.

INTRODUCTION

Human tumor viruses are estimated to account for up to 15%–20% of all cancer cases worldwide.1 Viral oncogenic mechanisms include subversion of host cell DNA damage and cell control, resistance to apoptosis, alterations in DNA repair mechanisms, and evasion mechanisms of the antiviral immune response.2 In most cases, viral DNA persists within a subset of the tumor cells providing continuous expression of viral oncogenes and potential for alteration in host chromatin control through direct DNA integration or tethering of viral episomes to host chromosomes. The mechanisms of viral episome persistence in tumor cells vary for each of the different tumor viruses.3 For viruses with circular genomes, such as the gamma herpesviruses, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV), and human papillomavirus (HPV), viral genomes persist predominantly as extrachromosomal circular DNA, referred to as episomes, and these viruses encode proteins with dedicated functions in maintaining the viral episomes during latent and persistent long-term infections, especially in proliferating cells.3

KSHV, also designated as human herpesvirus 8 (HHV8), is the biologic agent of Kaposi’s sarcoma, primary effusion lymphoma (PEL), and multicentric Castleman’s disease.4–9 KSHV has a double stranded DNA (dsDNA) genome of 165–170 kb consisting of a long unique coding region, which is ~140 kb in length and is flanked with 30–40 copies of a highly GC-rich 801 bp long terminal repeat (TR) sequence.10–12 Like other herpesviruses, KSHV has two distinct life cycles, the productive lytic phase required for host-to-host infection, and the latent phase, where viral DNA is transmitted to daughter cells by mitotic transmission. During latency, only a limited number of viral proteins are expressed, including the latency-associated nuclear antigen (LANA) encoded by open reading frame 73 (ORF73).13–17 LANA is expressed in all forms of KSHV tumors and is essential for viral episome persistence and oncogenesis.18

KSHV LANA is a large multifunctional nuclear protein of ~1,162 amino acids and 220–230 kda mass due to phosphorylation and other post-translational modifications.14,19 The LANA C-terminal domain (aa 900–1,162) confers its DNA binding function and shares structural homology with the EBV EBNA1 and HPV E2 DNA binding domains. The LANA N-terminal domain contains a chromatin-binding domain (CBD) that is required for the efficient tethering of the viral episome to host metaphase chromosomes and maintenance of stable copy number in mitotically dividing cells.13,19 LANA has also been shown to regulate host oncogenesis and gene expression through interactions with cellular proteins,20 as well as through its ability to bind to cellular gene regulatory elements.21–24 LANA can also suppress viral lytic gene transcription to further stabilize and maintain latency.25,26 Thus, LANA is a multifunctional protein that plays a central role in the persistence and pathogenesis of KSHV.

KSHV TR contains three LANA-biding sites (LBSs): a higher affinity site (LBS1), a lower affinity site (LBS2), followed by an adjacent 32 bp long GC-rich replication element, which contains the third binding site (LBS3).27 While two LANA binding sites may constitute a minimal origin of DNA replication,28,29 at least 8 TR elements are required for stable episome maintenance.13,30 X-ray crystal structures of LANA DNA binding domain (DBD) reveal an obligate dimer with structural similarity to EBNA1 and E2.27,31,32 LANA can also form higher order structures, including a decameric ring that utilizes a dimer-dimer interface important for cooperative DNA binding and DNA replication activity.31 Structures of LANA bound to DNA reveal an arrangement of three LANA binding sites at the minimal origin and the ability to form long helical oligomeric filaments that are thought to assemble along the 30–40 TRs.27 It remains unclear how LANA oligomerization on the TR contributes to episome maintenance and how DNA replication machinery overcomes the LANA TR high-order structures to maintain stable TRs and viral episome copy numbers.

The structural similarities between KSHV LANA and EBV EBNA1 suggest that they share some fundamental mechanisms of episome maintenance.3 Our previous studies have found that EBNA1 can form cell cycle-dependent crosslinks with oriP DNA.33 This interaction was dependent on Y518 in the EBNA1 DBD and contributed to the resolution of episome dimers during the completion of DNA synthesis. However, protein-DNA adducts are known to present major obstacles to the replicative DNA polymerases, and typically require additional translesional repair DNA polymerases to repair or bypass such structures. Other difficult-to-replicate genomic regions such as hairpins, G-quadruplexes (G4s), repetitive DNA sequence, transcription-replication conflict sites, DNA-protein crosslinks (DPCs), common fragile sites (CFSs), telomeres, pericentromeres, and centromeres, also require specialized DNA synthesis mechanisms to prevent instability of these complex regions.34 The role of specialized DNA polymerases and accessory factors in the regulation of DPCs is not completely understood, nor is their potential role in viral episome maintenance.

In this study, we show that translesion DNA synthesis (TLS) is an essential component of the KSHV episome maintenance mechanism. We show that KSHV LANA forms cell cycle-dependent DPCs at TR, and the amino acids Y1014 and Y1066 are required for DPC formation. Depletion of Polη significantly reduces KSHV episome copies, and Polη forms a stable complex with LANA in vitro and with LANA-nuclear bodies (LANA-NBs) in living cells. A small molecule inhibitor of Polη enzymatic activity selectively blocks KSHV+ PEL cell viability and tumor growth in mouse xenograft models. Polη inhibition causes replication fork collapse and DNA-strand breaks at KSHV TR in the absence of exogenous replication stress. These findings identify Polη as an essential cellular co-factor required to bypass LANA-DNA crosslinks and maintain KSHV episomal DNA during latent oncogenic infection. These findings also demonstrate that Polη and perhaps other translesional repair polymerases provide novel therapeutic opportunity to treat KSHV-driven cancers.

RESULTS

LANA forms DPCs at the KSHV TR

The LANA DBD shares structural homology with the DBDs of the episome maintenance proteins of EBV EBNA1 and HPV E2 (Figure 1A). Previous studies revealed that EBNA1 can form cell cycle-dependent DPCs with EBV oriP DNA and that this depended on Y518.33 LANA and E2 share similar DNA recognition helices with tyrosines that make close contact with DNA phosphate backbone (Figure 1A). We, therefore, tested whether LANA could also form cell cycle-dependent DPCs. We first used the rapid approach to DNA adduct recovery (RADAR) assay, which isolates highly stable DNA-protein complexes that are resistant to detergent (SDS) and chaotropic (guanidinium isothiocyanate) agents.35 KSHV+ BCLB1 cells were fractionated by centrifugal elutriation for different stages of cell cycle (Figure 1B). The cell cycle fractions were then subject to RADAR assay for recovery of DPCs and assayed by western blot for LANA, PCNA, or topoisomerase II (TopoII) (Figure 1C). RADAR assay revealed that LANA-containing DPCs are highly enriched in fractions 29 and 30, corresponding to late S and G2 phases of the cell cycle. As controls for RADAR specificity, PCNA was not detected in any fraction, while TopoII was detected in the same fractions as LANA. Nearly identical results were obtained when RADAR assay was applied to another KSHV+ B cell line BC-1 (Figure S1). These findings suggest that LANA forms cell cycle-dependent DPCs in latently infected B cell lines where KSHV is maintained as a stable episome.

LANA Y1066 shares structural similarity with EBNA1 Y518, which was found to be essential for EBNA1 DPC and episome maintenance function. However, LANA also has a second tyrosine, Y1014, that makes close contact with DNA phosphate backbone and has previously been shown to be important for DNA binding and replication functions27,31 (Figure 1D). Single and double point mutations of either Y1014 or Y1066 to either alanine or phenylalanine resulted in significant loss of DNA binding and DNA replication functions (Figure S2). Alanine mutation of Y1014 showed the most pronounced defect in DNA binding by both electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) (Figures S2B and S2C) and ChIP (Figure S2D), and it reduced DNA replication by 2.3-fold (Figures S2E and S2F). Double mutations of Y1014 and Y1066 to phenylalanine (Y1014F/Y1066F) had only minor effects on DNA binding by EMSA and ChIP, but it most severely affected DNA replication (~3.8-fold reduction) (Figures S2E and S2F). We, therefore, tested whether double mutation Y1014F/Y1066F had any effect on DPC formation. Using a RADAR-IP assay to enrich for LANA DPCs in asynchronous cells, we found LANA wild type (WT) but not LANA Y1014F/Y1066F among the recovered DPCs (Figure 1E). To assess whether Y1014F/Y1066F could bind DNA, we performed ChIP assays in transfected cells. We found that LANA Y1014F/Y1066F bound TR DNA better than LANA WT when assayed by standard ChIP conditions with formaldehyde cross-linking (Figure 1C). In contrast, when ChIP conditions were conducted in the absence of formaldehyde as an exogenous crosslinking agent (native ChIP), only WT, but not Y1014F/Y1066F bound to the TR (Figures 1D and 1F). Thus, while Y1014F/Y1066F is capable of binding TR DNA, it is severely defective in forming DPCs in living cells.

LANA Y1014F/1066F compromise episome maintenance function

We next tested whether phenylalanine mutations in Y1014 and/or Y1066 had any effect on the episome maintenance functions of LANA (Figure 2). For EBNA1, mutation of Y516F had no effect on transient DNA replication, but it was required for stable episome maintenance.33 As noted earlier, single mutations in Y1014 and Y1066 had significant effects on DNA binding and transient DNA replication activity. Y1014F/Y1066F had little effect on DNA binding by EMSA or ChIP but was most reduced for transient DNA replication of an 8xTR plasmid (Figure S2). We, therefore, tested the effects of single and combined mutations of Y1014 and Y1066 on the longer-term episome maintenance function of LANA with the p8xTR episome, which requires both the DNA replication as well as segregation and DNA integrity functions. Episome maintenance assay revealed that single and combined mutations of Y014F and Y1066F reduced episome maintenance ~10- to 20-fold by day 15 post-transfection (Figures 2A–2D). We also generated a KSHV bacmid genome with mutation in LANA Y1014F/Y1066F and assayed colony formation (Figures 2E and 2F). We found that KSHV genomes with Y1014F/Y1066F formed ~3-fold fewer colonies relative to LANA WT (Figures 2E and 2F). These findings indicate that Y1014 and Y1066 contribute to both DNA replication and the episome maintenance activity of LANA.

KSHV episome maintenance required host specialized DNA polymerases

The KSHV TR DNA is highly GC-rich and likely bound by LANA DPCs that could obstruct DNA polymerase replication. We, therefore, tested whether known DNA polymerase accessory factors, especially those important for translesional repair synthesis were required for KSHV episome maintenance. We used lentivirus delivered shRNA targeting Pol N, Pol K, Pol i, Rev 1, Rev 3L, Pol Q, or Polη (also called Pol H) in two different KSHV PEL cell lines, BCBL1 (Figures S3A–S3E) and BC1 (Figures S3F–S3H). The efficiency of knock down was assessed by RT-qPCR for each gene and a > 4-fold reduction for all gene targets was observed, with the exception for Rev3L (Figures S3A and S3F). KSHV episome maintenance was assessed by pulse-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) followed by Southern blot detection (Figures S3B, S3C, S3G, and S3H). We found that KSHV episomes were reduced significantly for shPolN (~2.2-fold in BCBL1 and ~3.2-fold in BC1) and shPolη (~3-fold in BCBL1 and ~5.7-fold in BC1) (Figures S3C and S3H). These findings indicate that the translesional repair polymerases PolN and Polη are important for the stable maintenance of KSHV episomes.

Polη depletion causes loss of episomal KSHV genomes from latently infected PEL cells

Polη is known to have an essential role in translesional repair at replication forks stalled at DPCs and at difficult to replicate DNA sequences, such as GC-rich repetitive DNA.36 To determine whether Polη plays a role in KSHV episomal maintenance in multiple different PEL cells, we assayed the effect of shPolη depletion on KSHV genomes in latently infected PEL cells BCBL1, JSC1, BC1, and BC3 (Figure 3). PEL cells were infected with lentivirus expressing either Polη shRNAs or control shRNA. Western blot analysis showed Polη protein was efficiently depleted (>85%) with no detectable loss of β-actin protein expression (Figure 3A). To monitor KSHV episomes, we assayed KSHV+ cells by PFGE, followed by Southern blotting (Figure 3B). Quantification of several (n = 4) independent PFGE experiments revealed that shPolη depletion led to an ~4.5-fold loss of KSHV circular episomes relative to cellular DNA for each of the KSHV+ cell lines (Figure 3C).

Polη co-immunoprecipitates and colocalizes with LANA nuclear bodies

To investigate the mechanism of Polη action at the KSHV TR, we tested whether Polη may associate with LANA by co-immunoprecipitation (coIP) (Figure 4A). We found that Polη efficiently coIPs with LANA but not isotype control IgG in both BCBL1 and BC1 cells (Figure 4A). Reciprocally, LANA efficiently coIPs with Polη but not with control IgG (Figure 4B). No coIP was observed in KSHV-negative cell line BJAB indicating that the Polη IP requires LANA (Figure S4A). We next performed ChIP assays to determine whether the interaction of Polη and LANA occurs at the TR DNA (Figures 4C and 4D). We assayed different stages of the cell cycle using centrifugal elutriation in both BCBL1 and BC1 cells. We found that LANA bound the TR in asynchronous cells (fraction 16), and was highly enriched in late S (fraction 29) and G2/M (fraction 36) (Figure 4C). LANA did not bind to a KSHV control region near the ORF70 gene encoding viral thymidylate kinase (KSHV 20kb) (Figure 4D). Polη bound to the KSHV TR in late S and G2/M phases, tracking the elevated enrichment of LANA. Polη did not bind to the control KSHV 20kb region in BCLB1 but showed some background binding in BC1 cells. LANA is known to form LANA-NBs that colocalize with KSHV episomes.37 Confocal immunofluorescence imaging revealed that Polη strongly colocalizes with LANA-NBs in BCBL1 and BC1 cells (Figures 4E–4G). Pearson’s correlation coefficients for the colocalization analysis shows colocalization of LANA and Polη with medians of R p values of 0.52 (interquartile range [IQR]: −0.27–0.87) in BC1 and 0.65 (IQR: −0.24–0.92) in BCBL1 (Figure 4G). The percentage of cells with LANA and Polη colocalized foci were ~28% for BC1 (n = 100) and ~49% for BCBL1 (n = 100), likely reflecting variation in colocalization across cell cycle stages (Figure 4H). Higher resolution stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy imaging further confirmed the colocalization of Polη with LANA-NBs (Figures S4B–S4D). These findings strongly indicate that Polη interacts with LANA and colocalizes with LANA at TR DNA and LANA-NBs.

Small molecule Inhibitors of Polη selectively kills KSHV+ PEL cells

The direct binding of Polη to LANA and KSHV TR suggests that it may be essential for KSHV genome maintenance during latency, and may potentially be a drug target for KSHV-associated cancers. A small molecule inhibitor for Polη has been developed and is commercially available.38 PNR-07–02 (PNR) has ~10-fold selectivity for Polη relative to other DNA polymerases, and it prevents drug tolerance to chemotherapeutic agents that cross-link DNA, such as cisplatin. We assayed the effects of PNR on the viability and apoptosis of KSHV+ and KSHV− B cell lymphoma cell lines using flow cytometry and annexin V/7-AAD apoptosis assay (Figures 5 and S5). We found that PNR selectively inhibited growth and induced apoptosis in KSHV+ B cell lymphoma cell lines at 5–10 μM but not in KSHV− cell lines BJAB or DG75, EBV+ BL lines Mutu I or Raji, nor EBV transformed lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) (Figures 5A, 5B, and S5A–S5E). Although PNR has not been optimized for in vivo studies, we found that it reduces tumor growth of KSHV+ BC 3, relative to KSHV− BJAB in mouse xenograft models for pleural effusion ascites (Figures 5C and 5D, and S5F–S5H). These studies suggest that inhibitors of Polη provide selective inhibition of KSHV+ B cell lymphoma cell lines.

PNR treatment causes breaks in KSHV TR DNA, loss of episomes, and DNA damage-associated apoptosis

To better understand the mechanism of PNR-selective killing of KSHV+ B cell lymphoma cell lines, we assayed the stability and integrity of the KSHV episomes (Figures 6A–6E, and S6A–S6E). PFGE followed by Southern blot analysis revealed that PNR treatment led to a complete degradation of KSHV episomes within 6 h post treatment. At 2 and 4 h, discrete breaks were detected in KSHV episomes in BCLB1 cells (Figures 6A–6C). Similar patterns of KSHV episome degradation were observed in JSC1 cells, which were co-infected with EBV (Figures S6A and S6B). Break-seq assays for both single- and double-strand breaks revealed PNR-selective DNA break formation within the KSHV TR (Figures 6D, 6E, S6D, and S6E). PNR-dependent breaks were found near LANA binding sites (Figure 6E). Some DNA breaks were also observed at the ORF73 (LANA) internal CAG repeats, but these were not dependent on PNR (Figures 6D and S6D). ChIP-qPCR for LANA (Figure 6F, top) or Polη (Figure 6F, bottom) showed strong enrichment after treatment with PNR after 1 or 2 h relative to DMSO and IgG controls, suggesting that PNR treatment was trapping both LANA and Polη to the TR (Figure 6F). RADAR assays further confirmed that LANA crosslinks with DNA were highly enriched after treatment with PNR (Figure 6G).

Given the effects of PNR treatment on KSHV genome integrity and cell cycle progression, we next tested whether PNR treatment induced a DNA damage response. Western blot revealed that PNR induced p53 levels and S15 phosphorylation, along with activation of pChk2 and γH2AX in BCBL1 (Figure 6H) and JSC1 cell lines (Figure S6C). PNR treatment also induced PARP1 and caspase 3 cleavage as early as 4 h post treatment, consistent with the activation of apoptosis pathways. KSHV lytic antigen ORF45 was not induced by PNR treatment (Figures 6H and S6C). The induction of cleaved forms of PARP1, caspase 3, markers of apoptosis, and γH2AX, marker for double-strand breaks were most prominent in KSHV+ BCBL1 and JSC1, compared to EBV+ LCL, MutuI, Raji, or EBV− /KSHV− BJAB or DG75 (Figure S6F). These findings demonstrate the PNR treatment leads to the selective degradation of KSHV episomal DNA by inducing DNA breaks in the TR and activating the p53-Chk1-Chk2-caspase 3 apoptosis pathway.

To determine whether LANA DPC formation was required for the recruitment of Polη and other DNA replication factors, we performed ChIP assays in cells expressing either LANA WT or LANA Y1014F/1066F (Figure 7A). We found that LANA Y1014F/Y1066F bound TR as well or better than LANA WT. However, other Polη, as well as replication fork protection factor Timeless (Tim) and replicative helicase subunit MCM3, were significantly reduced for binding to the TR with LANA Y1014F/1066F compared to LANA WT. In contrast, ORC2, which bound weakly, was not significantly affected by the LANA mutant. These finding suggest that DPC formation, which is disrupted by Y1014F/Y1066F, is necessary for the enrichment of Polη and other factors regulating replication fork progression (Tim and MCM3) at the TR.

DISCUSSION

The mechanisms enabling the episome maintenance of the KSHV genome during latent oncogenic infection provide new opportunity for therapeutic intervention, as well as insight into the more general mechanism of genome stability in cancer. Here, we show that KSHV LANA forms cell cycle-dependent DPCs with the episome maintenance element in the viral TR. We found that human cellular translesional repair DNA polymerases, especially DNA Polη, are essential for the integrity of the KSHV genome during latent infection in PEL cells. DNA Polη co-immunoprecipitated with LANA protein, colocalized with LANA-NBs in living cells, and bound to the TR in a cell cycle-dependent manner. A selective small molecule inhibitor of Polη, PNR-07–02, reduced KSHV+ tumor cell growth in cell culture and mouse xenograft models. Mechanistically, PNR treatment led to the accumulation of strand breaks at the LANA binding sites in the KSHV TR, followed by the degradation and loss of the KSHV episomes, and activation of DNA damage checkpoints. Together, these findings indicate that the LANA DPC formation is coordinated with the recruitment of DNA polymerase translesional repair machinery to maintain TR integrity and function in DNA replication and episome maintenance (Figure 7B).

Our findings indicate that LANA, like EBNA1, can form DPCs at their respective episome maintenance elements.33 While many proteins have been found to form such DPCs, it remains unclear whether these events are byproducts of incidental DNA damage due to oxidative stress or are part of a programmed protein-DNA interaction required for functional episome maintenance activity. Studies with EBNA1 suggest that the cross-linking with oriP DNA through Y518 is a programmed component of EBNA1 function important for efficient episome maintenance.33 Similarly, our data suggests that the LANA tyrosine residues involved in DPC formation, Y1066 and Y1014, are also required for efficient episome maintenance (Figures 1, 2, S1, and S2). We suggest that viral episome maintenance proteins, such as LANA and EBNA1, and perhaps HPV E2, have intrinsic DNA crosslinking activity associated with recruitment of cellular repair enzymes and the resolution of DNA replication structures that form at these protein-bound repetitive DNA elements.

The KSHV TR is a complex regulatory element that confers both episome maintenance and transcription control of the latent KSHV genome. In most KSHV genomes, the TR consist of ~35 copies of 810 bp GC-rich unit that contains three tandem LANA-binding sites, a CTCF binding site, and up to four nucleosomes.39 LANA binding to the TR can form higher-ordered pentamers and helical structures27,31; assembly of multi-protein complexes that include replication-associated ORC, MCMs, Tim/Tipin, and DNA polymerases39–41; and transcriptional regulators such as BRD4, MLL1 and RNA polymerase II.42–44 The LANA N-terminal domain interacts directly with histone H2A/B.45 The TR can also serve as a transcriptional enhancer of KSHV lytic cycle genes, suggesting that its activity varies depending on cellular conditions, such as activators of the lytic cycle.25,46 Cell cycle coordination of transcription and replication may lead to conflicts that result in the formation of RNA-DNA hybrids and factors required to resolve these structures.44 The findings presented here indicate that the TR depends on specialized DNA polymerases involved in translesional repair synthesis. The translesion repair requirement may be due in part to LANA-DNA crosslinks, as well as the complexity of many other protein-DNA structures, such as R-loops, that form at the TR. The DNA structures that form at the TR may be similarly formed at cellular telomeres, centromeres, and other CFSs that are known to require translesional repair synthesis to maintain their integrity.47 Thus, KSHV TR may provide a valuable model to study replication of chromosome fragile sites.

DNA polymerase η encoded by the PolH gene (originally termed hRAD30) has a well-established function in DNA replication bypass at DNA lesions caused by UV-crosslinked,48,49 and platinum-induced lesions associated with cancer chemotherapies.50,51 Polη is also known to be important for the synthesis of difficult to replicate regions of the genome, such as CFSs,52 telomere repeat elongation by alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT),53,54 ribonucleotide incorporation and R-loop formation,55 and generation of somatic hypermutation of Ig locus in B cells.56 Polη mutations are in the class of xeroderma pigmentosa (XP) spectrum leading to high incidence of malignant skin cancers, including melanoma. Mutations in Polη may be expected to lead to a genetic instability, which may promote KSHV oncogenic transformation through aberrant integration, gene expression, or lytic replication, all of which could promote viral tumorigenesis. Among the most frequent mutations in KS were those associated with GEO terms “G2M transition of the mitotic cycle” and “DNA-dependent DNA replication”.57 While KS is most commonly observed on the skin, its occurrence at many other sites, including oral and gut mucosa, are observed. However, KSHV driven PEL is not likely subject to UV-associated DNA damage. Interestingly, CRISPR dependencies screen in PEL, although did not identify Polη, did identify TFIIH1 required for transcription-coupled DNA repair and also a member of the XP family of genes.58 Polη is also important for prevent C>T transitions, which could be strongly detrimental to maintaining the high GC-rich content of the TR.59 Thus, Polη may be required to maintain the GC-rich content of the TR, and mutations in Polη or translesional repair may contribute to KSHV-associated malignancies.

The requirement for DNA Polη at the KSHV TR is highly consistent with the findings that LANA forms DNA protein adducts at the TR. Since LANA DNA crosslinks correlate with efficient episome maintenance, we propose that these crosslinks are not incidental damage but evolved protein-DNA interactions that facilitate the maintenance of repetitive DNA structures required for the long-term stability of large episomal genomes. Similar findings with EBNA1 covalent binding to the EBV oriP suggest that this is a common feature of large episomal DNA viruses. Recent studies with extrachromosomal circular DNA in many cancer cells found common features of episome maintenance correlating to epigenetic modifications and mitotic transcription.60 Transcriptional activity and R-loop formation were also observed at the TR44 but the mechanistic relationship between R-loops and DNA Polη require further investigation.

The small molecule inhibitor PNR-07–02 was developed to selectively inhibit DNA Polη to overcome chemotherapy resistance.38 DNA Polη is a major pathway for repair and bypass of DNA lesions induced by platinum-based chemotherapeutics. PNR-07–02 was found to have low micromolar potency in cell culture models, but it has not yet been reported to have any efficacy in mouse tumor models. We show here that PNR-07–02 can selectively block KSHV+ PEL cells in cell culture and reduce PEL growth in mouse models. PNR treatment led to the rapid degradation and eventual loss of the KSHV genome from PEL cells, and Break-seq identified single- and double-strand breaks near the LANA binding sites in the KSHV TR (Figures 6 and S6). These findings strongly suggest that DNA Polη is required for the completion of DNA synthesis within the TR where replication forks may be blocked by repetitive GC-rich DNA, LANA DNA binding and cross-linking, and RNA-DNA hybrids (R-loops) that may further conflict with replication fork progression.

Limitations of the study

Limitations of our study include the potential off-target effects of shRNA depletion of Polη that could also affect the expression and activity of other translesional repair enzymes, which we did observe at the transcriptional level (Figure S3E). Similarly, the pharmacological inhibitor PNR-07–02 may have additional inhibitory effects on other translesional repair enzymes, leaving open the likelihood that other translesional repair enzymes also contribute to the regulation of KSHV TR replication and episome maintenance functions. In this study, we did not determine if PNR is a selective inhibitor for KSHV+ non-PEL cells such as in KS, limiting our conclusions to KSHV+ PEL. We can also not rule out that the mutations in LANA Y1014F/Y1066F may have additional effects on LANA function, including recruitment of other cellular factors or tethering to host chromosomes. Also, since the recruitment of Polη to the TR was disrupted by Y1014F/Y1066F (Figure 7A), it is possible that the interaction measured by coIP is due to DNA bridging and DPC formation.

In conclusion, inhibitors of translesional repair DNA polymerases are presently being explored in clinical trials for treatment of cancer therapy and chemotherapy resistance.61–63 Our findings, and those of others, show that translesional repair DNA polymerases are also required for the completion of viral DNA replication for several other DNA and retroviruses, including EBV, herpes simplex virus (HSV), cytomegalovrus (CMV), and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).64–68 Viral dependencies on translesion repair synthesis may provide a therapeutic window for the development novel, broadly acting antivirals. While inhibition of DNA Polη may reduce centromere stability and telomere elongation, this would not necessarily be cytotoxic to non-cancer cells, including stem cells that replicate slowly or express high levels of telomerase. It will be interesting to test whether inhibitors of DNA Polη have similar selectivity for other viruses, or other regions of the cellular genomes, such as telomeres and centromeres for more selective cancer therapeutics. In conclusion, the mechanism of KSHV episome persistence through LANA DNA cross-linking to the viral TR and the dependency on DNA Polη provides a novel opportunity for therapeutic intervention in KSHV-associated cancers.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Mice

All mice in this study were managed based on Animal Care (AAALAC) per the NIH Office of Laboratory Animal Welfare: “PHS Policy on the Humane Care and Use of Research Animals”. National and institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals were followed. All procedures were approved by The Wistar Institute Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) in compliance with the Animal Welfare Act (AWA) and PHS Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Both male and female NSG mice (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/ScJ; Jackson Laboratory’s stock) bred in-house at The Wistar Institute were used for the xenograft studies. All mice were fed with sterile food and water ad libitum and enrolled in their respective studies at 8 weeks of age. The mice were housed in micro-isolator cages in a designated, specific pathogen-free facility at The Wistar Institute (Philadelphia, PA). Mice were euthanized via CO2 administration according to IACUC, AWA, and PHS guidelines.

Cells, plasmids, and shRNAs

KSHV-positive Primary effusion lymphoma (PEL) BCBL 1, JSC 1, BC 1, and BC3 cells were grown in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco BRL) containing 20% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics penicillin and streptomycin (50 U/mL). EBV positive MUTU 1, Raji and LCL and KSHV, EBV negative cells DG75 and BJABs were grown in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco BRL) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics penicillin and streptomycin (50 U/mL). METHOD DETAILS

Cell cycle synchronization

Cell cycle fractionation using centrifugal elutriation was performed with a modified Beckman JE 5.0 using counter flow rates for BCBL 1 and BC 1 cells as described previously.33

Cell cycle profile analysis

To determine the cell cycle profiles of cells, cultures were fixed in ice-cold 70% ethanol for at least 30 min. After fixation, the cells were mixed with staining solution (0.5 mg/mL propidium iodide, 100 m g/mL RNase A) for 30 min in the dark. Samples were analyzed using an EPICS XL (Beckman-Coulter, Inc., Miami, FL), and 50,000 events were recorded. For all flow cytometry experiments, the WINMDI software program (Scripps Institute) was used to analyze the data.

RADAR assay

Cell lysis and DNA protein covalent crosslink (DPCC) isolation was performed according to Kijanista and Maizels.35,69 Cells were washed once in ice-cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and then resuspended in lysis buffer MB (6 M guanidinium ithiocyanate, 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 6.5), 20 mM EDTA, 4% Triton X-100, 1% Sarkosyl and 1% dithiothreitol) at a ratio of 1 mL MB for every 2 × 106 cells. A total of 20 × 106 cells was used for each cell cycle sample. An aliquot of lysate (10–20%) was saved for analysis of the unfractionated extract, and nucleic acids and DPCC were precipitated from the remainder by addition of 0.5 volume of 100% ethanol followed by centrifugation. The precipitate was washed twice in 75% ethanol and immediately resuspended in freshly prepared 8 mM NaOH. To quantify DNA, a small aliquot was digested with 50 μg/mL proteinase K (Invitrogen) for 3 h at 50°C and quantified using nanodrop 2000C (Thermo-scientific) according to manufacturer instructions to determine DNA concentration.

Detection of DNA-protein crosslinks detection (DPC)

Specific DPCs were detected using an SDS PAGE followed by immunodetection. In brief, equal amounts of DNA were diluted in Tris-buffered saline (TBS; 10 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl) and analyzed on an SDS PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF, Millipore) membrane.

Immunoprecipitation of LANA-DNA crosslinks

Equal amounts of DPCs based on DNA concentration were diluted 10-fold in IP buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM NEM, 0.5% Nonidet P40, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA) and 1 mM phenylmethane sulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), Protease inhibitors (Sigma) and Phosphatase inhibitors (Roche) and precleared with Sepharose beads (Millipore) for 1 h. After incubation for 4 h to overnight with Anti-Flag M2 affinity beads (Sigma-Aldrich), complexes were washed twice with IP buffer containing 300 mM NaCl, and twice with IP buffer containing no detergents. Samples were then eluted with 3 × Flag peptide (250 ng/mL) in Elution buffer (25 mM Na-HEPES 100 mM NaCl).

Site-directed mutagenesis

Primers (IDT) were designed to generate the point mutations (Y1014A, Y1014F, Y1066A, Y1066F, Y1014A/Y1066A and Y1014F/Y1066F) in CMV Flag-LANA containing plasmid (N811). A two-stage PCR protocol for site-directed mutagenesis was adapted from Stratagene.70 Following DpnI digestion and heat inactivation, PCR products were transformed into DH5α cells. Purified plasmids from colonies were sequenced to confirm the mutation.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP assays were performed as previously described.71 Briefly, 293T (~1 × 106 cells) were plated in 10 cm dishes. 24 h later cells were co-transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (12 μL, Invitrogen), 4 μg of 8xTR and 4 μg plasmids expressing either FLAG-LANA 1or Tyrosine mutations. Plasmid replication assays

Plasmid DNA replication assays have been described previously.70 Briefly, 293T (~1 × 106 cells) were plated in 10 cm dishes. 24 h later cells were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (12 μL, Invitrogen) and 4 μg 8xTR with either 4 μg FLAG-LANA 1 or mutant LANA 1 or Flag Vector plasmids. Cells were split after 48 h and then harvested at 72 h post transfection for both episomal DNA and protein. Episomal DNA was extracted by Hirt Lysis.70 The DNA pellets were dissolved in 150 μL of 10 mM Tris HCl, 1 mM EDTA buffer (pH 7.6) and 15 μL was subjected to restriction digestion with EcoRV alone and 135 μL was subjected to EcoRV and DpnI digestion overnight at 37°C. DNA was extracted with phenol: chloroform (1:1), precipitated, and electrophoresed on a 0.9% agarose gel and DNA was Southern blotting to a nylon membrane (PerkinElmer). The blots were hybridized with TR probe and visualized and quantified using a Typhoon 9410.

Plasmid maintenance assays

Plasmid maintenance assays have been described previously.70 Briefly, 293T cells were seeded in 6 well plates at concentrations of 0.75–1.5 ×105 cells/mL. Twenty-four hours later, cells were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (6 μL) and 2 μg 8xTR with either 2 μg FLAG-LANA 1 or mutant LANA, as indicated. Two- or three-days post transfection an equal portion of the cells were split into 15 cm plates to continue passaging and the remaining cells were harvested for Hirt Lysis and Western blot analysis. Isolated DNA was subjected to EcoRV digestion and resolved on 0.9% agarose gels. Southern blotting was performed as described above. After the first passage, 100 μg/mL hygromycin was added for 3–5 days. After initial selection and first passage, cells were continuously split upon reaching confluency for an additional 10–15 days without selection.

Colony formation assay

As described previously,70 293T cells were co-transfected with 10 μg of WT BAC 16 or Y1014F/Y1066F LANA1. The cells were split after 24 h and selected with 100 μg/mL of hygromycin. After 2 weeks of selection, the resulting hygromycin-resistant colonies were stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Colonies that were at least 2 mm in size were scored as positive. Colonies were counted using a colony counting macro written with NIH Image.

shRNA-mediated knockdown of TLS polymerases

KSHV-positive cells were infected by spin infection with pLKO.1 vector-based lentivirus expressing shRNA targeting PolH, Polk, Poli, PolN, Rev1, Rev3, PolQ or shRNA control. Lentiviruses were produced by co-transfection with envelope and packaging vectors pMD2.G and pSPAX2 in 293T cells. Lentiviruses for TLS polymerases were produced by shRNAs targeting to each polymerase. BCBL 1 cells were infected with pool of five lentiviruses carrying shRNA pLKO.1-puro vectors by spin-infection at 450 g for 90 min at room temperature. The cell pellets were resuspended and incubated in fresh RPMI medium, then treated with 2.5 μg/mL puromycin at 48 h after the infection. The RPMI medium with 2.5 μg/mL puromycin was replaced every 2 to 3 days. The cells were collected after 5 days of puromycin selection, then subject to PFGE assays.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from KSHV positive BCBL1 or BC 1 cells using TRIzol (Ambion) and then further treated with DNase I (New England Biolabs). Two micrograms of total RNA were reverse transcribed using random decamers (Ambion) and Superscript IV RNase H− reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). Specific primer sets were used in real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays to measure transcript levels in shCtrol or shTLS know out cells. The values for the relative levels were calculated by ΔΔCT method.

KSHV episome maintenance by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE)

BCBL1, BC-1 and BC-3, and JSC-1 cells were infected with lentivirus expressing shPolH or shCtrl shRNA and Puromycin (2.5 μg/mL) was added after 48hr postinfection. After 5 days of puromycin selection, cells were resuspended in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and an equal amount of 2% agarose to form agarose plugs containing 1.5 × 106 cells that were then incubated for 48 h at 50°C in lysis buffer (0.2 M EDTA [pH 8.0], 1% sodium lauryl sulfate, 1 mg/mL proteinase K). The agarose plugs were washed twice in TE buffer (10 mM Tris [pH 7.5] and 1 mM EDTA). Pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) was performed for 23 h at 14°C with an initial switch time of 60 s and a final switch time of 120 s at 6 V/cm and an included angle of 120° as described previously (Bio-Rad CHEF Mapper).72 DNA was transferred to nylon membranes by established methods for Southern blotting.73 The DNA was then detected by hybridization with α−32P-labeled probe specific for the KSHV TR region or for cellular α-satellite repeat DNA (5′-tttcttttgatagtgcagttttgaaacattctttttaaaaaatctgcagt-3′) and visualized with a Typhoon 9410 variable-mode imager (GE Healthcare Life Sciences).

Drug treatments

KSHV positive BCBL 1, JSC 1, BC 1, BC 3 and KSHV negative BJABs, DG75 cells or EBV positive MUTU 1, Raji, lymphoblastoid cell line (LCL) (0.5 × 106 cells/mL) were treated with vehicle control (DMSO; 0.016%, vol/vol) or PNR-07–02 (10 μM) for 12 h. PNR-02–02 was purchased from MedChem Express (Cat. No.: HY-117664). Cells were harvested and flow cytometry was used for the evaluation of apoptosis as per the protocols described earlier using an annexin V and 7-AAD-based kit (#ab214663, Abcam, Melbourne, VIC, Australia). For PFGE studies cells were treated with either vehicle control (DMSO; 0.016%, vol/vol) or PNR-07–02(10 μM) for 2, 4,6 and 8hr and PFGE was performed as described above.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were extracted with lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4], 1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM EGTA, 2 mM MgCl2, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM NaF, 20 mM sodium glycerophosphate, 5% glycerol, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 1× protease inhibitors [Sigma], 1× phosphatase inhibitors [Sigma], and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride [PMSF]). Add 100 U of Benzonase and MgCl2 to a final concentration of 1.5 mM to the lysate to remove DNA and RNA molecules. After rotation for 60 min at 4°C, the lysate was centrifuged for 20 min at 16,000 × g, and the supernatant was recovered. The cleared extracts were used for immunoprecipitation with antibodies as indicated in the figures.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were grown on Poly-D-Lysine-coated 22 mm glass coverslips in 6-well plates. Cells were rinsed in PBS and the cytoplasm was extracted with 4 M glycerol, 100mM Pipes pH6.9, 1mM EGTA and 0.25% Triton X-100 solution on ice for 90 s. Nuclei were then immediately fixed in either 3.5% formaldehyde room temperature for 15min. Nuclei were rinsed in PBS containing 1 mg/mL BSA. Nuclei were then saturated for 1 h in 1 × Perm/Wash buffer. Target proteins were detected using the indicated antibodies diluted at 1/100 in 1 × Perm/Wash buffer overnight at 4°C in a humid chamber. Coverslips were rinsed 3 × 5 min in 1 × Perm/Wash buffer and incubated 1h at room temperature with secondary antibodies (Orgon Green, Alexa Fluor), diluted at 1/1000 in 1 × Perm/Wash buffer. Coverslips were rinsed 3 × 5min in 1 × Perm/Wash buffer and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) for 5 min at room temperature. Coverslips were mounted in Prolong Diamond Antifade and observed on a Leica Stellaris Confocal with an X63 objective. Pictures were saved and processed using the Aivia and LAS X, advanced image analysis software solutions from Leica Microsystems software.

Confocal and stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy image acquisition

Acquisition parameters were set in Leica software to 1024 × 1024 pixels, a scan speed of 400 Hz, line accumulation of 12, and a 5X zoom, resulting in a resolution of 22 nm/pixel. Tumor implantation, grouping, and equalization

Mice (six males and six females per treatment group) were engrafted with a cell suspension (>98% viability) of 5 ×105 BC3-mCherry/eLUC (KSHV+), or 5 × 105 BJAB-mcherry/eLUC (KSHV−) cells were resuspended in 1× PBS, pH 7.4 and maintained on ice. Animals were weighed three-times per week and monitored daily. The Spectrum IVIS CT Bioluminescent Imaging System (Revvity; Waltham, MA) was used to randomize mice into groups of 12 per treatment (six males and six females) on day five so ensure the average Flux (Photons/sec) was equivalent across groups and to monitor cell growth and metastasis throughout the study. For imaging studies, mice were injected with D-luciferin (Gold Biotechnology), i.p. at a dose of 7.5 mg/kg in a dose volume of 10 mL/kg body weight 15 min prior to imaging; this was the optimal interval between luciferin injection and bioluminescent imaging as determined by an initial kinetic curve for these cell lines in mice. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane prior to imaging. Total body Flux (Photons/Sec) was quantitated throughout the study using the IVIS Living Image software.

PNR07–02 formulation and treatment schedule

PNR07–02 was weighed and vehicle was added immediately prior to dose. The vehicle was 2% DMSO in PBS, boiled at 95°C for 5 min. The vehicle control comprised formulation reagents without compound. PNR-07–02 was administered q. d., p. o. at 25,100 and 200 mg/kg in a dose volume of 10 mL/kg body weight.

Mapping DNA breaks

DNA breaks were mapped by Next-Generation Sequencing according Baranello et al.74

Purification of genomic DNA

Genomic DNA from 5 × 107 cells treated with DMSO or PNR07–02 was purified and treated with 5 μg of pancreatic RNase (Roche) fallowed by with or without 1 μg of TDP1 in 80 mM KCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA,2 mM DTT for 1 h at 37°C, adjusted to 0.5% SDS and incubated for 1 h at 55°C with proteinase K (200 μg/mL). DNA was extracted twice with phenol and precipitated with ethanol in the presence of 2 M ammonium acetate.

SSB-seq

Single-strand breaks were labeled by nick translation with digoxigenin-11-dUTP (Roche). Immunoprecipitated the labeled DNA with Anti-digoxigenin antibody (Roche) and the DNA was purified using QIAquick PCR Purification Kit Protocol (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified.

DSB-seq

Double-strand breaks were labeled with biotin by 3′ end tailing reaction. Then, biotinylated fragments were captured with streptavidin-coated beads by using Dynabeads kilobaseBINDER Kit (Invitrogen Dynal, Oslo, Norway) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The DNA fragments were purified with a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN). The biotinylated tails from DNA were removed by incubated with 30 U of S1 nuclease (Fermentas, Clen Burnie, MD, USA) and then DNA was purified with a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN).

Template preparation for sequencing analysis

The Epicentre DNA END-Repair kit (Epicentre Biotechnologies, Madison, WI, USA) was used to generate blunt-ended DNA. SSB and DSB DNA libraries were prepared using NEBNext utra II DNA library prep kit for Illumina (NEB). The purified DNA was used directly for cluster generation and sequencing analysis using the Illumina Genome Analyzer (Illumina, San Diego, USA) following manufacturer protocols.

Processing of sequencing data

Sequencing data were preprocessed using the Illumina Analysis Pipeline (image analysis and base calling, Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The reads o were aligned to the human gamma herpesvirus 8 BCBL1 genome (GCA_027939675.1) using Bowtie 2 program (version 2.2.2).75 Generate a read density visualization of the aligned sequencing data viewed in UCSC Genome Browser. Break-seq DNA is deposited in NCBI GEO datas.

Statistical methods

For biochemical and molecular biology experiments (n = 3) p-values were calculated using two-tailed student t test using GraphPad/PRISM. For mouse xenograft studies (n = 5). For selective cell killing assays p-values determined by ordinary one-way ANOVA and Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. For microscopy, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated for the colocalizations.
