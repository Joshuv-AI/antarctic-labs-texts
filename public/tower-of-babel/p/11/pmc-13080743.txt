Biological Age Acceleration as a New Lens for Aortic Stenosis Risk Stratification
Authors: Dae-Hee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Aortic valve stenosis (AVS) is classically framed as a disease of aging, and chronological age (CA) remains central to contemporary risk stratification, guideline recommendations, and clinical decision making.1 However, reliance on CA assumes biological uniformity among individuals of the same age, an assumption that is increasingly difficult to reconcile with clinical reality. Patients with similar CA frequently exhibit strikingly different rates of disease progression and outcomes, highlighting a critical limitation of age-based risk assessment. CA measures time lived, not biological vulnerability, and this discrepancy may partially explain the persistent heterogeneity in prognosis observed across the spectrum of AVS.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Zheng et al2 directly address this unmet need by reframing risk assessment through the lens of biological aging. By introducing biological age acceleration (BAA), defined as biological aging beyond that expected for a given CA, the authors propose a clinically pragmatic approach to capture interindividual differences in aging burden that are invisible to conventional age-based models. Their findings suggest that biological aging, rather than CA alone, may represent a more relevant determinant of prognosis in patients with moderate-to-severe AVS.2

Aging is a complex, multisystem process that unfolds heterogeneously across individuals.3,4 Whereas CA reflects time elapsed, biological age (BA) aims to quantify the cumulative physiological burden of aging at the organ and cellular levels.3 BAA captures the extent to which an individual’s biological aging exceeds or lags behind what would be expected based on CA alone. This concept formalizes a long-recognized clinical intuition: patients of the same CA do not age at the same rate.

In the present study, BA was estimated using 2 well-validated, biomarker-based algorithms, the Klemera-Doubal method and PhenoAge, both derived from routinely available clinical and laboratory data.5,6 By defining BAA as the regression residual of BA on CA, the authors ensured that BAA was largely independent of CA itself. Supplemental analyses demonstrate that although BA correlates strongly with CA, BAA does not, thereby isolating interindividual differences in biological aging among patients of the same age.2

Among 559 patients with moderate-to-severe AVS, higher BAA was consistently associated with increased all-cause mortality.2 Each standard deviation increase in BAA conferred approximately a 35% to 36% higher mortality risk, even after comprehensive adjustment for demographics, comorbidities, medication use, and conventional clinical variables.2 The association was linear across the observed range of BAA and remained robust across clinically relevant subgroups, including disease severity. These findings extend prior observations linking biological aging to adverse outcomes across cardiovascular and systemic diseases.6, 7, 8

Several aspects merit emphasis. First, the prognostic value of BAA extended to patients with moderate AVS, a population in which risk stratification remains challenging and therapeutic decision making is often uncertain.9 Second, incorporation of BAA into baseline risk models resulted in meaningful improvements in discrimination and reclassification metrics, suggesting that biological aging captures prognostic information not reflected by traditional risk factors or echocardiographic severity alone.2

From a mechanistic perspective, the association between BAA and adverse outcomes is biologically plausible. Systemic aging processes characterized by chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, oxidative stress, and cellular senescence have been increasingly implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of calcific aortic valve disease.10 Consistent with this framework, Zheng et al2 observed associations between BAA and indices of left ventricular remodeling, supporting a link between systemic biological aging and cardiac structural adaptation.

One of the most compelling features of this study lies in its clinical pragmatism. Numerous aging-related markers have been proposed, including leukocyte telomere length, clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, and frailty indices.4,6,8 Although biologically informative, these measures often require specialized testing, genetic sequencing, or time-intensive assessments, limiting routine clinical applicability. In contrast, BAA as implemented here relies on routinely obtained blood biomarkers and standard clinical measurements.5,6 This accessibility, combined with low cost and minimal additional burden, substantially enhances its translational potential.

Furthermore, BAA introduces an important conceptual shift. Unlike CA, biological aging may be modifiable. Intensive management of cardiovascular risk factors, lifestyle interventions, and emerging geroscience-informed strategies have all been shown to influence components of biological aging.3,7 Whether attenuation of BAA can translate into improved outcomes in AVS remains speculative, but the possibility raises important questions for future investigation.

Despite its strengths, limitations warrant consideration. The observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. The cohort consisted exclusively of Chinese patients, underscoring the need for validation in Western and multi-ethnic populations.2 It also remains unclear whether BAA represents merely a marker of cumulative disease burden or a mediator on the causal pathway to adverse outcomes.

Zheng et al2 challenge us to reconsider how risk is conceptualized in aortic stenosis. By shifting the focus from CA to BAA, this study highlights aging as a measurable and clinically relevant dimension of vulnerability rather than a simple function of time. Importantly, the proposed framework leverages routinely available clinical data, enhancing its potential for real-world application. As the field moves toward more individualized approaches to valvular heart disease, BAA offers a promising lens through which prognosis, timing of intervention, and future therapeutic strategies may be redefined.
