Association of soluble apoptotic biomarkers (FAS,TNFR1 and TRAIL-R2) with β-cell dysfunction in early glucose dysregulation
Authors: Rama Ayash, Younes Kabalan, Sahar Chamaa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association of soluble death receptors (FAS, TNFR1, and TRAIL-R2) with β-cell function in individuals with naïve prediabetes and newly diagnosed, treatment – naïve type 2 diabetes, and to assess the strength of this association to determine the independent variable that contributes to the variance of HOMA-B.Methods and materialsThis was a cross-sectional study done at the Endocrine Clinics of National Hospital. There were two groups: group 1 included 49 newly diagnosed, drug-naive prediabetic patients, and group 2 consisted of 29 newly diagnosed, drug-naive,T2DM patients. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Damascus University (No. m/471 -12/5/2021) and registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (Trial ID: ACTRN12624001135505).Inclusion criteria included newly diagnosed prediabetic or T2DM subjects who had received no treatment, based on the 2015 American Diabetes Association criteria. Exclusion criteria entailed past histories of severe cardiovascular, renal, tumor, or autoimmune diseases and use of drug therapies including oral hypoglycemic agents.General and demographic information (Sex, age, BMI) was obtained, and blood samples were taken after fasting for 12 h. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels were measured using an automatic analyzer (AMS, Italy). HbA1c levels were measured photometrically using the HemoCue (HbA1c 501 analyzer,America).Insulin levels were determined via an ELISA kit (Diametra, Italy), and soluble death receptors (TNFR1, Fas, TRAIL-R2) were measured using ELISA kits (Raybiotech, USA). The following formula calculates Homeostatic Model Assessment of Beta Cell Function( HOMA-B), HOMA-B (%) = [360 × basal insulin (µIU/mL)] / [basal glucose (mg/dL) – 63].Statistical analyses were carried out in SPSS version 25. Distribution of the data was carried out using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for normality. Student t test for independent samples were done for normally distributed data, while Mann–Whitney U test was used for nonnormal variables. The relationships between HOMA-B and FBG in addition to other variables with significance level were performed using linear regressions with p values < 0.05 were deemed to be statistically significant.ResultsThe levels of TNFR1, Fas, and TRAIL-R2 were significantly higher in T2DM compared with prediabetes (p < 0.0001). TNFR1 and Fas exhibited a strong negative correlation with HOMA-B in both groups. Regression analysis indicated that Fas, followed by TRAIL-R2, was the main predictor of β-cell function. They combined accounted for 60% and 42% of the HOMA-B variance in prediabetes and T2DM, respectively.ConclusionElevated soluble death receptor levels, particularly Fas, are a marker of impaired β-cell function in prediabetes and T2DM. These observations suggest that death receptors-mediated apoptosis may play a role in the progression of β-cell dysfunction from prediabetes to T2DM and point toward possible use of these markers as early indicators of β-cell dysfunction.

Introduction

The progressive loss of pancreatic β-cell mass is vital for the pathogenesis of dysfunctional glucose homeostasis, which greatly contributes to the mechanisms responsible for developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Primarily, hyperglycemia will arise when the insulin secretion fails because of the dysfunction of β-cells to compensate the insulin resistance. These metabolic changes might be observed before the overt manifestation of the disease during the impaired glucose tolerance phase and become aggravated with progressive fullness of T2DM [1, 2]. The decline in β-cell mass is a critical factor in the progression of T2DM. Many studies have shown by the time diabetes is diagnosed clinically, approximately 50% of β-cell mass has already been lost, following which there may be a further reduction of 4–5% per annum [3, 4]. Apoptosis appears to be a significant contributor to the progressive decline in β-cell mass, rather than inadequate replication/insufficient neogenesis. The apoptosis of β-cells is known to involve several different mechanisms, but the latest reports reveal that both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways of apoptosis are substantially involved. Among them, ER stress is emerging as a significant initiator of apoptosis in β-cells, mainly under conditions of metabolic stress. ER stress arises due to an accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins, subsequently leading to the activation of a complex signaling network called the UPR. Initially, it causes the restoration of cellular homeostasis, but its chronic activation under prolonged metabolic stress may switch its role into that of apoptosis. This response induces transcription of proapoptotic proteins, induction of proinflammatory cytokines, and activation of the nuclear factor kappa B (NFκB) signaling pathway placing significant stress on β-cells [5, 6]. NFκB (nuclear factor kappa B) is a transcription factor for stress response implicated in inflammation and apoptosis. Its activation results in the expression of death receptors on β-cells, which belong to the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor superfamily. These receptors belong to the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor gene superfamily and are implicated in mediating signals that initiate the extrinsic pathway of apoptosis leading to the activation of caspases, thereby triggering pancreatic beta cell. The main three death receptors being most important in the process include TNF receptor-1 (TNFR1), CD95 (Fas or APO-1), and TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand receptor 2 (TRAILR-2) [7].

Each of the receptors is characterized by the presence of the highly conserved cytoplasmic region known as the death domain (DD), which is 80 to 90 amino acids long. This death domain is indispensable for the transduction of the apoptotic signals. When engaged by their ligands, these to receptors recombine adapter proteins containing DDs themselves-such as Fas-associated death domain (FADD) and TNFR-associated death domain (TRADD)-to serve as molecular bridges to connect death receptors to their downstream signaling components [8].

Subsequently, these adapter proteins recruit caspases through interaction motifs with a novel fold that are generally referred to as death effector domains (DEDs). This assemblage leads to the formation of the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC), a crucial multiprotein platform important for amplification of the apoptotic signal by activation of initiator caspases, such as caspase-8. Activation of these caspases instigates a cascade of proteolytic reactions, which ultimately causes cell death [9, 10].

Owing to the strong association between β-cell apoptosis and the progression of T2DM, soluble death receptors and their ligands have been proposed as serological biomarkers for β-cell mass and function. In contrast with traditional measures of glycemic control, such as HbA1c, which reflect systemic glucose regulation, apoptosis-related biomarkers would seem to provide a more direct assessment of β-cell viability. Their potential utility in identifying at-risk individuals, monitoring disease progression, and evaluating the intervention provides justification for more research into elucidating the mechanistic role of death receptor signaling in β-cell loss. Serological biomarkers of β-cell apoptosis or β-cell mass possess the great advantage over conventional diagnostic methods of representing a noninvasive means of monitoring the health of the pancreas [11, 12]. In contrast to pancreatic biopsies or imaging studies, which are not invasively feasible on a routine basis to monitor β-cell status, measurement of apoptosis-related biomarkers in blood samples enables one to monitor β-cell function on a continuous and longitudinal basis. Such biomarkers would augment traditional glycemic markers such as HbA1c, which more accurately reflect systemic glucose control than β-cell integrity per se. Through the combination of serological biomarkers and traditional diagnostic thresholds, the clinician will be in a position to stratify those at increased risk of diabetes, individualize therapeutic approaches, and monitor the effectiveness of treatment in maintaining β-cell mass.

Additional research is warranted to confirm the predictive potential of these biomarkers and determine standardized cut-offs for clinical use. Nevertheless, their prospective future use in early disease diagnosis, risk stratification, and personalized medicine recognizes the important contribution of death receptor-mediated apoptosis to T2DM pathophysiology.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the association between soluble death receptors (TNFR1, Fas, and TRAIL-R2) and β-cell function in prediabetes and T2DM. Specifically, we sought to determine whether these apoptotic markers correlate with HOMA-B and fasting blood glucose levels and evaluate the contribution of these biomarkers to β-cell dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Study protocols and population selection

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Department of Endocrine clinic at National Hospital. The study protocol involving human participants was reviewed and approved by Medical Ethics Committee of Damascus University (No. m/471 −12/5/2021) and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study is registered in Australian New Zeeland Clinical Registry in 20/9/2024. Clinical Trial ID: ACTRN12624001135505 (https://www.anzctr.org.au/ACTRN12624001135505.aspx).

In total, the patients/participants provided written informed consent to participate in this study.

The study involved two groups: Group (1): newly diagnosed and drug-naive prediabetes patients (n = 49, aged 50 ± 9.5 yrs), Group (2): newly diagnosed and drug-naive T2DM patients (n = 29 aged 54.5 ± 10.3yrs).

All enrolled subjects were nonsmokers and diagnosed on the basis of based on 2015 American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: Adults aged 35–65 years, Newly diagnosed individuals with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes who had not received any prior treatment with oral hypoglycemic agents (i.e., treatment-naïve patients).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with a history of cardiovascular disease, renal disease, tumor or autoimmune disease. Type 1 diabetes, Type 2 diabetes was treated with oral hypoglycemic agents and prediabetes was treated with metformin.

Data collection and grouping

Data including sex, age, and BMI were obtained during the diagnosis of patients in the study groups. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m2). Blood samples were obtained after fasting for 12 h. The samples were collected in a sterile tube containing heparin, and centrifuged, at 3,000 rpm, to obtain the plasma. fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels were measured in all patients. This was done by centrifuging the plasma for 10 min at 2000 rpm and using an automatic analyzer (AMS, Italy) with a standard glucose oxidase method.

HbA1c levels were measured photometrically using the HemoCue (HbA1c 501 analyzer, America), which is based on the boronate affinity method.

Insulin levels were determined using the ELISA metho-d. Beta cell function was also calculated using fasting insulin levels and the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Beta Cell Function [13]. HOMA-β method: 20 × fasting insulin (μU/mL)/(FPG [mmol/L]—3.5). Quantitative determination of plasma levels of CRP were measured by turbidometry using (Biorex kit, UK).

Enzyme -linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Blood samples were immediately collected from patients meeting the inclusion criteria upon their admission to the endocrine clinic. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatants were stored in aliquots at −80 °C until further use. Insulin levels were measured in plasma samples via ELISA (diametra kit). TNFR1, FAS and TRAIL-R2 levels were measured via ELISA (Raybiotech, USA).

Homeostasis model assessment was determined via the HOMA-beta (%), which represents beta cell function, and was calculated as 360 × basal insulin/(basal glucose – 63). Glucose is presented at mg/dL and insulin is presented at µIU/mL.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of the clinical and biomedical parameters were performed via SPSS ver 25 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The data distributions were assessed via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The data are presented as the ± standard error of the means (SEM). Normally distributed data were examined by the two-sample independent Student’s t_test. Nonnormally distributed data were examined via the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. And compare the predictive value of every independently associated factor or united factor to T2DN. Multiple linear regression was performed to validate the relationships between FBG and HOMA-B with other parameters and to compare the predictive value of every independent variable associated with T2DM, with 95% confidence interval (CI). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Glycemic and metabolic parameters

Fasting blood glucose (FBG) was higher in the T2DM group (225.04 ± 58.3 mg/dL) than in the prediabetes group (114.04 ± 6.5 mg/dL; t = −13.25; p < 0.0001). The HbA1c levels were similarly elevated in T2DM (10.88 ± 9.17%) compared to the IFG group (8.53 ± 3.87%, z = −5.247; p < 10.0001) (Table 1).

HOMA-B, an indicator of beta-cell function, was significantly lower in the T2DM group (1.54 ± 1.74) than in the prediabetes group (2.93 ± 1.07; z = −0.524, p = 0.018), suggesting a type of impaired beta-cell function in diabetes.

Discussion

The study revealed significant differences between the study groups, suggesting that apoptotic pathways might be involved in the development of prediabetes to overt diabetes.Mean age of participants with T2DM was significantly higher than that of the IFG group at (p = 0.038), indicating that increased age was significantly associated with the progression of diabetes based on international studies [14].

Our study showed that gender distribution between groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.064), indicating that sex-based differences may not be a major factor in Beta-cell dysfunction in this study. Notably, Fasting blood glucose in theT2DM diabetes group is significantly higher than in the IFG group (p < 0.0001).This was due to the prolonged deterioration of beta cell in T2DM.Similarly, HbA1c levels were significantly elevated in the T2DM group (p = 0.018), confirming that the decline in beta cell function accompanies with poor glycemic control [15]. The HOMA-B index is remarkably reduced in T2DM (p < 0.0001) compared to IFG group. This decline was a result of the impaired insulin secretion capacity that diminishes over time as IFG progresses to T2DM, emphasizing that insulin resistance results in cell exhaustion and apoptosis [13]. High levels of TNFR1 were found in T2DM patients compared to IFG (p < 0.0001). It was thus hypothesized that apoptosis via TNFR1 trigger extrinsic pathway of beta cell apoptosis and thus worsening glucose homeostasis. Fas levels were significantly higher in T2DM patients than IFG (p < 0.0001), which involved in apoptosis contributing to beta-cell loss during diabetes progression.

Similarly, TRAIL-R2 levels were significantly higher in T2DM patients than in IFG (p < 0.0001), which would seem to allude to its action in inducing beta-cell apoptosis. This may indicate that TRAIL-R2 contributes along with TNFR1 and Fas in providing β-cell dysfunction in diabetes status. The highly significant p-value, therefore, substantiates the hypothesis that death receptor-mediated apoptosis is a crucial mechanism for beta-cell loss in diabetes progression [16, 17].

Therefore, quantification and comparison of death receptor expression levels in beta cells of patients with IFG and T2DM can provide valuable insights into the role of the extrinsic apoptosis pathway in the progression from IFG to T2DM.

Thus, the assessment and comparison of death receptor expression levels in beta cells of subjects with IFG and T2DM could provide valuable insights into the potential role of the extrinsic apoptosis pathway in the development of IFG to T2DM disease.

CRP levels did not differ between T2DM and IFG patients (p = 0.812). This indicates that systemic inflammation emerges in the early stages of impaired glucose homeostasis; thus, chronic inflammation is implicated in beta-cell dysfunction [18].

This suggests that systemic inflammation arises early in glucose dysregulation and contributes to beta-cell apoptosis.Our study demonstrated a strong association between death receptors and fasting blood glucose in both groups studies. These findings confirm that death receptors are involved in glucose dysregulation.On the other hand, the study showed a strong inverse correlation between TNFR1 and FAS with HOMA-B across both IFG and T2DM groups, that indicates that TNFR1 and FAS have detrimental impact on beta cell function prior incident diabetes. In addition, this implies that beta cell apoptosis is initiated in an early stage of beta cell loss. Since TNFR1 is activated upon binding with TNF, which pro inflammatory cytokine released in an early stage of inflammation, by recruiting adaptor protein TRADD that activates caspase cascade causing apoptosis.Moreover, FAS mediated apoptosis drives a crucial role in beta cell apoptosis, contributing to decreased insulin secretion.This is attributed that FAS receptor is activated upon binding with FAS ligand and recruits adaptor protein FADD and ultimately activates executioner caspase causing apoptosis [6, 17]. Furthermore, correlation study between TRAIL-R2 and HOMA-B across both study groups is slightly weak due to another signal transduction pathway (FADD independent) non-cell death signal transduction pathways, such as PKC (protein kinase C), PI3K (phosphatidylinositide 3-kinases), and AKT/PKB, which decrease the apoptotic effect of the death receptor [19].

Regression analysis showed that 60% and 42% of variance in beta cell function is ascribed primarily to death receptor FAS in both IFG and T2DM groups respectively. Whilst, TNFR1 and TRAIL-R2 lost their significance in T2DM.This is explained that TNFR1 may activate survival pathways via adaptor protein TRAF2 and RIPK1, that boost beta cell survival within certain conditions. Additionally, TRAIL-R2 can trigger multiple survival signaling pathways such as PKC and PI3K,which diminish apoptotic pathways.

Our results underscore that FAS receptor is the primary driver of beta cell apoptosis due to multiple proapoptotic pathways either via FADD -caspase signaling or indirect pathway as it decreases expression of transcription factors susch as Mafa and Pdx-1involved in beta cell differentiation and survival.

Soluble FAS may have potential as a clinical biomarker for early β-cell dysfunction, but further research is needed to determine appropriate cutoffs and clinical utility.

This study has a number of limitations to their results. The cross-sectional study design does not allow establishing causality between death receptor levels and beta-cell function, thus necessitating longitudinal studies so as to identify and confirm temporal relationships. The relatively small sample size-exclusively in the T2DM group-might impose limitations on generalizability and statistical power. While it is widely accepted, HOMA-B infers beta-cell function indirectly, and more thorough, direct assessments may boost the accuracy of the results. The study's reliance on soluble death receptor measurements in plasma, instead of direct tissue analysis, restricts conclusions about receptor activity in beta-cells specifically. These biomarkers may not represent this dynamic relationship, as single time-point measures can fail to encompass such behaviors. The highlighted apoptosis path is not representative of other pathways of beta-cell death. Future studies that will address these limitations could shed more light on the role that death receptors play in the progression from prediabetes through diabetes mellitus type 2.

Conclusion

Our study reveals a strong correlation between soluble death receptors, especially FAS in both study groups, and beta-cell dysfunction as prediabetes progresses to type 2 diabetes.This indicates their crucial role in glucose dysregulation and beta cell apoptosis, even in the early stages of the disease. This study underscores the potential of death receptor FAS, as promising biomarker of early detection of stress on beta-cells and as possible therapeutic targets. Albeit with certain limitations, the results provide further knowledge on the pathophysiology of T2DM and pave the way for longitudinal studies to assess the potential of these biomarkers for predicting β-cell dysfunction and targeted interventions.
