Effects of Aerosol Extract From Heated Tobacco Products on Senescence‐Associated Gene Expression in Human Lung Fibroblasts
Authors: Ayami Sato, Satsuki Kasahara, Akihito Ishigami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimCellular senescence is associated with an increased risk of various diseases. Although heated tobacco products (HTPs) have become widespread substitutes for conventional combustible cigarettes, their role in cellular senescence remains unclear. Therefore, we evaluated the effects of HTP aerosol extract on cytotoxicity and expression of senescence‐associated genes in human lung‐derived normal diploid fibroblasts (TIG‐1).MethodsTIG‐1 cells were exposed to smoke extract from a 1R6F reference cigarette (RC) or aerosol extract from a Ploom X HTP. Cell membrane damage and cellular metabolic activity were analyzed by lactate dehydrogenase and WST‐8 assays, respectively. The mRNA levels of senescence‐associated genes and genes encoding xenobiotic‐metabolizing enzymes were analyzed using RT‐qPCR following single or repeated exposure. Replicative senescence and doxorubicin (DOX)‐induced senescence were also evaluated.ResultsHTP was less cytotoxic than RC. Repeated exposure to RC and HTP (HTP at a 10‐fold higher concentration) increased the expression levels of CDKN2A, IL1B, MMP1, AHR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1, and decreased that of MMP13, showing changes similar to replicative senescence or DOX‐induced senescence. MMP2 and MMP7 expression levels increased only by RC, whereas MMP12 expression was selectively induced by HTP.ConclusionAlthough less cytotoxic than RCs, repeated exposure to HTP aerosols may influence senescence‐associated factors.Keywords: cigarette smoke, doxorubicin‐induced senescence, heated tobacco products, replicative senescence, senescence‐associated genes

Introduction

Cellular senescence is a state of stable cell cycle arrest accompanied by phenotypic changes, such as altered gene expression, secretion of pro‐inflammatory mediators, and impaired tissue repair [1, 2]. Accumulating evidence indicates that senescent cells contribute to the pathogenesis of respiratory diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and lung cancer [3, 4, 5].

Combustible cigarette smoke (CS) is known to induce cellular senescence in the lungs [6]. Clinical data have shown an association between aging hallmarks and smoking, including accelerated epigenetic age and telomere shortening, with cigarette smokers having a shorter life expectancy than never‐smokers [7, 8, 9]. Studies show that CS triggers senescence in alveolar epithelial cells and lung fibroblasts through activation of the p21CIP1/WAF1 (encoded by CDKN1A) and p16INK4a (encoded by CDKN2A) pathways [10, 11]. Senescent cells secrete pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL6 and IL1B, and chemokines such as CXCL2 and CXCL5, thereby constituting the senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which promotes chronic inflammation, extracellular matrix degradation, and tissue remodeling via the induction of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [12]. These senescence processes contribute to respiratory pathogenesis [13].

Heated tobacco products (HTPs) have recently been proposed as less harmful alternatives to traditional combustible cigarettes. Recent surveys in Japan indicate that approximately 35%–40% of current smokers use HTPs [14]. Analytical studies have indicated that aerosols from HTPs contain higher levels of propylene glycol and glycerol than those from CS [15]. Previously, we reported the effects of exposure to HTP aerosol extract on lung epithelial cells [16]. Our findings indicated that HTPs are less cytotoxic than CS; nevertheless, they may carry a risk of altering DNA methylation and gene expression. Comprehensive analyses revealed that HTPs affect CYP1A1 through epigenetic alterations. CYP1A1 is a cytochrome P450 enzyme that metabolizes environmental toxins, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons [17]. Furthermore, HTP aerosols induce oxidative stress, DNA damage responses, cytotoxicity, and inflammation in human airway cells, microglia, vascular cells, and animal models to a lesser extent than CS [18, 19, 20, 21]. However, evidence of HTP‐induced cellular senescence is limited, indicating the need for further studies.

In this study, we investigated the effects of HTP aerosols on senescence‐associated gene expression in human lung‐derived fibroblasts that undergo replicative senescence or doxorubicin (DOX)‐induced senescence, and compared these effects to those of CS.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of Smoke and Aerosol Extracts

The 1R6F reference cigarette (RC) was purchased from the University of Kentucky, Kentucky Tobacco Research and Development Center (Lexington, KY, USA). A commercial HTP (Ploom X) was obtained from Japan Tobacco Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). The details of the tobacco heating system have been described previously [22]. Briefly, the HTP consisted of two parts: a tobacco stick and a battery‐powered heater that heats the product to 295°C. Mainstream RC and HTP aerosols were generated using smoking machines under the Health Canada Intense puffing regimen (55 mL puff volume, 2 s duration, 30 s puff interval, and bell‐shaped puff profile). The RC was smoked until the butt length reached 35 mm (ISO, 4387). For the HTP, the total number of puffs per tobacco stick was set to 10 for aerosol collection. Total particulate matter (TPM) was collected on a 44 mm Cambridge filter pad and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan) at a concentration of 40 mg/mL for the RC extract and 100 mg/mL for the HTP extract. These extracts were aliquoted and stored at −80°C until use. The chemical constituents of the RC and HTP aerosols have been characterized previously [22].

Cell Culture

Human fetal lung‐derived normal diploid fibroblasts (TIG‐1) were originally established at the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute for Geriatrics and Gerontology (Tokyo, Japan). The cells were cultured in Eagle's minimum essential medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan), non‐essential amino acids (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Gibco) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The population doubling level (PDL) was calculated using the following equation: PDL = 3.32 (logN f − logN i ) + X, where N f is the number of cells at the end of the subculture, N i is the number of cells inoculated at the beginning of the subculture, and X is the PDL of the cells inoculated for the subculture. Serial cultivation of TIG‐1 cells was initiated at a PDL of 27–30. The replicative senescence group used cells with a PDL of ≥ 55. Replicative senescence was induced by extended passaging of cells. A DOX‐induced senescence model was established as described previously [23]. TIG‐1 cells were treated with 1 μM DOX hydrochloride (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co.) for 24 h, after which the medium was replaced with normal culture medium, and the cells were cultured for an additional 5 days during which the medium was changed once. The control group was treated by adding water to the medium.

Cytotoxicity

Cells (6 × 104) were seeded in 24‐well plates and cultured to confluence. RC and HTP extracts were diluted with DMSO to the indicated TPM concentrations and added to the culture medium at a concentration of 1% (v/v). For repeated exposure, the cells were treated with the extract on alternate days (Figure 1A). After incubation in media containing different concentrations of RC and HTP extracts for 1 and 5 days, the levels of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), a marker of membrane damage, were measured, and intracellular dehydrogenase activity (cellular metabolic activity) was evaluated using the WST‐8 assay with the Viability/Cytotoxicity Multiplex Assay Kit (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Absorbance was measured at 490 and 450 nm for the LDH and WST‐8 assays, respectively, using a VICTOR Nivo Multimode Microplate Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

The HTP was less cytotoxic than the RC but altered cellular metabolic activity. RC and HTP aerosol extracts were diluted in DMSO to the indicated total particulate matter (TPM) concentrations and added to the culture medium at a concentration of 1% (v/v). TIG‐1 cells (PDL27–30) were incubated with different concentrations of RC and HTP extracts for 1 or 5 days. (A) Experimental design. (B) LDH levels in culture supernatants were measured. (C) Cellular metabolic activity was evaluated using the WST‐8 assay. Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 3). Statistical analysis was performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's test (✻ p < 0.01, ✻✻ p < 0.0001 vs. 0 μM).

Reverse Transcription‐Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐qPCR)

Cells (8 × 105) were seeded in 6‐well plates and cultured to confluence. After incubation with RC and HTP extract‐containing media for 1 and 5 days, total RNA was extracted using ISOGEN (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co.), according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized from RNA using SuperScript III reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Real‐time PCR was performed using the QuantStudio 3 System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), according to the manufacturers' instructions. Primers were purchased from Eurofins Genomics (Tokyo, Japan); the primer sequences are listed in Table S1. The amplification protocol consisted of denaturation at 95°C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. Gene expression was normalized to that of 18S rRNA.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Welch's t‐test, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or two‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's test was used as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Gene Expression Changes Induced by Replicative Senescence and DOX Treatment

The effects of replicative senescence and DOX treatment in TIG‐1 cells on the mRNA expression of senescence‐associated genes (CDKN1A, CDKN2A, CCND1, IL6, IL1B, CCL2, CXCL2, CXCL5, MMP1, MMP2, MMP7, MMP12, and MMP13), and genes encoding enzymes involved in tobacco metabolism (AHR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1) were evaluated using RT‐qPCR. Compared with the control group (PDL27–30), the replicative senescence group (PDL ≥ 55) showed significantly increased expression of CDKN1A, CDKN2A, IL6, CCL2, MMP1, MMP7, and MMP12, and decreased expression of CXCL2, CXCL5, and MMP13 (Figure 2A). In the DOX‐treated group, the expression of CDKN1A, CDKN2A, CCND1, IL6, IL1B, CCL2, CXCL2, MMP12, AHR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1 was significantly increased, whereas that of MMP7 was decreased compared to that in the control (Figure 2B).

Gene expression changes induced by replicative senescence and DOX treatment in TIG‐1 cells. (A) mRNA levels in TIG‐1 cells were compared between the control (PDL27–30) and replicative senescence (PDL ≥ 55) groups using RT‐qPCR. (B) mRNA levels in TIG‐1 cells were compared between the control and 1 μM DOX‐treated groups using RT‐qPCR. Gene expression was normalized to that of 18S rRNA. Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 5–6). Statistical significance was determined using Welch's t‐test (✻ p < 0.05, ✻✻ p < 0.01, ✻✻✻ p < 0.001).

HTP Is Less Cytotoxic Than RC but Alters Cellular Metabolic Activity

Cytotoxicity was assessed by measuring LDH release into the medium during a single 1‐day exposure experiment (Figure 1A). RC was found to significantly increase LDH release at concentrations above 0.05 mg TPM/mL, whereas HTP caused only slight increases at concentrations above 0.5 mg TPM/mL (Figure 1B). The effects of RC and HTP extracts on the metabolic activity of TIG‐1 cells were evaluated using a WST‐8 assay (Figure 1A). Notably, low concentrations (0.05–0.1 mg TPM/mL) of the RC enhanced cellular metabolic activity, while concentrations ≥ 0.2 mg TPM/mL markedly reduced it in the 5‐day repeated exposure experiment (Figure 1C). These changes were greater than those observed after a single 1‐day exposure to the RC (Figure 1C). In contrast, HTP exposure increased cellular metabolic activity in a concentration‐dependent manner, regardless of single or repeated exposure (Figure 1C).

Gene Expression Changes Caused by RC and HTP Exposure

The effects of exposure to the RC (0.1 mg TPM/mL) and HTP (1 mg TPM/mL) on mRNA expression in TIG‐1 cells were evaluated using RT‐qPCR (Figure 3). A single exposure to the RC increased the expression of MMP1, MMP2, MMP7, AHR, and CYP1A1, and decreased that of CCL2 and CXCL2 (Day 1). Repeated exposure to the RC increased the expression of CDKN2A, IL1B, CXCL5, MMP1, MMP2, MMP7, AHR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1, and decreased that of CCL2, CXCL2, and MMP13 (Day 5). A single exposure to the HTP increased MMP1 expression and decreased CCL2 and CXCL2 expression (Day 1). Repeated exposure to the HTP increased the expression of CDKN2A, IL1B, MMP1, MMP12, AHR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1, and decreased that of MMP13 (Day 5). The expression levels of CDKN1A, CCND1, and IL6 were not significantly affected by either RC or HTP exposure (Figure 3).

Gene expression was normalized to that of 18S rRNA. Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 5–6). Statistical analysis was performed using two‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's test (✻ p < 0.05, ✻✻ p < 0.01 vs. control).

Discussion

This study evaluated the effects of exposure to HTP aerosol extract on cytotoxicity and gene expression in TIG‐1 cells and compared them with those of RC smoke extract.

First, we revealed the effects of replicative senescence and DOX treatment on the expression of senescence‐associated and drug‐metabolizing genes to validate senescence markers in TIG‐1 cells. Consistent with a previous report [24], replicative senescence in TIG‐1 cells, triggered by an increased number of cell divisions, was accompanied by the induction of canonical senescence markers CDKN1A and CDKN2A. Similar induction of these genes was observed following DOX treatment. Regarding inflammation‐related genes, both replicative senescence and DOX treatment increased IL6 and CCL2 expression. In contrast, IL1B expression increased only in the DOX‐treated group, and CXCL2 showed distinct expression patterns between the two conditions. In the replicative senescence group, MMP1, MMP7, and MMP12 were upregulated, whereas MMP13 was downregulated. Among these, only MMP12 showed a similar change in the DOX‐treated group. These results indicate that although replicative and DOX‐induced senescence do not completely overlap with senescence‐associated gene expression patterns, they share several common alterations. In addition, these findings support the notion that senescent fibroblasts acquire a SASP, which may contribute to chronic inflammation and tissue remodeling in the lungs [25]. In contrast, the expression of AHR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1 was not altered during replicative senescence but was increased in the DOX‐treated group. Although these enzymes are not major contributors to the direct metabolism of DOX, their induction may modulate oxidative stress and DNA damage responses, thereby potentially influencing senescence induction [26].

We evaluated the effects of RC and HTP exposure by measuring LDH release as a marker of membrane damage and WST‐8 reduction as an index of metabolic activity. The RC exerted stronger membrane‐damaging effects than the HTP. Furthermore, although both extracts influenced cellular metabolic activity, their effects differed. Repeated exposure to the RC elicited a biphasic response, enhancing cellular metabolic activity at very low concentrations but markedly inhibiting it at higher concentrations. In contrast, exposure to the HTP consistently increased metabolic activity in a dose‐dependent manner after both single and repeated exposures. The increase in WST‐8 reduction at low RC concentrations and after HTP exposure may reflect metabolic activation and adaptive stress responses rather than simply an increase in cell number, consistent with a hormetic effect. Aromatic amines, carbonyls, and phenolic compounds are responsible for the mutagenicity, genotoxicity, and cytotoxicity of tobacco smoke [27]. These harmful components are less abundant in HTPs than in RCs [22, 28]. Our results suggest that HTP aerosols induce metabolic changes without triggering acute cytotoxicity.

In the present study, we analyzed the effects of RCs and HTPs on the expression of senescence‐associated genes. The exposure concentration for the HTP was set at a level 10‐fold higher than that of the RC extract based on the cytotoxicity assay results. Both the RC and HTP upregulated CDKN2A expression after repeated exposure. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that CS exposure increases p16INK4a expression in lung fibroblasts [11]. Our data suggest that HTPs present similar senescence‐related risks through upregulation of CDKN2A. In contrast, the expression of CDKN1A and IL6 was increased in replicative senescence and in the DOX‐treated group but was not altered after exposure to either tobacco product. Notably, IL6 is reportedly associated with CS. We previously demonstrated that exposure to an RC with the same composition as that used in the present study significantly altered IL6 mRNA expression in A549 cells. The fibroblasts used in this study may exhibit a lower inflammatory response than lung epithelial cells [29]. In addition, the downregulation of CCL2 expression was observed after both RC and HTP exposure, in contrast to the changes observed in replicative senescence and the DOX‐treated group. Previous studies have shown that RCs and HTPs increase the expression of inflammatory biomarkers, including CCL2, in monocytes [30]. Furthermore, HTP exposure, similar to RC exposure and replicative senescence, reduced CXCL2 expression. These findings suggest that HTPs can modulate chemokine signaling, although this effect may vary depending on cell type and exposure conditions.

Notably, repeated HTP exposure selectively upregulated MMP12 expression. However, this effect was not observed after RC exposure. In addition, similar to RC exposure, repeated HTP exposure altered the expression levels of MMP1 and MMP13. These changes were comparable to those observed during replicative senescence. MMPs, particularly MMP12, have been strongly implicated in lung tissue destruction and the development of emphysema [31]. Therefore, our findings suggest that repeated exposure to HTPs may alter extracellular matrix remodeling pathways, raising the possibility of long‐term fibrotic or emphysematous effects [32]. The induction of MMPs from HTP exposure may be related to the high humectant content of HTP aerosols, such as propylene glycol and glycerol [15]. Exposure to e‐cigarette aerosols, which contain high levels of propylene glycol and glycerol, increased MMP12 expression in mouse lungs [33]. Moreover, carbonyl compounds, such as acrolein, generated during the heating of propylene glycol and glycerol, have been reported to activate MMPs [34, 35]. These studies support the validity of the results of this study. In addition, although RC exposure was tested at a lower concentration than the HTP, it strongly increased the expression of MMP2 and MMP7, whereas HTP exposure did not affect these genes. This difference suggests that RC and HTP exposure affect the regulation of MMPs via distinct signaling mechanisms.

We previously reported that exposure to RCs and HTPs increases CYP1A1 expression in A549 cells through DNA demethylation [16]. Accordingly, we extended our analyses to include TIG‐1 cells. Exposure to the RC increased the expression of AHR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1 in TIG‐1 cells. Notably, repeated HTP exposure significantly upregulated the expression of these genes. The induction of CYP1A1 and CYP1B1 via AHR signaling enhances the metabolic clearance of xenobiotics and generates reactive intermediates, potentially exacerbating DNA damage and senescence [36, 37]. Thus, frequent exposure to HTPs may increase these risk factors.

A limitation of this study is that only the mRNA expression of senescence‐associated genes was analyzed, whereas protein expression and other indicators were not assessed. In addition, we could not fully exclude the potential effects of contact inhibition. However, the altered genes are supported by literature demonstrating their modulation by tobacco extracts. Clinically, it remains unclear which HTP aerosol constituents reach the lungs; however, the concentrations used in this study are relevant to repeated exposure of the pharyngeal and laryngeal mucosa and may therefore indicate a potential risk to the upper airway [38].

Taken together, although less cytotoxic than RCs, repeated HTP exposure induced CDKN2A and MMP12 expression, similar to replicative senescence and DOX treatment, suggesting a potential contribution to senescence in lung fibroblasts. Further studies are warranted.

Conclusion

Although less cytotoxic than RCs, repeated exposure to the HTP aerosol extract affected the expression of several senescence‐associated and xenobiotic‐metabolizing genes in TIG‐1 cells. HTPs may be involved in the induction of cellular senescence.
