Cancer neuroscience at the brain–body interface
Authors: Jeremy C Borniger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this Outlook, Borniger discusses a view of cancer as a homeostatic challenge and how various cancers disrupt communication between the brain and body, leading to aberrant brain activity and accelerated disease; emphasizes cutting-edge work; and finally, addresses outstanding questions in the field.Keywords: brain–body, physiology, symposium

At the 88th CSHL Symposium on Quantitative Biology, top researchers laid out the current state of affairs and open questions in brain–body physiology. The inclusion of cancer in the program highlighted a change in the way scientists are thinking about cancer and how it interfaces with the host organism. A session on the nascent subdiscipline of “cancer neuroscience” centered several important pathways by which cancer interfaces with the nervous system to regulate systemic physiology and cancer progression. Here, I provide an outlook on areas of inquiry and specific research directions that build on topics and themes discussed at this stellar symposium.

To survive and proliferate, cancer cells need to remodel their local environment to ensure an adequate supply of metabolic fuel and to evade immune destruction (Hanahan and Weinberg 2011; Hanahan and Monje 2023). To accomplish this, cancer cells use a multipronged approach, secreting numerous proteins and metabolites that act in paracrine, juxtacrine, and autocrine signaling loops within the tumor microenvironment (TME) (Anderson and Simon 2020). In this way, solid tumors are not simply composed of random mixtures of cancer cells and extracellular matrix components but include complex and coordinated collections of malignant cells and the supportive frame of the organ—stroma—embedded in a dynamic extracellular matrix (ECM) (Egeblad et al. 2010). Like normal organs, tumors further influence and interface with the entire organism, and it is the systemic effects of cancer that often ultimately kill the patient (e.g., cachexia) (Ferrer et al. 2023). Additionally, common symptoms across different cancers involve the brain even though the cancer does not reside in the brain itself (e.g., sleep disruption in primary breast cancer) (Palesh et al. 2014). The severity of these symptoms often correlates with quality of life and cancer mortality (Sephton et al. 2000; Bower et al. 2005; Palesh et al. 2014), highlighting brain–body interactions as a major factor that must be considered when designing approaches to eliminate cancer (de la Vega 2023).

Until relatively recently, studies on tumor-innervating nerves and associated glia (e.g., Schwann cells) (Deborde et al. 2022) were relegated largely to the periphery of scientific discourse (Monje et al. 2020). This was due in part to the relatively poor integration and communication among cancer biologists, neuroscientists, and clinicians, each of whom are usually siloed into separate departments and use highly field-specific language in their work. Additionally, the cell bodies of neurons that innervate solid tumors in the body are not usually located within the tumor itself but originate in sensory, autonomic, or enteric glia lining the spinal cord and viscera, making histological and molecular profiling of nerves in tumors difficult. Two major lines of work thrust this new subdiscipline of “cancer neuroscience” into the spotlight: the finding that brain tumors (glioblastoma) can form bona fide synapses with normal neurons in the brain (Venkatesh et al. 2019) and that autonomic nerves powerfully regulate prostate and breast cancer growth and metastasis via norepinephrine signaling (Sloan et al. 2010; Magnon et al. 2013). The effect sizes reported in work such as this demanded that cancer biologists take nerves in the TME seriously. On the other side, the advent of powerful tools for monitoring and manipulating neuronal activity (e.g., calcium imaging and optogenetics) in freely moving animals galvanized neuroscientists toward examining how the nervous system interacts with cancer at unprecedented spatiotemporal resolution (Amit et al. 2024).

Sensing cancer in the body

The brain is constantly monitoring critical physiological factors to determine the overall state of the body and maintain systemic homeostasis. It does this primarily via transduction of peripheral signals by sensory or vagal afferent nerves that relay information from superficial tissues and visceral organs, terminating in the brainstem. Sensory nerves express chemoreceptors, osmoreceptors, mechanoreceptors, and acid-sensing channels that detect important cues from the body, including cytokines, tissue deformation, hypoxia, pH, and metabolic “waste” including lactate (Berntson and Khalsa 2021; Francis and Borniger 2021). Additionally, central receptors within discrete brain areas (primarily the hypothalamus and brainstem) further sense humoral signals arriving from circulation to complement bottom-up neuronal signaling, although this process is highly regulated by the blood–brain barrier and circumventricular organs (Ganong 2000). Disruptions in these signaling pathways can cause or accelerate disease. For example, humans lacking the gene encoding leptin (ob) become obese due to the lack of negative feedback control driven by leptin receptors in the hypothalamus (Montague et al. 1997). Low-dose leptin therapy completely returns these patients back to a healthy body weight (Licinio et al. 2004).

In its struggle to remodel the TME, tumors disrupt virtually all signals that sensory/vagal nerves detect, including cytokines (King et al. 2017), metabolites (Apostolova and Pearce 2022), hormones (Artac and Altundag 2012), hypoxia (Gysler and Drapkin 2021), and tissue stiffness/composition (Makale 2007). We know very little about how specific signals in the TME activate sensory/vagal nerves, how these signals propagate to the brain, and how this information interacts with humoral cues arriving from circulation. Intravital imaging and viral tract tracing experiments will shed light on the anatomical and functional connections between cancers in the body and the brain.

Neural integration of cancer-related sensory signals

How are tumor-related physiological signals integrated and “interpreted” by the brain? As cancer initiation, growth, progression, and metastasis usually occur over protracted timescales (i.e., months to years), it is likely that neuronal representations of cancer in the body change dynamically over time. There are specific brain structures that allow for the integration of information over long timescales, especially within the hypothalamus (Levine 2000). For example, neurons that regulate (the progressive feeling of) hunger within the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus express a specialized voltage-gated sodium channel (NaV1.7) that allows them to achieve “near-perfect” integration of neuronal inputs over long timescales, something not possible for other neuronal populations that use conventional “leaky” integration strategies (Branco et al. 2016).

However, we should not assume that tumor-associated changes in brain activity do not occur early in tumor development. Indeed, seminal work from Besedovsky et al. (1985) revealed that neoplastic cells (syngeneic or autochthonous), but not hepatocytes, cause diverse changes in circulating hormone concentrations (which the brain senses) before tumors become palpable. These hormonal changes were already evident 2 days following transplantation of syngeneic tumor cells but not normal cells, suggesting that progressive tumor growth is characterized by worsening endocrine function involving multiple neuroendocrine systems (Holly and Perks 2008). How cancer-related signals arriving from the body interact with other sensory signals (e.g., hunger, thirst, and pain) to modify neuronal function is still unknown.

Outputs from the brain that regulate cancer-relevant physiology

How does the brain reciprocally influence systemic physiology relevant for cancer? Obviously, the pathways and specific molecules involved will differ across cancer types and stages; however, the primary pathways by which the brain signals to the body are via the autonomic nervous system and via neuroendocrine signaling involving hypothalamic–pituitary output (Francis and Borniger 2021). Norepinephrine (noradrenaline) is among the most well-studied neuropeptides released by sympathetic nerves in the TME, where it acts directly on tumor cells, endothelial cells, and immune cells to largely facilitate cancer progression (Creed et al. 2015; Zahalka et al. 2017; Zahalka and Frenette 2020; Devi et al. 2021). Dr. Elizabeth Repasky spoke at the symposium on the critical role sympathetic neuronal signaling plays in transducing cold stress into a protumor signal, reminding us that essentially all our preclinical work is being done below thermoneutrality (Kokolus et al. 2013). This chronic sympathetic activity induced by cold housing conditions can powerfully promote tumor growth and impair responses to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, potentially confounding the interpretation of results. Dr. Erica Sloan expanded on the role that the sympathetic nervous system plays in cancer by demonstrating its pleiotropic influences on tumor vascularization, ECM composition, immune cell polarization, and tumor cell plasticity. She further highlighted recent data from human clinical trials, highlighting the improved outcomes experienced by patients prescribed β-adrenergic receptor blockers (β-blockers) (Hiller et al. 2020). Parasympathetic nerves are also present in some cancers, such as gastric cancer, where acetylcholine drives tumor cells to release nerve growth factor, further promoting neuronal innervation, acetylcholine release, and tumor growth (Hayakawa et al. 2017). However, the role different arms of the autonomic nervous system play in cancer critically depends on the tissue in which the cancer resides, highlighted by the fact that in contrast to gastric cancer, parasympathetic nervous system-derived acetylcholine inhibits pancreatic cancer tumorigenesis (Renz et al. 2018). Sensory nerves also seem to play an active role in the TME. Rather than acting as passive relays that transmit information to the brain, sensory nerves also release neuropeptides (e.g., calcitonin gene-related peptide [CGRP]) that can directly influence antitumor immune responses (Balood et al. 2022).

Neuroendocrine signals controlled by the brain also play a significant role in cancer. Glucocorticoids (e.g., cortisol) are among the most studied given their broadly anti-inflammatory role and pleiotropic effects on metabolism, immune function, and cancer metastasis (Obradović et al. 2019). Glucocorticoid release is coordinated by paraventricular neurons in the hypothalamus that express corticotropin-releasing hormone (i.e., PVNCRH neurons), which projects to the median eminence and pituitary venous system to promote the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) into circulation. ACTH then binds receptors in the adrenal cortex to elicit glucocorticoid release. Cancer patients commonly display aberrant glucocorticoid concentrations, and the severity of these changes correlates with cancer mortality in breast, (Sephton et al. 2000; Abercrombie et al. 2004), lung (Sephton et al. 2013), and ovarian (Schrepf et al. 2015) cancer. Testosterone, insulin, and melatonin are additional humoral signals regulated by the brain, and the circadian rhythm in these proteins was recently shown to powerfully drive diurnal variation in the shedding of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) from the primary tumor, where significantly more CTCs were observed in circulation during the rest phase compared with the active phase (in mice and humans) (Diamantopoulou et al. 2022). We are just at the beginning of understanding how signals from the brain (either neuronal or humoral) become dysregulated in cancer, and how these pathways can be manipulated to block cancer growth.

Looking ahead

There are several open questions in cancer neuroscience that we need to address at the brain–body interface:

What brain regions, cell types, and receptors are essential for relaying cancer-related changes in physiology to the brain?

To intervene in the dialog between cancer and the brain, we need to pinpoint the brain structures and circuitry that go awry during cancer progression. Early hints suggest that critical neuronal populations that respond to cancer-induced changes in physiology reside in the lateral hypothalamus (Borniger et al. 2018), parabrachial nucleus (Campos et al. 2017), and at the symposium, Dr. Bo Li (Sun et al. 2024) introduced the area postrema as an additional key structure. Whole-brain cFos mapping experiments are beginning to identify different brain networks disrupted during cancer progression and during the transition to terminal disease in an unbiased manner (Kaltenecker et al. 2024). The next critical steps will be to “zoom in” to differentially activated brain structures to determine their histochemical and molecular composition (e.g., using single-cell RNA sequencing), their circuit connectivity (e.g., using viral tract tracing), and their firing dynamics (e.g., using electrophysiology) in mice with cancer. Finally, connecting aberrant neuronal activity to downstream changes in physiology and behavior is essential for identifying novel therapeutic targets.

Do different cancers elicit separate responses from the host, and are there commonalities across malignancies?

Obviously, cancers arising from different tissues display diverse phenotypes and produce different chemical messengers to remodel their local environment, although common strategies exist (e.g., production of MMPs to remodel the ECM) (Anderson and Simon 2020). At the present time, we have little information regarding the effects that different types of cancers have on the brain. We need to collaborate with preclinical and clinical oncologists to map the neuronal input to tumors in different tissues of origin (e.g., breast and pancreas) and quantify the key humoral signals released by cancer or stromal cells that influence the brain (e.g., leptin in breast cancer) (Anderson and Simon 2020). Additionally, the effects that tumors have on the brain may be indirect, where tumors disrupt the functioning of other host tissues that then relay that information to the brain. For example, primary lung adenocarcinoma (Masri et al. 2016) and nonmetastatic breast cancer (Borniger et al. 2018) can remodel liver gluconeogenesis/glycolysis, leading to hyperglycemia. There are many subcortical neuronal populations that directly sense circulating glucose (Burdakov et al. 2005). Therefore, identifying humoral factors that transduce information about cancer in the body to the brain will be difficult but essential.

How does host physiology change throughout cancer initiation, growth, metastasis, treatment, and remission?

As tumors grow and spread, the way they interface with the host organism changes. For example, early in tumor development (when tumors have not yet established a blood supply), tumors largely reflect inconsequential dysregulation of molecular and cellular phenotypes (Crosby et al. 2022). Later in development, tumors are often comprised of well oxygenated tissues with hypoxic and acidic cores. This microenvironment coordinates a program that promotes angiogenesis and facilitates the removal of metabolic waste. Later in tumor progression, different proteins that regulate ECM composition become upregulated (e.g., MMP2), allowing cancer cells to move more freely throughout the tumor parenchyma and provide space for new blood vessels to grow. During metastasis, cancer cells often revert to a more stem-like state (epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition), which is again accompanied by a more motile and plastic phenotype, facilitating dissemination (Kalluri and Weinberg 2009). We need to take these different stages/processes into account when examining how the brain senses and responds to cancer in the body.

When and how do cancer-induced changes in physiology reach conscious awareness (e.g., pain and nausea)?

Cancer patients experience a wide array of cognitive and behavioral problems throughout their diagnosis, treatment, surgery, and remission journey. What is the “trigger” that pushes cancer-induced changes in physiology to conscious awareness? As most of the work so far has focused on subcortical systems regulating arousal (Borniger et al. 2018), malaise (Campos et al. 2017), and anorexia (Sun et al. 2024) in the context of cancer, we still know very little about the consequences this dysfunction has on higher-order brain structures that regulate executive and cognitive functions (e.g., the cortex). Most of the time, we are not aware of the automatic processes taking place in our bodies. Upon sufficient stimulation, however, we become consciously aware of these processes, feeling nausea, hunger, pain, or thirst. How this transition between subconscious and conscious awareness occurs in cancer is unknown but likely involves interoceptive circuits (e.g., insula) (Berntson and Khalsa 2021) that were recently shown to store information about prior immune challenges (Koren et al. 2021).

How does the brain influence the (pre)metastatic niche and emergence from dormancy?

To metastasize, cancer cells must traverse through several difficult steps comprising the “metastatic cascade” (Klein 2008). An interesting aspect of this cascade is that cancers often remodel the “soil” at the metastatic site prior to the establishment of full-blown metastasis (establishing a premetastatic niche) (Kaplan et al. 2006). This helps ensure that when cancer cells arrive, they can settle in a less hostile environment and more readily establish macrometastases. We know essentially nothing about how the brain (and the nervous system in general) regulates the premetastatic niche. However, recent work demonstrated that psychological stress (which is transduced by the brain) can promote diverse changes in normal lung tissue, facilitating metastatic seeding of breast cancer (He et al. 2024). Another relatively poorly understood aspect of cancer stems from the clinical finding that cancers can recur years or even decades following surgical resection of the primary tumor. This is largely due to cancer cells entering a state of quiescence (cellular dormancy) induced by changes in ECM composition, tissue oxygenation, and endoplasmic reticulum stress (Endo and Inoue 2019). These dormant cancer cells are often resistant to various cancer therapies, making them one of the biggest hurdles to overcome in eliminating cancer (Aguirre-Ghiso 2007).

How long do cancer-induced changes in brain activity last following tumor removal?

Upon primary tumor resection or curative therapy, do aberrantly active brain circuits return to their “precancer” state? How long does this take (weeks…years?), and what factors modify this process? In breast cancer, we know that fatigue and sleep disruption can persist long after tumor resection and cessation of cancer treatment (Ruiz-Casado et al. 2021); however, we do not know the mechanisms underlying this long-lasting change in brain function. Animal models of cancer survivorship (Otto et al. 2022) will be valuable in answering these questions. Finally, I believe that the benefits of investigating brain–body cross-talk in cancer will extend beyond tangible outcomes for treating cancer, as we will learn important aspects about how cancer-derived signals influence brain function to disrupt systemic physiology and behavior. It is likely that the mechanisms that drive these changes are not unique to cancer and may occur in other diseases (e.g., autoimmunity) and contexts.
