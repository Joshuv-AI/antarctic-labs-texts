Reliability of data-driven versus expert-driven composite indicators in between-hospital comparisons on quality of oesophagogastric cancer surgery: a population-based retrospective cohort study
Authors: Margrietha van der Linde, Frank Eijkenaar, Maurits R Visser, Bas PL Wijnhoven, Hester F Lingsma, Martijn AH Oude Voshaar, Suzanne S Gisbertz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo construct a data-driven composite from (a subset of) currently used quality indicators for oesophagogastric cancer surgery and to evaluate whether this approach enhances the reliability of between-hospital comparisons on outcome relative to the expert-driven composite indicator ‘textbook outcome (TO)’.DesignIn this retrospective cohort study, we applied Item Response Theory (IRT) to construct a data-driven continuous composite indicator reflecting a single latent variable—the quality of surgical care—and estimated latent variable scores for all individual patients. Reliability was compared between the expert-driven (TO) and data-driven (IRT) composite indicators.SettingAll Dutch hospitals providing oesophagogastric cancer surgery.ParticipantsAll patients who underwent oesophagectomy (n=3588) or gastrectomy (n=1782) between 2018 and 2022 as registered in the Dutch Upper GI Cancer Audit (DUCA).Primary and secondary outcome measuresWe evaluated the reliability of between-hospital comparisons using ‘rankability’, which quantifies the proportion of observed variation in indicator scores between hospitals not attributable to chance.ResultsSeven out of 15 quality indicators were included in the IRT composite indicator. Most of the patients were assigned the artificial maximum of the continuous quality score (ie, ceiling effect), resulting in similar average hospital scores. Relative to TO, rankability increased when using the IRT composite for oesophagectomy (57% vs 41%) but declined for gastrectomy (38% vs 47%).ConclusionsThe selected seven quality indicators for oesophageal and gastric cancer surgery represent a single latent variable but are not yet optimal for differentiating surgical care quality due to ceiling effects. Despite using fewer indicators, the continuous IRT score showed a promising increase in rankability for oesophagectomy, suggesting that data-driven composite indicators may enhance hospital benchmarking reliability.

Introduction

Hospital comparisons on quality of care across medical conditions have gained significant attention worldwide.1 This interest stems from the potential to drive quality improvement, provide patients with informed choices, support contracting by insurers and aid regulatory evaluations.25 Assessing hospital performance, however, is challenging because quality of care cannot be directly observed. Instead, it is commonly assessed using indicators intended to reflect quality as best as possible.6 To enable fair comparisons, it is important that indicator scores reflect true differences in performance between hospitals rather than noise.7

Reliability, or the consistency of measurements, applies at both the patient and hospital level. Patient-level precision directly impacts hospital-level reliability, as hospital scores are based on aggregated patient data. Previous research has shown that between-hospital comparisons frequently lack reliability,79 primarily due to the use of indicators with low event rates and small patient volumes in hospitals. A proposed solution to enhance reliability is data aggregation, achieved either by analysing data across longer periods (eg, multiple years) or by combining multiple individual indicators into composite measures.9 10

Composite indicators can be constructed using two main approaches: expert-driven and data-driven. Expert-driven approaches are widely used.1113 They rely on domain knowledge to select and weigh individual indicators, ensuring content validity by aligning the indicator with what it aims to measure. An example is textbook outcome (TO), an all-or-none score requiring the achievement of all expert-selected component indicators for an ideal surgical outcome.1416 In contrast, data-driven methods use statistical techniques to select and aggregate indicators based on the underlying data structure. These methods can be grounded in a reflective measurement model, where the indicators reflect an underlying unobserved (ie, latent) construct.17 18 Because these indicators are presumed to measure the same latent variable, they tend to be inherently positively correlated. This correlation enhances reliability by amplifying the overall signal as multiple indicators are combined, while noise diminishes through aggregation.

Item response theory (IRT) is an example of a data-driven approach that can be used to model the relationship between observed data and latent variables, making it potentially suitable for constructing composite indicators.19 However, Normand et al20 caution that ceiling effects in individual indicator scores can limit the ability of composites to effectively differentiate hospital quality. This finding raises questions about the applicability of data-driven methods to indicators not originally designed to measure the latent variable surgical quality.

This study has two aims. First, to determine whether (a subset of) currently used quality indicators for oesophageal and gastric cancer resections (from now on referred to as oesophagectomy and gastrectomy) reflect a single latent variable (ie, the quality of surgical care) by constructing a data-driven composite indicator using an IRT framework. Second, we evaluated whether this data-driven approach improves the reliability of between-hospital comparisons compared with the expert-driven approach (ie, TO).

Methods

This nationwide cohort study was conducted and reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies.21

Data

We included all patients who underwent oesophagectomy or gastrectomy for cancer in the Netherlands between 2018 and 2022, as registered in the Dutch Upper Gastrointestinal Cancer Audit (DUCA). The DUCA measures and biennially publishes quality indicator scores of individual hospitals, aiming to improve the quality of care of oesophagogastric cancer surgery in the Netherlands.22 Study approval was given by the DUCA scientific committee (DUCA-2022–169).

Outcomes

In 2017, the textbook outcome (TO) for oesophagectomy and gastrectomy was developed through expert consultation and has been used ever since.14 TO is achieved when patients fulfil all 10 component indicators: radical resection according to the surgeon at the end of surgery, no intraoperative complication, microscopically radical resection (R0), ≥15 lymph nodes (LN) retrieved and examined, no severe postoperative complications (≥Clavien-Dindo (CD) grade IIIa),23 no reintervention, no readmission to intensive care unit (ICU) or medium-care unit, no prolonged hospital stay (≤21 days), no postoperative 30-day/in-hospital mortality and no readmission after discharge from hospital within 30 days.14

For the construction of the data-driven composite using IRT, we considered the following available indicators: all TO component indicators, minimally invasive procedure, pulmonary complications, anastomotic leakage, severe postoperative complications which require ICU admission (≥CD grade IVa),23 prolonged length of ICU stay (>2 days) and for oesophageal cancer specifically, transthoracic resection. We thus considered 15 potential indicators for gastrectomy and 16 potential indicators for oesophagectomy.

All considered quality indicators were binary and were recoded such that a value of 1 indicated a positive signal of patients’ received quality, while 0 indicated otherwise. As we could not calculate TO for patients with missing outcomes, for both TO and the IRT-constructed composite, we listwise deleted patients with missing outcomes (ie, removing all patients with any missing outcome) to ensure meaningful comparisons of reliability between the two composites.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R 4.3.2 using the ‘mokken’, ‘mirt’, ‘rms’ and ‘lme4’ packages.

Data-driven composite indicator

We used item response theory (IRT) for constructing a data-driven composite indicator by modelling the relationship between a presumed latent variable (in this case defined as the quality of surgical care) and scores on individual quality indicators (in the context of IRT referred to as items).24 The relationship between the continuous latent variable and the indicator scores can be modelled separately for each indicator using a two-parameter cumulative logistic (2-PL) function, known as the item characteristic curve.25 This curve is described by two parameters. The first parameter, a, represents the slope and indicates how effectively an indicator discriminates between individual patients based on the quality of surgical care delivered. To develop a precise measure, it is necessary to include indicators with a large slope and thus high discrimination. The second parameter, b, reflects the indicator’s location on the underlying continuum (ie, latent variable scale). Higher b-values locate the indicator towards the higher end of the underlying latent variable (ie, higher quality of surgical care). In an IRT model, measurement is most precise when the indicator locations match the true distribution of patients on the latent continuum.

We started with identifying (a subset of) indicators that demonstrated a monotonic relation with the latent variable through nonparametric analysis using the mokken package.26 Aiming to develop a composite indicator that is applicable to both types of resections, we fitted a multiple group 2-PL model in which we fixed the mean and variance for the scale of the largest group (ie, oesophagectomy group), but estimated these for the gastrectomy group.27 Additionally, we fixed the values of a- and b-parameters across both groups (ie, resection types). We calculated residual correlations for all indicators in the model to detect potential local dependence—defined as statistical independence between indicators—and excluded indicators with a correlation exceeding 0.2.

We assessed the presence of differential item functioning (DIF), which occurs when oesophagectomy and gastrectomy patients have the same latent variable scores, but these composite scores exhibit different individual indicator scores.27 To observe DIF, we used visual inspection of the empirical plots of the mirt package, which compare observed proportions with the model’s predicted probabilities across the latent continuum. Indicators that showed a poor fit in the empirical plot for one of the resection types were adjusted by relaxing the constraints of either the a, b or both parameters, selecting the approach that best improved the fit.

We assessed whether the indicators in the final IRT model indeed collectively represent a single latent variable through an analysis of unidimensionality. This involved evaluating the fit of the final IRT model using the root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI). A good fit was defined as an RMSEA below 0.05 and CFI/TLI values above 0.95.2830

Using weighted likelihood estimation, we estimated latent quality scores for each individual patient from the final IRT model, separately for oesophageal and gastric cancer resections.31 These scores represent each patient’s position on the underlying latent continuum of surgical care quality.

Patient-level reliability

In our IRT model, each individual indicator is treated as an item of an instrument measuring the quality of surgical care. The rate at which the probability of achieving an individual indicator varies with the underlying quality level reveals how effectively that indicator differentiates between quality of surgical care. This rate of change—the slope of the item characteristic curve—reflects its sensitivity to variations in care quality. A steep slope indicates that even small differences in care quality result in significant changes in the probability of achieving the individual indicator, thereby providing a large amount of information. Test information in a 2PL model is the sum of the information provided by each indicator, reflecting the precision with which the test can estimate the latent trait at various levels.

Instruments developed using IRT are most informative at locations along the latent continuum where the items (ie, indicators) are located. To determine the range of surgical quality levels where the set of selected indicators provides the most discriminative information, we calculated the test information function by summing the information provided by each individual indicator.25 A higher value of the test information function indicates greater patient-level measurement precision at that specific location on the latent continuum. Separately for both types of resections, we calculated rankability for the expert-driven composite TO, the continuous and all-or-none data-driven constructed IRT composites. We also created an all-or-none score from the selected indicators in the final IRT model (like TO but using a different subset of indicators) to evaluate the rankability of this subset of indicators both as a binary (all-or-none score) and a continuous measure (IRT score). Additionally, the rankabilities of all individual indicators considered for the development of the IRT score were evaluated. Rankability was assessed over the two most recent years, in line with public reporting practice in the Netherlands as well as a sensitivity analysis over 5 years to yield more robust results.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Study population

From 2018 to 2022, 3588 patients with oesophageal cancer and 1782 patients with gastric cancer underwent a surgical resection in the Netherlands including 1414 and 650 procedures performed between 2021 and 2022, respectively (table 1; Online supplemental table 1). The listwise deletion of patients with missing outcomes resulted in the exclusion of 488 (8%) patients in total: 245 (6%) patients that underwent oesophagectomy and 243 (12%) patients that underwent gastrectomy. The event rates of the individual quality indicators varied between 74% and 99% for oesophagectomy and between 82% and 97% for gastrectomy. For the expert-based composite TO, the event rate was 57% and 60% for oesophagectomy and gastrectomy, respectively. The greatest variation among hospitals was observed for transthoracic oesophagectomy (IQR 27%) and minimally invasive gastrectomy (IQR 19%).

Data-driven composite indicator

For oesophagectomy, the expected indicator scores for 9 of the 16 indicators were monotonically increasing with the latent variable score (online supplemental figure 1): no severe postoperative complications (≥CD grade IIIa), no reintervention, no readmission to ICU or medium-care unit, no prolonged hospital stay (≤21 days), no postoperative 30-day/in-hospital mortality, pulmonary complications, anastomotic leakage, severe postoperative complications which require ICU admission (≥CD grade IVa) and prolonged length of ICU stay (>2 days). For gastrectomy, the results were similar, except that the indicator ‘no 30-day readmission’ was monotonically increasing with the latent variable score (online supplemental figure 2). As this relation did not seem strong, and to ensure a comparable measurement instrument for the two cancer resections, we moved forward with the nine indicators that satisfied the monotonicity assumption for both types of resections. In the 9-item model, the indicators ‘no severe complications requiring reintervention’ and ‘no severe complications requiring ICU admission’ showed extreme slopes and high residual correlations (>|0.2|), suggesting local dependence. This means that these two items may be capturing overlapping information or are too closely related, which violates the assumption that item responses should be independent after accounting for the underlying latent variable. Therefore, these indicators were excluded from the final IRT model. We observed differential item functioning (DIF) for the indicators ‘no 30-day mortality’, ‘no reintervention’ and ‘no prolonged ICU stay’ (online supplemental figures 3 and 4). Item fit improved most when fixation of the b-parameter for these items was relaxed (online supplemental table 2).

The final 7-item IRT model demonstrated good fit, with a root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA) of 0.044 (95% CI 0.038–0.051), and comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) values of 0.97. These findings suggest that these indicators collectively measure a single latent variable. The relationship between the latent variable (quality of surgical care) and the indicators was similar for both types of resection (figure 1).

Patient-level reliability

The latent quality score distributions for both oesophagectomy and gastrectomy revealed that most patients had quality-of-care scores concentrated at the higher end of the scale, indicating a clear ceiling effect (figure 2). These patients were all assigned the artificial maximum of the quality score (ie, ‘perfect’ quality of care delivered). The latent quality score could be reliably measured (at the patient level) for 40% of oesophagectomy patients and for 24% of gastrectomy patients, as the SE of measurement did not exceed 0.447 (equivalent to r<0.8). These results were similar in the 5-year period (online supplemental figure 5).

For oesophagectomy, reliable measurement of the individual quality score (SE<0.447) was achieved for 564 patients (40%), represented by the dark bars in the histogram. For gastrectomy, reliable measurement was observed for 156 patients (24%).

Hospital-level reliability: rankability

Although TO outperformed the rankability of most individual indicators, its rankability for both types of resections was poor with over 50% of the observed between-hospital variation in TO being noise, even when evaluated over a 5-year period (table 2; online supplemental table 4). For oesophagectomy, rankability improved substantially (relative to TO) when using the continuous IRT score (36% vs 52%). For gastrectomy, the rankability of the IRT quality score was lower than that of TO (37% vs 46%). The all-or-none score constructed from the seven indicators of final IRT model demonstrated an even higher rankability for oesophagectomy (67%, increasing up to 85% when based on 5 years of data (online supplemental table 3)) but was similar for gastrectomy.

Discussion

Main findings

Our study has three main findings. First, our results suggest that the subset of quality indicators in our IRT model reflect a single latent variable, which can be interpreted as the ‘quality of surgical care’. Second, we found a substantial misalignment between these indicators and the quality of care delivered to patients: a substantial proportion of patients were assigned the artificial maximum of the latent quality score. This means that there is insufficient information in the data (ie, no indicators) to distinguish among them. Finally, although patient-level measurement was reliable for only 40% of oesophagectomy patients, the use of the data-driven composite showed a promising improvement in rankability (ie, hospital-level reliability) relative to the expert-driven composite TO. However, for gastrectomy, patient-level reliability was achieved in only 24% of patients, and rankability remained poor. Below, these three findings are discussed, along with their implications.

Latent variable: quality of surgical care

The subset of quality included in our final IRT model seems to capture the common underlying dimension of surgical care quality (ie, the latent variable), as evidenced by the strong model fit. Furthermore, the consistent relationship between these indicators and the latent variable across both types of surgery supports the validity of our model. This consistency indicates that the selected indicators measure the underlying construct of surgical care quality, regardless of the specific type of surgery.

Patient-level reliability

The indicators in our IRT model lacked sufficient discriminative ability to differentiate between levels of surgical quality, resulting in high and uniform quality scores (ie, ceiling effects) and consequently minimal variation in average hospital scores. This ‘misalignment’ between the indicators that reflect the underlying latent variable and the actual quality of care delivered is more pronounced for gastrectomy, which is likely due to the more extreme event rates observed in the individual indicators for this procedure (ie, most patients almost reach the maximum on the individual indicators). Also, a substantially lower proportion of patients for whom reliable patient-level measurement was achieved for gastrectomy (24%) versus oesophagectomy (40%). These findings confirm those of Normand et al20 as described in the introduction. There are two possible explanations for the low variation between hospitals: either a true lack of quality differences between hospitals (uniformly high standards of care, suggesting the quality indicators may have served their purpose), or that current indicators fail to capture these due to ceiling effects.

Reliability of hospital comparisons: rankability

We found that the rankability of TO was poor, with over half of the observed between-hospital variation in TO attributable to noise. Given that these scores are publicly reported, our finding is particularly relevant as low reliability may lead to misinterpretations and incorrect conclusions by various stakeholders. Although we were not yet able to fully assess the rankability of the IRT-constructed continuous composite (due to the similar hospital scores), the results are promising.

We showed that comparing hospitals using a composite of seven indicators achieved equal or higher reliability than using the expert-driven composite consisting of 10 indicators, supporting the argument for eliminating indicators to reduce the measurement burden.36 Over the 5-year period, the rankability for oesophagectomy even reached 78% (compared with 47% for TO), something that, to our knowledge, has not been reported before for surgical outcome indicators.9 37 38 In contrast, for gastrectomy, TO outperformed the IRT-constructed quality score in rankability when using 2 years of data, while the two composites demonstrated similar rankability over the 5-year period.

There are two main explanations for only finding an increase in rankability for oesophagectomy. First, for gastrectomy, the IRT composite exhibits less variation between hospitals than for oesophagectomy. Second, the individual indicators generally have more extreme event rates for gastrectomy. Consequently, reliable individual quality scores could be measured for only 24% of gastrectomy patients compared with 40% of oesophagectomy patients.

For oesophagectomy, the IRT-based 7-item all-or-none score showed an even higher rankability compared with the continuous IRT composite, which is likely because all-or-none scores inherently increase between-hospital variation. In addition to its potentially easier interpretation, this indicator may serve as a more reliable indicator for hospital performance compared with TO.

Expert-driven versus data-driven

Although our data-driven approach showed promising results with regards to the reliability of between-hospital comparisons, it does narrow the definition of the composite indicator (compared with TO) which may have important implications for its clinical application. Ultimately, the key challenge in constructing composite quality indicators is to improve reliability without sacrificing content validity. For example, although a higher LN yield is typically seen as a marker of thorough surgery, it may also be associated with an increased risk of having postoperative complications. Consequently, while each indicator individually might have a positive relation with surgical quality, their combination in a composite indicator can paradoxically reduce reliability.34

For both resection types, TO is derived from clinical expertise and consensus, with the primary aim of ensuring complete tumour resection while minimising patient harm. Typically, TO encompasses ten clinically meaningful quality indicators, each representing a facet of optimal surgical care. Recent studies, however, have underscored considerable heterogeneity in the TO definitions.39 40 Moreover, our results show that TO used in the Netherlands is not reliable for between-hospital comparisons of oesophagogastric cancer surgery and that extending the reporting period does not affect its reliability. Ideally, the two approaches (eg, statistical insights combined with clinical judgement) are combined to create reliable and valid quality indicators.

Strengths and limitations

We used a nationwide cohort including all Dutch hospitals performing oesophagogastric cancer surgery and applied advanced statistical methods that are well developed in psychometrics research to the context of quality of care. However, our study has some limitations. First, we listwise excluded patients with any missing outcome data, which led to the exclusion of 8% of patients in the total cohort. Second, it is important to confirm that the identified latent variable and the associated indicator relationships are generalisable to different populations and care contexts, especially since our analysis only included indicators currently used in the Netherlands. Model validation in other datasets can achieve this by evaluating whether the structure and relationships remain consistent. Such validation would enhance the robustness and applicability in different clinical settings. Third, when comparing quality of care between hospitals, it is important to consider not only short-term outcomes but also long-term outcomes that are meaningful to patients, such as quality of life and functional recovery. Besides being directly meaningful for patients and society, they may prove to be more sensitive to quality differences between hospitals. From a statistical perspective, this may be what we need to reliably measure differences; however, these outcomes may be more influenced by case mix. In this study, we did not have data on such outcomes, as most surgical registries focus on short-term outcomes.

Practical implications

In addition to the need for further research to validate the IRT score for broader applicability, our results have two specific implications for (clinical) practice. First, many current DUCA indicators show ceiling effects, making it difficult to detect meaningful differences between hospitals. An IRT analysis can clarify which indicators provide useful information and should be retained, which contribute little and may warrant revision or limited external transparency, and where additional indicators are needed along the underlying quality score. These data-driven insights, together with clinical judgement, can support experts in developing an indicator set that remains representative of surgical care while allowing more reliable hospital comparisons. A second implication is that the currently observed low between-hospital variation may be difficult to interpret: it could reflect genuinely uniform quality of care, but it might also result from ceiling effects that mask existing differences. By further developing an IRT-based scale as described above, ceiling effects can be reduced and a more informative composite created. This would allow more definitive conclusions about the presence or absence of meaningful differences in quality of care between hospitals, provided that the resulting IRT score represents a comprehensive measure of surgical quality.

Conclusion

The subset of quality indicators for oesophagectomy and gastrectomy selected using an IRT framework represents a single latent variable, interpreted as the 'quality of surgical care'. However, the included indicators do not effectively differentiate the quality of surgical care for patients undergoing oesophagectomy, as most of the patients were assigned the artificial maximum score. Although this artificial ceiling effect prevents reliable measurement at individual patient level, data-driven composite indicators are promising for more reliable hospital benchmarking.
