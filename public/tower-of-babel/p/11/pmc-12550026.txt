Association of intermediate monocytes with dengue severity among the pediatric population of Odisha, India
Authors: Manoj Kumar Dash, Sagnika Samal, Shailesh Rout, Rajesh Kumar Bhola, Janeka Gartia, Ishani Saha, Chinmay Kumar Behera, Hamida Hamdi, Mahesh Chandra Sahu, Biswadeep Das
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dengue virus (DENV) infection in children exhibits varied clinical presentations, wherein the role of monocytes is important in the innate immune response. In this study, laboratory-confirmed DENV pediatric patients (n = 120), with DENV-2 infection, were categorized into dengue fever (DF), dengue with warning signs (DWS) and severe dengue (SD) were assessed for monocyte subpopulation analysis using immunophenotyping involving CD14 and CD16 host-surface markers. Molecular docking was performed using HADDOCK 2.4 to analyze the interactions between CD14, CD16 and DENV envelope and capsid proteins. Among the cases, 84 (70%) were classified as DF and 36 (30%) as DWS & SD. Hematological and biochemical parameters indicated that thrombocytopenia and elevated hematocrit (> 40%) were significantly more common in DWS & SD, with markedly elevated liver enzymes (ALT and AST) in severe cases. Classical monocytes (CM-CD14++ CD16−) constituted 72.51% and 66.25% of the monocyte population in DF and DWS & SD cases, respectively. Intermediate monocytes (IM-CD14+ CD16+) comprised 9.89% and 30.86% in DF and DWS & SD cases, respectively. Non-classical monocytes (NCM-CD14+ CD16++) comprised 5.75% and 8.12% in DWS & SD and DF cases, respectively. In silico analysis revealed host CD16 and CD14 exhibited potential interactions with DENV capsid and envelope proteins, with binding energies − 8.9, − 10.1, − 8.6, and − 11.1 kcal/mol, respectively. IM was significantly increased in DWS & SD compared to DF (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that IM could act as host markers of DENV severity in children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21089-0.

Introduction

Dengue represents a significant global public health challenge and is among the most prevalent vector-borne diseases with substantial morbidity and mortality in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide1,2. Dengue virus (DENV) infection manifests in a broad spectrum of clinical presentations, such as dengue fever (DF), dengue with warning signs (DWS), and severe dengue (SD) and comprises of four genetically diverse serotypes (DENV-1,2,3, and 4)3. DENV is a positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus with an 11 kb genome and one open reading frame. This is translated into a polyprotein consisting of three structural proteins (envelope (E), membrane precursor (prM, which is cleaved to M), and capsid (C)) and seven non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2A, NS2B, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, and NS5)4. During dengue infection, virions can target host cells such as monocytes/macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), B cells, T cells, basophil/mast cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and hepatocytes via several viral receptors5. The DENV envelope (E) protein interacts with host cell receptors to facilitate the fusion of viral and endosomal membranes, initiating infection, particularly with the assistance of DII and DIII domains. The DIII domain is primarily involved in receptor binding, initiating infection6. Meanwhile, the DENV capsid protein interacts with various cytoplasmic proteins, influencing apoptosis and immune responses7.

Aberrant immunological changes, including immune cell activation, complement activation, cytokine production, inflammatory mediator production, antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) of infection, and autoimmunity, have been postulated to participate in the immunopathogenesis of dengue8,9. The clinical manifestations of dengue are multifactorial, driven by a complex interplay of viral and host factors, including viral serotypes, host immune status, genetic composition, gender, and age. Monocytes have been identified as a key player in this complex puzzle10,11. Monocytes are key components of innate immune system and serve as the first line of defense during host’s response to DENV infection12. Human blood monocytes can be categorized on basis of CD14, membrane receptor for lipopolysaccharide, and CD16, isoform fragment crystallization (Fc) gamma receptor III-A (FcϒRIII-A) of low affinity receptor for Fc region of IgG antibodies12. Three distinct subpopulations of monocytes include CD14++CD16− classical monocytes, CD14++CD16+ (intermediate), and CD14+CD16++ (non-classical) monocytes12. Among healthy individuals, classical monocytes (CM) are most prevalent (80–90%), followed by NCM (3–10%), and intermediate monocytes (IM) (3–8%)13. A common stem cell precursor in the bone marrow first produces CM, which enter the circulation and either undergoes apoptosis within 1–2 days or sequentially transforms into IM and NCM14. The NCM fraction has the longest longevity, being active for around 7 days, whereas the IM subset lasts for about 4–5 days. Using in vitro models, numerous investigations have attempted to clarify the functional properties of these three subsets. However, it is widely known that monocytes’ innate plasticity, which allows for quick changes in phenotype and function upon exposure to tissue culture plastic, can complicate such functional experiments15,16. In various physiological and pathological conditions, this differentiation pathway contributes to understanding their functional diversity and immune responses13. Several studies demonstrated that during DENV infection, chemokine receptors exhibited differential expression patterns that help regulate migration and localization of monocyte subsets17. In pediatric dengue patients, CM and IM expressed elevated levels of chemokine receptors (CCR) 2 and CCR 5, in addition to reduced expression of CX3 chemokine receptor 1 (CX3CR1)12,17–19. In contrast, elevated CX3CR1 expression and diminished CCR2 and CCR5 levels were observed in NCM12,17–19. During SD, monocytes have been implicated in a cascade of pathogenic events, including enhanced viral replication, cytokine storm, and ADE, leading to aggravated disease severity20. Moreover, studies suggest that monocytes activated by the dengue virus can upregulate CD16 expression and support the growth and differentiation of dengue-specific antibody-secreting cells21. However, the interactions between DENV surface proteins and monocyte markers CD14 and CD16 remain underexplored. Elucidating the role of monocytes in the pathogenesis of SD in pediatric patients and understanding the interaction of monocyte markers CD14 and CD16 with the virus could provide valuable insights into host-virus dynamics. This knowledge may pave the way for developing treatment strategies to mitigate SD manifestations in the pediatric population.

Results

Clinico-serological characterization of patients

The study involved a total of (n = 120) pediatric patients, all individuals tested positive for either DENV-NS1 ELISA or DENV-IgM ELISA, confirming a diagnosis of acute dengue viral infection (DENV). DENV-IgG was not detected in any patient, suggesting primary DENV infection. Molecular analysis specifically revealed that all patients were infected with DENV-2. Out of 120 confirmed cases of dengue, 84 patients (70%) were diagnosed with DF, while the remaining 36 patients (30%) were diagnosed with more severe forms, DWS & SD. Among the patients, the age group distribution was nearly equal, with 49.2% aged 2–6 years and 50.8% aged 7–16 years, showing no significant association with disease severity. Males constituted 52.5% and females 47.5%, again with no significant gender-based differences. However, thrombocytopenia and elevated hematocrit levels (> 40%) were both significantly more frequent in DWS & SD patients compared to DF cases (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Biochemical parameters also demonstrated marked differences. Hemoglobin levels were slightly lower in DWS & SD patients (10.62 ± 1.42 g/dL) compared to DF (11.45 ± 1.10 g/dL), though the difference was not statistically significant. Liver enzymes showed significant elevation in severe cases, with ALT levels at 251.3 ± 159.1 IU/L and AST at 350.2 ± 271.4 IU/L in DWS & SD, compared to 84.2 ± 54.5 IU/L and 95.6 ± 51.2 IU/L, respectively in DF patients (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

Flow cytometric and western blot analysis

Flow cytometric analyses of monocyte subsets revealed significant alteration in the proportions of CM, IM, and NCM across the DF, DWS & SD patients (Fig. 1). CM was determined as the predominant monocyte subset in all patient groups. In patients diagnosed with DF, CM constituted 72.51% of the total monocyte population. However, in the DWS & SD cases, CM proportion decreased slightly to 66.25%, indicating subtle differences in the CM population in both groups. IM represented only 9.89% of the total monocyte population in DF cases, but exhibited a significant increase to 30.86% in DWS & SD (F = 197.4, p < 0.05, one-way ANOVA test). Additionally, NCM constituted the smallest proportion of monocyte subsets in all patient groups. In DF cases, NCM accounted for 8.12% of the total monocyte population. However, in the DWS & SD cases, NCM proportion decreased moderately to 5.75% (Fig. 2). CD14 and CD16 protein bands (~ 50–70 kDa) were detected using specific antibodies by western blot analysis (Fig. 3).

Molecular docking analysis

To evaluate the molecular interactions between CD14/CD16 and DENV structural proteins (E and C proteins), we performed protein-protein docking using HADDOCK 2.4. The models generated in the docking were ranked using the HADDOCK score (a weighted sum of several energy terms such as van der Waals energy, electrostatic energy, and desolvation energy). The top-ranked models were subsequently clustered according to structural similarity. The highest-ranked cluster, consisting of 27 models from CD16-DENV Capsid docking, exhibited an average HADDOCK score of − 64.2 ± 6.2, implying a potential interaction (Table 3) The top model from this cluster, had a HADDOCK score of − 74.54 (van der Waals energy: − 65.47 kcal/mol, electrostatic energy: − 248.41 kcal/mol, desolvation energy: 2.52 kcal/mol), and revealed a buried surface area of 2131.36 Å2. Binding affinity calculations using PRODIGY estimated a ΔG of − 8.9 kcal/mol. Furthermore, analysis of the interactions by PDBsum identified 17 hydrogen bonding interactions (11.25%), 1 salt bridge (0.6%) and 133 non-bonded interactions (88%). Key residues implicated in the hydrogen bond interactions are Lys114, Asn115, Asn136, Asp138, Pro142 from CD16 and Arg63, Asn74, Arg80, Arg81 from DENV Capsid. Additionally, Glu103 and Arg63 from CD16 and DENV capsid, respectively mediate the salt bridge interactions (Table 4; Fig. 4).

Discussion

Dengue severity outcomes could be linked to several factors, which include viral load, immune activation, host genetic predisposition, nutritional status, and the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses22–24. Dengue disease management can be complemented by targeting monocyte subpopulations12,19 in the absence of specific antiviral drugs or vaccines. Monocytes play a crucial role in modulating the immune response during primary DENV infection, particularly in pediatric patients who possess a developing immune system with limited adaptive immunity24,25. The association between monocyte subpopulations and dengue severity was analyzed among pediatric populations in Odisha, India and underscored a significant correlation between elevated CD14++CD16+ IM subset in DWS & SD compared to DF. IM (CD14 + CD16+) population was found to be elevated, exceeding the normal physiological range of 3–8% reported in healthy individuals26,27 This might be attributed to baseline activation during initial DENV infection. Several studies have discussed the role of monocyte subsets in healthy versus dengue cases17,21; however, our study specifically examined monocyte populations in DF and SD among pediatric patients in Odisha, India. This is the first study depicting the distribution and plausible characterization of monocytes in the DENV-2 infected pediatric population of Odisha, India that brings a regional and clinical perspective to the existing knowledge.

Notably, DENV envelope protein primarily interacts with various host receptors to facilitate viral entry and modulate the immune response. Through molecular docking, we were able to gain insights into the predictive interactions between monocyte immune markers CD14 and CD16 and DENV surface proteins. Docking analysis predicted energetically potential interactions between CD16 and both DENV envelope and capsid proteins. Similarly, potential interactions were observed for CD14 with these viral proteins. In silico analysis indicated that CD14 and CD16 might participate in virus-host interactions, potentially contributing to viral entry into monocytes. Such interactions might influence monocyte activation and result in exacerbated inflammatory response. Further studies could be performed using approaches such as site-directed mutagenesis, blocking assays, and co-immunoprecipitation, which may help to assess these predictions. LPS receptor, CD14, has previously been identified as a key player in DENV binding to monocytes and macrophages28. However, when LPS was added before infection in cultured cells, DENV replication was inhibited independently of the LPS-induced inflammatory response. Notably, antibodies against CD14 failed to reduce viral infection, although blocking CD14 receptor completely abolished the inhibitory effect of LPS on DENV infection. This suggests that DENV is dependent on a cell surface component associated with the LPS-CD14 complex for attachment to monocytes and macrophages28. Our study further elucidates the interaction between CD16 and DENV envelope and capsid proteins. As CD16 serves as a coreceptor for LPS29, its involvement in DENV binding might be hindered when cells are pretreated with LPS.

IM subset demonstrated enhanced expression of genes linked to chemotaxis, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular permeability21. IM are characterized by their increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), chemokine ligand (CXCL)-8, CXCL-2, and CXCL-332. Furthermore, this subset expresses plasminogen activator urokinase (PLAU), which is essential for preventing clot formation and regulating the TNF receptor superfamily (TNFSF), as well as cytokines such as IL-27, IL-23, IL-18, and IL-1229,33. Increase in IM level suggests a pivotal role in the intensified inflammatory response associated with SD12,13,19,34. Additionally, IM subset is implicated in the differentiation of B lymphocytes into plasmablasts, potentially exacerbating DENV infection through ADE19,21. Previous studies have reported increased frequencies of IMs to be negatively correlated with platelet counts in thrombocytopenic patients, and positively associated with elevated AST, ALT, and hematocrit levels, which is consistent with our findings33,34. This study had few limitations such as no secondary infection was obtained that could further confirm the role of IM in DENV severity. In addition, western blot analysis was performed in PBMCs and not in purified monocytes, which might not reflect actual monocyte subsets; however, flow cytometric analysis was the preferred immunophenotyping analysis that could precisely determine the monocyte subsets. IM was revealed as the significant marker in DWS & SD patients, and so, routine monitoring of IM during DENV infection could assist in delineating DENV severity, thereby reducing the disease burden.

Materials and methods

Clinico-laboratory investigations

This study was approved by the KIIT/KIMS/IEC/1266/2023. Informed consent was obtained was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s) for the study. Pediatric patients aged > 1 year to < 16 years with two or more days of febrile illness and clinical manifestations of dengue, without any signs of other infections reporting to the outpatient department of KIMS, Bhubaneswar were screened by clinicians over a period of two years (February 2023 to February 2025) and 3 ml of blood was collected in EDTA vials on the first day of hospital admission. All collected samples were subjected to serological tests (DENV NS1-Ag ELISA, DENV IgM ELISA, and DENV IgG sandwich ELISA), routine biochemical tests, and blood cultures to rule out bacterial infections. Patients with a previous history of DENV infection were excluded from the analysis to rule out the possibility of ADE that could lead to dengue severity. Of the analyzed samples, 120 serologically and RT-PCR confirmed dengue pediatric patients of East India origin were obtained and categorized following WHO guidelines (2009) into: DF, DWS, & SD. DENV serotyping was performed using RT-PCR35. Patients with undetectable levels of serum dengue-specific IgM and IgG are considered seronegative. Patients with dengue-specific IgM levels higher than IgG are typically classified as having primary dengue infections. Patients who exhibit only IgG or have IgM levels lower than IgG are generally regarded as having secondary dengue infections36.

The data obtained were analysed for significance differences among the DF and DWS & SD groups. Comparison of continuous variables (haemoglobin, ALT, AST, leukocyte counts) between groups were performed using the unpaired Student’s t-test after assessing the normality of the distribution using Shapiro–Wilk Test, while categorical variables (age group, gender, thrombocytopenia, haematocrit) were analysed using the Chi-square test. P-value (< 0.05%) was considered statistically significant. The analysis was performed using R Studio 4.3.11.

Flow cytometric and Western blot analysis

For flow cytometric analysis, 100 µL blood was taken in a FACS tube and incubated with CD45-KrO (Beckman Coulter), CD14-APC (Biolegend), CD16-PE (Biolegend), and HLA-DR-FITC (Biolegend) antibodies for 15 min. Then, 500 µL of OptiLyse C RBC lysing solution (Beckman Coulter) was added and incubated for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 500 x g for 5 min and two washes with 1% PBS. Finally, the cells were resuspended in 500 µL PBS for analysis. Flow cytometry data acquisition was performed using DXFlex Flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Flow cytometry data was analyzed using Beckman CytExpert software. For data analysis, an acquisition threshold was set, and singlets were isolated by gating forward scatter (FSC)-A and FSC-H plot to eliminate dead, pyknotic cells, debris, and doublets. All nucleated cell populations were gated further in an FSC-A vs. side scatter (SSC)-A plot. Monocytes were first visualized and separated from granulocytes, lymphocytes and neutrophils based on SSC-A and CD45 expression. Then, sequential gating of the monocyte cell population against CD14 and CD16 was performed to identify monocyte subsets37,38. (Supplementary Fig. 1)

Western blot was performed to confirm the activity of CD14 and CD16 antibodies used in flow cytometry using representative samples. PBMCs were isolated using HiSep™ LSM 1077, and proteins were purified with RIPA buffer. After Bradford assay quantification, equal protein amounts were denatured, separated via 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA, incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CD14 and anti-CD16 anti-human mouse antibodies, followed by goat anti-mouse HRP-conjugated secondary antibody incubation. Protein bands were detected using ECL and imaged via chemiluminescence with a loading control.

In silico analysis of host receptor and DENV structural proteins

The X-ray crystal structures of CD14 (PDB ID 4GLP), CD16 (PDB ID 1T83), DENV capsid (PDB ID 6VG5), and DENV Envelope (PDB ID 6FLB) proteins were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The active sites of the target proteins CD14 and CD16 were identified and validated using the CASTp server (https://cfold.bme.uic.edu/castpfold/) (Fig. 8). Structural insights from previous studies further validated these sites for CD1441, while experimental ligand-bound complex structures of CD16 (PDB ID 1T83) in PDBsum (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/databases/pdbsum/) further confirmed the active sites. For DENV structural proteins, active sites were identified using structural insights from available ligand-bound structures (PDB ID CODES 6VGF, 6FLB, 6FLA) and previous literature40,41. Investigations of CD14 (active site residues: 26–40 and 65–95)/CD16 (active site residues: 85–165) interactions with dengue viral proteins, specifically DENV Envelope (active residues: 361–397) and DENV Capsid (active residues: 61–81), were performed using the HADDOCK 2.4 server (https://rascar.science.uu.nl/haddock2.4/) following the default HADDOCK parameters, with passive sites automatically identified by the server.
