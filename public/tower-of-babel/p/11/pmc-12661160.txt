Exploring the potential of Schmidtea mediterranea as a model for reproductive toxicity through analysis of sterility caused defective m6A RNA regulation☆
Authors: Junichi Tasaki, Shujie Liu, Labib Rouhana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) testing is critical for identifying chemical hazards that may affect fertility, embryonic development, and offspring health. Traditionally, DART assessments have relied on mammalian models, which raise significant ethical concerns, are resource-intensive, and often face challenges in throughput and mechanistic resolution. In response to these limitations, there is an increasing demand for alternative methods that can complement or replace traditional animal-based tests, aligning with global initiatives such as the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) and regulatory frameworks like the EU REACH and the OECD’s Test Guidelines. As part of advancing alternative approaches to reproductive toxicity assessment, integrating biomarkers for mechanistic events (such as RNA modifications) into non-mammalian models, as well as developmental events (such as completion of intermediate and final stages of gametogenesis), could provide deeper insight into conserved developmental processes.

N6-methyladenosine residues (m6A) are one of the most abundant modifications in RNA, as these account for as much as 0.2–0.6 % of all adenosines in eukaryotic transcripts (Lavi et al., 1977, Desrosiers et al., 1974, Roundtree et al., 2017). Deposition of m6A leads to alterations in RNA structure (Roost et al., 2015), and influences nuclear processing and export of mRNA (Fustin et al., 2013, Zheng et al., 2013), as well mRNA stability (Wang et al., 2014) and translation (Wang et al., 2015). The presence of this reversible regulatory mark is orchestrated by competition between a methyltransferase complex (Bokar et al., 1994, Bokar et al., 1997, Liu et al., 2014) and demethylase enzymes (Zheng et al., 2013, Jia et al., 2011) known as “writers” and “erasers”, respectively (reviewed by Zhao et al., 2017). The writer complex contains the catalytic subunit Methyltransferase-Like 3 (METTL3), which cooperates with METTL14 for substrate recognition (Wang et al., 2014, Wang et al., 2016) and with the regulatory subunit Wilms’ Tumor 1-Associated Protein (WTAP) (Ping et al., 2014, Little et al., 2000). Demethylation of adenosine occurs passively or through eraser demethylase proteins such as Fat-mass and Obesity-associated protein (FTO) and Alpha-ketoglutarate-dependent Dioxygenase Homologue 5 (ALKBH5) (Zheng et al., 2013, Jia et al., 2011). When present, the m6A mark is recognized by “readers” such as YTHDF1, YTHDF2, and YTHDF3, which belong to YT521-B homology domain family of proteins. Readers recruit downstream regulators of mRNA to secure accurate regulation of targets and progression of biological processes (Wang et al., 2014, Dominissini et al., 2012, Xu et al., 2014, Zhu et al., 2014, Luo and Tong, 2014).

Proper regulation of m6A dynamics is essential for development and viability of plants and animals (Wang et al., 2014, Zhong et al., 2008, Zhao et al., 2017, Geula et al., 2015, Lence et al., 2016, Wu et al., 2018). Inhibition of m6A-associated machinery has been shown to affect several developmental processes, including stem cell differentiation in vertebrate embryos (Wang et al., 2014, Geula et al., 2015, Batista et al., 2014), sex determination in flies (Lence et al., 2016, Haussmann et al., 2016), and development of the germline in Drosophila and in mammals (Zheng et al., 2013). In flies, the catalytic subunit of the m6A methylation complex regulates oogenesis by modulating Notch signaling (Hongay and Orr-Weaver, 2011). In mammalian testes, ablation of Mettl3 severely inhibits spermatogonia differentiation and initiation of meiosis (Xu et al., 2017), whereas increased m6A abundance leads to apoptosis of meiotic metaphase stage spermatocytes (Zheng et al., 2013). These and other studies support the hypothesis that regulation of RNA by m6A based processes plays a role in cellular differentiation. Whether m6A machinery regulates germ cell differentiation through conserved processes or regulation of homologous targets across metazoans remains to be determined. However, exposure to environmental toxins is known to result in changes in m6A distribution and expression of RNA methylation machinery in different animals (Malovic et al., 2021, Cayir et al., 2019), which suggests a mechanistic association between toxicity-induced sterility and m6A RNA dysregulation.

Planarians are free-living freshwater flatworms that are members of the phylum Platyhelminthes (superphylum Lophotrochozoa; reviewed by Newmark and Sanchez Alvarado, 2002, Sluys and Riutort, 2018). Planarians have bilateral symmetry, derivatives of all three germ layers, and distinct digestive, nervous, nephridial, and reproductive systems (Agata et al., 1998, Issigonis and Newmark, 2019, Rink, 2018, Ross et al., 2017, Scimone et al., 2011, Forsthoefel et al., 2011). Planarians can reproduce asexually by transverse fission and regeneration, or sexually as cross-fertilizing hermaphrodites, although some species only exhibit one reproductive mode (Nodono and Matsumoto, 2022, Vila-Farre et al., 2023). Classically, planarian flatworms have been studied mainly for their capacity of full-body regeneration (Salo and Baguña, 2002, Agata and Watanabe, 1999). More recently, planarians have been used for ecotoxicology and developmental neurotoxicology experiments (Hagstrom et al., 2016, Wu and Li, 2018, Ireland and Collins, 2022) demonstrating their potential as a model for toxicology studies.

There is a growing interest in studying germline development in planarians, as formation of the entire reproductive system can be observed post-embryonically and derived from adult pluripotent stem cells known as neoblasts (Issigonis and Newmark, 2019, Newmark et al., 2008). In fact, the entire process of male and female germline development can be analyzed in adults, which circumvents issues in analysis of processes that only occur during a limited window of development. In addition, flatworms belong to a different clade of protostomes than flies or nematodes, and their study has facilitated the analysis of conserved genes whose function during germ cell development had not been revealed in decades of genetic screens in ecdysozoans. Examples of this include homologs of cyhr and SMARCB1 (Rouhana et al., 2017); EIF3C, BRG1, and NF-YB (Wang et al., 2010); components of the CCT complex (Counts et al., 2017); and TTBK (Magley and Rouhana, 2019). Studies in planarians have also uncovered genetic requirements during sperm development for factors whose function was known in oogenesis but not spermatogenesis (e.g., BicC and RAP55/LSM14 (Wang et al., 2010)). Indeed, genes found to contribute to sperm development in planarians (e.g., elav1, brg1, (Wang et al., 2010)) have since been validated by studying conditional alleles in mice (Chi et al., 2011, Kim et al., 2012, Wang et al., 2012). These multiple lines of evidence suggest that planarians can be a particularly valuable and cost-effective resource for the study of developmental processes conserved in humans and to inform studies in mice. Despite these features, planarians remain underexplored in the context of reproductive toxicology.

In this study, we assess the presence and function of m6A writers, readers, and erasers in Schmidtea mediterranea planarian hermaphrodites. Biological functions of m6A were analyzed during planarian germline development and regeneration. Expression of methyltransferase genes, Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14, was detected in the nervous system, ovaries, testes, and germline stem cells in sexual animals. Both Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 were also expressed in the nervous system in asexual animals. Perturbation of the m6A pathway using RNAi did not affect maintenance of germline or pluripotent somatic stem cells. However, RNAi targeting the writers, Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14, resulted in oocyte and sperm development defects. In addition, homologs of YTHDF m6A-binding proteins were expressed in the same anatomical regions as the methyltransferases in S. mediterranea. RNAi targeting expression of specific readers resulted in germline development defects or exacerbated defects observed upon knockdown of writer genes. Collectively, these results extend previous studies of m6A RNA regulation in asexual planarians (Dagan et al., 2022, Cui et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2023, Zhen et al., 2023) by providing evidence of its contribution in male and female germ cell development in S. mediterranea hermaphrodites. These findings highlight the role of RNA modification machinery in planarian germ cell differentiation and underscore their potential utility as an alternative in reproductive toxicity testing with developmental and mechanistic endpoints.

We predicted that genes responsible for m6A-mediated regulation of mRNA would share similar distribution patterns of expression. Thus, we assessed the distribution of expression of the eight potential m6A pathway components by whole-mount in situ hybridization (WMISH) in asexual and sexual strains of the planarian S. mediterranea. Expression of Smed-METTL3, Smed-METTL14, Smed-WTAP, Smed-YTHDF1-1, and Smed-YTHDF2-3 was detected in the brain of asexual planarians, which can be observed as a horseshoe-shaped structure in the region surrounding the eyes (Fig. 1, A-E). Expression of Smed-YTHDF2-2 and Smed-YTHDF1-2 were detected in the planarian gut (Fig. 1, F and G), whereas Smed-YTHDF2-1 expression was not conclusively detected in any specific structure (Fig. 1H). Some expression signal was detected in the gut for Smed-METTL3, Smed-METTL14, and Smed-WTAP samples (Fig. 1, A-C). Given that background signal in the gut is often observed in planarian samples analyzed by colorimetric WMISH, we reassessed the distribution of expression Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). Expression of Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 in the brain was validated using FISH, which also revealed some signal in the ventral nerve chords, but not in the gut (Fig. 1, A’ and B’). Expression of m6A writer and reader genes in neoblasts was not evident from in situ hybridization analyses. To go a step further in assessing the possibility that this regulatory mechanism contributes to the biology of planarian stem cells, neoblasts were depleted from asexual planarians by X-ray irradiation and expression of Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 was compared between intact control and irradiated asexual planarians by reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). The expression Smedwi-1 and nanos were measured as indicator of neoblast (Reddien et al., 2005) and germline stem cell (Wang et al., 2007) depletion efficiency, respectively, seven days after irradiation. As expected, the abundance of Smedwi-1 and nanos mRNAs dropped by 90 % or more in planarians subjected to X-ray irradiation (Fig. 1I). On the other hand, the abundance of Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 transcripts was not significantly different upon neoblast depletion (Fig. 1I), providing no evidence of Smed-METTL3 or Smed-METTL14 expression in planarian adult somatic stem cells. We consulted single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data of Plass et al. (Plass et al., 2018) and Zheng et al. (Zeng et al., 2018), which revealed some expression of Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 in neoblasts, and peak levels of expression during neuronal differentiation. These results indicate that S. mediterranea homologs of m6A writer complex genes are preferentially expressed in the nervous system in asexual animals.

To assess the possibility that m6A regulation plays a role in planarian germline development, we performed WMISH analyses in adults of the sexual strain of S. mediterranea (Fig. 2, A-I). In addition to corroborative detection of expression of Smed-METTL3, Smed-METTL14, Smed-WTAP, and Smed-YTHDF1-1 in the brain (Fig. 2, A-D), expression of some of these genes was also detected in gonads. More specifically, expression of Smed-METTL3, Smed-METTL14, and Smed-WTAP was detected in testes and ovaries (Fig. 2, A-C and A’-C’). Furthermore, faint detection of Smed-YTHDF2-3 (Fig. 2E) and Smed-YTHDF2-2 (Fig. 2F) expression was detected in testes. Expression of Smed-YTHDF1-2 was detected in the region of the ovaries (Fig. 2G). Expression of Smed-YTHDF2-1 was not detected in any specific organ of sexual planarians (Fig. 2H), as was the case during analysis of asexual samples (Fig. 1H). Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 are co-expressed in ovarian germline stem cells and oocytes of S. mediterranea

Expression of Smed-METTL3 was observed in cells co-expressing the germline stem cell marker nanos in the periphery of the ovary, and prevailing in mature oocytes (Fig. 3, A-A”). m6A RNA regulation contributes to normal development of male and female gonads in S. mediterranea

RNA interference (RNAi) was performed to assess the function of m6A methylation in sexual planarians. To do this, S. mediterranea hermaphrodites were subjected to a diet of calf liver containing double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) targeting either Smed-METTL3 or Smed-METTL14. Ingestion of dsRNA induces systemic RNAi in planarians and is sustained by weekly feedings to allow cellular turnover from neoblasts under knockdown conditions (Rouhana et al., 2013). A group of planarians were fed dsRNA corresponding to firefly luciferase sequence, which is known not to interfere with normal physiology or germline development in S. mediterranea (Magley and Rouhana, 2019, Lesko and Rouhana, 2020). After six weeks of RNAi, the anatomy of gonads was analyzed by DAPI staining and confocal microscopy as per Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2010). All of the control planarian samples displayed ovaries with fully developed oocytes (Fig. 4A; asterisks) and testis lobes with plentiful spermatozoa (Fig. 4E; arrows) indicating normal development of reproductive structures. In contrast, subsets of planarians from the groups subjected to Smed-METTL3 RNAi (METTL3(RNAi); Fig. 4, B and F) or Smed-METTL14 RNAi (METTL14(RNAi); Fig. 4, C and G) displayed underdeveloped female and male gonads that lacked spermatozoa and oocytes, respectively. The frequency of underdeveloped gonads and incomplete germline development phenotypes increased by simultaneous knockdown of Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 (METTL3;METTL14(RNAi)), resulting in 85 % of the ovaries lacking oocytes (Fig. 4D) and 71 % of samples with azoospermia (Fig. 4H). Given the gonad development phenotypes observed upon Smed-METTL3, Smed-METTL14, and METTL3;METTL14 RNAi, we hypothesized that regulation via m6A deposition had been disrupted upon knockdown of components of the writer complex. To test this, m6A abundance was assessed by m6A antibody blot on total RNA extracts from knockdown planarians. The m6A signal levels were noticeably decreased in RNA from METTL3(RNAi) animals and METTL14(RNAi) animals, and most severely in RNA from METTL3;METTL14(RNAi) animals (Fig. 4I). These results show that m6A RNA abundance is dependent on normal expression of Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14, which indicates that regulation of gene expression via m6A RNA methylation is required for normal development of gonads in S. mediterranea.

m6A RNA writers and readers support normal progression of male and female germline development in S. mediterranea

To gain insight into events that may be most sensitive to m6A RNA regulation during planarian germline development, we performed RNAi analyses in asexual and sexual planarians followed by FISH using stage-specific germ cell markers. In asexual planarians, neoblasts give rise to clusters of presumptive germline stem cells marked by expression of nanos, but these do not progress into later stages of gametogenesis (Wang et al., 2007). Knockdown of methyltransferases, Smed-WTAP, or readers did not change the presence or distribution of presumptive germline stem cell clusters in asexual planarians (Supplementary Fig. S1). Likewise, there were no noticeable deficits on regeneration (Supplementary Fig. S2) or fission (Supplementary Fig. S3) observed in asexual planarians with disrupted expression of m6A methylation writers or readers during these analyses. These results suggest that, while m6A writers and readers may be expressed in asexual planarians, differentiation of neoblasts into germline stem cells are not sensitive to disruption of m6A regulation.

To further analyze the germline development defects caused by disrupting expression of m6A regulators, we performed combinatorial RNAi of readers and writers in sexual planarians followed by analyses by FISH. We hypothesized that knocking down readers expressed in male and female gonads would exacerbate the oogenesis and spermatogenesis defects observed upon knockdown of METTL genes. We also hypothesized that knockdown of the writer complex accessory protein WTAP would cause germline development defects similar to those observed upon disruption of expression of methyltransferases. As in our initial experiments (Fig. 4), all the samples from the control group (i.e. planarians treated with luciferase dsRNA) possessed readily detectable oocytes in their ovaries (Fig. 5A) and sperm in the innermost region of testis lobes (Fig. 6A). The presence of oocytes in these experiments was validated not by their morphology alone, but also by detection of the oocyte marker Smed-CPEB1 (Rouhana et al., 2017) (Fig. 5A’). Similarly, the presence of germline stem cells and/or cells in early stages of gametogenesis in the gonads of control planarians (e.g., oogonia and spermatogonia) were validated by detection of the gH4 marker (Wang et al., 2007, Saberi et al., 2016) (Fig. 5A’ and 6A’). In contrast, simultaneous knockdown of Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 disrupted oogenesis (Fig. 5B) and spermatogenesis (Fig. 6B) in a subset of samples (Fig. 5G and Fig. 6I). However, cells expressing the gH4 marker were detected in all the ovaries (Fig. 5B’) and testes (Fig. 6B’) of Smed-METTL3,METTL14(RNAi) samples that lacked gametes, indicating the presence of germ cells in early stages gametogenesis in these gonads. Loss of gametes was also observed in ovaries and testes of planarians subjected to disruption of Smed-WTAP expression alone (Fig. 5C-C’ and 6C-C’) or in combination with Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 (Fig. 5D-D’ and 6D-D’). Knockdown of Smed-YTHDF1-2 expression, which was detected in ovaries (Fig. 2G), phenocopied oogenesis defects in planarians with disrupted expression of writer complex subunits (Fig. 5D-D’). The penetrance of this phenotype increased from 25 % to 83 % when Smed-METTL3 and Smed-METTL14 RNAi was combined with Smed-YTHDF1-2 RNAi (Fig. 5F-F’ and 5G). Nevertheless, germ cells expressing gH4 were observed in the gonads of all the samples (Fig. 5, A’-F’; Fig. 6, A’-H’). Expression of two reader homologs, Smed-YTHDF2-3 and Smed-YTHDF2-2, was detected in testis lobes of sexual planarians by whole-mount in situ hybridization (Fig. 2, Fig. 2). We tested whether the function of these readers is required for spermatogenesis by disrupting their expression individually and in combination Smed-METTL3;METTL14 RNAi (Fig. 6E-I). Disruption of Smed-YTHDF2-3 expression alone caused spermatogenesis defects similar to those of Smed-METTL3;METTL14(RNAi) in half of the samples in the analysis (Fig. 6E-E’). The penetrance of this phenotype increased to 100 % when Smed-YTHDF2-3 knockdown was combined with Smed-METTL3;METTL14 RNAi (Fig. 6F-F’ and 6I). In contrast, Smed-YTHDF2-2 RNAi alone did not result in spermatogenesis defects (Fig. 6G-G’ and 6I), but did increase the penetrance of the azoospermia to 100 % of samples when combined with Smed-METTL3;Smed-METTL14 RNAi (Fig. 6H-H’ and 6I). These data indicate that members of the writer complex, as well as Smed-YTHDF2-3 and YTHDF2-2, support sperm development in S. mediterranea. The detection of gH4(+) cells in the testes and ovaries of every animal displaying spermatogenesis and oogenesis defects in these experiments (Fig. 5B’-F’ and Fig. 6B’-6H’) corroborates with the continuity of germline stem cell clusters observed upon disruption of m6A writer and reader genes in asexual planarians (Supplementary Fig. S1). We hypothesize that regulation of mRNA by m6A is not required for development or maintenance of germline stem cells in planarian flatworms, but it is crucial for completion of spermatogenesis and oogenesis.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the presence of m6A RNA modification and regulatory factors in S. mediterranea sexual hermaphrodites and uncovers the critical role that this regulatory machinery plays in planarian germ cell development. These findings highlight mechanistic similarities in epigenetic regulation of RNA between planarians and mammals, indicating that planarian flatworms hold promise as models not only for ecotoxicology assays but also as options in reproductive toxicology testing that evaluates production of gametes.

The data presented revealed expression of m6A writer complex components and readers in gonads of S. mediterranea, as well as their functional requirement for development of sperm and ova. These findings corroborate with studies in Drosophila, zebrafish, and mice that identified the requirement of m6A RNA methyltransferase machinery for completion of gametogenesis (Hongay and Orr-Weaver, 2011, Xu et al., 2017, Wells and Pillai, 2024, Wan et al., 2015). The apparent conservation of m6A regulation during gametogenesis in planarians, flies, and mice supports the hypothesis that regulation of RNA through this post-transcriptional modification is ancestral to Bilateria, while evidence from studies of reproductive organs in Arabidopsis (Zhong et al., 2008, Wan et al., 2015) and meiotic cells in yeast (Ensinck et al., 2023) indicate that the function of m6A regulation during gamete development may be shared across eukaryotes.

Previous studies characterized components of the m6A RNA methylation machinery in asexual planarians (Dagan et al., 2022, Cui et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2023, Zhen et al., 2023). Regeneration and growth defects were observed upon RNAi-mediated knockdown of components of the writer complex (WTAP; KIAA1429 and METTL14) and one reader (YTHDC-1) in some of these studies (Dagan et al., 2022, Cui et al., 2023). Unlike previous studies, we failed to detect defects in the somatic homeostasis or regeneration upon knockdown of m6A writer machinery, which may be due to differences in RNAi protocols (e.g. the use of higher amounts of dsRNA by Dagan et al. (Dagan et al., 2022). One collective observation amongst the current and previous studies is that neoblasts are viable in the absence of normal m6A RNA deposition. However, differentiation processes (whether of neoblasts into somatic cell types or during progression of spermatogenesis and oogenesis) require proper expression of m6A writer and reader machinery. The post-transcriptional control of cell cycle regulators by m6A, as observed in asexual planarians (Cui et al., 2023), may be crucial for progression meiosis and differentiation in the planarian germline.

The outcome of m6A deposition on RNA in planarians remains to be elucidated and is likely to require transcript-specific analyses. One conserved feature that has been observed in transcriptomic analyses of S. mediterranea is the abundance of m6A near the stop codon of mRNAs (Cui et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2023). The presence of m6A in the coding region of mRNAs proximal to the stop codon triggers mRNA degradation via a translation-dependent pathway in human cells (Zhou et al., 2024, Murakami et al., 2025). Interestingly, Cui et al. (Cui et al., 2023) detected increased neoblast abundance, decreased levels of m6A modification, and increased abundance of cyclin‐dependent kinase 7 (cdk7), cdk9, and other mRNAs upon WTAP knockdown, supporting the idea that destabilization of mRNAs through this post-transcriptional modification is required in the process of cellular differentiation in planarians.

While components of the writer complex and readers are conserved between planarians, Drosophila, and vertebrates, erasers seem to have been lost in S. mediterranea. AlkB family demethylases, namely FTO and ALKBH5, are conserved between organisms as distant as humans and plants, where they remove m6A modifications from RNA. Homologs of m6A demethylases were not found in S. mediterranea in the current or a previous study (Dagan et al., 2022), suggesting that addition of m6A on mRNA is not a reversible process in planarians. Interestingly, ALKBH5 is required for normal testis size and fertility in mice (Zheng et al., 2013), indicating the importance of making m6A methylation reversible during spermatogenesis. One possibility is that the loss of dynamic m6A regulation was superseded by the continuous cellular turnover that characterizes the homeostatic plasticity of planarian flatworms. Planarians develop, grow, degrow, regenerate, and maintain their tissues by balancing rates of neoblast proliferation and cellular apoptosis (Hill and Petersen, 2015, Pellettieri et al., 2010). Aside from the possible exception of germline stem cells, which have been reported to give rise to somatic tissues under a specific regeneration experiment (Gremigni and Miceli, 1980), the neoblast commitment to differentiate not known to be reversible (see Pearson, 2022). Once a neoblast commits to a specific somatic lineage it may no longer have the ability to proliferate, de-differentiate, or slow down the process of differentiation. Germline stem progeny proliferate, complete meiosis, and differentiate in the process of spermatogenesis or oogenesis. Regulation of mRNA during these transitions may rely on alternative post-transcriptional mechanisms in the absence of m6A removal. Nevertheless, the absence of erasers in S. mediterranea does pose a limitation in studying the reversible aspect of methylation in this system.

The outcome of m6A deposition depends on the molecular function of reader proteins that identify this modification in mRNA. Dagan et al. (Dagan et al., 2022) identified six reader homologs in S. mediterranea (one YTHDC homolog and five YTHDF homologs). Of these, only knockdown of the YTHDC homolog resulted in a phenotype in asexual planarians. It is unlikely that the lack of observed RNAi phenotypes for YTHDF homologs is explained by functional redundancy, as these are expressed in limited non-overlapping cell types (Dagan et al., 2022). Interestingly, our experiments in sexual planarians revealed roles for three different YTHDF readers in germline development. Smed-YTHDF1-2 expression is enriched in ovaries and crucial for oocyte development (Fig. 5E). The mammalian homolog YTHDF1 stimulates translation directly and indirectly via stimulation of eIF3C translation, and amplification of YTHDF1 stimulates proliferation in ovarian cancer (Liu et al., 2020). We hypothesize that Smed-YTHDF1-2 regulates proliferation and differentiation of oogonia by ensuring timely translation of its targets. On the other hand, Smed-YTHDF2-3 and Smed-YTHDF2-2 are preferentially expressed in testes and support spermatogenesis (Fig. 6, E-H). YTHDF2 is known to recruit the CNOT1/CCR4 complexes to promote deadenylation and decay of its targets (Du et al., 2016). We hypothesize that YTHDF homologs in S. mediterranea promote oogenesis progression by degradation of mRNAs that support stemness at cost of differentiation. Further studies of these specific readers and identification of their targets in S. mediterranea will provide insight into the mechanisms by which mRNA regulation via m6A modification regulates germline development.

Beyond characterization of m6A machinery requirements in planarian germ cell development, our study represents an economical and rapid approach that could be adopted in developmental and reproductive toxicology studies, more specifically in initial or early assessment of chemicals that may modulate production of male and/or female gametes using a small model organism. The planarian model has already showed value in studies of neurotoxicity and developmental neurotoxicity (Hagstrom et al., 2015). Specifically, m6A RNA methylation abundance and development of oocytes and sperm may serve as mechanistic endpoints for alternative reproductive toxicity assays. Indeed, alternation of m6A modification by environmental toxicants such as phthalates and bisphenol induced testicular injury and defects in steroidogenesis in rats causing reproductive toxicity (Chen et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2024, Zhao et al., 2021). The observation that targeted disruption of m6A writers and readers selectively impairs germ cell differentiation while sparing stem cell maintenance (Geula et al., 2015, Batista et al., 2014, Wells and Pillai, 2024; this study) offers mechanistic insight into gametogenesis that can be used as phenotypic endpoints to complement conventional reproductive toxicity assays. Furthermore, RNA modifications such as m6A could define molecular signatures or epigenetic landscape of reproductive toxicity, providing sensitive and mechanistically informed biomarkers that may detect toxicant effects before overt morphological changes. However, this study has several limitations. The functional analyses were restricted to a subset of predicted m6A regulators, and we did not perform transcriptome-wide mapping to identify specific mRNA targets affected by m6A disruption. In addition, the impact of exogenous chemical exposure on m6A dynamics in planarians remains to be tested. Future work should integrate global m6A profiling and toxicant exposure assays to assess whether changes in m6A abundance or distribution correlate with reproductive toxicity outcomes. Such studies could help establish RNA modification patterns as robust mechanistic endpoints, advancing the utility of planarians as an alternative model in reproductive toxicology.

While our findings clarify the biological relevance of the m6A pathway and demonstrate the suitability of the planarian model in studies of germ cell development, further studies are needed to directly evaluate the predictive potential of this model for toxicological assessment. For example, although the m6A-modification machinery is conserved between flatworms and mammals, evolutionary differences may limit the model’s predictive capacity to specific chemical classes. Nevertheless, the planarian system offers advantages in cost-effectiveness and high-throughput capability, making it suitable as an initial screening tool to prioritize chemicals for reproductive toxicity testing before studies in mice or other vertebrates. In contrast to more commonly utilized invertebrate models, S. mediterranea is a simultaneous hermaphrodite, possessing both ovaries and testes within a single organism (Issigonis and Newmark, 2019). This anatomical feature enables concurrent observation of male and female gametogenesis under identical physiological conditions and provides a unique opportunity to investigate conserved molecular mechanisms regulating integrity. Thus, the planarian model provides a valuable complementary platform for studying conserved mechanisms of reproductive toxicity rather than a full replacement for mammalian DART models.

The present study identifies conserved m6A RNA-modifying enzymes in Schmidtea mediterranea and demonstrates their critical roles in germ cell development, providing a molecular foundation for establishing planarians as an alternative model for reproductive toxicity research. While our findings clarify the biological relevance of these enzymes, further studies are needed to evaluate the predictive potential of this model for toxicological assessment.

The applicability domain of the planarian model within the current DART testing paradigm must be carefully defined. Although the m6A-modification machinery is conserved between flatworms and mammals, evolutionary differences may limit the model’s predictive capacity to specific biological processes or chemical classes. Nevertheless, the planarian system offers advantages in cost-effectiveness and high-throughput capability, making it suitable as an initial screening tool to prioritize chemicals for reproductive toxicity testing before animal studies. The primary context of use lies in detecting disturbances of m6A modification and germ cell development, including ovary and testis formation. Thus, the planarian model provides a valuable complementary platform for studying conserved mechanisms of reproductive toxicity rather than a full replacement for mammalian DART models.

Comparative consideration of small model organisms (SMOs) helps define the applicability domain and translational potential of alternative systems for reproductive and developmental toxicity studies. Caenorhabditis elegans is an androdioecious nematode species composed of self-fertile hermaphrodites and a small proportion of males, a reproductive organization that enables high-throughput assays of brood size and germline function (WormBook, 2018). Drosophila melanogaster is dioecious, with distinct male and female individuals and well-established reproductive endpoints such as fecundity and offspring viability. In contrast, S. mediterranea is a simultaneous hermaphrodite, possessing both ovaries and testes within a single organism. This organization enables concurrent observation of male and female gametogenesis under identical physiological conditions and provides a unique opportunity to investigate conserved molecular mechanisms, including m6A-mediated regulation of germline integrity. Together, these distinctions highlight the complementary role of the planarian model among existing SMOs, particularly its ability to integrate sex-specific and molecular endpoints within a unified experimental framework relevant to mammalian reproductive toxicity.

Within an integrated chemical safety assessment strategy, the planarian model may serve as a cost-effective, high-throughput system in the early screening phase of DART evaluation, complementing mammalian testing and supporting the 3Rs principle (replacement, reduction, refinement). Although the planarian has traditionally been used in ecotoxicology, its molecular conservation with mammals, particularly in RNA modification and germline integrity pathways, supports its translational relevance. Analogous to the Ames test, which detects mutagenic potential using bacteria, planarians illustrate how simple non-mammalian systems can provide mechanistic insight into early hazard identification. While genotoxicity represents a non-threshold effect and reproductive toxicity is generally threshold-dependent, disturbances in m6A modification and germ cell development may serve as early indicators of reproductive toxicity potential. Thus, the planarian system could function as a bridging tool linking ecotoxicological findings to mammalian reproductive toxicology within an integrated DART testing framework.

This study establishes the biological significance and conservation of m6A-modifying enzymes in planarian germ cell development but does not yet provide toxicological validation. To enhance the predictive utility of this model, future research should (1) conduct systematic exposure studies using reference reproductive toxicants to define the relevant chemical space, (2) perform comparative analyses with established vertebrate models to identify shared key events in germline outcomes, and (3) explore upstream regulatory mechanisms that may mediate chemical responses. Because most small molecules, including endocrine disruptors, are unlikely to directly inhibit m6A enzymes, comparative analyses of upstream regulatory regions across planarians and other eukaryotes will be critical to evaluate conserved regulatory nodes. To utilize the planarian model system for reproductive toxicity studies, it is crucial to identify conserved features governing reproductive systems in both flatworms and mammals. Such conserved mechanisms could represent key events within an adverse outcome pathway (AOP) for reproductive toxicity, as well as serve as measurable endpoints of toxicant exposure. Following the present study, key events of the m6A machinery should be examined using reference reproductive toxicants. This strategy will strengthen the feasibility of the planarian model and contribute to the development of an AOP framework for reproductive toxicity.

Materials and methods

Planarian cultures

A laboratory line of S. mediterranea sexual hermaphrodites (Zayas et al., 2005) was used for the bulk of experiments reported in this study with the exception of analyses specifically indicated as performed using asexual animals, in which case a CIW4 clonal laboratory strain (Newmark and Sanchez Alvarado, 2000) was used. Sexual animals were maintained at 18 °C in 0.75X Montjuïc salts (Cebria and Newmark, 2005). Asexual animals were housed in incubators at 21 °C filled with 1x Montjuïc salts. Planarians were maintained in plastic containers of ∼2 L capacity and in the dark except during twice-per-week feedings of beef calf liver, which occurred on benchtops at room temperature. Animal husbandry containers were cleaned after each feeding and replenished with fresh media. Animals were starved for at least one week prior to experimentation or fixation.

Ablation of stem cells by X-ray irradiation

Asexual planarians were exposed to x-ray irradiation as described in Tasaki et al. (Tasaki et al., 2016) minus coverage by lead shields. Total RNA was extracted from irradiated planarians 7 days following irradiation using TRIzol as per the manufacturer protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and used for reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) as previously described (Rouhana et al., 2014, Miller and Newmark, 2012). The primers used in RT-qPCR analysis were: CCATGCTTGCAAACAGAAGG and TCCTTGCTCCATTGCTCTTC for Smed-METTL3; CTGGTGTGGTAGTGGTGAAG and TGAAATAGTGCACCAGGCTC for Smed-METTL14; and CAAGGACAAATGTTGCCTGTA and CAACCCATCGATCCAACTCT for nanos. Smedwi-1 and ß-tubulin primers were described in (Rouhana et al., 2014, Miller and Newmark, 2012).

Cloning of m6A methyltransferase machinery and YTHDF homolog cDNAs

Partial cDNA sequence clones were generated from PCR-based amplification of reverse transcription products generated from sexual S. mediterranea total RNA primed with oligo-(dT) and random primers as per GoScript cDNA synthesis kit manufacturer protocols (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin). PCR using gene-specific primers was performed using Long PCR Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI) and product were ligated into pGEM-T vectors as per manufacturer instructions for T/A cloning (Promega, Madison, WI). The oligos used for amplification of gene specific sequences are the following: ATTAATGGGCGATACTTGGAAAG and TCTTTCAACAACTTCTGGATCTACC for METTL3; TGGCTGATAACTCAATCAATGATAC and ATTATCGACGAGATCCTCCAGTAG for METTL14; GCCCAAATTGAAGACATGAAG and TCCTCATCATCCTCCTCCTC for WTAP; CAAATCCATTTTGTGAATCAGG and TCCAGAACATTGGATCGATTAC for ythdf2-3; TGGAGTCTTTACCACATTCCTG and AATAATAAAGGCCTCCGCTTC for ythdf2-2; TATGCCCCAATTCAGAATCC and CCGCCATAAATGTATCCAATG for ythdf1-1; GCTCCATCATTTACGCCAAG and AGGAGGGATACCCCTGTATG for ythdf2-1; AAGATCAGCCCAATGAATCG and CAATCACGCGAAAGATATGC for ythdf1-2.

Whole-mount in situ hybridization

Whole-mount colorimetric in situ hybridization was performed as described by Pearson et al. (Pearson et al., 2009) while FISH and DAPI staining were performed following the protocol described by King and Newmark (King and Newmark, 2013) and Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2010), respectively. Large animals (∼1.0 cm or larger) were used in in situ hybridization analyses of sexual animals, whereas animals of 0.5–0.7 cm were used in analyses of asexual specimens. Digoxigenin-11-UTP (Cat. No 11209256910, Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) and Fluorescein-12-UTP (Cat. No. 11427857910, Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) were used in riboprobe synthesis by in vitro transcription using cDNA clone sequences as templates. Low magnification images of colorimetric and FISH samples were obtained using a Zeiss V.16 SteREO microscope coupled to a Canon EOS Rebel T3 camera. For high magnification imaging of FISH and DAPI staining, single confocal plane images were generated from mid-point depth of gonads using a Nikon C2 + confocal microscope with NIS-Elements software.

Disruption of gene expression by RNAi

Double-stranded RNA was generated by in vitro transcription using T7 RNA Polymerase from templates generated by PCR from cDNA clones as previously described (Rouhana et al., 2013, Curry et al., 2025). Planarians were fed twice per week with dsRNA 100–200 ng/μL (final concentration) delivered using a 2:1 beef liver puree to water mixture, which was fed to groups of planarians to satiation. Animals were amputated of fixed for analyses by DAPI, immunostaining, and/or in situ hybridization a week following the final of five dsRNA feedings (regeneration experiment in asexual planarians) or 8–9 dsRNA feedings (sexual planarians and germline stem cell cluster analysis in asexual planarians).

Analysis of testis distribution and sperm development

A week following completion of RNAi treatments, samples were fixed and bleached as described above for in situ hybridization (minus methanol dehydration and rehydration steps). After bleaching, the samples were washed in PBSTx twice and then incubated in DAPI (ACROS Organics, Morris, NJ) solution (1:1000 dilution of 1 mg/ml DAPI stock solution in PBSTx) overnight while rocking at 4 °C. After incubating overnight, the samples were washed 4 times with PBSTx and then mounted on slides with 4:1 glycerol:PBS and imaged under UV light with a Zeiss V.16 SteREO microscope equipped with a Canon EOS Rebel T3 camera (for low magnification) or a Nikon C2 + confocal microscope using a 20X objective and running NIS Elements C software (for high magnification).

Analysis of asexual planarian regeneration by immunofluorescence and DAPI staining

Asexual planarian subjected to RNAi treatments were amputated anterior and posterior to the pharynx a week following the last dsRNA feeding. Then samples were fixed and analyzed using DAPI and anti-SYNORF1 (1:250 dilution; clone ID: 3C11, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, Iowa City, IA) as described previously (Almazan et al., 2021).
