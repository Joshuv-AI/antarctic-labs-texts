Integrating Evidence on Dignity Therapy in Cancer Care: A Review
Authors: Maria Vinu, Poonam Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As psychosocial treatments become increasingly important in palliative care, dignity therapy (DT) emerges as a promising intervention. This systematic review aims to synthesize and critically evaluate the evidence on DT interventions in adults with cancer by integrating findings from randomised controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies. The search terms ‘Dignity Therapy’ AND ‘Cancer’ were used to identify RCTs, quasi-experimental, mixed methods and qualitative studies related to DT implemented in adult cancer patients from Scopus and Web of Science databases on 20th November, 2024. Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklists were used to assess the risk of bias in the studies. Twenty studies were included after a two-stage screening process, out of which 7 were RCTs, 6 were quasi-experimental studies, 5 were qualitative studies and 2 were mixed-method studies. These studies demonstrate that DT enhances dignity, reduces psychological distress and improves family dynamics, particularly in culturally adapted formats. Quantitative findings demonstrate significant improvements in dignity-related outcomes (p < 0.01; d = 1.04–2.45), anxiety (d = 1.73), depression (d = 0.94) and physical symptoms including nausea and insomnia (p < 0.05). Qualitative themes reveal core therapeutic mechanisms: Legacy creation, meaning-making, communion, being heard and cultural adaptation reinforcing DT’s psychosocial value. DT was effectively delivered by diverse providers, with family-integrated approaches showing promise in collectivist cultures. The geographical scope restricts generalisability to other cultural settings. High attrition rates (up to 44.2%) and small sample sizes weaken statistical power, and the absence of long-term follow-up data obscures DT’s sustained effects. The lack of standardised outcome measures also introduces subjectivity, particularly in qualitative analyses, potentially biasing results. This systematic review concludes that while the report underscores DT’s promise in palliative care, mixed findings and methodological gaps highlight the need for more robust, inclusive research to solidify its therapeutic role.

INTRODUCTION

Dignity therapy (DT) and traditional psychotherapeutic techniques

DT is a brief psychotherapeutic intervention designed to alleviate psychological and existential distress in patients with life-limiting illnesses, particularly in palliative care settings. Formulated by Dr. Harvey Chochinov in 2002, DT focuses on the following aspects: Generativity, continuity of self, role preservation, maintenance of pride, hopefulness, aftermath concerns and care tenor. It aims to enhance patients’ sense of dignity by helping them articulate their life stories and values, ultimately creating a legacy document for their loved ones.[1] Medical models though successful in controlling physical symptoms, typically overlook the psychological, existential and spiritual concerns of terminally ill patients, leading to distress, reduced sense of purpose and quality of life (QoL).[2-4] Similarly, research suggests out that pharmaceutical therapies for depression in cancer patients are frequently ineffective, emphasising the necessity for additional psychosocial therapy.[5] These gaps in standard treatment highlight the necessity of therapies such as DT, which is particularly designed to address the psychological and existential issues experienced by patients with life-limiting diseases. DT has shown promise as a helpful intervention that not only addresses these critical aspects of patient well-being but also improves the overall QoL.[6] DT facilitates patient reflection on life, values and legacies, aiming to reduce significant anxiety and depression[7,8] associated with advanced illness. Several studies have shown that this form of psychotherapy is effective in a multicultural setting.[8,9] Furthermore, the feasibility of implementing DT in clinical practice has been supported, although it requires adequate training for healthcare professionals and multidisciplinary team support.[10] Overall, DT represents a valuable non-pharmacological approach to enhancing the QoL and providing meaning during the end-of-life (EOL) phase.

Effectiveness of DT

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 studies from 2002 to 2022 revealed that hope, QoL and distress connected to dignity were all improved by DT.[11,12] The psychological and spiritual well-being of the patients did not change significantly, according to a meta-analysis.[12] Haneef and Abdullah reported differing results. DT significantly improved palliative care patients’ QoL and well-being, as well as their depression and anxiety levels. Improvements were seen in all three domains of QoL. Family caregivers reported increased spiritual well-being, hope, family togetherness and flexibility. However, not all outcomes improved consistently between trials, showing that the effectiveness of DT varies.[13] Another systematic review and meta-analysis of 10 articles revealed that DT considerably reduced dignity-related suffering in the domains of social support and existential distress but had no discernible effect on depression or anxiety. According to narrative summaries, DT improved patients’ psychological health, QoL and sense of dignity.[14] Park and Cha reported that several quantitative data demonstrated that DT has the power to alleviate poor moods or enhance emotions, which leads to a boost in the QoL for individuals with terminal diseases, including self-integration and generativity.[15]

Use of DT in India and globally

In Western nations, the dignity paradigm and DT are both widely accepted and have had a significant impact on the evolution of palliative care legislation and practices.[16] Due to its value-laden character, dignity has varied meanings in different cultures and ethnic groups. In light of the Eastern societies’ reliance on families for EOL care and the Asian tradition of prioritising collectivism over individualism, Ho et al. conducted a comprehensive study examining the concept of dignity from the viewpoints of elderly Asian patients who are terminally ill and their family caregivers.[17,18] Family ties and family processes have become important aspects of dignity in the Asian model, even though the results of these studies largely supported the Western dignity model and its three main conceptual categories pertaining to the physical, psychosocial and existential domains. According to reports, older Asian patients in particular suffer greatly from feeling stuck in the transitional period between life and death, which is made worse by their diminished mobility and growing dependence.[17,18]

Most of the reviews were carried out in Western nations, such as Canada, Australia and the United States. Only Japan and Taiwan were included in the limited investigations that were done in Asia.[19] According to a study conducted in India, the majority of participants (60%) expressed satisfaction with the comments made about DT: 80% said that they were satisfied with DT, 73.33% said that DT had given them the highest sense of purpose, and 66.66% said that DT had the highest sense of dignity. Regarding family concerns, 40% of participants reported that DT was beneficial to their loved ones. Overall, 80% of cancer patients undergoing palliative care expressed satisfaction with the psychosocial support provided by DT.[20]

DT across sections and diseases

DT is a psychotherapeutic approach designed to enhance the QoL for individuals grappling with terminal illnesses. This method supports patients in articulating their personal narratives, providing a meaningful avenue to alleviate psychological distress and cultivate a renewed sense of dignity and self-worth.[21,22] Research has shown that DT is effective across a variety of medical conditions, including cancer and motor neuron disease, and its benefits have also been explored in chronic illnesses such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).[23-25]

While DT has been extensively studied, the existing literature remains fragmented, with randomised controlled trials (RCTs) focusing on quantifying the efficacy of DT in improving psychological and existential outcomes[26] and qualitative studies exploring subjective experiences and perceived benefits of DT from the perspectives of patients, families and healthcare providers.[6] Despite calls for more comprehensive approaches,[27,28] there is a lack of integrated synthesis that bridges these methodologies to provide a holistic understanding of DT’s effectiveness, mechanisms and contextual factors. This fragmentation obscures a holistic understanding of how DT works, for whom it is most effective and under what conditions it can be successfully implemented. This gap also limits the ability to fully capture the therapeutic potential of DT and inform its implementation in diverse clinical settings. This review addresses this critical gap by systematically integrating quantitative and qualitative evidence published between 2020 and 2024, a period marked by significant shifts in psychosocial care delivery during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

Aim

This systematic review aims to synthesise and critically evaluate the evidence on DT interventions in adults with cancer by integrating findings from RCTs, mixed-method studies, qualitative studies and quasi-experimental studies published from 2020 to 2024.

Objectives

Information sources

A systematic search was conducted on 20th November 2024 using Scopus and Web of Science, two peer-reviewed databases providing comprehensive coverage of psycho-oncology, palliative care, nursing and psychology literature. This dual-database approach is a recognised methodology for ensuring high recall of relevant studies.[30,31]

Search strategy

The search terms ‘Dignity Therapy’ AND ‘Cancer’ were applied, capturing a broad range of cancer diagnoses as evidenced by prior literature.[6,14,15,27,30] Subject areas were limited to Psychology, Social Sciences, Nursing and related fields; the time period was restricted to 2020–2024. After removing 17 duplicates, 105 articles remained. A two-level screening process was applied: First, titles and abstracts were reviewed for relevance; second, full texts of shortlisted articles were critically evaluated. RCTs, quasi-experimental, qualitative and mixed-method studies were included. Figure 1 presents the data extraction process.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 flow diagram showing the data extraction process.

Data collection and analysis process

Study quality was assessed using Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklists[32]: The RCT checklist for RCTs, the Qualitative checklist for qualitative studies and the Cohort checklist for quasi-experimental studies. Responses of ‘yes’, ‘no’ and ‘can’t tell’ were scored 1, 0, and 0.5, respectively. Although CASP does not prescribe a composite score, a quality rating scale was applied as seen in prior literature[6,30,33,34]: 0–3 (low quality: C), 4 to 6 (medium quality: B) and 7-12 (high quality: A).

DISCUSSION

The current study synthesises evidence from 20 studies on DT, including RCTs, quasi-experimental studies, qualitative research and mixed-methods designs.

Objective 1: Integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence

Quantitative findings demonstrated significant reductions in anxiety (d = 1.73), depression (d = 0.94), dignity-related distress (d = 1.04–2.45; p < 0.001) and physical symptoms including nausea and insomnia (p < 0.05),[45] though QoL metrics yielded inconsistent results.[40] Qualitative synthesis identified complementary themes of meaning-making, legacy building and strengthened interpersonal relationships, with patients reporting enhanced dignity through being heard and acknowledged. Together, these findings position DT as a multilevel intervention addressing both measurable clinical outcomes and the existential dimensions of the cancer experience.

Objective 2: Therapeutic mechanisms

Four consistent therapeutic mechanisms emerged across studies. First, legacy creation and life review: The structured opportunity to reflect on one’s life and produce a lasting document facilitated meaning-making and a sense of purpose.[48] Second, communion and relational connection, characterised by loving, emotionally intimate exchanges during communal narration, reinforcing that DT’s benefits are interpersonal and not merely intrapsychic.[46] Third, being heard and witnessed by an attentive, non-judgemental facilitator validated patients’ life experiences and directly enhanced self-worth. Fourth, cultural adaptation, particularly family-oriented DT in Chinese contexts, strengthens outcomes by making the intervention more contextually resonant.[37,38,40,41]

Objective 3: Contextual factors influencing effectiveness and timely synthesis (2020–2024)

Early introduction of DT may be very advantageous in the course of the disease.[47] Although one study revealed that spiritual distress may affect results,[36] further research is needed to determine patient-level modifying factors. Diverse practitioners (psychologists, chaplains, and nurses) successfully administered DT, and family-integrated forms showed potential in collectivist cultures. Cultural context has a major role in implementation; in China, modified versions have been shown to improve patient benefits and family results. Emerging research supports adapted forms, such as family-integrated DT, which showed significant promise in collectivist cultural settings, even though the majority of studies administered DT in conventional individual formats. The development of a legacy document served as the constant endpoint, while the length and number of sessions varied throughout research, usually falling between one and three sessions. Future research is still needed to determine the ideal amount and format. This post-pandemic period saw accelerated interest in DT, emergence of culturally adapted family-oriented models, and maturation of the evidence base through methodological diversity. These developments position DT as particularly relevant for addressing isolation and existential distress in contemporary palliative care.

Methodological considerations and common lacunas across studies

Across all four study types, common methodological limitations included single-centre designs, small and culturally homogeneous samples, high attrition rates driven by disease progression and the absence of long-term follow-ups. These collectively limit the generalisability of findings and raise questions about the cost-benefit balance of conducting palliative care research. Future studies should prioritise larger, multi-centre designs with diverse samples, standardised outcome measures and longitudinal follow-up.

Study specific lacunas

RCTs were confined to China and the USA, limiting global applicability. Blinding was feasible in only three of seven RCTs, given the intervention’s nature, and dropout rates ranged from 0% where sessions were insurance-covered[38] to 44.2% at T2 due to health decline,[40] highlighting the role of structural supports in retention. Quasi-experimental studies lacked randomisation and control groups, limiting internal validity. Qualitative studies maintained credibility through reflexivity, independent coding and participant verification of legacy documents, though delayed referrals and rapid patient decline disrupted implementation. Mixed-method studies call for standardised DT process evaluation tools to reduce subjectivity. Cultural, geographic and site-specific factors must be considered before generalising any findings.

Recent evidence supports these findings: A randomised trial in COPD patients found DT reduced anxiety as a nonpharmacological intervention,[25] and a meta-analysis of 16 studies (n = 1202) reported significant group differences in dignity distress, hope and QoL.[12] However, DT does not demonstrate uniform efficacy across all outcomes or populations. Inconsistencies in anxiety, depression and QoL results underscore that DT should not be regarded as a standalone intervention. It is most effectively positioned as one component of a broader, tailored palliative care approach, ideally combined with other evidence-based psychosocial interventions to more comprehensively address patients’ psychological and existential needs.[13]

This review’s strengths include its integration of diverse study designs and rigorous CASP quality assessment, offering a comprehensive view of DT across quantitative and qualitative dimensions. Key limitations include geographic concentration in China and the USA, high attrition rates (up to 44.2%), small sample sizes and absence of long-term follow-up, which collectively restrict generalisability and statistical power. Future research priorities include multi-centre RCTs with diverse samples, validated and standardised dignity outcome measures, cross-cultural comparisons, evaluation of DT in non-cancer populations and assessment of cost-effectiveness and facilitator training requirements.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review of 20 studies (2020–2024) suggests that DT is associated with improvements in dignity-related outcomes, reduces psychological distress and enhances family dynamics in adults with cancer. Core therapeutic mechanisms, namely legacy creation, communion and the experience of being heard, were consistently identified across quantitative and qualitative evidence. However, mixed findings and methodological limitations, including limited geographic scope, high attrition, small samples and absence of standardised tools and long-term follow-up, highlight the need for more rigorous and inclusive research to fully establish DT’s therapeutic role in palliative care.
