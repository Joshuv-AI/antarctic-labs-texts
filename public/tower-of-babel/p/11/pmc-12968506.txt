Understanding the Cause and Diversity of Women's Drink and Drug Driving—A Qualitative Exploration
Authors: Kerry A Armstrong, Hanna Watling, Taren Mieran, Jessica Marigold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDrink and drug driving continues to pose a significant public health risk, with incidents increasing among women. Building on limited prior research, this study situates women's substance‐impaired driving within a psychosocial and gender‐responsive criminological framework to explore women's experiences with impaired driving.MethodsSemi‐structured interviews were conducted with N = 27 women apprehended for drink or drug driving within the past 5 years in Queensland, Australia. Data were analysed using ideal‐type analysis, identifying typologies based on similarities and differences across accounts.ResultsFour distinct typologies emerged from the analysis: (i) Inadvertent Drink Drivers, who unintentionally exceeded the general alcohol limit due to misunderstandings about alcohol metabolism and reliance on outdated consumption guidelines; (ii) Post‐Apprehension Separators, who continued using substances but implemented deliberate strategies to separate substance use from driving; (iii) Drivers Engaged in Recovery, who had ceased substance use at the time of the interview; and (iv) High‐Risk Offenders, who demonstrated a high likelihood of reoffending, often influenced by permissive social environments and low responsiveness to legal sanctions.Discussion and ConclusionsSome women's profiles aligned with previous research where substance‐impaired driving was shaped by gendered stressors, whereas others reflected characteristics more consistent with traditional male‐typed profiles. Distinct intervention needs were identified across typologies, including substance use and mental health treatment, education initiatives, self‐regulated separation strategies and legislative sanctions. Recognising how individual behaviour is shaped by social and structural factors is essential for designing equitable, gender‐responsive interventions and supports that enhance women's capacity to avoid reoffending.Keywords: drink driving, drug driving, impaired driving, qualitative, women

Introduction

Despite decades of law enforcement and public awareness efforts, drink and drug driving continue to be a leading cause of road injuries and fatalities. In Australia, nearly seven people are hospitalised daily and more than one person dies every second day in crashes involving an alcohol‐impaired driver [1]. Drug driving is also a major concern, with around 20%–25% of all road fatalities in some jurisdictions involving a driver with drugs in their system [2, 3].

While women are underrepresented among substance‐impaired drivers, data show a steady narrowing of the gap between male and female offending rates [4, 5, 6]. For some drugs, such as opioids and benzodiazepines, research shows similar apprehension rates for men and women [7]. Some evidence also suggests that the number of women involved in fatal alcohol and drug‐related crashes is increasing [8, 9, 10], despite other research indicating that the gender disparity for fatality rates has changed only marginally [6]. Women's increased participation in substance‐impaired driving reflects broader shifts in substance use, with consumption levels becoming more similar between the genders, particularly among younger populations [11, 12].

Despite these trends, research on women's substance‐impaired driving remains limited and theoretical understanding is underdeveloped. Therefore, this study adopts a psychosocial and gender‐responsive criminological framework, recognizing that substance‐impaired driving reflects the interaction of individual behavior with broader social and contextual influences. Within the criminological literature, gendered pathway perspectives highlight that women's offending, including substance misuse, often occurs in the context of cumulative psychosocial stressors rather than in isolation. These pathways emphasize the role of experiences such as trauma exposure, mental health difficulties, caregiving responsibilities and economic insecurity in shaping women's substance use and related offending behaviors [13, 14]. Examining women's substance‐impaired driving within this broader context enables a more nuanced interpretation of risk rather than focusing on individual‐level characteristics.

Current research indicates that women's substance‐impaired driving is more often shaped by gender‐specific risk factors not adequately captured by traditional male‐centred frameworks. For example, a study comparing drink driving behaviours between men and women hypothesised that women's offending would be primarily linked to alcohol misuse, whereas men's would be associated with impulsivity, sensation‐seeking and response inhibition [15]. However, impulsivity and alcohol use severity were stronger predictors of offending among women, while sensation‐seeking and response inhibition were non‐significant for both genders. This aligns with other research showing that although men generally exhibit higher levels of sensation‐seeking and low self‐control, these traits are more predictive of women's drink driving [16, 17]. Moreover, Robertson et al. [18] found distinct gendered predictors of drink and drug driving recidivism, with heavy alcohol use and criminal history predicting men's recidivism and symptoms consistent with substance use disorder uniquely predicting women's. Overall, these findings highlight the need for a gender‐responsive approach, as traditional male risk‐taking frameworks may not elucidate the mechanisms behind gender differences.

Qualitative research provides additional insight to these findings by exploring the personal and social context surrounding women's substance‐impaired driving. These studies reveal that many women report consuming alcohol to cope with psychological distress or to unwind and that apprehensions are often triggered by major life stressors such as family conflicts, custody battles, job loss or bereavement [19, 20], suggesting that psychosocial factors are key drivers of women's pathways to drink driving. Although some research has begun to explore women's involvement in substance‐impaired driving, more is needed to uncover the psychosocial, behavioral and situational factors that shape their risk and outcomes in this area.

In this study, we explore women's experiences with drink and drug driving through qualitative interviews. Recognising that individuals who are apprehended for substance‐impaired driving, regardless of gender, are a heterogenous group [21, 22] we employ a typology approach to identify meaningful subgroups of female offenders and gain deeper insights into the interplay between substance use patterns, life circumstances and driving behaviour by addressing the following research aim to understand: How do women describe their experiences of and pathways into impaired driving and what typologies can be developed from these accounts. In doing so, this research seeks to contribute to the knowledge base needed to inform gender‐responsive policies, enforcement strategies and intervention responses to substance‐impaired driving.

Methods

The study protocol was approved by the University of the Sunshine Coast Ethics Committee (reference: A231995) and informed consent was sought from the participants before commencement of their interview.

Participants

Interviews were conducted with 27 women aged between 21 and 63 years throughout May to July 2024. Participants were recruited among persons identifying as women, who resided in the Australian state of Queensland, were aged 18 years or older and had at least one drink or drug driving apprehension within the previous 5‐year period. Recruitment occurred via paid social media advertisement, with a small number of participants recruited with the assistance of a research marketing company. Apprehension status was self‐reported and documented verification (e.g., court or police documentation) was not requested due to ethical and privacy considerations. While roadside detection in Queensland typically results in formal charges, apprehension is separate from conviction. As such, the term ‘apprehension’ is used to refer to detection by the Queensland Police Service rather than the legal outcome determined by the court.

Five participants were unlicensed due to suspensions following their apprehensions. Two held provisional licences, which in Queensland imposes additional restrictions including a zero blood alcohol concentration (BAC) limit. Drivers entering the licensing system as well as those apprehended for substance‐impaired driving must progress through the provisional licence phase before gaining an open licence. The remaining women held an open licence in which a general BAC limit of less than 0.05% applies. Table 1 provides an outline of the participants' demographic information.

Materials and Data Collection

Demographic information was collected using a survey capturing details about licensure and driving exposure (i.e., length of time licence held and weekly driving frequency), relationship status, dependent children, area of residence, educational attainment and annual household income. Self‐reported substance use was assessed using the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT [23]), a widely validated measure across diverse populations. The AUDIT has been consistently demonstrated as a valid and reliable tool for identifying harmful alcohol use, abuse and dependence [24, 25]. The 10‐item Drug Abuse Screening Test (DAST‐10 [26]) was included to assess consequences associated with drug use. This measure has been validated across multiple studies [27].

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted by the first two female authors (K.A. and H.W.), each with postgraduate training and experience interviewing women regarding sensitive topics. Interviews were conducted via an online communication application, using audio only to protect participants' privacy and encourage forthcoming and honest responses. The interviews explored participants' alcohol and drug use, impaired driving history, life circumstances leading up to the apprehension, experiences during the event and any changes to substance use and driving behaviour following apprehension. Interviews ranged from 20 to 84 min (average 37 min) and participants received a $50 supermarket gift card as reimbursement for their time.

Data Analysis

Ideal‐type analysis was used to examine patterns across behaviours, narratives and contextual circumstances, guided by the qualitative analytic approach described by Stapley et al. [28]. The unit of analysis was each participant's complete account of events and perspectives. Cases were examined across the full dataset to identify recurring patterns and points of divergence across accounts. The analytic process was collaborative, involving multiple members of the research team at each stage. When disagreement or differing perspectives arose, the data were revisited and discussed to determine which interpretation was best supported by the accounts. This approach helped ensure that the emerging typologies remained grounded in the data.

Initially, all transcripts were independently reviewed by three authors (K.A., H.W. and J.M.) who recorded preliminary observations. These observations informed group discussions in which provisional ‘prototype’ typologies were developed based on patterns of similarities and differences across cases. Two team members (HW and JM) then produced descriptive case summaries, which were systematically examined and contrasted to identify key themes that characterised some groups while distinguishing them from others. Within‐group analysis also documented experiences that were shared across group members, while noting areas of variability. For each typology, an ‘optimal case’ was identified, defined as a narrative account and set of contextual circumstances that most clearly embodied the defining characteristics of that group. Identifying optimal cases assisted in clarifying within‐group variability and provided illustrative examples of how key themes were expressed across the data. Although the typologies were developed inductively, their interpretation was informed by existing psychosocial and gender‐responsive criminological perspectives on women's substance use and related offending.

To assess typology credibility, blinded case summaries were provided to the lead author (K.A.) and the third author (T.M.), who independently reviewed the summaries and attempted to reassign cases into the four typologies. A comparison matrix was developed to examine concordance between the original and independent classifications. Cases where classification differed were reviewed and discussed by the team until consensus was reached. This process supported the refinement and consolidation of the final typologies. The analysis concluded with a detailed description of the defining features and optimal case for each typology, alongside a summary of the remaining group members and consideration of similarities and differences across typologies.

Results

Ideal‐type analysis identified four typologies of women apprehended for drink and/or drug driving: Inadvertent Drink Drivers, Post‐Apprehension Separators, Drivers Engaged in Recovery and High‐Risk Offenders. These typologies were not conceptualised as fixed; rather, the accounts suggested that a few women had moved between typologies as their circumstances, substance use and driving practices changed over time. For reporting purposes, cases were assigned to the typology that best reflected participants' circumstances at the time of the interview.

The composition of each typology varied by offence type: Inadvertent Drink Drivers comprised women apprehended for drink driving only, whereas Post‐Apprehension Separators were predominantly drink drivers, with one drug driving case involving delta‐9‐tetrahydrocannabinol (THC). The remaining two typologies included a relatively even distribution of drink and drug driving cases. Although inclusion criteria permitted multiple apprehensions, the interviews primarily focused on the most recent event. While time since apprehension varied across the sample, recency did not align consistently with typology membership (i.e., women across typologies described both recent and more distal events). Ideal Type 1: Inadvertent Drink Drivers

The Inadvertent Drink Driver typology comprised four women, all first‐time, low‐range 1 drink driving offenders. Their offending stemmed from misconceptions about the dose‐dependent relationship between alcohol consumption and BAC and when apprehended, they had been surprised their BAC exceeded the legal alcohol limit for their licence type (all held an open licence). Following apprehension, participants described changing their approach to driving after consuming alcohol to minimise the likelihood of future offending.

The Optimal Case

The optimal case for this typology was Amy (26, drink driving), a light‐to‐moderate social drinker apprehended with a low‐range BAC of 0.089%. She described relying on an incorrect interpretation of outdated advice regarding the number of standard drinks considered safe for driving. Further, she misunderstood what constituted a standard drink, equating it to the actual drinks, in this instance two cocktails, she had consumed. This misunderstanding occurred in the context of evolving drink driving messaging in Australia, where authorities increasingly advocate for a complete separation of alcohol and driving due to the difficulty of accurately estimating BAC levels. Despite these recommendations, counting the number of drinks consumed over time persists in the public consciousness as a strategy for remaining under the legal BAC limit. For Amy, this contributed to unintentional overconsumption and impaired driving. Being ‘fairly certain’ she was under the general BAC limit, she offered to drive a friend who held a provisional licence with a zero‐BAC requirement:

Then when I was back at the station doing that additional test and I was talking to [the police officer] about like, I was saying like, ‘Oh, I'm confused. I thought I was doing the right thing by dropping my friend home, so she didn't have the chance of blowing over, and only having two drinks’. He [the police officer] almost said, ‘I'm going to hold your hand when I say this, but you've had more than two standard drinks’.

Amy described feeling embarrassed and disappointed in herself and attributed the incident to ‘being uneducated, young, and silly’. Reflecting on prior instances of driving after drinking alcohol, she acknowledged that she had likely exceeded the general BAC limit on other occasions as well.

Thinking back, there would definitely be times where I'd had maybe one or two drinks after work with dinner and then just driven home and not really given it a second thought.

Reflecting on her actions, she described the experience as prompting a reassessment of her knowledge and driving practices and reported being motivated to change her behaviour to avoid a similar experience in the future:

I didn't want to go through that again, knowing how much more alcohol affects my body than I had realized. It changed my view a lot.

Summary of Other Cases Within This Type

Consistent with the optimal case, other accounts reflected reliance on strategies perceived to keep them under the general BAC limit, such as spacing drinks, allowing time before driving and limiting total consumption. However, as for the ideal type, these strategies were based on outdated advice and a limited understanding of individual variation in alcohol metabolism. For example, Erin (48, drink driving) was apprehended the morning after heavy drinking. After commuting to work by train, she borrowed the company car and was stopped for a random breath test.

Surprisingly, I was still over the limit. I felt fine; I didn't feel impaired or anything like that. Also, the fact that I'm driving the company vehicle, I didn't think anything of it. Yes, just very surprised, but I'm aware of that now.

Prior to apprehension, some also described convenience and perceived safety as a motivator for driving in situations when uncertain if they would be over the general alcohol limit. Others described occasional lapses in implementing strategies. Belinda (35, drink driving) recalled that her one‐drink‐per‐hour strategy became less consistent over the course of a social event she was attending:

I did try and time them, so it was like an every one‐hour kind of thing, but I think there were bits and pieces where my glass was topped up and I may have lost count at some point.

All women in this typology reported changes following apprehension, such as reduced consumption, adopting a zero approach when driving and purchasing a breathalyser to confirm a zero BAC reading. Motivation was commonly linked to avoiding further legal or emotional (e.g., shame, embarrassment) consequences that may follow apprehension.

Ideal Type 2: Post‐Apprehension Separators

Four cases represented the Post‐Apprehension Separators typology to varying degrees. This typology comprised three women apprehended for drink driving and one apprehended for drug driving involving THC. Unlike the Inadvertent Drink Drivers, these accounts reflected awareness or suspicion at the time of driving that they were over the general BAC limit or would be detected with a positive THC reading via a roadside drug test. These women described their apprehension as a turning point that prompted them to reassess their substance use and/or their driving practices. Following their apprehension, they implemented deliberate strategies they considered effective to separate their substance use from their driving.

The Optimal Case

The optimal case was Sophia (35, drink driving), whose narrative described daily alcohol use that could begin at any point during the day. She understood her drinking as problematic and described it as a way of managing the stress and expectations associated with motherhood:

Mums, we do have a pretty tough job and trying to juggle everything does become very stressful and it's easy to lean towards the alcohol. It's like if you can't hold all of those balls up in the air, you're not a good mum.

Prior to her apprehension, Sophia described drink driving as routine and a normalised aspect of her daily life. She reported relying on subjective self‐assessments to gauge her level of intoxication and acknowledged instances of driving when she felt ‘borderline’ impaired.

Oh, I could be over, but I'm fine. I know my suburb like I know the back of my hand. I'll be fine. It's just literally down the road, grab some Macca's and we'll be fine. But at the end of the day, your consciousness knows it's wrong. You know it's illegal. You could get caught, but then on the other side, it's like, it'll only take you five minutes.

On the day she was apprehended, a typical day that required driving, she explained she had begun drinking around lunchtime:

I was running around in the morning, got home, had a couple, and then I was like, ‘I'll just have one more before I get the kids’ … I didn't really think about it, to be honest, at the time.

Although she explained she did not feel impaired, she suspected she might be over the general BAC limit and felt that this was wrong. She was apprehended by a random breath test outside her children's school and recorded a low‐range BAC of 0.061%. She described she was surprised by the reading, recognising she ‘obviously had one too many’ but otherwise ‘felt normal’. She described the apprehension as significant, emphasising the problematic nature of her alcohol use and prompting reflection on prior engagement in the behaviour:

You don't realise it's a habit or a problem until you get caught. I actually think I've been drunker than this driving and not caught. You start to think back on the actions.

Sophia took accountability for her actions, describing a deep sense of guilt and shame that affected her mental wellbeing:

You feel depressed all the time because you've done this, you've impacted your entire family, but then you've got to remember that you've done it every single day for the amount of time you've lost your licence.

After the apprehension she described adopting a separation approach when driving: ‘I won't have any more than two a day, and I don't do it until after I know I've done all my errands. I don't want to have to do that again.’ However, she also reported ongoing difficulties regarding her alcohol consumption: ‘I'm never going to [drink drive] again. I also haven't stopped drinking. I've learned not to drive, but it hasn't fixed the problem with the drinking’. Sophia described the apprehension and its aftermath as a ‘lonely’ experience with limited perceived support from the police, the legal system and her broader social environment. She linked this to continued reliance on alcohol as a coping strategy:

It was like, ‘here's your punishment’, then you just continuously punish yourself and there was no support. Not having support leads to depression, which leads to more drinking. It's a vicious cycle.

Summary of Other Cases Within This Type

Two accounts described apprehensions for drink driving following emotionally heightened or distressing situations. Victoria (29, drink driving) described ‘making the stupid decision to drive’ after an argument with her partner at a pub. Amanda (63, drink driving), who reported a history of intimate partner violence, described a similar decision after a confrontation with her then‐partner at his house: ‘At that stage, I was in fight or flight mode. My only option was to get out.’

After apprehension, Post‐Apprehension Separators described adopting strategies such as organising alternative transport, abstaining from alcohol when out, or establishing waiting periods after consuming substances. Christina (47, drug driving), who holds a prescription for medical cannabis, described waiting 24 h before driving following use while Victoria described abstaining from alcohol when out for dinner: ‘I used to have one or two if I went for dinner. I just don't do it now’. For Amanda, the apprehension prompted changes designed to ‘regain control’ over her drinking and reduce the likelihood of future offending. While the strategies to prevent overlap between substance use and driving varied across accounts, they were all self‐imposed and motivated by a desire to avoid further apprehensions in the future.

Ideal Type 3: Drivers Engaged in Recovery

The Drivers Engaged in Recovery typology comprised nine cases, five involving drink driving and four involving drug driving. Like the Inadvertent Drink Drivers and Post‐Apprehension Separators, women in this typology reported no longer engaging in substance‐impaired driving; however, unlike the other typologies, they also described having stopped using substances. Participants described their past substance use as ‘excessive’ or ‘out of control’, with some acknowledging addiction.

The Optimal Case

The optimal case for this typology was Ashley (52, drink driving). By her own account, Ashley had struggled with alcohol addiction driven by mental illness (i.e., post‐traumatic stress disorder, depression and anxiety). She recalled periodic episodes of alcohol binges: ‘Most days were a blur, really. I was a binge drinker. It was not, every now and again or at nighttime. It would go for an extended period, anywhere from one to five days’. Prior to her apprehension, she reported driving after drinking ‘maybe once every couple of weeks’.

Over time, Ashley described driving under the influence more frequently and at higher levels of intoxication. On the day of her apprehension, she reported drinking ‘a ridiculous amount of wine’ and her driving had been so erratic that it was reported to the police. She was charged with a BAC of 0.247%. Reflecting on this period, she described alcohol use as her primary coping strategy: ‘To tell you the truth, it was expected. My lifestyle and the way that I coped with my mental health at the time was basically to drink it away.’ Her daughter was in the car at the time of her apprehension and after this event, she reported temporarily losing custody following the event.

Ashley described that, during this period, she has limited capacity to fully appreciate the consequences of her behaviour: ‘When you're an addict, you don't think about consequences, just your next drink or drug’. Following her licence suspension, she sold her car to remove ‘the temptation’ to drive after drinking alcohol. She continued to drink for some time; however, she described the apprehension and its aftermath as prompting a shift in insight to recognise the seriousness of her behaviour:

In a way, it was probably a Godsend losing my licence because God knows what would have happened if I'd continued to drink‐drive. When I got caught, I had my daughter in the car … I could have killed her.

Consistent with this typology, Ashley reported that she no longer consumes alcohol. She described gaining insight into the root cause of her alcohol use and the harm it had inflicted on herself and others, noting that attending rehabilitation was central to this process.

Summary of Other Cases Within This Type

Among drink driving cases within this typology, most reported at least one mid or high‐range offence and described knowingly driving with a BAC in excess of the general limit. Three of the drink driving cases reported being involved in a motor vehicle crash when affected by alcohol.

For some, substance‐impaired driving had become increasingly normalised as their substance use escalated. Olivia (42, drink driving) described that this pattern was reinforced by perceived success in avoiding detection: ‘You start getting more complacent and I just thought I'll be fine, I've done this before’. Two women described engaging in alcohol consumption while driving. Brittany (41, drink driving), a social worker experiencing work‐related post‐traumatic stress disorder, explained that her car had become a ‘safe space’ in which to drink:

I didn't have a place to drink at home safely. I went out in the car, and that was my safe place. Obviously, it wasn't safe, but it was the only place where I could hide and do my own thing and make my own choices.

Those who were apprehended for drug driving also recounted engaging in highly unsafe driving behaviours. Stephanie (44, drug driving) described hallucinating while driving when she was sleep‐deprived, malnourished and affected by methamphetamine: ‘I've gone around the corner and I swear to God, this three‐legged wolf bounced next to my car’. In other instances, she described feeling ‘invincible’ and engaging in risky driving behaviours (e.g., overtaking on low‐speed suburban streets).

Across cases, many participants linked their substance use to psychosocial stressors (e.g., parenting stress, relationship breakdown), traumatic experiences (e.g., intimate partner violence) and/or mental health difficulties. Samantha (27, drug driving) recounted arriving in Australia with her young son and not having a support system. In an effort to provide for him, she described she started selling and using drugs given the lack of financial security and limited access to childcare:

I couldn't get a job because I couldn't afford daycare. I had no help, so the only way to make money was through drugs. Most of my customers were up at night, so I had to be up at night. The only way to be up at night was to indulge in [methamphetamine] and get in among it all.

At the time of the interview, women in this typology reported they had stopped using substances, with many stating they had gained insight into the cause and consequences of their use and driving practices. Several identified rehabilitation or counselling as important, as reflected by Brittany: ‘Every time I go, I'm learning more and more about myself to stay further away from the drink … I don't know if I would have stopped [drinking] if I wasn't in the program’. Many described the apprehension and its aftermath as a pivotal moment prompting recognition of the risks. Olivia reflected: ‘maybe it was meant to be that I crashed. It's almost like it had to be really bad for me to just stop.’

Discussing the impact of their apprehensions, many spoke about the financial strain associated with fines and interlocks, the effects of licence loss on work opportunities and challenges in meeting family responsibilities. Feelings of fear, shame, embarrassment, and guilt were common, as illustrated by Rebecca (52, drink driving): ‘I was just ashamed of myself, I actually had to admit that I had been done for drink driving’. While it was explained that these feelings often prompted reflection on substance use and driving behaviours, participants also described a negative impact on their emotional wellbeing.

Ideal Type 4: High‐Risk Offenders

Ten cases represented the High‐Risk Offenders typology to varying degrees, including four drink driving and six drug driving cases. This typology was characterised by ongoing substance use and a heightened risk of recidivism, with some women disclosing current or intended future instances of substance‐impaired driving. The risk of reoffence reflected a combination of factors, including risky substance use patterns (e.g., high‐frequency consumption), limited intention to reduce or cease use, low perceived harm associated with substance‐impaired driving, permissive or enabling social environments and a high threshold for punishment where severe legal consequences would be required to deter future offending behaviour.

The Optimal Case

The optimal case for this typology was Sarah (28, drug driving), an unlicenced, long‐term methamphetamine user who described using the substance to self‐medicate symptoms of diagnosed but untreated attention‐deficit hyperactivity disorder. Sarah's job necessitates driving and she reported using methamphetamine before commencing her morning drive: ‘I'll usually take it in the morning as I'll get up. I'll prepare myself for the day, then I'll probably have some more just before I head out’. She described her drug driving as unproblematic: ‘I'm a better driver on this stuff. If I feel sluggish or tired, I'll just stop. I won't keep driving if I feel impaired.’

Sarah reported she had obtained a learner licence in the past but had not progressed further in the licensing system, with her learner licence eventually being disqualified. In addition to unlicensed driving, she described driving an unroadworthy vehicle, stating the tyres ‘are so bald’, adding that ‘it's really bad.’

Sarah reported two drug driving apprehensions in the previous 5‐year period. Following the first apprehension, she described using mobile applications designed to inform her of roadside police operations and adjusted her driving to avoid times and routes where there was a higher risk of apprehension. She explained her parents supported these efforts: ‘After I got pulled over [my mother] was like, “this is what I do”. I might as well listen to her because she hasn't been pulled over by the police’.

Sarah described her second apprehension as a result of ‘getting comfortable’ and inadvertently driving into a roadside testing operation along the highway. She expressed regret regarding the apprehension itself, but not for engaging in the behaviour:

I realised where I went wrong. I didn't check the feeds before that trip. I just got lazy for a second. Now, I get in the car, I check the maps and feeds, I make sure the route is safe every time now.

Sarah indicated that the convictions have not deterred her and explained she continues to drive unlicensed and after using methamphetamines. She also explained her fines have been registered with a debt collection agency and expressed no intention of settling her debt in the foreseeable future: ‘One fun thing, I don't pay my [debt]. They keep threatening to suspend my disqualified learners’. The penalties, in her case licence loss and fines, have not served as a deterrent, as her ability to avoid enforcement appears to reinforce her continued engagement in this behaviour.

Summary of Other Cases Within This Type

Women in this typology reported frequent engagement in substance‐impaired driving, other traffic offences (e.g., speeding) and in some cases, non‐traffic offending. Few described efforts to reduce substance use, with some describing their substance use as necessary or beneficial to their lives. For example, Nicole (44, drug driving), a self‐described heavy cannabis user, stated it ‘motivates’ her and that previous attempts to quit had resulted in increased fatigue. Isabelle (40, drug driving), a truck driver, reported using methamphetamine to drive for extended period without sleep. Despite multiple driving apprehensions through workplace testing, with each resulting in job loss, she described the ease of securing new employment due to a lack of licence checks.

Across accounts, social and professional contexts were often described as permissive, with entrenched norms around substance use and, at times, substance‐impaired driving and other traffic offences. Natalie (30, drink driving) described how her work environment encouraged alcohol consumption:

I drink alcohol a lot because of my work. I have to mingle with a colleague, sometimes a business proposal as well. It's like I have to drink almost every day. I was at the bar with my colleague to have dinner and drinks and a business meeting. When I finish, I drive home from the bar.

Some accounts described highly unsafe driving behaviours, including operating heavy machinery and public transport vehicles while impaired, consuming substances while driving and nearly falling asleep at the wheel. Emily (38, drug driving), a self‐identified heroin addict, described her experience of working while impaired.

I'd be driving a 40‐ton dump truck and I'd be thinking, ‘How am I getting worse at my job?’ I'd get loaded by the excavator and I'd already have a load on the back of the truck, or I'd go tip off a load and I'd be tipping nothing. I literally could not keep it together.

Unlike other typologies, participants in this group rarely expressed concern or remorse about their substance‐impaired driving. Two women specifically mentioned that despite using substances, their driving skills allowed them to remain safe on the road. Nicole stated: ‘I've never had any incidents under the influence. I'm a very good driver, very conservative.’

Several women described only temporary behaviour change following apprehension (e.g., avoiding driving during the licence suspension period only). Natalie described how she quickly reverted to drink driving following her apprehension: ‘After that, for a few weeks, I started driving again. I do normal things. To be honest with you, I still drink, but not that much.’ Others described engaging in evasion tactics such as using apps to locate roadside testing operation sites. For some, fines and suspensions had little effect as they continued to drive unlicenced or left fines unpaid. Isabelle, who had multiple drug‐driving charges from her trucking job, described accumulating approximately AUD 15,000 (9500 USD) in unpaid fines and had warrants issued for her arrest. She noted that her whereabouts were unknown to police, simply stating: ‘Well, I haven't been caught yet’; however, she did comment that she had given up her job as a truck driver.

Discussion

Research is beginning to recognise the gendered dimensions of substance‐impaired driving; however, there remains limited recognition of the heterogeneity among female offenders and the diverse factors that may contribute to their engagement in this behaviour. The current study applied ideal‐type analysis to qualitative interview data from 27 women apprehended for drink or drug driving. Four distinct typologies were identified, each characterised by unique combinations of risk factors, substance use patterns, driving behaviour regulation and situational influences. Two of these typologies reflected gendered‐influenced pathways into substance‐impaired driving, suggesting that both gender‐specific and more traditional explanatory frameworks may hold relevance for understanding women's engagement in the behaviour. The variation in contributing factors across typologies further indicated differential needs and degrees of receptiveness to intervention, indicating that both the content and approach of interventions will need to be tailored to these differences.

Drivers Engaged in Recovery and Post‐Apprehension Separator typologies revealed evidence of gendered pathways into substance‐impaired driving. Participants in these groups reported complex and interrelated psychosocial risk factors that shaped both their substance use and impaired driving behaviour, reflecting patterns more commonly noted among female offenders [30, 31, 32]. For some, these challenges were further compounded by gendered experiences such as interpersonal violence and the stresses of caregiving and motherhood. Both groups also expressed strong feelings of shame and perceived stigma following apprehension, a response that prior research suggests may be more pronounced among women who engage in substance‐impaired driving [19, 33].

Despite these shared features, important distinctions between the two groups were observed, with implications for intervention design. While the Post‐Apprehension Separators refrained from using substance when driving, among Drivers Engaged in Recovery, past substance‐impaired driving had been intertwined with their substance use patterns and the broader psychosocial context in which it occurred. These women frequently reported histories of excessive substance use and described difficulty separating substance use from driving behaviour, often disclosing instances of driving while severely impaired. Their substance use was commonly linked to self‐reported mental health issues and psychosocial stressors. This is consistent with previous research showing that female substance‐impaired drivers are more likely than their male counterparts to engage in daily substance use, meet diagnostic criteria for substance use disorders and report co‐occurring mental health conditions [34, 35, 36].

Substance‐impaired driving is commonly addressed through enforcement and legal punishments, such as licence suspension. For the Drivers Engaged in Recovery, the consequences of being apprehended for substance‐impaired driving often served as a catalyst for initiating changes to their substance use behaviour, which in turn addressed their engagement in impaired driving. Their narratives emphasised the role and transformative potential of interventions such as rehabilitation and counselling, suggesting that integrating support services with existing punitive measures may reduce recidivism. While the current study captured the experiences of women who had reached a pivotal point of change, a holistic and integrated approach that engages with the issues underlying substance‐impaired driving may be particularly beneficial for those who continue to struggle with substance use in the absence of adequate support.

Similar to the Drivers Engaged in Recovery, the Post‐Apprehension Separators often described their apprehension as a catalyst for the reconsideration of their substance use and driving behaviours. However, rather than pursuing abstinence, this group reported continued substance use while implementing self‐imposed strategies aimed at preventing impaired driving. These strategies included not using any substances until all driving tasks had been completed for the day or refraining from consuming alcohol at dinner if driving. Both the Drivers Engaged in Recovery and Post‐Apprehension Separators narratives revealed that they were able to reduce or eliminate their offending behaviour, suggesting that both separation and treatment can serve as viable pathways to lasting change.

In contrast, the High‐Risk Offenders typology consisted of women who actively used substances, committed traffic violations over and above substance‐impaired driving and had limited awareness or concerns regarding the risks associated with this behaviour. Participants in this group often described deliberate efforts to evade detection, expressed little remorse and presented a high likelihood of recidivism. Compared to the other typologies, these participants were less responsive to legal sanctions, with some reporting they continued to drive while unlicenced and avoided paying their fines. They also experienced little or no social deterrence as substance‐impaired driving was frequently normalised or overlooked by their friends, family, or coworkers.

While existing research attributes women's substance‐impaired driving to psychosocial distress and substance dependence [19, 34, 35, 36, 37], the High‐Risk Offenders characterised in this study more closely resembled male offenders, exhibiting histories of traffic violations and continued engagement in illegal behaviour despite prior legal punishment [18]. This finding suggests the possibility of dual pathways into substance‐impaired driving among women, one shaped by psychosocial distress and another more closely aligned with male offending patterns. This suggestion is supported in the broader criminological literature; for example, research on juvenile offenders has found that while factors such as impulsivity and criminality shaped pathways for both boys and some girls, a distinct pathway characterised by abuse, mental illness and substance use appears unique to female offenders [38].

The High‐Risk Offenders identified in this study present challenges for intervention given their tendency to evade detection, disregard legal consequences and resist change. For this group, more effective enforcement strategies (e.g., targeted enforcement) and harsher penalties may be necessary to reduce the likelihood of reoffending. However, while legal sanctions play a role, research on deterrence suggests that the perceived certainty and severity of punishment are experienced differently across offender groups [39]. The findings of the current study indicate that increasing the severity of punishment alone, without considering how social and psychological factors interact to shape its impact, may fail to produce meaningful or lasting change. As such, interventions targeting this typology may need to extend beyond legislative approaches and address the broader social context. For instance, community‐level interventions that challenge permissive attitudes towards substance‐impaired driving (e.g., collaborations with local communities and opinion leaders to shift social norms) could compliment enforcement‐focused approaches.

The final typology, the Inadvertent Drink Drivers, comprised first‐time offenders who unintentionally exceeded the general BAC limit, primarily due to ineffective strategies and misconceptions about alcohol metabolism. Following apprehension, participants in this group reported that they successfully modified their behaviour and ceased engaging in drink driving. Their experiences highlight the difficulty of accurately estimating BAC and the continued reliance on outdated strategies, such as counting the number of drinks consumed over time, a method once promoted by public health messaging but now largely replaced by campaigns promoting the complete separation of alcohol and driving (e.g., ‘You lapse, you lose’ [40]). Unlike the other typologies, the aetiology of offending in this group appeared less complex and more situational. Notably, participants demonstrated a strong willingness to change when presented with corrective information, suggesting they may be particularly responsive to education‐based interventions, such as social media or public awareness campaigns. Although not captured in our study, providing educational information on drug driving laws and the detection time of drugs may also be beneficial for drug drivers [29].

Collectively, these findings suggest that engagement strategies should be aligned with women's position along the typology continuum. Inadvertent Drink Drivers may be most effectively reached through population‐level education delivered at licensing milestones or via targeted public messaging that corrects misconceptions about alcohol metabolism. Post‐Apprehension Separators who were not yet ready to modify their consumption patterns appeared receptive to brief, practical interventions delivered at the point of enforcement, such as guidance on effective separation strategies and access to planning tools that support substance–driving separation. For Drivers Engaged in Recovery, pathways to treatment, counselling and integrated support services are likely to be critical, particularly when delivered alongside enforcement processes. In contrast, High‐Risk Offenders may require sustained, enforcement‐led approaches combined with interventions that disrupt permissive social norms and address structural contributors to ongoing offending.

Limitations

To the authors' knowledge, this study is the first to apply ideal‐type analysis to identify distinct typologies of female substance‐impaired drivers, offering novel insights into the diversity of their characteristics and intervention needs. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although four typologies were identified, it is possible additional offender profiles may exist that were not captured due to self‐selection bias or the recruitment methods employed. Women with different experiences or perspectives may have remained unseen, limiting our understanding and highlighting the need for further research.

Second, researcher perspectives and prior knowledge may have influenced the interpretation of participant narratives. Despite efforts to minimise bias through robust design, existing frameworks on gendered pathways and substance use may have shaped how the characteristics of the typologies were identified, potentially overlooking factors that are less commonly identified in the literature.

Although participants were apprehended within a 5‐year window, the temporal distance from the event varied across the sample. This may have influenced how experiences were recalled and interpreted, particularly for women whose substance use or life circumstances had since changed. Future research incorporating longitudinal designs or time‐sensitive analyses would help clarify how typology membership shifts in relation to the timing of apprehension.

Finally, our study included both drink and drug drivers without explicitly differentiating between them. Failing to account for potential differences between drink and drug driving may obscure important nuances in motivations, risk factors and intervention responses, warranting further research into how typologies may be shaped by particular substances.

Conclusions and Future Research

This study provides important evidence that gender influences substance‐impaired driving, reinforcing and extending the limited research that highlights the relevance of gendered experiences in understanding this behaviour [18, 19, 20]. However, by allowing for heterogeneity to emerge, the current findings demonstrate that gendered experiences are not universal; rather, women's experiences with substance‐impaired driving are diverse and shaped by different psychosocial, behavioural and situational factors.

Critically, these findings emphasise differences in the types of resources, supports and constraints that influence women's capacity to avoid reoffending. For women who experience gendered pathways and circumstances, interpreting these findings through an equity lens highlights that women's capacity to avoid substance‐impaired driving is shaped not only by individual choice, but also by broader social and structural conditions. National strategies that prioritise gender equity, including those addressing women's mental health, family violence, economic security and caregiving burden, are therefore relevant to preventing substance‐impaired driving among women. Without recognising these contextual constraints, there is a risk that shame and stigma are reinforced rather than mitigated. Aligning impaired driving responses with broader gender‐responsive policy frameworks may enhance women's agency to change and support engagement with intervention efforts.

Future research should seek to validate these typologies using quantitative methods (e.g., cluster or latent class analysis) and examine their fluidity over time and predictive utility for recidivism and interventional responsiveness. Further, co‐design approaches involving women who have lived experience of substance‐impaired driving should be employed to help inform these typologies and guide the design of gender‐responsive, acceptable and effective interventions. By acknowledging the complexity of substance‐impaired driving among women and addressing the interplay of individual, social and systemic factors, interventions can be more effectively tailored to reduce recidivism and promote lasting behavioural change.

Finally, while psychosocial influences on women's impaired driving are likely relevant across countries, typology distribution may vary depending on enforcement intensity, BAC and drug‐driving laws, justice system structure and cultural norms around alcohol and drug use. Replication in jurisdictions with differing legal or social contexts is necessary to test cross‐national generalisability.
