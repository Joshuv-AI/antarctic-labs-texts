Long-term occupational exposure to volatile organic compounds and progressive pulmonary function decline: a 5-year cohort study in the printing industry
Authors: Reza Ghasemi Elah Abadi, Mohsen Sadeghi-Yarandi, Abolfazl Mohammadbeigi, Ahmad Soltanzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The printing industry involves continuous exposure to volatile organic compounds (VOCs), potentially leading to pulmonary impairments. This study aimed to evaluate long-term pulmonary impairments among printing workers over a five-year period. A five-year retrospective cohort study (2020–2024) was conducted involving 87 operational printing workers (exposed group) and 86 administrative staff members (unexposed group). Occupational exposure to VOCs (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene, isopropyl alcohol, ethyl acetate, ethanol) was assessed using NIOSH-based air sampling and GC–FID analysis. Pulmonary function was measured annually using standardized spirometry according to American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines. Respiratory symptoms were evaluated with the ATS questionnaire. Groups were comparable in demographics except working hours (p < 0.05). A significant upward trend in VOCs concentrations—particularly toluene, xylene, and isopropanol—was observed throughout the follow-up period (p < 0.05). Exposed workers showed declines in FEV1, FVC, PEF, and FEF (p < 0.001), with lower values than controls in 2024 (p < 0.001, except FEV1/FVC p = 0.059). Respiratory symptoms—including dyspnea, cough, wheezing, sputum production, and hoarseness—were reported significantly more frequently among exposed workers (p < 0.05). Long-term occupational exposure to VOCs in the printing industry is associated with progressive decline of pulmonary function and an increased prevalence of respiratory symptoms, even at concentrations below established occupational exposure limits. These findings underscore the need for continuous monitoring of air pollutants, regular spirometry screening, improved ventilation, substitution of hazardous solvents, and stricter adherence to protective measures within the printing industry.

Introduction

The printing industry is one of the oldest and most dynamic sectors. It holds a unique position in the social, economic, and cultural life of societies1. Solvents such as benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene are among the most widely used compounds in this industry due to their unique physicochemical properties2. These substances enhance pigment solubility and facilitate ink transfer to printing surfaces, thereby improving product quality. However, these compounds are volatile, causing their vapors to easily disperse into the work environment and enter the body via the respiratory system2–4.

Several studies have indicated that occupational exposure to volatile organic compounds (VOCs) can cause acute and chronic pulmonary disorders. Acute effects may include upper airway irritation, coughing, shortness of breath, throat irritation, and eye discomfort. Severe cases may trigger allergic reactions or anaphylaxis. Chronic effects are more commonly observed as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), occupational asthma, chronic bronchitis, and reduced lung vital capacity5–7. Spirometry parameters such as forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), the FEV1/FVC ratio, forced expiratory flow (FEF), and peak expiratory flow (PEF) are recognized as key indicators for monitoring respiratory effects from such exposures. Declines in these indices reflect functional lung damage and serve as markers of disease progression8–10. Sensitive indices of impairment, such as FEF25–75, are considered early markers of airway damage and may decline before traditional spirometric parameters, such as FEV1 or FVC, show abnormalities10–12. In addition to BTEX compounds, other solvents such as isopropanol, ethyl acetate, and ethanol are commonly used in printing operations, contributing to complex and cumulative exposure profiles. Emerging evidence suggests that VOCs may impair pulmonary function not only through direct airway irritation but also via oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and small airway remodeling. Several studies have demonstrated that exposures below established occupational limits (e.g., threshold limit values [TLVs] or occupational exposure limits [OELs]) can still contribute to progressive pulmonary impairment, highlighting the inadequacy of relying solely on these threshold values11,13,14.

According to the International Labor Organization (ILO), 15–20% of workers in the chemical and printing industries in developed countries are at risk of developing occupational pulmonary diseases. In developing countries, this figure is estimated to exceed 30%, primarily due to inadequate protective infrastructure and insufficient workplace ventilation10,12,15. In Iran, printing workers are exposed to high concentrations of VOCs, and a significant proportion of them exhibit early symptoms of respiratory disorders11,13,16. Over the past decade, research has revealed that mean concentrations of toluene and xylene in some printing workshops have exceeded the permissible limits set by international standards (TLVs). Furthermore, spirometric evaluations of these workers have shown significant declines in parameters such as FEV1 and PEF compared to control groups10,12,13,15.

Pulmonary disorders resulting from occupational exposure are not merely individual health concerns; they also impose significant social and economic burdens. These consequences include both direct and indirect costs related to the treatment of chronic respiratory diseases, as well as reduced work capacity, increased absenteeism, decreased productivity, and a diminished quality of life. In the competitive printing industry, which relies heavily on production quality and speed, such outcomes can severely threaten economic sustainability17–19.

Research gap

Although previous studies have documented both acute and chronic respiratory effects of solvent and VOCs exposure in the printing industry, most of this research has been cross-sectional, focusing on single-time assessments of exposure or pulmonary function5–9,13,20. Such study designs limit the ability to capture progressive, cumulative declines in lung function over time. Furthermore, only a few investigations have simultaneously measured longitudinal exposure levels and serial spirometric changes, particularly in developing countries where protective measures and ventilation systems are often inadequate. Evidence also remains limited regarding early impairment of airway function, which may precede declines in conventional spirometric indices. This gap highlights the need for well-designed longitudinal cohort studies that monitor both environmental exposures and respiratory outcomes to clarify the trajectory and mechanisms of pulmonary function decline among printing workers.

To further reduce selection and confounding biases, baseline comparability between groups was systematically assessed using standardized questionnaires and archival records. Statistical analyses (e.g., independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables) confirmed group equivalence on most factors, except for working hours and gender, which were controlled for in subsequent multivariate models. History of infection was assessed via self-report questionnaires and medical records (12% in exposed vs. 10% in unexposed; p = 0.72, chi-square). Sensitivity analyses excluded infected cases. COVID-19 infection status was assessed annually using a standardized question: ‘Have you had a medically confirmed COVID-19 infection (PCR or rapid antigen test) since the last assessment?‘”. During national COVID-19 restrictions, office staff could work from home, while printing workers were required to be on-site daily.

Data collection was standardized annually and integrated environmental monitoring, spirometry, and symptom questionnaires. Quality assurance measures included instrument calibration and inter-rater reliability checks for assessments.

Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Qom University of Medical Sciences (approval code: IR.MUQ.REC.1404.050). All procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study steps are present in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Demographic and personal characteristics

Demographic and individual data were collected at baseline using a structured checklist developed by the research team and validated by occupational health experts. Variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), job tenure, smoking status, marital status, and average weekly working hours. Smoking history was verified through a structured interview. Working hours were self-reported and cross-checked with company records to ensure accuracy.

Exposure assessment

Airborne concentrations were determined using standardized protocols in accordance with validated National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) methods. Specifically, NIOSH Method 150121,22 was employed for BTEX compounds (aromatic hydrocarbons), NIOSH Method 1400 for alcohols (ethanol and isopropyl alcohol)23, and NIOSH Method 1457 for ethyl acetate (an ester)24. This Information was extracted from the company’s industrial hygiene records covering the five-year study period (2020–2024).

Personal samples were collected using calibrated low-flow sampling pumps (SKC Pocket Pumpequivalent, with flow rates of 200–300 mL/min, equipped with activated charcoal sorbent tubes positioned in the workers’ breathing zone. The sampling frequency was annual, with at least three measurements per worker per year in the exposed group to account for seasonal and operational variations. Monitoring of the unexposed group was conducted similarly but focused on office areas to confirm negligible VOC levels. Collected samples were desorbed using carbon disulfide (CS₂) or other appropriate solvents (e.g., CS₂ with 1% methanol for alcohols) and analyzed by gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC–FID).

Quality control measures included field blanks (10% of samples), laboratory blanks, spiked samples for recovery assessment, calibration standards, and duplicate analyses (relative standard deviation < 10%). Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were determined for each analyte. Time-weighted averages (TWAs) were calculated and compared to ACGIH threshold limit values (TLVs). Unexposed group exposure was negligible.

Pulmonary function testing

Pulmonary function measurements—including forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), the FEV1/FVC ratio, forced expiratory flow at 25–75% of FVC (FEF25-75), peak expiratory flow (PEF), and volume exhaled at the end of forced expiration (VEXT, reflecting residual expiratory volume and efficiency)—were obtained annually from all participants throughout the five-year follow-up period (2020–2024)10.

Spirometric indices, including FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC, PEF, and FEF25–75, were expressed as percent predicted (%pred) using the Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) multi-ethnic reference equations to account for age, sex, height, and ethnicity.

The start of forced expiration was assessed using the extrapolated volume (Vext), which was expressed as a percentage of the measured forced vital capacity (FVC). Maneuvers were considered acceptable when Vext was ≤ 5% of FVC. All included maneuvers met this criterion, with Vext values of approximately 4%, indicating an appropriate and rapid start of forced expiration25.

Spirometry was conducted by trained occupational health technicians in strict accordance with the 2019 American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society (ERS) guidelines for spirometry standardization. Pre-test instructions included abstaining from smoking, caffeine, alcohol, heavy meals, and vigorous exercise for at least 2–4 h prior to testing. A minimum of three acceptable and reproducible maneuvers were obtained. The best effort (highest sum of FEV1 and FVC) was selected for analysis10. Finally, all parameters were analyzed longitudinally to capture progressive declines, with clinical thresholds applied according to ATS/ERS interpretive guidelines10–12.

Respiratory symptom assessment

Respiratory symptoms were evaluated annually in both the exposed and unexposed groups throughout the five-year follow-up period (2020–2024). The assessment employed the standardized American Thoracic Society (ATS). Key symptoms assessed included chronic cough, exertional or resting dyspnea, sputum production, wheezing, and hoarseness, chosen for their relevance to VOC-induced irritation and inflammation of the upper and lower airways10–12. Workers were asked about the presence, frequency, and duration of symptoms, with chronicity defined according to ATS guidelines as symptoms occurring on most days (at least three days per week) for at least three months within the preceding year.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, complemented by visual inspection of Q–Q plots and evaluation of skewness and kurtosis. Homogeneity of variances was tested using Levene’s test. Descriptive statistics are presented as means with SD for continuous variables and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Baseline differences between the exposed and unexposed groups were evaluated using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. To assess longitudinal changes in pulmonary function indices over the five-year follow-up period, repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was employed. Between-group differences in pulmonary outcomes in the final year (2024) were examined using independent t-tests. The prevalence of respiratory symptoms was compared between groups using chi-square tests. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the assessment of respiratory symptoms, significant differences were observed between the exposed and unexposed groups. Shortness of breath was reported exclusively among exposed workers, with nearly one-third experiencing this symptom, while none of the unexposed workers reported it (p < 0.001). Discussion

This five-year study investigated pulmonary function impairments in printing industry workers and provided valuable insights into the effects of exposure to volatile organic compounds (VOCs) on spirometric indices. Data analysis revealed significant declines in key pulmonary function parameters—including FEV1, FVC, PEF, and FEF—in the exposed group over the study period. However, the FEV1/FVC ratio did not show a significant change. These findings enhance our understanding of the consequences of prolonged exposure to common chemicals in the printing industry.

There were no significant differences in mean age, work experience, or BMI among the groups. However, the unexposed group worked significantly more hours per week. There were also significant differences in gender distribution and marital status: a higher proportion of men and married individuals were in the exposed group. Smoking status did not vary between groups. Controlling for age, BMI, and smoking reduces the risk of confounding respiratory outcomes. Nevertheless, gender differences may influence spirometric reference values. Using “percent predicted” values largely mitigates such differences through age-, height-, and sex-based reference equations. International spirometry reference standards (GLI/ERS) emphasize converting raw values to percent-predicted values to minimize the effects of demographics. Consequently, subsequent differences in pulmonary outcomes observed between groups, despite similarities in age, BMI, and smoking status, are consistent with these standardized approaches26.

The balance of age, BMI, smoking status, and other demographic factors, as well as the use of standardized spirometry reference values, strengthens the study’s ability to compare pulmonary outcomes. This supports the conclusion that the observed differences are primarily due to occupational exposure rather than major confounders15,27.

Environmental monitoring data revealed that printing plant workers were consistently exposed to benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene while operating machinery. From 2020 to 2024, the mean exposure levels of these chemicals fluctuated significantly (Table 2). While these concentrations are below the occupational exposure limits set by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), and the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH), evidence suggests that chronic exposure to these chemicals, even at low levels, may negatively impact respiratory health15,28. Repeated measures analyses confirmed a significant increase in exposure over the five-year period. Toluene and xylene levels were notably higher in the latter years compared to the initial study period. This gradual rise likely reflects increased workloads, reduced ventilation efficiency, or greater use of VOC-containing chemicals.

Previous studies have demonstrated an association between low-level exposure to air pollution and respiratory symptoms, as well as a decline in pulmonary function. These effects are exacerbated by cumulative exposure. Dose-response evidence indicates that higher cumulative exposure is associated with decreased forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and an increased risk of respiratory issues. The present findings of progressive, multi-compound exposure align with this pattern15,27,29. The studied chemicals may act through sensory irritation and airway inflammation, which are mediated by oxidative stress. Population studies have reported that toluene and xylene influence pulmonary function through oxidative stress mechanisms. Toxicological evaluations of BTEX mixtures also suggest potential synergistic interactions. Therefore, gradual, multi-compound exposure provides sufficient biological and epidemiological evidence to predict subtle yet significant declines in pulmonary indices, even at concentrations below traditional occupational limits29,30.

All evaluated indices, including FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC, PEF, and FEF showed significant declines between 2020 and 2024, with reductions ranging from approximately 4 to 12%-predicted units. This pattern is consistent with “subclinical/mild pulmonary impairment,” which could represent an early stage of small airway disease or a non-obstructive pattern. ATS/ERS spirometry interpretation standards emphasize paying attention to sequential changes, even within the normal range31.

The results revealed a significant downward trend in mean FEV1 from 2020 to 2024. Since FEV1 is a sensitive measure of airway obstruction, a decline indicates impaired lung function. This sustained decrease suggests that VOCs exposure may accelerate chronic obstructive processes32–34.

A concurrent reduction in FVC was observed throughout the study. Decreased FVC generally reflects restricted lung volumes. In an occupational context, it may result from chronic inflammation or structural changes in the lung parenchyma or airway walls. The simultaneous decline in both FEV1 and FVC indicates that VOC exposure negatively affects vital capacity and airflow32–34. A significant reduction in PEF was also reported. PEF is a sensitive indicator of large airway function; therefore, a decrease suggests exposure has compromised the central airways as well32–34. FEV1/FVC ratio decreased in the exposed group over five years. However, the difference compared to the unexposed group in the final year was not statistically significant (p = 0.059). A significant decline in FEV1 accompanied by a smaller reduction in FVC typically results in a lower ratio, which is characteristic of an obstructive pattern32–34. In this study, however, the nearly balanced decline in FEV1 and FVC led to a preserved ratio. This suggests that VOC exposure may induce a mixed (obstructive–restrictive) pattern rather than purely obstructive changes35.

Concurrent reductions in forced expiratory flow (FEF) demonstrate that occupational exposure affects both the central and peripheral regions of the lungs. This indicates that the impact extends beyond peak flows to expiratory volumes. Similar findings have been reported in industrial settings where long-term VOCs exposure reduces expiratory dynamics, even when FEV1/FVC remains normal36,37.

Studies of occupational exposure in refineries, the paint industry, and the resin industry, as well as BTEX dose-response investigations, have shown that long-term exposure is associated with gradual declines in FEV1, FVC, and flow rates. In some studies, the FEV1/FVC ratio remains normal while other indices decrease, indicating small airway involvement. The present results align with these observations38. Mechanistically, inhaling BTEX/VOCs can induce oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, which can lead to epithelial damage and airway wall remodeling. Human evidence supports the role of toluene and xylene in impairing pulmonary function through these pathways30,39.

The repeated-measures design and use of multiple flow/volume indices in this study strengthen the findings. The continuous, small, yet significant, declines across all indices in the exposed group over five years are consistent with known dose-response relationships and biological mechanisms, which reinforces a causal role for occupational exposure29.

In the final study year (2024), comparisons between the exposed and unexposed groups revealed significantly lower FEV1, FVC, and PEF in the exposed group, though the FEV1/FVC ratio remained non-significant. This pattern of reduced volumes and flows with a relatively preserved ratio is consistent with a “non-obstructive/restrictive” or small airway pattern rather than classical obstruction. ATS/ERS guidelines caution that a normal ratio does not exclude the effects of exposure, particularly when supported by other indices and exposure evidence9,26. These results emphasize that cumulative VOC exposure leads to measurable reductions in lung capacity and airflow over time. Unexposed office personnel in the same industry did not experience similar declines, which provides direct evidence of an occupational role in pulmonary impairment.

A gradual decline in FEV1 and FVC over five years suggests that long-term VOC exposure may result in chronic respiratory diseases, such as COPD or occupational bronchitis. Reduced PEF indicates large airway involvement, which can result in clinically significant exertional dyspnea. An absence of significant FEV1/FVC decline suggests early-stage pulmonary injury manifests as a mixed restrictive-obstructive pattern rather than pure obstruction32–35. Similar occupational studies in refineries, fuel stations, and solvent industries have reported meaningful, though often subclinical, declines in FEV1, FVC, and flows compared to reference groups31. Biologically, exposure to multiple VOC/BTEX compounds can cause persistent mild inflammation and dysfunction of the airway epithelium and smooth muscle. This initially manifests as reduced flows with preserved ratios30. Given the study’s strong control of major confounders (e.g., age, BMI, and smoking) and use of standard reference values, the significant multi-index spirometric decline observed in 2024, despite preserved ratios, aligns well with recorded exposures. This decline indicates a subthreshold but harmful effect on pulmonary function9,40.

Furthermore, the prevalence of major respiratory symptoms, including dyspnea, cough, wheezing, sputum production, and hoarseness, was significantly higher in the exposed group. Dyspnea showed a particularly marked difference, with 30 cases versus zero in the unexposed group. This pattern clearly aligns with the observed small, persistent declines in pulmonary function. Multiple occupational studies have linked VOCs exposure to an increased prevalence of cough, wheezing, and dyspnea. The consistency between exposure, spirometry changes, and symptom prevalence, along with an adequate sample size for statistical testing (e.g., chi-square), strengthens the inference of a causal relationship between exposure and respiratory outcomes41.

A decline in pulmonary function among printing industry workers has multiple consequences. At the individual level, affected workers may experience reduced ability to perform daily activities, an increased need for medical care, and diminished quality of life. From an occupational perspective, decreased pulmonary function can result in increased absenteeism, reduced productivity, and higher direct and indirect costs for employers and the healthcare system. These findings underscore the importance of preventive interventions and continuous occupational monitoring. The significant decline in FEV1 observed over the five-year study period aligns with previous research in this industry and other occupational settings. Other studies have reported that the mean FEV1 in exposed groups is significantly lower than in control groups. This aligns with our observation of a proportional decline in FEV1 with exposure duration27,42,43. The reduction in FVC indicates restrictive lung impairment. Findings from Alif et al. (2019) and Hosseininejad et al. (2016) support our results, suggesting that the pattern of FVC decline among printing workers is persistent and notable44,45.

The significant reduction in PEF in the present study is important because PEF is sensitive to large airway narrowing. Abdulateef and Talib reported that the mean PEF of exposed workers was significantly lower than that of the control group46. This is consistent with our findings of a significant five-year decline and a pronounced difference by the end of the study. Interestingly, no significant difference in FEV1/FVC was observed between the exposed and unexposed groups. This suggests that VOCs exposure primarily reduces overall pulmonary function rather than producing an obstructive pattern33,35. Taken together, these results suggest that VOCs mixtures in the printing industry may have obstructive and restrictive effects on lung function. The progressive increase in exposure to the studied chemical compounds over five years, the gradual yet significant decline in pulmonary indices among the exposed group, the significant cross-sectional differences favoring the unexposed group in 2024, and the higher prevalence of respiratory symptoms provide a clear picture of the harmful effects of occupational VOCs exposure on respiratory health, even at exposure levels below traditional occupational limits.

Recent review study show that COVID-19 can cause transient or persistent lung function decline and respiratory symptoms47. However, in our cohort, the 2% absolute difference in infection history between groups (12% vs. 10%, p = 0.72) is small, and sensitivity analyses excluding all infected participants did not alter any spirometric findings. Moreover, the progressive decline in pulmonary function began before widespread COVID-19 circulation and followed a steady linear trend over five years, consistent with cumulative VOCs exposure rather than post-infection sequelae. Therefore, COVID-19 does not explain the observed occupational effects.

Finally, our finding of a significantly higher prevalence of respiratory symptoms in the exposed group aligns with results from a study in South Korean beauty salons, which linked higher concentrations of acetone, benzaldehyde, and toluene to the experience of adverse health effects. Furthermore, that study indicated that the carcinogenic risk of formaldehyde exceeded standard limits, strengthening concerns about the consequences of chronic VOCs exposure even in non-industrial settings like printing and cosmetology48. Another research among beauty salon personnel found that respirable PM levels varied widely and were strongly driven by ambient air quality, yet still showed a clear association with increased respiratory symptoms49. This highlights a common pattern: in many urban professions, workers face a dual burden from both specific occupational agents and general environmental pollution, culminating in measurable respiratory effects.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

This study has several notable strengths. First, its five-year longitudinal design enabled the evaluation of cumulative and progressive changes in pulmonary function, rather than relying on cross-sectional snapshots. Second, the simultaneous assessment of workplace chemical exposures, pulmonary function tests, and clinical symptoms allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of respiratory health outcomes. Third, the use of census sampling within the target population minimized selection bias and enhanced representativeness. Fourth, pulmonary function was measured using standardized ATS/ERS 2019 protocols and expressed as percent-predicted values based on GLI reference equations, ensuring accuracy and comparability across individuals. Finally, the application of validated statistical methods for both within- and between-group analyses improved the reliability of the results and strengthened causal inference.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted within a single industry in Iran, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other occupational or geographic settings. Although major confounders such as age, BMI, and smoking were controlled for, the study had limited capacity to account for lifestyle factors like physical activity and diet, which may influence pulmonary outcomes. Furthermore, the study did not include biological assessments, such as biomarkers of exposure, oxidative stress, or inflammation, which could have provided more robust mechanistic insights. Exposure data were obtained from industrial hygiene records; therefore, individual-level cumulative exposure metrics were not available for every worker, raising the possibility of exposure misclassification. Additionally, spirometry was performed annually, which, while sufficient for detecting long-term trends, may have missed short-term or seasonal variations. Moreover, respiratory symptoms were assessed only at the end of follow-up (2024). Although no participant had a diagnosed pulmonary disease at baseline, we cannot completely exclude the possibility of pre-existing symptom differences between groups. Future studies should include baseline symptom assessment. Finally, although the sample size was adequate for detecting significant differences, it limited the ability to conduct detailed subgroup analyses (e.g., by age). Potential residual confounding from COVID-19, despite non-significant infection rate differences and robust sensitivity analyses; VOCs increases may stem from post-pandemic production surges.

Future research directions

Future studies should build upon these findings by incorporating multi-center cohorts from diverse industries and geographic regions to enhance generalizability. Integrating biological assessments, such as biomarkers of exposure, oxidative stress, and inflammation, would offer more robust mechanistic insights into the observed pulmonary effects. Research should also investigate the influence of lifestyle factors (e.g., physical activity, diet, and environmental exposures outside the workplace) on susceptibility to VOC-related respiratory impairment. Additionally, longer-term follow-ups extending beyond five years are necessary to determine whether early subclinical declines progress to chronic respiratory diseases, such as COPD. Finally, intervention-based studies—evaluating the effects of improved ventilation, substitution of hazardous solvents, and the use of personal protective equipment—are crucial to identify the most effective preventive strategies for reducing occupational risks in the printing industry.

Conclusion

This five-year cohort study demonstrated that occupational exposure to VOCs in the printing industry—including benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene, isopropyl alcohol, ethyl acetate, and ethanol—results in progressive declines in pulmonary function and an increased prevalence of respiratory symptoms. Despite demographic similarities between groups, exposed workers experienced significant reductions in FEV1, FVC, PEF, and FEF accompanied by increased symptoms such as dyspnea and cough. The pattern of a preserved FEV1/FVC ratio alongside declines in other indices suggests early small-airway and mixed ventilatory impairment rather than classic obstructive lung disease.

Importantly, exposure levels were often below established occupational limits; however, cumulative exposure was still associated with measurable respiratory harm. This underscores that current ventilation and control measures are insufficient. The findings highlight the need for stricter occupational policies, including continuous environmental monitoring, periodic spirometry testing, engineering controls, substitution of hazardous chemicals, and consistent use of personal protective equipment. Education for both workers and management is also essential, as the health effects of VOCs exposure are cumulative and may only manifest after years of work.

In summary, the printing industry presents significant long-term respiratory risks. Neglecting preventive measures not only endangers workers’ health but also results in substantial social and economic costs, making proactive monitoring and a stronger safety culture an urgent necessity.
