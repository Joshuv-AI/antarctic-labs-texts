Artificial Intelligence‐Enhanced Electrocardiogram for Longitudinal Evaluation of Right Ventricular Dysfunction After Acute Pulmonary Embolism: Rational and Design of CURES‐CARE Study
Authors: Feiya Xu, Yanshuang Lyu, Zijing Li, Guohui Fan, Jing Ma, Xiaomeng Zhang, Xiaomao Xu, Lisong Qiao, Yaodan Liang, Yong Qi, Junyi Shang, Jing Han, Weijia Liu, Chengpeng Gao, Kun Yan, Fajiu Li, Xuechun Wang, Yue Li, Zhe Cheng, Shijia Geng, Aili Li, Peiran Yang, Shenda Hong, Zhenguo Zhai, Dingyi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRight ventricular dysfunction (RVD) due to pulmonary embolism (PE) is associated with poor prognosis and serves as a potential contributor to the occurrence and development of post‐PE syndrome (PPES). However, the longitudinal evolution of right ventricular function remains poorly understood due to the limited feasibility of repeated assessments. We aim to characterize the dynamic progression of RVD after acute PE using longitudinal electrocardiogram (ECG) signals analyzed with artificial intelligence (AI), together with selected biomarkers. China pUlmonary thromboembolism REgistry Study‐CARioElectro (CURES‐CARE) study, a prospective multicenter cohort study, will enroll 500 patients with intermediate‐ or high‐risk acute PE from seven tertiary hospitals in China. All enrolled patients will be followed up for 12 months. During hospitalization, patients will undergo continuous ECG monitoring for 72 h, followed by daily ECG recording by a portable device throughout the follow‐up period. Blood samples will be collected at admission, discharge, and at 3, 6, and 12 months. AI‐based approaches will be applied to identify dynamic patterns in ECG signals and biomarker trajectories, integrated with biomarkers and imaging, to identify phenotypes of RVD progression and prognosis. CURES‐CARE study will determine the incidence and risk factors of persistent RVD after acute PE, explore ECG‐derived features associated with RV function, and evaluate the predictive value of multimodal data integration for long‐term outcomes of PE. The study is expected to provide new insights into evolution of RVD, enable earlier risk stratification, and inform personalized management strategies to improve long‐term outcomes in PE survivors. Introduction

Acute pulmonary embolism (PE) is a common and potentially fatal cause of both short‐ and long‐term health consequences [1]. Despite receiving standard anticoagulation, 40%–60% of patients still suffer from persistent symptoms such as dyspnea and impaired physical capacity after acute PE, which were defined as post‐pulmonary embolism syndrome (PPES) [2]. The 2019 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) clinical guidelines on the management of PE also emphasize the clinical importance of recognizing and managing PPES [3].

Right ventricular dysfunction (RVD) secondary to PE is a major determinant of poor prognosis in PE and has been considered as an important contributor to the development of PPES [4]. Our recent meta‐analysis reported that the prevalence of RVD at 3 months, 6 months and 1 year post‐PE was 34%, 26%, and 34%, respectively [5], emphasizing the clinical and economic burden of chronic RVD. However, most available data are retrospective and may be biased in estimating the incidence of RVD after acute PE. Meanwhile, existing cohort studies mainly focus on treatment efficacy and long‐term functional outcomes, with limited cohorts providing comprehensive cardiac assessment, leaving a notable evidence gap [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

Echocardiography is currently the standard tool for assessment of the right heart [12]. However, higher level of equipment and technical expertise are required due to the complex anatomy of the right ventricle [13]. As a result, repeated assessments during follow‐up are not always feasible, and reliable data on the long‐term prevalence of RVD remain limited. Electrocardiography (ECG) could offer advantages such as portability, cost‐effectiveness, and ease of use, demonstrating significant potential for longitudinal monitoring. Recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled the detection of subtle ECG features associated with various chronic cardiopulmonary diseases. An analyzes of over 30,000 ECGs from patients with suspected PE have shown that AI‐derived ECG features, when combined with d‐dimer levels, improved diagnostic accuracy compared to traditional PE probability score [14]. Furthermore, the first AI foundation model trained on millions of real‐world ECG datasets showed the capacity to classify a broad spectrum of cardiac conditions across diverse populations [15]. Together with the development of wearable health technologies, AI‐based analysis of portable ECG data could serve as a promising method for assessing right heart function in PE patients, with the potential for rapid and early identification of cardiac dysfunction [16].

Therefore, this study aims to explore the progression of RVD following acute PE by integrating ECG signals, specific biomarkers and clinical characteristics. The objectives of this study are: 1) Integrate ECG and biomarkers to characterize the progression of cardiac function after PE. 2) Using AI algorithms to extract ECG features of patients with acute PE and evaluate their association with clot burden and right ventricular function. 3) Investigate the incidence and risk factors of persistent RVD after acute PE.

Methods and Analysis

Study Design and Flow

This prospective China pUlmonary thromboembolism REgistry Study‐CARioElectro (CURES‐CARE) cohort will enroll 500 patients with acute intermediate‐ or high‐risk PE from seven tertiary hospitals in China as a part of the CURES study [17]. All participating centers follow standardized protocols for PE diagnosis and treatment and have long‐standing experience in PE and pulmonary hypertension management. The study protocol does not dictate any diagnostic or therapeutic interventions. Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, diagnostic and therapeutic interventions, as well as outcome variables are systematically collected and entered into an electronic case report form (eCRF).

The inclusion criteria are as follows: patients aged 18 years or older, diagnosed with intermediate‐ or high‐ risk PE according to the 2019 ESC Guideline [3], onset of symptoms of PE within the prior 14 days, and agree to sign informed consent. The exclusion criteria include chronic PE without recurrence; unable to use ECG acquisition system due to chest surgery, local skin lesions, or allergies to patches; refuse or unable to complete 1‐year follow‐up; inability to operate a portable ECG device due to cognitive impairment or lack of smartphone; or refuse to participate due to other reasons.

Baseline clinical data will be collected during hospitalization, including symptoms, risk stratification, imaging and laboratory tests, treatment details, and echocardiographic evaluations at admission and prior to discharge. All patients will be regularly followed up at 3,6 and 12 months through face‐to‐face evaluations or telephone calls by pulmonologists (Figure 1). During each visit/contact, doctors interview patients to obtain symptoms, information about the date and type of clinical events (recurrent venous thromboembolism (VTE), bleeding, and death) and adjustments of anticoagulants. Researchers at each study site prospectively collect clinical data, diagnostic and therapeutic processes of care, laboratory parameters, and information about health‐related quality of life for all enrolled patients using standardized data collection forms (Table 1).

ECG Signal Acquisition and Long‐Term Monitoring

In‐Hospital ECG Acquisition

During hospitalization, enrolled patients will undergo continuous electrocardiographic monitoring using the WenXinWuYang Dynamic ECG Patch Monitor, a wearable three‐lead long‐term ECG acquisition system validated for clinical and real‐world applications [18]. The system consists of a main recording unit, a charging dock, and disposable ECG patch sensors. The ECG patch sensors are affixed to predefined anatomical locations on the chest and right lateral thorax to acquire leads II, V1, and V5, enabling continuous multi‐lead ECG monitoring for 48–72 h. This configuration allows for comprehensive rhythm surveillance and enhanced detection of transient arrhythmic events. The device supports real‐time signal visualization through the WenXinWuYang WeChat Mini Program, which enables researchers to assess signal stability and quality during hospitalization. Upon completion of the recording period, ECG data are automatically uploaded to a secure cloud platform and analyzed using AI‐based deep learning algorithms. A standardized ECG analysis report is generated to support early identification of arrhythmias and clinically significant rhythm abnormalities.

Post‐Discharge Ambulatory ECG Monitoring

After discharge, all participants will be provided with a portable single‐lead ECG monitor named CardioLearn to perform scheduled ECG recordings three times daily (morning, afternoon, and evening) for a total duration of 1 year. The device records Lead I ECG signals via fingertip electrodes, capturing cardiac electrical activity through bilateral index finger contact (Supporting Information Figure S1). Each measurement requires the participant to place both index fingers steadily on the built‐in metal electrodes for at least 30 s, after which ECG acquisition begins automatically via Bluetooth connection to the smartphone. Real‐time ECG waveforms are displayed in the WeChat Mini Program, enhancing user engagement and facilitating correct device use. All recordings are uploaded automatically to the cloud for near‐real‐time AI‐assisted analysis. Meanwhile, participants are encouraged to self‐report symptoms, including chest tightness, dyspnea, fatigue, dizziness, and peripheral edema, through structured questionnaires embedded in the Mini Program, allowing symptom‐ECG correlation during follow‐up.

The portable device is lightweight, handheld, and non‐invasive, requiring minimal operational steps, which substantially lowers the user burden compared with conventional Holter monitoring. Besides, the WeChat Mini Program–based interface leverages a widely used mobile platform in China, reducing technical barriers and training requirements. Previous study has demonstrated stable long‐term usage of this system among tens of thousands of users across multiple provinces, with high data completeness and sustained engagement over extended follow‐up periods [19]. Moreover, scheduled short‐duration recordings (30 s per session) are less complicated for daily life than continuous monitoring, making three daily measurements over 1 year practically achievable.

Management of Long‐Term ECG Data

All ECG recordings are transmitted via encrypted channels and stored on a secure cloud infrastructure with role‐based access control. Data are de‐identified at upload, and unique study identifiers are used to link ECG data with clinical and symptom information. The cloud architecture has been validated to support large‐scale, high‐frequency ECG data ingestion, with the capacity to process hundreds of thousands of recordings per day [19].

Laboratory Variables

At enrollment, discharge and each follow‐up point, the 5 ml blood and 10 ml urine samples are collected from enrolled patients who are able to provide consent. Blood samples are centrifuged for 10 min at 1500 g at 4°C and urine samples are centrifuged for 30 min at 2580 g at 4°C. The supernatant is then divided into aliquots. The aliquoted blood and urine supernatant and blood cell pellets will be temporarily stored at −80°C at local site. Then all samples will be packed in dry ice and sent to the CURES biobank where the samples are subsequently long‐term stored in a centralized, 2‐D barcoded biobank at −80°C. Participants can still be enrolled into the study even if they are unable to provide samples.

From the processed specimens, a broad panel of biomarkers will be quantified. Particular emphasis will be put on biomarkers associated with right ventricular function, myocardial fibrosis and inflammation, immunity, and kidney disease, given their central role in cardiovascular remodeling and disease progression. Both established and newly identified protein biomarkers will be assessed at predefined time points and through serial measurements during follow‐up. These longitudinal data are expected to provide insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying right heart recovery and might be of prognostic relevance.

Functional Evaluation

Quality of life will be assessed at multiple time points after pulmonary embolism using the Pulmonary Embolism Quality of Life Questionnaire (PEmb‐QoL) and the EuroQol 5‐Dimension 5‐Level questionnaire. The Chinese version of the PEmb‐QoL questionnaire has been validated to be a both valid and reliable tool for evaluating health‐related quality of life in patients after PE [20].

If RVD or residual thrombus is confirmed, further examinations (cardiopulmonary exercise tests or right heart catheterization) will be performed for screening of pulmonary hypertension. If RVD or residual thrombus are excluded, alternative causes of symptoms (e.g., dyspnea) will be evaluated further.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint is the occurrence of RVD during follow‐up, diagnosed if any of the following criteria are met [21, 22]: (1) CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA): an Right ventricular (RV)/Left ventricular (LV) diameter ratio ≥ 1.0; (2) Echocardiography: all measured echocardiographic parameter are prospectively classified into three categories (Table 2): ① chamber size; ② RV systolic function; ③ hemodynamics. This classification was based on the main pathophysiologic mechanisms implicated in RV dysfunction.

Statistical Analysis

ECG Data Processing

Missing ECG data are expected in long‐term ambulatory monitoring and will be managed using analytical strategies. Missed recordings may occur due to non‐adherence, technical issues, or temporary device malfunction. The monitoring system automatically logs recording time stamps and durations, allowing missing data to be classified as intermittent missing measurements or prolonged data absence. Intermittent missing data will be addressed using appropriate longitudinal statistical methods, such as mixed‐effects models, which are robust to unbalanced repeated measures. Extended data loss due to withdrawal or sustained non‐use will be documented, and sensitivity analyzes will be performed to assess the potential impact of missing data on study outcomes.

All ECG recordings uploaded to the cloud platform will undergo standardized preprocessing before analysis. Preprocessing steps include removal of high‐frequency noise, correction of low‐frequency baseline drift, and detection of signal drop‐out or lead inversion. ECG signals are segmented into analyzable epochs, and recordings with insufficient stable waveform duration are flagged. These procedures are applied automatically to ensure consistency across participants and time points. Preprocessed ECG data are then forwarded to downstream algorithms for rhythm analysis and feature extraction.

Quality Control

Data is collected using an electronic data capture (EDC) system based on the eCRF. Patient information is de‐identified, confidentially collected, and entered into the secure CURES platform which was built on a secure website (http://47.93.199.67/portal/root/pw6372_1/in_index.jsp). All electronic data are protected by passwords. Data management and quality control involve several strategies: the EDC system and eCRF are designed to automatically ensure data plausibility during entry; a dedicated team monitors data quality according to predefined procedures; the EDC system automatically sends queries to local investigators for abnormal or missing values; periodic reports on research and data quality are shared with all centers; and trained coordinators visit centers with high missing data or low follow‐up rates.

Quality control is also implemented at multiple stages to ensure the reliability of ECG data used for analysis. At the signal level, recordings are evaluated for signal stability, noise burden, and minimum analyzable duration. At the algorithm level, automated confidence indicators are used to identify recordings with uncertain or unreliable interpretations. Longitudinal quality checks are also conducted to detect abrupt changes in ECG parameters that are inconsistent with physiological patterns and may indicate artifacts. Only ECG data meeting predefined quality criteria will be included in the primary analyzes, while excluded data will be retained for documentation and sensitivity assessments.

Comprehensive ECG features will be extracted and analyzed using deep learning models across time, frequency, and latent feature domains. A feature indexing system will be developed for structured, segment‐based ECG analysis. A Restricted Update strategy will be implemented to reduce overfitting and catastrophic forgetting during model training on multi‐type time‐series data.

Statistical and Analytical Methods

Continuous variables will be reported as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, or as median (interquartile range) if not. Categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages. According to the data type and distribution, group comparisons will use the chi‐square test, Mann‐Whitney U test or t test, and so forth. Univariate and multivariate analyzes will be used to identify risk factors for outcomes (significance set at p < 0.05). All tests will be two‐sided with α = 0.05. Analyzes in the following subgroups will be conducted: (1) initial treatment (reperfusion therapy or anticoagulation only); (2) patients with different risk stratification (based on 2019 ESC guidelines); (3) different baseline definitions of RVD (elevated troponin or Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP)/N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) with normal echocardiographic findings; abnormal echocardiographic findings with normal troponin and BNP/NT‐proBNP; both cardiac biomarkers and echocardiography are abnormal); (4) patients with pre‐existing sign of chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) on CTPA (Supporting Information Table S1); (5)patients with concomitant cardiopulmonary diseases which may affect the RV function, including atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction involving the right ventricle and cor pulmonale etc.

Discussion

This study is the first prospective cohort aiming at characterizing the progression of RVD after acute PE. By combining advanced physiological and biochemical data, we aim to delineate the longitudinal trajectory of RVD following intermediate‐ or high‐risk PE and to identify patients at higher risk for adverse long‐term prognosis. As of January 2026, a total of 202 patients has been included at seven sites across China, with a drop‐out rate of < 5%. Previous longitudinal studies on PE have primarily focused on therapeutic approaches and short‐term mortality [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11], with relatively limited emphasis on long‐term cardiopulmonary function. In the multicenter Pulmonary Embolism Thrombolysis (PEITHO) trial, 709 patients were followed up for 24.6 to 54.8 months, yet echocardiography was performed in only 290 (40.9%) patients and revealed no significant differences in residual pulmonary hypertension or RVD between thrombolysis and placebo groups [23]. Importantly, incomplete or absent recovery of echocardiographic parameters at 6 months were identified as predictors of CTEPH or PPES at long‐term follow‐up [24]. However, the absence of systematic imaging in all survivors limited definitive conclusions regarding the prevalence and persistence of RVD.

Recent prospective studies have shifted attention from short‐term mortality to long‐term functional outcomes after PE [25]. The Evaluation of Long‐term Outcomes after Pulmonary Embolism (ELOPE) study showed that nearly half of patients had reduced exercise capacity one year after acute PE, which was associated with impaired quality of life. The Follow‐Up after acute pulmonary embolism (FOCUS) study, with systematic follow‐up of 2 years, identified PPES as an indicator of increased risk for subsequent CTEPH and impaired quality of life [26]. However, the associations between persistent RVD with functional limitations or residual pulmonary vascular obstructions remain poorly characterized. Recent work by Morris et al. has identified distinct chronic thromboembolic phenotypes in approximately 12% of PE survivors, with more than half of these patients not meeting diagnostic criteria for PH [27]. This highlights the presence of chronic thromboembolic disease without PH and underscores a broader type of post‐PE conditions. Such findings complement the conventional paradigm that prioritizes CTEPH as the primary long‐term complication and instead emphasize the need for refined phenotyping and comprehensive follow‐up strategies. Similarly, our study seeks to capture the progression of right ventricular adaptation, integrating multimodal data to better understand how residual thrombotic burden contributes to persistent RVD, even in the absence of overt PH.

Although RVD is a key determinant of poor prognosis after PE, few studies have depicted its long‐term trajectory. Data from PEITHO‐2 study suggested that almost one out of four patients maintained at least one echocardiographic abnormality at 6 months [28]. Consistently, our prior meta‐analysis showed the incidence of acute RVD as 69% at disease onset, and 34% and 26% at 3 months and 6 months respectively [5]. Patients with persistent RVD are more likely to experience functional impairments, such as chest tightness, dyspnea, fatigue, and even CTEPH, underscoring the need for systematic follow‐up and refined long‐term management.

The mechanisms underlying persistent RVD remain incompletely understood. Acute RVD is mainly caused by sudden increases in right ventricular afterload, leading to subendocardial ischemia and myocardial injury related to acute pulmonary artery obstruction [29]. In contrast, unrecovered RVD is likely driven by chronic processes, including ventricular remodeling and fibrosis, which may be influenced by comorbid conditions and residual thrombotic burden [30]. Although residual clot is a well‐established risk factor for CTEPH, its impact on right ventricular recovery is less clear [31]. Importantly, RVD may persist even in the absence of overt PH, particularly in patients with chronic thromboembolic disease or subtle pulmonary vascular abnormalities. Differentiating RVD phenotypes with and without PH is therefore essential, as these conditions may represent distinct pathophysiological pathways with different prognostic implications. Moreover, the long‐term effects of reperfusion strategies, including systemic thrombolysis or catheter‐directed therapies, on right ventricular recovery warrants further study [32]. In this study, we will perform subgroup analyzes to investigate these therapeutic effects, aiming to clarify the phenomenon and provide evidence for refining management strategies.

While echocardiography remains the reference standard for RVD assessment, the technical complexity and limited feasibility for repeated evaluations restrict its use in long‐term follow‐up. In contrast, ECG is portable, cost‐effective, and suitable for continuous monitoring. Although conventional ECG signals lack specificity in reflecting cardiac dysfunction, recent advances in AI have enabled detection of subtle ECG features that reflect structural and functional abnormalities [33]. Since the first comparative analysis of AI‐ECG models in myocardial infarction by Safdarian et al. in 2014, algorithmic performance has steadily improved through deeper convolutional networks and multimodal integration [34]. Dhingra LS's team developed an image‐based AI‐ECG model that could identify individuals at high risk of left ventricular systolic dysfunction in the future and predict heart failure across diverse international cohorts, indicating the predictive accuracy and generalizability of AI‐ECG across population [35]. Similarly, Hilary M. DuBrock and co‐workers developed The Pulmonary Hypertension Early Detection Algorithm based on retrospective ECG voltage–time data, which could detect pulmonary hypertension at diagnosis and 6–18 months prior [36]. More recently, Son Q. Duong et al. trained a deep learning–ECG model which could identify significant right ventricular dysfunction and dilation as defined by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging [37]. However, no research has yet applied AI‐ECG to investigate the long‐term cardiac outcomes following acute PE. By integrating AI‐ECG analysis with serial biomarkers, our study will address this critical gap.

Our study design offers several strengths, including prospective data collection, comprehensive longitudinal follow‐up, and integration of multimodal physiological and biochemical measures. In addition, immediate visualization of ECG waveforms and AI‐generated interpretations increases participants' engagement and adherence. These features will allow robust characterization of RVD trajectories and identification of modifiable factors that influence recovery.

Ultimately, exploring the natural history of RVD and providing clues for mechanisms underlying post‐PE ventricular remodeling may enable earlier intervention, individualized follow‐up, and improved long‐term outcomes for patients surviving intermediate‐ or high‐risk PE.
