Background

Since the first percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation (PPVI) reported in 2000,1 the minimally invasive transcatheter approach has become a widely performed safe and effective alternative to surgical pulmonary valve replacement. However, patients with large-dimension native right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) have been a challenge given limitations in the size of available transcatheter valves. In recent years, the availability of self-expanding transcatheter valves has offered the possibility of PPVI in patients with larger RVOT dimensions. To avoid the risk of surgical pulmonary valve replacement with cardiopulmonary bypass in high-risk patients, mainly those with severely compromised systolic ventricular function, hybrid PPVI procedures have been reported using balloon-expendable valves after surgical reduction of pulmonary artery diameter.2,3 As recently reported, hybrid PPVI using self-expanding pulmonary valves has been performed with the Harmony valve (Medtronic)4 and the Pulsta valve (TaeWoong Medical).5

Hybrid percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation using pulmonary artery plication through a minimal left lateral thoracotomy is a feasible approach for pulmonary valve replacement in patients with high operative risk and pulmonary artery dimensions unsuitably large for commercially available transcatheter pulmonary valves.

Here, we report for the first time the use of the Venus P-Valve (Venus Medtech) in a hybrid PPVI procedure.

Medical history

A 60-year-old man with tetralogy of Fallot and right-sided aortic arch who had undergone surgical repair with patch closure of the ventricular septal defect, infundibulotomy, and a transannular patch in 1975, presented for the first time in our institution with decompensated heart failure. The diagnostic work-up revealed acute coronary syndrome. He underwent emergency cardiac catheterization with recanalization of the occluded left anterior descending artery and implantation of a drug-eluting stent (Synsiro Pro 2.5 mm/22 mm, Biotronik).

Investigation

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) after revascularization showed severe biventricular systolic dysfunction with a right ventricular ejection fraction of 20% and left ventricular ejection fraction of 28%, severe pulmonary valve regurgitation with regurgitation fraction of 80%, an enlarged right ventricle with an indexed end-diastolic right ventricular volume of 216 mL/m2, as well as a very enlarged RVOT (Figure 1). Echocardiography additionally revealed severe tricuspid valve regurgitation. The patient was in heart failure corresponding to NYHA functional class III and under initiated medical heart failure treatment. Electrocardiogram showed a right bundle branch block (QRS: 160 ms); arrhythmia was excluded by 24-hour Holter monitoring.

Management

The patient met the criteria for pulmonary valve replacement.6,7 In view of severe biventricular systolic dysfunction, a surgical pulmonary valve replacement was considered high risk. Conventional PPVI was not feasible because the large dimensions of the RVOT (Figure 1) exceeded commercially available valve sizes. After interdisciplinary heart team discussion, we therefore decided to perform a hybrid PPVI without the use of cardiopulmonary bypass.

Hybrid procedure

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia in the hybrid suite. After intubation, an endobronchial blocker (Supplemental Figure 1) was placed in the left main bronchus to achieve single lung ventilation. Because the patient's dilated ascending aorta was abutting the sternum (Figure 1), a minimal left lateral thoracotomy using a Bookwalter retractor was chosen as surgical access (Figure 2). Femoral vascular access was obtained in the right femoral vein and in both the left femoral vein and artery (Figure 2). Pulmonary artery angiography showed a minimal native diameter of the main pulmonary artery (MPA) of 42.7 mm. Banding of the MPA was not considered practicable owing to adhesions between the ascending aorta and the MPA as well as the lack of direct visualization of the coronary arteries. Longitudinal clamping was also not feasible given the fragility of the dilated MPA tissue. Eventually, a lateral longitudinal plication of the MPA was realized using 3-0 Prolene (Ethicon) horizontal mattress pledgeted sutures (Figure 2). A residual diameter of the MPA exceeding 38 mm was visualized upon sizing with a 34-mm Amplatzer sizing balloon (Abbott Laboratories), therefore a redo plication was performed to further reduce the diameter. Finally, a diameter of 36 mm of the plicated pulmonary artery was achieved as visualized by angiography, providing an appropriate landing zone. A Lunderquist extra-stiff wire (Cook Medical) was placed in the right pulmonary artery, and a 26-F Gore DrySeal Flex introducer sheath (W.L. Gore & Associates) was advanced. A 36-mm/25-mm Venus P-Valve was positioned in the pulmonary artery bifurcation and slowly deployed under fluoroscopic visualization and repetitive angiographies as previously described8 (Figure 3).

Because the plication of the massively dilated pulmonary artery was located shortly above the pulmonary valve annulus, the Venus P-Valve could not be implanted in a supra-annular position but had to be positioned slightly deeper than initially intended, thereby extending somewhat further into the RVOT than planned. However, no clinically relevant ventricular arrhythmias were noted during the postprocedural course. The postimplantation angiographic and echocardiographic evaluation revealed minimal central pulmonary valve regurgitation, as well as a minor paravalvular leak without stenosis of the valve. After hybrid PPVI, we observed an increase in the left ventricular end-diastolic pressure from 19 mm Hg to 30 mm Hg. However, with otherwise stable hemodynamics and no signs of pulmonary edema, we decided to close the lateral thoracotomy and remove the sheaths. The patient was extubated 4 hours after the procedure and was discharged home on day 7.

Follow-up

Follow-up echocardiography at 5 months after hybrid PPVI showed excellent function of the valve leaflets, without any regurgitation or stenosis (Figure 4). The paravalvular leak had decreased to trivial. Systolic function of the right ventricle had significantly improved, with a tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion increasing from 13 mm at the time of the procedure to 20 mm. Favorable right ventricular remodeling was seem, with dimensions decreasing from a basal end-diastolic diameter of 58 mm to 47 mm. The tricuspid valve regurgitation had also improved and was moderate. Clinically, the patient reported a significant improvement in his physical capacity corresponding to NYHA functional class II as well as improvement of his subjective quality of life.

Discussion

Hybrid PPVI is a feasible approach allowing pulmonary revalvulation in patients with RVOT dimensions unsuitably large for conventional PPVI and a high risk for surgical pulmonary valve replacement.2 The avoidance of cardiopulmonary bypass surgery increases procedural safety in high-risk patients and also reduces blood loss and transfusion requirement, procedure time, and length of hospital stay compared with surgical pulmonary valve replacement.9 In a limited number of case reports, to reduce the size of large MPA and create a suitable landing zone for the accommodation of pulmonary valve prostheses, a combination of MPA banding or plication via sternotomy or thoracotomy and PPVI with implantation of balloon-expandable pulmonary valves has been performed.2,3 Recently, this technique has been adapted using various self-expanding pulmonary valves.4,5 Given its design, we favored the Venus P-Valve for this type of procedure, as it offers anchoring within the MPA and RVOT via the large diameters of the proximal inflow and distal outflow flares, as well as the large valve housing diameter (36 mm), providing the largest anchoring surface and thereby reducing the risk of embolization or the requirement of a surgical suture fixation of the valve.

To prevent proximal migration of the valve and achieve a supra-annular position, a resulting MPA diameter of 1 to 2 mm less than the outer diameter of the valve's tubular portion and as distal in the MPA as possible would be desirable. In our case however, a third attempt with further reduction of the MPA by additional plication sutures was deemed too risky owing to the extreme fragility of the heavily dilated MPA tissue, with risk of vessel rupture. Also, circumferential pulmonary artery banding might provide a more stable landing zone compared with plication. For pulmonary artery banding or a more distal MPA plication, a sternotomy approach would have been necessary. Unfortunately, in our case, the patient's dilated ascending aorta was abutting the sternum, posing a high risk for sternal entry, which is why we opted for a minimal left lateral thoracotomy.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of hybrid PPVI with plication of the MPA and implantation of the self-expanding Venus P-Valve. Previous case reports, as well as our own, demonstrate the feasibility of a variety of surgical access (sternotomy or thoracotomy) to proceed with the surgical reduction of landing zone diameter, as well as the successful use of different percutaneously implantable pulmonary valves (balloon-expandable or self-expanding), emphasizing the broad applicability of the hybrid strategy.
