Analysis of the Predictive Effect of Cardiac Color Doppler Ultrasound Combined With Carotid Ultrasound on Major Adverse Cardiac Events Following Percutaneous Coronary Intervention
Authors: Jun Xue, Di Wu, Yudong Fan, Miaomiao Zhang, Xu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThis study sought to investigate the predictive effect of combining cardiac color Doppler ultrasound and carotid ultrasound for major adverse cardiac events (MACE) following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 475 coronary heart disease (CHD) patients who underwent PCI between May 2021 and April 2024. Cardiac ultrasound parameters (left atrial diameter [LAD], left ventricular diameter in diastole [LVDd]) and carotid ultrasound metrics (plaque score, intima‐media thickness [IMT], total plaque area [TPA], and gray scale median [GSM]) were collected within 48 h post‐PCI. Patients were monitored for a 12‐month period to record MACE incidence. After 12 months of follow‐up, patients were assigned to a Without MACE group (n = 324) and a With MACE group (n = 151). Correlation and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves evaluated predictive accuracy.ResultsAmong 151 patients (31.8%) who experienced MACE, significant predictors included diabetes requiring medication (OR = 1.80, p = 0.039), prior myocardial infarction (OR = 1.93, p = 0.009), elevated discharge heart rate (OR = 1.10, p = 0.013), Charlson Comorbidity Index (OR = 1.60, p = 0.002), maximum left ventricular thickness (OR = 1.181, p = 0.003), higher Tei index (OR = 1.386, p = 0.001), plaque score (OR = 5.032, p = 0.001), carotid IMT (OR = 2.216, p = 0.002), and TPA (OR = 1.039, p = 0.016). Protective factors included beta‐blocker use (OR = 0.468, p = 0.027), higher LVEF (OR = 0.957, p = 0.009), and higher GSM (OR = 0.894, p = 0.045). The combined ultrasound model achieved an AUC of 0.833, demonstrating robust predictive accuracy.ConclusionCardiac color Doppler ultrasound combined with carotid ultrasound effectively predicts post‐PCI MACE by integrating cardiac function and atherosclerotic plaque characteristics. These non‐invasive imaging tools offer a valuable framework for early risk assessment and personalized treatment strategies in CHD patients following PCI.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD), the most prevalent form of cardiovascular disease, has become a significant public health issue threatening human health [1]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), as the primary method for myocardial revascularization in CHD, has markedly improved patients' clinical symptoms and prognosis [2]. However, there remains a certain proportion of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) after PCI, including cardiac death, non‐fatal myocardial infarction, target vessel revascularization, and recurrent angina pectoris. These events not only seriously impact the quality of life for patients but also add to the strain on the healthcare system [3].

Existing risk prediction models, such as the GRACE and SYNTAX scores, rely heavily on clinical characteristics and angiographic findings [4, 5]. Although these tools offer some predictive value, they have notable limitations. Firstly, these scoring systems predominantly depend on static indicators (e.g., age, renal function, degree of coronary stenosis), which fail to capture dynamic changes in postoperative cardiac function and vascular status [6]. Furthermore, traditional scoring methods inadequately assess critical pathophysiological mechanisms like plaque vulnerability and microvascular dysfunction. Therefore, developing repeatable and multi‐dimensional predictive tools is crucial for enhancing the MACE risk assessment framework [7].

Cardiac color Doppler and carotid ultrasound represent promising non‐invasive imaging modalities for assessing cardiovascular risk [8, 9]. These techniques offer detailed insights into plaque morphology, hemodynamics, and vascular function, which can provide complementary information to traditional risk scores [10]. Recent studies have shown that indices derived from these imaging modalities correlate strongly with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, suggesting their potential utility in predicting MACE post‐PCI [11, 12, 13]. By integrating these modalities, we aim to develop a more comprehensive risk assessment tool that leverages both structural and functional parameters.

This study aims to establish a combined predictive model of cardiac color Doppler and carotid ultrasound in identifying patients at high risk for MACE following PCI. Furthermore, employing multivariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis allows for a systematic evaluation of the predictive value of each parameter, facilitating the development of clinically applicable predictive models.

Study Design and Methods

Selection Criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients diagnosed with CHD confirmed by coronary angiography and meeting the clinical diagnostic criteria for CHD [14]; (2) Age between 18 and 80 years; (3) Identification of atherosclerotic plaque (AP) through carotid/subclavian ultrasound examination [15]; (4) Postoperative attainment of Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) grade 3 flow; and (5) Complete medical records without missing data.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Coexisting severe valvular heart disease or cardiomyopathy; (2) Malignant tumors or other serious diseases with an expected survival of less than 1 year; (3) LVEF less than 30% and/or New York Heart Association (NYHA) class IV heart failure at the time of hospital discharge; (4) Scheduled coronary artery bypass surgery (CABG); and (5) Ultrasound image quality not meeting the requirements for analysis.

Study Protocol and Data Acquisition

A retrospective analysis was conducted on 475 CHD patients who underwent PCI from May 2021 to April 2024 in our hospital. All patients received standardized cardiac color Doppler ultrasound and carotid ultrasound assessments within 48 h after PCI. Based on whether they experienced MACE within 1‐year post‐surgery, patients were divided into the no‐MACE (Without MACE) group and the MACE (With MACE) group.

Patients’ data were collected through the electronic medical record system, including demographic characteristics, clinical features, drug treatment, and ultrasound data. Follow‐up was conducted using a combination of outpatient visits and telephone interviews at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post‐surgery. The occurrence of MACE was recorded during these regular follow‐ups. In this study, MACE is defined as a composite endpoint, including cardiovascular death, non‐fatal myocardial infarction, or non‐fatal ischemic stroke. Cardiovascular death is defined as any death resulting from direct cardiovascular causes (myocardial infarction, heart failure, arrhythmia), or sudden death without a clear non‐cardiovascular cause. Non‐fatal myocardial infarction is diagnosed according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction [16], requiring ischemic symptoms, electrocardiogram changes consistent with ischemia, and typical elevation and/or decline in cardiac biomarkers (primarily troponin). Non‐fatal ischemic stroke is defined as an acute episode of persistent neurological deficit caused by cerebral ischemia, confirmed by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, with neurological deficits lasting more than 24 h or leading to hospital admission for treatment.

Ultrasound Examination

Cardiac color Doppler ultrasound examinations were performed according to a standardized protocol by certified sonographers using an expert‐level ultrasound scanner (Affiniti 50, Philips, Netherlands) and a linear 5–12 MHz transducer. Patients were placed in the left lateral decubitus position, and standard apical four‐chamber, two‐chamber, and long‐axis views were obtained. Parameters measured included left atrial diameter (LAD), diastolic left ventricular diameter (LVDd), LVEF, maximal left ventricular wall thickness, and the Tei index.

Carotid ultrasound examinations were conducted using an ultrasound diagnostic system (HDI 5000, Philips, Netherlands) with a linear 5–12 MHz transducer. Patients were placed in the supine position with their head slightly extended backward. Both common carotid arteries, the carotid bifurcation, and the proximal internal carotid arteries were examined. Parameters measured included carotid IMT (measured as the thickest point within 1 cm of the far wall), and total plaque area (TPA). Additionally, plaque scores and normalized GSM were calculated. TPA was determined using a semi‐automated segmentation algorithm available in the ultrasound system software. The segmentation process involved manually delineating the boundaries of each plaque, followed by automatic calculation of the plaque area. The areas of all identified plaques were summed to obtain the TPA for each patient. Plaques were defined as structures with local intima‐media thickness ≥1.5 mm or at least 0.5 mm thicker than the surrounding intima [17]. Plaque scoring was performed using a semi‐quantitative method: in each standard segment (six segments in total: bilateral common carotid arteries, bifurcations, and internal carotid arteries), a score of 1 was assigned if a plaque was present and 0 if absent, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 6. GSM was calculated through histogram analysis of gray values within the plaque region. Initially, the plaque region was manually outlined on the ultrasound image. Subsequently, gray values within these regions were extracted to generate the histogram. To quantify the echogenicity of the plaque for assessing its composition, GSM analysis was performed on each plaque. First, the image dynamic range was standardized to 0–255 (8‐bit grayscale). A region of interest (ROI) was manually selected within the plaque, avoiding calcified areas and their acoustic shadows. Then, the images were normalized to eliminate device gain differences: the GSM value of the blood region was calibrated to 0, and that of the adventitia or nearby muscle tissue was calibrated to 190–210. Finally, the median gray level of all pixels within the ROI was calculated to obtain the standardized GSM value. Lower GSM values indicate higher lipid content and thinner fibrous caps, suggesting a more unstable plaque. All the aforementioned ultrasound examinations were performed by the same experienced sonographer, ensuring consistency, and reliability.

Ethical Statement

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of our hospital on December 5, 2022 (Approval Number: K23‐8). Considering that all patient data used in this study have been de‐identified and there is no potential risk or adverse impact on participants during the study, the IRB and Ethics Committee jointly decided to waive the requirement for informed consent, adhering to the regulatory and ethical standards governing retrospective studies.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 29.0 statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). First, the Shapiro–Wilk test was conducted to determine whether the data followed a normal distribution. After verification, it was found that all data in this study conformed to a normal distribution. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages [n (%)], and Chi‐square tests were performed using basic formulas, with the test statistic represented as χ 2. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD) and independent samples t‐tests were performed, with the test statistic represented as t. p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Pearson correlation was used for continuous variables, and Spearman's rank correlation was used for ordinal or non‐normally distributed variables. First, parameters that showed significant differences between groups were included in a multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify the independent predictors of adverse cardiovascular events following I. This method is a standard approach for binary outcomes, allowing us to estimate the probability of MACE based on multiple predictors. Subsequently, the independent risk factors identified through multivariate logistic regression were included in ROC analysis to develop a predictive model for MACE after PCI that incorporates parameters from cardiac Doppler and carotid ultrasound. The model includes the following variables: maximal left ventricular wall thickness, Tei index, IMT, GSM, TPA, the presence of diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and the use of beta‐blockers. The AUC was calculated using the trapezoidal rule, and the 95% confidence interval (CI) was determined using the DeLong method.

Given our strict inclusion criteria, which required complete medical records for all participants, there were no missing data cases in our dataset. This ensures that our analysis is based on complete‐case data without the need for imputation techniques such as multiple imputation. To ensure adequate statistical power and minimize the risk of overfitting, we considered the events‐per‐variable (EPV) ratio. The EPV is defined as the number of events (MACE occurrences) per predictor variable included in the model. An EPV of at least 10 is generally recommended to avoid overfitting. Given that we had 151 MACE events and initially considered 12 candidate predictors, our EPV was approximately 12.6, which meets this guideline. To further reduce the risk of overfitting, we applied LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression, a penalized method, during the model‐building process. LASSO regression helps in variable selection by shrinking less important coefficients toward zero, thereby improving model stability and generalizability.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

When comparing the demographic characteristics between the Without MACE group (n = 324) and the With MACE group (n = 151), several similarities and key differences were noted (Table 1). No significant differences were found between the two groups regarding age, gender distribution, body mass index (BMI), employment status, marital status, education level, method of payment for medical expenses, levels of moderate‐intensity and vigorous physical activity, or adherence to physical activity guidelines (all p > 0.05). However, notable differences were observed in the prevalence of diabetes and history of myocardial infarction. The MACE group exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes, with more individuals using oral medications or insulin compared to the no MACE group (29.80% vs. 17.90%, p = 0.003). A history of myocardial infarction was also significantly more common in the MACE group (40.40% vs. 27.78%, p = 0.006). Additionally, although there was a trend toward higher proportions of smoking (54.30% vs. 44.75%), hypertension (68.21% vs. 59.88%), family history (27.15% vs. 19.44%), and history of stroke (11.92% vs. 7.10%) in the MACE group, these differences did not achieve statistical significance (all p > 0.05). These results indicate that diabetes requiring medication and a history of myocardial infarction may be linked to an elevated risk of MACE.

Clinical Characteristics

When comparing the clinical characteristics between the Without MACE group and the With MACE group, no significant differences in time from pain onset to PCI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), hemoglobin, creatinine, eGFR, LDL‐C, troponin levels, type of CHD, SYNTAX score, or PRECISE‐DAPT score (all p > 0.05) (Table 2). However, the Charlson Comorbidity Index was significantly higher in the MACE group compared to the no MACE group (4.43 ± 1.07 vs. 4.16 ± 1.05, p = 0.010). Additionally, while there was no notably difference in heart rate at admission between the two groups, the heart rate at discharge was notably higher in the MACE group compared to the Without MACE group (66.21 ± 2.84 bpm vs. 65.31 ± 3.26 bpm, p = 0.004). These findings suggest that an elevated heart rate at discharge and a higher burden of comorbidities may be linked to an elevated risk of MACE.

However, the prescription rate of beta‐blockers was notably lower in the With MACE group compared to the Without MACE group (80.13% vs. 89.81%, p = 0.004). These results indicate that beta‐blocker therapy may play a protective role against major adverse cardiovascular events post‐discharge.

Ultrasound Examination

The comparison of cardiac ultrasound results between the Without MACE group and the With MACE group revealed notable differences in LVEF and maximum left ventricular thickness (Figure 1). The LVEF was notably lower in the With MACE group compared to the Without MACE group (61.52% ± 7.43% vs. 63.59% ± 7.31%, p = 0.005). Additionally, the maximum left ventricular thickness (18.39 ± 2.12 mm vs.17.64 ± 2.16 mm, p < 0.001) and the Tei index (0.52 ± 0.13 vs. 0.47 ± 0.14, p < 0.001) were notably higher in the With MACE group than in the Without MACE group. No significant differences were observed in LAD, and LVDd (all p > 0.05). These findings suggest that reduced LVEF, increased left ventricular thickness, and elevated postoperative Tei index may be associated with a higher risk of MACE.

The comparison of carotid artery ultrasound results between the Without MACE group and the With MACE group revealed notable differences across multiple indicators (Figure 2). The plaque score was notably higher in the With MACE group compared to the Without MACE group (1.38 ± 0.16 vs. 1.25 ± 0.18, p < 0.001). Similarly, the carotid IMT and TPA were also significantly greater in the With MACE group (IMT: 0.79 ± 0.11 mm, TPA: 51.63 ± 10.48 mm2) than in the Without MACE group (IMT: 0.75 ± 0.13 mm, p < 0.001; TPA: 49.17 ± 5.48 mm2, p = 0.007). Additionally, standardized GSM values were lower in the With MACE group than in the Without MACE group (92.11 ± 10.29 vs. 95.45 ± 15.81, p = 0.006). These findings suggest that increased plaque burden and lower plaque stability may be linked to an elevated risk of MACE.

Correlation Analysis

The correlation analysis of MACE in patients with CHD after PCI revealed several significant associations (Table 5). Higher plaque score (r = 0.346, p < 0.001), increased carotid IMT (r = 0.135, p = 0.003) and bigger TPA (r = 0.146, p = 0.001) were strongly correlated with MACE. Other significant positive correlations included diabetes requiring oral medication/insulin (r = 0.134, p = 0.003), history of myocardial infarction (r = 0.126, p = 0.006), heart rate at discharge (r = 0.145, p = 0.001), Charlson Comorbidity Index (r = 0.097, p = 0.034), maximum left ventricular thickness (r = 0.153, p < 0.001), and Tei index post‐operation (r = 0.178, p < 0.001). Conversely, the use of beta‐blockers at discharge (r = −0.133, p = 0.004), LVEF (r = −0.123, p = 0.007), and standardized GSM (r = −0.119, p = 0.010) showed significant negative correlations with MACE. These findings highlight the importance of these factors in predicting and potentially mitigating MACE risk post‐PCI.

Multivariate Logistic Regression Analysis

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several significant predictors of MACE in patients with CHD after PCI (Table 6). Significant predictors included diabetes requiring oral medication/insulin (OR = 1.800, 95% CI [1.031, 3.144]), history of myocardial infarction (OR = 1.929, 95% CI [1.178, 3.161]), heart rate at discharge (OR = 1.104, 95% CI [1.021, 1.194]), Charlson Comorbidity Index (OR = 1.601, 95% CI [1.185, 2.163]), maximum left ventricular thickness (OR = 1.181, 95% CI [1.057, 1.320]), Tei index (OR = 1.386, 95% CI [1.140, 1.678]), plaque score (OR = 5.032, 95% CI [1.905, 13.331]), carotid IMT (OR = 2.216, 95% CI [1.348, 3.648]), and TPA (OR = 1.039, 95% CI [1.007, 1.071]). Protective factors included the use of beta‐blockers at discharge (OR = 0.468, 95% CI [0.239, 0.915]), higher LVEF (OR = 0.957, 95% CI [0.926, 0.989]) and higher standardized GSM (OR = 0.894, 95% CI [0.697,0.951]). These findings highlight the importance of managing these risk and protective factors to reduce MACE risk post‐PCI.

ROC Analysis

The AUC results of independent predictors for MACE in patients with CHD after PCI were identified by ROC analysis. (Table 7). Among the indicators analyzed, plaque score (AUC = 0.718), Tei index (AUC = 0.692), and carotid IMT (AUC = 0.691) demonstrated significantly higher discriminatory power compared to other variables, indicating their potential as strong predictors for adverse cardiovascular outcomes post‐PC. Discussion

The present study systematically analyzed the predictive value of cardiac color Doppler ultrasound combined with carotid ultrasound for MACE after PCI. Key findings revealed significant associations between clinical demographics, ultrasound metrics, and MACE risk, providing valuable insights for post‐PCI risk stratification [18].

Clinical factors such as diabetes and prior myocardial infarction can amplify MACE risk through multifactorial pathways. Diabetes‐associated hyperglycemia and insulin resistance promote endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and microvascular impairment, which synergize with plaque instability to worsen outcomes [19]. A history of myocardial infarction likely reflects pre‐existing myocardial damage and residual ischemia, creating a substrate for arrhythmias or pump failure. The Charlson Comorbidity Index's predictive value emphasizes the cumulative impact of non‐cardiac conditions (e.g., renal dysfunction, chronic lung disease) on cardiovascular resilience [20]. Although the discharge heart rates between the two groups showed a statistically significant difference, this difference must be interpreted in the context of clinical relevance. It is unlikely to result in any meaningful clinical impact given the small magnitude of the change. Minor variations in heart rate do not translate into noticeable differences in patient outcomes or management strategies.

The protective effect of beta‐blockers aligns with their known cardioprotective mechanisms, including heart rate reduction, anti‐arrhythmic properties, and attenuation of sympathetic‐driven myocardial remodeling. Lower beta‐blocker usage in the MACE group may reflect underutilization in high‐risk cohorts or contraindications due to comorbidities, suggesting opportunities for guideline‐directed optimization [21]. Regarding the discrepancy in LDL‐C levels, our findings contrast with earlier studies that identified LDL‐C as a significant predictor of MACE [22, 23]. However, the high statin adherence observed in both groups in our cohort likely attenuated the prognostic impact of LDL‐C, highlighting the importance of lipid‐lowering therapy in mitigating cardiovascular risk. This observation underscores the need for continued focus on optimizing statin adherence and other lipid‐lowering strategies in post‐PCI care.

The observed associations between reduced LVEF, increased left ventricular wall thickness, and elevated Tei index with MACE highlight the prognostic significance of myocardial dysfunction. A diminished LVEF reflects impaired systolic capacity, limiting cardiac output during ischemic stress [24], while heightened wall thickness and Tei index—a composite measure of systolic and diastolic dysfunction—signal advanced ventricular remodeling and impaired relaxation. These abnormalities may predispose patients to arrhythmias, recurrent ischemia, and heart failure, particularly in the context of persistent coronary microvascular dysfunction or residual stenosis. Similarly, carotid ultrasound metrics, including higher plaque scores, IMT, and TPA, alongside lower GSM values, underscore the systemic nature of atherosclerosis. Unstable plaques, characterized by low GSM (<80), are histologically associated with lipid‐rich cores, thin fibrous caps, and intraplaque hemorrhage, rendering them prone to rupture and distal embolization [25]. The correlation between carotid plaque burden and MACE suggests shared pathophysiological pathways, such as inflammatory cascades and oxidative stress, driving both coronary and extra coronary vascular events [18, 26].

Carotid ultrasound revealed a significantly higher plaque score, IMT, and TPA in the MACE group, reflecting a greater systemic atherosclerotic burden. Our findings that increased IMT and TPA predict MACE align with large‐scale studies, which identified IMT as a robust marker of carotid artery atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk [27, 28]. Similarly, our observation that lower standardized GSM values, indicating lipid‐rich, unstable plaques, are associated with MACE is strongly supported by the work of Lee et al., who demonstrated that ultrasound‐based plaque scores can effectively guide primary prevention strategies [29]. The predictive power of the Tei index for post‐PCI events echoes recent findings by Naomi Niari Dalimunthe et al., who reported its incremental value in predicting MACE among high‐risk populations such as those with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [30]. Although these individual modalities are valuable, the novelty of our study lies in their integration. The AUC of the joint model reached as high as 0.833, representing a substantial improvement with a ΔAUC of 0.12–0.19 compared to models relying solely on clinical factors. This suggests synergistic value, a concept increasingly recognized in recent literature advocating for multi‐parametric imaging approaches [31].

Multivariate analysis identified diabetes, myocardial infarction history, elevated discharge heart rate, Charlson index, left ventricular wall thickness, Tei index, plaque score, IMT, TPA, and low GSM as independent predictors of MACE. The combined ultrasound model demonstrated a high predictive accuracy, suggesting that integrating cardiac and carotid ultrasound parameters could enhance risk stratification beyond traditional clinical factors. From a clinical perspective, this integrated model offers substantial promise for risk stratification in post‐PCI patients. The non‐invasive, repeatable nature of ultrasound imaging enables cost‐effective, serial monitoring of cardiovascular health, facilitating early identification of high‐risk individuals. For instance, patients with elevated Tei index (indicating systolic/diastolic dysfunction) and low GSM (reflecting unstable plaques) could be prioritized for intensified interventions, such as beta‐blocker optimization, aggressive glycemic control, or lipid‐lowering therapy. Early intervention based on these predictors may mitigate disease progression, reduce MACE incidence, and improve long‐term outcomes. However, it is important to acknowledge that the model's performance was evaluated using a single ROC analysis on the development data, which may result in an overly optimistic performance estimate due to potential overfitting. Future studies should employ internal validation techniques, such as bootstrapping or cross‐validation, to provide a more realistic assessment of the model's accuracy. These methods will help ensure that the model's performance is robust and generalizable to broader patient populations.

Moreover, the study's findings have implications for personalized medicine in post‐PCI care. The identified predictors (e.g., diabetes, high plaque score) can guide stratified management strategies, ensuring that resources are directed toward patients at highest risk. For instance, individuals with both diabetes and unstable carotid plaques may benefit from tighter glycemic control and more aggressive lipid‐lowering therapy to mitigate inflammatory pathways driving plaque vulnerability. The protective effect of beta‐blockers also highlights the importance of adhering to guideline‐directed medical therapy, particularly in patients with reduced LVEF or elevated heart rates. By integrating ultrasound‐derived metrics into clinical decision‐making, clinicians can tailor interventions to address each patient's unique pathophysiological profile, fostering a more precision‐based approach to cardiovascular care.

Although this study provides robust evidence for the utility of combined ultrasound imaging, its retrospective design and single‐center sample limit generalizability. To confirm these findings, prospective, multicenter investigations should be conducted. Clinically, the results support incorporating carotid and cardiac ultrasound into post‐PCI follow‐up to identify high‐risk patients for intensified management, such as aggressive glycemic control, lipid‐lowering therapy, and beta‐blocker optimization. Secondly, although all ultrasound measurements were performed by the same experienced sonographer using standardized protocols, we did not conduct intra‐observer variability analysis (using Intraclass Correlation Coefficient [ICC] or Coefficient of Variation [CV]) for key indices such as the Tei index, IMT, GSM, and TPA. These analyses are important considerations for validating the reliability of these measurements. Future studies should incorporate these analyses to enhance the generalizability and reliability of the results. Finally, a significant limitation is that the inclusion criteria required the presence of plaques in carotid or subclavian artery ultrasound. This criterion introduces selection bias, as patients without detectable plaques were excluded from the study. Therefore, our findings may not be applicable to all patients undergoing PCI, particularly those without evidence of carotid or subclavian atherosclerosis. This selection bias may limit the applicability of our results to the broader PCI population, which includes patients with varying degrees of atherosclerotic burden. Patients without detectable plaques may have different risk profiles and outcomes compared to those with visible plaques, potentially affecting the predictive value of the combined ultrasound model. To mitigate the impact of this bias, future studies should consider including a more diverse patient population, comprising individuals with and without carotid or subclavian plaques. Despite these limitations, our study provides strong evidence supporting the utility of combined ultrasound imaging in predicting MACE in patients with detectable carotid or subclavian plaques. Future research should aim to validate these findings in larger and more diverse cohorts to enhance the generalizability of the results.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that cardiac and carotid ultrasound parameters, alongside clinical comorbidities, effectively predict MACE after PCI. The identified risk factors and protective measures offer actionable insights for personalized cardiovascular care.
