Enhancing rabies epidemic modeling with neural networks and fractional calculus
Authors: Ramsha Shafqat, Imran, Ashraf Al-Quran, Abdelhamid Mohammed Djaouti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRabies remains a major public health concern, particularly in regions where dog-mediated transmission sustains human infection risk. Classical rabies models often rely on integer-order dynamics and overlook the long incubation periods and delayed behavioral responses that characterize the disease. Moreover, neural-network-based surrogate frameworks have rarely been developed for multi-host rabies systems incorporating biologically realistic incidence mechanisms and fractional memory effects.In this study, we propose an eight-compartment human–dog rabies transmission model governed by the Atangana–Baleanu–Caputo (ABC) fractional derivative, which captures nonlocal memory through a non-singular Mittag–Leffler kernel. Transmission between hosts is modeled using a harmonic-mean incidence rate to reflect saturation effects in dog–human and dog–dog contacts. To efficiently approximate the resulting fractional dynamics, we develop a Levenberg–Marquardt-based deep neural network (LMB–DNN) surrogate trained on reference solutions generated by a fractional Adams–Bashforth–Moulton (ABM) predictor–corrector scheme. The neural surrogate accurately reproduces the fractional model dynamics across all eight state variables, achieving mean squared errors in the range –, absolute errors below , and regression coefficients close to unity. A systematic investigation of the fractional order reveals that memory effects significantly influence outbreak timing, peak magnitude, and persistence, with moderate memory (–0.95) producing smoother trajectories consistent with rabies incubation delays. Results from the sensitivity analysis indicate that parameters associated with dog-to-dog transmission and the incubation process exert the strongest influence on the basic reproduction number, suggesting that effective rabies control should primarily focus on dogs. The proposed ABC fractional LMB–DNN framework provides a computationally efficient and biologically meaningful tool for analyzing rabies dynamics with memory effects. The approach offers a practical alternative to repeated fractional simulations, supports rapid scenario exploration, and establishes a foundation for future integration of epidemiological data, uncertainty quantification, and adaptive control strategies in rabies modeling.Keywords: Rabies transmission modeling, Infectious-disease spread, Numerical simulation, Neural-network methods, Levenberg–Marquardt training, Fractional calculusSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Rabies is an acute viral infection of the central nervous system that affects mammals, humans included. Transmission most often follows the bite of an infectious animal, commonly a dog or bat, although exposure can also occur when saliva from an infected animal contacts broken skin or mucous membranes (eyes, nose, or mouth)1,2. Early clinical signs are typically nonspecific fever, headache, myalgias, and paresthesia or pruritus at the wound site and may progress to seizures, hallucinations, and paralysis. After symptom onset, the disease is almost uniformly fatal. Nevertheless, timely wound care and post–exposure prophylaxis (PEP) with vaccine, with or without rabies immunoglobulin, can prevent illness.

Rabies occurs worldwide, its impact is greatest in Africa and Asia, where dog-mediated transmission accounts for the overwhelming majority of human deaths. Global estimates suggest tens of thousands of fatalities each year, with children and underserved populations at disproportionate risk. In high income regions, wildlife reservoirs such as foxes, raccoons, and bats sustain transmission and occasionally spill over to domestic animals and humans. Historical reports document human animal conflicts related to rabies stretching back millennia3,4.

Mathematical modelling has substantially advanced our understanding of rabies dynamics and the expected effects of control strategies5–7. Numerous studies have quantified vaccination impacts in wildlife reservoirs, including raccoons8,9, often formulating vaccination as a control input10,11; related approaches have also been explored in China with setting-specific modifications12. Beyond classical systems, fractional-calculus methods have been successfully applied to epidemic models13,14. Fractional operators can encode memory and nonlocal effects, often improving fit to surveillance data and capturing biological persistence. The Caputo operator is widely used because of its favorable analytical and numerical properties and its ability to model nonsingular, nonlocal dynamics15. More generally, fractional derivatives can be derived from the Riemann–Liouville fractional integral, extending standard differentiation and integration to non-integer orders18,19. These tools have seen broad application in mathematical biology, including diffusion in porous media, hemodynamics, and pathogen spread. The literature includes both singular and nonsingular kernels21,22 and comprehensive analyses of their implementation23,24.

Among nonsingular-kernel operators, the Atangana–Baleanu (AB) derivative introduced in 201615 generalizes Caputo-type formulations and has shown advantages for representing diffusion-like and epidemiological processes. Applications span infectious-disease transmission, tumor-growth models, and pollutant transport in porous media, where nonlocality and memory effects are important. We formulate the rabies dynamics using the ABC fractional derivative to incorporate incubation-driven and behavior-driven memory effects in a physically meaningful way. While the classical Caputo operator is widely used in epidemiology, its power-law kernel is singular near , which can overweight very recent history and may lead to stiffness in simulations. In contrast, the ABC operator replaces the singular kernel by a non-singular, nonlocal Mittag–Leffler kernel, yielding a flexible fading-memory mechanism that has been reported as advantageous for diffusion-like and epidemiological processes where hereditary effects are important16,17. This makes ABC a suitable choice for rabies, where long and variable incubation periods and delayed responses imply that past exposure continues to influence present infection progression.

Despite substantial advances in rabies and epidemic modeling, existing studies offer limited understanding of memory-dependent multi-host transmission mechanisms, particularly for dog to human interactions modeled through biologically meaningful incidence structures. Most classical epidemic models, as well as many fractional formulations, either consider single-host populations or employ integer-order dynamics, which are unable to adequately represent the long incubation periods, delayed behavioral responses, and persistent effects of exposure observed in viral infections. Recent works have demonstrated that fractional-order operators, combined with deep neural network–based numerical solvers, provide an effective framework for incorporating nonlocal memory effects and reducing the computational burden associated with fractional dynamics25–27. In particular, learning-assisted fractional models have been successfully applied to poliomyelitis, rabies, and hepatitis B dynamics, highlighting their capability to capture complex temporal behaviors while maintaining numerical efficiency25–27. The extension of such fractional learning-based approaches to two-host rabies systems, especially those incorporating realistic incidence formulations, remains largely unexplored. These gaps motivate the development of a modeling framework that integrates fractional calculus based memory effects with computationally efficient neural solvers for multi-host rabies transmission dynamics.

In this work, we address this gap by formulating an eight-compartment human–dog rabies model governed by the ABC derivative and by employing a LM trained neural network to learn its nonlinear time evolution. This framework provides a mathematically novel treatment of harmonic-mean transmission under fractional memory, a computationally efficient alternative to repeated fractional simulations, and an epidemiologically relevant platform for analyzing intervention strategies in settings where incubation delays and historical exposure significantly influence disease progression. We adopt a modified version of the rabies model of Eti Dwi et al28., following related developments in29,30. The transmission term is specified using a harmonic-mean incidence, which is well suited for rapid spread between infectious dogs and susceptible humans when the interacting populations are measured in comparable units. The choice of a relatively shallow neural architecture is motivated by the regularity properties of the underlying ABC fractional dynamics. The ABC derivative with a non-singular Mittag–Leffler kernel produces state trajectories that are continuous, smooth, and free of sharp discontinuities. As a result, the input–output mapping from normalized time to state variables is well behaved and does not require highly expressive deep architectures. In this setting, a compact feed-forward network with one or two hidden layers is sufficient to approximate the solution manifold accurately, consistent with universal approximation results for smooth functions. The number of hidden neurons was selected to balance approximation capability and computational efficiency. Sensitivity analysis shows that validation errors stabilize for moderate widths (approximately 20–30 neurons), with negligible improvement from further increases. This behavior arises from the smooth evolution of the fractional solutions, which limits the need for highly expressive neural networks. Adding excessive model capacity does not yield noticeable accuracy gains and can instead introduce over-parameterization. The use of nonlinear activations in the hidden layers is adequate for capturing the system’s nonlinear features, whereas a linear output layer preserves the continuity of the state variables.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis is used to examine how changes in model parameters affect the behavior of a system. By varying one parameter at a time within a specified range while keeping the others fixed, it becomes possible to identify which parameters exert the strongest influence on the model output. This approach is particularly useful for understanding model behavior, assessing uncertainty, and highlighting parameters that are most relevant for control and intervention. For the proposed rabies transmission model, sensitivity analysis of the basic reproduction number indicates that the dog-to-dog transmission rate and the dog incubation parameter have a positive effect on , whereas the dog mortality rate and the removal rate contribute negatively, as illustrated in Fig. 10. From a public health perspective, these results suggest that control strategies focusing on dogs such as mass vaccination, rapid detection and removal of infectious animals, and reducing dog-to-dog contact are more effective than interventions targeting humans alone. This finding is consistent with existing rabies control guidelines and supports the epidemiological relevance of the proposed model.

Computational complexity and training efficiency analysis

The computational behavior of the LM optimizer can be better understood by comparing it with the widely used Adam algorithm. The LM method incorporates curvature information through a Gauss–Newton–type update, which requires forming and solving a linear system at each iteration. As a result, its per-iteration complexity grows approximately as for a network with n adjustable parameters. In contrast, Adam is a first-order gradient method whose update rules only involve simple vector operations, giving it a much lower per-iteration cost of O(n). However, practical efficiency cannot be judged by per-step complexity alone. Because LM uses second-order information, it typically converges in far fewer iterations than Adam for smooth, well-behaved problems. This is exactly the setting encountered in the fractional rabies model, where the system dynamics are continuous and the neural approximation is compact.

The training times reported in Table 2 reflect this advantage. Despite the higher cost per update, LM reached its best validation performance within 186 epochs in Case 1 (13.08 s), 123 epochs in Case 2 (10.43 s), and only 6 epochs in Case 3 (1.39 s). The fast convergence suggests that the LM algorithm is able to locate a good solution without the long iteration counts usually required by first-order optimizers. In practical terms, this reduces the computational load, especially when the network must be retrained many times for parameter studies, uncertainty analysis, or scenario exploration. The method also delivers stable and accurate fits across all tested cases, as reflected in the regression results, the error histograms, and the consistently low MSE values. Taken together, these results indicate that LM achieves a good compromise between computational effort and convergence speed, making it a suitable choice for training neural models that describe fractional-order epidemic dynamics.

Biological interpretation of the fractional order

This section complements the numerical results presented in Section "Effect of the fractional order α on rabies dynamics" by offering a biological interpretation of the fractional order . In the context of rabies transmission, should not be regarded simply as a mathematical tuning parameter, but rather as a quantity that reflects memory effects inherent to the disease process. When , the system behaves similarly to a classical ordinary differential equation model, with infection progression and recovery occurring without appreciable memory. As decreases below unity, the influence of past states becomes more pronounced, leading to slower dynamics, reduced infection peaks, and a spreading of transmission events over longer time intervals. From a practical standpoint, smaller values of correspond to delays between exposure and the onset of active infection. This behavior is consistent with the variable and often prolonged incubation period observed in rabies. The numerical results shown in Fig. 9, where the validation error is minimized for in the range 0.90–0.95, further support the conclusion that moderate memory effects provide a more realistic representation of rabies dynamics.

Biologically, the fractional order can be interpreted as an aggregated description of several real-world mechanisms. These include persistent contact patterns within dog populations, the environmental survival of the virus on surfaces or in saliva, and immune memory processes that affect host susceptibility over time. Each of these introduces a temporal dependence that is difficult to capture using traditional integer–order models. By adjusting , the proposed framework accounts for how past exposures influence current infection levels, resulting in simulated dynamics that better align with observed rabies epidemiology. Overall, the fractional modeling approach offers both numerical flexibility and meaningful biological insight by embedding memory-driven effects directly into the transmission structure.

Limitations and future work

This study offers a detailed analysis of a fractional–order rabies transmission model and demonstrates the usefulness of the LMB–DNN framework, several limitations should be noted. First, the proposed model does not include spatial structure. As a result, it does not explicitly account for dog movement, territorial behavior, or transmission between neighboring communities, all of which can influence the spread of rabies. Incorporating spatial diffusion, metapopulation frameworks, or simple movement rules would improve the biological realism of the model and allow spatial heterogeneity in disease dynamics to be explored. Second, the model is formulated as a deterministic system of fractional differential equations. In real epidemiological settings, randomness arising from demographic variability, heterogeneous contact patterns, and environmental fluctuations can substantially affect outbreak trajectories, particularly during the early stages of an epidemic or in regions with low incidence. Extending the present framework to stochastic or hybrid deterministic–stochastic formulations would provide a more realistic description of rabies transmission in the presence of uncertainty.

The current formulation also assumes a constant dog recruitment rate, whereas free–roaming dog populations often exhibit seasonal birth pulses. Incorporating seasonal forcing or time–dependent demographic processes could help capture the cyclical or irregular outbreak patterns commonly observed in rabies surveillance data. In addition, control measures are modeled through fixed parameters. Future studies could consider optimal or adaptive intervention strategies, such as time–varying vaccination coverage or feedback–based control policies, to increase the relevance of the model for public–health planning. From a computational perspective, the neural network surrogate is trained on temporal trajectories generated for selected parameter configurations. As a result, its predictive accuracy outside the sampled parameter space cannot be guaranteed. Future work could focus on extending the training domain or developing surrogate models that more directly learn the full parameter space. In this regard, physics–informed neural networks (PINNs) offer a promising alternative, as they can be trained directly on the governing fractional equations without relying exclusively on pre–computed datasets. Furthermore, Bayesian neural networks or ensemble learning techniques could be incorporated to provide explicit uncertainty quantification, which is particularly important for epidemiological decision–making.

Another limitation of the present study is the lack of direct validation using reported rabies incidence data. In practice, epidemiological surveillance data are often subject to underreporting, delays in reporting, and measurement noise, which makes model calibration and evaluation challenging. For this reason, the proposed neural surrogate is first validated against a reliable fractional numerical solver, allowing the numerical accuracy and learning performance of the framework to be assessed independently of data-related uncertainties.

Future work will focus on incorporating real epidemiological data, such as time series of human rabies cases, records of dog bites, vaccination coverage, and estimates of dog population size. These data can be integrated through parameter estimation and data-assimilation approaches. For instance, the neural surrogate could be combined with calibration procedures based on least-squares fitting, Bayesian inference, or ensemble filtering to align model outputs with observations while preserving the underlying mechanistic structure of the fractional model. Such extensions would improve the realism and practical applicability of the proposed framework, and enhance its relevance for public-health decision-making, while retaining the benefits of fractional memory modeling and computational efficiency demonstrated in this study.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated an eight–compartment rabies transmission model describing interactions between human and dog populations, formulated using the ABC fractional derivative. To efficiently approximate the resulting memory–dependent dynamics, we introduced an integrated intelligent–computing framework that couples a LMB–DNN. High–accuracy reference solutions were obtained using a fractional ABM predictor–corrector scheme on a normalized time interval, and the neural surrogate was trained using an 80%/10%/10% split for training, validation, and testing. A compact baseline network architecture was adopted, while deeper configurations were also explored to examine robustness. The numerical results show close agreement between the LMB–DNN predictions and the corresponding fractional reference solutions across all eight state variables. This agreement is supported by several diagnostic measures, including regression plots with near–unity correlation, smoothly decreasing MSE curves with effective early stopping, error histograms centered near zero with small variance, and semilogarithmic absolute–error profiles that remain low throughout the simulation period. Together, these indicators demonstrate that the LMB–trained network is able to reproduce the ABC fractional rabies dynamics accurately while maintaining numerical stability and good generalization. Beyond numerical accuracy, the proposed approach contributes to ANN–based epidemic modeling by combining biological realism with computational efficiency. The fractional order naturally captures memory–driven features of rabies transmission, such as prolonged incubation and delayed responses, while the harmonic–mean incidence formulation accounts for saturation effects in host contacts. At the same time, the LMB–DNN surrogate provides a computationally efficient alternative to repeated fractional simulations, enabling rapid exploration of scenarios, sensitivity analyses, and decision–oriented investigations. A hyperparameter sensitivity study further confirms the robustness of the framework. Validation errors remain low over wide ranges of the LM damping parameter and network width, with optimal performance typically observed around and fractional orders . These results suggest that relatively compact neural architectures are sufficient to capture the smooth, memory–driven dynamics of the ABC fractional model, avoiding unnecessary over–parameterization while preserving high predictive accuracy. The proposed LMB–DNN framework offers an accurate, efficient, and reliable surrogate modeling strategy for fractional epidemic systems with harmonic–mean incidence and multi–host coupling. While the present study focuses on rabies transmission, the methodology is readily extensible to other multi–host or multi–scale fractional epidemic models, as well as to broader scientific applications where memory effects and nonlocal dynamics play a central role, including fractional process modeling, bioinformatics with temporal dependence, and complex transport–reaction systems. Its MATLAB based implementation supports transparent parameter sweeps, reproducible training, and rapid what if analyses, making it a practical and flexible tool for researchers studying fractional–order infectious–disease dynamics and control.
