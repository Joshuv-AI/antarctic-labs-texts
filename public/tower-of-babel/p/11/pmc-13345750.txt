Association of Age at Menopause With Lean Body Mass and Sarcopenia Risk in Postmenopausal Korean Women
Authors: Nayoung Jeong, Yujin Jeong, Hye Gyeong Jeong, Ki‐Jin Ryu, Hyuntae Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate whether menopause at or before 45 years of age is associated with lower lean body mass and a higher risk of sarcopenia in postmenopausal Korean women.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study used data from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, 2008–2011. Postmenopausal women aged 40 years or older who underwent whole‐body dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry were included. Lean body mass was defined as fat‐free soft tissue mass excluding bone mineral content. Multivariable linear regression was used to examine total lean body mass, and logistic regression was used to estimate sarcopenia defined by the appendicular skeletal muscle mass‐to‐body mass index ratio.ResultsA total of 4244 postmenopausal women were included. Women with menopause at or before 45 years had significantly lower trunk, leg, and total lean body mass than those with menopause after 45 years. After adjustment for age, body mass index, cardiometabolic factors, menopausal hormone therapy, and walking activity, menopause at or before 45 years remained independently associated with lower total lean body mass (regression coefficient, −430.76; p = 0.0084). In the overall population, the association between early menopause and sarcopenia was not significant after adjustment. However, among women with body mass index of at least 23 kg/m2, menopause at or before 45 years remained significantly associated with sarcopenia (odds ratio, 1.304; 95% confidence interval, 1.017–1.726; p = 0.0432).ConclusionsEarlier menopause was independently associated with lower lean body mass, and its association with sarcopenia was evident in women with higher body mass index.

Introduction

Menopause is a major biological transition in women, marked by the permanent cessation of menstruation and a substantial decline in ovarian estrogen production. Beyond its reproductive significance, menopause is associated with broad metabolic and musculoskeletal changes, and an earlier age at menopause has been linked to an increased risk of adverse health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction, osteoporosis, and all‐cause mortality [1, 2]. Because age at menopause reflects the timing of reproductive aging and cumulative exposure to endogenous estrogen, it may also have important implications for skeletal muscle health.

A growing body of evidence suggests that menopause contributes to unfavorable changes in body composition. During the menopausal transition, women tend to accumulate more visceral and central adiposity, while energy expenditure declines [3]. At the same time, estrogen deficiency is thought to accelerate age‐related deterioration of the musculoskeletal system by affecting muscle protein turnover, mitochondrial function, inflammation, and physical performance [4]. Skeletal muscle mass is not only a determinant of strength and mobility, but is also closely related to glucose homeostasis and cardiometabolic health; lower relative muscle mass has been associated with insulin resistance and prediabetes, and sarcopenia has been linked to systemic inflammation, dyslipidemia, and other metabolic abnormalities [5, 6].

Particular attention has recently been paid to sarcopenic obesity, generally defined as the coexistence of excess adiposity and reduced skeletal muscle mass and/or function. This condition confers a greater risk of physical disability, cardiometabolic disease, and mortality than either obesity or sarcopenia alone [7]. In postmenopausal women, the concurrent increase in fat mass and decline in lean mass makes this condition particularly relevant. Body size–adjusted indices, such as appendicular skeletal muscle mass divided by body mass index (ASM/BMI), have therefore been proposed to better identify sarcopenia in individuals with excess adiposity [8], and this approach has been applied in Korean women using Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) data [9].

Despite the biologic plausibility linking earlier menopause to skeletal muscle loss, evidence directly examining the association between age at menopause and dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DXA)‐derived lean body mass or sarcopenia remains limited. Most prior studies have focused on adiposity, cardiovascular risk, or bone health [2, 10], whereas fewer investigations have specifically evaluated lean mass changes according to menopausal timing [11]. This gap is clinically important because women with premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) or early menopause are exposed to prolonged estrogen deficiency, potentially predisposing them to both reduced lean mass and an adverse fat–muscle phenotype. Furthermore, whether overweight/obesity modifies this association has not been sufficiently explored.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between age at menopause and lean body mass, as well as the risk of sarcopenia, in postmenopausal Korean women using nationally representative KNHANES data. We hypothesized that natural menopause at or before 45 years of age would be associated with lower lean body mass and a greater likelihood of sarcopenia compared with menopause after 45 years. We also explored whether this association differed according to overweight/obesity status.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Data Source, and Study Participants

This study was a cross‐sectional secondary analysis of data from the KNHANES, a nationwide population‐based survey conducted by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to assess the health and nutritional status of the Korean population. We analyzed data from the fourth and fifth KNHANES cycles (2008–2011), which include standardized health interviews, health examinations, and nutritional assessments.

The inclusion criteria were postmenopausal women aged ≥ 40 years who underwent DXA scanning and completed the health survey in the KNHANES database. Menopause was defined as the permanent cessation of menstruation for at least 12 consecutive months based on self‐reported questionnaire data obtained through standardized face‐to‐face interviews conducted by trained KNHANES interviewers. Age at natural menopause was also self‐reported.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) menopause induced by pharmacologic or surgical interventions (e.g., hysterectomy and/or bilateral oophorectomy); (2) unknown age at menopause; (3) a history of cardiovascular disease (myocardial infarction, ischemic angina, or stroke); (4) chronic kidney disease; and (5) a history of malignant disease. A detailed flow diagram of participant selection is provided in Figure S1.

The KNHANES protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The present secondary analysis was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Anam Hospital (IRB no. 2020AN0124).

Participants were categorized into three groups according to self‐reported age at natural menopause: POI (< 40 years), early menopause (40–45 years), and menopause after the age of 45 years. For regression analyses, participants were further classified into two groups according to age at menopause (≤ 45 vs. > 45 years).

Measurement of Body Composition and Definition of Sarcopenia

Total body composition and bone mineral density (BMD) were assessed using DXA. Body composition measurements were performed using a standardized DXA system (Hologic Inc., Bedford, MA, USA) with routine calibration and quality control procedures conducted according to the manufacturer's guidelines and survey protocols. From whole‐body scans, total fat mass, percentage body fat, bone mineral content, and lean soft tissue mass were obtained. Lean body mass was defined as fat‐free soft tissue mass excluding bone mineral content. Lean mass was measured for the total body, trunk, arms, and legs. Fat mass and percentage body fat were assessed for the total body and trunk regions. BMD was measured at the femoral neck and lumbar spine, which are standard skeletal sites used in the diagnosis of osteoporosis.

ASM was defined as the sum of lean soft tissue mass in both arms and both legs measured by whole‐body DXA. Sarcopenia was defined using the ASM‐to‐BMI ratio (ASM/BMI), with a cut‐off value of < 0.50 m2, based on two standard deviations below the mean of a young reference group, as previously described [8, 9]. BMI was calculated by dividing body weight by height squared (kg/m2).

Because KNHANES is a cross‐sectional survey of community‐dwelling women, body composition was measured at the time of the KNHANES examination rather than at the time of menopause. Participant age at examination and self‐reported age at menopause were therefore considered separately in the present analysis. Accordingly, in some participants, DXA assessment may have been performed several years, or even decades, after menopause. Thus, the present analysis evaluated the association between recalled age at menopause and body composition measured later in postmenopausal life.

Covariates

Participants were surveyed regarding their medical history, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and use of menopausal hormone therapy (MHT), as well as lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption and walking activity. Walking activity was assessed by asking participants whether they had engaged in walking for at least 10 min per session on one or fewer days, or on two or more days during the past week.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were compared among the three menopausal timing groups using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test. For variables showing significant overall differences on ANOVA, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test.

Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between age at menopause and lean body mass. For statistical analysis, postmenopausal women were categorized into two groups according to age at menopause: ≤ 45 years and > 45 years. Women who experienced menopause at > 45 years (considered normal menopause) were used as the reference group. Three models were constructed. Model 1 was unadjusted (crude model). Model 2 was adjusted for age and BMI. Model 3 was further adjusted for age, BMI, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, MHT, and walking activity (≥ 10 min per session at least once per week).

Logistic regression analysis was conducted to estimate odds ratios (ORs) for sarcopenia. In this analysis, Model 1 was unadjusted, Model 2 was adjusted for age, and Model 3 was adjusted for age, BMI, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, MHT, and walking activity (≥ 10 min per session at least once per week).

Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed using logistic regression after stratifying participants by BMI according to the Asia‐Pacific criteria (BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 vs. < 23 kg/m2). In the subgroup models, Model 1 was unadjusted, Model 2 was adjusted for age, and Model 3 was adjusted for age, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, MHT, and walking activity (≥ 10 min per session at least once per week).

A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Among 4244 postmenopausal women included in the analysis, 239 (5.6%) had POI, 442 (10.4%) experienced early menopause, and 3563 (84.0%) experienced menopause at > 45 years. The age at examination ranged from 38 to 80 years, and the self‐reported age at menopause ranged from 20 to 62 years. Women with menopause after the age of 45 years were, on average, the youngest group at the time of the KNHANES examination, whereas women with POI and early menopause were older at the time of assessment. Post hoc analysis using Tukey's HSD test showed that the menopause‐after‐45‐years group differed significantly from the POI and early menopause groups for age at examination, height, and weight, whereas the POI and early menopause groups did not differ significantly for these variables. Discussion

In this cross‐sectional study of postmenopausal Korean women using KNHANES data, menopause at or before 45 years of age was associated with lower DXA‐derived lean body mass, particularly in the trunk and legs, independent of age, BMI, and major cardiometabolic covariates. By contrast, total and trunk fat mass did not differ significantly according to menopausal timing. Although the association between early menopause and sarcopenia was attenuated after adjustment in the overall population, a significant association persisted among women with BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2. Taken together, these findings suggest that earlier reproductive aging is more strongly related to reduced lean mass than to absolute fat accumulation, and that its clinical impact may be most evident in the context of excess adiposity.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that earlier ovarian aging—including both early menopause and POI—is associated with adverse muscle outcomes. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis by Divaris et al. [12] showed that early menopause (≤ 45 years) was associated with lower muscle mass, whereas POI was more strongly associated with impaired muscle strength and physical performance. Similarly, Douchi et al. [13] reported that trunk and total body lean mass were inversely associated with menopausal status, and other observational and longitudinal studies have shown that the menopausal transition is accompanied by declines in lean mass and that menopausal status is independently associated with lower skeletal muscle mass [14, 15, 16]. In addition, Li et al. [17] found that women with spontaneous POI had lower DXA‐derived muscle mass and a higher prevalence of low muscle mass than healthy controls. Taken together, these findings support the view that earlier cessation of ovarian function and shorter lifetime estrogen exposure contribute to muscle loss beyond the effects of chronological aging, and our study extends this evidence in a nationally representative population.

A notable finding of the present study is that the association between early menopause and sarcopenia was not independent in the overall sample after adjustment but remained significant among women with BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2. This suggests that the adverse muscular consequences of early menopause may become more clinically relevant in the presence of higher body weight. Previous studies in Korean women have shown that the coexistence of excess adiposity and reduced muscle mass becomes more common during the menopausal transition and is associated with adverse metabolic and musculoskeletal outcomes [18, 19, 20]. Taken together, these findings suggest that earlier menopause may contribute to increased vulnerability to sarcopenia among women with higher BMI. Our findings extend the existing literature by suggesting that age at menopause may help identify women with higher BMI who may be at increased risk of sarcopenia, even when differences in absolute fat mass are not evident.

Several biologically plausible mechanisms may explain these findings. Early menopause and POI prolong the duration of exposure to estrogen deficiency, and estrogen appears to play an important role in maintaining skeletal muscle mass and function [4, 21]. Experimental and translational studies suggest that reduced estrogen signaling may impair satellite cell activity, mitochondrial function, muscle protein turnover, and muscle recovery, while promoting oxidative stress and inflammation [4, 21]. In addition, low muscle mass is closely linked to insulin resistance and cardiometabolic dysfunction, and obesity further amplifies these processes through chronic low‐grade inflammation, ectopic fat deposition within muscle, and reduced muscle quality [5, 6]. The combination of prolonged hypoestrogenism and excess adiposity may therefore create a biologic environment that favors relative muscle insufficiency. This interpretation is consistent with our observation that early menopause was associated with lower lean mass even in the absence of significant between‐group differences in total or trunk fat mass.

The role of MHT in preserving muscle mass remains uncertain. In our fully adjusted models, MHT was not significantly associated with lean mass. This is consistent with a systematic review and meta‐analysis of randomized trials showing no statistically significant overall benefit of estrogen‐based hormone therapy on lean body mass in postmenopausal women [22]. Accordingly, although MHT remains important for symptom control and for prevention of selected long‐term consequences of early estrogen deficiency, it should not be assumed to be sufficient as a stand‐alone strategy for preserving skeletal muscle mass.

Women with early menopause or POI are already recognized as being at increased risk for long‐term bone and cardiometabolic complications [1, 2]. Our findings suggest that skeletal muscle health should also be considered in this population, particularly among women with BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2. From a public health perspective, age at menopause may serve as a simple and readily available marker for identifying postmenopausal women at increased risk of low lean body mass and sarcopenia‐related vulnerability. This may be particularly relevant in women with higher BMI, for whom earlier screening, lifestyle counseling, and preventive strategies targeting muscle health could help reduce subsequent functional decline and metabolic complications [23, 24, 25].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, age at menopause and medical history were self‐reported and therefore subject to recall bias. In addition, the three menopausal timing groups differed in age at the time of the KNHANES examination, with women who underwent menopause after the age of 45 years being younger, on average, than those with POI or early menopause. Although age was adjusted for in the multivariable analyses, residual confounding by chronological aging cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, body composition was assessed at the time of the survey rather than at menopause; thus, in some women, the interval between menopause and DXA assessment may have been substantial and may have influenced the observed associations. Third, sarcopenia was defined using a low muscle mass index without measures of muscle strength or physical performance, so the outcome may better reflect low relative muscle mass than sarcopenia under current consensus definitions [23, 24]. In addition, residual confounding from factors such as diet, exercise, MHT exposure, and inflammatory or hormonal biomarkers cannot be excluded. Finally, because this study included only Korean women, the generalizability of the findings may be limited.

In conclusion, menopause at or before the age of 45 years was independently associated with lower lean body mass in postmenopausal Korean women, and its association with sarcopenia was most evident among women with BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2. These findings support the concept that earlier reproductive aging may contribute to an adverse muscle phenotype, particularly in the presence of excess adiposity. Prospective studies incorporating muscle strength, physical performance, and longitudinal changes in body composition are needed to clarify causality and to determine whether targeted screening and early intervention can reduce the risk of sarcopenia in women with early menopause.
