The estimated potential birth loss from female accidental mortality in Iran, 2011–2023
Authors: Reza Eshraghi, Mostafa Amini-Rarani, Mohammad Esmaiel Motlagh, Alireza Rahimi, Mohammad Mohseni, Ashkan bahrami, Mahshid Amini, Negin Mousavi, Hamid Galehdari, Salman Bashzar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRoad-traffic injuries remain an important cause of premature death among women of reproductive age in Iran. We quantified the number of potential births not realised that are attributable to female deaths from road-related accidents, to inform injury-prevention and fertility policy.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, population-based counterfactual analysis for 2011–2023. Annual female deaths from road-traffic injuries by age (Global Burden of Disease 2023) were regarded “aged forward” to identify women who would have been 15–49 years in a given year had they survived. For each age-year cell, we multiplied the resulting count of “missing” women by the contemporaneous age-specific fertility rate (ASFR) from national sources, and summed across ages to estimate potential births not realised each year. Uncertainty was propagated using a deterministic bound approach based on lower/upper bounds of mortality and fertility inputs.ResultsFemale accidental mortality was associated with an estimated 121 929 (cumulative) potential births not realized from 2011 to 2023. Annual losses declined by 55%, from 12 889 (conservative uncertainty bounds 4 053–24 936) in 2011 to 5 829 (conservative uncertainty bounds 3 200–7 685) in 2023, indicating a significant downward trend. Although small relative to Iran’s multi-decade decline in overall births, these counterfactual losses persist at non-trivial levels.ConclusionFurther reductions in preventable road-traffic deaths among women of reproductive age could modestly increase realised births while delivering primary health gains. Priorities include safer speeds and infrastructure, seat-belts/helmets, safer vehicles, fair enforcement, and strengthened prehospital and trauma care, measures that complement existing fertility initiatives without coercion.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12963-026-00499-w.

Introduction

Over the past several decades, Iran has undergone profound demographic changes. One of the most striking shifts has been a dramatic decline in fertility. Iranian women went from having on average 6–7 children in the 1980s to about 1.6 children in recent years [1]. This rapid fertility decline is one of the fastest ever recorded and has brought the total fertility rate (TFR) well below the replacement level of 2.1. By 2019, Iran’s TFR was estimated around 2.1 after a slight rebound, but it has since fallen back to roughly 1.7 or lower. This trend has raised alarm about population aging and future workforce decline. In response, since 2014 the Iranian government has reversed earlier family planning policies and adopted explicit pro-natalist policies aimed at increasing fertility rates [2]. These policies include incentives for childbearing and restrictions on contraceptive services, reflecting high-level concern that the nation’s demographic future is at risk. Beyond the direct demographic implications of low fertility, the premature loss of women during reproductive and economically active ages may also create a broader social and economic burden. Women contribute not only to future birth counts and childbearing, but also to household income and productivity. Therefore, female mortality in young and middle adulthood may affect both population replacement and the productive capacity of households and communities.

At the same time, it is increasingly recognized that fertility outcomes are intertwined with health and mortality conditions. In particular, the premature death of women of childbearing age can reduce the number of children born in a population. If a young woman dies before or during her reproductive years, the children she might have had in the future are “lost” to the population. This mortality-fertility nexus has been studied in some contexts: for instance, a recent analysis in the United States estimated that nearly 200,000 children were not born in the 2010s due to the early death of potential mothers [3]. In high-income countries, rising mortality among young adults (e.g. from overdoses or other causes) has prompted concern about its impact on birth counts. However, in many developing or middle-income countries, this issue remains under-examined. This gap is particularly important because the consequences of premature adult mortality are not distributed equally across societies. Previous road safety literature shows that the burden of road traffic mortality is often greater among socioeconomically vulnerable groups, including people with lower income, lower education, limited access to safe transport, and those living in areas with weaker road infrastructure or emergency response systems [4].

In Iran, a critical area to consider is female mortality from road accidents. Iran historically has had a high burden of injuries, particularly road traffic accidents [5]. In fact, among young Iranians, “injuries” have been the most common cause of mortality in recent years, with road traffic injuries the single largest component (sub-cause) of those injury deaths [6]. These fatalities disproportionately involve males (who tend to take more risks on the road), but thousands of females are also killed by accidents each year. According to one report using Global Burden of Disease data, transport injuries alone were the third most common cause of death in Iran (all ages, both sexes) in recent years, accounting for an estimated ~ 21,000 deaths per year [5].

To our knowledge, the impact of female mortality on foregone births has not been quantified for Iran. And this is the first study which evaluate this hidden impact of female mortalities through road accidents. Given Iran’s current push to boost birth rates, it is timely to ask: How many additional births might occur each year if fewer women died prematurely? In other words, how many potential births are “lost” because women who would be bearing children are no longer alive to do so? Addressing this question also allows road traffic mortality to be viewed not only as a cause of immediate death, but also as a contributor to longer-term demographic loss, household vulnerability, and reduced social productivity.

Road accidents are largely preventable through public health and safety interventions, so this subset of mortality is of particular policy interest. We leverage four decades of data (1980–2023) on female accident mortality and age specific fertility rates (ASFRs) in Iran to calculate the expected number of births that did not happen each year because women died in accidents, called “potential birth loss”. By quantifying these “potential birth loss,” we aim to highlight another hidden dark burden of road-associated mortality. These findings quantify the non-trivial impact of road accident mortality on Iran’s fertility potential. By clarifying the magnitude of this loss, the study provides policymakers with a crucial metric to weigh investments in road safety against other, more direct, pronatalist policies aimed at increasing birth rates.

Methods

Data sources

This study utilized two main data sources: (1) female mortality data by age from accidental causes, and (2) age-specific fertility rates of Iranian women.

For mortality, we obtained annual numbers of female deaths due to accidents in Iran for each year 1980 through 2023, stratified by age group. These data were extracted from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study 2023 results, which provide comprehensive estimates of deaths by cause, age, and sex for Iran (which are demonstrated in details in Supplementary materials) [7]. The GBD data are modeled from multiple sources (vital registration, verbal autopsy, police reports, etc.) and include 95% uncertainty intervals (CI) reflecting statistical uncertainty in the estimates [8]. Age groups in the mortality data were mostly 5-year bands (e.g. <5, 5–14, 15–19, 20–24, ., 45–49 years, etc., up to 95+), which we used for analysis. We focused on deaths occurring before age 50 (prior to the end of female reproductive age).

For fertility, we compiled age-specific fertility rates (ASFR) for Iranian females for each year 1980–2023. These rates represent the annual number of births per 1,000 women in each age group (typically 15–19, 20–24, ., 45–49). Historical ASFR were obtained from the Persian national report published by Tehran University of Medical Sciences/National Institute of Health Research, which was based on the GBD 2017 fertility estimates [9, 10]. For years beyond the coverage of that report, fertility values were updated using the report from Statistical Centre of Iran [11]. The fertility dataset included 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each age-year-specific rate, where available, to account for sampling or estimation uncertainty (for example, around survey-based estimates). These fertility rates capture the prevailing childbearing behavior of women at each age group in each year. Our analysis uses these rates as the counterfactual birth probabilities for women who died, i.e., what their chances of giving birth would have been, on average, had they survived.

Estimating potential births lost

We define “potential births lost” due to female mortality as the number of children that would have been born in a given year if those women had not died prematurely. Our approach to estimate this is essentially a counterfactual population calculation, aligning with methods used to compute net vs. gross reproduction in demography [12, 13]. In simplified terms, it compares the actual scenario (where some women died and therefore could not have children) with a hypothetical scenario in which those women survived and had children at normal rates. The difference in birth count is attributed to the mortality.

We repeated the above for each year from 2011 to 2023. We chose to focus on the recent decade to highlight current levels and trends, though the methodology can be applied to earlier years as well in case of the existence of the required data.

It should be noted that a woman who died contributes to lost births potentially in multiple years, essentially, for each year until she would have exited reproductive age (50 years). Our calculation inherently accounts for that: e.g. a woman who died at age 20 in 2010 will be counted among missing age-21 women in 2011, missing age-22 women in 2012, and so on until she would have turned 50 (or until 2021). Thus, the annual estimates are not independent but overlapping in terms of cohorts. This approach mirrors the concept of the net reproduction rate (NRR) in demography, which reduces the gross reproduction rate by the probability of surviving to each age of childbearing [3]. Here we are quantifying in absolute terms the effect of less-than-100% survival to the end of reproductive years.

Assumptions

Our calculation requires a few key assumptions. First, we assumed that if a woman had not died in an accident, she would have survived to at least the end of the year in question (and in fact we assume survival up to age 50 for the purpose of counting all potential births). In reality, some women might have died later from other causes or could die in subsequent accidents; we did not model competing risks. We effectively assume accident victims would otherwise have the same life expectancy as the general population of women. Second, we assume that a woman who died would have experienced the average fertility of her age group in each subsequent year. This ignores any individual characteristics. We also did not account for parity (how many children a woman already had at death); a woman who already had several children might on average have different additional children remaining, but we use age-specific averages which smooth over such differences. Despite these simplifications, at the population level these assumptions provide a reasonable expected value of births lost, as also done in standard demographic rates like the net reproduction rate (3). Finally, our analysis is limited to female mortality; we did not consider any fertility impact of male deaths (e.g. a shortage of males might affect marriage patterns, but in general female survival is the limiting factor for births).

Statistical analysis

Both mortality and fertility inputs include 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To propagate this uncertainty into the estimated number of lost births, we used a conservative deterministic bound approach based on the monotonic relationship. Because full posterior draws and covariance structures for both mortality and fertility inputs were not consistently available across the full analytic period, we propagated uncertainty using a deterministic bound approach. This approach multiplies the lower mortality-derived missing-women estimate by the lower fertility estimate, and the upper mortality-derived estimate by the upper fertility estimate. These intervals should therefore be interpreted as conservative uncertainty bounds rather than fully distribution-derived 95% posterior intervals. Using 5-year ASFR for single-year ages

Because age-specific fertility rates (ASFR) were available only in 5-year bands (e.g., 35–39), we assigned the band rate to each single-year age within that band (e.g., age 37 = ASFR (35–39)). This is a standard demographic approximation when age schedules are smooth, and it preserves the observed total fertility within each band. The trade-off is that any within-band curvature (e.g., a peak at 27 or 32) is flattened, which can slightly misallocate expected births across adjacent single ages without materially changing totals. This choice is unlikely to affect our headline estimates materially because our outcome aggregates across all reproductive ages, but it could marginally bias age-specific contributions. Where finer ASFRs are unavailable, this approach is transparent and reproducible; nonetheless, future work could refine it using simple interpolation (e.g., Beers/Sprague or spline methods) if single-year ASFRs become accessible.

Allocating 5-year mortality data into individual years

For some historical years, female mortality was reported only as an aggregate period. In the absence of a breakdown by single year, we distributed these deaths evenly across included ages to enable cohort aging in our counterfactual. This assumption is conservative and transparent but imperfect.

Results

In 1980, more than 40 000 people in Iran died from road traffic injuries; by 2020 this annual toll had fallen to approximately 20 000 (Fig. 1). Male mortality from road injuries was substantially higher than female mortality throughout this period, although both sexes experienced marked declines in road injury deaths over time (Fig. 1).

Iran’s fertility has undergone a similarly dramatic transition. The total fertility rate (TFR) declined from 7.56 births per woman in 1980 to 1.45 in 2023 (Fig. 2). Age-specific fertility rates (ASFRs) fell across all reproductive age groups, with the steepest relative declines among younger women (especially those aged 20–29 years), whereas fertility at older ages (e.g. ≥30 years) changed comparatively little (Fig. 2). By the 2010s, Iran’s TFR had persistently remained below the replacement level of 2.1, reflecting an era of ultra-low fertility.

Between 2011 and 2023, premature female accidental deaths in Iran resulted in an estimated cumulative loss of 121 929 potential births. The annual number of births not realized due to these premature female deaths demonstrated a significant declining trend, falling from a peak of roughly 12 889 (conservative uncertainty bounds 4 053–24 936) in 2011 to 5 829 (conservative uncertainty bounds 3 200–7 685) in 2023 Fig. 3). This corresponds to an approximate 55% reduction in the yearly “birth deficit” over the 13-year period. There were minor year-to-year fluctuations in the estimated lost births, but the overall trajectory was downward. Notably, after 2018 the decline in annual lost births accelerated, indicating a more pronounced reduction in foregone births in the later years of the study period (Fig. 3).

Trends in road-traffic injury mortality among women less than 50 years old in EMRO, 1980–2023

Across the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region, most countries experienced substantial declines in mortality among females younger than 50 between 1980 and 2023 (negative Mortality rate mean difference (rate-MD) for 18 of 20 countries). As demonstrated in Table 1, the largest absolute reduction was observed in Iran (from 138.37 to 16.71; rate-MD − 121.66), with notable declines also in Oman (− 56.34), Saudi Arabia (− 35.02), Sudan (− 33.58), UAE (− 29.17), and Qatar (− 21.23). A small number of countries showed increases over the period, Pakistan (+ 2.10) and Afghanistan (+ 2.39), underscoring persistent heterogeneity in regional progress. By 2023, the lowest mortality rates were observed in UAE (3.77), Qatar (4.31), Syrian Arab Republic (4.72), and Lebanon (6.22), whereas Sudan (21.76) and Iran (16.71) remained higher relative to the regional minimum. These descriptive patterns provide context for our main analyses by indicating that, while broad survival gains among women of reproductive age have occurred across EMRO, the pace and completeness of improvement vary, highlighting opportunities for continued investment in injury prevention, road safety, trauma/EMS strengthening, and women’s health services tailored to each country’s profile. Four decades of fertility decline across the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region (1980–2023)

Across EMRO, TFR, as defined per WHO/World Bank, fell in every country between 1980 and 2023, with the largest absolute declines in Saudi Arabia (− 6.81), Iran (− 6.12), Oman (− 5.43), Libya (− 5.11), Jordan (− 4.90), and Syria (− 4.85) (presented in Tabled 2). By 2023, several countries had sub-replacement fertility ( ≈ ≤ 2.1 births/woman), including Qatar (1.38), Iran (1.45), Kuwait (1.52), Saudi Arabia (1.64), Tunisia (1.68), Bahrain (1.61), UAE (2.12), Libya (1.96), and Syria (1.95), whereas higher levels persisted in Afghanistan (5.20), Yemen (3.91), Pakistan (3.26), Sudan (2.99), and Palestine (2.98). These patterns confirm a broad regional transition to low fertility, with considerable heterogeneity in end-levels: many Gulf and North African countries now sit near or below replacement, while parts of the Region (e.g., Afghanistan, Yemen, Pakistan) remain well above it (Table 2).

Discussion

This study set out to quantify an invisible consequence of premature mortality: the forgone childbearing due to women dying young. Our findings for Iran in 2011–2023 suggest that while the scale of lost births is relatively modest in percentage terms, it is not negligible. Each year, on the order of several thousand births are directly effectively wiped off the balance sheet of population growth because of accidental deaths among women. Over the study period, accumulated 120,000 potential births lost carries real significance in demographic planning. These “missing” children represent an additional burden of tragedy: beyond the immediate grief of lost daughters, sisters, and wives, there are also unborn sons and daughters who won’t be there to contribute to families and society.

Premature female death not only causes the immediate loss of the woman’s own future births but also reverberates through her family and community. For example, Polizzi & Tilstra (2024) estimate that roughly 200,000 U.S. births were “missing” in the 2010s due to maternal deaths [3]. Our calculation for Iranian female accident deaths (tens of thousands of lost births) similarly highlights the direct demographic cost. However, this tally omits broader repercussions. A woman’s death imposes psychological, social and economic shocks on her husband, children, kin and peers, which in turn can delay or reduce their childbearing. In effect, each maternal mortality event generates fertility ripples beyond the deceased woman herself.

To put these numbers in context, the total births in Iran per year during this period were on the order of one to one-and-a-half million. Thus, the lost births due to accidents constituted on average less than 1% of annual births. For instance, in 2015 Iran had ~ 1.5 million births per year, versus ~ 8–9 thousand lost (≈ 0.6%) [14]. By 2020, births were closer to 1.0 million, versus ~ 7 thousand lost (≈ 0.7%). Thus, the demographic impact of eliminating these deaths, in terms of immediate birth count, would be modest but non-negligible. Over the entire 2011–2023 decade, summing the yearly estimates yields roughly 120,000 direct cumulative births lost. This is equivalent to the number of babies that would populate a medium-sized Iranian city. These “missing” children underscore the hidden toll of accidents beyond the direct fatalities.

Female accidental mortality in Iran has been decreasing, thanks to improvements such as better road safety, vehicle standards, health care, and public awareness. GBD data indicate that Iran’s age-standardized death rate from transport injuries dropped by about 62% between 1990 and 2021 [5], and similar declines have been seen in other accident categories. Our input data show that the absolute number of reproductive-age female deaths from accidents fell significantly over the last decade. For example, in 2011 there were over 4,313 road accidental deaths among females of all ages, of which 3,022 cases were younger than 50 years old [5]. By 2023, female accidental deaths were down to around 4,050 for all ages, of whom 2,636 cases were younger than 50. With fewer women dying prematurely, the pool of “missing” potential mothers has shrunk, directly reducing lost births.

To put Iran’s situation in perspective, the United States, a country with a larger population but different mortality profile, lost an estimated ~ 200,000 births in just the 2010s from premature maternal deaths of all causes [3]. The fact that Iran’s relative impact is a bit higher might be due to the still-elevated rates of accidental death in a middle-income setting. Iran has worked to reduce injuries and has succeeded to a large extent [5], yet its traffic fatality rates, for example, remain higher than those in most developed countries. This implies there is room for further improvement and thus room to save more births indirectly. Meanwhile, in very high-mortality environments (say in parts of sub-Saharan Africa with high maternal mortality or HIV), the impact of female deaths on birth totals would be even larger; Iran thankfully is far from those extremes [15]. In low-fertility, low-mortality countries (like many in Europe or East Asia), the number of births lost to female death is very low because both fertility and young female mortality are low [16–18].

The premature loss of a woman in her reproductive years can send shockwaves through her family’s demographic behavior. The fertility intentions and outcomes of surviving kin, including widowed husbands, siblings, children, and extended family, are often reshaped by the interplay of grief, practical caregiving burdens, and social adjustments. Drawing on global evidence, this section discusses how such a tragedy influences psychological readiness for childbearing, alters socioeconomic capacity for raising children, triggers remarriage or other behavioral responses, and even reverberates across kinship networks and generations (Table 3). Another implication is more qualitative: support for women’s health and safety can achieve population goals without coercion. Iran’s pro-natalist shift has raised some concerns about women’s rights, as policies have included restricting access to contraceptives and penalizing sterilization [19]. These can impinge on reproductive autonomy and have other unintended consequences (e.g. unsafe abortions [20]). In contrast, measures like making roads safer or improving healthcare are unequivocal positives that both save lives and indirectly allow women the freedom to have children later (rather than losing that chance to early death). As noted by demographers, improving survival up to age 50 increases the net reproduction rate, essentially, a higher fraction of the potential fertility is realized [3].

Limitations

We must acknowledge several limitations of our study. First, our estimates rely on the accuracy of the input data. While GBD 2023 and national statistics are among the best available sources, there is uncertainty especially in earlier years when death registration was less complete. We incorporated CIs to reflect this, but there could still be biases. Without detailed data on the deceased women’s backgrounds, we could not adjust for covariates. Thus, ASFR was used as the empirical population-level proxy for the likelihood of future birth, while reproductive intent and socioeconomic realities were not modeled individually but were indirectly embedded in the observed fertility rates of Iranian women in each age-year group. However, given the large population-level approach, these differences likely cancel out to some degree when considering totals. In addition, we assumed women saved from accidents would survive through their childbearing years. Another limitation is that we could not stratify potential birth loss by the mode of road-traffic crash. The magnitude of birth loss may differ between pedestrian deaths, drivers, passengers, motorcyclists, or other vehicle occupants, because these groups may have different age distributions, socioeconomic profiles, and documentation accuracy. Since our analysis relied on aggregated mortality data, crash-mode-specific information was not available. Future studies using linked police, forensic, and hospital trauma records could estimate potential birth loss separately by road-user category.

Despite these limitations, we believe the overall trends and magnitudes presented are reasonably robust. The consistency of decline and the fact that multiple data sources (GBD, national stats) align in direction give confidence in the big-picture message.

Conclusion

This analysis suggests that Iranian women’s premature road-traffic deaths cost over 120,000 potential births in 2011–2023. Although this is under ~ 1% of total births (on the order of 1 million per year), it represents a substantial and largely preventable deficit of future children. Our method, combining age-specific female mortality with cohort fertility schedules, is a novel transparent and reproducible, providing a template that could be applied in other settings to quantify such “foregone births” and population-health policy.

Encouragingly, female road-traffic mortality in Iran has fallen sharply since the mid-2000s, but continued investment in road safety (safer infrastructure, law enforcement, trauma care) and women’s health programs is needed to sustain this progress. Such measures serve humanitarian and pro-fertility goals alike without impinging on reproductive autonomy.

Finally, women’s deaths reverberate throughout families. Children of mothers who die are often deprived of care, with worse health and social outcomes, and kinship networks can be destabilized. By preventing premature female mortality, Iran can conserve potential births and preserve family support systems, strengthening communities while fully respecting women’s reproductive rights.
