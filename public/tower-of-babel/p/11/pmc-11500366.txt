Drying Kinetics and Sorption Isotherms of Biocomposite Materials: Experimental Investigation and Modeling Analysis
Authors: Yosra Hfaiedh, Houda Hachem, Daoued Mihoubi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractModern materials science has made significant progress in creating new materials and processes for waste recovery. One such advancement involves the development of a new porous composite material made from clay and an environmentally sustainable eggshell powder. In this study, the dynamic behavior of water vapor during desorption and adsorption processes within this composite material was investigated. The moisture diffusivity coefficient was determined within the clay/eggshell powder composite material. The drying kinetic data were analyzed using various models such as Newton, Page, and Wan and Singh. The static gravimetric vapor adsorption/desorption experiments were conducted at different temperatures (30, 40, 50, 60, and 70 °C) to depict the material’s capacity for absorbing and releasing moisture over time. The experimental isotherm data were fitted using different mathematical models (Guggenheim, Anderson, and De Boer, Peleg, Adam and Shove, Oswin, and Modified Henderson). The findings suggest that integrating eggshell powder with clay can significantly influence the behavior and characteristics of the resultant material. The combination of eggshell powder and clay reduces moisture sorption and accelerates the drying process. Consequently, this affects the porosity, permeability, and response to humidity changes.

Introduction

In today’s world, where environmental sustainability and resource conservation are becoming increasingly important, the efficient handling of waste materials has taken on unprecedented significance. Among the areas that demand heightened attention, the disposal of eggshells (ESs) is a particularly crucial focal point. Conventional waste disposal methods have a significant environmental impact, which is why ESs, once considered byproducts of the food industry, are now being reevaluated. Instead of being sent to landfills or incinerators, ESs can be repurposed for various applications, and this has drawn attention to the need for innovative solutions to waste and pollution. A promising approach has been the development of new bioceramic materials by incorporating ES waste into clay (CL) matrices. This field is rapidly evolving and has the potential to minimize environmental impact while enhancing the properties and functionalities of ceramics. These novel materials have a wide range of potential applications across various industries, offering both ecological and technological progress. It is crucial to explore the potential benefits of managing ES waste by creating innovative ceramic materials. This symbiotic relationship between waste management and materials science can lead to significant advancements and a future that prioritizes ecological sustainability and technological innovation.

Many researchers have made contributions to our understanding of the transport phenomena involved in the mechanisms of heat and mass transfer that occur in deformable environments during the solid–liquid separation process. In the drying process, some issues are typically highly coupled: the product’s deformation, cracking, and drying kinetics. The removal of the water causes the solid matrix to compress, which affects both the material’s mechanical properties and structural durability. A good prediction of the behavior of the materials during drying is crucial for its control.1 Drying CL is a crucial step in manufacturing industrial products, such as tiles, pottery, and bricks. In a recent study by Hachem et al.,2 the drying characteristics of a new bioceramic material called ES-reinforced kaolin (K) composite were examined using convective (CV) and infrared (IR) drying methods. The study found that IR drying reduces the drying time and energy consumption while improving moisture diffusion. This was particularly noticeable in the K-50% ES composite at 90 °C, although optimal quality was achieved at a lower temperature of 60 °C. Proper control of all manufacturing phases, including the drying process, is necessary to ensure the quality of the final product and improve the production process. To this end, Zagrouba et al.3 developed a mathematical model that factors in heat, mass, and momentum transfer during the CL drying process. The results of the study show that temperature and material distribution can cause nonuniform displacement within the material. The process of drying CL pastes is a crucial and intricate industrial procedure. It is expensive and can result in defects such as deformations and splits, which can quickly lead to the formation of cracks. This process involves several physical phenomena, some of which are not yet fully comprehended.4 Due to its abundance, CL has been studied by many researchers. In a study by Saeidi and Parvini,5 various isotherm models were compared to determine the micropore volume in bentonite samples. The study concluded that these models are effective in calculating micropore volumes in bentonite samples with differing textural properties. CL possesses exceptional physicochemical properties, including a structural design that facilitates the circulation of air and water and a significant ability to absorb water between its layers. Due to these characteristics, CL is an excellent quality material for various applications. Several researchers have contributed to our understanding of the transport phenomena that occur during the solid–liquid separation process in deformable media. Mihoubi and Bellagi6 conducted a numerical evaluation of temperature, drying curves, and stress distribution in different configurations of a convectively dried CL slab. Their study showed significant differences between the two configurations in terms of temperature, moisture, and drying-induced stresses.

Moisture adsorption and desorption isotherms provide crucial information about how samples interact with water and the mechanism behind the sorption and desorption. In a study by Akin et al.,7 various mathematical models were evaluated for water vapor sorption on expansive CLs. The results describe how strongly water is bound to a solid and provide valuable thermodynamic data about the material being studied. These isotherms are useful for predicting the final moisture content of a product at the end of drying,8−11 designing and optimizing drying equipment, forecasting shelf life stability, and calculating moisture changes that can take place during storage.10 Furthermore, they can also be used to predict the storage stability of different construction materials.12 A moisture sorption isotherm can predict the moisture content of a material exposed to air at a certain relative humidity (RH) and temperature. It is dependent on the temperature, environmentalRH, and composition of the material.8,13 By explaining the correlation between the equilibrium moisture content and water activity, these isotherms can help in predicting the overall moisture content of a product.

Our previous study14 compared the use of three heat-treated ES powder qualities to build bioceramic materials. The results showed that bioceramic composites made with heat-treated ES at 600 °C significantly improved the mechanical strength of the composite, reaching up to 7 MPa at approximately 7.7% moisture content. We also found that using a calcined ES at 800 °C resulted in a reduced net isosteric heat value, indicating weaker water–solid interactions. Building on our previous study, which focused on CL composite materials containing heat-treated ES powder, we now shift our focus to the use of raw ESs. Our objective is to explore the drying kinetics and hygroscopic behavior of raw ES-based composites in their natural state, particularly in relation to their interaction with the external environment. This study further contributes to the development of sustainable solutions using natural ESs in composite materials. In this study, equilibrium isotherms are constructed to comprehend the equilibrium moisture content at various RH levels. The adsorption and desorption isotherms are systematically compared and fitted to establish models, offering insights into the hysteresis behavior and moisture interaction mechanisms within both the CL and the CL/ES powder biocomposite materials. The objective is to examine and contrast the drying kinetics of two materials: CL and CL loaded with 20 wt % ES powder. The experimental investigation aims to explore how reinforcing the CL matrix with ES powder affects the kinetic behavior and the moisture diffusion coefficient of the composites during drying. Additionally, the study sought to determine the sorption isotherms of the two materials under consideration along with their respective thermodynamic properties.

Materials and Methods

Raw Materials and Composite Preparation

CL was gathered in its natural state and then dried in an oven at 60 °C for 3 h. Afterward, it was finely ground using a Retsch RS 200 grinder operating at a rotation speed of 700 rpm for 2 min. The resulting powder was then sifted through a sieve with a diameter of 500 μm (Figure 1). This CL powder was then used to prepare the samples.

View of the used CL: (a) naturel dried CL, (b) grinding phase, (c) sieving phase, and (d) used CL powder.

The chicken ESs were gathered from restaurant garbage and cleaned with tap water to get rid of any dirt or contaminants. The ESs were then sun-dried for 24 h and then subjected to a 3 h drying process at 60 °C in an oven made by Binder GmbH in Germany. Subsequently, they were finely ground into a fine powder using a Retsch RS 200 grinder at a speed of 700 rpm for 6 min. The obtained ES powder was then sifted through a 1 mm sieve.

The obtained ES powder was used to prepare the samples. Two kinds of samples were prepared: CL mixed with water noted and clay reinforced with 20 wt % (by weight) of ES powder mixed with water noted (CL-20% ES). All samples had an average initial moisture content (w0) of approximately 0.4 kg/kg on a dry basis. Using a piston–cylinder mechanism, samples were produced by extruding them into cylindrical shapes. The created cylindrical samples had an initial 20 mm diameter and 10 mm height.

Raw Materials Characterization Using X-ray Diffraction Analysis

To examine the chemical structure of the CL and ES powder used, X-ray diffraction (XRD) examination was performed. A Bruker D8 ADVANCE diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα (λ = 1.541)) radiation was used for XRD analysis. Using Cu Kα radiation at 40 kV and 40 mA, calcined ESs at three different temperatures were scanned in the 2θ range from 20 to 70° at room temperature with a step size of 0.020°.

Experimental Method for Sorption Isotherm Determination

The sorption and desorption isotherms of CL and CL-20% ES materials were measured using a standard gravimetric method. The samples were placed inside a hermetic desiccator, and various concentrations of sulfuric acid solutions (Figure 3) were added to achieve RH levels ranging from 5 to 100%.14,15 The RH of the samples was controlled with an accuracy of 0.1%. The desiccators were kept at constant temperatures of 30, 40, 50, 60, and 70 °C.

Experimental apparatus used for measuring different samples of CL desorption/adsorption isotherms: static gravimetric method.

The process of obtaining an experimental isotherm point involves placing a sample of the product (CL/ES powder composite) inside a desiccator containing sulfuric acid solution and maintaining at a constant temperature (T). The desiccator needs to be briefly opened daily to measure the weight of the sample. The sample is weighed daily until its mass no longer changes, indicating that it has reached equilibrium with the surrounding atmosphere. Equilibrium was considered achieved when a constant weight was obtained (±0.001 g). Once equilibrium is reached, the sample is exposed to the next temperature.

The time required for the samples to reach weight equilibrium varies depending on the temperature and water activity (aw), ranging from 4 to 7 days.

Knowing its equilibrium mass, we then determined its dry mass to calculate its equilibrium moisture content. The pair (RH, weq) provides a point on the sorption isotherm. The dry mass was obtained by drying the CL/ES powder biocomposite material in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h. More details about this method are provided in our previous research (Hachem et al. 2024).14

Results and Discussion

Chemical Characterization of Raw Materials Using X-ray Diffraction Analysis

The comparison of the XRD patterns of CL and ES powder (Figure 4) reveals significant differences in their mineralogical composition and crystalline characteristics. Natural CL contains various silicate mineral phases such as kaolinite, illite, and montmorillonite, often with impurities like quartz and calcite. XRD patterns of CL display diverse peaks at characteristic angles corresponding to these minerals, typically with broader and less intense peak profiles. In contrast, ES powder is mainly composed of calcium carbonate in the form of calcite. The XRD patterns of ES powder show well-defined peaks at specific angles indicative of the crystalline structures of calcium carbonate. This difference in mineralogical composition results in distinct physical properties, which can influence their potential uses in construction.

Drying Kinetics

Figure 5 presents the temporal evolution of the dimensionless moisture content of CL and CL-20% ES composites during the IR drying at four different temperatures (60, 70, 80, and 90 °C). During the drying process, the MR decreases gradually vs time. The same evolution was observed for all the prepared composites at different drying air temperature: a sharply falling kinetics slope is seen at the start of the drying process; then the slope becomes smoother in the succeeding phases. As time goes on, the product’s moisture content decreases and moisture diffusion slows, reducing the drying rate in the biocomposite layers and lengthening the drying time in the final drying stages.25 At the same drying temperature of 90 °C, the MR decreases from 1 to 0.077 in 40 min for natural CL. However, it decreases from 1 to 0.05 in 25 min for CL-20% ES. It is clear that the addition of ES inside the composite decreases the drying time.

Evolution of moisture content vs time of prepared composites: (a) CL and (b) CL-20% ES.

After complete drying, the MR curve reaches a constant level, indicating that the moisture content of the mixture remains relatively constant unless it is exposed to higher humidity conditions. Moreover, it is clear that the temperature of the drying process has a significant influence on the drying time. The comparison of curves shows that the drying time decreases with increasing drying temperature.

The data presented in Figure 6 clearly show that the relationship between Ln(MR) and time is nonlinear. This suggests that the drying process does not follow a simple diffusion mechanism as indicated by the classical second Fick’s law. Although the effective diffusion model is commonly used due to its simplicity, the nonlinearity observed in the data suggests that it may not be the best choice for these systems. The deviations from linearity indicate that other factors, such the dependence of the diffusion coefficients on the moisture content, the shrinkage of sample during drying, or additional mass transfer resistances, are likely to have significant effects on the drying behavior of the natural CL/ES powder composites. Therefore, a more complex model that takes these factors into account may be necessary to accurately describe the drying kinetics in these systems.

In Figure 7, the IR drying kinetics of the product samples are illustrated across temperatures ranging from 60 to 90 °C. Initially, a pronounced decline in the kinetics slope is evident during the early stages of the drying process, followed by a gradual smoothing in subsequent stages.26 This phenomenon arises from a heightened moisture diffusion at the onset of drying. As time progresses, the moisture content diminishes, resulting in reduced moisture diffusion and, consequently, a decrease in drying rate within the biocomposite layers. This prolonged drying time characterizes the final stages of the IR drying. Furthermore, the IR drying process exhibits patterns analogous to those of the three classical phases commonly documented in the literature.

Evolution of drying rate vs moisture content of (a) CL and (b) CL-20% ES.

The drying process of biocomposite materials typically involves multiple stages, each influenced by different factors. The initial stage is characterized by an increasing drying rate, which is due to the ample availability of free water on the material’s surface. As the drying progresses, a constant rate phase is observed, where the removal of water happens at a steady pace, indicating a balance between the heat provided and the moisture evaporated. However, once the surface water is depleted, the drying enters a falling rate stage, where moisture must diffuse from within the material to the surface, thus slowing the process.

IR drying techniques initially lead to a rapid evaporation of water due to the direct absorption of IR energy by the water molecules. This process is highly efficient at the start but gradually reduces as the moisture content decreases.

From the analysis of Figure 7, the impact of the drying air temperature on the drying rate is evident. Higher temperatures facilitate a quicker transition through the drying phases by increasing the energy available for evaporation, thus reducing or even eliminating the constant speed phase. This suggests that at elevated temperatures, the drying process is more efficient, leading to a shorter overall drying time.

The ES content within the composites also plays a role in the drying dynamics. The presence of ES likely influences the material’s porosity and thermal conductivity, thereby affecting the rate at which moisture is removed. However, the effect of the ES content is less pronounced compared with the impact of higher temperatures, particularly above 60 °C, where the thermal energy significantly dominates the drying behavior.

In conclusion, optimizing the drying conditions for biocomposite materials involves carefully balancing temperature and material composition to achieve the desired drying efficiency. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for improving industrial drying processes, reducing energy consumption, and enhancing the quality of the final product.

Experimental results for the equilibrium moisture content of CL and CL-20% ES materials at different water activity levels (aw) and temperatures are presented in Figure 8 for both adsorption and desorption. Comparison of experimental adsorption and desorption equilibrium moisture contents in CL and CL-20% ES materials at varied temperatures.

The relationship between the temperature and equilibrium moisture content is well-known. Our study of CL and CL-20% ES materials supports this idea. We observed differences in the desorption and adsorption behaviors of these materials, but they exhibited similar trends in the water activity range from 0 to 0.8, with the curves at several temperatures overlapping. However, at water activity values above 0.8, the equilibrium moisture content of both materials increased as the temperature rose. These findings are in line with previous research.16

Nevertheless, García-Perez et al.8 recommended that an optimal sorption model should possess parameters with both physical significance and sound statistical indices. The GAB model has found extensive application in elucidating equilibrium moisture content in many products and earned endorsement from the European Project COST90.8 A strong alignment between the experimental data and the equilibrium moisture contents was projected by the GAB model for adsorption and desorption. The GAB model parameters offer valuable insights into the thermodynamics of moisture desorption and adsorption in these CL samples.

The GAB model is widely used to describe the moisture sorption isotherms of the materials. This model includes three parameters: Xm, C, and K. Each of these parameters has physical significance. Xm (monolayer moisture content) represents the amount of moisture content at which a single monolayer of water molecules covers the entire surface of the material. It is often considered the most critical parameter because it is related to the material’s stability and the point at which water molecules are strongly bound to the surface. C (Guggenheim constant) is related to the energy of the interaction between the adsorbent (material) and the first layer of water molecules. A higher C value indicates a stronger binding energy between the material and water molecules in the monolayer. In addition, the parameter K (correction factor for multilayer molecules) accounts for the interactions between water molecules in the multilayer beyond the monolayer. It reflects how easily water molecules can form subsequent layers after the monolayer is complete.

The GAB model simplifies the moisture sorption process, but the actual interactions between water molecules and the material can be more intricate. Factors such as the pore size distribution, surface roughness, and presence of functional groups can all affect the sorption behavior. In summary, while the parameters of the GAB model Xm, C, and K have clear theoretical meanings related to the monolayer moisture content, binding energy, and multilayer formation, their physical interpretations can be obscured by the complexities of real-world materials and experimental conditions. This makes it challenging to straightforwardly and unambiguously interpret these parameters in practice.

A crucial criterion for the GAB model is that when parameter K is close to 1, the model produces a sigmoidal-shaped curve. This study validates the earlier prediction by consistently observing that the value of K closely aligns with that of 1 across all temperatures. Moreover, the values of GAB constants, Xm and C, decreased gradually with increasing temperature. This decline in monolayer moisture content for desorption can be attributed to structural changes occurring at elevated temperatures.

The Adam and Shove model is based only on empirical data. In contrast, the GAB model provides a physical interpretation of the sorption/desorption process. The Oswin and Modified Henderson models are less accurate than the GAB model due to their simplifications and underlying assumptions. Their fitting criteria did not meet acceptable standards, so they are not included in Tables 4 and 5. The GAB model is more reliable in many situations because it is based on a better thermodynamic understanding of moisture sorption. It considers multilayer sorption, which makes it capable of describing the complexity of interactions between moisture and the material. However, the GAB model may be more complex to use as it requires more adjustable parameters.

Net Isosteric Enthalpy and Differential Entropy of Desorption/Adsorption

In Figure 9, the net isosteric enthalpy of desorption and adsorption is shown for CL and CL-20% ES samples as a function of the moisture content. These parameters are strongly influenced by the moisture content. The net isosteric enthalpy increases at low moisture content and decreases as the moisture content rises, indicating a decline in water interactions. This decline happens rapidly as active sites become saturated, leading to lower heats of sorption.

Variation of net isosteric enthalpy of (a)desorption and (b) adsorption with moisture content of CL and CL-20% ES materials.

During desorption, the net isosteric enthalpy values are 49.33 kJ/mol for CL and 16.06 kJ/mol for CL-20% ES at the same moisture content of 0.0125 kg/kg d.b. The decrease in the net isosteric enthalpy during adsorption on natural CL can be attributed to several factors. The net isosteric enthalpy measures the interaction between adsorbed molecules and the adsorbent surface. As the first layer becomes saturated with water, the enthalpy decreases, leading to subsequent adsorption on less active sites. Natural CL’s structural changes during adsorption can modify the surface characteristics and the energy landscape, affecting the net isosteric enthalpy. The nature and strength of the binding between adsorbed molecules and the CL surface can influence the net isosteric enthalpy. Variations in temperature and pressure can also affect net isosteric enthalpy, with higher temperatures leading to lower enthalpy values as adsorption becomes less exothermic.

During adsorption, the net isosteric enthalpy values are about 18.3 kJ/mol for CL and 10.58 kJ/mol for CL-20% ES at a water content of 0.0115 kg/kg d.b. The decrease in net isosteric enthalpy for the CL-20% ES biocomposite material compared to that for natural CL is due to the properties introduced by the ES powder. The addition of ES powder may reduce available adsorption sites as it could occupy potential sites and decrease the number of sites available for water adsorption in the composite. Incorporating ESs changes the porous structure, making water interactions less efficient and reducing the overall enthalpy. The ES powder also acts as a physical barrier, hindering water molecules from accessing high-energy adsorption sites within the CL matrix.

For the CL material, at lower moisture contents (w = 0.0125 kg/kg d.b), the net isosteric enthalpy of desorption (49.33 kJ/mol) is higher than during adsorption (16.19 kJ/mol), indicating that desorption requires more energy. In contrast, for the CL-20% ES material, the net isosteric enthalpy during desorption (16.06 kJ/mol) is only slightly higher than that during adsorption (9.09 kJ/mol). This small difference likely results from the ES powder modifying the adsorption/desorption dynamics.

Figure 10 presents the net isosteric entropy (ΔSis) of the desorption and adsorption processes as a function of the moisture content for CL and CL-20% ES samples. The evolution of (ΔSis) with respect to the moisture content follows a similar trend to that of the isosteric sorption enthalpy (ΔHis) notably increasing at very low moisture contents.

Variation of the net isosteric entropy of (a) desorption and (b) adsorption with moisture content of CL and CL-20% ES materials.

Specifically, during desorption, the net isosteric entropy (ΔSis) for the CL material was 123.34 J/kg·mol, while for CL-20% ES materials, it was 35.07 J/kg·mol at the same low moisture content (w = 0.013 kg/kg d.b). It was observed that the net isosteric entropy is lower for the CL-20% ES material. The lower net isosteric entropy in CL-20% ES materials compared to that in pure CL suggests that the addition of ES powder may restrict the degree of freedom of water molecules or induce more ordered arrangements within the composite. This reduced entropy implies a more constrained or ordered state of the water molecules within the composite, which could result from specific interactions between the components of the composite material.

Nevertheless, during adsorption, ΔSis for the CL material was 31.48 J/kg·mol, while for CL-20% ES materials, it was 20.18 J/kg·mol at the same low moisture content (w = 0.013 kg/kg d.b).

For the CL material, the significant decrease in the ΔSis value from desorption to adsorption can be explained by differences in the states of adsorption and desorption. During desorption, molecules released from the CL surface have greater freedom of movement and orientation, leading to increased entropy. In contrast, during adsorption, molecules are attracted and bound to the CL surface, reducing their freedom of movement and orientation and resulting in decreased entropy. Changes in the distribution of molecules and molecular interactions with the CL surface also contribute to this entropy difference. The maximum value of entropy indicates the highest water mobility, which could be attributed to the strongest interactions between water molecules and the adsorbent surface containing the most active sorption sites.20 At low water content, binding is primarily governed by the interaction between water molecules and the adsorbent surface. This trend has been explained by the differences in attractive forces between water molecules and sorption sites as well as between water molecules themselves.21

Nevertheless, the similarity in the net isosteric entropy between adsorption and desorption in the CL/ES powder (CL-20% ES) biocomposite material can be explained by the balanced interactions and possible compensation effects between CL and ES powder, along with the specific nature of adsorbed molecules and potential synergistic effects, leading to minimal differences in the entropy values between adsorption and desorption. This observation depends on the unique characteristics of the mixture, the adsorbed molecules, and the experimental conditions.24

Enthalpy–Entropy Compensation Theory

The enthalpy–entropy compensation theory, or isokinetic relationship, has been widely investigated for many different physical and chemical processes. Compensation arises from changes like the interaction between the solute and solvent causing the reaction and that the relationship between enthalpy and entropy for a specific reaction is linear. Physical and chemical phenomena that can be used to describe the molecular interaction of water and to understand the organization or order of the system (related to enthalpy) and the disorganization of these molecules (related to entropy) are described by the compensation theory, and in this way, it allows the evaluation of the reduction in the freedom of water molecules in products.

The enthalpy–entropy compensation is used in thermodynamics to describe a phenomenon where changes in enthalpy (ΔH) and entropy (ΔS) are interconnected, often in a way that compensates for each other. In other words, when one of these thermodynamic parameters changes, the other tends to change in such a manner that the overall free energy change (ΔG) remains relatively constant. This phenomenon is often observed in chemical reactions, molecular interactions, or physical processes. This compensation displays the evolutions of ΔHis and ΔSis of the CL and CL-20% ES samples. Conclusions

The current experimental investigation primarily delves into two aspects. First, it explores the drying kinetics at different drying air temperatures of two materials: natural CL and natural CL reinforced with 20 wt % ES powder (CL-20% ES). The study involves the assessment of effective moisture diffusion coefficients of both materials under different conditions.

Second, the study entails the experimental determination of desorption/adsorption isotherms for both studied materials, namely, CL and CL-20% ES, across different temperature ranges. Subsequently, the data are smoothed using the GAB model, which yielded satisfactory results overall. The selection of the GAB model was based on its efficacy in accurately describing the experimental desorption and adsorption data with minimal standard error. Furthermore, the GAB model was utilized to estimate isotherm heats and entropies, providing valuable insights into the mechanism of binding of moisture to the material.

The results demonstrate that the effective coefficient of moisture diffusivity increases with higher drying temperatures and the presence of an ES in the CL matrix. It can be suggested that the reduction in the net isosteric enthalpy value during adsorption compared to desorption stems from a decrease in the energy of interaction between the adsorbed molecules and the surface.

Further analysis revealed a decrease in net isosteric entropy in the CL-20% ES material compared to that in pure CL, suggesting that the incorporation of ES powder may limit the mobility of water molecules or induce a more structured arrangement within the composite.

This investigation into the thermodynamics of moisture desorption/adsorption on CL-based materials is particularly fascinating as it enhances the understanding of the thermodynamic behavior exhibited by these CL-based materials, especially CL loaded with ES powder. Overall, this research not only expands our fundamental understanding of CL-based materials but also paves the way for the development of innovative applications with practical implications in various industries.
