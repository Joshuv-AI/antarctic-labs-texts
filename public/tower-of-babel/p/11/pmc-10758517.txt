Global Dialysis Perspective: Ghana
Authors: Elliot Koranteng Tannor, Sampson Antwi
Published: 2023

Introduction and Country Profile

Ghana is a lower-middle income country with a population of 30.8 million people according to the latest Population and Housing Census.1 It is located in West Africa bounded by Burkina Faso (north), Togo (east), Côte d'Ivoire (west), and the Gulf of Guinea (south) and covers a total surface area of 238,533 km2. The urban population in Ghana is currently 58% surpassing the rural population from 2009. The average life expectancy is 66 years for female and 61.6 years for male with a death rate of 7.6 per 1000 inhabitants per year.2 Economically, it has a gross domestic product (GDP) of US 77.59 billion and a GDP per capita of US$ 2445.39 according the World bank.2 The daily minimum wage is 13.53 Ghana Cedis (US$ 2.06) or 365.31 (US$ 55.7) per month.3 Ghana spent 7.6% of her annual budget and 1.38% of her GDP on health care in 2022, which is less than the recommended 15% of the annual national budget recommended by the Abuja Declaration.4 Ghana has a National Health Insurance Scheme, which does not reimburse for KRT; hence, most patients pay out-of-pocket leading to inadequate care with increased mortality and poor quality of life.5,6

Prevalence of CKD

The incidence of CKD in Ghana is unknown but has a prevalence of 13.3%.7 Chronic glomerulonephritis, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension are the most common causes of CKD in Ghana.5 According to the first Ghana renal registry published in 2022,8 the median age of patients undergoing KRT was 45.5 years.8 Patients in Ghana develop kidney failure (KF) at a relatively younger age and mostly present late with advanced disease in over 75% of cases with up to 50% in-hospital mortality for patients admitted with KF in a single-center retrospective study due to inability to pay for KRT.9

History of KRT in Ghana

Hemodialysis was initiated in Ghana as the first KRT modality in 1972 at the Korle bu Teaching Hospital. The number of hemodialysis centers has increased over the years from six centers (three public and three private) in 201510 to 40 functional units currently.11 There is inequitable distribution of dialysis centers in Ghana.12 Most patients with KF are on hemodialysis (96.2%), functioning kidney transplant (3.5%), and 0.3% on continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (PD) according to the first renal registry published in 2021.8 Acute PD was initiated in Ghana in 2012 by Antwi et al.10 and mostly used in pediatric population in the teaching hospitals and some district hospital but not for chronic dialysis in both adults and children in Ghana.

Dialysis for AKI

AKI accounted for 5.05% of medical admissions13 and 24.9% of patients with kidney diseases in hospital admissions in a single-center study.14 Owing to late reporting with advanced AKI, hemodialysis is required in over 40% of cases but only 14.5% were able to undergo acute dialysis due to cost, and this was associated with in-hospital mortality of 51.3%.14 There is a documented policy for the provision of hemodialysis for AKI to be covered by the National Health Insurance Scheme,10 but practically, patients pay out-of-pocket as it is not reimbursed similar to those with KF on chronic hemodialysis.

Maintenance Hemodialysis

There is still inequitable distribution of hemodialysis centers in Ghana, although there has been an increase from three of the ten regions in 201510 to nine of the now 16 regions with dialysis centers in Ghana (Figure 1). This leaves seven regions in Ghana inhabited by 5,702,791 (18.5%) of the population without any form of KRT. This is due to lack of resources or political will to set up more public dialysis centers, and private facilities are mostly set up in urban area to maximize their profit. Currently, there are 299 hemodialysis machines in Ghana with 9.7 dialysis machines per million population (pmp) in our latest survey.11 The capital city, Accra, has the highest number of dialysis machines with 50.8 dialysis machines pmp with the least being western region with 0.97 pmp11 (Figure 1).

From 1972, there has been a gradual increase in dialysis centers in both private and public centers. There are now more dialysis centers in the private centers (n=27) than in public centers (n=13), and there are more patients in the private centers (609) as compared with public centers (586).11 The cost of dialysis is higher in the private than the public centers. Although the cost of dialysis in US Dollars may be relatively low in Ghana, cost is still prohibitory with the majority only able to afford twice a week dialysis schedule instead of the recommended three times a week. In a single-center study in Ghana, only 14.5% of patients requiring dialysis were able to afford it leading to high mortality of 32.9%–45.6%.6,14 This affects significantly the disposable income in most families with untoward financial hardships for families with patients with KF.15 The average cost of a session of dialysis is US $53.9, which is averagely subsidized by about US$ 8 in public centers and variable amount in some private centers, yet most patients in Ghana are unable to afford. Therefore, there are challenges in sustaining dialysis centers by most private centers and lack of interest to invest more into hemodialysis in public centers.

Peritoneal Dialysis

PD is the least used KRT modality in Ghana accounting for 0.3% of patients on KRT in adults.8 PD is mostly used for acute dialysis in pediatric patients but not for chronic dialysis in both adults and children.10 The high cost of PD fluid for chronic PD has been the challenge, although there is local expertise among nephrologists to run the PD program.

Maintenance Dialysis—Current Practice and Outcomes

The duration of intermittent hemodialysis in most dialysis units is 4 hours. In some cases, where patients cannot tolerate the 4-hour sessions shorter durations are prescribed. Frequency of dialysis is reduced in most centers from three times a week to twice a week and even once a week. Most patients on chronic hemodialysis for less than 3 months use temporary catheters as vascular access (85.2%) and tunneled vascular access (14.8%). This is because most patients report late with complications and have to be started urgently on dialysis with temporary catheters. For those on hemodialysis for over a year, 12% had temporary catheters, 52% had arteriovenous fistula, and 36% had tunneled catheters16 (Table 1).

The outcomes of patients with KF has not been recently studied, but a previous study in 2009 showed a 90-day mortality of 35.9% after dialysis initiation as about half could only afford 20 sessions before discontinuation. Cardiovascular disease and sepsis are the most common causes of death among dialysis patient.6

Kidney Transplantation in Ghana

Kidney transplantation began in Ghana in 2008 at the Korle Bu teaching hospital, led by Osafo et al. with over 20 patients with KF having undergone kidney transplant till date.17 Unfortunately, most patients still travel outside the country for their kidney transplantation as we do not have a sustainable kidney transplant program to meet their transplant needs as we still rely on external expertise for occasional kidney transplantation. We need to build local capacity for sustainable kidney transplant program.

The Nephrology Workforce

There are 15 trained nephrologists in Ghana with a nephrologist pmps of 0.44 nephrologist pmp. There has been an increase from 0.1 nephrologist pmp in 2010 and 0.26 pmp in 2018 to the current 0.44 pmp.12 This steady increase is accounted for in part by the establishment of local training centers by the national and subregional postgraduate training colleges: the Ghana College of Physicians and the West African College of Physicians. It takes averagely 6 years after medical school training to pass out as a nephrologist in Ghana. Nephrology training is relatively young about two fellows pass out per year on the average. Trainees have however increased recently in both adult and pediatric nephrology in the two training centers in Ghana, and they get basic training in KRT management. Non-nephrologists get some basic training in KRT as medical student and through regular continuous medical education. Despite this steady increase, the situation still leaves 57.5% of dialysis centers without an attending or locum nephrologist leading to poor outcomes among patients on hemodialysis.11 There are postgraduate nephrology training opportunities for nurses in Ghana.

Challenges of Dialysis in Ghana

Kidney care in Ghana is fraught with many challenges ranging from low health literacy rate and poor screening of high-risk patients to challenges in the management of patients with KF. These challenges are because of weak heath systems, poverty, less attention to noncommunicable diseases such as CKD, lack of sustainable preventive programs, poor and late identification of kidney disease, low nephrology workforce, low transplant rates, and lack of comprehensive up-to-date renal registries.18 The use of alternative medicines has been suggested as causes of AKI and CKD lead to progression to KF and also account for late reporting of patients for care. Future Strategies

There has been a lot of work done by the Ghana Kidney Association to increase awareness through World Kidney Day activities as well as annual continuous medical education of non-nephrologists. There need to be more advocacy and training nationally and regionally in conjunction with the African Association of nephrology to increase nephrology workforce to meet the demands of increasing burden of kidney disease in Ghana. Public–private partnership, efforts of nongovernmental organizations, and more funding opportunities for infrastructure and training should be sought to bridge the gap for better kidney care in Ghana.18
