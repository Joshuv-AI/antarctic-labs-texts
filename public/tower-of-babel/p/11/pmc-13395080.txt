Introduction

Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) has become an important therapeutic option for thoracic aortic aneurysms and aortic dissection. However, several serious complications have been reported following this procedure. Among them, aorto-oesophageal fistula (AEF) is a rare but catastrophic complication, with a reported incidence of 1.5%–2.1%.1 Conservative treatment is associated with extremely high mortality, whereas long-term survival generally requires surgical intervention.

We herein report a case of AEF that developed nine years after TEVAR and was confirmed by autopsy. The prolonged interval between TEVAR and AEF onset makes this case particularly rare. This case underscores the importance of considering AEF as a potential diagnosis in patients with a history of TEVAR, regardless of the time elapsed since the procedure.

Case report

An elderly man in his 80 s presented with worsening dyspnoea. His medical history included chronic heart failure due to non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy, atrial fibrillation, and rheumatoid arthritis, for which he had been receiving long-term corticosteroids and immunosuppressive therapy. Nine years earlier, he had undergone TEVAR for a thoracic aortic aneurysm.

On admission, his body temperature was 38.3°C, heart rate was 124 beats per minute with an irregular rhythm, blood pressure was 115/76 mmHg, and oxygen saturation was 94% while receiving oxygen at 3 l/min via a mask. Physical examination revealed jugular venous distention, bilateral coarse crackles on lung auscultation, and bilateral pitting oedema of the lower extremities. Cardiac auscultation revealed no audible murmurs.

Laboratory testing showed mild anaemia (haemoglobin 10.7 g/dl; reference range, 13.7–16.8 g/dl), elevated C-reactive protein (2.21 mg/dl; reference ≤0.14 mg/dl), and markedly elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (1675 pg/ml; reference ≤18.4 pg/ml). Renal function was preserved. Chest radiography revealed cardiomegaly with bilateral pleural effusions, predominantly on the right side. Electrocardiography demonstrated atrial fibrillation with a ventricular rate of 133 beats per minute. Transthoracic echocardiography showed global left ventricular hypokinesis with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 26.2%, along with mild to moderate aortic and tricuspid regurgitation and moderate mitral regurgitation.

The patient was admitted for treatment of acute decompensated heart failure, and medical therapy including diuretics was initiated. On hospital Day 20, he developed persistent fever. Urinalysis showed pyuria, and urine culture later grew ceftriaxone-sensitive Proteus mirabilis. Antibiotic therapy with ceftriaxone was initiated; however, the fever persisted.

Computed tomography performed during hospitalization revealed air within the descending thoracic aortic aneurysm and loss of the fat plane between the aneurysm and the oesophagus, raising suspicion of an AEF (Figure 1).

Notably, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed two months before admission demonstrated a deep oesophageal ulcer at a site corresponding to pulsation from the adjacent aorta (Figure 3). The oesophageal ulcer was considered atypical in appearance, and external compression from the adjacent aortic stent graft was suspected. However, the patient was asymptomatic, and no therapeutic intervention was deemed feasible. Therefore, conservative management with careful observation was selected. Importantly, the ulcer was not recognized as a precursor of AEF at that time.

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy findings. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed 2 months before admission demonstrated a deep oesophageal ulcer at a site corresponding to pulsation from the adjacent aorta, suggesting close contact between the oesophagus and the aorta.

Because of his advanced age and comorbidities, conservative management was selected; however, the patient died on hospital Day 41.

An autopsy was performed with the consent of the patient’s family. Gross examination revealed a fistulous connection between the thoracic aorta and the oesophagus, confirming the diagnosis of AEF (Figure 4A and B). Histopathological examination showed localized inflammatory cell infiltration in the oesophageal wall surrounding the fistula (Figure 5). No findings suggestive of an infectious aortic aneurysm were observed.

Discussion

The classical presentation of AEF has been described as Chiari’s triad: midthoracic pain, sentinel haematemesis, and fatal exsanguination.2 However, this triad is observed in only a minority of cases, and the clinical presentation is often non-specific. Secondary AEF most commonly occurs in association with thoracic aortic aneurysm or prior aortic surgery.3,4

Aorto-oesophageal fistula after TEVAR is rare but carries a high mortality rate.4 Previous studies have demonstrated that conservative management of AEF is associated with extremely poor outcomes, with 1-year mortality approaching 100%. In contrast, aggressive surgical strategies, including oesophagectomy and aortic replacement, have been associated with improved survival rates of approximately 40%–50%, although these approaches are highly invasive and carry substantial perioperative risk.1 In the present case, surgical or endovascular re-intervention was not pursued because of the patient’s advanced age, severe heart failure, immunosuppressed status, and overall frailty, which were considered to confer a prohibitively high operative risk. In addition, the patient had no massive gastrointestinal bleeding at the time AEF was suspected. Therefore, after multidisciplinary discussion, conservative management with broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy was selected, despite the expected poor prognosis.

The incidence of AEF after TEVAR has been reported to be 1.5% to 2.1%, with a mean interval from TEVAR to diagnosis of approximately 195 days.1 The present case, occurring nine years after TEVAR, represents an extremely late presentation compared with previously reported cases.

The exact mechanisms underlying AEF development after TEVAR remain unclear. Proposed mechanisms include chronic mechanical irritation from the stent graft, ischaemic injury to the aortic or oesophageal wall, and infection.1,3–5 In the present case, no endoleak was observed after TEVAR, and autopsy findings did not support the presence of an infectious aortic aneurysm. In addition, a deep oesophageal ulcer corresponding to pulsatile movement had been documented prior to diagnosis. Previous reports have suggested that elevated pressure in the posterior mediastinum and chronic radial force from the stent graft can cause secondary oesophageal ischaemia, leading to gradual fistula formation after TEVAR.5 In this context, chronic external compression from the stent graft may have compromised perfusion of the adjacent oesophageal wall, leading to mucosal vulnerability and ulcer formation.

Corticosteroids are known to impair wound healing by inhibiting fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, and angiogenesis, thereby reducing tissue resistance to chronic mechanical stress and delaying repair of micro-injuries.6 Immunosuppressed status has also been reported as a potential contributing factor in AEF development.7 Although blood cultures were not obtained in the present case, the clinical course and histopathological findings suggested that infection was unlikely to be the primary mechanism of fistula formation. In the present case, long-term corticosteroid use may have contributed to tissue fragility, potentially facilitating oesophageal ulcer formation and subsequent fistula development in the setting of chronic mechanical stress. Taken together, the presence of a pre-existing oesophageal ulcer, the imaging findings, and the autopsy results suggest that AEF in the present case developed as a chronic evolving process rather than an acute event during hospitalization.

At the time the oesophageal ulcer was identified, external compression by the adjacent stent graft was suspected; however, the patient was asymptomatic and no therapeutic intervention was considered feasible. No scheduled follow-up endoscopy or imaging had been arranged before the present admission. In retrospect, additional evaluation with CT angiography may have been warranted given the unusual appearance of the ulcer and its suspected relationship to the stent graft.

In the present case, AEF was not initially suspected because of the very long interval since TEVAR and the non-specific nature of the presenting symptoms. Persistent fever was initially attributed to a urinary tract infection, and rheumatoid disease flare was also considered; however, no worsening of joint symptoms was observed. Blood cultures were not obtained at that time, which represents a limitation. As the clinical response to antibiotic therapy was suboptimal, further evaluation with contrast-enhanced CT was performed, leading to the diagnosis of AEF.

From a clinical perspective, AEF should be suspected in patients with a history of TEVAR who present with unexplained fever, systemic inflammation, or gastrointestinal symptoms. In particular, the combination of an oesophageal ulcer adjacent to the stent graft and unexplained fever or systemic inflammation should raise strong suspicion for AEF and prompt urgent CT angiography rather than observation alone.

Given the poor prognosis associated with conservative management, early recognition is crucial, and the possibility of surgical or endovascular intervention should be considered whenever feasible. There is currently no established surveillance strategy; therefore, clinical suspicion plays a key role in diagnosis.

This case highlights that AEF can occur even many years after TEVAR and may initially present with non-specific symptoms such as persistent fever. Clinicians should consider this rare but fatal complication in patients with a history of TEVAR who present with unexplained inflammatory findings.
