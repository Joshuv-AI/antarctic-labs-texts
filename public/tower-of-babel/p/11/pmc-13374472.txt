Communicating with families during armed conflict: when healthcare systems fracture but phones remain
Authors: Ripudaman Singh Minhas, Iryna Dvoretska, Assel Al-Bayati, Saisujani Rasiah-Shaidev
Published: 2026

Armed conflict disrupts nearly every system that protects children’s health. Hospitals may be damaged or inaccessible, healthcare workers displaced, schools closed and supply chains interrupted. Families may be forced to flee their homes or navigate prolonged instability while coping with grief, trauma and uncertainty. Yet even in fragile and conflict-affected settings, one infrastructure often persists: mobile connectivity. Across humanitarian crises, families increasingly turn to digital platforms to seek guidance about their children’s health, development and emotional well-being.1 In these contexts, communication itself becomes a form of care and a key navigational tool.2

It is well-established that armed conflict has both immediate and long-term impacts on children’s physical, mental and developmental health, with effects extending across generations.36 While humanitarian responses often prioritise food, shelter and medical services, less attention has been paid to how families access timely, trustworthy information to care for their children when systems are fractured.2 3 Displaced families frequently rely on social media (eg, Facebook, Telegram chats) to navigate resettlement, access social services and understand supports and resources.1 7

This article asserts that communicating with families during armed conflict is an essential component of paediatric care. Trusted, culturally relevant digital communication—particularly through social media—can support families when traditional pathways fail. However, this approach also carries ethical and practical considerations that must be addressed.

What families need when systems fail

In conflict settings, the questions families ask are immediate, practical and deeply relational. They extend beyond biomedical concerns to include survival, parenting and child development under extreme stress.

Through the Our Kids’ Health Network8—a multilingual, community-centred digital health initiative that co-develops, translates, adapts and disseminates evidence-based child health information in partnership with healthcare providers and community organisations, reaching over 100 million people in 2025—we have engaged with families affected by conflicts in regions such as Gaza, Ukraine and Venezuela. Content available through the Our Kids’ Health Network is translated and culturally adapted for each community through partnerships with community organisations and healthcare professionals, and is updated in response to evolving evidence and changing community needs. This initiative is also informed by questions submitted by parents and caregivers who have asked urgent questions such as: ‘How long should I boil stream water to make it safe for my child?’, ‘How do I support my child after we have lost a loved one?’, ‘How can I help my child continue learning when schools are closed?’ and ‘How do I talk to my children when they see me crying?’. These questions, summarised across key domains, reflect the multidimensional impact of armed conflict on children’s lives. Caregivers must simultaneously manage basic survival needs, support children’s emotional well-being and maintain developmental routines, often while experiencing their own trauma and loss. Many of these needs fall outside the reach of formal healthcare systems, particularly when infrastructure is damaged or access is limited.3

When communication pathways are disrupted

In stable contexts, families access paediatric guidance through primary care, schools, public health messaging and community services. Armed conflict disrupts these pathways. Health facilities may be destroyed or inaccessible, clinicians displaced, citizens in lockdown or in shelter and transportation systems unreliable. Families who are displaced or resettled may encounter additional barriers, including language discordance, unfamiliar health systems and structural inequities.3

At the same time, information ecosystems shift rapidly. Families increasingly rely on informal digital networks, including messaging applications, social media platforms and diaspora communities, to seek guidance.1 7 9 These spaces can support connection and information sharing but are also susceptible to misinformation, particularly in crisis settings, where uncertainty is high, and access to trusted sources is limited.9

The absence of accessible, culturally relevant communication from health systems creates a vacuum that is often filled by unverified information or awkwardly auto-translated content.2 9 This presents a critical opportunity—and responsibility—for paediatric professionals and advocacy groups to engage more intentionally in digital communication.

Social media as a frontline communication tool

Digital platforms offer several advantages in conflict settings.1 They enable rapid dissemination of information at scale, often rapidly reaching families who cannot access traditional services.1 They allow for multilingual, visually accessible content that can overcome literacy, language and geographic barriers.1 10 Importantly, they can leverage trusted community messengers who share cultural and linguistic connections with families.7 10

Community-centred digital health models, such as the Our Kids’ Health Network, demonstrate how social media can function as a frontline communication tool. By co-creating content with community partners and responding to real-time questions, such initiatives can provide practical, evidence-based guidance that is both culturally relevant and widely accessible.1 10

In this context, communication is not merely informational; it is relational and supportive. Guidance on topics such as supporting a grieving child or maintaining routines during displacement can help caregivers buffer the effects of toxic stress on young children.4 Given that early childhood development is highly sensitive to environmental stressors, such support is critical.4 5

However, while social media offers important opportunities, its use in paediatric care during conflict must be approached with intention and caution.

The limits and risks of digital communication

Several considerations must be acknowledged. First, digital access is not universal. Families experiencing extreme poverty or living in remote areas may lack reliable connectivity or be impacted by state-sanctioned censorship, raising concerns about equity.10

Second, misinformation and disinformation remain a significant risk.9 Social media platforms can amplify inaccurate or harmful health information, particularly in crisis contexts.1 9 Paediatric professionals engaging online must prioritise accuracy, transparency and clarity.

Third, privacy and safety concerns arise when families share sensitive information in digital spaces.10 Safeguards must be considered to protect confidentiality and minimise risk.

Fourth, digital communication cannot replace clinical care when children require medical assessment or intervention. Online guidance should therefore be framed as complementary to, rather than a substitute for, healthcare services.

Fifth, conflict settings raise important questions about trust.10 Families may receive information from governments, humanitarian organisations, community groups and informal online networks that vary in quality, trustworthiness and credibility. Digital health initiatives must therefore build trust through engagement with affected communities, culturally relevant communication and transparency regarding the sources of information being shared.10

Recognising these limitations is essential to ensuring that digital communication supports safe and effective paediatric practice.

These approaches align with child-rights frameworks that emphasise access to health information as a fundamental component of care.3

In situations of armed conflict, communication is not simply the transfer of information—it is an intervention that can support safety, emotional well-being, family cohesion and child development.4 5 When healthcare systems are disrupted, trusted communication may be one of the few tools available to reach and empower families.2 3

Social media platforms, despite their limitations, offer a scalable and accessible means of delivering guidance to caregivers navigating crises.1 The challenge for paediatric and humanitarian communities is not whether these platforms will be used, but how to engage with them responsibly and effectively. This may require cultivation of partnership with unlikely allies (such as social media companies themselves).

Investing in trusted, community-centred communication models is necessary to help ensure that families in crisis receive accurate, culturally relevant and compassionate support when they need it most.1 In conflict settings, the ability to communicate effectively with families may be as important as the ability to deliver care itself.
