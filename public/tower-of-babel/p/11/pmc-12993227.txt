First molecular detection and characterization of Cyclospora cayetanensis in an Italian clinical case
Authors: Marianna Marangi, Giorgia Lepore, Felice Valzano, Settimia Altamura, Daniela Pisanelli, Maria Rosaria Lipsi, Donato Lacedonia, Sonia Boughattas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the first molecularly confirmed, genetically sequenced, and phylogenetically traced case of locally acquired Cyclospora cayetanensis infection in Italy. Earlier Italian reports described clinical cases of cyclosporiasis. However, sequence-based molecular confirmation and phylogenetic traceability were not available. An 86-year-old Italian woman with no recent travel history developed diarrhea during hospitalization. Routine microbiological investigations were negative, whereas multiplex real-time PCR detected C. cayetanensis. The diagnosis was confirmed by amplification and Sanger sequencing of the 18S rRNA gene. Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that the Italian isolate clustered with strains associated with outbreaks linked to tropical and subtropical regions, suggesting a probable imported foodborne origin. Although cyclosporiasis is generally self-limiting, infection in frail elderly patients with multiple comorbidities may contribute to clinical deterioration. Diarrheal symptoms resolved after antimicrobial therapy, but the patient subsequently died due to pulmonary edema. While a direct causal relationship cannot be established, extra-intestinal involvement has been reported. This case highlights the importance of molecular diagnostics and phylogenetic analysis in non-endemic settings to improve surveillance and source attribution of C. cayetanensis infection.Keywords: Cyclospora cayetanensis, 18S gene characterization, Pulmonary edema, Italy

Introduction

Cyclospora cayetanensis (Phylum: Apicomplexa) is an intestinal coccidian protozoan that causes cyclosporiasis, a gastrointestinal illness in humans [1]. Symptoms typically include watery and malodorous diarrhea, bloating, abdominal cramps, nausea, headache, appetite and weight loss [2]. Although fatalities are rare, prolonged diarrhea can lead to dehydration and/or malnutrition, particularly dangerous for children [3].

Infection occurs through the faecal-oral route by ingesting sporulated oocysts in contaminated water or food, particularly fruits and vegetables [3], [4]. Person to person transmission is unlikely since C. cayetanensis oocysts are not immediately infectious upon excretion and require environmental maturation [1]. Incubation period generally ranges from a few days to two weeks, averaging one week in healthy individuals [2].

C. cayetanensis is globally distributed, with higher prevalence in tropical and subtropical regions with poor sanitation [3]. In high-income countries, outbreaks are usually linked to imported products or travelers returning from endemic areas [5]. Some individuals may remain asymptomatic [4], though children, expats, and immunocompromised people are more vulnerable.

As of now, 54 countries have reported C. cayetanensis cases, with 13 experiencing outbreaks [4]. Prevalence is significantly higher in low-income countries (7.6%) compared to high-income countries (0.4%) [6]. In Italy, a non-endemic country, only two clinical reports exist. The first, from 2006 to 2008, documented seven infections, five in Italian nationals, including three with no travel history [7]. The second involved a local immunocompetent woman with no recent travel [8]. However, these reports lacked molecular confirmation with gene sequencing and phylogenetic analysis, limiting epidemiological traceability and source attribution.

Diagnosis of C. cayetanensis remains challenging. Although microscopy has traditionally been considered the reference method, identification may be difficult due to the small size (7.5–10 μm), intracellular localization, and variable staining properties of the oocysts [2]. Moreover, C. cayetanensis may be often confused with Cryptosporidium due to morphologic and clinical similarities. PCR-based molecular techniques (e.g., Real-Time PCR) provide greater sensitivity and are increasingly used in diagnostics [9].

Case

On 24 May 2025, an 86-year-old Caucasian Italian women, housewife, non-smoker, with no recent travel experience abroad and any contact with people from endemic countries, was admitted to the Emergency Department (Policlinico Foggia, Foggia, Italy) for progressively worsening dyspnea of acute onset. On arrival, she exhibited markedly elevated blood pressure (BP 180/100 mmHg), an oxygen saturation of 95% while receiving 5 L/min of oxygen via nasal cannula, indicating underlying respiratory compromise, and a normal lactate level (0.9 mmol/L). The patient did not refer any allergy and had received three doses of SARS-CoV-2 vaccine (namely one dose of Astra Zeneca and two doses of Moderna) as well as vaccination against Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenza. The patient presented chronic renal failure, chronic heart failure, multifactorial anemia, insulin-dependent diabetes, dyslipidemia, ischemic-hypertensive heart disease, vascular dementia, cholecystectomy, hysterectomy, adnexa and thyroid surgery for goiter.

The Chest X-rays showed pulmonary consolidation in the right upper and middle lobes complicated by acute partial respiratory failure. Consequently, the woman was transferred to the Respiratory System Diseases Unit of the same hospital. Following an ab ingestis pneumonia suspect, blood cultures, a multiplex viral/bacterial throat swab, and urinary antigens for Legionella, Chlamydia and Mycoplasma were performed, with all results reported as negative. Sputum culture was not performed.

The patient benefited from antibiotic therapy (ceftobiprole 250 mg, twice daily for 15 days), bicarbonate integration (20 mEq/L twice daily) and diuretic therapy (furosemide 20 mg, three vials twice daily).

The patient was also administered oxygen therapy in nasocannulas (subsequently suspended), prophylactic enoxaparin for the prevention of deep and superficial vein thrombosis. Her usual home medications were continued, including haloperidol 50 mg (1/2 vial every 15 days), linagliptin, olmesartan medoxomil 40 mg (1 tablet daily), carvedilol 25 mg (1/2 tablet daily), barnidipine hydrochloride 10 mg (1 tablet daily), and epoetin alfa (once weekly).

Due to ischemic findings on electrocardiography performed on the day of admission, cardiology consultation was requested. Cardiologists identified inveterate alterations and excluded the need for invasive diagnostic procedures. They recommended transdermal nitrate therapy (5 mg/day) and two blood transfusions to adjust the anemia related to chronic kidney failure.

On 5 June 2025, due to the presence of diarrheic stools (about 3 evacuations per day), several diagnostic tests were performed. These included stool cultures for Salmonella spp., Shigella spp. Campylobacter spp. and Yersinia spp. (all negative), Clostridioides difficile GDH antigen detection by Chemiluminescence Immunoassay (negative), coproparasitological tests (negative), and the multiplex real-time PCR Allplex™ GI-Virus, GI-Bacteria and GI-Parasite panel (Seegene Inc. Seoul, Korea). The multiplex real-time PCR returned positive for C. cayetanensis, only. This finding was subsequently confirmed by end-point PCR targeting the 18S rRNA gene [9], followed by Sanger sequencing of the amplified gene (accession number PV803172).

Following infectious disease consultation, the patient was treated with ciprofloxacin 500 mg once daily (adjusted for eGFR) for 10 days [10], which led to resolution of the diarrheal symptoms. On 10 June 2025, after an initial improvement in her overall condition, the patient experienced a sudden worsening, with evolution into an irreversible comatose state (GCS 3) and a progressive oliguric state evolving to anuria, and subsequent death. Five days before the death, blood tests showed an increase in inflammation indices and neutrophilic leukocytosis (76.70%), with a C-reactive protein of 97.10 mg/L and procalcitonin of 0.42 ng/ml. The cause for death was attributed to pulmonary edema. A timeline with the evolution of the symptoms has been reported as Supplementary Figure 1 (S1).

Discussion

Here we reported a case of cyclosporiasis potentially acquired in Italy, in an autochthonous woman with no history of recent travel abroad, diagnosed by molecular methods. All other common enteric pathogens were ruled out and the presence of C. cayetanensis was confirmed by PCR amplification targeting the 18S rRNA gene plus sequencing. The obtained sequence was submitted to GenBank with Accession Number PV803172. This case highlights the value of molecular methods in the detection of parasitic infections, particularly when direct microscopy may yield inconclusive or unreliable results [11]. Given the clinical significance of this protozoan and the challenges associated with its diagnosis, correct identification and effective surveillance remain difficult. In the present case, the patient died due to pulmonary complications, but the parasitic infection may have exacerbated her pathologic condition. Indeed, while cyclosporiasis is typically self-limiting in immunocompetent patients, it can in some cases manifest as severe, protracted or chronic diarrhea [2], [3]. Moreover, Cyclospora can be disseminated beyond the gastrointestinal tract and colonize extra-intestinal sites [1], including the pulmonary tract. This is supported by reports in which C. cayetanensis oocysts were detected in sputum samples after acid-fast staining, with no other pathogens identified in neither the sputum nor laryngeal biopsy, suggesting the possibility of pulmonary colonization or infection [12].

The transmission of the parasite is mainly related to travel and via foodborne or waterborne contamination [3], [4]. In this clinical case, travel-borne transmission can be ruled out since our patient did not have a record of recent travelling. Given her prolonged hospital stay, which overlaps with the incubation period for C. cayetanensis, a nosocomial acquisition could be hypothesized. However, person-to-person transmission appears highly unlikely, as C. cayetanensis oocysts are not immediately infectious upon excretion and require a period of environmental maturation. Moreover, the few reported hospital-related cases of cyclosporiasis have been associated with drinking tap water potentially contaminated from rooftop reservoirs [13]. The absence of other cases within the same unit allows us to reasonably exclude this hypothesis. Therefore, foodborne or waterborne transmission seems to be the most plausible route of infection.

Several outbreaks due to imported fruit and vegetable from tropical region were reported in UK, USA and Canada [14]. To determine the genetic relationship of the Italian C. cayetanensis isolate with previously reported isolates and to investigate its possible origin, a phylogenetic analysis using IQ-TREE (Fig. 1) was carried out. Results showed that the Italian isolate groups with strains reported in North American outbreaks originated from Latin American and/or Asian countries, which are classified by the FAO as high- or medium-risk for infection [15], [16]. These findings support the hypothesis of an imported origin for the present Cyclospora case, which may be attributed to the recent increase in global trade of fresh products from these countries.

Reference Cyclospora lineage A (Sequence ID: RDRQ01), lineage B (Sequence ID: RDRG01), and lineage C (Sequence ID: PDMJ01) as well an outgroup of Cryptosporidium parvum (Sequence ID: AF093490.1) were included. Despite the clinical and molecular insights provided by this case, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single case report, the findings cannot be generalized to broader populations, and conclusions regarding transmission and clinical outcomes remain limited. The absence of sputum culture or pulmonary sampling prevents confirmation of potential extra-intestinal colonization by C. cayetanensis. Additionally, no environmental or food investigations were performed, limiting our ability to identify the precise source of infection or to exactly confirm nosocomial versus foodborne acquisition. The phylogenetic analysis was based on the 18S rRNA gene, which has limited discriminatory power and does not allow clear differentiation between C. cayetanensis lineages [17]. Indeed, although the 18S rRNA gene is widely used for species identification and broad phylogenetic placement of Cyclospora, its highly conserved nature limits its discriminatory power for fine-scale epidemiological investigations. Therefore, phylogenetic clustering based solely on this locus cannot reliably distinguish between recently imported infections and strains that are locally circulating. Future studies incorporating higher-resolution markers, such as MLST schemes, mitochondrial markers, microsatellites, or whole-genome sequencing, are warranted to improve source attribution and transmission tracking. Moreover, no comparative sequences from the Mediterranean region were available for inclusion, preventing a more comprehensive analysis, despite the protozoan has been previously detected in various food and environmental sources in Italy, including imported berries [18], Atlantic blue crab [19], ready-to-eat salads [20], and environmental samples as soil, mollusc wastewater and vegetables [21], [22] as well as from neighboring country within mollusk bivalves [23] and water [24] matrixes which highlight another analysis gap.

The patient multiple comorbidities may have influenced both the clinical presentation and outcome, complicating the attribution of symptoms to Cyclospora infection, only. Finally, the retrospective nature of this report and the lack of routine pre-symptomatic stool screening hinder precise determination of the timing of infection, and no epidemiological linkage or trace-back analysis was possible due to the absence of other reported cases in the same unit.

Nevertheless, this case was associated with a fatal outcome in a senescent person admitted with signs resembling pneumonia, in the context of chronic renal insufficiency and receiving multiple medications and highlights the need for a broader surveillance, routine testing, and more comprehensive molecular and environmental studies to better understand Cyclospora epidemiology and its clinical impact, particularly among vulnerable populations (immunosuppressed, older people with chronic diseases). Also, clinical laboratories may be consider adding molecular testing for Cyclospora for diarrheal patients without travel history and negative routine tests, especially among vulnerable populations are recommended.

In conclusion, this report of a locally acquired, autochthonous case, confirmed and characterized by a combined molecular-phylogenetic approach, contributes novel sequence-based epidemiological evidence for C. cayetanensis infection in Italy. Moreover, future national surveillance efforts would benefit from implementing higher- resolution genotyping methods, such as analysis of the hsp70 gene or whole- genome sequencing, to enable more precise outbreak detection and source attribution within European supply chains.
