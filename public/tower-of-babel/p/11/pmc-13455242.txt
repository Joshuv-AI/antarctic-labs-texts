A survey on occupational medicine residency programs across european countries
Authors: Veruscka Leso, Kari Reijula, Fabriziomaria Gobba, Ivo Iavicoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe European Union of Medical Specialists aims at improving post-graduate training in different disciplines developing European Training Requirements (ETRs) that reflect scientific progress. The study assessed the completeness, coherence, and applicability of the current Occupational Medicine (OM) ETR document and identified areas requiring revision and implementation.MethodsAn online survey was conducted among 22 European specialty training representatives to address specific items concerning the training of the OM specialists, including requirements for trainees, trainers and Institutions.ResultsAn OM specialty was present in all the countries investigated, with universities responsible for training in 56% cases. In every country theoretical knowledge and practical skills covered the following areas: framework for practice; clinical practice; hazard recognition, evaluation and control of risk; epidemiology and preventive health. Other topics, e.g., effective teaching and educational supervision; research methods; leadership, policy development and professionalism; business continuity, disaster preparedness and emergency management were not consistently covered, with gaps reported in 19-31.8% of countries. Suggested additions to the OM curriculum included disability, protection of conditions of susceptibility, future of work, ethics, interprofessional training, communication strategies, and emergency management.ConclusionsAlthough several common approaches to OM training emerged, among participating countries, some differences were identified in specific competency areas, governance processes, and assessment procedures. These findings suggest the need for further reshaping OM training according to the evolving features of the world of work.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09587-9.

Introduction

The European Union of Medical Specialists (UEMS) is a non-profit organization that includes key professional associations of medical specialists across EU countries with the purpose to enhance patient care promoting excellence in medical specialists’ practice [1]. While postgraduate medical training remains under national authority responsibility, the UEMS plays a coordinating and advisory role in Europe by developing European Training Requirements (ETRs), which provide reference frameworks for core competencies and harmonized training standards across European countries.

The UEMS Section of Occupational Medicine (OM Section) was established in 1997 and today comprises delegates from most European nations [2]. It promotes the harmonisation of training, continuing medical education and shared core competencies for OM specialists [3]. Changes in the world of work, including technological progress, organizational variability, demographic changes, globalization, climate change, economic dynamics, requires to continuously re-shape the approach to the OM practice and to provide OM specialists with the updated knowledge, practice and skills on an on-going basis [4]. Therefore, in line with all other UEMS specialist Sections, which are collectively responsible for developing the cross-sectional ETR applicable to every medical specialty, the OM Section is committed to improving post-graduate training across Europe. The ETR set out the knowledge, skills, and competencies that a specialist must acquire during their training to ensure safe and autonomous clinical practice [5]. The last version of the UEMS document “Training Requirements for the Specialty of OM” was produced in 2013. This document should be regularly updated by UEMS Specialist Sections and European Boards to ensure alignments with scientific and medical advancements.

In this perspective, a survey was performed to verify the validity of the document’s content in terms of completeness, coherence, and applicability, but also to identify areas that may require revision and implementation according to the experience of the European representatives involved in OM Specialty training. These findings may contribute to implement OM training programs in European countries and to ensure that specialists are adequately prepared to deliver high-quality care to workers in the evolving world of work.

Materials and methods

The survey

General overview

Between October 16, 2024, and January 8, 2025, a survey was conducted involving national representatives with professional experience in OM training from the 22 countries represented in the UEMS OM Section. Only members of the UEMS OM Section or experts nominated by them when responsibility for training was held by other professionals at the national level were involved in the study. They included national delegates to the UEMS Section, university professors responsible for specialist training programmes, residency programme coordinators, representatives of national professional societies, and other experts involved in postgraduate training governance (e.g., examiners, institutional trainers, or experts from health authorities). These individuals were selected as key informants because of their direct knowledge of the organization and implementation of OM training in their respective countries.

To obtain a general overview at a country-level, respondents were asked to complete the questionnaire on behalf of the national training system, rather than reflecting mainly the practices of their Institution. This approach was adopted because training requirements in OM are generally defined at national level, even when multiple Institutions provide specialist training within the same country. Accordingly, respondents were invited to synthesize information across Institutions and reflect the official national framework. Each response reflected the consolidated information available at the national level.

Data were collected through a structured questionnaire developed specifically for this study by a small working group of the OM Section and subsequently discussed and approved by all members. The questionnaire was implemented using Google Forms and distributed following the standard UEMS procedure, whereby questionnaires are circulated through national representatives responsible for coordinating and consolidating country-level information. During the data collection period, reminder emails were sent, and national representatives who had not responded were also contacted individually to encourage participation. The questionnaire addressed three main areas: training requirements for trainees, training requirements for trainers, and institutional requirements for training centres (see Supplementary Material). It included a mix of yes/no, multiple-choice and open-ended questions and required approximately 20 min to complete. Participation was voluntary.

Respondents’ information and training requirements

The survey collected information on respondents and the national context of OM training, including country, role in the national training system, the existence and duration of an OM specialty programme, the Institution responsible for specialist training, the number of training centers. In countries where the specialty was not formally established, respondents were asked to indicate alternative training pathways and the responsible Institutions.

Specific sections explored training requirements for trainees, trainers, and training institutions. For trainees, the survey explored the content of training and the intended learning outcomes defined within OM residency programmes, in terms of both theoretical knowledge and practical/clinical competencies referring to the ten competency domains included in the current ETR (Table 1). The questionnaire examined formal training requirements and competency expectations defined at programme level, rather than the subjective learning experiences of trainees. In this study, the term learning outcomes refers to the expected knowledge and competencies defined in national curricula, i.e. what training programmes intend trainees to acquire during residency. Additional items addressed the structure of training programmes, including course duration, prior clinical training before entering OM training, and the availability of practical experience in occupational health services and workplace assessment. Moreover, the typical timeframe for completing training, the possibility of out-of-programme academic activities, and requirements related to language proficiency and access to international scientific literature were investigated. Respondents were also asked to indicate their level of agreement regarding the inclusion of specific clinical focus areas within the curriculum, such as occupational risk assessment, occupational diseases and injuries, work ability assessment and psychosocial risk factors.

The survey also assessed whether Institutions had structured systems for assessment, evaluation, and governance of specialist training, including written or clinical examinations, trainee feedback, annual reviews conducted by a competent authority—such as the program director or an appointed institutional committee—based on an objective appraisal of evidence mapped to the competency framework, and external quality assurance procedures.

The second part of the survey examined the requirements for trainers and training Institutions including trainer qualifications, experience and competencies, as well as quality assurance measures. Respondents were also asked to indicate their level of agreement with institutional criteria for recognition as a training centre, including staffing, clinical activity, infrastructure and quality management processes.

Results

Overview

All 22 invited national representatives completed the survey. The geographical spread of the respondents is summarized in Fig. 1. The presence of an OM Specialty programme was confirmed in all the participating countries. Universities were the main institutions responsible for specialist training (56%), followed by Hospitals or Occupational Health services (42%). In isolated cases, the Ministry of Health and the College of Physicians were indicated as responsible for training (two countries each, approximately 10%). The number of OM schools for training ranged from single entities to 52 occupational health teaching units, reflecting different organizations of the training courses. In four countries, the presence of larger Institutions/schools was mentioned together with the coexistence of approved smaller occupational health services. The length of the OM specialty was in 4 years, as declared by 62% of the respondents. Smaller percentages reported 5 or 6 years, while one country reported a specialization course lasting 3 months.

Training requirements for trainees: content of training and learning outcome

Theoretical knowledge

All the respondents confirmed that the theoretical knowledge for medical trainees in OM, in their respective countries, covered areas including framework for practice; clinical practice; hazard recognition, evaluation and control of risk; epidemiology and preventive health (refer to Table 1 for details). However, some domains such as effective teaching and educational supervision; research methods; leadership, policy development and professionalism; business continuity, disaster preparedness and emergency management were reported as not covered in national training programmes by 31.8%, 23.8%, 22.7% and 19% of the respondents, respectively. Respondents suggested that these discrepancies may reflect the lack of the inclusion of such topics into the training programs. Particularly, effective teaching and educational supervision were often considered pertinent solely to academics and certified trainers, while research methodology was indicated as less relevant outside research settings. However, understanding evidence-based data to critically appraise medical articles was deemed important for occupational physicians. One country excluded training on fitness for work, rehabilitation, and disability assessment, citing the absence of legal requirements for occupational health services in companies. Suggested curriculum enhancements included early diagnosis in health surveillance, e-health tools (e.g., tele-monitoring), occupational rehabilitation, data protection, medical ethics, health economics, safeguarding, communication, first aid and emergency response, and sports medicine.

Practical and clinical skills

Among practical and clinical domains, there was unanimous agreement on including the framework for practice, clinical practice, fitness for work, rehabilitation, and disability assessment, and epidemiology and preventive health (Table 2). Lower inclusion was reported for business continuity, disaster preparedness, and emergency management (31.8%); service delivery and quality improvement (9.5%); leadership, policy development, and professionalism (22.7%); research methods (13.6%); and teaching and educational supervision (18.2%). These differences mainly reflected variations in university programs, with lower coverage of service delivery, quality improvement, and research methods because the curriculum is primarily targeted on first-level occupational health services. In one case, limited coverage reflected the recent introduction of a new specialization program. Proposed training areas included rotations in Internal and Emergency Medicine, Pneumology or Immuno-allergology, Dermatology, Orthopedics, Forensic Medicine, and experience at regional or local Labor Inspectorates or other occupational health institutions.

Competence

Concerning the general organization of the OM training, most respondents (90.5%) reported a staged training structure, with the first years finalized to develop foundational knowledge, and skills required for OM practice (90.5%) and the later years devoted to advanced competencies (90.9%), including the management of complex clinical situations and the development of management and leadership skills within the specialty and across specialties. In one country, the first three years were dedicated to general medical training, followed by two years focused on OM knowledge. Another respondent described a progressive increase in competency complexity following an annual review system.

Organization of training

Schedule of training

Concerning the schedule of training, most respondents indicated an OM specialist training consisted of a 4-year full-time programme including practical experience in occupational healthcare services and workplace assessment (Fig. 2). Trainees had to show sufficient evidence that they have reached all the curriculum competencies and sufficient linguistic ability to communicate with patients, clients and other staff together with the ability to regularly read and comprehend international literature and to communicate with foreign colleagues. Conversely, prior clinical training (e.g. in internal medicine or other clinical specialties) or training in another specialty before entering OM specialization was less commonly undertaken. Less than half of respondents declared that that training could usually be completed within two years beyond the expected programme duration, and a comparable percentage declared that this excluded out-of-program activities, such as research and studying for a higher degree (e.g. PhD).

Content of curriculum for specialist training (Focus areas)

Overall, high agreement (“quite”, “very much”, or “total”) was reported across most domains of the OM specialist training curriculum, limited disagreement was pointed out for specific topics (Table 3). Within occupational health practice and risk assessment, limited disagreement concerned the inclusion of workers’ compensation, ethics, occupational safety, industrial hygiene and pesticides. In the field of occupational injuries and diseases, lower agreement was expressed for the inclusion of eye injuries, clinical immunology, occupational hematology, genetic toxicology and reproductive toxicology. In relation to work ability and psychosocial aspects, slight disagreement was reported for vocational rehabilitation, mental disorders and workplace violence prevention. Some criticisms were also expressed regarding the inclusion of emerging and cross-cutting topics, including the future of work, workforce changes, climate change and workers’ health, as well as chemical/biological/radiological/nuclear/explosive preparedness, and responder safety and health (Table 3).

Additional feedback from respondents highlighted several areas that should be further strengthened in the curriculum, including disability and work (e.g., permanent disability, disability integration and ill-health retirement decisions), protection of susceptible workers (e.g., advanced age and vulnerable conditions), new technologies and work (e-health, telemedicine and artificial intelligence), the future of work, ethics, multidisciplinary training, increased practical training, communication, emergency preparedness.

Regarding the knowledge, skills, competencies and attitudes expected from trainees, general agreement was reported for core areas such as fitness for work, rehabilitation and disability assessment, epidemiology and preventive health. However, some disagreement emerged for hazard identification and risk assessment and management (4.5%), business continuity, disaster preparedness and emergency management (18.2%), service delivery and quality improvement (4.8% of 21 responses), leadership, policy development and professionalism (4.8% of 21 responses), research methods (9.1%), and effective teaching and educational supervision (13.6%).

Assessment and evaluation of specialist training were identified as key components of effective training programmes (Fig. 3). Most respondents reported that trainees were required to demonstrate achievement of expected competencies, through objective assessments and structured feedback processes. However, annual competency reviews and external assessments of training programmes were less consistently adopted, and only a minority reported the use of review panels involving both trainees and supervisors.

Process for recognition as trainer

Assessing trainers’ recognition and quality is crucial for effective training (Fig. 4). Regarding recognition, 90.9% of 22 respondents reported that educational supervisors were accredited OM specialists recognized by the national authority, and 81.8% indicated that supervisors should be trained in medical education, including program design, evaluation, and managing underperforming trainees. Supervisors oversee the local delivery of training programs. For trainer quality management, about half of respondents reported regular competency training for trainers, usually every three years and formal written feedback from trainees, while less than half indicated the use of peer review processes for poorly performing trainees, periodic feedback from supervisors to training authorities, and written performance-based evaluations identifying areas for improvement.

Training requirements for training Institutions

Respondents agreed that a training centre should be an Institution or organization formally accredited by a competent authority to provide specialist training in accordance with an approved curriculum and relevant national or European regulatory frameworks, either within the same institution or in collaboration with other occupational health providers or training institutions. In addition, respondents agreed that the educational supervisor should oversee training delivery and review trainees’ progress. Some respondents disagreed on the need for tailored practical training for unacquired competencies (13.6%), on the weekly supervisor- trainee contact during the first two years (9.1%) on providing resources for research or thesis work (9.1%), and more significantly on the ideal supervisor-to-trainee ratio of a maximum of two trainees per supervisor (27.3%), with a proposed maximum of two trainees per supervisor to allow close monitoring. Respondents agreed that institutions should provide access to appropriate resources, including a library or online occupational health texts and journals, and that examination and screening equipment should be properly maintained, updated, and suitable for program requirements.

Quality management within training institutions

Regarding quality management within training Institutions, most respondents reported that Institutions must provide written evidence of resources supporting the training program and that the accreditation board should review available resources to ensure that the curriculum could be delivered with (Table 4). Many indicated quality assurance through targeted visits to Postgraduate Deaneries, while a lower percentage reported institutional site visits every three years. More than half of respondents pointed out the importance of effective workforce planning, with a recommended maximum of two trainees per clinical supervisor and providing additional staff for specific training activities. Most respondents indicated initial approval of training programs by the educational supervisor and external review by the accreditation board. Auditing processes, such as annual reviews and written reports, as well as oversight by accrediting authorities, were also commonly reported.

Discussion

This study explored how OM training is currently organized across participating European countries in relation to modern medical practice and scientific developments. OM residency programs should evolve with changes in workplaces, healthcare systems, and professional opportunities to ensure trainees acquire competencies essential for the contemporary OM practice [6, 7]. The survey provides an overview of current training systems and pointed out areas that may support future revisions of the UEMS OM training requirements. Some differences emerged in the organization of training, particularly in course duration, responsible institutions, and governance procedures. However, several common structural features were also identified, including a staged progression from foundational OM knowledge to more advanced practical and clinical competencies. OM specialists faces a series of clinical, preventive, organizational, and social issues related to workers’ health. In line with this multidisciplinary role, most respondents reported training programmes focused on practical experience in occupational healthcare services, workplace assessment, and communication with patients, employers, and multidisciplinary teams.

Across the surveyed countries, several knowledge areas were consistently included, such as the role of OM and occupational health services, evidence-based practice for workers’ assessment, risk assessment and communication, prevention of physical injury and illness, workplace risk management, health surveillance, and workplace health promotion. Corresponding practical skills were also widely addressed, particularly the application of legislation in OM practice, worker health assessment, evaluation of disability or ill health, determination of fitness for work or workplace adjustments, and implementation of preventive and health promotion measures. Lower agreement was observed for domains related to teaching, research, leadership, and emergency preparedness, both in terms of knowledge and practical competencies. These differences may reflect variations in national curricula, institutional priorities, and perceptions regarding the relevance of such areas to routine OM practice. Some clinical areas showed more variable agreement regarding their inclusion in curricula. Topics such as clinical immunology, occupational hematology, and genetic toxicology may be perceived as mainly research-oriented and less relevant to routine clinical practice. Partial disagreement was also observed for male and female reproductive toxicology, although this area is particularly relevant in light of the Directive (EU) 2022/431 amending Directive 2004/37/EC on the protection of workers from exposure to carcinogens or mutagens at work [8]. The directive highlights that reprotoxic substances can cause serious and irreversible health effects and require adequate workplace protection measures. This is especially important given the increasing participation of women in the workforce, including sectors traditionally dominated by men, which calls for greater OM attention to gender-related occupational risks and the protection of female workers’ health [9, 10].

Additionally, areas regarding the future of work, the changing nature of workforce, climate change and workers’ health, biological, radiological, nuclear, and explosive preparedness were not consistently integrated into training programs, although respondents frequently identified them as areas requiring greater attention in future curriculum development.

Adequate training of OM specialists may benefit multiple stakeholders, including workers, employers, and public health systems, by supporting effective workplace risk assessment and management, worker health protection, and preventive strategies. In this context, proper management of training requirements for both trainers and training institutions is essential. While institutional aspects such as accreditation, collaboration among training providers, and the role of educational supervisors were generally aligned, some disagreement emerged regarding personalized practical training with regular trainee–supervisor contact, resources to support research activities, and the ratio of clinical supervisors to trainees together with external review processes, peer evaluation, and systematic trainee feedback that were less consistently adopted. These findings suggest that, beyond defining shared competencies, additional attention may be needed regarding governance and quality assurance processes within OM specialist training.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the representativeness of the sample across European countries was not fully comprehensive. Participation was voluntary and not all European countries were represented. In this context, for example, no respondents from France— where no representatives are included in the OM section of the UEMS— could be enrolled in the study. This may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although respondents were asked to provide a national overview, heterogeneity between institutions within the same country may not have been fully captured. Additionally, some responses may have been affected by the intrinsic difficulty of item comprehension, reflecting differences in the organization of OM courses and the applicability of certain domains in specific national contexts. Furthermore, the survey was descriptive and exploratory in nature and was not designed to evaluate the effectiveness of specific training models or their impact on professional outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the study provides a comparative overview of OM training across multiple European countries, highlighting both shared core domains and areas of variability. Overall, the findings may support future discussions on the progressive harmonization of OM training standards promoting the continuous professional development of OM specialists in Europe. In this view, an important issue concerns how greater harmonization of OM specialist training across Europe could realistically be promoted. In fact, although the organization of medical education and specialist training remains primarily under national responsibility, the UEMS may play a facilitating and advisory role. UEMS recommendations, training charters and ETRs can provide reference frameworks that support voluntary alignment of training standards and foster mutual recognition of professional competencies across countries. From a practical perspective, progress toward greater harmonization could rely on the identification of a shared set of core competencies for OM specialists while maintaining sufficient flexibility to allow adaptation to national healthcare systems and occupational health structures. Concrete initiatives may include the development of common competency frameworks, periodic updates of ETRs, the promotion of European training networks and exchange programmes for trainees and trainers, and the sharing of best practices in curriculum design and assessment methods. Additional opportunities could involve joint workshops among national training bodies, collaborative research and educational projects, and the development of common guidance documents on emerging topics such as the future of work, climate change and workers’ health. In this perspective, the findings of the present survey may help identify priority areas for future dialogue and collaboration among European OM training institutions, professional organizations and policy stakeholders. Future work could build on these findings to support the progressive development of shared educational standards for OM training across Europe.

Conclusion

Although the present survey identified several shared features of OM specialist training across participating European countries, some evident differences remain in specific competency domains, governance procedures, and quality assurance systems. The findings suggest that current OM training programmes are progressively adapting to changes in the world of work, although emerging areas such as climate change, technological innovation, emergency preparedness, and evolving workforce characteristics are not yet consistently integrated into all curricula. The survey also highlighted differences in assessment methods, trainer evaluation, and external quality assurance processes, indicating that further efforts may be useful to support greater consistency in specialist training standards across countries. In this context, the UEMS ETRs may continue to provide a useful reference framework for promoting dialogue and collaboration. Moreover, coordinated efforts among academic institutions, professional bodies, and policymakers would also benefit from an active involvement of accreditation and regulatory authorities, ideally within a European accreditation framework based on shared training standards and requirements. Institutions of this type would play an essential role for setting training standards, overseeing compliance with approved curricula, and ensuring that both theoretical and practical components meet established quality benchmarks. Training programmes should strengthen competencies in risk assessment and management, interdisciplinary collaboration, and leadership engagement in promoting healthy and inclusive workplaces. At the same time, robust quality assurance mechanisms are needed, including standardized evaluation procedures, peer review processes and, possibly, an external assessment eventually implemented under the guidance and supervision of accrediting bodies. Systematic feedback from trainees should also be integrated as a core element for continuous improvement to better inform the future development of OM specialist education in Europe.
