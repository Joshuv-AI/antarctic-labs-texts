Displacement of synthetic cranioplasty implant due to mechanical failure of titanium fixation plating: illustrative case
Authors: Graham A. Branscom, Evan M. Ren, Michael B. Lemonick, Bahie Ezzat, Ziad Rifi, Shahram Majidi, Christopher P. Kellner, Tomoyoshi Shigematsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDCranioplasty is a common procedure following decompressive craniectomy for traumatic brain injury or stroke, among other reasons, in order to repair a skull defect. Cranioplasty failure is an infrequent occurrence, but in the case of synthetic implants, it is usually caused by infection and incorrect sizing. However, trauma-induced displacement, breakage, and deformities of cranioplasty implants are less common.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present a unique case of a cranioplasty failure due to displacement of a polyetheretherketone (PEEK) implant secondary to catastrophic breakage of the titanium fixation plates. The cause of displacement is unknown but is suspected to be trauma. A subsequent review of the literature contextualizes common reasons for failure among synthetic implants and suggests that fracture of synthetic implants or their fixation plates is extremely rare.LESSONSThe displacement of a PEEK cranioplasty implant due to breakage of titanium fixation plates is a rare, albeit possible, complication of this neurosurgical procedure. Lifestyle modifications are paramount to reduce trauma risk and resultant cranioplasty failure. The addition of miniplates may also reduce implant displacement risk. Finally, since rates of failure differ by material, careful consideration of the type of material used for the implant should be incorporated into neurosurgical planning. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE2694

Cranioplasty is a surgical procedure to repair a defective or damaged part of the skull and is usually performed following decompressive craniectomy (DC), although congenital malformations, traumatic deformations, or cosmetic anomalies are additional reasons for performing cranioplasty repair.1,2 DC relieves intracranial pressure following a variety of pathologies, such as traumatic brain injury, malignant middle cerebral artery syndrome, or intracranial hemorrhage.3 Once the intracranial pressure has been stabilized following DC, cranioplasty is ultimately performed to replace the missing area of skull, restoring brain protection, cosmetic appearance, and CSF dynamics while reducing the impact of atmospheric pressure on the brain.2

The exact rates and causes of cranioplasty failure remain incompletely understood, although retrospective reviews of cranioplasty cases reveal that failure rates may be as high as 10%–20%. Reasons for failure include unfixed bone flaps and spontaneous resorption for autologous grafts, infection, and cement extrusion.4,5 Some evidence suggests that synthetic grafts may have higher failure rates than autologous grafts.5 Among synthetic grafts, polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) may have a higher failure rate than titanium.4 However, the reasons for cranioplasty failure, regardless of implant material, remain to be completely elucidated and warrant further examination, especially through detailed analyses of rare cases that may augment existing literature on the subject.

Here we present the unique case of a patient who was treated with a coil embolization for a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) and then received a decompressive hemicraniectomy at postbleed day 20. After 7 months of rehabilitation, he received a cranioplasty with a synthetic polyetheretherketone (PEEK) cranioplasty graft. However, 3 years later, the cranioplasty became dislodged from the calvarium due to breakage of the titanium fixation plating. CT of the head (CTH) before the revision surgery revealed posterior displacement of the cranioplasty implant. The exact reason for the catastrophic hardware failure remains unknown, but trauma was suspected. This report highlights a rare instance of mechanical failure of a synthetic skull graft and proposes clinical considerations to ensure successful future cranioplasties.

Illustrative Case

The patient is a middle-aged male with no significant past medical history who experienced a severe, debilitating headache following straining at home. The patient then developed nonsensical speech and was brought to the emergency department by a family member.

On arrival, the patient was noted to have right facial droop, aphasia, and ongoing severe headaches. CTH revealed both diffuse SAH and intracranial hemorrhage with hydrocephalus affecting the left frontal lobe. The accompanying CT angiography (CTA) examination revealed a left M2 bifurcation aneurysm measuring 3.5 × 3 mm and 1.8 mm at the neck (Fig. 1A). An extraventricular drain (EVD) was urgently placed, followed by coil embolization of the aneurysm, achieving a Raymond-Roy class 1 occlusion (Fig. 1B and C). The patient was brought back for cranioplasty 7 months later. The cranioplasty was performed using a custom 3D-printed PEEK implant (Stryker). After the incision made during the original hemicraniectomy was reopened, the skin flap was lifted up and separated from the dura without any durotomy. The dura appeared full. Once completed, a small cerebral corticotomy was placed to access the ventricle at the damaged and thinned frontal lobe, and the CSF was drained. After this maneuver, the dura became slack so that the custom-made PEEK implant fit the craniectomy site well without a gap. After draining the CSF, watertight closure of the dura was performed, before fixing the implant using titanium plates (Stryker Universal Neuro III 16 mm) and screws (Stryker Universal Neuro III self-drilling, axial stability, latex-free, 1.5 × 4 mm). A Hemovac was placed in the middle of the craniectomy, and the skin was closed. The implant fit appropriately (Fig. 3) and the incision healed well. In follow-up appointments, the patient was awake but unable to follow commands other than responding to his name. His right-sided extremities demonstrated 2/5 movement to pain, whereas his left-sided extremities showed no response to pain. Three months postcranioplasty, repeat CTH showed stability without expansion of the ventricles, and an EVD or other CSF diversions were not indicated. Three years later, the patient presented to the emergency department from a skilled nursing facility with posterior displacement of the PEEK implant with a depressed anterior margin and raised posterior margin, which overlapped with the occipital calvarium. There was also a fluid collection subjacent to the implant noted on CTH (Fig. 4 left). An MRI study was also obtained, which showed extensive encephalomalacia of the left hemisphere with corresponding mild ex vacuo dilatation of the left ventricle. Furthermore, midline structures were observed to be shifted 8 mm to the right. Trauma was suspected to be the cause of the displacement, but the patient remained cognitively impaired and unable to recall whether a traumatic event had occurred. The cranioplasty was explanted and washout of the wound was performed. During the procedure, it was found that the titanium fixation plates at the superior aspect were fractured (Fig. 4 right). Additionally, a defect was noted behind the left ear in which half of a titanium dog bone was visible, and a second defect was noted midway between the orbit and the zygoma. Intraoperative medications included neosynephrine. One-month postoperative CTH showed stability of the craniectomy and no expansion of the ventricles, so an EVD or lumbar drain was not indicated. A 6-month reevaluation is planned for implantation of a new synthetic cranioplasty. FIG. 4.Left: Head CT scan showing posterior displacement of the cranioplasty implant secondary to loss of fixation. Discussion

Literature Review

We searched the PubMed database for literature discussing the incidence rates and potential causes of cranioplasty failure. The following search terms were used: (cranioplasty[Title/Abstract]) AND (failure[Title/Abstract] OR failed[Title/Abstract] OR complication*[Title/Abstract] OR infection[Title/Abstract] OR resorption[Title/Abstract] OR revision[Title/Abstract]). This search yielded retrospective studies, case reports, and systematic reviews discussing this area of research.

Observations

There have been several retrospective studies investigating the overall incidence rate and causes of cranioplasty failure. While several reasons for failure are discussed in the studies reviewed, titanium fixation plating breakage is a rare and previously unreported cause.

A study by Hacherl et al. retrospectively analyzed 329 cases of cranioplasty defects in which a synthetic implant was used following craniectomy.6 The authors found that infection was the most common complication (39% of cases), followed by incorrect size or brand of the implant (30%) and breakage of implant hardware (6%).6 Although hardware failure was cited as a cause of cranioplasty failure, the authors were specifically referring to the implant device as opposed to the skull fixation mechanisms, as seen in our case.

In most of the documented instances of failure secondary to trauma, the implant itself was made of titanium. A retrospective study of 127 titanium cranioplasties across 113 patients found that there were 2 instances of trauma to the site of the cranioplasty, responsible for 2% of all complications.7 Several case reports have documented similar trauma-induced deformities in titanium cranioplasties.8,9 In these cases, the implant itself was deformed rather than the fixation plates. We found only one documented instance of cranioplasty failure due to trauma-induced breakage of fixation plating; Igarashi et al. presented the case of a 5-year-old boy who received a cranioplasty following DC for an arteriovenous malformation removal.10 This child’s cranioplasty implant was made of hydroxyapatite and was secured using two bioresorbable LactoSorb plates and one titanium plate. He received a cranioplasty revision following head trauma, and the surgeons discovered breakage of the two bioresorbable plates while the titanium plate remained intact.10 This case is the closest in nature to ours since the cranioplasty failure was due to fixation plating breakage instead of failure of the implant itself. However, we were unable to identify reports mentioning breakage of titanium plates in cranioplasty, likely due to the fact that titanium can withstand more force than bioresorbable materials.11

A study by Rashidi et al. looked specifically at cranioplasty implant displacements following DC.12 They found that among the 329 cranioplasty cases following DC performed at their institution, implant displacement occurred in 13 (4%) of them. They also calculated the correlation of certain factors, including the use of miniature fixation (miniplates), the use of titanium clamps, fixation technique, skull implant material, and cranioplasty timing, with skull implant displacement. Among the factors examined, they found significantly lower rates of implant displacement only related to miniplate usage in cranioplasty (p < 0.05), suggesting the use of miniplates to be protective against skull implant displacement and thereby elucidating a potentially preventative strategy for cranioplasty failure. Additionally, they found no significant difference in the rates of autologous, PEEK, or PMMA implant use among the displacement group versus no displacement group, indicating that the material of the implant may not be correlated with displacement of the implant.12

Although our reported cranioplasty failure was due to the fixation plates instead of the implant itself, our review of the literature suggests that the choice of the implant material may also affect the rate of general cranioplasty failure. The broad classifications of cranioplasty implants are biological grafts, including autologous grafts, allografts, and xenografts, and synthetic implants. Common synthetic materials include titanium mesh, PEEK, and PMMA, with each offering its own benefits in terms of cost-effectiveness, durability, and patient-tailored customization.13 Koller et al. showed that synthetic implants may have higher rates of failure compared to autologous grafts.5 Examining PEEK implants specifically, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Punchak et al. found that, of the 183 patients with PEEK implants included in their study, 15% of patients developed postoperative complications and 9% experienced failure requiring revision surgery.14 Compared to the rates observed in patients with either autologous bone grafts or titanium implants, patients with PEEK implants had lower risks of postoperative complications and implant failure, although these findings were not statistically significant.14 In contrast, in a retrospective study of 287 cranioplasty cases that used synthetic implants, Millward et al. found that PEEK implants had the highest rate of any surgical morbidity among the six different synthetic implant materials studied.15 Similarly, Rosinski et al. found that, among a retrospective cohort of 54 cranioplasty cases, the infection rate was higher in the custom implant group (consisting mostly of PEEK implants) than in the titanium implant group (p < 0.005).16 However, their study used a small cohort, so these results may not reflect the true differences between these implant materials and are therefore difficult to generalize.16 In aggregate, these studies paint a mixed picture of the favorability of PEEK implants as compared to other synthetic implants or biological grafts, proposing an avenue for future research.

We also aimed to identify additional reasons for PEEK implant failure. A study by Jonkergouw et al., who sought to identify the causes for cranioplasty failure among PEEK implants, provides an important clinical analysis of this implant type with clear applicability to our case. These authors retrospectively studied 40 PEEK implants among 38 patients, finding a 28% overall complication rate. The complications consisted of infections, hematomas, a CSF leak, and wound-related problems; breakage, implant displacement, and plate failure were not found to be among the complications.17

In summary, there is one documented case of cranioplasty failure due to bioresorbable fixation plate breakage,10 but there are no reports of catastrophic breakage of titanium fixation plating causing displacement of an intact implant, making our case particularly novel. Additionally, our review of the literature examined overall rates and reasons for the occurrence of cranioplasty failure in different implant materials. There is a lack of consensus as to whether PEEK implants have lower rates of failure compared to other materials, and there have been no reports of cranioplasty implant displacements for PEEK implants.

Lessons

As this study is limited to a single case, it is difficult to generalize our findings and make definitive conclusions regarding the risk factors for cranioplasty failure. Future studies can conduct large retrospective analyses of failed cranioplasties to identify the reasons for failure. Also, while we suspect a trauma-induced breakage, the reason for failure in our case still remains idiopathic, thereby complicating the narrow application of our findings to would-be similar cases in the future.

This case represents a rare case of cranioplasty failure due to the displacement of a PEEK implant secondary to catastrophic breakage of titanium fixation plating. A review of the current literature demonstrates that cranioplasty failure due to breakage of fixation plating is an extremely rare occurrence compared to much more common reasons like infection or incorrect implant sizing. There is only one documented case of failure due to fixation plating breakage, although that was a bioresorbable plate.10 Additionally, one study found miniplates to be potentially protective in preventing cranioplasty failure.12 Surgeons may consider using miniplates when conducting cranioplasties to minimize the chance of failure.

Although the failure in the case of our report was due to plate breakage, our literature review also revealed that most studies discuss the benefits and disadvantages of the different materials used in the implants themselves. Although there is no clear superiority of PEEK implants as the choice of cranioplasty implant material, PEEK implants remain a widely used, safe option for surgeons. However, it is important that surgeons remain cognizant of both the limitations of implant material and the potential complications of implant displacement due to failure of fixation devices when treating patients in both acute and long-term settings. Close in-person follow-up to assess the incision and the implant after cranioplasty could help to identify any traumatic cause of fixation failure or continuous stress to the fixation device.
