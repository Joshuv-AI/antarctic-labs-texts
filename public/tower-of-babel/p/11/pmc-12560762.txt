A Quality Improvement Pilot to Increase Hepatitis B Screening and Optimize Patient Selection for the Switch to Two-Drug Antiretroviral Regimens in People With HIV
Authors: Melissa Margolis, Shilpa Vasishta, Judith A Aberg, Antonio E Urbina, Douglas T Dieterich, Michael Mullen, Rachel Chasan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTwo-drug antiretroviral regimens (2DRs) without tenofovir are increasingly prescribed for HIV; however, these regimens lack full activity against hepatitis B virus (HBV). An electronic health record (EHR)–based quality improvement project was developed to optimize the switch to a 2DR within New York City HIV primary care clinics.MethodsAn EHR best practice advisory was linked to 2DR orders (dolutegravir-lamivudine, dolutegravir-rilpivirine, cabotegravir-rilpivirine) reminding clinicians to check HBV serologies prior to the switch and providing guidance for prescribing 2DRs to reduce risk of HBV reactivation in people with HIV. Prescribing patterns were monitored before and after implementation of the alert, including number of individuals screened for HBV—surface antigen (HBsAg), core antibody (HBcAb), and surface antibody—and the number of switches among those with a positive HBsAg (considered at highest risk) or an isolated positive HBcAb (elevated risk). Outcomes were compared for a 1-year period before and after alert implementation on 1 February 2023.ResultsAfter alert implementation, 103 of 145 (71%) individuals had HBV screening prior to a 2DR switch, as compared with 48 of 161 (30%) preimplementation (P < .0001). Postimplementation there were fewer 2DR switches among those at increased risk for HBV reactivation (pre- vs postimplementation, 12 vs 1; P = .003).ConclusionsHBV reactivation risk may be overlooked by clinicians when people with HIV switch to 2DRs. In this study, an EHR-based intervention increased HBV screening rates and decreased switches in those at highest risk of HBV reactivation (positive HBsAg or isolated positive HBcAb). With the increasing uptake of 2DRs, optimizing patient selection is vital to prevent HBV reactivation.Keywords: 2DR, hepatitis B, HIV, quality improvement, screening

In the United States, 5% to 10% of people with HIV-1 (PWH) are coinfected with hepatitis B virus (HBV), and as many as 1 in 3 PWH may be at risk for HBV reactivation [1–3]. Therefore, HBV screening is recommended upon entry into HIV care, and repeat testing should be considered prior to a switch to regimens without tenofovir [4, 5]. Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the noninferiority of 2-drug antiretroviral regimens (2DRs) for HIV, such as dolutegravir-lamivudine, dolutegravir-rilpivirine, and cabotegravir-rilpivirine, as compared with 3-drug regimens [6]. Since 2017, multiple 2DRs without full HBV activity or with suboptimal HBV activity have been approved for treatment of HIV, and PWH increasingly switch to 2DRs to simplify treatment and limit toxicities. In response, the opportunistic infection guidelines of the Department of Health and Human Services now recommend that individuals without prior chronic HBV have HBV serologies checked within 3 months before the switch to 2DRs [7].

Notably, discontinuing HBV-active antiretroviral therapy (ART) poses a significant risk of HBV reactivation among those with a positive hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), and it is crucial to recognize this prior to a switch to 2DRs. Multiple studies have shown an elevated HBV reactivation rate among persons with a positive HBsAg after discontinuation of HBV-active medications [8, 9], including PWH [10–12]. In the Swiss HIV Cohort Study, 3.4% of PWH with a positive HBsAg had severe alanine aminotransferase elevations (>10× upper limit of normal) within 6 months after stopping HBV-active ART [10]. In the recent OPERA cohort, the incidence of HBV reactivation was 9.92 per 100 person-years for PWH with a positive HBsAg who stopped taking tenofovir [13].

A significant percentage of PWH have an isolated positive hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAb) (17%–41%), conferring potential for HBV reactivation when switching to a 2DR [14]. These individuals were excluded from major randomized controlled trials establishing the noninferiority of 2DRs to triple therapy (eg, SWORD-1/2, IMPALA) [15, 16], although large observational studies published since 2023 (Veterans Ageing Cohort Study, OPERA, GEMINI-STAT-SALSA-TANGO) have demonstrated HBV reactivation rates of 0% to 2% in this population. The December 2024 guidelines of the Department of Health and Human Services note that individuals with a positive HBcAb may be considered for a switch if benefits outweigh risks, with regular laboratory monitoring postswitch. Several case reports describe clinically significant hepatitis after a 2DR switch in those with a positive HBcAb, particularly in the setting of unsuppressed HIV or other immune suppression [3, 17–29], reinforcing the need for individualized risk assessment for a 2DR switch and close postswitch monitoring in this population.

Case surveillance in our institution over a 1-year period in 2022 revealed that out of 332 people who switched to 2DRs, 4 had HBV infection or reactivation after the switch: 1 with overlooked HBsAg-positive infection, 1 with HBcAb and a low CD4 count, and 2 with low or absent hepatitis B surface antibody (HBsAb) titers after vaccine. All had elevated transaminase levels postswitch, 2 required intensive care for liver failure, and 1 ultimately required liver transplantation [24]. These events highlighted the need for careful selection of patients when switching to a 2DR, as well as the utility of dedicated clinical guidance on this topic.

Therefore, in late 2022 we developed guidance regarding HBV screening before a 2DR switch, and in February 2023 we implemented an electronic health record (EHR)–based quality improvement (QI) project within HIV primary care clinics in the Mount Sinai Health System. A hard-stop best practice advisory was created, hereafter referred to as an alert, that provides clinicians with guidance to optimize the selection of PWH switching to a 2DR. We developed a best practice advisory QI intervention based on established evidence that EHR alerts have effectively improved the implementation of clinical guidelines and increased rates of HBV screening in a range of settings [30]. This intervention aimed (1) to increase the percentage of people who had HBV screening prior to switching to a 2DR and (2) to decrease the number of switches to a 2DR among those at elevated risk of HBV reactivation or infection.

METHODS

Study Population

The study population included adults receiving HIV care between 1 February 2022 and 1 February 2024 at 1 of 4 HIV clinics in Manhattan affiliated with Mount Sinai Health System's Institute of Advanced Medicine (IAM). Participants were included if they (1) were prescribed a 2DR as a switch from an ART regimen with full HBV activity and (2) had an encounter at any of the IAM clinics between 1 February 2022 and 1 February 2024. Individuals were excluded if they had not switched de novo to a 2DR (eg, no prior ART, switch from a prior 2DR, or continuation of therapy), had not engaged at Mount Sinai for ongoing primary care (ie, at least 2 clinic encounters during the study period), had received additional HBV coverage (eg, entecavir), or had switched to a 2DR as an inpatient.

QI Intervention

Starting 1 February 2023, an alert was programmed to appear in the EHR when a clinician ordered, during the initial switch and reorder, one of three 2DRs without full HBV activity: dolutegravir-lamivudine, dolutegravir-rilpivirine, cabotegravir-rilpivirine. The alert (Figure 1) required clinicians to confirm that HBV serologies had been checked prior to the switch and that the patient did not have a positive HBsAg or an isolated positive HBcAb. In this pilot intervention, HBV screening was suggested within 6 months prior to a switch to ensure up-to-date serologies. The alert linked to institutional guidance for switching to 2DRs according to risk stratification for HBV reactivation or infection based on HBV serologies.

Electronic health record alert from a quality improvement intervention for 2-drug antiretroviral therapy orders.

An institutional guidance algorithm (Figure 2) was developed in late 2022 as a collaboration between the infectious diseases and hepatology divisions. The algorithm recommends that individuals with a positive HBsAg continue or start an ART regimen with full HBV activity. With HBsAg negativity, further recommendations were based on one’s HBcAb and HBsAb status. Persons immune to HBV from prior vaccination (HBsAb positive, HBcAb negative) can switch to a 2DR. Those susceptible to HBV (HBsAb negative, HBcAb negative) should first receive HBV vaccination and then proceed with a switch to a 2DR after evidence of postvaccination immunity. People with immunity from prior infection (HBsAb positive, HBcAb positive) should be counseled about the potential risk of HBV reactivation and proceed with the 2DR switch after shared decision making. Last, the initial version of the guidance classifies individuals with isolated positive HBcAb as being at elevated risk for HBV reactivation and does not recommend a switch to a 2DR for this group, given the available literature and events observed within our institution.

Institutional guidance for switching to 2-drug antiretroviral therapy. HBcAb, hepatitis B core antibody; HBsAb, hepatitis B surface antibody; HBsAg, hepatitis B surface antigen; HBV, hepatitis B virus.

Between 1 February 2022 and alert implementation on 1 February 2023, there was dedicated clinician-level education to support the goal of increasing HBV screening rates prior to a 2DR switch. Activities included presentations on general HBV complications and how to use the algorithm to stratify risk based on PWH’s HBV serologies. At departmental conferences, there was also education dedicated to increasing awareness of cases of HBV reactivation after switching to a 2DR. Prescribing patterns were monitored for 1 year after alert implementation (1 February 2023–1 February 2024) and compared with retrospective chart review data from 1 year prior to the intervention (1 February 2022–31 January 2023).

Patient Consent Statement

Our project was deemed a quality project by the Quality Improvement Committee in the Department of Medicine at Mount Sinai Hospital; thus, an institutional review board submission was not required. No factors were included necessitating patient consent.

Data

Data were collected as part of routine clinical practice. A dedicated internal data team for the IAM identified a list of eligible participants. For each of these, a manual review of clinic notes and laboratory data was conducted to confirm study eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data included the following: demographics (eg, age at time of regimen switch and self-identified gender); baseline clinical data (eg, CD4 count and HIV-1 RNA level from the most recent encounter prior to the switch to a 2DR, new 2DR selected, date of switch to a 2DR); HBV serology (HBsAg, HBsAb, and HBcAb) prior to switching to a 2DR and the date of HBV serology monitoring; and HBV serology (HBsAg, HBsAb, and HBcAb) after switching to a 2DR and the date of HBV serology monitoring. When quantitative HBsAb titers are performed, levels ≥10 mIU/mL are classified as HBsAb positive per our institution's laboratory cutoff values.

Participants were considered to have complete HBV serology if they had HBsAg, HBsAb, and HBcAb measured within 1 year prior to the 2DR switch. In contrast to the institutional guidance's original recommendation to check HBV serology 6 months prior to switching to a 2DR, the time interval utilized was 1 year, as this was ultimately found to be clinically practical given the frequency of HIV primary care visits for patients with well-controlled HIV. Participants were categorized by level of risk for HBV reactivation based on their HBV serology prior to switching to a 2DR. Per guidance, the switch to a 2DR was not recommended for PWH who were part of the composite group with either a positive HBsAg (considered the highest risk) or an isolated positive HBcAb (elevated risk).

Outcomes and Analytic Approach

To evaluate the impact of the alert, practice patterns were compared between the preimplementation period (1 February 2022–31 January 2023) and the postimplementation period (1 February 2023–1 February 2024). The overall objectives were to

Increase the percentage of individuals with complete HBV serologies checked within 1 year prior to a 2DR switch

Increase the percentage of individuals with complete HBV serologies checked within 1 year prior to a 2DR switch

Decrease the number of switches to a 2DR among the composite group who had either a positive HBsAg or an isolated positive HBcAb

Decrease the number of switches to a 2DR among the composite group who had either a positive HBsAg or an isolated positive HBcAb

Demographic data were analyzed by descriptive statistics. We used χ2 tests of independence to assess the impact of the intervention on the 2 outcomes. Due to small sample sizes in the composite group, Yates correction for continuity and Fisher exact probability test were utilized. Statistical significance was set as P < .05. Statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS version 29.0 (IBM).

RESULTS

During the intervention period, there were 9545 active patients and 55 542 clinic encounters annually across all clinic sites. Among the patients, 36% identified as Black, 19% as White, 5% as multiracial, and 2% as Asian; additionally, 34% reported their ethnicity as Latinx. In terms of insurance status, the majority had Medicaid (43%), followed by Medicare (23%), private insurance (19%), AIDS Drug Assistance Program (13%), and Medicare/Medicaid dual enrollment (2%).

An overall 501 were prescribed a 2DR between 1 February 2022 and 1 February 2024, and 306 met the study's inclusion criteria. The most common reason for exclusion was that the regimen was not a de novo switch to a 2DR (n = 170, 87.2%). Other exclusion criteria included <2 primary care visits (n = 15, 7.7%), a switch during inpatient admission (n = 6, 3.1%), and additional HBV coverage (n = 4, 2.0%).

Of the 306 study participants, 161 switched to a 2DR before alert implementation (1 February 2022–31 January 2023) and 145 after (1 February 2023–1 February 2024). In the study cohort, 81% identified as cisgender men, and the median age at the time of regimen switch was 49 years (Table 1). At the time of regimen switch, 96% had a baseline CD4 count ≥200 cells/mm3 and 72% had CD4 count ≥500 cells/mm3. The majority had an undetectable viral load (76%), and of the those with a detectable viral load (≥20 copies/mL), 71% had a baseline HIV-1 RNA between 20 and 200 copies/mL. During the study period, 78% switched to an oral 2DR regimen and 22% changed to a long-acting injectable (cabotegravir-rilpivirine).

HBV Screening Prior to 2DR Switch

The intervention's primary objective was to increase the number with complete HBV serologies obtained within 1 year prior to the 2DR switch. Before the alert, 30% (48/161) had HBV screening within 1 year prior to the 2DR switch. After alert implementation, significantly more participants (103/145, 71%) had complete HBV serologies within 1 year prior to the 2DR switch as compared with preimplementation (n = 48, 30%; P < .0001).

2DR Switches Among Individuals at Increased Risk for HBV Reactivation

The second objective was to decrease the number of 2DR switches among the composite group at increased risk of HBV reactivation. Individuals with a positive HBsAg were considered at highest risk, and those with an isolated positive HBcAb were considered at elevated risk. This analysis included participants with complete HBV serology obtained at any time before the 2DR switch. There were 260 individuals with complete HBV serology (126 preimplementation, 134 postimplementation).

Prior to the alert, 1 participant with a positive HBsAg (1/126, 0.79%) and 11 with an isolated positive HBcAb (11/126, 8.73%) switched to a 2DR; after the alert, there were no switches among those with a positive HBsAg and only 1 switch in a person with an isolated positive HBcAb (Table 2). In summary, after implementation of the alert, there were significantly fewer 2DR switches among the composite group at increased risk for HBV reactivation (pre- vs postimplementation, 12/126 [9.52%] vs 1/134 [0.75%]; P = .003).

During the study period, 13 participants who were considered to be at elevated risk for HBV reactivation switched to 2DRs (12 prealert, 1 postalert). The single person with a positive HBsAg had acutely elevated liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase, 169 IU/L) and detectable HBV DNA (32 IU/mL) within 2 months of the switch to cabotegravir-rilpivirine and was switched back to a fully HBV-active regimen. Among the 12 individuals with an isolated positive HBcAb, 8 had repeat HBV serologies, and 4 each had HBV DNA and liver enzymes checked after switching to a 2DR. While none of these participants had detectable HBV DNA or a positive HBsAg following the switch to 2DRs, 1 developed elevated transaminases.

Individuals were considered susceptible to HBV if they were HBsAb and HBcAb negative or had an isolated positive HBcAb. Of the 36 who were HBV susceptible during the study, 18 of 29 were vaccinated prealert and 7 of 16 were vaccinated postalert.

DISCUSSION

This QI project aimed to improve HBV screening rates and optimize individual selection for a switch to 2DRs among PWH. Integrating an EHR alert into 2DR orders resulted in a 37% increase in rates of complete HBV screening prior to the switch to a 2DR. The intervention also decreased the number of people at increased risk of HBV reactivation who switched to 2DRs without full HBV activity. Prior to the alert, 12 individuals (∼10%) who were at an elevated risk, including 1 with a positive HBsAg, switched to a 2DR, as compared with only 1 person (<1%) after implementation.

The study's findings of infrequent HBV serology monitoring at HIV primary care clinics within a large academic center before a dedicated intervention highlight the importance of an HBV screening intervention before switching to a 2DR. Before the intervention, only 30% had complete HBV screening within 1 year prior to switching to a 2DR. Furthermore, 22% did not have complete HBV serologies ever documented in our system's EHR preintervention, which is similar to the screening rate among US veterans with HIV [31]. These findings demonstrate the importance of increasing HBV screening through initiatives such as clinical decision tools and educational campaigns. We hope that our data encourage other clinics and centers to examine their HBV screening rates and institutional practices.

Our QI project recommended obtaining HBV serology within 1 year before switching to a 2DR. This is consistent with guidelines recommending repeat HBV screening before an ART switch, due to the possibility of a new infection since last screening or waning of HBsAb after vaccination [32–34]. In our cohort, 2% who had a positive HBsAb on initial screening lost their protective titers on subsequent checks. HBV serologies may be overlooked by clinicians, given long-standing use of tenofovir-based ART regimens, as well as the reduced response of PWH to older HBV vaccines [5, 35, 36]. With increasing use of 2DRs, there is a growing population of PWH who would benefit from vaccination, particularly with the availability of a highly effective cytosine phosphoguanine–adjuvanted HBV vaccine [37, 38]. Our findings highlight the importance of ongoing HBV screening and appropriate vaccination, particularly prior to switching to an ART regimen without full HBV activity [5, 39].

Since 2017, the Food and Drug Administration has approved multiple 2DRs without full HBV activity, and in response national guidelines have evolved to emphasize the importance of awareness of HBV serology when switching regimens. More research needs to be conducted on determining which PWH are optimal candidates to switch to 2DRs. A recent scoping review highlighted the lack of high-quality evidence on the risk of HBV reactivation after switching to ART without HBV activity [40]. Our study provides insight on real-life practice patterns in HBV screening and individual selection for 2DRs and evaluates the impact of a QI intervention across a large health system. To our knowledge, this is the first publication detailing the effects of implementing institutional guidance on HBV screening and patient selection for 2DRs using an EHR alert system in a large health system. This QI project demonstrates that an institutional guidance paired with an EHR intervention can lead to increased HBV screening rates and thus optimal selection of individuals to switch to a 2DR.

Since this intervention was developed, evidence has accumulated from large cohort studies demonstrating an overall low reactivation rate in those with an isolated positive HBcAb. In the Veterans Ageing Cohort Study, 1.6% of people with an isolated positive HBcAb without viremia had HBV reactivation after switching to a 2DR. However, reactivation risk significantly differed between those who never had a prior positive HBsAg (1%) and those with a prior positive HBsAg (20.2%) [3]. In a pooled analysis of 46 participants with an isolated positive HBcAb from the GEMINI-1/2, STAT, SALSA, and TANGO studies, there were no HBV reactivation events when individuals switched to dolutegravir-lamivudine [26]. In the OPERA study's cohort of 5343 PWH with 6252 tenofovir interruptions, the incidence of reactivation was 0.67 per 100 person-years for PWH with an isolated positive HBcAb and 0.08 for PWH with a positive HBsAb and HBcAb [13]. In response to these emerging data, our institutional guidance will evolve to support less restrictive recommendations in the future.

Another aspect of our guidance where we anticipate continued evolution is the optimal HBV monitoring strategy after a switch to 2DRs. PWH who switch to 2DRs are a heterogenous population with a variety of distinct risk factors for HBV reactivation (eg, immunosuppressive therapies, chronic liver disease) or acute HBV infection (eg, ongoing exposures, suboptimal vaccination response). The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases’ 2018 hepatitis B guidance recommends annual surveillance, particularly if there is a risk of ongoing exposure to HBV [41], and our guidance recommends annual serologies for all patients not undergoing HBV-active ART [41]. Further research is needed to identify an optimal and flexible monitoring strategy with innovative HBV markers (eg, hepatitis B core-related antigen) [42] after switching to 2DRs. At our institution, a multidisciplinary working group is reviewing and optimizing our guidance in response to the evolving literature.

There are several limitations to this study. For our pilot study, we linked the alert to the three 2DRs that represented the majority of non-HBV active 2DRs in our system, but we acknowledge that this does not account for all potential regimen combinations without optimal HBV activity. The study population was largely older (median age, 49 years), and the majority of patients had well-controlled HIV with CD4 >500 cells/mm3 and HIV-1 RNA <20 copies/mL. Across the IAM clinics, there are low rates of people who experience unstable housing and engage in active injection drug use. In the study cohort, there was a low prevalence of HBV infection, which may limit the external validity in settings with a higher incidence of acute HBV infection and/or increased prevalence of chronic HBV.

Several data points of interest could not be gathered due to the retrospective chart review methods of our study. We were not consistently able to discern from chart review the clinician's specific rationale for decisions around HBV screening, vaccination, or regimen switches. As such, our analysis may overlook important factors that affect clinical decision making around a 2DR switch. Moreover, we were not able to systematically gather clinician feedback to understand the experience of using the alert. Notably, after implementation of our alert, HBV screening was still absent in 29% of switches; we are uncertain if clinicians overrode the alert due to individual discretion, “alarm fatigue,” or other factors, which would be useful in designing future similar interventions.

In the study, the majority of regimen switches were to oral regimens (78%), although long-acting injectable ART is becoming increasingly common. In its 2024 recommendations on antiretroviral drugs for the treatment and prevention of HIV in adults, the International Antiviral Society–USA Panel states that long-acting cabotegravir-rilpivirine can be used for individuals with HIV viremia who are not able to consistently take oral ART and are at high risk for HIV disease progression [43]. This is another unique population in whom it will be important to monitor long-term HBV outcomes and develop tailored prevention and treatment strategies.

CONCLUSION

Clinicians may overlook the risk of acute HBV infection or reactivation during a switch to a 2DR without full HBV activity. In this QI project, a simple EHR alert increased the rate of HBV screening prior to a switch and decreased the number of 2DR switches among individuals at elevated risk of HBV reactivation (HBsAg positive or isolated positive HBcAb). With the rising popularity of 2DRs, optimizing individual selection is vital to prevent HBV-related complications.
