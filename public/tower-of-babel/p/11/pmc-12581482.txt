Estimating the burden of adult diabetes in China: a systematic review and meta-analysis with attributable risk assessment
Authors: Rui Wang, Shuoming Luo, Peng Wang, Meihui Yu, Zhenglu Liu, Jiabi Qin, Yang Xiao, Xia Li, Zhiguang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes represents a significant public health challenge in China. This comprehensive meta-analysis systematically investigated temporal patterns in diabetes prevalence and evaluated key modifiable risk factors among Chinese adults over a 20-year period (2000–2020).MethodsWe conducted a systematic search across six major electronic databases to identify population-based epidemiological studies reporting diabetes prevalence and/or associated modifiable risk factors. Eligible studies underwent rigorous quality assessment. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed to calculate pooled prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals. Subgroup analyses examined variations across critical demographic and geographic strata, including sex, age groups, urban-rural residence, geographic region, and body mass index (BMI) categories.ResultsA total of 109 studies involving over 1.74 million adults were included. The results showed that the overall combined prevalence of diabetes among Chinese adults was 9.51% (95% confidence interval: 8.73% -10.30%), corrected for standardization to 8.68% (95% CI: 7.80% - 9.57%) from 2000 to 2020. Prevalence was higher in males (10.19%), urban/suburban residents (10.44%), older adults (≥ 60 years: 19.27%), and regions like Chongqing Municipality (15.74%), Sichuan province (15.40%), and Henan province (15.16%). Higher BMI was associated with increased prevalence (18.84 %, BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2). Key modifiable risk factors included nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD, OR: 2.87), dyslipidemia (1.94), hypertension (1.84), and obesity (1.24). Among these major risk factors, central obesity had the highest attributable risk proportion (PAF = 20.9%), corresponding to approximately 20.28 million patients. ConclusionDiabetes prevalence among Chinese adults remains alarmingly high, with substantial heterogeneity linked to demographic characteristics and geographic location. NAFLD, hypertension, and specific dyslipidemia are the most established modifiable risk factors. Our findings underscore an urgent public health imperative for implementing regionally tailored strategies and evidence-based interventions addressing these risk factors to mitigate the expanding diabetes burden in China.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24626-8.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, Prevalence, Risk factors, China, Adult population, Epidemiology

Background

Diabetes has become a major public health concern globally, with its prevalence rising over the past decades [1–3]. According to the IDF Diabetes Atlas 2025, there were 589 million adults aged 20–79 living with diabetes worldwide in 2024, accounting for 11.1% of the global population in that age group. The total number of people with diabetes is projected to rise to 853 million by 2050 [4]. A recent pooled analysis confirmed these trends, showing that the prevalence of diabetes continues to rise globally from 1990 to 2022, with significant differences between regions [5]. The prevalence of diabetes is of great concern in a country like China with a large population and rapid socio-economic development.

National surveys have shown that the prevalence of diabetes in China has risen dramatically from 5.5% in 2000 to 12.8% in 2017 [6–10], highlighting the urgent need for in-depth research on this escalating health problem. While these surveys provide valuable insights into the overall prevalence, in-depth analysis of regional differences, demographic changes, and specific risk factors contributing to the diabetes epidemic is still lacking. Therefore, it is important to analyze risk factors for diabetes prevalence.

The aim of this research is to synthesize the available evidence on the prevalence of diabetes in Chinese adults from 2000 to 2020 and to identify modifiable risk factors that contribute to its development. By pooling data from different studies from different regions and population groups, this meta-analysis attempts to reveal temporal trends, regional differences, etc., in the prevalence of diabetes in China. In addition, by examining modifiable risk factors such as physical activity, obesity, and smoking and drinking behaviors, it strives to provide actionable insights for policy makers, healthcare providers, and public health practitioners to develop targeted interventions to curb the diabetes epidemic. This study will help to provide a scientific basis for prevention and management by delving into the pathogenesis of the disease and its associated factors.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

The national and provincial and regional level representative data were mainly obtained through literature search and literature tracing method, covering all relevant historical information from January 1, 2000 to December 31, 2020. Literature from Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane, PubMed, Chinese Journal Full Text Database (CNKI), Wanfang Data Resource System (Wanfang Data), and Wipu Database (CQVIP) were systematically searched to collect national and provincial and district level studies on the prevalence of diabetes and its influencing factors. This process was independently executed and checked and verified by three researchers, and finally summarized to form a literature search database, and the detailed search strategy is shown in eTable 1 and eTable 2.

Literature was initially screened by the title and abstract to determine whether it met the exclusion criteria, and for those that could not be definitively determined based on title and abstract, the full text was read further to determine inclusion. When multiple literatures reported on the same study, we prioritized those that reported earlier and were more comprehensive. For studies in ethnic minority areas, such as the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, we paid special attention to those reports with the most complete coverage of ethnic groups. This selection process was carried out independently by two researchers, and any disagreement would be discussed by a third researcher who would join in the discussion to reach agreement, and the information was then collated and summarized to form the final database for document analysis. Inclusion Criteria: (1) The study population originates from Chinese individuals; (2) Participants are aged 18 and above; (3) The primary research objective includes either the prevalence of diabetes or factors influencing diabetes; (4) Literature with original data is considered; (5) Clear reporting of study design and survey timing is required; (6) A clear reporting of the diagnostic criteria and methods for diabetes is essential; (7) For literature concerning prevalence, national representative studies must include a sample size of at least 3,600 individuals to adequately represent the national level during the same period; provincial representative studies must include a sample size of at least 2,300 individuals, capable of adequately representing a specific province or direct-controlled municipality; (8) The prevalence must be clearly reported or can be straightforwardly calculated; (9) Clear reporting of diagnostic criteria and methods for factors influencing diabetes is necessary. The determination is based on blood sample testing; (10) National representative studies must report prevalence by gender and age groups; (11) Only studies published in Chinese or English.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Studies on prevalence not aimed at the general population, e.g., people undergoing physical examination, specific professions, outpatient or inpatient hospital patients, minority ethnic groups, or specific rural areas; (2) Prevalence studies at the provincial level with fewer than three sampling points; (3) Self-reported prevalence rates; (4) Prevalence studies that are case-control studies; (5) Reviews, conference reports, theses, reports, announcements, etc.; (6) Duplicate publications; (7) Studies in other languages.

In order to ensure the reliability and relative accuracy of the fitted prevalence rates, the minimum requirements for sample size inclusion were set with reference to the sample size formula based on the opinions of the expert group and the epidemiological and statistical experts of the writing group, and the detailed calculation process is described in eMethods.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Three authors independently extracted the following data from each article using a standardized form: first author, year of investigation, year of publication, region, sample size, diagnostic method, diagnostic criteria, age range, and number of people surveyed, with inconsistencies discussed by the third author. If the prevalence of diabetes was reported separately according to different diagnostic criteria in the same article, the article was divided into multiple studies and the raw data were extracted.

The year of investigation was divided into four groups: 2000–2004, 2005–2009, 2010–2014, and 2015–2020, and when the year of investigation of the literature crossed groups, it was assigned to the group in which the majority of the literature was located at the time; the educational level was divided into two groups: junior high school and below, and senior high school and above, the latter includes vocational high school or technical secondary school; the age of the prevalence was divided into three groups: < 40 years old, 40 ~ 59 years old, and ≥ 60 years old; and the body mass index (BMI) was divided into four groups for Chinese criteria (< 18.5 kg/m2, 18.5–23.9 kg/m2, 24–27.9 kg/m2, and ≥ 28 kg/m2) and three groups for World Health Organization (WHO) criteria (< 25 kg/m2, 25–29.9 kg/m2, and ≥ 30 kg/m2). Both were combined within each age group in each study, and then combined across studies. To account for demographic heterogeneity across studies, we prioritized the use of standardized prevalence estimates (e.g., age- or sex-standardized) wherever available. Crude prevalence was only used when standardized data were not reported. For the national estimates, we recalculated pooled prevalence using only standardized data to reduce bias. See eTable 4 for details of the survey area subdivisions.

Three authors independently assessed risk of bias for cross-sectional studies using the JBI scale (Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Tools) and for cohort studies using the NOS (Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for cohort studies). See eTable 5 and eTable 6 for details on scoring.

Risk factor attribution analysis

To assess the impact of risk factors on the burden of diabetes among Chinese adults from 2000 to 2020, this study used the Levin formula to calculate the population attributable fraction (PAF). The combined odds ratios (OR) from the 2000–2020 meta-analysis were used as an approximation for relative risk (RR). Exposure rate data were obtained from the ‘China Chronic Disease and Risk Factor Surveillance Report 2018,’ [11] which was based on a nationally representative sample and reflected the population exposure levels of risk factors. Due to data availability constraints, the attributable analysis was conducted for five risk factors with nationally representative exposure rates: hypertension, central obesity, hypertriglyceridaemia, hypercholesterolaemia, and low HDL-C.

Diabetes prevalence rates were derived from the pooled rates obtained from the meta-analysis conducted in this study for the period 2000–2020. The estimated total adult population was based on the average of data from the fifth (2000) [12], sixth (2010) [13], and seventh (2020) [14] national population censuses for individuals aged 18 years and older, approximately 1.021 billion people. The number of attributable cases for each risk factor was calculated accordingly (unit: million).

Statistical analysis

To study time trends in the prevalence of diabetes in adults across the Provinces or Autonomous Regions, we estimated and plotted trends in diabetes prevalence over time using linear model (LM) fitting and locally weighted scatter smoothing (LOESS) fitting based on annual prevalence, respectively, with the number of respondents in each study as weights. We used I2 to assess the heterogeneity of prevalence estimates in the study: a fixed-effects model was used if I2 < 50% and P ≥ 0.1, and a random-effects model was used otherwise. Because of the high heterogeneity (as expected and observed), a random-effects (DerSimonian and Laird method) meta-analysis was used in this study to calculate the overall pooled prevalence and 95% CIs.

Given the substantial heterogeneity (I²>50%) observed, a random-effects model was applied. The DerSimonian and Laird method was chosen for its widespread use in prevalence meta-analyses and comparability with previous national and global diabetes studies. Sensitivity analyses using the Hartung-Knapp method for a subset of data produced similar pooled estimates, supporting the robustness of our results.

Subgroup analyses were performed for gender, urban and rural areas, education background, BMI, age, and region, and sensitivity analyses were performed using the one-by-one exclusion method to determine the stability of the results. Egger’s test and visual inspection of funnel plots were used to characterize publication bias for ≥ 10 studies, and a P > 0.05 suggests that the likelihood of publication bias is low.

Results

Study characteristics

From 2000 to 2020, a total of 76 articles totaling 174,1668 subjects were included in the literature related to the prevalence of diabetes in adults in China including 21 provinces, 2 autonomous regions, and 4 municipalities directly under the central government. The national level diabetes prevalence survey finally included 11 articles; 74 representative diabetes prevalence surveys at the provincial level in China were selected for inclusion, excluding duplicate surveys and insufficient samples, and 64 articles were included in the analyses, of which no available effective data for Shanxi province, Qinghai province, Ningxia Hui Autonomous Regions, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Regions, and Tibet Autonomous Regions. Hong Kong and Macao Special Administrative Regions did not search for relevant reports, and Taiwan Diabetology in Taiwan province of China published the Taiwan Diabetes Yearbook (2019) Type 2 Diabetes in October 2019, but due to the type of article does not match, it was not included in the analysis.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

By using the visual inspection method of funnel plots, the initial analysis showed possible stability issues. To further validate this, a sensitivity analysis of the data was performed using a drop-by-study method. The results of this method showed that the estimates of combined prevalence did not change significantly after excluding any single study, thus confirming the stability of the analysis. In addition, to assess potential publication bias, we performed an Egger’s regression test. The result of the Egger’s test was t = 0.84, P = 0.403, suggesting no significant publication bias. In summary, we concluded that the results of this study were reliable and stable, see eFigure 7.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Through stepwise exclusion method for sensitivity analysis, the results indicate that there is no significant change in the combined prevalence, suggesting that the findings are relatively stable. Publication bias was described using Egger’s test and visual inspection of funnel plots for studies with ≥ 10 articles, and publication bias was present at P < 0.05. In this study, Egger’s test was performed on 2 influential factors, hypertension and central obesity, and the results showed that P = 0.790 for hypertension and P = 0.347 for central obesity, suggesting that there was a low likelihood of publication bias, see eFigure 7 and eFigure 8.

Attributable risk analysis results

According to the analysis, the standardised prevalence of diabetes among adults in China from 2000 to 2020 was 9.51%, corresponding to approximately 97.1 million patients. Among the five major risk factors, central obesity had the highest attributable risk proportion (PAF = 20.9%), corresponding to approximately 20.28 million patients; followed by hypertension (PAF = 18.8%, 18.22 million) and hypertriglyceridaemia (PAF = 14.2%, 13.79 million). Low HDL cholesterol and hypercholesterolaemia were attributed to 8.52 million and 2.78 million diabetes cases, respectively. See eFigure 9.

Discussion

The high prevalence of diabetes

This study conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence data of diabetes, aiming to provide insights into the status of diabetes within the global context.The results showed that the total prevalence of diabetes among Chinese adults from 2000 to 2020 was 9.51%, and the standardized prevalence was 8.68%. Notably, this figure is comparatively high when compared to the global prevalence data from the IDF Diabetes Atlas 2025. This high prevalence not only poses a significant health challenge within China but also contributes to the global diabetes epidemic.

A study by Liu et al. found a 102.9% increase in diabetes cases from 1990 to 2017 and an increase in the age-standardized incidence rate during this period. According to a recent study by Xu et al., [10] China accounts for 22% of the world’s diabetes cases, and the prevalence has risen significantly since 1980, reflecting the increasing prevalence of diabetes globally [15]. A global study further confirmed this association, showing that from 1990 to 2021, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children and adolescents increased significantly, and this trend is projected to continue until 2050, which may further exacerbate the diabetes epidemic in the future [16]. In the present study, we found that the prevalence of diabetes in China during the past two decades has broadly an increasing trend by linear model fitting analysis, consistent with the findings of Liu et al. and in line with the global pattern. Meanwhile, the results of the subgroup analysis of the survey time further confirmed the increasing trend of diabetes prevalence. However, it is worth noting that when we analyzed the data using the LOESS model, we noticed a more moderate increase or even a slight decrease in prevalence between 2007 and 2010. The exact reason for this phenomenon remains unclear and might be attributed to various factors such as improved screening and management strategies or changes in lifestyle patterns.

Interestingly, we found that there were significant differences in the prevalence among provinces. In particular, prevalence rates exceeded 15% in Chongqing Municipality (15.74%), Sichuan province (15.40%), and Henan province (15.16%), with Guangdong province having the lowest prevalence (2.80%). This difference may be related to dietary habits: the diets of Chongqing and Sichuan are characterized by spiciness, high salt, sugar and oil, which may increase the risk of diabetes [17]. Comparatively, the diets in Guangdong province are light, and the high consumption of seafood and vegetables may help reduce the prevalence of diabetes. Unhealthy dietary habits, such as high-fat and high-sugar diets, have been shown to be directly associated with a high prevalence of diabetes [18–20]. In addition, a study by AlEssa et al. [21] found through a meta-analysis that high levels of fruit and vegetable intake significantly reduced the risk of diabetes. In contrast, Henan Province is is a major agricultural and grain producing province, and its diet is dominated by rice or pasta rich in carbohydrates. Some researchers have analyzed the relationship between carbohydrate intake and the prevalence of diabetes, particularly in a large European population, and showed that high carbohydrate intake may increase the risk of diabetes development [22]. These findings imply that the carbohydrate-rich dietary habits may contribute significantly to its local diabetes prevalence.

At the same time, we conducted a subgroup analysis of the prevalence in the surveyed regions, and the results showed that the prevalence of diabetes was the highest (12.05%) in Central China including Hubei, Hunan, and Henan Provinces. Central China has a larger population, which may lead to a faster pace of life, and a diet structure that may be skewed towards high-sugar and high-fat foods; while the prevalence of diabetes in South China was the lowest (5.67%). South China includes Guangdong Province, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, and Hainan Province, which may rely more on healthy diets such as seafood, vegetables, and fruits. While dietary habits likely contribute to regional variation in diabetes prevalence, these differences are also shaped by socioeconomic factors (e.g., provincial GDP, urbanization), healthcare access (including screening availability), obesity rates, and public awareness. These contextual factors may influence both the true prevalence and detection rates, and their absence from quantitative analysis represents an important limitation.

According to the subgroup analysis, the prevalence of diabetes in males was 10.19%, which was slightly higher than that of females at 9.01%. Generally speaking, men have heavier burdens than women, need to earn more money to maintain the family, are busy with work, have more social activities, often stay up late and work overtime, and have less sleep, which are prone to insulin resistance and diabetes.

The prevalence of diabetes is higher in urban areas than that in rural areas, which may be related to lifestyle factors. Urban dwellers have a higher risk of diabetes due to longer sedentary time, less physical activity, and more stressful lives and jobs. Sedentary behaviour may lead to insulin resistance [23], and chronic stress affects glucose regulation by activating the the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system to elevate cortisol levels [24]. In addition, the relatively lower prevalence of diabetes among those with higher education may be related to their higher health awareness, better occupational and economic conditions, easier access to medical resources and healthier lifestyle choices.

In the results of the subgroup analysis, we also observed that the higher the BMI, the higher the prevalence of diabetes. The association between high BMI and the prevalence of diabetes may be due to multiple mechanisms. First, a high BMI is often accompanied by an increase in body fat, especially abdominal fat. These adipose tissues release substances such as free fatty acids, inflammatory factors, and other signaling molecules associated with insulin action that may interfere with insulin function [25]. Free fatty acids enhance insulin resistance by interfering with insulin signaling pathways such as the IRS-1 and PI3K pathways [26, 27].

In addition, obese states are accompanied by low-grade inflammation, and inflammatory factors released from adipose tissue may increase insulin resistance [28–30]. Obesity is also associated with other metabolic abnormalities such as high blood pressure, high triglycerides, and low HDL cholesterol levels, which constitute the metabolic syndrome and increase the risk of diabetes [31]. Similarly, our meta-analysis of diabetes risk factors identified central obesity and generalized overweight or obesity as risk factors for diabetes. Central obesity may mechanistically affect insulin resistance more directly. The main reason for this is that free fatty acids and inflammatory factors released from visceral adipose tissue directly affect insulin sensitivity in the liver [32]. In contrast, widespread overweight and obesity may affect insulin resistance primarily indirectly through generalized adiposity and altered cell size [33].

Meanwhile, we have found that the prevalence of diabetes tends to increase significantly with age. This phenomenon can be attributed to a number of factors, including changes in the body’s metabolism, insulin resistance, and loss of muscle mass. In addition, researchers at Yale University found that the activity of a gene in mitochondria decreases with age, which may lead to mitochondrial damage and increase the risk of developing diabetes [34]. Enhancing the activity of this gene in older mice prevented mitochondrial damage and insulin resistance.

Modifiable risk factors for diabetes

In this study, we identified multiple modifiable risk factors that are strongly associated with the development of diabetes in Chinese adults, including hypertension (OR = 1.84), central obesity (OR = 1.75), overweight and obesity (OR = 1.24), dyslipidemia (OR = 1.94), hypertriglyceridemia (OR = 1.90), hypercholesterolemia (OR = 1.36), low HDL-C (OR = 1.46), snoring ((OR = 1.13), and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (OR = 2.87). These factors interact through complex physiologic and metabolic mechanisms to influence the onset and progression of diabetes.

Specifically, sympathetic nerve activity may increase in hypertensive states, leading to elevated levels of catecholamines in the body. This can affect insulin release and tissue sensitivity to insulin, which can lead to insulin resistance and diabetes [35]. In addition, hypertension may lead to disturbances in intracellular calcium homeostasis, affecting insulin signaling and intracellular metabolism. This may exacerbate insulin resistance and diabetes.

The risk factors we analyzed also included dyslipidemia, such as high triglycerides, high total cholesterol, and low HDL-C. Elevated levels of high triglycerides and low HDL-C are strongly associated with increased inflammatory factors, which directly affect insulin function and pancreatic beta cell health [36]. Although all of these dyslipidemias increase insulin resistance, the mechanisms that lead to the development of diabetes are not identical: high triglyceride and cholesterol levels may cause direct toxic damage to pancreatic β-cells [37–40], whereas low HDL-C affects pancreatic β-cell function indirectly, mainly by decreasing cholesterol efflux capacity (CEC) [41], which is itself associated with decreased pancreatic β-cell function [42]. Overall, these dyslipidemias affect insulin sensitivity through different metabolic pathways and increase the risk of diabetes. Therefore, maintaining normal lipid levels is important for the prevention of diabetes.

NAFLD leading to diabetes mainly involves insulin resistance, lipid metabolism disorders and inflammatory response. Insulin resistance is not only one of the key mechanisms of NAFLD, but also a major factor in the development of diabetes. Disorders of lipid metabolism lead to accumulation of fat in the liver, which in turn triggers inflammation, and these inflammatory responses may further worsen insulin resistance [43]. In addition, excessive accumulation of fat in the liver leads to insulin resistance that is not limited to the liver itself, but may also affect muscle and adipose tissues, thus increasing the risk of developing diabetes. Similarly, the presence of diabetes increases the risk of developing liver disease [44]. Thus, NAFLD can be considered an important driver of the development of diabetes, as well as one of its potential consequences.

Snoring is strongly associated with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), which causes obstruction of the airway, interferes with sleep quality, and may trigger overactivity of the sympathetic nervous system [45]. Studies have shown that OSA is an important risk factor for insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, which affects glucose homeostasis through multiple mechanisms [46]. Snoring may directly interfere with the normal release and action of insulin, while chronic hypoxemia may impair insulin secretory function and intracellular metabolism, increasing the risk of diabetes [47]. In addition, reduced sleep quality due to snoring also affects insulin sensitivity. Studies have confirmed that poor sleep quality and short sleep duration are associated with a higher incidence of diabetes [48, 49].

Of note, based on our analysis of the literature included so far, physical inactivity has not been found to be a risk factor for the prevalence of diabetes at this time. The relationship between physical activity (PA) and diabetes has been of interest to researchers, with a large number of studies demonstrating positive ameliorative effects of PA on type 2 diabetes [50, 51]. We consider that the present study failed to adequately analyze the association between physical inactivity and diabetes, and that this shortcoming stems primarily from the limited amount of literature, and the high heterogeneity.

Additionally, family history of diabetes, prolonged sedentary behavior, and clinical sleep disorders (e.g., insomnia) are recognized risk factors not comprehensively addressed here due to inconsistent reporting across studies. No evidence of a direct association between smoking, alcohol consumption, magnesium intake, and H. pylori infection and diabetes has been found through combined analysis. Due to insufficient data from existing studies, we are unable to assert the influence of these factors on diabetes for the time being. Future research should standardize metrics for these factors to clarify their attributable risk in the Chinese population.

Population attributable risk analysis

In addition to examining the pooled prevalence and risk ratios, our study estimated the PAFs of five major metabolic risk factors using the Levin formula. The results suggest that central obesity, hypertension, and hypertriglyceridemia were the dominant contributors to the national diabetes burden, together accounting for more than half of the attributable cases. These findings reinforce the need for targeted public health interventions that address these risk factors as a priority. Although the exposure rates were derived from the 2018 China Chronic Disease and Risk Factor Surveillance report, which may not represent the entire 2000–2020 period, they still provide a robust midpoint estimate due to the large national sample and the typically slow-changing nature of chronic disease risk factors in adults. However, the PAFs calculation included only five risk factors with available national exposure data, excluding other relevant risks like physical inactivity and NAFLD due to data limitations.

Based on our findings and the extensive discussion above, it is clear that the prevalence of diabetes in China is on the rise, a trend that underscores the urgency of adopting preventive measures and effective management strategies to combat diabetes. These include preventing the onset of the disease by promoting a healthy diet, increasing physical activity, and maintaining a healthy body weight, as well as raising public awareness of diabetes risk factors through public health campaigns and educational programs. Early detection and timely intervention are key to controlling the condition of diabetes.Specific measures should prioritize salt and oil reduction policies in high-prevalence provinces such as Sichuan and Henan, and Chongqing Municipality; strengthen annual oral glucose tolerance test screening for the elderly population; and address modifiable risk factors closely associated with diabetes including incorporation NAFLD into diabetes risk assessment models. These comprehensive measures are expected to mitigate the rising prevalence of diabetes.

These results align with China’s national health strategies, including the Healthy China 2030 Plan’s emphasis on chronic disease prevention and the National Comprehensive Chronic Disease Prevention and Control Plan’s targets for diabetes management. Our identification of central obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia as priority modifiable factors provides actionable leverage points for these initiatives.

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several strengths.This study is the first time to clearly determine the quantitative risk ratio of diabetes modifiable risk factors. Furthermore, by estimating PAFs, this study provides a novel quantification of how risk factors contribute to the national diabetes burden. By examining factors like obesity, hypertension, and others, it provides insights crucial for public health policy making and preventive measures, ultimately enhancing societal health. The detailed findings contribute valuable data and insights to medicine and epidemiology, guiding future research and facilitating more targeted approaches to diabetes prevention and management.

However, several limitations should be noted. Firstly, the adoption of the ADA 2010 criteria, which incorporated HbA1c ≥ 6.5% as a diagnostic standard, may have influenced prevalence estimates compared with the WHO 1999 criteria based solely on fasting plasma glucose and/or oral glucose tolerance test. HbA1c testing facilitates detection of undiagnosed diabetes in individuals with stable hyperglycemia but may miss cases with isolated postprandial hyperglycemia, potentially leading to differences in prevalence. In earlier years (pre-2010), limited HbA1c availability likely resulted in underestimation of true.

Secondly, because the PAF analysis used a classical single-factor Levin approach, it did not account for overlaps or interactions between risk factors. This limitation means that the true joint impact of multiple co-occurring risk factors may be underestimated, while the sum of individual PAFs may overestimate the total burden. Future analyses should consider multivariable or joint modeling methods to more accurately capture the combined effects of correlated risk factors. The PAF analysis used exposure utilized 2018 risk factor exposure rates, which may not reflect changes in risk factor prevalence across the 2000–2020 period. This static estimate may result in under- or overestimation of the attributable burden in earlier or later years.

Thirdly, some subgroup analyses and certain risk factors (e.g., NAFLD, low HDL-C) in this study were based on a limited number of studies or small sample sizes, which may reduce statistical power and increase uncertainty in the pooled estimates. In addition, substantial heterogeneity was observed in several analyses, likely reflecting differences in study design, sampling strategies, diagnostic criteria, and population characteristics. These factors may affect the precision and generalizability of our subgroup findings, and thus the results should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

This study comprehensively analyzed the prevalence and key risk factors of diabetes in Chinese adults from 2000 to 2020. The results showed that the prevalence of diabetes among Chinese adults from 2000 to 2020 was 9.51%, and showed a rising trend year by year. The prevalence of diabetes varied significantly among different provinces and was related to regional dietary habits. Overweight and obesity, hypertension, high triglyceride and cholesterol levels, low HDL-C, dyslipidemia, snoring, and NAFLD were identified as important and common factors that increase the risk of diabetes. This study highlights the importance of individualized prevention strategies and provides a scientific basis for public health policies, especially in the development of diabetes prevention and management measures. These findings promote a deeper understanding of the epidemiology of diabetes and provide direction for optimizing public health interventions.
