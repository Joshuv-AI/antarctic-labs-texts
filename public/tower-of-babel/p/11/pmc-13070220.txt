In Vivo Effect of a Synthetic Amniotic Fluid on Fetal Lung and Gastrointestinal Tract: A Pre‐Clinical Rodent Model
Authors: Braxton Forde, Stephanie Finoti, Marc Oria, Jose L. Peiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAmnioinfusions in anhydramnios aim to promote fetal lung development, but currently used fluids (Normal Saline [NS], Lactated Ringer's [LR]) fail to mimic the intrauterine environment and increase reactive oxygen species (ROS). We developed a synthetic amniotic fluid (Amnio‐well, AW) designed to reduce intrauterine ROS. This study evaluated the pulmonary and gastrointestinal effects of 2 formulations of AW compared with those of NS and LR in a pre‐clinical model.MethodAt gestational age E17.5, pregnant rats underwent amniotic fluid replacement with NS, LR, AW, AW plus epidermal growth factor and transforming growth factor‐β (AW++), or sham control. Fetal lungs were harvested at E20.5 for histology, fractional airspace, and blinded pathological evaluation. Surfactant protein (SP‐A, SP‐B, SP‐C) expression and inflammatory gene panels were assessed in lungs and gastrointestinal (GI) tissue.ResultsNS and LR lungs demonstrated edema, macrophage infiltration, and reduced airspace (p < 0.001). AW improved SP‐B and SP‐C relative to control, whereas AW++ suppressed SP‐B and SP‐C (p < 0.05). Lung gene profiling showed NS/LR induced alterations in histamines, annexins, and immune recruitment, while AW closely resembled control. GI histology was similar across groups, though NS/LR altered TNF, prostaglandin, and adhesion pathways (p < 0.05).ConclusionAW reduced lung inflammation and enhanced surfactant expression compared with NS or LR, with minimal GI effects.

Introduction

Serial amnioinfusions to achieve pulmonary development in the setting of in‐utero renal failure with resultant oligo/anhydramnios are controversial. Although success can be achieved, the complication rate is higher than other minimally invasive procedures with similar access [1, 2, 3]. Furthermore, serial infusions with commercially available fluids (Normal Saline [NS], and Lactated Ringer's [LR]) miss the mark in achieving the optimal outcome. For example, Miller et al. reported in the RAFT trial that 71% of patients require intubation within the first 48 h of life and 29% of survivors have pneumothoraxes [1]. Thus, while the lungs can grow secondary to amnioinfusions, they may not achieve normal function.

The reasons for the increased complication rate specific to amnioinfusions and the suboptimal lung growth and function are numerous and clinical factors are likely to play a role. For example, performance of the procedure in the setting of anhydramnios significantly increases the surgical complexity, and the risk of complications (including PPROM, preterm labor, chorion‐amnion separation, and placental abruption) that prevent additional amnioinfusions throughout an entire pregnancy cannot be discounted. However, one factor with emerging evidence may also be that the difference in composition between the infused fluids and normal amniotic fluid (Table 1) leads to a suboptimal response. Further supporting this is that amnioinfusions at the time of fetal surgery have been reported to be associated with adverse outcomes [4, 5]. Further, human amniotic membrane exposure to NS and LR leads to reactive oxygen species (ROS)‐mediated injury, triggering apoptosis in vitro and in tissue explants [6]. In response, our lab developed a novel synthetic amniotic fluid (Table 1) [6]. This artificial amniotic fluid, termed “Amnio‐well,” successfully prevented ROS‐mediated damage in vitro. Subsequent in vivo studies reproduced these findings [7]. Given the significant role of the amniotic fluid in the development of the fetal lung and gastrointestinal tract, we hypothesized that a similar inflammation pattern could affect these organ systems. To investigate this, we conducted an in vivo study of amniotic fluid exchange with either NS or LR compared with formulations of a synthetic amniotic fluid, with subsequent evaluation of lung and GI samples. We hypothesized that there would be less inflammation in the synthetic amniotic fluid samples compared with NS and LR.

Methods

Experimental Design and Animal Model

As described previously [7], wild type pregnant rats underwent a mini laparotomy to complete amniotic fluid exchange of selected gestational sacs on E17.5. Pregnancy was confirmed by the presence of vaginal smear, with the first day after that E0 and delivery expected on E21. Rats were all harvested on the morning of E20.5, prior to the onset of labor. This gestational age window was selected based upon rodent‐specific developmental milestones and their relevance to human development. Rats are born in the saccular phase of lung development (whereas humans completely complete the saccular phase before 37 weeks). E17.5–20.5 would reflect the late canalicular phase through the saccular phase of lung development, which stretches across the corresponding human timepoints for lung development in which amnioinfusions occur. There was one control group (fetuses from pregnancies that did not undergo amniotic fluid exchange but did undergo maternal mini‐laparotomy), and four treatment groups: complete amniotic fluid exchange with either 1. NS 2. LR 3. Synthetic amniotic fluid #1 (AW), 4. Synthetic amniotic fluid #2 (AW++). This study received institutional animal care and use committee approval (IACUC 2022–0078) and best humane practices were used in all care.

Composition of Fluid Selections

Hospital grade NS and LR were used. Two versions of a synthetic amniotic fluid were used (AW and AW++), with the full composition listed in Table 1. In brief, AW was pH, electrolyte, protein and glucose matched to amniotic fluid. Additionally, known antioxidants and protease inhibitors ascorbic acid, zinc and urinary trypsin inhibitor were added at the highest normal physiological concentrations. These concentrations were determined in prior in vitro work to be optimal for mitigation of tissue injury [6] and have been implicated in the prevention of preterm prelabor rupture of membranes or preterm birth [8, 9]. Amnio acids known to reduce oxidative stress and/or promote fetal lung and gastrointestinal tract development were added (taurine, glutamine, l‐arginine) [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. As for AW++, the composition was the same as AW, with the addition of epidermal growth factor (EGF) and transforming growth factor beta (TGF‐b) at high physiological concentrations (200 pg/mL and 80 pg/mL) [15, 16]. These two growth factors were selected because of their association with fetal development and inverse correlation with adverse pregnancy outcomes [16, 17].

Histologic Examination of Lung and Gastrointestinal Tract

Fetal lungs were harvested immediately after euthanasia and prepared for histological analysis using a standardized volume inflation–fixation protocol. After opening the thoracic cavity, the trachea was cannulated with a 24‐gauuge needle. Lungs were slowly inflated with 50 μL of 4% paraformaldehyde using a fixed volume appropriate for gestational age and delivered manually. The same inflation volume and technique were applied uniformly across all experimental groups. Next, the lungs were carefully excised and immersed in fresh 4% paraformaldehyde for overnight fixation at 4°C. Tissues were then processed, paraffin embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm for hematoxylin and eosin staining. To reduce orientation bias and ensure representative sampling of the parenchyma, sections were obtained in both the vertical and horizontal planes through each lung, as recommended in quantitative lung morphometry literature. These specimens were then reviewed and graded by a clinical pathologist blinded to the treatment allocation. Lungs were graded based on the ATS‐recommended lung histology framework. For airspace analysis, digital whole‐field images of H&E‐stained sections were acquired at 10x and 20x magnification, focusing on parenchymal regions and avoiding large conducting airways, pleura, and major blood vessels. To ensure systematic and equivalent sampling across specimens, a grid‐based sampling approach was applied and non‐overlapping fields were selected at random. This approach ensured an even spatial distribution of sampled fields across the lung section and minimized selection bias.

Fractional airspace was quantified on fixed lung sections using a particle‐based thresholding workflow in Image J (v1.54). For each animal, fields of view were selected by a pre‐specified random sampling scheme from predefined parenchymal regions, excluding large airways and vessels. Images were acquired at identical magnification and illumination settings, converted to 8‐bit, and segmented using a single thresholding approach applied uniformly across all groups to classify airspace (luminal low‐intensity regions) versus non‐airspace (septa, interstitium, inflammatory cellularity, and intra‐alveolar material). Binary masks were generated and ImageJ “Analyze Particles” was used to identify contiguous airspace regions and compute the summed airspace area per field. Fractional airspace was calculated as the summed airspace area divided by total field area, averaged across fields to yield one value per animal. Because segmentation‐based metrics can be sensitive to edema/inflammatory remodeling and image thresholding choices, threshold parameters and sampling rules were fixed a priori and applied identically across groups, and analysis was performed blinded to experimental condition. This area‐based metric was prioritized because marked edema/inflammation can thicken septa and blur septal boundaries, increasing variability in intercept‐based measures such as mean linear intercept, whereas fractional airspace more directly reflects air versus non‐air compartment. The average fractional airspace was normalized to control samples, and the fractional airspace in treatment samples werecompared to control to assess the percent increase or decrease in lung airspace relative to control.

Regarding the GI tract, the fetal stomach was identified, the bowel was transected immediately distal to the pylorus and the entire intestine was removed en bloc with minimal manipulation. The bowel was laid flat on a moist surface, oriented from proximal (post‐pyloric) to distal, and prepared as a “Swiss‐roll” beginning at the proximal intestine immediately distal to the stomach. The completed roll was secured in orientation and immediately fixed in 4% PFA using a standardized fixation protocol for paraffin embedding and sectioning. Also, these gastrointestinal slides were reviewed by an institutional pathologist blinded to the group allocation. Because gross fetal landmarks can be difficult to standardize across specimens, anatomic location was assigned post hoc based on the histologic (pathologic) signature of each region, and each section was categorized as small bowel, cecum, or colon prior to scoring. The pathologist graded each tissue according to institutional standards, and all histologic assessments were performed blinded to the experimental group (control vs. treatment).

RNA Extraction and RT‐PCR

Harvested lungs were kept in liquid nitrogen while ground into a fine powder, which was then placed into a tube of Trizol. Serial application of chloroform along with centrifuging was used to separate the mixture into layers. The RNA layer was removed, and 2‐propanol was added along with centrifuging to produce pellets. Pellets were then washed in 70%EtOH and resuspended in RNase free water, with sample concentration and purity being determined by a NanoDrop One, Thermoscientific. RNA was then converted to cDNA via Qiagen RT2 Profiler. Each PCR plate was then set up with 8 μl of PCR grade water, 1 μl of the desired TaqMan Probe, 10 μl of Applied Biosystems TaqMan Fast PCR Master Mix, and 1 μl of cDNA. The plate was then run on a StepOnePlus Real‐Time PCR System from Applied Biosystems.

Surfactant Expression Comparison

As surfactant production is a critical final stage in lung maturation, RNA expression for surfactant production was assessed via reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RTqPCR) as described above. Surfactant protein (SP) A, B, and C expression were selected due to their role in lung homeostasis, with SP‐B and SP‐C used for production and spreading of surfactant and the maintenance of said surfactant reduced surface tension in the alveoli [18], and SP‐A in modulation of the innate immune system, particularly with regard to pregnancy [19, 20]. Given most of SP‐D's role in pathogen clearance postnatally as well as prior rat models showing inconsistent SP‐D activity [21, 22, 23], this was not evaluated. Relative fold expression between groups and control was calculated via the ΔΔCT method, and GAPDH and 18‐S were used as housekeeper genes. A total of 6 fetuses were used for each group, and each sample was run in duplicate.

Evaluation of Inflammatory Pathways

To further evaluate subtle and transcriptional signs of inflammation, activated with amnioinfusion with the various treatment groups, TaqMan Array Rat Inflammation (4414081) was used to evaluate 92 genes implicated in various inflammatory pathways in rodents. Fold changes in gene expression relative to control were compared via ANOVA, via Prism v10. In an attempt to minimize the risk of false discover rate and only identify relevant pathway alterations, two steps were taken. First, Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate methodology was planned. Briefly, p‐values were ranked in order of significance, and a critical value was then calculated based upon a predetermined level of significance and the number of tests performed. The critical value is then used to adjust the p‐value and subsequent p values are re‐ranked [24, 25]. To further minimize over‐powering, a fold‐change threshold of either 2‐fold upregulation or downregulation was also applied to ensure biological plausibility and relevance. Pathway analysis was planned using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. A total of 6 fetuses were used for each group, and each sample was run in duplicate.

Results

Histologic Examination of Fetal Lungs and Fractional Airspace

To first evaluate the hypothesis that fetal lungs are affected by the fluid used during amnio‐exchange, 8 random samples per fetus, from 8 different fetuses from each group, were sent for pathologic grading and evaluation. Histological grading of fetal lung specimens revealed no discernible differences among the Control, AW, and AW++ groups. However, both LR and NS exhibited pronounced interstitial inflammation, significant airway injury, and notable immune cell infiltration (Figure 1A). This corresponded with a significant reduction in fractional airspace, with LR at 87% ± 8% and NS at 89% ± 5% of control values (p < 0.05 for both). In contrast, no significant differences were observed between AW (98% ± 6%) and control or between AW++ (104% ± 10%) and control (Figure 1B).

(B) Fractional airspace differences between groups. There was a significantly lower percent airspace in NS and LR relative to control, and no difference was observed between control, AW, and AW++.

Histologic Examination of Fetal Gastrointestinal Tract

Similarly, to evaluate the hypothesis that the fetal gastrointestinal tract is affected by the fluid used during amniotic fluid exchange, samples from 8 fetuses per group were sent for pathological evaluation. Representative segments of the small bowel, large bowel, and cecum were noted in the samples and the pathologist did not note significant differences in crypt or villous architecture or any immune cell infiltration into the crypts in any sample.

Surfactant Expression Evaluation

Surfactant (particularly surfactant B/C) is critical in late gestation rat fetuses because it lowers alveolar surface tension, enabling effective gas exchange. Given the heightened respiratory needs in human fetuses undergoing serial amnioinfusions with NS and LR, we hypothesized that surfactant expression would be decreased in NS and LR, and equivalent in AW and AW++ to control. Upon evaluation of surfactant expression, significant differences were observed across all groups (Figure 2). For surfactant protein A (SP‐A), NS and LR groups exhibited substantial downregulation relative to control, with NS showing a fold change of 0.04 (95% CI 0.04–0.05) relative to control. Similarly, LR treated lungs showed a relative fold expression of 0.03 (95% CI 0.03–0.04) compared to control. In contrast, SP‐A expression in the AW group was equivalent to control (1.19‐fold change, 95% CI 0.99–1.35). AW++ demonstrated a reduction in SP‐A expression to control (0.77‐fold change, 95% CI 0.71–0.82). Surfactant protein B (SP‐B) followed a similar pattern, with approximately 20‐fold decreases in NS (0.05‐fold change, 95% CI 0.05–0.05) and LR (0.03‐fold‐change, 95% CI 0.03–0.03 in LR) relative to control. Interestingly, AW exhibited a slight but significant increase in SP‐B expression (1.62‐fold change, 95% CI 1.46–1.78), whereas AW++ showed significant decrease (0.26 fold‐change, 95% CI 0.25–0.28). Surfactant protein C (SP‐C) expression showed a similar trend to that of SP‐B, with extreme downregulation in NS (0.06‐fold change, 95% CI 0.06–0.07) and LR (0.05‐fold change, 95% CI 0.05–0.05) treated lungs relative to control. Again, AW demonstrated upregulation (2.26‐fold change, 95% CI 2.48–2.07), and AW++ showed downregulation (0.71‐fold change, 95% CI 0.63–0.79).

Inflammatory Pathways in the Fetal Lungs

Becauss of the short interval between amnio‐exchange and harvest, we sought to evaluate more subtle findings of inflammation, which might act as precursors to structural signs of injury. Thus, gene expression analysis was performed and revealed distinct transcriptional changes across groups, with most pronounced alterations observed in NS and LR. After false discovery adjustments, NS exhibited the greatest number of differentially expressed genes, with 22 genes upregulated and 16 downregulated (p < 0.05 for all, Table 2, Table S1). LR was next, with 14 genes upregulated and 18 downregulated (p < 0.05 for all, Table 2, Table S1). AW ++ displayed moderate changes, with 9 genes upregulated and 2 downregulated. AW showed the fewest changes, with 6 genes upregulated and only 1 downregulated (p < 0.05 for all, Table 2, Table S1). Both NS and LR induced changes in the annexin family, the histamine receptor complex, and the phospholipase family, all pointing to degradation in membrane integrity and increased cellular permeability (Table 3). Additionally, both groups displayed upregulation of COX‐2‐prostaglanding pathway and downregulation of COX‐1 pathway, indicative of a pro‐inflammatory state. AW++ did not exhibit changes in membrane integrity but did show similar COX‐2/COX‐1 pathway alterations suggesting an inflammatory response (Table 3).

Inflammatory Pathways of Interest in the Fetal Gastrointestinal Tract

To evaluate the possibility of similar subtle changes in the GI tract, gene expression analysis was performed in rat gastrointestinal tract samples. After false discovery adjustments, NS (14 genes) and LR (15 genes) exhibited numerous genes upregulated related to control, with notable upregulation in tumor necrosis factor (TNF and TNF‐a) and prostaglandin receptors. The histamine receptor complex was upregulated in LR only (p < 0.05 for all, Tables 4 and 5). NS and LR also exhibited downregulation of genes involved in cell adhesion (p < 0.05 for all, Tables 4 and 5), suggesting potential alterations in tissue integrity. Interestingly, and conversely to the lungs, AW exhibited greater changes than AW++ (p < 0.05 for all, Table 4). Seven genes were upregulated in the GI tract treated with AW versus only 1 gene, prostaglandin E receptor (Ptger3) in AW++ (p < 0.05, Table 4). Ptger3 was upregulated in all groups, thus this change may be secondary to the surgery itself. In AW, genes associated with both protease activity and protease inhibition were upregulated, indicating a controlled inflammatory response (Table 5). The fold‐change and p‐values for each significantly altered gene are listed in the Table S1.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the fluid composition used for amnio‐exchange has significant interaction with the fetal lungs. There are notable differences in histologic findings, surfactant expression, and transcriptional markers of inflammation inflammatory pathways. More specifically, NS and LR change cell membrane integrity, decrease airspace, and reduce surfactant expression, findings not observed with AW. In contrast, the developing fetal gastrointestinal (GI) tract appears less affected by fluid composition. Although inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory pathways in the fetal GI tract were altered based on the infused fluid, histological analysis revealed no discernible differences between the groups. Further research is necessary to determine whether infusions of these fluids into the human amniotic sac would induce similar effects.

Concern regarding the impact of amnioinfusion fluid content and fetal development dates back over 30 years [28], with studies in ovine models demonstrating changes in fetal blood gases following NS infusions. However, subsequent comparison between NS and LR in labor amnioinfusions found no difference in the cord blood gas [29], leading to an assumption of equivalence. Importantly, those studies focused on short‐term labor amnioinfusions rather than serial amnioinfusions, which differ in key aspects: (1). Labor amnioinfusions last only for a period of a few hours before delivery, (2). the presence of ruptured amniotic membranes allows for fluid leakage, and (3). NS and LR were compared to each other, rather than to physiologic conditions.

Our findings and prior research suggest that the mismatch between normal amniotic fluid, and NS or LR may pose a risk to the amniotic membranes [6]. These differences have been implicated in reactive oxygen species (ROS) mediated injury both in vitro and in vivo [6, 7]. Given the known interactions between amniotic fluid and developing fetal organs, it is plausible that these injuries extend to the fetal lungs. While not confirmatory, this work provides further evidence for this hypothesis, demonstrating, at least in this model, that the fetal lungs are particularly sensitive, whereas the fetal GI tract was more resilient. This is consistent with studies of both amnioinfusions and intrauterine inflammation, where lung complications are more frequent than GI complications [1, 29]. Additionally, our data reinforce the theory that inflammatory drivers in the amniotic fluid directly impact fetal lung development.

Previous studies by Habli and Miller [1, 2] have indicated that serial amnioinfusions for renal failure leading to oligo/anhydramnios are still associated with high respiratory needs after birth. The present study builds evidence that fluid composition is a critical factor influencing lung development. This study may also help explain why prior attempts to improve outcomes with amnioinfusions have been less successful than anticipated. Luton et al. did not find a benefit of amniotic fluid exchange in cases of gastroschisis with LR [29]. Similarly, evidence from the general surgery literature suggests that NS (and like fluids) are suboptimal for the bowel when performing peritoneal washes [30, 31]. Furthermore, a meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials on serial amnioinfusions for very early preterm prelabor rupture of membranes (PPROM), did not report improvement in perinatal survival even though amnioinfusions may aid in increasing pregnancy latency in early PPROM [32]. While further studies are required before clinical implementation of Amnio‐well, our findings call into a question past and ongoing clinical trials using NS and LR for serial amnioinfusions.

This study lays the groundwork for a study in long‐gestating mammals (i.e., sheep), particularly in animals that have an established surgical creation of lower urinary tract obstruction [33], where normal fetal lung development is then contingent upon the infused fluid composition. We plan to create a surgical model of anhydramnios in the mid‐gestation (E75 in sheep) and will be able to place an amnioport similar to what is used in some human models of amnioinfusions for in utero renal failure. We plan to perform smaller volume amnioinfusions two times per week (again as performed clinically) to establish whether Amnio‐well should be evaluated compared with NS or LR for serial amnioinfusions. Our findings regarding surfactant changes are remarkable. The significant reduction in Surfactant protein A, B, and C in NS and LR compared to control underscores the direct impact of fluid composition on lung maturation. Interestingly, the AW++ group also exhibited a decreased surfactant production. We speculate this was due to the inclusion of growth factors added to the fluid. TGF‐b is known to drive differentiation of alveolar type 2 cells into alveolar type 1 cells, thereby reducing surfactant production [34]. Additionally, EGF may negatively regulate surfactant production [35]. Given these findings, adding growth factors to the amniotic fluid may be neither beneficial nor warranted. Additionally, given the more clinically relevant gestational age window of E17.5–20.5 for human relevant lung development, we favor AW rather than AW++ as the most promising formulation, though it may be that growth factors should be added or removed as gestation progresses, and this will require further evaluation in a long‐gestating mammal.

A strength of this study is the comprehensive evaluation of fetal tissue. For fetal lungs to properly transition to postnatal life, they must be of sufficient size, maturation, and function, and we examined each of those relative to amnioinfusion content. These results provide information on what is happening at the cellular level when the amnioinfusion content changes. Similarly, the gastrointestinal tract was evaluated both histologically and via inflammatory pathways, which highlighted some changes not seen by histology alone. The inclusion of histopathological grading by a pathologist blinded to group‐allocation minimizes bias and enhances the reliability of our findings. Additional strength is the novel nature of this work, which is the first to look at the effect of amnioinfusion content upon fetal lungs at such a detailed level. Lastly, while it is important to interpret the transcriptional evaluation of inflammation with caution, many of the inflammatory signatures seen in this work have been seen in other evaluations of intrauterine inflammation [36]. It is also interesting to note that protease inhibitors are upregulated in AW‐exposed lungs, which may highlight a potential protective effect of this fluid relative to NS or LR.

There are several limitations; hence, the results must be contextualized. Firstly, this work was conducted in rodents, which prevents recurring amnio‐exchanges that can be performed due to their short gestation. Additionally, the rodents were healthy prior to amnio‐exchange and did not have conditions such as pulmonary hypoplasia or renal failure. Also, laparotomy was required for uterine access and the areas of uterine access were stereotactically sutured to ensure a water tight closure on the amniotic sac, potentially influencing the results. With specific regard to the gastrointestinal findings, the findings may not be directly translatable. Firstly, as we elected to align amnioinfusions with fetal lung development windows equivalent to humans (given that amnioinfusions are performed for fetal lung development), and given that rat gastrointestinal development is significantly accelerated relative to humans, it is possible the GI tract outcomes are not completely applicable to the clinic. Additionally, given the short interval between intervention and harvest, it may be challenging to detect morphologic maturation differences. Furthermore, this short interval might explain the gene regulation findings being incongruent with the morphology, as the morphological changes may come much later, or the transcription changes may reflect a brief inflammatory response without a significant impact on long term health. The absence of histologic or junctional protein differences should therefore be interpreted in the context of this experimental design rather than as definitive evidence of long‐term equivalence. With specific regard to the lung tissue, due to the nature of the treatment (amnioinfusions without altering the fetal anatomy), it was not possible to know to what group each individual fetus would belong after vaginal birth. Thus, cesarean delivery prior to the onset of labor, was performed in all cases, and thus, no mechanical or physiological lung function measurements could be performed postnatally. In future experiments in a large animal model, this limitation will be further addressed and examined. Regarding lung evaluation, while ATS guidelines were followed, we were unable to perform three dimensional evaluation (i.e., micro‐CT) and it must be noted that even with random sampling, 2‐D metrics rely on some degree of geometric assumption that does not perfectly interpret 3‐D structures.

Conclusion

The use of synthetic amniotic fluid leads to reduced inflammation in the fetal lungs at the time of amniotic fluid exchange. Further work is needed in longer gestating mammals to evaluate synthetic amniotic fluid for use in a variety of high‐risk obstetric indications.
