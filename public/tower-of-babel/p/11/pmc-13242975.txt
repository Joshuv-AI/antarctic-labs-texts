IL‐25 Enhances Eosinophil‐Associated Inflammation in Angiostrongylus cantonensis ‐Infected Mice
Authors: Chih‐Cheng Chuang, Wen‐Bin Wu, Jiun‐Jr Wang, Ming‐Chieh Ma, Wen‐Yuan Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to investigate the role of interleukin (IL)‐25 in the pathogenesis of angiostrongylosis. We used ICR mice as non‐permissive hosts, divided them into groups and experimentally infected them with infective larvae of the Angiostrongylus cantonensis . Two groups of infected mice were injected intraperitoneally with either mouse IL‐25 or an anti‐IL‐25 monoclonal antibody (mAb) at 3 days post‐infection (dpi), followed by booster injections at the same dose every 5 days. Serum samples and brain tissues were collected weekly from each group for immunological and pathological examinations. The IL‐25‐treated group exhibited significant increases in eosinophil percentages and levels of immunoglobulin E (IgE), as well as in the levels of IL‐5 and IL‐13. The severity of eosinophilic meningitis was exacerbated in the IL‐25‐treated group 21 dpi. These results suggest that IL‐25 may enhance eosinophil‐associated inflammation in mice infected with A. cantonensis .Keywords: Angiostrongylus cantonensis , eosinophilic meningitis, IL‐25

Introduction

Eosinophils are granulocytic white blood cells that originate in the bone marrow. Once activated, eosinophils function as immunoregulatory cells and produce chemical mediators, including prostaglandins, leukotrienes and cytokines [1, 2]. Several factors, including granulocyte macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF), IL‐5, IL‐13 and IgA, enhance the anti‐apoptotic properties and biological functions of mature eosinophils [3, 4, 5, 6].

In addition, eosinophils can release highly toxic granule proteins, such as matrix metalloproteinase‐9 and collagenase, as well as free radicals, to eliminate microorganisms and certain parasites. However, this process can also cause severe damage to host tissues [7]. In chronic inflammatory responses, eosinophils may participate in the remodelling of respiratory tissues [8].

Angiostrongylus cantonensis , commonly known as rat lungworm, is a major cause of eosinophilic meningitis, encephalitis and encephalomyelitis in humans, and can also cause neurological complications in domestic and wild animals [9, 10]. The disease is endemic in Taiwan, mainland China, Southeast Asia and some Pacific islands [11, 12]. Human cases have also been reported in the United States, parts of South and Central America, Australia and Europe [12, 13, 14]. The most common route of infection in humans is through the consumption of raw or undercooked molluscs (Figure 1).

The life cycle of Angiostrongylus cantonensis .

In humans (a non‐permissive host), ingested larvae enter the intestinal vasculature and eventually reach the meninges. Most larvae die rapidly and do not develop into adults. Infection with A. cantonensis triggers a predominantly Th2‐type immune response. One study observed elevated expression of Th2‐type cytokines (particularly IL‐5, IL‐4, IL‐13 and IL‐33) in the central nervous system, accompanied by eosinophil infiltration into the subarachnoid space and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) eosinophilia [15, 16, 17].

In mice experimentally infected with A. cantonensis , eosinophils release granules containing cytotoxic proteins that destroy the larvae. Some of these proteins, such as eosinophil protein X (EPX) and eosinophil cationic protein (ECP), can damage host neural tissue and exacerbate symptoms [18, 19]. Activation and degranulation of eosinophils are thought to play a key role in the pathogenesis of eosinophilic meningitis in murine angiostrongylosis [20].

IL‐25 is structurally similar to IL‐17 and can be produced by a variety of cells, including activated Th2 cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, mast cells and epithelial cells. However, its biological properties differ significantly from those of IL‐17 and other cytokines in the IL‐17 family [21, 22].

IL‐25 enhances antigen‐induced allergic airway inflammation, including the production of Th2 cytokines (such as IL‐4, IL‐5 and IL‐13), the recruitment of eosinophils and CD4+ T cells and the proliferation of goblet cells [23, 24, 25]. IL‐25 expression is essential for initiating type 2 immune responses during some parasite infections [26, 27, 28]. IL‐25 may be involved in the pathogenesis of Th‐2 cell‐mediated inflammatory diseases and may provide another therapeutic target for controlling inflammation [23].

This study investigated the role of IL‐25 in the immunopathogenesis of angiostrongylosis by experimentally infecting mice with A. cantonensis larvae and administering either IL‐25 or an anti‐IL‐25 monoclonal antibody (mAb). The effects of IL‐25 were assessed by measuring circulating eosinophil percentages, IgE levels and cytokine expression, combined with brain tissue pathology.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Prior to the commencement of the study, the experiments were reviewed by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fu Jen Catholic University (approval No. A10462).

In our laboratory, we use the freshwater snail Biomphalaria glabrata as the intermediate host and male Wistar rats (150 g/body weight) as the definitive host to maintain A. cantonensis . In this study, male ICR mice (20 g body weight) were used as an animal model for human angiostrongylosis. Rats and mice were purchased from Biolasco Co. Ltd. (Yilan Breeding facility, Taiwan), confirmed to be pathogen‐free and maintained in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Infection and Treatments

We experimentally infected snails with L1 larvae collected from the faeces of infected rats. After 28 days, the infected snails were crushed between two glass slides and cut into small pieces with dissecting scissors. The specimens were then immersed in distilled water and agitated to release the L3 larvae. The released larvae were collected and counted under a dissecting microscope.

The ICR mice were randomly divided into six groups (A–F), with at least 10 mice in each group used for serum sample collection and 12 mice used for pathological examination. The division of Groups A–F is shown as follows:

In the A. cantonensis infection group, each mouse was orally inoculated with 35 infective larvae. In the injection groups, mice were intraperitoneally injected with mouse IL‐25 (0.7 μg in 0.1 mL phosphate‐buffered saline; R&D Systems Inc., USA) or anti‐IL‐25 mAb (10 μg in 0.1 mL phosphate‐buffered saline; R&D Systems Inc., USA) 3 days after infection. Subsequently, the same dose was intraperitoneally injected every 5 days (on Days 8, 13, 18, 23, 28 and 33). During the experimental period, weight gain was similar among mice in the infection group, normal control group and treatment groups.

Tail vein blood was collected from all mice weekly, thin blood smears were prepared and serum was collected. Serum samples from each group were combined for cytokine and IgE assays. Brain tissues were extracted from the mice for pathological examination.

Cytokine and Antibody Assays

Serum cytokine concentrations were measured by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (IL‐5 and IL‐13: ELISA and Activity Assay kits, R&D Systems Inc., USA. IL‐25: Mouse IL‐25/IL‐17E ELISA KIT, AVIVA SYSTRMS BIOLOGY, USA). IgE levels were measured using a mouse IgE ELISA kit (Innovative Research Co. Ltd., USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cytokines and IgE in serum react with specific antibodies adsorbed to the surface of a polystyrene microtiter plate. After appropriate washing to remove unbound proteins, a polyclonal antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) was used as a secondary antibody, and colour was developed using the chromogenic substrate 3,3′,5,5′‐tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) in a spectrophotometer at 450 nm. This amount can be interpolated from a standard curve and corrected for sample dilution. The sensitivity of the cytokine kit is < 7 pg/mL for IL‐5 and < 1.5 pg/mL for IL‐13.

Worm Recovery and Pathological Examination

On Days 14, 21, 28 and 35 after infection, six infected mice from each group were sacrificed under ether anaesthesia. The brains of the mice were submerged individually in phosphate‐buffered saline and teased gently with forceps and needles. The worms were then retrieved with a needle under a dissecting microscope.

The brain tissues of infected mice were immediately removed and fixed with 10% (v/v) neutral formalin. The tissue samples were embedded in paraffin and cut into 4–5 μm sections. The sections were obtained from the frontal lobe, middle cerebrum, midbrain and medulla‐cerebellum and stained with haematoxylin–eosin (H&E) [29]. At least 10 fields of view per section were observed under a light microscope.

Statistical Analysis

The data are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using Student's t‐test or analysis of variance software. A p value of less than 0.001 was considered extremely significant.

Worm Recovery and Pathological Examination

At 14 days post‐infection (dpi), there were no significant differences in worm recovery between the untreated (11.7 ± 2.12) and treated groups (12 ± 1.0 in IL‐25‐treated group, 11.4 ± 1.3 in mAb‐treated group). The worm recovery in each group was less than 5 at 21, 28 and 35 dpi.

No pathological changes were observed in the uninfected groups. All groups infected with A. cantonensis developed traumatic and hemorrhagic lesions, resulting from the migration of larvae within the brain parenchyma. Moderate meningitis (severity: ++) was observed in the untreated, IL‐25‐treated and mAb‐treated groups at 14 dpi (Figure 5A,C,E). Moderate meningitis (severity: ++) was also observed in untreated mice (Figure 5B) and in mice injected with anti‐IL‐25 monoclonal antibodies at 21 dpi (Figure 5D). However, severe eosinophilic meningitis (severity: +++) was observed in mice injected with IL‐25 at 21 dpi, characterized by a large infiltration of inflammatory cells, particularly eosinophils (Figure 5F).

Discussion

Eosinophilia is associated with a variety of diseases, such as parasitic infections, atopic ailments, asthma and some neoplastic diseases [30, 31, 32]. Eosinophilia is a hallmark of angiostrongylosis, and eosinophils play a crucial role in clearing the young adult (L5) of A. cantonensis from the subarachnoid space [29].

In this study, mice that received exogenous IL‐25 and/or were infected with A. cantonensis had a significant increase in the percentage of eosinophils in their white blood cells. IL‐25 can enhance eosinophil production in uninfected mice, but it did not alter the percentage of eosinophils during infection. However, when infected mice were treated with an anti‐IL‐25 mAb, the percentage of eosinophils decreased significantly. This finding suggests that IL‐25 could play a role in eosinophil production.

IL‐25 and CD4+ TH2 cells promote IgE‐mediated experimental food allergy by enhancing IL‐13 production [33]. Our results showed that IgE levels were elevated in mice injected with IL‐25 or infected with A. cantonensis . IgE levels were significantly elevated in infected mice treated with IL‐25. In contrast, treatment with an anti‐IL‐25 mAb resulted in a significant decrease in IgE levels. These results indicate that IL‐25 promotes IgE production in A. cantonensis ‐infected mice.

IL‐5 is essential for the development and release of bone marrow eosinophils. It also strongly stimulates eosinophils, promoting functions such as superoxide production and degranulation [4, 34, 35]. Specific inhibitors of IL‐5, such as anti‐IL‐5 monoclonal antibodies, can reduce eosinophil‐associated inflammation in diseases such as asthma and certain parasitic infections [36, 37]. IL‐5 expression also plays an important role in the immunopathogenesis of angiostrongylosis [20, 29]. In addition to being involved in the growth and differentiation of B cells, IL‐13 is essential for the synthesis of IgE. It is also associated with goblet cell hyperplasia, mucus hypersecretion, airway hyperresponsiveness and fibrosis [38]. It is crucial in mediating allergic inflammation and diseases such as asthma and atopic dermatitis [39, 40].

Our results show that exogenous IL‐25 significantly increases IL‐5 and IL‐13 expression in both uninfected and A. cantonensis ‐infected mice. Injection of an anti‐IL‐25 mAb reduces the expression of these cytokines in infected mice. This study suggests that IL‐25 regulates IL‐5 and IL‐13 expressions in angiostrongylosis.

In previous studies, we found that mice infected with A. cantonensis experienced the most severe eosinophilic meningitis at 14 dpi, followed by gradual improvement to moderate meningitis [20, 29]. The pathological examinations revealed that the count of infiltrated cells in non‐treated mice at 21 dpi was lower than at 14 days.

In infected mice that were treated with IL‐25, meningitis with an increase in the number of infiltrating cells, particularly eosinophils, was observed at both 14 and 21 dpi. However, a significant increase was noted at 21 dpi, and the severity of meningitis did not improve. Injection of an anti‐IL‐25 mAb did not alter the severity of eosinophilic meningitis at 14 dpi. However, a significant decrease of infiltrated cells was observed at 21 dpi. These results indicate that the severity of eosinophilic meningitis was exacerbated in the IL‐25‐treated group at 21 dpi.

In IL‐25‐deficient mice, helminth parasite expulsion is delayed [26, 27]. In addition to eosinophilic meningitis, all mice infected with A. cantonensis showed varying degrees of mechanical damage to the brain parenchyma by parasite larvae. However, because the number of larvae in the brain had decreased to a low level 3 weeks after infection, we were unable to assess the effect of IL‐25 and antibody on parasite recovery rates.

The results of this study demonstrate that exogenous IL‐25 not only increases the production of IgE, IL‐5 and IL‐13 in uninfected mice, but also increases the proportion of eosinophils. Furthermore, IL‐25 enhances the production of IgE, IL‐5 and IL‐13 in mice infected with A. cantonensis . Moreover, administration of an anti‐IL‐25 mAb inhibits the expression of IgE, IL‐5 and IL‐13 in these infected mice. Eosinophilic meningitis in the IL‐25‐treated group was exacerbated 21 days after infection. Taken together, our findings suggest that IL‐25 may play an important role in the immunopathogenesis of angiostrongylosis.
