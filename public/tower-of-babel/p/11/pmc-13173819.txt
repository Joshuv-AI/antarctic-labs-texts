ADAM9 in tumor biology: molecular functions, clinical implications, and therapeutic targeting
Authors: Kuo-Hao Ho, Chao-Jung Wu, Yi-Chieh Yang, Ming-Hsien Chien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA disintegrin and metalloproteinase 9 (ADAM9), a member of the ADAM family, is expressed across multiple organs and is crucial to multiple physiological processes. Increasing evidence implicates ADAM9 in cancer progression through extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, protein shedding, and tumor microenvironment modulation. This study comprehensively reviews the literature on the clinical significance of ADAM9 and the mechanistic roles of ADAM9 in cancer. The results of our pan-cancer analysis demonstrated that ADAM9 is frequently upregulated and consistently associated with poor prognosis across tumor types. The results of in silico analyses also revealed that increased ADAM9 expression is correlated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and the activation of cancer-promoting pathways, such as cell cycle progression, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and metabolism. This study also reviewed therapeutic strategies targeting ADAM9 and evaluated their potential in cancer treatment. This review provides insights into ADAM9 as both a biomarker of malignancy and a promising therapeutic target.Keywords: A disintegrin and metalloproteinase 9 (ADAM9), Extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, Protein shedding, Tumor microenvironment (TME), Cancer progression

Background

Metalloprotease-disintegrin proteins, also termed metalloprotease/disintegrin/cysteine-rich or ADAM (a disintegrin and metalloprotease) proteins, are a family of membrane-anchored glycoproteins involved in multiple physiological processes, such as neurogenesis, the binding and fusion of sperm/egg cells or myoblasts, and the regulation of signaling pathways, such as the Notch-, Wnt-, and NFκB-pathways [1–3]. More than 20 ADAM family members have been identified in human cells, and approximately half exhibit functional metalloprotease activity and participate in proteolytic processes. ADAM family members are critical regulators of both physiological and pathological cellular processes. For example, ADAMs are crucial to the proteolytic processing of pro-inflammatory cytokines [4] and the ectodomain shedding of membrane-bound precursors such as pro-heparin-binding epidermal-growth-factor (pro-HB-EGF), a ligand of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) [5]. Additionally, ADAMs are involved in the regulation of key signaling pathways [6] and the remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM) [7].

Structure and functional domains of ADAM9

As a member of the ADAM family, ADAM9, is extensively expressed across multiple organs and involved in a diverse range of physiological processes such as cell adhesion, proliferation, apoptosis, and angiogenesis [8, 9]. Structurally, all members of the ADAM family consist of a multidomain extracellular region, a transmembrane segment, and a cytoplasmic tail. To guide secretions, ADAM9 contains the first 29 amino acids (AAs) on the N-terminal as signal peptides. Its extracellular region is central to its functional activity and comprises a signal peptide, a metalloproteinase domain, a disintegrin domain, a cysteine-rich region, and an EGF-like domain [10] (Fig. 1A). Extracellular domains of ADAM9

Members of the ADAM family are synthesized as inactive proenzymes containing pro-domains such as the N-terminal pro-domain of ADAM9 spanning amino acids (AAs) 30–205, which maintains the enzyme in an inactive state. The recombinant pro-domains of ADAM9 and ADAM10 competitively inhibit the enzymatic activity of their respective proteins [12, 13]. The pro-domain of ADAM9 harbors two distinct furin-like (RXXR) proprotein convertase cleavage sites [14], the upstream and boundary sites, which regulate its activation. Mutations in the upstream proprotein convertase site substantially reduce the catalytic activity of ADAM9, ADAM10, and ADAM17 [15]. Additionally, the free sulfhydryl of cysteine-switch residues present in the pro-domain can bind to the Zn2+ ion in the catalytic site and determines when the protease functions through a cysteine switching mechanism [16]. Moreover, the pro-domain functions as an intramolecular chaperone, ensuring the proper folding of the ADAM9 protein.

The metalloproteinase domain of ADAM9 is located on AAs 206–412 and contains a conserved zinc-binding motif (HExGHxxGxxHD; AAs 347–358) essential to catalytic activity [17]. As a zinc-dependent metalloproteinase, this domain functions as an active protease capable of degrading ECM components and cleaving the ectodomains of various cell surface proteins such as growth factors and cytokines (Fig. 1C).

The disintegrin (AAs 413–503) and cysteine-rich ADAM domains (AAs 504–643) are critical to substrate recognition and interaction [18, 19]. For example, the disintegrin domain of ADAM9 interacts with multiple β1 renal integrins and functions as a ligand that influences cell–matrix interactions [19]. In human melanoma at the tumor–stroma interface, the disintegrin-like and cysteine-rich domains of ADAM9 mediate the interactions between melanoma cells and stromal fibroblasts [20]. Additionally, the cysteine-rich domain of ADAM family members likely contributes to ECM binding independently of disintegrin–integrin interactions. However, the precise functional role of the cysteine-rich domain in ADAM9 remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

With the exception of ADAM10 and ADAM17, all ADAM family members contain an EGF-like domain, the biological functions of which remain poorly characterized [18]. However, both the EGF-like domain and the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains are absent from the secreted (soluble) ADAM9 (ADAM9-S) isoform formed through alternative splicing. This soluble isoform lacks membrane anchorage and exhibits distinct biological activity, suggesting that the EGF-like domain in the full-length membrane-bound form of ADAM9 plays a crucial role in cancers (Fig. 1A).

Two distinct isoforms can be generated from alternatively splicing the ADAM9 transcript: ADAM9-S and ADAM9-L (long) [21, 22]. ADAM9-S contains unique amino acid sequence (LSLKFHAPF) encoded by exon 12 that are absent from ADAM9-L. Additionally, because ADAM9-S lacks both the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains, it functions in a secreted form within cells. Fry et al. examined the contrasting roles of ADAM9-S and ADAM9-L in breast cancer progression [22]; they reported that ADAM9-S exerts a tumor-promoting effect through its metalloproteinase activity, whereas ADAM9-L exerts a tumor-inhibiting effect. However, silencing the expression of ADAM9 in cancer cells strongly attenuates cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [23–25]. Therefore, the precise roles of ADAM9 isoforms in cell behaviors warrant further investigation.

Intracellular domain of ADAM9

The cytoplasmic domain of ADAM9 has two proline-rich sequences, RPPPPQP and RPAPAPP, and Src-homology 3 (SH3) domains located at AAs 746–759, 754–767, 785–798, and 802–816 [26]. This intracellular domain is crucial to modulating ADAM9’s catalytic activity and is strongly correlated with the binding of ADAM9 to SH3-domain-containing proteins. Most SH3 proteins that interact with ADAMs are adapter proteins or kinases associated with sorting and endocytosis. These binding partners comprise SNX33 (sorting nexin-33), SNX9 (sorting nexin-9), SNX18 (sorting nexin-18), neutrophil cytosol factor 1, the tyrosine protein kinases Tec and Lyn, and ArgBP2 (sorbin and SH3-domain-containing protein 2). Additionally, the cytoplasmic domains of ADAM proteins interact with proteins involved in intracellular signaling, transport, and structural organization. Some ADAM members also function as SH3 ligand domains by directly binding to Src family kinases through their SH3 domains.

Involvement of ADAM9 in human diseases

Because of its diverse regulatory mechanisms and expression across multiple organs, dysregulated ADAM9 has been implicated in various inflammatory, neurodegenerative, retinal, and vascular disorders. Moreover, because of its multifunctional properties—both those dependent and independent of catalytic activity—ADAM9 is crucial to cancer progression across tumor types.

Inflammatory diseases

In encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) infection, ADAM9 plays a role in the innate immune response by interacting with melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5) [27]. This interaction promotes MDA5 oligomerization, thereby enhancing interferon production in response to EMCV infection and contributing to protection against virus-induced cardiac injury [27].

In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Th17 cells produce IL17, a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine that drives inflammation and tissue damage [28]. Th17 cells from patients with SLE exhibit increased ADAM9 expression [29]. Mechanistically, ADAM9 promotes the cleavage of latency-associated peptide, resulting in the activation of TGF-β1 and facilitating Th17 cell differentiation [29]. The inhibition of ADAM9 suppresses Th17 development, indicating ADAM9’s vital role in SLE pathogenesis [29]. Th17 cells are also involved in other autoimmune diseases, such as multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and inflammatory bowel disease. These findings suggest that targeting ADAM9 may be a promising strategy for treating multiple Th17-driven autoimmune disorders.

Neurodegenerative diseases

The formation of amyloid beta (Aβ) peptides is a major contributor to Alzheimer disease (AD) pathogenesis [30]. These peptides are generated from the amyloid precursor protein (APP) through sequential cleavage by β- and γ-secretases. By contrast, cleavage of APP by α-secretases such as ADAM9 prevents Aβ formation and produces soluble APPα (sAPPα), which exerts neuroprotective effects [31]. A genetic polymorphism in the promoter region of the ADAM9 gene is associated with sporadic AD. Cong et al. reported that individuals carrying the −1314C allele have a lower incidence of AD than do those with the −1314 T allele and that the −1314C allele is associated with increased ADAM9 transcriptional activity [32]. These findings suggest that increased ADAM9 expression may reduce AD risk by enhancing sAPPα production through nonamyloidogenic APP processing [32]. However, Moss et al. demonstrated that the inhibition of ADAM9 proteolytic activity reduces the shedding of ADAM10, another α-secretase implicated in AD [13]. They also observed that ADAM9 inhibition is associated with increased sAPPα levels, suggesting that ADAM9 exerts a neuroprotective effect. Although this finding is inconsistent with those of Cong et al., Cong et al. did not provide direct evidence linking ADAM9 activity to changes in sAPPα levels. These findings reveal the complexity of ADAM9’s role in AD and indicate that further investigation is required to clarify the interactions among ADAM9, ADAM10, and APP processing in AD.

Vascular disorders

The integrins αvβ3 and α5β1 are critical to atherosclerosis progression. ADAM family members are crucial modulators of integrin–ECM interactions through their disintegrin domains. For example, Fakhri et al. reported that the expression of ADAM9 and ADAM15 is upregulated in the integrins αvβ3 and α5β1 and in vascular smooth muscle cells in response to platelet-derived growth factor stimulation [33]. They also observed increased ADAM9 mRNA levels in atherosclerotic human tissue compared with normal vascular specimens, suggesting that ADAM9 may be involved in atherosclerotic plaque development. However, the precise contribution of ADAM9 to integrin regulation and its involvement in atherosclerosis pathogenesis remain unclear.

Shen et al. suggested that ADAM9 may play a role in the development of abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) [34]. They demonstrated that ADAM9 expression is upregulated in both cellular and animal models of AAAs. Functionally, ADAM9 activation was found to be associated with increased macrophage infiltration, enhanced cell apoptosis, and AKT/NF-κB signaling pathway activation in a murine model of AAAs. Furthermore, microRNA (miR) such as miR-126 was identified as a regulator of ADAM9 by directly targeting its 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR). Subsequent experiments verified that miR-126 suppresses ADAM9 expression and, in turn, attenuates inflammatory cytokine production and AAA formation.

Retinal diseases

Ocular neovascularization is a major contributor to vision loss in eye diseases such as premature retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, and diabetic retinopathy. Because ADAM9 has been implicated in the promotion of neovascularization, researchers have investigated its role in pathological ocular angiogenesis [35, 36]. For example, Guaiquil et al. established mouse models of oxygen-induced retinopathy and laser-induced choroidal neovascularization and reported that pathological neovascularization was substantially reduced in mice with ADAM9 knockout [35]. In a separate study, Parry et al. reported that a null mutation of ADAM9 is associated with early-onset cone–rod dystrophy, a genetically inherited retinal degenerative disease [36]. Progressive retinal degeneration has also been observed in ADAM9-deficient mice, suggesting that ADAM9 may be involved in the pathogenesis of cone–rod dystrophy. However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which ADAM9 contributes to this disorder remain unclear.

Roles of ADAM9 in human cancer

As a multifunctional transmembrane protein highly involved in cell growth, differentiation, and microenvironment remodeling, ADAM9 is highly expressed in multiple cancer types. Collectively, accumulating evidence extends the role of ADAM9 beyond that of a cancer-associated metalloprotease and positions it as a context-dependent regulator of tumor progression. Recent studies indicate that ADAM9 could coordinate cancer cell-intrinsic signaling with dynamic interactions within the tumor microenvironment, including immune modulation. By integrating updated mechanistic insights, our comparative in silico pan-cancer analyses, and clinical correlations, the following sections highlight ADAM9 as a molecular node linking tumor aggressiveness, immune landscape remodeling, and clinical outcome. Importantly, the strengthened evidence presented herein underscores the translational potential of ADAM9 as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target, providing a conceptual framework for future studies aimed at patient stratification and the development of ADAM9-informed precision oncology strategies.

Prognostic value of ADAM9 in human cancers

Fan et al. observed substantially lower ADAM9 expression in lower-grade glioma (LGG) than in glioblastoma (GBM) [37]. Additionally, in LGG, ADAM9 levels were higher in aggressive astrocytic tumors than in oligodendroglial tumors. Furthermore, ADAM9 served as an independent prognostic marker of poor progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in patients with LGG.

O’Shea et al. reported that ADAM9 mRNA expression was enhanced in both malignant and early benign breast lesions compared with normal breast tissue [38]. However, no significant correlation was observed between ADAM9 mRNA or protein levels and tumor grade, estrogen receptor status, or progesterone receptor status. However, the mature form of ADAM9 was positively associated with HER-2/neu expression and nodal metastases, both of which are indicators of poor prognosis in breast cancer. In another study, Zhou et al. conducted an immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of 44 tumor specimens and reported that ADAM9 protein levels tended to be higher in triple-negative breast cancer than in other breast cancers. Survival analysis using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) data also indicated that increased ADAM9 gene expression was associated with poorer outcomes, such as OS, relapse-free survival (RFS), and distant-metastasis-free survival (DMFS) [39].

Oh et al. reported an association between enhanced ADAM9 expression and poorer OS in an analysis of TCGA data on hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [40]. Oh et al. also observed considerably higher plasma ADAM9 levels in the blood samples of patients with HCC than in those of healthy donors. Moreover, patients who responded clinically to the anti-programmed death (PD)-1 antibody nivolumab exhibited lower serum ADAM9 mRNA expression than those who did not respond to this antibody [40].

Studies have also reported increased ADAM9 protein levels in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) tissues compared with normal tissues, although the association of ADAM9 with tumor differentiation remains unclear [41, 42]. For example, Tanasubsinn et al. identified a correlation between ADAM9 protein levels with increased cell differentiation [41]. By contrast, Wu et al. reported higher ADAM9 expression in poorly differentiated OSCC, with increased ADAM9 expression being associated with shorter OS [41, 42]. These contradictory findings may partly be explained by the use of different ADAM9 antibodies in the two studies. Additionally, this inconsistency may be explained by the inability of IHC to distinguish between the pro-form and the mature form of ADAM9 in human specimens.

In their study on renal cell carcinoma (RCC), Fritzsche et al. conducted both quantitative PCR (qPCR) and IHC and reported that ADAM9 gene expression and protein levels were elevated in malignant tissues compared with normal tissues in a sample of 108 patients with RCC [43]. Other clinical analyses have indicated that ADAM9 protein levels are correlated with positive nodal status and distant metastasis. Survival analysis results have also verified that high protein levels of ADAM9 predicted poor prognosis in individuals with RCC. For example, Xu et al. reported that patients with metastatic RCC with increased ADAM9 expression exhibited a reduced response rate to combined immunotherapy and tyrosine kinase inhibitor treatment [44]. Immune profiling revealed that these patients exhibited an exhausted T cell phenotype with reduced granzyme B expression.

Zhang et al. analyzed 64 cases of stage I non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) using IHC to detect ADAM9 protein levels. They reported that ADAM9 expression was substantially higher in tumor tissues than in adjacent normal tissues. Enhanced ADAM9 levels were associated with poorer 5-year survival rates and identified as an independent predictor of survival [45]. Similarly, Lin et al. analyzed two merged cDNA microarray data sets of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GSE11969 and GSE8894) and demonstrated that enhanced ADAM9 gene expression predicted poor prognosis. Furthermore, the results of a qPCR analysis conducted on 84 patients with lung cancer revealed substantially increased ADAM9 expression in brain metastatic lesions than in lesions that in primary lung tumors [46].

In their analysis of ovarian cancer, Guo et al. employed IHC and reported that ADAM9 protein levels were considerably increased in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues. Increased ADAM9 expression was associated with higher tumor grade, advanced tumor stage, and metastasis. Additionally, ADAM9 mRNA expression was identified as a diagnostic marker for ovarian cancer that was correlated with both poor OS and reduced DFS [47].

Grützmann et al. analyzed 59 pancreatic cancer tumor specimens using IHC staining to assess ADAM9 protein expression [48]. Their findings revealed that cytoplasmic expression of ADAM9 was associated with shorter OS and poorer tumor differentiation. Moreover, ADAM9 expression was predominantly observed in ductal-type pancreatic cancers rather than in endocrine-type tumors, suggesting that ADAM9 may play a role in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) rather than in nonductal pancreatic neoplasms [48]. Although Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS) signaling drives PDAC malignancy, current KRAS-targeting therapies have limited clinical efficacy. However, Huang et al. reported that ADAM9 is upregulated in patients with PDAC whose tumors exhibit high KRAS activity regardless of KRAS mutation status, indicating that ADAM9 may be an alternative therapeutic target [49].

In their study of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), Lin et al. demonstrated that increased ADAM9 gene expression was correlated with poor prognosis [50]. Additionally, elevated ADAM9 expression in GSE20347 has been observed in tumor specimens. After conducting IHC staining, Lin et al. observed that the protein levels of ADAM9 were highest in stage IV [50]. Their IHC analysis results also revealed that ADAM9 protein levels were a predictor of poor prognosis only in the early stages of ESCC. The nonsignificant association of ADAM9 expression with late-stage ESCC may be explained by the short survival times of those with late-stage disease.

In their study on prostate cancer (PCa), Fritzsche et al. analyzed 198 tumor specimens through qPCR and identified enhanced ADAM9 expression in tumor tissues [51]. Additionally, they reported that enhanced ADAM9 expression was inversely correlated with prostate-specific antigen RFS [51]. Notably, they also reported that increased ADAM9 expression was a significant indicator of poor prognosis in patients receiving antiandrogen therapy but not in those who have not undergone this treatment [51]. These findings implicate ADAM9 expression in therapeutic resistance to androgen ablation. Similarly, Le et al. reported enhanced ADAM9 expression in metastatic PCa [52]. They also identified increased ADAM9 expression in the tumor stroma, particularly in cancer-associated fibroblasts. Additionally, they detected ADAM9-S in patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia and in patients with PCa, observing significantly higher levels of ADAM9-S in those with PCa. These findings indicate that ADAM9 may serve as a potential antiandrogen therapy indicator in PCa and suggest that ADAM9-S may be a biomarker of PCa.

The aforementioned studies have indicated that ADAM9 is aberrantly expressed across cancer types and is associated with poor clinical outcomes. The present study conducted a pan-cancer analysis to evaluate the expression of ADAM9 across 28 tumor types using data from TCGA and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database. Of the 28 tumor types analyzed, 19 exhibited substantially increased levels of ADAM9 compared with normal tissues, whereas 6 exhibited a substantial downregulation of ADAM9 (Fig. 2A). We also conducted a Cox regression and used the optimal cutoff with the minimum p value to assess the associations between ADAM9 expression and OS, progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS). ADAM9 was identified as a predictor of poor prognosis in 17 cancer types for at least one of these survival outcomes, whereas in 5 cancer types, greater ADAM9 expression was associated with a more favorable prognosis (Fig. 2B). The cancer types in which increased levels of ADAM9 were associated with poor prognosis comprised BRCA, cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), GBM, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), kidney chromophobe (KICH), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), LGG, liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), LUAD, lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), OV, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD). These results are consistent with those of studies that have demonstrated ADAM9’s tumor-promoting functions and association with poor survival outcomes in BRCA [38, 39], HNSC [41, 42], LGG [37], LIHC [40], LUAD [45, 46], OV [47], and PAAD [48]. By contrast, in KICH and KIRP, increased ADAM9 expression is associated with poor prognostic outcomes, whereas in kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), increased ADAM9 expression is correlated with prolonged survival. Moreover, although Fritzsche et al. reported that increased ADAM9 protein expression predicts poor prognosis in patients with RCC—especially patients with KIRC—their study had a small sample size (n = 22) in contrast to that of the TCGA KIRC cohort (n = 534). Additionally, Fritzsche et al. assessed ADAM9 expression in protein using IHC, whereas TCGA data were used to evaluate ADAM9 expression in RNA. Because ADAM9 undergoes post-translational modifications [53–55], RNA and protein expression levels may not be directly correlated with each other. Therefore, further research is required to clarify the clinical relevance of ADAM9 across RCC subtypes. Notably, the present study observed a significant association between increased ADAM9 levels and poor prognosis in patients with GBM. By contrast, Fan et al. did not observe a prognostic association between ADAM9 expression and survival in patients with GBM in TCGA data [37]. This discrepancy may be due to differences in the stratification methods used. Fan et al. classified patients on the basis of the median expression level of ADAM9, whereas we applied the optimal cutoff using the minimum p value to clearly distinguish survival differences between cohorts. Using this approach, we revealed that a smaller percentage of patients (n = 19, 11.4%) exhibited enhanced ADAM9 expression, which was associated with poorer survival outcomes compared with those who exhibited low ADAM9 expression (n = 147, 88.6%). These findings indicate that the criteria used to evaluate the prognostic value of ADAM9 in patients with GBM in TCGA can substantially affect the results. Therefore, the prognostic value of ADAM9 in GBM requires validation in larger cohorts.

One study reported increased ADAM9 expression in tissues positive for colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) compared with normal samples [56]. This observation is consistent with those of our TCGA pan-cancer analysis, which revealed upregulation of ADAM9 in tumor tissues. However, in our survival analysis, increased ADAM9 expression was associated with increased OS, a finding inconsistent with the reported oncogenic role of ADAM9 in COAD. Therefore, the prognostic value of ADAM9 in COAD warrants further investigation.

An additional consideration is whether the expression levels of ADAM9 or its isoforms in protein can predict prognostic outcomes. In the literature on CESC, only one study reported that increased ADAM9 expression was detectable in tumor specimens and was associated with larger tumor size and distant metastasis [57]. However, our in silico analysis results support the oncogenic role of ADAM9 in CESC, revealing that enhanced ADAM9 expression was significantly correlated with poorer OS, DSS, and PFI. These results indicate the potential oncogenic role of ADAM9 in CESC and justify further investigation.

ADAM9 regulates ECM remodeling and signaling in cancers

Similar to other members of the ADAM family, ADAM9 functions as an integrin-binding metalloprotease, regulating cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions and the activation of cell surface receptors [58]. Membrane-anchored ADAM9 functions as a tumor regulator, and ADAM9-S has been implicated in cancer progression. For example, Chang et al. revealed that primary PCa cells secrete ADAM9-S, transforming osteoblasts into prostate-cancer-associated osteoblasts (PCa-OBs) [59]. These PCa-OBs increase Wnt inducible signaling pathway protein 1 expression, facilitate the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) process, and increase the invasiveness of primary PCa. However, one study on breast cancer revealed that ADAM9-S and ADAM9-L may serve opposing functions in cancer cell migration [22]. ADAM9-S promoted cancer cell migration through its metalloprotease activity, whereas ADAM9-L suppressed migration independently of its metalloprotease function. Through these processes, ADAM9 modulates intracellular and extracellular signaling. The following section explores the extracellular regulatory functions mediated by ADAM9.

Modulating the ECM and integrins

The ability of ADAM9 to regulate cancer cell adhesion, migration, and invasion is linked to its interactions with cell surface integrins through its disintegrin domain [60]. Integrins are crucial transmembrane proteoglycans that function as coreceptors to which matrix components bind in response to the ECM, which consists of α and β subunits [61]. Several integrin subunits are influenced by ADAM9, such as α2β1, α3β1, α6β1, α9β1, α6β4, and αVβ5 [60, 62, 63]. Additionally, ADAM9 is essential to β-subunit integrin internalization and regulates cell migration [64]. For example, increased ADAM9-S expression has been observed on the surface of colon carcinoma. ADAM9 interacts with α6β4 and α2β1 integrins through its disintegrin domain and cleaves laminin, promoting cancer invasion [21]. As also indicated by Oh et al., ADAM9 silencing promotes the expression of integrin β1 on the cell membrane by impairing endocytosis. This increased expression enhances integrin-mediated cell adhesion and suppresses cell migration in vitro [40]. However, treatment with the metalloproteinase inhibitors GN6001 and batimastat did not reverse these effects on integrin β1 expression or integrin-mediated migration, suggesting that ADAM9’s proteolytic activity is not involved in this mechanism.

ADAM9 also participates in the ectodomain shedding of various ECM components, such as collagen XVII, fibronectin, laminin, and gelatin [65, 66]. For example, Galiger et al. demonstrated that ADAM9-mediated shedding of the adhesion molecule collagen XVII promotes the invasiveness of squamous cell carcinoma [65]. This pro-invasive effect is mediated through the collagenous domain of collagen XVII, which interacts with α5β1 and αVβ1 integrins. Smith et al. also documented the interaction of collagen XVII with ADAM9 through tandem disintegrin and cysteine-rich domains, which affects the cell–cell adhesion of fibroblasts and melanoma cells [67]. Although ADAM9 is typically regarded as pro-tumorigenic, studies investigating melanoma have demonstrated that the loss or deletion of ADAM9 in stromal (host) cells such as fibroblasts may not suppress tumorigenesis. For example, the in vitro coculture of melanoma cells and ADAM-9−/− fibroblasts resulted in increased melanoma cell proliferation rates. Contrary to its presumed pro-tumorigenic role due to its proteolytic functions, the absence of stromal ADAM9 was associated with increased soluble tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP-1) in the ADAM-9−/− fibroblast culture supernatant, which led to melanoma cell proliferation and reduced apoptosis at the tumor periphery. These observations suggest that stromal ADAM9 may play an antitumorigenic role in melanoma by restraining tumor growth [68]. This tumor-suppressive effect is likely also linked to alterations in the peritumoral ECM observed in ADAM9−/− hosts, particularly the enhanced expression and accumulation of collagen type I at the tumor–stroma interface, which creates a microenvironment conducive to tumor growth [69]. These findings indicate that stromal ADAM9 contributes to the suppression of melanoma progression by modulating the composition and remodeling of the peritumoral ECM—particularly collagen type I—through both direct proteolytic cleavage and indirect regulation of other collagen-degrading enzymes.

Shedding of growth factors, cytokines, and receptors by ADAM9

The shedding of pro-HB-EGF by ADAM9 is a well-studied growth factor involved in cancer progression. In this process, the interaction of MDC9/meltrin‐γ/ADAM9 and PKCδ is activated by the protein kinase C activator TPA, which cleaves pro-HB-EGF and facilitates cancer progression through the activation of EGFR [5, 70]. Another ligand in the EGF family, neuregulin 1β (NRG1β), has also been identified as a substrate of ADAM9 [71]. NRG1β is an isoform of NRG1, which has been implicated in promoting the malignant features of cancer—such as enhanced proliferation and cancer stemness—primarily through the NRG1–ERBB3–ERBB2 signaling axis [72, 73]. However, the role of ADAM9 in mediating the ectodomain shedding of NRG1β and its influence on cancer progression remains understudied. Nevertheless, ADAM9 has been implicated in the ectodomain shedding of several ligands and receptors other than those in the EGF family that are critical to tumorigenesis and angiogenesis, such as FGFR2IIIb, Tie-2, Ephrin receptor B4 (EphB4), CD40, VCAM, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [74–76]. For example, an in vivo study using a murine model demonstrated that ADAM9 overexpression in prostate epithelial cells resulted in marked pathological alterations. These findings were attributed to the proteolytic release of EGF and FGFR2IIIb, suggesting that ADAM9 contributes to the initiation and progression of PCa [77]. Moreover, Guaiquil et al. reported that ADAM9 knockout inhibits pathological neovascularization and melanoma tumor growth and suggested that this inhibition may result from the ADAM9-mediated ectodomain shedding of molecules such as Tie-2, Flk-1, EphB4, CD40, and VCAM [35].

One study also reported that ADAM9 and ADAM12 cleave delta-like 1 (Dll1), a ligand of the Notch pathway, in mouse embryonic fibroblasts, facilitating the activation of Notch signaling [78]. Because the Notch pathway is crucial to various types of cancer [79], ADAM9 may contribute to cancer progression through this mechanism. However, direct evidence demonstrating that ADAM9 activates Notch signaling through Dll1 cleavage in cancer is lacking.

Crosstalk of ADAM9 and the tumor microenvironment

In addition to its oncogenic roles within tumor cells, ADAM9 influences the tumor microenvironment (TME) and contributes to cancer malignancy by interacting with immune cells.

Tumor–immune cell interactions

In HCC, ADAM9 mediates the shedding of MHC class I polypeptide-related sequence A, a ligand recognized by natural killer (NK) cells. Such shedding impairs NK cell recognition and enables cancer cells to evade immune surveillance [80]. In lung cancer cells, ADAM9 promotes proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type-9 (PCSK9)-mediated degradation of the low-density lipoprotein receptor, enhancing cholesterol production and IL6 secretion. IL6 inhibits IL12B release from dendritic cells, attenuating CD8+ T cell activation [81].

Pan-cancer analysis of the association of ADAM9 expression with immune cell infiltration

Because ADAM9 modulates the TME in multiple respects, we conducted an in silico analysis using the TCGA pan-cancer data set to explore the association of ADAM9 with immune cell infiltration. Immune cell infiltration data were obtained from the CIBERSORT database. We focused on immune cell types that exhibited significant correlations (p < 0.05) in more than half of the cancer types analyzed (Fig. 3).

Our results revealed that ADAM9 expression was significantly and inversely correlated with NK cell infiltration in 15 cancer cell types and with CD8+ T cell infiltration in 22 cancer cell types. These results extend the findings of earlier studies [81, 82] and suggest the role of ADAM9 in facilitating evasion of NK and CD8+ T cells across cancer types. Additionally, we observed that ADAM9 expression was negatively correlated with the infiltration of T follicular helper (TFH) cells (in 17 cancer types) and memory B cells (in 16 cancer types). TFH cells enhance immune responses by promoting the production of immunoglobulins and interferon-gamma, which supports B cell-mediated immunity [83]. B cells exert antitumor effects by generating tumor-specific antibodies and collaborating with other immune cells [84]. Increased infiltration of both TFH cells and B cells is associated with superior survival outcomes in cancer patients [85, 86]. However, few studies have investigated the role of ADAM9 in modulating these immune cells. Therefore, the present study explored whether ADAM9 contributed to the crosstalk among cancer cells, TFH cells, and B cells.

ADAM9 expression in genes is positively correlated with the infiltration of neutrophils, M2 macrophages, and resting CD4+ T cells. Neutrophils are a major member of the myeloid lineage and key TME regulators. Studies have indicated that a high neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio is a significant predictor of cancer metastasis [87, 88] and poor survival outcomes [89–91]. TGF-β released by cancer cells can prime neutrophils to suppress immune responses [92]. Notably, ADAM9 cleaves latency-associated peptide to generate activated TGF-β [29]. Additionally, in our earlier study, we revealed that the knockdown of ADAM9 inhibits TGF-β protein levels in OSCC [93]. These findings indicate a link between ADAM9-mediated TGF-β and neutrophil priming and suggest a direction for future research.

M2 macrophages promote cancer proliferation, metastasis, angiogenesis, and invasion [94]. Additionally, the anti-inflammatory properties of M2 macrophages contribute to resistance to immunotherapy [95, 96]. IL6, an M2 macrophage inducer [97–100], is released in cancers that overexpress ADAM9 such as lung cancer [81] and HCC [7]. Moreover, our TCGA analysis results reveal a positive correlation between ADAM9 and M2 macrophage infiltration in these two cancer types. These results suggest a link between ADAM9-driven IL6 secretion and M2 macrophage polarization. However, the role of ADAM9 in M2 macrophage polarization remains unclear. Nevertheless, the aforementioned findings reveal the role of ADAM9 in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Hence, elucidating how ADAM9 influences immune cell behavior within the TME is critical to designing effective immunotherapies. Although further mechanistic and functional studies are required to clarify the pathways through which ADAM9 exerts its effects, potential mechanisms involve ADAM9-mediated shedding of cytokines and growth factors, modulation of cancer cell metabolism through interactions with other molecules, and the release of ADAM9-S to facilitate crosstalk with immune cells.

ADAM9 involvement in oncogenic signaling mediation through intracellular signaling regulation

ADAM9 is implicated in the regulation of multiple oncogenic processes, such as DNA damage response, angiogenesis, chemoresistance, and metastasis [46, 101, 102]. Although its metalloprotease activity is a key contributor to its tumor-promoting functions, increasing evidence suggests that ADAM9 also exerts significant oncogenic effects through protease-independent mechanisms, particularly by modulating the activation and expression of oncogenes during cancer progression. Several crucial oncogenic pathways involve ADAM9 mediation, such as the β-catenin pathway, VEGFA/ANGPT2 [103], IL8/CXCR2 [102], and MEK/ERK [104]. ADAM9 also mediates the EMT process by activating Snail. These pathways contribute to various malignant phenotypes with enhanced proliferation, angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis. Cell proliferation/growth and cell cycle progression

ADAM family members are involved in the proliferation of various cancer types through their role in mediating the proteolytic release of TNF-α and HB-EGF [105]. For example, one study of ESCC revealed that ADAM9–EGFR–AKT signaling directly contributes to the cancer’s malignant properties, including proliferation [106]. In HCC and PCa cells, the inhibition of ADAM9 substantially reduces the proliferative and clonogenic abilities of cancer cells [107, 108]. Although the underlying mechanisms of this inhibition in HCC remain largely unexplored, in PCa, ADAM9 regulates the expression of islet-derived family member 4 (REG4) to promote cell cycle progression. The knockdown of ADAM9 leads to G1 phase cell cycle arrest in PCa cells and is accompanied by the upregulation of p21Cip1/WAF1. In TNBC, ADAM9 promotes tumor aggressiveness—such as proliferation, migration, and invasion—through the activation of the AKT/NF-κB signaling pathway [39]. Notably, this ADAM9-mediated AKT/NF-κB activation is likely independent of EGFR stimulation. In the study of Zhou et al., ADAM9 knockdown in TNBC cells did not alter HB-EGF levels or EGFR activation but significantly reduced the phosphorylation of AKT, IκBα, and NF-κBp65. Zhou et al. proposed that ADAM9 may interact with integrins to activate PI3K/AKT and NF-κB signaling cascades in TNBC cells. By contrast, Wang et al. revealed that the nuclear receptor-binding SET domain protein 2 (NSD2) can directly upregulate the expression of ADAM9 and EGFR, which is essential to the hyperactivation of ADAM9 and the EGFR-AKT/STAT3 signaling axis in TNBC cells and sustains tumorigenesis and tumor growth [109].

DNA repair and therapeutic efficiency

The role of ADAM9 in promoting cancer cell survival is well established, and studies have highlighted its association with reduced therapeutic efficacy. Increased ADAM9 expression is associated with reduced sensitivity to cisplatin in gastric cancer cells. Mechanistically, ADAM9 enhances cancer cell survival by activating DNA damage repair pathways, such as upregulating ATM and CHK2 and reducing γ-H2AX expression [101]. In PCa, ADAM9 is implicated in mediating resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapeutic agents such as doxorubicin, cisplatin, and taxotere, primarily through the modulation of E-cadherin and integrin expression [110]. Additionally, the PCa-associated fibroblast (CAF)-secreted isoform of ADAM9 (sADAM9) has been identified as a chemoattractant for androgen-independent PCa cells. sADAM9 promotes the directed migration of these cells through the integrin-mediated activation of the FAK/AKT signaling pathway, suggesting that enhanced sADAM9 levels derived from prostate CAFs contribute to the metastasis of castration-resistant PCa. Furthermore, sADAM9 induces the expression of ubinuclein-2 (UBN2) and aldo–keto reductase family 1 member C3 (AKR1C3), which are implicated in driving the transition of androgen-dependent PCa cells into an androgen-independent phenotype, facilitating resistance to androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT) [52].

In PDAC, ADAM9 functions as a key regulator by stabilizing KRAS through the reduction of PAI-1 binding [49]. Targeting ADAM9 suppresses KRAS activation and enhances the sensitivity of PDAC cells to gemcitabine treatment. Additionally, treatment with resveratrol induces ADAM9 degradation through ubiquitination in ESCC and lung cancer cells and provides a synergistic therapeutic effect with dasatinib or 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) [111].

EMT, migration, and invasion

The involvement of ADAM9 in promoting cancer metastasis through the regulation of EMT-associated markers has been well studied. This phenomenon has been observed in various cell types, such as bladder cancer, HCC, oral cancer, pancreatic cancer, and lung cancer [58, 112–114]. For example, IL6 has been identified as a pivotal inducer of HCC initiation under chronic inflammatory conditions. Dong et al. demonstrated that IL6 upregulates ADAM9 expression and facilitates the EMT through the activation of the JNK signaling pathway [7]. ADAM9-induced reactive oxygen species also facilitate Snail expression and are essential to the IL6 mediation of HCC. Similarly, Ding et al. revealed that the induction of ADAM9 by histamine receptor H1 (HRH1) can stimulate the expression of Snail and Slug, which are crucial transcriptional factors in the EMT of oral cancer cells. Upregulated Snail and Slug form a positive feedback loop that regulates HRH1 levels and promotes lymph node metastasis and tumor growth [93].

ADAM9 is also implicated in the remodeling of the ECM in tumor metastasis through its proteolytic effects on several membrane-bound proteins. ADAM9 cleaves ephrin-B and inhibits the ephrin-B-dependent repression of the AKT pathway in CRC [56]. In addition to this protease activity and integrin/ECM interaction, the expression of ADAM9 directly affects the expression in RNA of some ECM regulators, such as PAI-1 (plasminogen activator inhibitor-1), ADAM15, and MMP2 [46, 115]. The aforementioned studies have revealed the dominant role of ADAM9 in promoting cancer motility.

Angiogenesis

Angiogenesis is fundamental to tumor progression and is driven by the coordinated regulation of multiple factors. In lung cancer, a positive association between ADAM9 and angiopoietin-2 (ANGPT2), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), and plasminogen activator (PLAT) has been observed [103]. ADAM9 upregulation promotes neovascularization by enhancing the expression of these angiogenic factors, whereas loss of ADAM9 substantially impairs tumor progression in xenograft models. Similarly, Kossman et al. observed that the stable downregulation of ADAM9 in A549 cells resulted in substantially reduced IL8 release and reduced tumor nodules in the lungs in an in vivo model of intravenous injection [102]. Kossman et al. reported that this ADAM9-induced IL8 production and the interaction of IL8 and CXCR2 led to tube formation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells. Treating these cells with anti-CXCR2 antibody inhibited new vessel formation. These findings demonstrate the pro-tumorigenic role of ADAM9 through the regulation of angiogenesis.

Although ADAM9 is primarily a transmembrane protein or secreted factor in the TME, it also has nuclear functions. For example, ADAM9 acts as a transcription factor under hypoxic conditions, promoting ESCC progression [50]. Nuclear ADAM9 interacts with transcription factors such as USF1, hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α), and TP53 to inhibit SERPIN1 and PGK1 transcription, stimulating angiogenesis. Consequently, ADAM9 contributes to angiogenesis, distant metastasis, and poor prognosis in patients with ESCC. However, the detailed mechanisms through which ADAM9 promotes nuclear translocation remain unclear.

Upstream regulators of the ADAM9-mediated axis in cancer cells

Because ADAM9 is a prominent oncogene in various cancer types, understanding the mechanisms underlying its aberrant expression is essential to formulating treatment strategies. Studies have identified transcriptional factors that activate ADAM9 gene expression, such as HIF-1α [116] and estrogen receptor (ER) α [32]. Umeda et al. reported that HIF-1α binds to the promoter region of ADAM9 and enhances its expression in hypoxic renal epithelial cells [116]. HIF-1α promotes cancer metastasis, angiogenesis, and drug resistance. However, whether HIF-1α can transcriptionally activate ADAM9 in cancer cells remains unclear. Cong et al. revealed that the promoter region of ADAM9 contains multiple estrogen response elements and that estrogen treatment leads to the upregulation of ADAM9 in neuronal cells. This finding suggests that ERs may enhance ADAM9 transcriptional activity. Additionally, because ERs are critical to gynecological cancers, the interaction between ERs and ADAM9 in these cancers warrants further investigation [32].

Tumor-suppressive miRNAs can target the 3′UTR of ADAM9 and inhibit its expression. For example, miR-126 is frequently silenced by hypermethylation in cancers such as ESCC and lung cancer. Studies have demonstrated that this epigenetic silencing leads to increased ADAM9 levels, contributing to tumor progression [106, 117, 118]. Another miRNA, miR-302a, is downregulated in tumor tissues compared with normal cells in osteosarcoma. This reduction in miR-302a is associated with increased ADAM9 expression, which promotes osteosarcoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [119].

Other post-transcription mechanisms that regulate ADAM9 have also been identified. For example, the ubiquitin-specific protease 39 (USP39) promotes the maturation of ADAM9 mRNA, facilitating metastasis and invasion in glioma [120]. Several studies have also demonstrated that ADAM9 protein levels can be post-translationally regulated by various molecules. For example, in their study on preeclampsia, Li et al. reported that tripartite motif-containing protein 72 (TRIM72) promotes ADAM9 ubiquitination and leads to proteasomal degradation [53]. By contrast, Liu et al. demonstrated that ubiquitin-specific protease 22 (USP22) stabilizes ADAM9 by removing ubiquitin chains, preventing its degradation [54]. In our earlier study, we determined that N-acetyltransferase 10 (Naa10) interacts with ADAM9 and protects it from proteolytic degradation, and that silencing Naa10 leads to enhanced degradation of ADAM9. This critical interaction contributes to enhanced invasive potential in androgen-independent PCa [55]. Elucidating how these regulatory mechanisms are altered across cancer types is crucial to understanding ADAM9-driven oncogenesis and can inform therapeutic strategies targeting the upstream regulators of ADAM9.

Uncovering ADAM9-associated signaling in pan-cancer analysis

We conducted a gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using TCGA pan-cancer data in the Hallmark database to elucidate the roles of ADAM9-associated signaling pathways (Fig. 5). We focused on the signaling that was significantly enriched in patients whose cancers exhibited increased ADAM9 expression (false discovery rate < 0.01) in more than 16 cancer types. The predominant enriched pathway was protein secretion. Because ADAM9 contains a metalloproteinase domain that is responsible for the ectodomain shedding of various cell surface proteins, this finding reinforces the reliability of our analysis. ADAM9 expression was also positively correlated with EMT and TGF-β signaling pathways. Increasing evidence suggests that ADAM9 facilitates ECM proteolysis and enhances cancer cell migration and invasion [65, 121]. Additionally, ADAM9 promotes the release of cytokines such as IL6 and TGF-β, which can induce mesenchymal-like traits in cancer cells and condition the TME to support EMT [7, 93]. Although evidence suggests that ADAM9 can activate TGF-β signaling in nontransformed cells [29, 116], its role in modulating this pathway in cancer remains poorly understood. At present, only our earlier study has demonstrated that ADAM9 may promote EMT through TGF-β activation in OSCC [93]. Because TGF-β is pivotal to the promotion of EMT in malignant cells, the association between ADAM9-activated TGF-β signaling and EMT across other cancer types warrants further investigation.

The present study observed a strong association between ADAM9 expression and cell cycle regulatory processes, such as mitotic spindle assembly, the G2/M checkpoint, and E2F signaling. Although studies have demonstrated that ADAM9 knockdown induces G0/G1 cell cycle arrest in gastric cancer [25], bladder carcinoma [112], and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [122], the underlying regulatory mechanisms through which ADAM9 influences cell cycle progression remain unclear. These findings highlight the need for further investigation into ADAM9-driven cell cycle progression.

Increased ADAM9 expression is associated with metabolic reprogramming processes such as glycolysis and lipogenesis across multiple cancer types. For example, one study demonstrated that ADAM9 depletion in lung cancer cells was associated with the reduced expression of key enzymes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis, such as squalene epoxidase, NAD(P)-dependent steroid dehydrogenase-like protein, and lanosterol synthase [81]. Another study reported that ADAM9 expression is upregulated in macrophages cultured under high-glucose conditions [123]. Because the literature has suggested that glycolysis and lipogenesis are involved in tumorigenesis, the involvement of ADAM9 in cancer-associated metabolic reprogramming warrants further investigation.

ADAM9 as a target in therapeutic applications

ADAM9 is a membrane-bound protease involved in tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance across various cancers by remodeling the TME and enhancing oncogenic signaling; it is a promising therapeutic target [58]. Multiple types of inhibitors—specifically small molecules, biologics, peptide-based agents, nucleic acid therapeutics, and natural compounds—have been explored for their ability to modulate ADAM9 activity and suppress tumor development. Classes of ADAM9 inhibitors

Small-molecule inhibitors

The activity of ADAM9 can be inhibited by broad-spectrum, hydroxamate-based metalloprotease inhibitors, such as batimastat (BB94), marimastat, TAPI-2, GM6001, and GW280264X, which suppress ADAM9-dependent shedding of EphB4 in cell-based assays [124]. Additionally, ADAM10-selective hydroxamate GI254023X, marimastat, TMI-1, and GM6001 inhibited ADAM9 activity in biochemical assays [125]. Although the aforementioned compounds are capable of suppressing ADAM9 activity, they exhibit limited specificity and may unintentionally inhibit multiple matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), interfering with normal physiological functions. This poor selectivity compromises therapeutic efficacy and increases the risk of side effects. For example, marimastat, a broad-spectrum MMP inhibitor, reached phase III in the treatment of various cancers but was discontinued due to dose-limiting musculoskeletal toxicity [126], highlighting the need for inhibitors with enhanced specificity and more precise targeting strategies. One small-molecule inhibitor specifically targeting the catalytic domain of ADAM9 to suppress its enzymatic activity was developed by Huang et al. [49]. The inhibition of ADAM9 by this small-molecule compound selectively induced KRAS degradation and enhanced the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer cells to chemotherapy [49].

Biologics

Biologic inhibitors, specifically monoclonal antibodies and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) such as RAV-18, MGC028, and IMGC936 [127–129], target the extracellular domains of ADAM9 to prevent progression in vitro and in vivo. For example, treatment of GC cells with RAV-18, a catalytic-site-directed antibody for ADAM9, suppressed proliferation and invasion of GC cells and tumor growth in a GC xenograft model through the downregulation of EGFR signaling [128]. Neutralizing the anti-ADAM9 antibody significantly reduced viability and enhanced chemosensitivity to cisplatin in ovarian clear cell carcinoma cells [130]. ADCs such as IMGC936 deliver cytotoxic payloads such as maytansinoid (a microtubule inhibitor) to ADAM9-expressing tumor cells, mitigating the systemic toxicity associated with traditional chemotherapy [127]. In addition to ADAM9-expressing tumor cells, IMGC936 exhibits bystander killing potential in ADAM9-negative tumor cells. Currently, a human trial of IMGC936 (NCT04622774) in patients with advanced solid tumors has been initiated. IMGC936 exhibited dose-limiting ocular adverse events such as blurred vision and keratopathy, in both preclinical models and a first-in-human study. These findings are consistent with the known toxicities associated with microtubule inhibitor–based ADC payloads. Aside from ocular toxicity, no additional prominent safety signals were observed. In contrast to microtubule inhibitor–based ADCs, topoisomerase I inhibitor (TOP1i)–based payloads have demonstrated favorable clinical activity with a lower incidence of dose-limiting ocular toxicities. MGC028 is an ADAM9-targeting ADC incorporating a TOP1i-based payload. MGC028 also showed in vitro cytotoxicity against ADAM9-positive cancer cell lines and mediated bystander killing. In vivo, MGC028 exhibited specific antitumor activity in ADAM9-positive cell line-derived and patient-derived xenograft models and was well tolerated in a repeat-dose non-human primate toxicology study, with no evidence of the ocular toxicities observed with IMGC936. A first-in-human study of MGC028 in patients with advanced solid tumors has been initiated (NCT06723236)[131].

Peptide-based inhibitors

Peptide-based inhibitors are molecules that can inhibit the activity of ADAM proteins, which are involved in numerous cellular processes and disease pathways. ADAM pro-domains can function as highly specific intra- and inter-molecular inhibitors of ADAM catalytic activity. For example, the mouse ADAM9 pro-domain (pro-A9; AA 24–204), expressed and purified from Escherichia coli, acts as a competitive inhibitor of the human ADAM9 catalytic/disintegrin domain with strong specificity and an inhibition constant of 280 ± 34 nM. In SY5Y neuroblastoma cells, pro-A9-mediated inhibition of ADAM9 enhances ADAM10 activity, promoting the cleavage of APP into soluble APPα, a process protective against Alzheimer disease [132]. Additionally, APP has been implicated in the progression of various cancers [133]. The recombinant disintegrin domain of ADAM9 (rADAM9D) binds to MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells through the β1, α3, αvβ3, αvβ5, and α2 integrins and inhibits the adhesion of this cell line [134]. Similarly, in DU145 prostate cancer cells, rADAM9D inhibits cell adhesion, migration, and invasion primarily through interaction with α6β1 integrin [135].

Nucleic-acid-based therapeutics

Although small-molecule drugs dominate the pharmaceutical market, their potential is limited due to their dependence on the druggability of targets. By contrast, nucleic acids are universal gene building blocks that encode proteins and have a greater potential in therapies for various diseases. RNA therapeutic drugs comprise antisense oligonucleotides (ASO), mRNA vaccines, small interfering RNA (siRNA) and miRNA, and single guide RNA (sgRNA) for CRISPR–Cas9 systems [136]. siRNA-mediated ADAM9 gene silencing inhibits the metastasis of various cancers [23, 137]. ADAM9 is also targeted by numerous miRNAs such as miR-126, miR-154, miR-520f, and miR-543 to modulate the tumor growth and metastasis of various cancer types [24, 113, 118, 138, 139]. ADAM9 knockout in TC1 lung cancer cells by a CRISPR–Cas9 system inhibits tumor growth in vivo by enhancing immunity in the TME [81] and reducing angiogenesis [103].

Natural compounds

Natural compounds such as flavonoids inhibit ADAM9 through various mechanisms, one of which involves downregulating its expression by inducing proteasomal degradation of the protein and inhibiting its enzymatic activity. For example, resveratrol inhibits ADAM9 protein expression in lung and esophageal cancer cells through the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway. Moreover, resveratrol exhibits synergistic anticancer effects when combined with clinical chemotherapeutics [111]. Other flavonoids such as fisetin and galangin also reduce the expression of ADAM9 in mRNA and protein by inducing ERK signaling in RCC and glioma, respectively [140–142]. Additionally, licochalcone A, a flavonoid isolated from licorice, inhibits the migration and invasion of glioma cells by downregulating ADAM9 [143]. In addition to flavonoids, retinoids inhibit the enzymatic activity of ADAM9 in HCC cells, as indicated by the reduction in soluble MHC class I polypeptide-related sequence A after retinoid treatment [144]. Bufalin (BUF), an effective natural compound, has been shown to downregulate ADAM9 and its protease activity by suppressing IL6 in HCC cells. Furthermore, BUF enhances antitumor immunity and potentiates the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [145].

Limitations and challenges of ADAM9 inhibition

Although ADAM9 inhibitors exhibit great therapeutic potential, their clinical application is hindered by several limitations. One major concern with small-molecule and natural compound inhibitors is their unintended off-target effects. Many ADAM9 inhibitors also target other proteases, such as ADAM10, ADAM17, and MMPs, resulting in off-target inhibition that often results in toxicity. Musculoskeletal toxicity is a major side effect that limits the tolerability and clinical use of these agents [150]. Biologic inhibitors, such as ADAM9-targeting ADCs, offer several advantages in cancer therapy, such as increased selectivity, enhanced efficacy, and reduced systemic toxicity. However, linker stability, payload potency, and overall optimization must be addressed to achieve a favorable balance between efficacy and safety [151]. Emerging technologies such as CRISPR–Cas9 genome editing also can target proteases such as ADAM9 in cancer. Despite their potential, the clinical use of CRISPR–Cas9 poses several challenges. These include the risk of off-target genetic modifications, which may cause unpredictable effects and raise substantial safety and regulatory concerns [152]. The immunogenicity of Cas9 proteins is another obstacle to in vivo applications; additional research is required to determine methods to mitigate immune responses against these components.

Future perspectives on therapies targeting ADAM9

Innovative drugs are being explored to enhance the specificity, stability, and therapeutic index of ADAM9 inhibitors. One promising approach involves the development of proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) designed to selectively degrade ADAM9. Unlike traditional inhibitors that merely inhibit enzymatic activity, PROTACs function by redirecting the ubiquitin–proteasome system to target ADAM9 and degrade it completely. This strategy irreversibly eliminates the protein, potentially reducing drug resistance and minimizing off-target effects compared with conventional reversible inhibition [153].

Nanoparticle-mediated drug delivery systems are another promising strategy to enhance the bioavailability and tumor selectivity of ADAM9 inhibitors. Platforms such as lipid nanoparticles, polymeric nanoparticles, and exosome-based systems are being investigated to improve the stability and intracellular delivery of siRNAs, peptides, and small molecules. These advanced delivery vehicles offer controlled drug release, enhanced tumor tissue penetration, and reduced systemic toxicity, rendering them strong candidates for future clinical applications [154].

Next-generation ADAM9 inhibitors—incorporating advanced drug delivery systems, gene-editing technologies, and artificial-intelligence-driven drug discovery—may transform cancer treatment. As technological innovations advance, the successful translation of these novel technologies into clinically effective therapies is critical to realizing the therapeutic potential of targeting ADAM9 in cancer cells.

Conclusions and directions for future research

Studies across multiple cancer types have highlighted the crucial roles of ADAM9 in tumor progression and the maintenance of malignancy. Through its regulation of the TME—including ECM remodeling and modulation of tumor-infiltrating immune cells—ADAM9 contributes to the establishment of a supportive niche for immune evasion, enhanced cell motility, and distant metastasis. Additionally, ADAM9-mediated ectodomain shedding of growth factors can activate oncogenic signaling pathways, promoting intrinsic tumor cell survival and proliferation. These findings reveal that ADAM9 is a promising therapeutic target. Strategies to inhibit ADAM9 expression thus have substantial potential to revolutionize cancer treatment.

Increased ADAM9 expression at both the transcript and protein levels has been consistently observed in cancer tissues. However, few studies have distinguished between the ADAM9-L and ADAM9-S isoforms. Because ADAM9-S is soluble and circulates, its measurement in body fluids may serve as a valuable diagnostic or prognostic biomarker for cancer. Nevertheless, established technological platforms designed to detect and differentiate ADAM9-S in clinical specimens are lacking, hindering the translation of this scientific insight into clinical practice. A comprehensive understanding of ADAM9 based on the literature, in silico database analyses, and cellular targeting approaches, is essential to advancing its translational application in oncology and providing clinical benefits to patients.
