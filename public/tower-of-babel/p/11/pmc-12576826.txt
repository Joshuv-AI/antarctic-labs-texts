Introduction of Periodic Ketone Units on Vinyl Polymers via a Radical Alternating Copolymerization and Postpolymerization Modification: Sequence-Oriented Photodegradation in the Bulk State
Authors: Keita Kuroda, Makoto Ouchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The synthesis and the sequence-oriented bulk-state photodegradation of sequence-controlled polymers with a periodic structure bearing acrylamide, ketone, and vinyl ether units is reported. The alternating terpolymers can be synthesized via a radical copolymerization of a silyl-protected 1,3-butadiene bearing a methyl ether group (SBD) with a pentafluorophenyl acrylate (PFA) monomer in an alternating and 1,4-regioselective fashion. This process is followed by the addition of an amine to trigger a cascade aminolysis–desilylation reaction. The pendant alkyl group on the acrylamide unit, which is derived from the added amine, is crucial for determining the physical properties of a series of polymers, particularly the glass-transition temperature (T g). Relatively flexible and sufficiently thermally stable polymers with T g values below room temperature could be degraded under UV irradiation in the bulk state. Control experiments revealed that synergistic interactions between the periodically arranged ketone, vinyl ether, and acrylamide units were essential for promoting the photodegradation.

Introduction

Polymers synthesized via the addition polymerization of olefin compounds have been utilized in various materials. The popularity of these polymers is due to their inherent advantages, which include the variety of available monomers, the tunability of their side chains, the variety of polymer architectures that are easily accessible, and above all, the high stability of the resultant C–C bonded polymers. The pollution of the environment with polymer materials, as highlighted by the current concern over microplastics, has encouraged us to seek ways by which such stable polymers can be degraded into oligomers in response to specific stimuli, followed by the biodegradation of these oligomers into environmentally benign substances. − Indeed, some efforts have been devoted toward the synthesis of on-demand degradable polymers, which are stable as materials but can be degraded under particular stimuli after use. One of the common methodologies to impart degradability into a polymer is the copolymerization with cyclic monomers to introduce labile bonds, such as esters, thioethers, and thioesters, on the backbone (Figure A). − Another possible method to degrade polymers is to introduce a trigger bond for the generation of radicals onto the pendant groups of the polymer. This radical species then migrates to the polymer backbone causing C–C bond cleavage in the backbone, thus degrading the polymer (Figure B). Several trigger bonds have been reported for radical generation, such as carbon–chlorine (C–Cl), , carbon–boron (C–B), carbon–carboxylic acid (C–COOH), and carbon–hydrogen (C–H) bonds. In most cases, a solution process is required for the generation of the radical in conjunction with a catalyst that is subjected to heating or light irradiation. Given these precedents, the synthesis of on-demand degradable polymers in the bulk state remains, at present, a challenge.

Strategies for the synthesis of degradable polymers via radical-addition polymerization. (A) Copolymerization with a cyclic monomer, (B) copolymerization with a monomer carrying a trigger pendant, (C) copolymerization with carbon monoxide, and (D) alternating copolymerization for periodic incorporation of ketones (this work).

Carbon–oxygen double bonds (CO) are known to undergo a photochemical bond cleavage, the so-called the Norrish reaction, which proceeds under light irradiation at a specific wavelength. Thus, the introduction of ketone units in the backbone of vinyl polymers represents a promising way to achieve on-demand degradation of polymers in the bulk state (Figure C). Carbonyl groups can be introduced into vinyl-polymer chains via metal-coordination polymerization in the presence of carbon monoxide. − The propagation proceeds in an alternating fashion, and the resultant polymers are degradable via photolysis. On the other hand, it is not straightforward to use carbon monoxide as a comonomer in radical copolymerization with conjugated vinyl monomers because after the addition of carbon monoxide the propagating radical species undergo a backbiting chain-transfer reaction to yield an aldehyde terminal and midchain radical species. Consequently, this process impedes the introduction of a carbonyl group in the middle of the chain via radical copolymerization with carbon monoxide.

In this study, we focused on keto–enol tautomerism to synthesize sequence-controlled polymers with ketone units that appear periodically on the backbone (Figure D) via radical copolymerization. We conducted a retrosynthesis of a vinyl polymer composed of a triad sequence containing a ketone group. Thus, we conceived that the alternating copolymerization of a vinyl monomer with a 1,3-butadiene derivative bearing a silyl ether could be used to create the desired polymer. We conducted the alternating copolymerization of a silyl ether 1,3-butadiene bearing a methyl-ether group (SBD) with an acrylate comonomer bearing a pentafluorophenyl ester (PFA) to create a polymer with alternating electron-rich and -poor monomers. In addition, the PFA unit can be transformed into an acrylamide moiety via an aminolysis postpolymerization modification (PPM). Here, a library synthesis with different amine molecules is useful for tuning the physical properties of the polymer. The aim of this strategy is the construction of sequence-controlled copolymers periodically composed of three different units carrying amide, ketone, and methyl-ether groups. This study presents a detailed investigation into the radical copolymerization behavior of SBD and PFA, the structural characterization of the resulting polymers, PPMs, and the catalyst-free bulk photodegradation of the resultant polymers.

Results and Discussion

First, a solution of SBD in toluene was heated to 30 or 60 °C in the presence of radical initiators (AIBN or v-70) for 24 h. However, under these conditions, SBD was not consumed and no polymer was obtained (Figure A). In contrast, when methyl acrylate (MA) was combined with SBD (1:1 molar ratio) and heated to 30 °C with v-70, both monomers were consumed (conv. (MA) = 22.1%; conv. (SBD) = 22.4% in 24 h). However, the molecular weight of the resulting polymer was relatively low (M n = 2500) (Figure S1). On the other hand, when PFA was used instead of MA in the radical copolymerization with SBD at 60 °C, both monomers were smoothly consumed (83%/97% in 24 h) (Figure entry 1) and the solution became viscous indicating the generation of a high-molecular-weight polymer. Indeed, the molecular weight determined by gel-permeation chromatography (GPC) was high (M n = 44000). In the 1H NMR spectrum of the resulting polymer solution (Figure B), the peak of the methine proton adjacent to the methoxy group ( a ) at 5.35 ppm decreased and some broad signals were observed indicating the formation of the polymer. However, peculiar sharp peaks appeared at 5.0–5.2 ppm, which were attributed to olefinic protons ( A ) of the adduct of the Diels–Alder (DA) reaction between PFA and SBD. Here, PFA workes as a dienophile for the diene and the DA reaction occurs in conjunction with the radical copolymerization. The integral ratio of the NMR peaks indicated that 20% of the monomer was converted to the copolymer and 80% to the DA adduct. Subsequently, we studied the polymerization conditions, varying e.g., temperature and initiator concentration, to promote the radical copolymerization over the DA reaction, and the following trends were observed: The copolymerization predominates over the DA reaction at lower temperatures, while higher concentrations of monomers and radical initiators promote the copolymerization. Eventually, at higher substrate concentrations ([PFA]0/[SBD]0/[AIBN]0 = 1500/1500/90 mM in toluene) at 0 °C under UV irradiation (Figure entry 7) a higher ratio of copolymerization (82%) than DA reaction (18%) was achieved (Figures A and S2). The copolymer obtained under these conditions exhibits a relatively high molecular weight (M n = 23000, M w/M n = 2.70; Figure S2). To further investigate the radical copolymerization behavior, the reactivity ratios [r 1 and r 2 for PFA (M1) and SBD (M2)] were determined (Figure C). For that purpose, the copolymerization was conducted with five different initial monomer feed ratios ([M1]0/[M2]0 = 250/750, 333/667, 500/500, 667/333, and 750/250 mM) and the amount of monomer consumed in the copolymerization was calculated by subtracting the amount used in the DA reaction from the total amount consumed. The r 2 value was 0, which is consistent with the inability of SBD to homopolymerize, and the fact that the r 1 value was nearly zero (r 1 = 0.016) is due to the electron-deficient character of PFA. Thus, the copolymerization of PFA and SBD proceeds in an alternating fashion. The r 1 value when n-butyl-vinyl ether was used as the M2 monomer instead of SBD was a little higher (r 1 = 0.17) (Figure S3). Thus, the conjugated structure and/or higher electron density of SBD is likely responsible for an increase in the degree of crossover propagation.

Next, the obtained copolymer was purified by preparative SEC in order to remove any lower-molecular-weight compounds and the structure was analyzed using 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figure A). The peak specific to the methine proton of the “in-chain” double bond of the 1,4 adduct (d) was observed at ∼ 4.5 ppm, whereas the signals attributable to protons of the side-chain double bond arising from 1,2-addition were not observed. Therefore, the addition to SBD was found to occur in a 1,4-regioselective fashion. The integral ratios of peaks b (methine proton of the PFA backbone; 3.3 ppm), d, and g (methoxy protons of the SBD side chain; 3.2 ppm) gave an averaged composition ratio [n (PFA):m (SBD)] of almost 1:1 (1.00:1.01), which is consistent with the alternating copolymerization suggested by the monomer reactivity ratios. A structural analysis using 1H–13C heteronuclear multiple bond correlation (HMBC) corroborated the presence of an alternating sequence (Figure B). Here, the correlation of the peak from the PFA backbone methine proton (b) at 3.3 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum with the peaks of the neighboring carbons in the 13C NMR spectrum is crucial for the two-dimensional analysis. Proton b is strongly correlated with the carbons of the SBD backbone (F and C) as well as the methylene carbon of the PFA backbone (A). On the other hand, proton b hardly showed a correlation with its own methine carbon B, indicating there are no continuous PFA units in the polymer. The lack of correlation between proton peak b and carbon peak E indicates that SBD was incorporated in a 1,4-regioselective manner (Figure S5). Figure C shows the proposed polymer structure based on the structural analyses and the copolymerization behavior. The two monomers, PFA and SBD, undergo alternating propagation, whereby SBD is incorporated in a 1,4-regioselective manner. The resulting copolymer can be regarded as an ABC-type alternating terpolymer composed of PFA (A), trimethylsilyl vinyl ether (TMSVE, B), and methyl-vinyl ether (MVE, C), although units B and C are connected via a carbon–carbon double bond.

The deprotection of the trimethylsilyl (TMS) group of the PFA-SBD alternating copolymer affords a ketone group in the backbone (Figure A). Meanwhile, the aminolysis reaction with the pentafluorophenyl ester pendant of the PFA unit permits its transformation into various amide pendants. Thus, we studied the conditions required to realize the two reactions quantitatively. First, we attempted the TMS deprotection using cesium fluoride (CsF, 1.1 equiv. per SBD unit). However, the corresponding 1H NMR spectrum of the polymer indicated the formation of a cyclic lactone structure via the attack of the resulting enolate anion on the neighboring pentafluorophenyl ester pendant (Figure S6B). Therefore, we examined the aminolysis by adding an excess of amine (benzylamine, 2 equiv. per PFA unit) to the copolymer. A 1H NMR structural analysis indicated the successful aminolysis of the PFA units as well as TMS deprotection to give ketone units (Figure S6C). However, the structural analysis also showed that an unfavorable reaction accompanied the aminolysis and deprotection reactions (Figures S6C and S7). Specifically, the added amine triggers the TMS deprotection and the resulting enolate ion undergoes an intramolecular aldol reaction with a ketone moiety to form a six-membered cyclic alcohol (Figure S6D).

Aminolysis/Si deprotection of the PFA-SBD alternating copolymer with benzylamine. (A) Optimized reaction conditions required to suppress side reactions. (B) NMR structural analysis of the resulting polymer. (C) Plausible mechanism for the aminolysis and Si deprotection cascade reaction.

To avoid this side reaction, we decided to use tert-butanol (t-BuOH) as a proton donor and an alcohol with lower nucleophilicity. We slowly added benzylamine (2 equiv. per PFA unit) to a solution of the PFA-SBD alternating copolymer in THF/t-BuOH (5/3, v/v) at 30 °C and the solution was allowed to stand for 24 h. A direct analysis of the reaction solution using 19F NMR spectroscopy revealed the quantitative elimination of pentafluorophenol (Figure S8). The product was purified to remove low-molecular-weight compounds by dialysis, followed by a structural analysis using 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy to ascertain the progress of the aminolysis and deprotection reactions (Figure B). The peaks from the trimethylsilyl group and internal olefin (f, d) disappeared and instead new peaks from the amide (h’, 8.4 ppm), methylene (i’, 4.2–4.3 ppm), and benzene (j’, 7.1–7.3 ppm) groups appeared. The monomer unit ratio (m:n) was calculated to be 1:1 from the integral ratio of peaks j’ and g’ (methoxy group of SBD), demonstrating that a quantitative aminolysis reaction had occurred. Importantly, tertiary alcohol signals at 5.6–6.0 ppm, which were attributed to intramolecular aldol reactions in the absence of the t-BuOH proton source, were not observed. The transformation of the carbonyl group was confirmed using 13C NMR spectroscopy and a peak characteristic of the carbonyl group was observed at 207.5 ppm in the 13C NMR spectrum (D, Figure B).

In general, fluorine anions are used for the deprotection of silyl-enol ethers , and amines are not suitable for this purpose. A plausible mechanism for the Si-deprotection upon addition of an amine is shown in Figure C. First, the nucleophilic attack of the amine at the PFA unit gives an alkoxy nucleophile and the resultant nucleophile reacts with the neighboring TMS group on the internal olefin to afford a carbonyl group with the assistance of t-BuOH. The resultant t-BuOH alkoxide attacks the TMS group of the amine-based acetal intermediate to yield an pendant amide group through the release of a pentafluorophenoxy anion. Therefore, the periodic sequence of the polymer is essential to accomplish both the aminolysis and Si-deprotection via a cascade reaction.

We used various amine compounds, such as n-butylamine, n-octylamine, 2-ethylhexylamine, and cyclooctylamine, instead of benzylamine in the PPM step to change the pendant group of the amide unit in the ketone-embedded copolymer (Figure A). The resulting copolymers were purified by dialysis, using either methanol or N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) as the solvent, depending on the solubility. We then studied the effects that the resulting chemical structures and/or T g values had on the degradation behavior of the polymers. The quantitative transformation to both acrylamide and ketone units was confirmed using H NMR spectroscopy (Figures S11, S14, S17, and S20). The resultant copolymers, P-Bzy (from benzylamine), P-nBu (from n-butylamine), P-nOct (from n-octylamine), P-EtHy (from 2-ethylhexylamine), and P-cOct (from cyclooctylamine) exhibited T g values that were dependent on the pendant structure (Figures S9, S12, S15, S18, and S21). P-nBu, P-nOct, and P-EtHy exhibit relatively low T g values (below room temperature), while P-Bzy and P-cOct exhibit high T g values.

Synthesis of ketone-embedded polymers with different amine compounds and their catalyst-free bulk photodegradation. (A) A series of ketone-embedded polymers and their glass-transition temperatures (T g). (B) Photodegradation of the polymer films in the bulk state.

Then, the photodegradability of these polymers in the bulk state was studied. Importantly, the polymers exhibited sufficient thermal stability (5 wt % weight-loss temperature (T d5%) = 170–200 °C), as supported by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Figures S10, S13, S16, S19, and S22). Polymer films of ∼ 20 mm width were prepared and were exposed to UV light (λ = 365 nm) under atmospheric conditions for 24 h (Figure B). The molecular weight of the polymers was compared to that of the corresponding pristine samples using GPC. The polymers with lower T g values (P-nBu, P-nOct, and P-EtHy) underwent obvious degradation and the curves shifted to lower molecular weight and the peak-top molecular weight (Mp) was significantly decreased. In the 1H NMR spectra, the peak intensities from the protons close to the ketone group were decreased, indicating that the Norrish reaction triggered the degradation (Figure S23). On the other hand, the polymers with higher T g values (P-CyO and P-Bzy) were hardly degraded. Incidentally, we also performed the degradation experiment with P-nBuMe under an argon atmosphere (without oxygen, Figure S24) and found that oxygen is required for the bulk degradation reaction. Accordingly, when the T g is high, the polymer exists in a glassy state at room temperature, leading to low oxygen permeability and, consequently, reduced degradability. Notably, when the P-Bzy film underwent heating at 80 °C (higher than its T g) under UV irradiation, the sample became insoluble in organic solvents. A radical species was probably generated from the carbonyl group that caused cross-linking rather than degradation at high temperature. These results indicate that not only is chain mobility essential for photodegradation in the bulk state but this must also be balanced with a degree of radical generation.

The polymers are characterized by a periodic sequence of acrylamide, ketone, and methyl-vinyl-ether units. To clarify the role of these groups in the photodegradation, we synthesized analogues of the copolymer and examined their degradation behavior (Figure A). A 1:1 copolymer of n-butyl acrylamide and ethyl-vinyl ether (EVE) was prepared as a ketone-free analogue (Figure S25). A Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) analysis confirmed the absence of a carbonyl absorption peak, indicating that the polymer lacked ketone functionalities (Figure S26). This ketone-free copolymer exhibited no degradation upon UV irradiation, demonstrating that the presence of ketone groups is essential for photodegradation (Figures A and S27). Addition of benzophenone (1 eq. for each EVE unit) to the ketone-free copolymer and subsequent UV irradiation rendered the resulting film insoluble in organic solvents (Figures A and S27). Most likely, the benzophenone was exited to generate a radical species in the polymer (C–H activation of the EVE pendant) to induce a cross-linking reaction. We also synthesized a copolymer without the methyl-ether pendant by using a butadiene monomer bearing only TMS ether instead of SBD (Figures S28-S30). Interestingly, this copolymer did not undergo degradation upon UV irradiation but became insoluble (Figure A). This result suggests that the ether group adjacent to the ketone is most likely responsible for the degradation. We propose that an oxygen-centered radical species, generated from the ketone moiety, abstracts hydrogen from the neighboring methyl group, leading to the formation of formaldehyde (Figure B). Indeed, in contrast to the copolymer without the methyl-ether, P–C4 gave formaldehyde after UV irradiation, which was confirmed using formaldehyde detection paper (Figure S32).

Most reported photodegradation reactions of ketone-containing polymers require irradiation with high-energy UV light at wavelengths below 300 nm. In contrast, the copolymer in this study underwent bulk degradation upon irradiation at 365 nm. We hypothesized that the ketone group interacts with the NH group of the neighboring amide unit via hydrogen bonding to enhance the photoactivity. We thus synthesized a copolymer free from NH bonds by employing N-methylbutylamine in the PPM to investigate the effect of the amide NH bond on the photodegradability (Figure S33). The NH-free copolymer was hardly degraded compared to P-nBu, despite having a T g value (26 °C) similar to that of P-nBu (Figures A and S34). We measured the UV–vis spectra of the thin films in order to examine the difference in light-absorption behavior. P-nBu exhibited absorption bands at 280 and 340 nm, whereas the NH-free copolymer only showed a single absorption peak at 280 nm (Figure S35). Notably, both polymers were degraded in the same manner upon irradiation at 280 nm (Figure S36). These results support our hypothesis that the hydrogen of the amide group interacts with the ketone to induce a bathochromic shift, thereby enabling effective photodegradation of P-nBu when irradiated at 365 nm in the bulk state. This insight highlights the critical role that the hydrogen bonding in the periodic sequence plays in enhancing the efficiency of the photodegradation of the copolymer.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we achieved the radical copolymerization of a silyl-protected 1,3-butadiene bearing a methyl ether (SBD) with PFA in an alternating and 1,4-regioselective fashion. The resulting copolymer is able to undergo a cascade aminolysis–desilylation reaction upon addition of an amine compound. The resulting alternating terpolymers that consist of acrylamide, ketone, and vinyl-ether units are thermally stable but degradable upon irradiation with UV light (365 nm). A library synthesis with different amine compounds and syntheses of various analogues clarified that backbone flexibility, carbonyl group activation by the neighboring amide groups, and radical trapping by the methyl-ether pendant are essential for promoting the photodegradation. The significance of this study lies in demonstrating the strategic utility of ketone skeletons as degradable motifs, realized through the synthesis of sequence-controlled polymers that periodically incorporate ketone groups.
