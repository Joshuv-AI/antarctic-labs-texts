MDFormer: a multi-scale dense dilated transformer model for 3D medical image segmentation
Authors: Qiuxia Li, Haiyue Zhao, Ying Zhai, Fangzuo Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To improve the precision of medical image segmentation for enhanced clinical diagnosis and treatment, this study focuses on overcoming the limitations of existing models in capturing multi-scale information under resolution constraints while maintaining efficiency without compromising accuracy. We developed a multi-scale dense dilated Transformer (MDFormer), which integrates the multi-scale dense dilated self-attention (MDDSA) module. This module dynamically adjusts the size of the dense dilated matrix based on the resolution characteristics, enabling spatial downsampling at a fixed resolution and cross-scale information aggregation to effectively reduce computational costs. Our model achieved DSC of 92.7% on the ACDC dataset and 86.88% on the Synapse dataset, with 37.7 M parameters and 47.39G FLOPs. Paired T-tests were performed, demonstrating statistically significant improvements in segmentation performance compared to other models. The proposed MDFormer significantly enhances medical image segmentation through the effective leveraging of multi-scale information extraction, demonstrating promising potential in various medical image segmentation tasks and laying a solid foundation for future advancements in transformer-based models.

Introduction

Medical imaging techniques primarily encompass computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), magnetic particle imaging (MPI), optical imaging (OI), and ultrasound imaging (US)1. Statistical evidence indicates that medical imaging accounts for roughly 90% of all medical data2, thereby playing an indispensable role in clinical decision-making. Precise segmentation of target structures within medical images furnishes critical information for pathological analysis and is essential for devising treatment strategies, monitoring disease progression, and assessing patient prognosis.

The integration of medicine and artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a highly promising field, driven by advancements in AI technology. Since the 1990s, the convolutional neural network (CNN) has become a predominant model in deep learning (DL), with extensive applications in medical imaging3. The U-Net model has been a leading force in medical image processing since its success in the 2015 ISBI Cell Tracking Challenge4, demonstrating superior performance. Unlike earlier architectures that relied solely on an encoder structure, U-Net features a symmetrical encoder-decoder architecture. The encoder is responsible for feature extraction, while the decoder reconstructs the original resolution. Additionally, skip connections are incorporated to combine low-level and high-level information, thereby enhancing image granularity. U-Net and its variants have been extensively utilized in medical image segmentation5–8, demonstrating exceptional performance. However, conventional CNNs encounter inherent limitations in achieving higher segmentation accuracy due to localized convolutions that prioritize local spatial structures over global context9. Although various strategies, such as deeper architectures10, recursive connections11, residual/skip connections12, and dilated convolutions13, have been proposed to address this issue, they still fail to fully resolve the fundamental constraint of limited receptive fields in early layers, thereby restricting the effective modeling of long-range dependencies. Therefore, alternative approaches are necessary to enhance global context modeling for medical image segmentation.

Inspired by the success of Vision Transformers in natural language processing (NLP)14, Transformer-based architectures have revolutionized medical image segmentation by integrating local feature extraction with global contextual modeling, thereby effectively mitigating the induction bias inherent in CNNs. Enhanced variants achieve comparable or superior accuracy to conventional CNNs15–17 by effectively integrating fine-grained spatial details with holistic structural awareness, thereby demonstrating robust performance across diverse anatomical targets. The seminal TransUNet18 was pioneering in integrating convolutional layers, which are adept at preserving fine-grained spatial details, with self-attention mechanisms that excel at capturing long-range dependencies. When evaluated across a variety of datasets, TransUNet achieved a DSC of 77.48% on the Synapse dataset, representing an 8% improvement over V-Net. To tackle the challenges of under-segmentation and over-segmentation in multi-object segmentation tasks, Yang et al.19 introduced TransNUNet. This model extends TransUNet’s encoder by incorporating multi-layer self-attention mechanisms and a convolutional attention module20 during the upsampling stage to refine feature maps. The hybrid architecture achieved a DSC of 92.1% on the MM-WHS 2017 dataset, thereby showcasing its superior performance in cardiac segmentation.

Building upon the successful application of the Swin Transformer in computer vision21, Cao et al.22 introduced a window-based self-attention mechanism for two-dimensional (2D) image segmentation. However, this approach exhibited limitations when applied to the context of three-dimensional (3D) medical data compatibility. The networks D-former23, nnFormer24, and UNETR + +25 have been evaluated on 3D datasets across various experiments. Among these, nnFormer integrates both convolutional and self-attention operations while extending the window-based and sliding window self-attention mechanisms of Swin Transformer from 2D to 3D. By incorporating volume-based self-attention modules, it constructs a hierarchical feature pyramid that effectively captures Z-axis information in 3D medical images for segmentation tasks. However, this approach inevitably increases the number of parameters and FLOPs, potentially compromising robustness. Recently, Shaker et al.25 introduced UNETR++, a lightweight and efficient model for 3D medical image segmentation. This model incorporates an effective pairwise attention (EPA) module, where the Q and K mapping functions share weights to integrate spatial interactions with channel interdependencies, thereby facilitating communication between spatial and channel branches for complementary advantages and reducing parameter overhead. UNETR + + achieved state-of-the-art performance on the Synapse dataset with a DSC of 87.22% and demonstrated strong generalization capabilities by achieving high segmentation accuracy on three additional datasets. Similarly, Dae-former26 and DA-TransUNet27 employ dual attention mechanism to more accurately capture critical features, further enhancing the segmentation outcomes of medical images.

Though Transformer-based models have significantly advanced medical image segmentation, three major challenges persist: (1) Computational Complexity: Standard Transformers are computationally demanding, and their adaptation to 3D tasks further intensifies this issue due to an exponential increase in FLOPs. (2) Limited Multi-Scale Feature Extraction: The original self-attention mechanism lacks the design for multi-scale information integration, thereby restricting its capacity to effectively aggregate features across diverse spatial scales. (3) Accuracy-Efficiency Trade-off: While many state-of-the-art models deliver superior segmentation performance, they often do so at the expense of reduced computational efficiency. To address these challenges, we propose the MDFormer architecture specifically designed for the segmentation of 3D medical images. The primary contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

(I)A novel MDFormer model is proposed, where the MDDSA module dynamically adjusts the size of the dense dilation matrix based on resolution characteristics at each stage. This mechanism enables efficient multi-scale global self-attention and generates multi-feature representations for multi-scale tensors. Additionally, the MDDSA module leverages the sparsity of the attention matrix to handle spatial downscaling and aggregate information for keys and values across multiple scales at a fixed resolution, thereby significantly reducing computational costs.(II)To enhance feature discriminability, we incorporate a 3D convolutional branch combined with the self-attention mechanism. This design allows the network to effectively capture both the critical features of small lesions within the global context and the fine-grained details in the local neighborhood. Experimental results demonstrate that on both the Synapse and ACDC datasets, the MDFormer model trained from scratch not only significantly improves overall segmentation performance but also achieves this with minimal computational cost, thereby rendering it highly suitable for resource-constrained medical imaging applications.

Taken together, Transformer-based medical image segmentation models face a critical limitation in 3D scenarios: they typically exhibit prohibitive computational complexity due to exponential FLOP growth in 3D, inadequate multi-scale feature integration that hinders segmentation across diverse anatomical structures, or compromised accuracy when efficiency is prioritized-rarely addressing all three challenges simultaneously. The proposed MDFormer is specifically designed to bridge this gap. Its MDDSA module, featuring dynamic dilation matrix adjustment and attention sparsity, effectively reduces the computational burden of 3D Transformers while enabling efficient multi-scale feature aggregation. Complementing this, a dedicated 3D convolutional branch allows the model to capture both global contextual information and local fine-grained details without sacrificing computational efficiency. These architectural innovations enable MDFormer to overcome the long-standing accuracy-efficiency trade-off in 3D medical image segmentation, thereby addressing a key deficiency in state-of-the-art methods that fail to balance computational feasibility, multi-scale representation, and high performance-factors essential for deployment in resource-constrained clinical environments.

Method

The overall architecture of MDFormer

The MDFormer, as depicted in Fig. 1(a), adopts a U-shaped architecture that integrates both encoding and decoding modules. Specifically, the encoder of the MDFormer comprises an embedding layer, two downsampling layers, and three MDDSA Blocks. It consists of three stages operating at different scales, with the feature resolution being halved at each stage. Correspondingly, the decoder includes three upsampling layers and three MDDSA Blocks. Skip connections are employed at various stages of the decoder to recover fine-grained and rich semantic features of the image during the decoding process. Additionally, residual blocks are utilized at the top layer to further enhance the high-frequency information of the output features.

In this subsection, we provide a detailed description of the key components of the MDFormer, specifically the encoder, MDDSA Block, and decoder.

Multi-scale dense dilated self-attention mechanism

Compared with traditional 2D images, 3D images are characterized by significantly larger data scales and higher computational costs, thereby posing new challenges to a series of Transformer-based networks. Most existing methods for reducing computation either segment the input into non-overlapping windows22,24,28, thereby restricting attention to fixed-size windows, or project the spatial matrices of keys and values into lower-dimensional spaces at different resolutions29. The former approach has achieved remarkable success in 2D networks but remains computationally expensive when applied to 3D networks. The latter approach leverages the characteristics of sparse matrices, which, while reducing computational costs to some extent, may still result in the loss of certain features. This could potentially affect the detection of smaller organs and critical edge details in medical imaging.

Based on the aforementioned research, we innovatively propose the Multi-scale Dense Dilated Self-Attention (MDDSA) Mechanism, which enhances the network’s segmentation capabilities in three key aspects. (1) The proposed MDDSA dynamically adjusts the size of the dense dilation matrix according to the resolution features at each stage, enabling efficient multi-scale global self-attention and generating diverse feature representations for multi-scale tensors. (2) By leveraging the sparse nature of the attention matrix, MDDSA performs spatial downscaling and aggregated information processing at multiple scales for fixed resolutions. This approach not only reduces computational costs but also enables the network to learn rich spatial and edge features effectively. (3) Unlike CPVT30, we introduce an additional 3D convolutional branch outside the self-attention mechanism. By utilizing the translation invariance property of convolutions, this design helps recover and guide the spatial information disorder caused by the self-attention mechanism31. Moreover, it captures high-frequency local features from a different weight perspective compared to the self-attention mechanism, allowing the network to capture both the global context of small lesions and the fine details of local neighborhoods simultaneously.

Dense expansion matrix principle

Most existing Transformer networks employ a U-shaped multi-scale architecture, which, despite its success, tends to neglect multi-scale features at fixed resolutions. Notably, the original self-attention mechanism was not designed to extract multi-scale information and thus struggles to fully integrate features with varying characteristics. Furthermore, it is worth emphasizing that attention weights typically become fixed post-calculation and are therefore unable to adapt dynamically to different contexts32.

Moreover, the computational complexity of the standard Transformer architecture increases quadratically with respect to the sequence length, rendering it inefficient for many medical image vision tasks that demand dense learning or pixel-level segmentation. Existing methods typically mitigate this issue by trading off some performance to achieve reduced complexity.

To address these two challenges, we have developed the Dense Expansion Matrix, represented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\wedge$$\end{document} in Fig. 1(b). Its conceptual framework is illustrated in Fig. 1(c), where a given input sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X \in {R^{N \times C}}$$\end{document} is reshaped into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat {X} \in {R^{H \times W \times D \times C}}$$\end{document} for the subsequent processing. To extract rich multi-scale and multifaceted feature information, we designed parallel dilated convolutions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({K_i}, {S_i}, {R_i}, i=1, 2, 3)$$\end{document} with three different receptive field sizes at each stage. Here, K represents the size of the convolution kernel, S denotes the stride, and R indicates the dilation rate. First, the sizes of convolutional kernels and strides in different stages are dynamically adjusted based on the resolution of current-stage features. Empirically, the kernel sizes and strides of three parallel dilated convolutions adapt dynamically to the feature map resolution as follows: when the feature map resolution decreases from large to small, the corresponding kernel sizes and strides are set sequentially to 7, 5, and 3. Larger kernels and strides are applied to large-scale feature maps to cover broader spatial regions, capture global context, and reduce redundant computations. Conversely, smaller kernels and strides are used for small-scale feature maps to focus on local fine-grained details, avoid confusion, and preserve structural nuances. This adaptive mechanism ensures that convolution operations align with the characteristics of features at varying resolutions, balancing effective feature extraction with computational efficiency. Second, dilation rates are set to 1, 2, and 3, respectively, generating three distinct receptive fields that progressively capture multi-scale features—from local details to global structures. By combining these configurations, the model achieves comprehensive extraction of multi-scale information across different image scales. Leveraging both dynamic kernel-stride adaptation and multi-rate dilations, the proposed approach effectively extracts multi-scale features from a single layer with fixed resolution, addressing the challenge of scale variance in visual tasks. Finally, this matrix undergoes dot-product self-attention. This constitutes the entire operation of the Dense Expansion Matrix. Such a design effectively enhances the network’s ability to model multi-scale and multi-feature information, strengthening its capacity to interact with spatial information, low-level features, and edge details.

More ingeniously, we leverage the feature aggregation capability of convolutions. Decoder

The decoder employs transposed convolution to upsample low-resolution feature maps into high-resolution ones. It subsequently integrates skip connections from the encoder to capture both semantic and fine-grained information. Analogous to the upsampling blocks, the final patch expansion block conducts a deconvolution operation, thereby generating the ultimate mask predictions24.

Datasets and evaluation metrics

The ACDC dataset is sourced from the official website of the ACDC Challenge (https://www.creatis.insa-lyon.fr/Challenge/acdc/). It comprises 150 cases categorized into five subclasses: normal cases, heart failure with infarction, dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and right ventricular abnormality. Each subclass contains 30 cases. Each case comprises a 4D NIfTI format image of one cardiac cycle, with diastole and systole clearly annotated. Within a single cardiac cycle, 28 to 40 time-point images are acquired, including short-axis images of the right ventricle (RV), myocardium (Myo), and left ventricle (LV) sub-organ structures, which are utilized for segmentation. Due to the limited number of labeled cases, only 100 cases were utilized in this experiment. By employing data augmentation techniques, such as random rotation and flipping applied consistently to both images and their corresponding labels, the preprocessing steps enhance the model’s ability to generalize across various perspectives and effectively handle diverse inputs.

The Synapse Abdominal Multi-Organ Segmentation dataset provides a comprehensive collection of annotated medical images for multi-organ segmentation tasks. This dataset comprises 30 abdominal CT scans, including a total of 3779 axial contrast-enhanced clinical abdominal CT images. Each abdominal CT scan consists of 85 to 198 slices with a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels and is designed for the segmentation of eight abdominal organs (aorta, gallbladder, spleen, left kidney, right kidney, liver, pancreas, and stomach).

We convert these datasets into NumPy format to establish a standardized input for deep learning models. By employing random data augmentation techniques such as rotation, flipping, contrast adjustment, and affine transformations, we effectively increase the scale of the training data and improve the model’s capacity to generalize various image variations.

The dice similarity coefficient (DSC) and the 95% Hausdorff distance (HD95) were employed to assess the segmentation performance of MDFormer and other methods. The DSC quantifies the degree of overlap between the segmentation prediction X and the ground truth Y. A higher DSC value signifies greater prediction accuracy. The DSC is mathematically expressed as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$DSC=\frac{{2\left| {X \cap Y} \right|}}{{\left| X \right|+\left| Y \right|}}$$\end{document}

The Hausdorff distance (HD) serves as a metric to quantify the maximum distance between the predicted segmentation boundary and the true segmentation boundary. A smaller HD signifies a more accurate prediction. The HD is mathematically expressed as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$HD(\widetilde {X}, \widetilde {Y})=max\left\{ {\mathop {max}\limits_{{\widetilde {x} \in \widetilde {X}}} \mathop {min}\limits_{{\widetilde {y} \in \widetilde {Y}}} d(x, y), \mathop {max}\limits_{{\widetilde {y} \in \widetilde {Y}}} \mathop {min}\limits_{{\widetilde {x} \in \widetilde {X}}} d(x, y)} \right\}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde {X}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde {Y}$$\end{document} denote the boundary point sets of the segmentation prediction and ground truth, respectively. HD95 represents the 95th percentile of the distances between the boundary points in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde {X}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde {Y}$$\end{document}, which is calculated to mitigate the influence of a small subset of outliers.

Results

The proposed MDFormer was evaluated on two widely used 3D public medical image segmentation datasets, namely ACDC and Synapse. Comprehensive quantitative and qualitative comparisons were conducted with state-of-the-art models reported in the literature to rigorously assess its performance. Furthermore, ablation studies were carried out to systematically demonstrate the effectiveness of each module within MDFormer.

Experimental results on the ACDC dataset

The quantitative results on the ACDC dataset are presented in Table 2. For all methods, we adopt the DSC as the primary evaluation metric. Our MDFormer achieves an average DSC of 92.7% without pre-training, which is the highest among all compared methods. Specifically, compared to CNN-based approaches such as R50 U-Net4 and R50 Att-UNet34, our MDFormer demonstrates an average DSC improvement of 5.15% and 5.95%, respectively. Moreover, when benchmarked against Transformer-based methods like nnFormer and MISSFormer, our MDFormer still outperforms them by 0.64% and 4.8% in terms of average DSC, respectively. The segmentation accuracy of our MDFormer outperformed other methods, showcasing superior performance in the RV, Myo, and LV regions. The quantitative results further indicate that the MDFormer architecture not only effectively captures long-range dependency relationships but also integrates local contextual information, enabling the formation of robust and discriminative feature representations.

For the ACDC dataset, paired T-tests were conducted. Since the correlation between R50 U-Net and R50 Att-UNet with our model was greater than 0.05 when grouped respectively, this indicates that the two samples are not strongly correlated. Therefore, a non-parametric test was employed. The inputs for the tests were the DSC values of organ segmentation results from two paired groups: one group using our method and the other using a comparative method. Detailed p-values are presented in Table 2, where the majority of p-values are below 0.05, indicating statistically significant differences between the segmentation results of our model and those of other models. For models with slightly higher p-values, we conducted a descriptive analysis, which reveals that our model exhibits a higher mean and a smaller standard deviation compared to other models. The combination of a high mean and low standard deviation suggests that our model is not only stable but also produces more consistent and superior results, thereby demonstrating better segmentation performance.

Comparison of performance and model complexity between 3D models

We systematically compared the proposed MDFormer against various state-of-the-art 3D medical image segmentation models in terms of performance, parameter efficiency, and floating-point operations (FLOPs), including UNETR, CoTr, TransBTS, nnFormer, and Swin-UNETR. The number of FLOPs is calculated using the sliding window method with an input image size of 64 × 128 × 128 on the Synapse dataset for fair comparison. It is worth noting that we have excluded the computational complexity introduced by activation functions and normalization layers. As shown in Table 5, our MDFormer has 37.7 M parameters and 47.39 G FLOPs, which represents a significantly lower computational cost compared to UNETR (92.25 M #Param, 86.02 G FLOPs), CoTr (41.86 M #Param, 377.48 G FLOPs), TransBTS (32.19 M #Param, 171.3 G FLOPs), nnFormer (150.5 M #Param, 213.4 G FLOPs), and Swin-UNETR (62.83 M #Param, 384.2 G FLOPs). In addition, compared with other Transformer-based models, MDFormer not only exhibits significant superiority in performance but also maintains comparable model complexity, thereby offering a more cost-effective solution for segmentation tasks.

We conducted a paired t-test on the Synapse dataset, using the DSC of organ segmentation as the input. The two paired groups compared were our proposed method and the comparison method. Table 5 presents the detailed p-values, the majority of which are below 0.05, indicating that the segmentation results of our model are statistically significantly different from those of other models. The t-tests conducted on the segmentation results of MDFormer and nnFormer yielded slightly higher p-values; therefore, we employed descriptive analysis for comparison. This revealed that the results of our model were more centrally distributed and exhibited greater stability. Furthermore, direct comparisons of mean values demonstrate that our proposed method outperforms other 3D models overall while also featuring fewer model parameters and computational flops compared to several alternative models.

To validate the effectiveness of MDDSA, a comparison between the proposed MDFormer and the baseline model reveals particularly significant performance improvements: in terms of accuracy, the average DSC increased by 7.32%, HD95 decreased by 16.23 mm, and the segmentation performance of all 8 abdominal organs was significantly improved. In terms of computational efficiency, the model parameters reduced by 54.55 M and FLOPs decreased by 38.63G, demonstrating that while maintaining high performance, it effectively reduces computational resource consumption, making it more suitable for the requirements of real-time performance and hardware deployment in clinical scenarios. The above results further confirm the value of the structural innovation of MDDSA - through the multi-scale feature capture of dense dilated matrices and the mechanism-complementary design of 3D convolution branches, it not only achieves a breakthrough in segmentation accuracy but also balances the optimization of computational efficiency, fully proving that its overall design has significant practical value in medical image segmentation tasks.

To further validate the effectiveness of the proposed model design, a comparison between MDFormer and the baseline model with DCA shows that the model integrated with MDDSA achieved an average DSC improvement of 0.41% and a HD95 reduction of 3.15 mm, with the spleen showing the best improvement (a 5.27% increase in DSC). Meanwhile, the model parameters reduced by 52.29 M and FLOPs decreased by 0.68G. This performance advantage directly confirms the effectiveness of the dynamically adjusted dense dilated matrix constructed through three parallel dilated convolutions in multi-scale feature modeling, clearly indicating that the MDDSA paradigm significantly enhances the algorithm’s performance and computational efficiency.

Discussion

The segmentation of medical images continues to be one of the most challenging tasks in medical image analysis. Accurately delineating structures and tissues within medical images not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also supports more informed clinical decision-making. The ongoing advancement of DL techniques has substantially improved the precision of medical image segmentation. Among these methods, CNNs have served as a foundational tool due to their capacity to capture local spatial dependencies effectively. Nevertheless, the necessity to model long-range dependencies in medical images, such as relationships between distant anatomical structures, has driven the increasing adoption of Transformer architectures, which are renowned for their powerful self-attention mechanisms.

Despite their advantages, many current approaches that integrate CNNs and Transformers still encounter substantial challenges in effectively managing multi-scale features within medical images. While traditional Transformer models excel at capturing global dependencies, they often struggle to preserve resolution when extracting multi-scale information, a capability that is crucial for precise segmentation, especially of smaller anatomical structures such as the pancreas or gallbladder. Moreover, the computational complexity of Transformer-based models in 3D segmentation remains a significant hurdle. The self-attention mechanism, which computes pairwise relationships between all tokens, leads to a substantial increase in computational cost when applied to large 3D medical images. As a result, efficiently processing high-resolution inputs becomes increasingly difficult.

To address these challenges, we propose the MDFormer, an architecture that incorporates a MDDSA module. This innovative approach not only retains the original input resolution but also empowers the model to capture contextual information across multiple scales while minimizing information loss. The MDFormer outperformed existing models on both the ACDC and Synapse datasets, achieving DSC of 92.7% and 86.88%, respectively. Notably, the model demonstrated its potential to accurately segment low-contrast structures, such as myocardial tissue, which has traditionally been a significant challenge in medical image segmentation. Beyond these dataset-specific achievements, the core design of MDFormer further underscores its strong generalization potential across diverse anatomical regions, imaging modalities, and disease contexts. Its dynamically adjusted dense dilation matrices and multi-scale attention mechanisms, unconstrained by specific organ structures, can capture fine-grained details in regions like the brain (e.g., complex subcortical structures) through balanced multi-scale feature learning; for modalities such as PET (functional activity imaging), its sparse attention-based spatial dimensionality reduction and dilated convolution feature aggregation capabilities adapt to varying contrast and noise characteristics of different modalities; in disease scenarios like tumors, its multi-scale modeling can capture both large tumor masses and tiny metastatic lesions, with the balance between precision and efficiency making it suitable for rapid processing needs such as intraoperative imaging. In the future, we will further validate its cross-scenario adaptability through diverse datasets such as BraTS and LiTS.

However, despite these advancements, there remain areas for improvement. Specifically, the MDFormer exhibited slightly reduced performance when segmenting smaller organs, potentially due to its tendency to prioritize larger structures within multi-scale feature representations-a challenge commonly encountered in current multi-organ segmentation tasks, namely class imbalance. This limitation highlights the necessity for further refinement, particularly in optimizing the model’s attention mechanism to ensure equitable representation of structures across all scales. This could include exploring more complex multi-scale attention mechanisms to balance attention allocation across organs of different scales39,40, designing jointly weighted loss functions to assign higher weights to small organs41, and considering the integration of auxiliary tasks such as small organ keypoint detection42. Concretely, it can adopt the closed-loop predict-and-optimize framework43,44. On one hand, the training objective should be shifted from “pixel-level proxy losses (e.g., cross-entropy)” to the downstream primary metric-the DSC for small organs. On the other hand, a lightweight small-organ feature adjuster can be integrated into the existing workflow: using the small-organ DSC as feedback, this module iteratively optimizes the weighting of high-resolution feature channels to enhance fine-grained details of small organs. Without modifying the main model architecture, this approach steers multi-scale feature representation toward improved sensitivity to small organs. Importantly, the optimization process must maintain a balance in overall segmentation performance, preventing degradation in the accuracy of larger or critical organs while enhancing small-organ segmentation. In the field of medical image segmentation, fully supervised segmentation remains predominant owing to its superior accuracy. However, this approach necessitates a substantial amount of high-quality annotated data. To mitigate this limitation, we intend to investigate extending MDFormer into a semi-supervised learning framework in future work, along with introducing federated learning and knowledge distillation strategies45. These extensions are expected to reduce reliance on large annotated datasets, ensure computational efficiency, and significantly enhance the model’s generalization capability.

Conclusion

We propose the MDFormer model, which integrates the MDDSA module. This module dynamically adjusts the dense dilation matrix size according to resolution-specific features, enabling spatial downscaling and multi-scale information aggregation at a fixed resolution. Such a design facilitates the efficient fusion of multi-scale contextual information in medical image segmentation while minimizing computational costs. Experimental results show that MDFormer substantially improves segmentation accuracy and robustness across various medical imaging tasks while preserving lightweight computational efficiency. The proposed framework offers a novel approach for lightweight design and cross-scale feature learning in Transformer-based models for medical image analysis, thereby effectively enhancing segmentation performance in clinical applications.
