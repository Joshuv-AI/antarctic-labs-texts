The influence of retaining osteophyte on the coronal alignment and joint line height following medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty
Authors: Guanghui Zhao, Chengyuan Ma, Jianbing Ma, Jianpeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe retention of osteophytes may influence the postoperative hip–knee–ankle (HKA) angle and joint line height (JLH) following unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA); however, the specific impact remains poorly characterized. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of retaining the medial femoral condylar osteophyte on these parameters after medial UKA.MethodsThis sequential historical cohort study included patients who underwent medial UKA between January 1, 2025, and December 31, 2025. The patient cohort comprised an osteophyte removal group and an osteophyte retention group. Preoperative HKA, medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA), osteophyte width, postoperative HKA, and the change in JLH were measured. The arithmetic HKA (aHKA) and the change in coronal alignment (ΔHKA) were calculated as follows: aHKA = MPTA − LDFA + 180°, and ΔHKA = aHKA − postoperative HKA. The postoperative outcomes of interest were the change in HKA and the change in JLH. The osteophyte removal group comprised 80 patients (mean age 65.5 ± 6.9 years), and the osteophyte retention group comprised 61 patients (mean age 65.9 ± 6.5 years). The postoperative HKA was significantly greater (176.11° ± 3.29° vs. 174.97° ± 2.73°, p = 0.032) and the ΔHKA was significantly smaller (0.57° ± 2.52° vs. 1.50° ± 1.99°, p = 0.041) in the removal group. Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between ΔHKA and osteophyte width (r = 0.527, p < 0.001). Radiographic assessment of JLH showed a distal migration of 2.67 ± 1.37 mm in the osteophyte removal group, compared with 1.79 ± 1.21 mm in the retention group (p = 0.001). Background

Medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) is an effective treatment for patients with symptomatic end-stage knee osteoarthritis (OA) confined to the medial compartment [1, 2]. However, its widespread use is limited by lower reported implant survivorship compared with total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [3]. Implant longevity depends on accurate component positioning and restoration of proper limb alignment [4]. Current consensus advocates for neutral or slightly undercorrected coronal alignment [5], as postoperative hip–knee–ankle (HKA) angle outliers are associated with a fivefold increased risk of tibial implant failure [6]. Furthermore, severe undercorrection has been identified as a cause of accelerated polyethylene wear [7]. Conversely, overcorrection of the preoperative varus deformity is a recognized risk factor for the subsequent development of osteoarthritis in the contralateral compartment [8].

While limb alignment in TKA is directly determined by implant positioning, in UKA it is corrected through restoration of the joint line within the affected compartment [9, 10]. In conventional UKA, distalization of the joint line is inevitable, as the joint line is lowered by the implant thickness minus the native cartilage thickness. This phenomenon has been associated with early implant failure, with some studies reporting distalization of 4.3 mm [11]. Finite element analyses have further indicated that prosthetic joint line installation errors exceeding ± 2 mm could significantly compromise polyethylene insert wear life [12].

Beyond surgical technique, residual osteophytes may also influence coronal alignment and joint line height (JLH). Theoretically, osteophytes located on the femoral trochlea, and the intercondylar fossa can affect the patellofemoral joint and knee flexion-extension movement, but have little impact on coronal alignment. Osteophytes on the posterior and the tibial side can simultaneously affect the flexion and extension gaps and may lead to an increase in the thickness of the tibial osteotomy [13]. The osteophyte at the distal end of the medial femoral condyle may not affect the flexion gap but can influence coronal alignment. In TKA, studies have demonstrated that osteophyte removal can result in coronal angle corrections of up to 3.4 degrees in extension, suggesting that retained osteophytes can contribute to residual varus deformity [14, 15]. However, the specific impact of a retained medial femoral condylar osteophyte on the postoperative HKA angle and JLH in UKA remains unknown.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of retaining medial femoral condylar osteophytes on postoperative limb alignment and the change in JLH after medial UKA.

Methods

This sequential historical cohort study was conducted at a specialized orthopedic center and included patients who underwent medial UKA between January 1, 2025, and December 31, 2025. Prior to June 30, 2025, our standard surgical protocol for UKA involved the routine resection of all macroscopically visible osteophytes—including those located on the medial femoral condyle, the femoral trochlea, the medial tibial plateau, and the posterior and intercondylar fossa of the femur. Subsequently, to align with the principles of minimally invasive surgery and reduce intraoperative bone and soft-tissue trauma, we revised this protocol such that osteophytes at the distal end of the medial femoral condyle were preserved, whereas those on the medial tibial plateau, the posterior aspect of the femur, the femoral trochlea and the intercondylar fossa remain subject to resection. The procedure was indicated for anteromedial osteoarthritis with intact ligaments, flexion contracture ≤ 15°, correctable varus deformity < 15°, and maintained range of motion [16, 17]. Inclusion criteria were: (1) UKA performed for varus OA, and (2) availability of standardized preoperative and postoperative weight-bearing full-leg radiographs. Patients with previous knee surgery, inflammatory arthritis, severe osteoporosis, extra-articular deformity, or radiographs of inadequate quality for reliable measurement were excluded. All procedures utilized an AiKang fixed-bearing prosthesis (AiKang Medical Instruments Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and were performed by a surgical team using conventional instrumentation. The study was approved by the institutional review board (reference number: 202409003). Patient consent for the use of radiographic data was obtained verbally via telephone, and images were retrieved from the hospital’s picture archiving and communication system (PACS).

Surgical technique

A medial parapatellar approach was utilized, with an 8–10 cm incision extending from the superomedial border of the patella to the medial aspect of the tibial tubercle. The osteophyte management protocol defined two groups: an osteophyte removal group, in which all intra-articular osteophytes were excised, and an osteophyte preservation group, in which osteophytes at the distal end of the medial femoral condyle were preserved. This preservation has no significant effect on the flexion gap; however, in extension, due to a “tent effect,” it exerts an influence on the medial collateral ligament (Fig. 1). Tibial osteotomy was performed using extramedullary instrumentation to achieve a cut perpendicular to the tibial mechanical axis. Based on the characteristics of the prosthesis (3 mm metal baseplate + 6 mm polyethylene insert), the distance between the tibial osteotomy line and the posterior femoral condyle was 9 mm. Femoral preparation was performed with intramedullary instrumentation. Component alignment was established such that the long axis of the femoral component was parallel to that of the tibial component at 90° of knee flexion. Polyethylene insert thickness was selected to achieve balanced flexion and extension gaps, prioritizing ease of insertion through a full range of motion. If the extension gap was smaller than the flexion gap, a second osteotomy was performed on the distal end of the femur, with the thickness of the osteotomy equal to the flexion gap minus the extension gap. In cases of preoperative varus deformity of varying degrees, a final alignment allowing for approximately 1 mm of medial opening under valgus stress or a slightly tighter extension gap was deemed acceptable.

Radiographic assessments

All patients received standardized preoperative weight-bearing long-leg radiographs, which were repeated on the first postoperative day once they could fully extend the knee and stand unassisted. Radiographic acquisition followed the protocol established by Paley et al. [18]. Osteophyte width was measured from the native medial margin of the femoral condyle to the medial margin of the osteophyte [19] (Fig. 2A). The change in JLH was assessed according to the method described by Weber et al. [20]. First, a line was drawn connecting the highest point of the intercondylar notch and the center of the femoral shaft 10 cm proximal to this point (Fig. 2A). This reference line was identical on the postoperative radiograph. By projecting the preoperatively measured angle between the reference line and the preoperative joint line onto the postoperative radiograph, the preoperative joint line could be drawn on the postoperative image. The difference between the preoperative joint line and the most distal point of the femoral component was measured, representing the change in joint line from preoperation to postoperation (Fig. 2B). Distal joint line migration was recorded as a positive value, and proximal migration as a negative value.

The measurement of osteophyte width (A) and the change in joint line height (B)

Preoperative HKA angle, medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA), osteophyte width, postoperative HKA angle, and change in JLH were measured using the institutional PACS. The arithmetic HKA (aHKA) was calculated as described by MacDessi et al. [21]. For the purposes of this study, aHKA was derived using the formula: aHKA = MPTA – LDFA + 180°, to enable direct comparison with the postoperative HKA angle. The change in coronal alignment (ΔHKA) was defined as aHKA minus the postoperative HKA angle. Imaging data were measured by two independent assessors. Because the presence or absence of osteophytes resulted in distinctly different radiographic appearances, blinding (randomization) could not be performed. Therefore, all images were distributed uniformly to the assessors, and they were informed of the research purpose and hypotheses only after the measurements were completed. General data and preoperative and postoperative imaging parameters were compared between the two groups.

Measurement reliability and intraclass correlation coefficient

Sample size was determined using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). A priori power analysis revealed that a minimum of 102 subjects would be required to achieve 80% statistical power with an α of 0.05 and a moderate effect size (r = 0.5) of the differences between the two groups in postoperative HKA. Intraobserver and interobserver reliability for angular measurements were assessed by having two investigators independently evaluate the first 10 consecutive knees on two occasions, separated by a one-week interval. Reliability was quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) (Table 1).

Results

This study included 141 patients (141 knees). The osteophyte removal group comprised 80 patients (80 knees) with a mean age of 65.54 ± 6.94 years, while the osteophyte retention group consisted of 61 patients (61 knees) with a mean age of 65.89 ± 6.48 years. Preoperative medial femoral condylar osteophyte width measured 4.18 ± 1.42 mm and 4.42 ± 0.98 mm in the removal and retention groups, respectively. Preoperative radiographic measurements of MPTA and LDFA yielded a calculated aHKA of 176.67° ± 3.34° in the removal group and 176.47° ± 2.87° in the retention group, with no statistically significant intergroup difference (p = 0.45; Table 3). The Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) of HKA is 0.30. The postoperative HKA angle was significantly greater in the removal group than in the retention group (176.11° ± 3.29° vs. 174.97° ± 2.73°, p = 0.032). Consequently, the change in coronal alignment (ΔHKA) was significantly smaller in the removal group (0.57° ± 2.52° vs. 1.50° ± 1.99°, p = 0.041). Using ANCOVA with preoperative HKA, MPTA and LDFA as covariates, we also found that ΔHKA remained significantly smaller in the removal group (p = 0.016, 0.010 and 0.016). A multivariable linear regression analysis was performed and showed that postoperative HKA was related to both preoperative HKA (p = 0.009) and the osteophyte retention (p = 0.02), but not related to the insert thickness (p = 0.069). Then, the ANCOVA was performed, adjusting for preoperative HKA as covariates, and showed that the osteophyte retention was also an independent predictor of postoperative HKA (p = 0.004). Linear analysis demonstrated a significant positive correlation between ΔHKA and osteophyte width (r = 0.527, p < 0.001; Fig. 3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the effect of preserving the osteophyte at the distal end of medial femoral condyle on postoperative limb alignment and joint line position following UKA. The principal findings indicate that osteophyte retention is associated with a significantly lower postoperative HKA angle, resulting in greater deviation from the preoperatively calculated aHKA. Furthermore, osteophyte retention correlates with significantly less distal joint line migration than does osteophyte removal.

Medial UKA is theoretically a surface replacement procedure that preserves the cruciate ligaments and the lateral meniscus, with the potential to restore both the HKA angle and JLH to a natural state [22]. Achieving these objectives, however, requires precise osteotomy, strict adherence to the principle of equal bone resection and prosthesis implantation, and thorough removal of periarticular osteophytes to restore native ligament length and tension [23]. In clinical practice, complete osteophyte excision during UKA is often challenging due to the limited exposure afforded by the minimally invasive approach and concerns regarding iatrogenic injury to the medial collateral ligament (MCL). Furthermore, the raw bone surface following osteophyte removal may contribute to postoperative intra-articular bleeding. Consequently, we attempted to preserve the medial femoral condylar osteophyte in selected cases to protect the MCL and potentially reduce postoperative hemarthrosis. The impact of this deliberate osteophyte retention on postoperative limb alignment and joint line position has remained unknown. This study was designed to address that specific knowledge gap, and its findings provide clinically relevant insights into the influence of retained osteophytes on UKA outcomes.

Recent reviews examining the HKA angle and outcomes have supported residual varus of 3°–4° but remained equivocal regarding a hard varus alignment limit [24, 25]. Some studies have also suggested that when postoperative kinematic alignment is defined as a postoperative HKA within ± 3° of the aHKA, kinematically aligned limbs demonstrate better survivorship and superior functional outcomes [26]. The present study found that the postoperative HKA in the osteophyte removal group aligned more closely with the aHKA. Notably, in both cohorts, a subset of patients exhibited a postoperative HKA exceeding their aHKA (12 in the retention group and 29 in the removal group). We attributed this finding primarily to the intraoperative use of a thicker polyethylene insert to address varus deformity, often in scenarios where the extension gap was smaller than the flexion gap. This technique may lead to over-tensioning of the medial collateral ligament and consequent overcorrection of alignment, a phenomenon previously documented in the literature [23].

Previous literature has indicated that joint line lowering of ≥ 2 mm correlates with inferior clinical scores and reduced survival rates at a mean follow-up of 10.8 years [27]. Foissey et al. [6] demonstrated that the combination of high residual varus and joint line lowering significantly elevates the risk of early failure in medial UKA. Finite element studies by Kwon et al. [28] and Kang et al. [29] reported a progressive increase in liner contact stress with joint line elevation from + 2 mm to + 6 mm, while a decrease was observed with joint line depression (− 2 mm to − 6 mm). Similarly, Xiong et al. [12] found that elevating the joint line by + 2 mm and + 6 mm increased the maximum linear wear depth by 10.7% and 24.1%, and volumetric wear by 5.9% and 35.7%, respectively, compared with a neutral joint line. Such increases substantially compromise the wear longevity of the polyethylene liner. Consequently, precise restoration of JLH is paramount for the long-term survivorship of UKA prostheses. In the present study, the mean joint line shift was 2.67 ± 1.37 mm in the osteophyte removal group, compared with 1.79 ± 1.21 mm in the osteophyte retention group. This finding suggests that osteophyte retention may contribute to a lesser degree of JLH lowering.

Postoperative HKA and JLH are strongly influenced by surgical technique. A principle of the classical Oxford UKA protocol, often described as “flexion determines extension,” is applied: if the extension gap is narrower than the flexion gap, additional distal femoral resection is performed to balance the gaps. This approach risks excessive distal femoral resection when a substantial disparity exists [30, 31]. In contrast, Malavolta et al. [23] described a kinematic alignment technique for medial UKA that targets approximately 1 mm of valgus opening with adequate soft-tissue tension in flexion, while accepting a slightly tighter extension gap with similar valgus alignment and modest spacer traction resistance. Crucially, this technique does not resort to additional distal femoral resection to equalize the gaps. This method better preserves the joint line but may lead to a postoperative HKA that exceeds the aHKA. Beyond surgical technique, retaining osteophytes may also influence postoperative HKA and JLH [14, 15]. The main mechanism of action is as follows: the osteophyte on the medial femoral condyle shortens the relative excursion of the medial collateral ligament due to a “tent-like effect.” In other words, when optimal tension is achieved for the medial ligaments at 0° of knee extension (mean distraction force, 149.9 N), retention of the osteophyte reduces the width of the joint space, thereby affecting the intraoperative procedure and subsequent coronal alignment [32].

The impact of osteophyte retention appears to vary with the preoperative aHKA. In limbs with an aHKA ≤ 180°, osteophyte preservation may perpetuate or exacerbate varus alignment. In this study, two patients in the osteophyte retention cohort exhibited a postoperative HKA angle lower than their preoperative value. Conversely, for limbs with an aHKA > 180°, osteophyte retention may be advantageous. Among the eight patients in the retention group with aHKA > 180°, most achieved a postoperative HKA < 180°, with only one case remaining > 180°. Furthermore, compared with the osteophyte removal group, approximately two-thirds of the retention group demonstrated a lesser degree of distal JLH shift (i.e., more favorable joint line preservation), and nearly one-fifth exhibited deviations beyond the ± 2 mm target range. These findings suggest that osteophyte management should be individualized and based on a comprehensive preoperative assessment of both aHKA and JLH.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cohort of patients with retained osteophytes included only 61 individuals, representing a relatively small sample size. Consequently, the analysis remained qualitative; future studies should enroll larger cohorts to enable quantitative assessment of how varying osteophyte dimensions affect postoperative outcomes. Second, the primary measurement method relied on preoperative and postoperative radiographs, which are inherently susceptible to measurement error. Incorporating computed tomography (CT) in future studies would allow for more precise three-dimensional evaluation. Third, while the calculation of ICCs typically involves 10 to 15 cases according to the published literature, it should be noted that larger sample sizes enhance the credibility of the results. In this study, only 10 cases were used to calculate the ICCs, which may have influenced the findings. Fourth, as a sequential historical cohort study, this investigation is subject to inherent limitations, including selection bias, lack of randomization, absence of follow-up data, and no assessment of clinical functional scores. Besides, subtle changes in the surgical team’s technique, post-operative mobilization speed, or variations in default component sizing across different parts of the year could affect outcomes. The MCID of HKA is 0.30 and the MCID of JLH is 0.32, the differences found in this study exceed them. However, without correlating these findings with Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs), the clinical utility of removing or retaining osteophytes remains speculative and we should not to over-interpret radiographic variances as definitive functional failure. Besides, while these differences are robust to measurement error, they remain sub-clinical on a short-term scale, highlighting the critical need for long-term survival data. Finally, the potential for technical variability inherent in conventional surgery may have introduced confounding effects. The use of robotic-assisted surgical techniques in future research could enhance procedural standardization and improve the accuracy of outcome assessment.

Conclusions

Based on the data presented, retention of the medial femoral condylar osteophyte appears to be associated with a reduction in the postoperative HKA angle and elevation of the joint line. Therefore, the decision to retain or excise the osteophyte requires further research using larger sample sizes and incorporating clinical outcomes.
