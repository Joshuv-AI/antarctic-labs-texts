Editorial: Research methods in biochemistry and biophysics
Authors: Daniel E Otzen
Published: 2024

As Executive Editor of BBA-Advances, I am delighted to introduce our special issue on different approaches being developed and implemented in biophysics and quantitative biochemistry. New techniques often lead to new research areas; conversely, reevaluation and refinement of existing techniques can cast new light on established topics. Under all circumstances, scientists are powerless without robust and validated methods to investigate and understand the topics they are engaged in. As practicing researchers, we are also obliged to keep abreast of the latest developments in our tool-boxes. Accordingly, I hope that readers will find inspiration in the current collection of 11 different articles that comprise this Special Issue. They cover a wide range of techniques, ranging from fluorescence to cell quantification, electrochemical techniques, molecular dynamics, cryo-electron microscopy, crystallization, small-angle scattering and NMR. Here follows a brief summary of their contents:

In “Applications of fluorescence spectroscopy in protein conformational changes and intermolecular contacts”, Tasic and coworkers describe a sturdy workhorse of protein biophysical structural analysis. The technique is not limited to proteins’ intrinsic Trp fluorescence emission, but also exploits quenching, time-resolved fluorescence and anisotropy to monitor conformational changes and ligand binding. Biological fluorescence is not limited to intrinsic Trp fluorescence but also benefits from GFPs for cellular tracking etc. but also (spectroscopically even more remarkable) fluorescent proteins with large Stokes shifts between excitation and emission, as well as photoswitchable and photoconvertible fluorescent proteins, which can be turned on and off with light of specific wavelengths. Old or new, fluorescence has countless applications in biology.

The Special Issue contains three papers on different aspects of electrochemistry and redox-active proteins. Silveira and coworkers in “Spectroelectrochemistry for determination of the redox potential in heme enzymes: dye-decolorizing peroxidases” compare UV–vis and surface-enhanced resonance Raman spectroelectrochemistry in which changes in UV–vis and Raman signatures are monitored as the potential is changed. This is useful for evaluating bioelectronic devices that use immobilized heme peroxidases. In “Cyclic voltammetry, square wave voltammetry or electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy? Interrogating electrochemical approaches for the determination of electron transfer rates of immobilized redox proteins”, Kelb and coworkers provide another focus on immobilized redox-active enzymes which are widely applied in biosensing. Heterogeneous electron transfer is an integrated part of the detection, and this is best measured by electrochemical approaches in which electro transfer is controlled via an external potential. However, several approaches are available and it is not always clear which is preferred. The authors compare three different approaches in a system employing immobilized cytochrome c and highlight the usefulness of combining this approach with simulations to validate and analyze the data. Finally, Fourmond and coworkers in “Transport limited adsorption experiments give a new lower estimate of the turnover frequency of Escherichia coli hydrogenase 1” treat protein film electrochemistry, where a redox-active protein is immobilized on an electrode which acts as a redox partner. While the flow of electrons is proportional to enzyme activity, the absolute activity is not known. The authors provide an ingenious approach to solve this: coat the electrode with enzyme under very dilute enzyme conditions (<1 nM) so formation is mass-transport limited. The results show that the immobilized enzyme has much higher turnover rates than enzymes in solution.

Computational science is rapidly becoming an integral part of all biochemical and biophysical investigations and can increasingly be relied on to provide new insights that are not available to experimentalists. This is nicely illustrated in “Recent Advances on Molecular Dynamics-Based Techniques to Address Drug Membrane Permeability with Atomistic Detail” where Machuqueiro and coworkers highlight the role of computational biology in providing both empirical estimates of drug permeability across membranes and understanding mechanisms which cannot be revealed experimentally anyway. As the simulations become increasingly realistic at the atomic level thanks to the advance in computing power, so the predictions become more and more robust, e.g. understanding where and when neutralization occurs during membrane translocation, effects of lipid components such as cholesterol etc.

Remaining within the lipid environment, Kalita and coworkers describe in “Automated image analysis method to detect and quantify fat cell infiltration in hematoxylin and eosin stained human pancreas histology images” a more macrostructural challenge. While fat cells are straightforward to detect in homogeneous white fat tissue, it is understandably more difficult to find and count them in heterogeneous tissue such as the pancreas. Nevertheless, it is very important to be able to do so, since the accumulation of fat cells (fat cell infiltration) has many adverse health consequences. The best approach still requires histological analysis; even so, it is difficult to quantify the stained tissue with automated programmes. This is addressed with elegant image processing approaches implemented in the programme Fatquant in the current paper.

As a segue to more structural analysis while retaining the lipid connection, we turn to “A solution NMR view of Lipidic Cubic Phases: Structure, dynamics, and beyond” by Yao and coworkers. NMR is useful not just to determine protein structures but also to probe biomolecular dynamics and diffusion. This is here applied to lipidic cubic phases (LCPs), made by small fatty molecules such as monoolein. The complex geometry of these phases makes them excellent hosts for membrane protein crystallization but also for drug/nutrient delivery. The authors show that solution NMR can inter alia be used to measure LCP hydration levels, effect of additives and release profiles of encapsulated proteins, providing a powerful tool both for basic and applied research.

Cryo-EM has taken the structural biology world by storm over the last decade. It is perhaps less appreciated that in addition to electron density, cryoEM can also provide information on the charge state of different atoms. This is discussed in depth by Wendler and colleagues in “Exploiting the full potential of cryo-EM maps”, which provides a new twist on cryoEM data by mapping out charge while taking account of perturbing factors such as radiation damage and sample drift.

Despite cryoEM's amazing successes, it is not a silver bullet, and crystallography remains a vital approach for atomic resolution. This is particularly the case for RNA molecules, whose structure can be difficult to determine due to their dynamic nature and highly charged surface. Nevertheless, structural information is still critically needed. In “Advances in chaperone-assisted RNA crystallography using synthetic antibodies”, Koirala and coworkers describe how to promote crystallization using custom-made antibody fragments (Fabs), which in effect act as crystallization chaperones. The Fabs can be made by phage display against the RNA molecules in question or by incorporating existing RNA motifs with known Fab binding partners into the RNA molecule. The article is a “nuts and bolts approach” which should provide a good starting point for those venturing into this exciting field.

Finally, we turn to two different takes on another widespread and versatile albeit low-resolution technique: Small Angle Scattering, in particular Small Angle Neutron Scattering (SANS). Although SANS is only available at large international facilities, sample preparation is relatively straightforward and the main bottleneck is to get the maximum information from analysis of the ensuing scattering curves. In “Ab initio reconstruction of small angle scattering data for membrane proteins in copolymer nanodiscs”, Edler et al. describe how to use SANS data to get structural information about membrane proteins embedded in styrene-maleimide nanoparticle discs. These nanodiscs or ``cookie cutters'' provide a good platform for determining membrane protein structures, since the discs can provide a native membrane environment to stabilize the native state. The great thing about SANS is that it allows removal of parts of the system through selective deuteration and an appropriate background which matches the contrast of the specified components. Edler and coworkers combine traditional analysis of SANS data, obtained under 3 different contrast conditions, with ab initio modelling to build up a low-resolution structure of the sample using two well-known proteins as test-cases, i.e. the outer membrane protein OmpF and the small channel-forming peptide gramicidin. This is promising because conventional SAS analysis relies on form factors with fixed shapes (e.g. elliptical cylinders) while the ab initio approach allows to incorporate irregular structures such as web-like polymer components. However, there are alternatives: In “DENSS-Multiple: A Structure Reconstruction Method using Contrast Variation of Small-Angle Neutron Scattering Based on the DENSS Algorithm”, Qian and coworkers avoid ab initio modelling. They base their approach on DENsity from Solution Scattering (DENSS), but extend it to DENSS-Multiple, which combines multiple contrast variation data sets from the same sample and does not require prior knowledge of volume fractions of different phases which can be difficult to determine in a novel system. More subtle features are represented by density variations from different phases of a structure. Their approach has the potential to be applied to a wide range of biological single-particle systems.

All in all, these different articles illustrate the breadth of techniques that can be used by biophysicists to their advantage and underline the vibrant and dynamic state of biophysical life sciences. There will always be a need for biophysical approaches to understand living systems!
