Functional roles and clinical potential of SOX1 in cancer as a biomarker and therapeutic target
Authors: Xianghua Zhou, Jiaqi Wang, Fanfan Yang, Yufan Gao, Chunxiao Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Malignant neoplastic diseases currently constitute a substantial global health burden. As a class of highly pathologically heterogeneous and complex disorders, addressing malignant tumors necessitates multifaceted efforts across various levels. SOX1, a member of the SOX transcription factor family B subgroup, plays a pivotal role in transcriptional activation during embryonic neurogenesis. Growing evidence indicates that SOX1 also contributes to tumor initiation and progression through multiple mechanisms. While SOX1 exhibits context-dependent functions, its tumor-suppressive action in epithelial cancers frequently involves direct antagonism of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and downregulation of targets like c-MYC and Cyclin D1. This review synthesizes current insights into the gene function of SOX1 and its roles within oncogenic signaling pathways, which are frequently subject to regulation by epigenetic modifications. The authors further evaluate the potential of SOX1 as a biomarker and therapeutic target in cancer diagnosis, treatment, prognosis, and associated complications. Although the functional heterogeneity of SOX1 presents challenges for clinical application, therapeutic modulation of its upstream and downstream pathways, as well as methylation-based assays for early detection, remain clinically promising.

Introduction

With the marked demographic shift toward an aging global population, malignant neoplasms have become the first or second leading cause of death in most countries [1]. This situation presents a formidable challenge to the vision of extending life expectancy and improving health outcomes across both urban and rural populations. The establishment of sustainable cancer-control platforms—particularly those that ensure adequate access to prevention and treatment services in transitioning countries—is essential for global cancer governance [2]. Tumors represent complex, heterogeneous diseases. Pathologically, tumors originate from normal cells that progressively acquire genetic alterations due to factors like genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, or viral infection. These alterations eventually give rise to dysregulated cellular populations. The malignant potential of these cell populations is further enhanced by perturbations in fundamental regulatory mechanisms, including cell-cycle control, apoptosis, and DNA repair pathways.

SOX genes constitute a family of transcriptional regulators characterized by a highly conserved high-mobility-group (HMG) DNA-binding domain. The acronym “SOX” stands for “Sex-determining region Y–related high-mobility-group box,” and these factors were originally identified for their primary roles in fundamental developmental processes such as embryogenesis, sex determination, gonad formation, and neural development [3, 4]. To date, more than 20 distinct SOX genes have been identified in mammals. With advances in tumor pathology, SOX factors have garnered increasing attention due to their significant influence on various malignant tumor characteristics, including tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, and responsiveness to therapy [5, 6].

In addition to the shared HMG domain, many SOX family members contain additional functional domains. SOX1, characterized by a transcriptional activation domain, is classified within the SOXB-1 subgroup [7]. It plays a significant role in various tumor types, including but not limited to lung cancer, cervical cancer, gastric cancer, and neuro-oncological diseases (Table 1). Furthermore, SOX1 is widely recognized as being associated with paraneoplastic neurological syndromes (PNS) [8, 9]. Given the incidence and severity of these diseases, the authors provide an overview of SOX1 gene expression and protein function, examine its involvement in critical cellular pathways, and discuss its clinical pathological significance. Additionally, the role of this transcription factor in tumorigenesis and progression is examined, together with a review of the latest developments concerning SOX1 in cancer prevention, diagnosis, SOX1 as a key member of the human SOX protein family

The human SOX1 gene is located at chromosome band 13q34 [10]. In the 1990s, researchers first identified the SRY gene in mouse models and recognized it as the long-sought Testis-Determining Factor [3, 11]. Subsequently, SOX1 was cloned and characterized because of its high sequence similarity to SRY [12]. SOX1 and SRY share a 79–amino-acid DNA-binding domain [13],termed the HMG box, and SOX1 is regarded as one of the family members most closely related to SRY [14]. The human SOX family currently comprises eight subgroups (A–H) [15] (Fig. 1). All SOX genes encode developmental transcription factors, yet they exhibit highly divergent functions (e.g., SRY mediates sex determination, SOX B1 group members influence neurogenesis, and SOX D group proteins regulate chondrogenesis). Likewise, SOX family proteins share the HMG box with SRY, which facilitates sequence-specific binding of SOX proteins to a common core motif in DNA, represented as (A/T)(A/T)CAA(A/T) [16].SOX factors initially bind the minor groove of DNA and subsequently induce an acute bend in the DNA helix [17, 18],an action that may facilitate transcriptional regulation. Moreover, the fact that numerous SOX proteins target the same DNA site may reflect an evolutionary strategy to increase functional redundancy among regulatory factors.Fig. 1SOX Gene Family. The human SOX1 gene consists of a single exon. Its open reading frame (ORF) contains four in-frame start codons located at positions 1, 4, 5, and 6. The gene encodes a protein of 391 amino acids with a molecular weight of approximately 39.02 kDa [19],and its expression is localized to the nucleus. The SOX1 protein contains a large C-terminal domain comprising 262 residues and a shorter hydrophobic N-terminal domain. Mouse studies have determined that its transcript size is approximately 4.0 kb [14, 19]. The SOX1 protein exhibits high sequence similarity between human and mouse [13]. Functionally, SOX1 primarily acts as a transcriptional activator in vivo and is classified within the SOXB-1 subgroup, which also includes the highly related factors SOX2 and SOX3.

Currently, SOX1 protein expression is predominantly observed in the central nervous system (CNS), where it plays a critical role [7, 20], It also contributes to the development of the lens [21, 22], neural crest [23], and the urogenital ridge [14]. SOX1 is a key transcription factor in neural fate determination, and its expression marks the initiation of ectodermal cells toward a neural lineage. By maintaining the proliferative state of neural progenitor cells, SOX1 is upregulated during neural induction and subsequently downregulated at appropriate times to permit differentiation, thus participating in the patterning of the nervous system. In the absence of exogenous inductive signals, overexpression of SOX1 is sufficient to drive neural differentiation, confirming its role as a neural determinant [24]. SOX genes exhibit dynamic expression during embryonic development and in adult tissues; notably, SOX1 mRNA is almost undetectable in adult tissues [25], consistent with its largely mutually exclusive expression relative to mature neurons. High levels of proneural proteins, such as Ngn2, suppress SOX1 expression, whereas certain microRNAs, including miR-184, can bidirectionally regulate the SOX1 circuit, thereby determining the fate of neural stem cells (NSCs)—self-renewal versus terminal differentiation [26, 27].

Cellular “stemness” refers to the potential of a cell to continue differentiating. For instance, pluripotent stem cells, such as embryonic stem cells, possess the capacity to differentiate into any cell type. Each differentiation event entails a partial loss of stemness. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) exhibit stem cell-like properties; however, due to dysregulated control mechanisms, they lose the ability to undergo normal differentiation [28]. CSCs generate heterogeneous tumors within tissues, disrupt normal physiological homeostasis, and exhibit strong resistance to conventional cancer therapies [29]. Given the critical role of SOX1 in human development, it is therefore reasonable to consider whether SOX1 exhibits similar properties in tumor tissues. Indeed, SOX1 participates in multiple key signaling pathways involved in tumor initiation and progression (Fig. 2), and its activity is regulated by multiple mechanisms. At the transcriptional level, SOX1 can be activated by CREB1, which binds to the SOX1 promoter upon stimulation of the ADH1B/cAMP/PKA pathway [30]. At the epigenetic level, EZH2 mediates H3K27me3 enrichment at the SOX1 promoter region, thereby repressing its transcription [31]. At the post-transcriptional level, several mechanisms regulate SOX1 RNA stability. For instance, miR-494-3p and miR-155 target SOX1 mRNA to inhibit its translation [32, 33]. And WTAP mediates N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification of SOX1 mRNA, which is recognized by YTHDF2, leading to accelerated degradation of the transcript [34]. In addition, PR-LncRNAs, located upstream of SOX family members in gliomas, can indirectly restrict SOX1 expression [35], which may contribute to its context-specific oncogenic features in subsequent central nervous system tumors. Promoter methylation is one of the most classical and critical modes of epigenetic regulation of gene expression. In general, high methylation of CpG islands blocks transcription factor binding and recruits methyl-binding proteins and repressive chromatin complexes, resulting in stable transcriptional silencing and functional inhibition of the gene. Notably, in multiple tumor types, the SOX1 promoter region is frequently hypermethylated due to viral infection, smoking, or other stochastic factors, and this epigenetic silencing markedly suppresses SOX1 transcriptional activity (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, due to structural similarity and overlapping expression with other members of the same subgroup (SOX2/3), these factors display complementary functional relationships [13]. In contrast, other members of the SOXB-1 subgroup, which tend to be oncogenic, may exhibit opposing functional characteristics relative to SOX1, which generally exhibits tumor-suppressive properties across various cancers (Fig. 3B) [6, 36]. Loss of SOX1 expression caused by promoter methylation disrupts its inhibitory effect on oncogenic pathways, particularly the negative regulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, leading to activation of proliferation-related genes. Therefore, SOX1 promoter methylation represents a key upstream event determining its expression pattern in tumors and provides the biological basis for its potential use in tumor diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and targeted therapy. Implications of SOX1 antibodies in paraneoplastic neurological syndromes

Paraneoplastic neurological syndromes refer to multisystem functional disorders indirectly triggered by malignant tumors, rather than by direct invasion, metastasis, or compression. Among the symptoms affecting distant organs, the nervous system is often predominantly involved. The underlying mechanism involves tumor expression of neural-related antigens, which elicits an autoimmune response, subsequently causing damage to neural tissues and leading to the associated clinical manifestations [37, 38].

In 2002, Yamada et al. identified an autoantibody in rat cerebellar Purkinje cells that specifically bound the nuclei of Bergmann glia [39]. Subsequently, Graus and colleagues detected this antibody in patients with small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) and named it the anti-glial nuclear antibody (AGNA). The presence of AGNA in humans was found to be associated with paraneoplastic neurological syndromes [40], with the highest positivity observed in patients with Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) (13/30, 43%). At that time, the antigen remained unidentified. In 2008, Sabater and Graus further screened a human fetal brain cDNA library using AGNA-positive sera and ultimately isolated the SOX1 gene, demonstrating that SOX1 antibodies serve as a serological marker for paraneoplastic LEMS. Detection of SOX1 antibodies in LEMS patients can therefore be used as a predictive tool for the development of small-cell lung cancer [41]. The hallmark of LEMS is an autoimmune impairment of neuromuscular transmission, mediated by antibodies against voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs). Notably, 40–60% of LEMS patients present with small-cell lung cancer, a highly aggressive tumor of neuroectodermal origin. SOX1 is highly expressed during neural development, including the embryonic and neurogenic stages, but its expression is markedly downregulated in the adult nervous system, remaining confined to specific cell populations [42]. In fact, SOX1 antibodies are highly associated with paraneoplastic neurological syndromes.

Symptoms related to SOX1 antibody-positive PNS most commonly manifest as Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (30%), followed by paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration (PCD, 18.2%) and paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis (PLE, 18.2%) [8].Rapidly progressive cerebellar syndromes and stiff-person spectrum disorders (SPS) are also part of the commonly observed disease spectrum [37]. Currently, SOX1 antibodies are primarily detected in PNS patients with small-cell lung cancer [43–45]. However, seropositivity has also been documented in PNS patients associated with other malignancies, including thyroid carcinoma [46], Hodgkin lymphoma [47], breast cancer [48], as well as in rare non-neoplastic conditions such as Crohn's disease [49]. Cytotoxic T cells are generally considered the main effector immune cells in PNS pathogenesis [50].And these PNS-associated malignancies are likely to harbor gene variants encoding onconeural proteins, which resemble highly immunogenic normal antigens expressed by neural cells, thereby triggering aberrant immune activation [51]. Indeed, expert consensus has recently updated diagnostic criteria for PNS, recognizing SOX1 antibodies, along with other onconeural antibodies (e.g., Anti-Yo, Anti-Hu, Anti-Ri), as high-risk serological markers associated with PNS [52].

Detection of SOX1 antibodies can facilitate the early identification of potential underlying malignancies, particularly in patients presenting with atypical clinical symptoms and lacking clear radiological features. Current evidence indicates that SOX1 antibodies function primarily as diagnostic markers rather than direct pathogenic mediators of neuronal injury. Their presence more likely reflects an abnormal immune response directed against the underlying tumor. In addition, treatment of PNS primarily focuses on management of the primary tumor through surgical resection, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. In some cases, immunosuppressive therapies, such as corticosteroids or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), may provide partial clinical benefit [53, 54].

Role of SOX1 in human malignant tumors

SOX1 in lung cancer (LUNG)

Lung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with approximately 2.2 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths reported in 2020, exhibiting significant regional variations in incidence and mortality. Low-dose spiral computed tomography screening has been shown to effectively reduce lung cancer mortality; however, its widespread application is limited by relatively high costs, disparities in regional medical resources, and high false-positive rates [55]. Several SOX family genes have been found to play roles in lung development [56]. Among them, SOX1 has been extensively studied in small-cell lung cancer. As a highly aggressive neuroendocrine tumor, SCLC patients often exhibit high titers of SOX1 antibodies [57, 58]. From an immunological perspective, this observation highlights that SOX1, which is primarily expressed in the immunoprivileged central nervous system, exhibits strong immunogenicity in the sera of SCLC patients [59]. Given the extremely low expression of SOX1 in normal adult tissues, such a robust humoral immune response suggests that cancer cells may reacquire properties resembling embryonic stemness. Side population (SP) cells represent a subset of lung cancer cells with enhanced tumor-initiating potential. Molecular characterization of SP cells by Salcido et al. revealed overexpression of SOX1, suggesting that SOX1 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for precision treatment of malignancies with high tumorigenic capacity [60]. Based on the key transcriptional regulators driving their activation, SCLC can be further classified into subtypes including SCLC-A, SCLC-N, SCLC-Y, and SCLC-P [61]. Kong et al. identified a novel epigenetic-genomic interaction mechanism in the SCLC-Aα subtype. In this subtype, which is driven by NKX2-1 super-enhancers, SOX1 forms a complex regulatory module with NKX2-1. This module cooperatively activates the expression of genes related to neural differentiation, promoting lineage-specific differentiation of lung cancer cells and enhancing intrinsic heterogeneity [62, 63]. This interaction network may represent a potential therapeutic target in SCLC, offering new insights into the lineage heterogeneity of SCLC and providing avenues for the development of subtype-specific therapies. Additionally, expression profiling revealed that miR-27a-5p and miR-34b-3p are significantly downregulated in SCLC clinical specimens and cell lines, and bioinformatic analyses identified SOX1 as one of their targets [64]; however, further functional studies are required to validate these findings. SOX1 antibodies are a prominent feature of SCLC-associated paraneoplastic syndromes, particularly LEMS, and also serve as SCLC-specific serological markers.

The clinical application of SOX1 in SCLC remains limited; however, serum SOX1 antibodies demonstrate good discriminatory capacity between SCLC-LEMS and NT-LEMS (sensitivity 67%, specificity 95%), and among SCLC-LEMS patients, positivity for SOX1 antibodies is associated with a trend toward reduced survival [65]. These findings provide high-level evidence supporting the use of SOX1 antibodies as a diagnostic tool for SCLC and underscore the potential benefit of combining multiple antigens to enhance screening efficacy. Nevertheless, it must be noted that although a positive result is highly suggestive, it is not exclusive to SCLC, and other PNS-associated tumors must be considered in clinical practice.

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the predominant pathological type of lung cancer, with an incidence markedly higher than that of SCLC. Its major subtypes include adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large-cell carcinoma. Molecular classification of NSCLC, such as EGFR mutations, significantly influences treatment response and survival, and precision studies at the subcellular level have driven the development of personalized medicine approaches. Autophagy is a degradative process by which cells maintain homeostasis under stress, such as during chemotherapy. In a cell model, Li et al. demonstrated that prolonged cisplatin exposure induces hypermethylation of the SOX1 promoter, resulting in its downregulation. This, in turn, activates protective autophagy pathways and promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), ultimately enhancing cancer cell survival and metastatic potential [66].

Promoter region methylation, particularly in CpG islands, represents a critical epigenetic regulatory mechanism: methylation alters DNA spatial structure, impedes transcription factor binding to promoters, and prevents formation of the transcription initiation complex, thereby affecting gene function. Kontic et al. observed a significant difference in SOX1 gene methylation levels between tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues in 65 NSCLC patients who underwent curative resection, with markedly higher methylation in tumor tissues (P < 0.001); hypermethylation of SOX1 was also associated with poor prognosis in NSCLC patients [67]. As a key transcription factor in embryonic development, hypermethylation of SOX1 leads to loss of its tumor-suppressive function [68]. Silencing of this gene disrupts actin cytoskeleton remodeling and promotes tumor metastasis in NSCLC patients [69], resulting in adverse clinical outcomes. These findings highlight the potential clinical value of precise SOX1 detection for optimizing existing therapies and developing novel targeted interventions.

SOX1 in gastric cancer (GC)

GC remains one of the most significant global health challenges. Most patients present with minimal symptoms at an early stage, and limitations in screening further contribute to disease progression and increased mortality risk [70]. SOX1 primarily functions as a tumor suppressor in gastric cancer, with its regulatory mechanisms involving upstream microRNAs and modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [71]. Myocardin-related transcription factor A (MRTF-A), a key regulator of smooth muscle cell differentiation, was found by Yin et al. to significantly upregulate the expression of the oncogenic miR-155 when overexpressed. This process is accompanied by activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and ultimately leads to downregulation of SOX1, promoting gastric cancer metastasis. Bioinformatic analyses predicted that miR-155 binds to the 3′ untranslated region of SOX1, and in vivo experiments demonstrated that inhibition of miR-155 reduces tumor growth and prolongs survival in nude mice, concomitant with upregulation of SOX1 expression [33]. Furthermore, in another bioinformatic study integrating single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and gene expression profiles in gastric cancer, the transcription factor FOXL1 was shown to regulate 26 differentially expressed genes containing risk-associated SNP loci, including SOX1, and SOX1 was observed to suppress gastric cancer cell proliferation by antagonizing the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [72]. Additionally, reports indicate that in gastric cancer patients with hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, the mutation frequency of SOX1 is elevated, with the viral oncoprotein HBx potentially inducing malignant transformation of gastric epithelial cells [73]. These findings collectively support the tumor-suppressive role of SOX1 in gastric cancer. In summary, SOX1-related pathways offer new avenues for molecularly targeted therapies in gastric cancer, particularly interventions aimed at miR-155.

SOX1 in colorectal cancer (CRC)

In colorectal cancer, SOX1 also exhibits a dynamic pattern of hypermethylation. From a classical pathological perspective, CRC progression follows the “normal epithelium–adenoma–carcinoma” sequence. Clinical symptoms of CRC often appear at advanced stages, and because there is a substantial latency period between polyp formation and malignant transformation, early diagnosis plays a crucial role in reducing disease-related mortality [74]. Hypermethylation of the SOX1 promoter represents a significant epigenetic hallmark in CRC. Molnár et al. reported that SOX1 hypermethylation is specifically detected at the CRC stage, whereas no alterations were noted among the top 50 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) during the adenoma (AD) stage. Hypermethylation of SOX1 occurs primarily in the promoter region, and concurrent analysis of mutational hotspots, such as TP53, also revealed significant methylation changes [75]. These findings suggest that epigenetic silencing of SOX1 may constitute a late event in the “trilogy” of CRC progression, wherein DNA methylation cooperates with genetic mutations to drive malignant transformation of normal epithelium. Tang et al. investigated a regulatory pathway involving SOX1. Wilms tumor 1-associated protein (WTAP), as a component of the m^6A methyltransferase complex, mediates methylation modification of SOX1 mRNA, whereas YTHDF2 facilitates degradation of SOX1 transcripts [34]. High-frequency promoter methylation of SOX1 in early-stage CRC patients underscores its role as a transcriptional repressor, consistent with its tumor-suppressive function.

Furthermore, SOX1 demonstrates potential clinical utility in predicting treatment outcomes and prognosis in CRC. While single-gene analyses of SOX1 have limited prognostic value, inclusion of SOX1 in a four-gene panel (SOX1/LMX1A/NKX6.1/ZNF177) substantially improves early-stage prognostic prediction [76]. SOX1 mRNA levels in CRC tissues are significantly lower than in adjacent normal tissues, and advanced tumors exhibit higher methylation levels, indicating its involvement in disease progression. Notably, PAX1 and SOX1 show concurrent hypermethylation in CRC [77]. An epigenomic analysis further identified SOX1 as a key gene within cytosine loci associated with drug-resistant phenotypes. Using bioinformatic tools and cell line experiments, the authors confirmed that SOX1 is a hotspot gene in CRC resistance to irinotecan or oxaliplatin, with its methylation status displaying substantial interpatient variability [78]. However, further mechanistic studies are still needed.

SOX1 in esophageal carcinoma (ESCA)

ESCA was the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide in 2020. Despite substantial progress in current research, it continues to pose a serious threat to human health. Continued investigation into its molecular mechanisms and the development of new therapeutic strategies remains essential [79]. Research on SOX1 in esophageal carcinoma is relatively limited, yet several valuable findings have emerged. A retrospective cohort study employing genome-wide analyses identified CpG methylation biomarkers in patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), revealing that SOX1 is significantly hypermethylated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues and is independently associated with poor prognosis [80]. This suggests that SOX1 may act as a tumor suppressor in ESCC and could serve as an independent predictor of patient survival. Similarly, Tang et al. confirmed through pyrosequencing that SOX1 hypermethylation represents a potential diagnostic marker for ESCC, with methylation levels significantly correlated with tumorigenic risk and exhibiting site-specific associations within the esophagus. Moreover, combining SOX1 with target genes PAX1 and ZNF582 substantially enhanced early diagnostic performance for ESCA (AUC = 0.911) [81]. From a pathological perspective, Rad et al. demonstrated that SOX1 functions as a tumor suppressor in ESCA, with its low expression significantly associated with tumor progression, invasiveness, and lymph node metastasis. Additionally, SOX1 expression negatively correlates with the overexpression of the stemness regulator SALL4 [82]. These findings indicate that SOX1 may modulate ESCA stemness and invasiveness by suppressing Wnt signaling and regulating SALL4.

SOX1 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

Approximately 75–85% of primary liver cancers are hepatocellular carcinoma, and the major risk factors include hepatitis virus infection, alcohol-related liver disease, and non-alcoholic liver disease [83]. SOX1, as a member of the SOXB-1 subgroup alongside SOX2 and SOX3, typically functions as a transcriptional activator. Interestingly, in hepatocellular carcinoma, SOX1 contrasts with its subgroup counterparts SOX2 and SOX3, which primarily exhibit oncogenic roles; to date, SOX1 is the only SOXB-1 member reported to possess tumor-suppressive functions. The canonical Wnt signaling pathway regulates pluripotent differentiation of stem cells, organ development, and tissue regeneration. However, aberrant activation of this pathway is frequently recognized as a central molecular event in malignant tumors. Tsao et al. demonstrated that SOX1 is silenced in HCC cell lines (e.g., HepG2, Hep3B) and clinical HCC specimens due to promoter hypermethylation. Mechanistically, SOX1 can directly bind to β-catenin, competitively inhibiting TCF transcriptional activity, which in turn downregulates c-MYC and cyclin D1 [84] (Fig. 4). In parallel, SOX1 induces G1-phase cell cycle arrest and promotes cellular senescence, collectively highlighting its role as a direct antagonist of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and a multi-faceted tumor suppressor in HCC. Consistent with observations in other gastrointestinal malignancies, SOX1 undergoes dynamic epigenetic modifications during HCC development. Notably, most HCC patients present with HBV infection, and the methylation frequency of SOX1 exhibits a progressive increase along the disease continuum—from healthy liver, chronic hepatitis B, cirrhosis, to HCC [85]. Importantly, hypermethylation of SOX1 is already evident at the early HCC (eHCC) stage and, together with DKK3, SALL3, and SFRPs, constitutes part of the initial methylation events in HCC. Furthermore, in a retrospective study comprising 96 HCC specimens, Lou et al. reported that SOX1, assessed via molecular biology approaches (RT-PCR, Western blot) and pathology (IHC), serves as an independent prognostic biomarker for HCC. Its expression is significantly correlated with tumor progression, including vascular invasion and advanced TNM stage [88]. SOX1 in breast carcinoma (BRCA)

Breast carcinoma accounts for approximately one-third of all malignant tumors in women, with a mortality rate representing nearly 15% of all diagnosed cases [89]. As a highly heterogeneous disease, the global burden of BRCA is shaped by molecular subtypes, accessibility to medical resources, and lifestyle-related factors. Although precision medicine has markedly improved patient outcomes, challenges remain in addressing healthcare disparities across countries with varying levels of medical advancement and in optimizing long-term quality-of-life management for survivors living with the disease.SOX1 has been identified as a tumor suppressor gene in BRCA [90], exerting its major function through inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Song et al. reported that SOX1 mRNA was markedly downregulated in both BRCA tissues and breast cancer cell lines, whereas ectopic overexpression of SOX1 suppressed malignant phenotypes including proliferation, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis. Consistent with Fig. 4, further studies demonstrated that SOX1 significantly repressed β-catenin/TCF transcriptional activity and reduced the expression of downstream effectors such as cyclin D1 (regulator of cell cycle progression) and c-Myc (driver of proliferation) [91]. A study focusing on CD44⁺/CD24⁻ and CD133⁺ cancer stem cell populations in Brca1-deficient BC revealed that high SOX1 expression coexisted with several stemness-associated markers, including Oct4 and CD133. SOX1 was subsequently incorporated into a “stemness-associated gene panel,” with its elevated expression linked to cancer stem cell self-renewal, therapeutic resistance, and tumorigenicity [92]. However, compared with other SOX family members such as SOX2 and SOX9, the mechanistic characterization of SOX1 in BRCA remains relatively limited.

Paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration represents one of the most common paraneoplastic neurological syndromes, with BRCA being a prototypical associated malignancy. Berger et al. reported five cases of paraneoplastic neurological disorders with anti-SOX1 antibody positivity, of which two were BRCA [93]. In addition, in a regional database including 520 anti-SOX1 antibody–positive patients, 93.5% (486 cases) were diagnosed with cancer, and BRCA accounted for 0.6% (3 cases) [8]. Importantly, BRCA patients with anti-SOX1 antibody positivity often exhibit coexistence of multiple antibodies (e.g., anti-Hu, anti-Yo), a finding associated with poorer prognosis. Of further note, a case report described male BRCA accompanied by demyelinating neuropathy, in which anti-SOX1 antibody was the only detectable paraneoplastic antibody [48]. This observation highlights the clinical importance of expanding tumor screening in patients presenting with unexplained peripheral neuropathy and concomitant anti-SOX1 antibody positivity.

SOX1 in bladder carcinoma (BLCA)

BLCA is among the most common malignancies worldwide and exhibits marked molecular heterogeneity. At the genomic level, BLCA is characterized by a high frequency of somatic mutations. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) study reported that each BC case carries an average of approximately 200 somatic mutations, a number considerably higher than that of most solid tumors. The 13q33-34 region has been reported to harbor loss of heterozygosity (LOH) across multiple cancers. Igci et al. identified two high-frequency LOH loci within this region, namely D13S285 (23.3%) and D13S1315 (20%) by comparing BLCA pathological tissues with adjacent normal counterparts. Notably, the SOX1 gene is located between these two markers, suggesting its potential interaction with the promoter of the classical tumor suppressor ING1. Thus, SOX1 may participate in tumor suppressive pathways through direct regulation of ING1 expression, making it an important candidate tumor suppressor gene (TSG) in BLCA [94]. The ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) is known to regulate diverse intracellular processes. In BLCA, USP2 promotes tumor progression by stabilizing EZH2 via deubiquitination, which in turn epigenetically silences SOX1 expression. Restoration of SOX1 expression in animal models markedly suppressed tumor growth, providing further evidence of its tumor suppressive role [31].

In parallel, Jose et al. demonstrated that hypermethylation of the SOX1 promoter was significantly enriched in BLCA samples and correlated with muscle-invasive disease. Further analyses revealed that SOX1 methylation could serve as an independent prognostic biomarker for cancer-specific mortality, and its predictive value was enhanced when combined with PITX2 and CSPG2 [95]. Collectively, these findings indicate that epigenetic silencing of SOX1 contributes to BLCA pathogenesis, likely through mechanisms associated with Wnt pathway regulation. Given the accessibility and non-invasiveness of urine sampling, substantial efforts have been directed toward developing urinary biomarkers for BLCA diagnosis. SOX1-OT, a long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) transcribed from the antisense strand overlapping the SOX1 locus, has emerged as one such candidate. A two-stage study design, including both retrospective case–control and prospective validation cohorts, evaluated the diagnostic potential of urinary SOX1-OT/H4C6 dual-gene methylation. This combined methylation assay demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity, offering a promising non-invasive diagnostic approach for BLCA [96]. Such strategies may serve as adjuncts or alternatives to cystoscopy, with particular utility in early screening and triaging patients presenting with hematuria. Although these findings are encouraging, they should be interpreted with caution. At present, such tests are best positioned as adjuncts to cystoscopy rather than direct replacements. SOX1 still requires further validation in real-world settings through large, multicenter cohort studies.

SOX1 in head and neck cancer (HNC)

Head and neck cancers rank among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, with pronounced regional variations. In China, the incidence of HNC has shown a steady upward trend, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) accounts for more than 90% of all cases. The disease is particularly common in southern China and in populations with betel nut–chewing habits, while infection with high-risk cancer-causing viruses such as human papillomavirus and Epstein-Barr virus further increases susceptibility. Despite advances, treatment of HNC remains challenging due to the significant toxicity of conventional chemoradiotherapy and the scarcity of actionable oncogenic driver mutations. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), the most common subtype of HNC, SOX1 expression is frequently silenced by promoter hypermethylation. Functional assays demonstrated that ectopic expression of SOX1 markedly inhibited colony formation in NPC cell lines. SOX1 restoration suppressed the Wnt signaling pathway by directly binding to β-catenin and inducing its non-proteasomal degradation, thereby reversing epithelial–mesenchymal transition and ultimately reducing tumor growth and metastasis [97]. These findings identify SOX1 as a potential therapeutic target in NPC. Considering that 95% of NPCs are undifferentiated squamous cell carcinomas, differentiation-based therapy represents a rational strategy. Retinoic acid (RA) and its analogs, which regulate tumor biology by modulating cell differentiation and apoptosis, are metabolized primarily by CYP26 enzymes and UDP-glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs). SOX1 was shown to transcriptionally downregulate UGT2B7, thereby reducing RA catabolism and leading to intracellular accumulation of RA metabolites, which in turn promoted differentiation of NPC cells. Targeting UGT2B7 consequently induced differentiation, suppressed proliferation, and enhanced senescence in NPC models [98], suggesting a promising avenue for differentiation therapy.

Adding further complexity, Lei et al. reported a context-dependent role of SOX1 in advanced NPC. Although SOX1 generally acts as a tumor suppressor by limiting proliferation and invasion, high SOX1 expression in late-stage NPC was associated with poor prognosis and increased risk of tumor recurrence following therapy. Mechanistically, SOX1 suppressed MYC transcription, a key regulator of ribosome biogenesis, driving NPC cells into a quiescent state and conferring paclitaxel resistance. Notably, resistance diminished when SOX1 expression was reduced, underscoring its role in a non-genetic drug resistance mechanism [99]. Targeting the SOX1 axis may therefore provide a novel therapeutic strategy for preventing recurrence and overcoming chemoresistance in NPC.

In patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), the SOX1 methylation index detected via oropharyngeal swabs was significantly higher than that of healthy controls. Notably, among betel nut chewers, the diagnostic performance improved, with the SOX1 methylation index yielding an AUC of 0.84, sensitivity of 77.78%, and specificity of 100%, suggesting superior screening potential for OSCC in populations exposed to betel quid [100]. Given the procedural simplicity of oral swab collection, this approach is particularly advantageous for screening in high-risk regions with limited healthcare resources. Wang et al. further demonstrated that the SOX1 promoter region exhibited markedly increased methylation in dysplastic oral keratinocyte (DOK) cells, with an average methylation level 4.5-fold higher than that in normal human gingival fibroblasts. Astaxanthin (Asx) was found to reduce SOX1 methylation by suppressing DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) expression. Functional restoration of SOX1 consequently inhibited DOK cell migration, induced cell-cycle arrest, and promoted apoptosis [101]. These findings provide additional evidence supporting SOX1 as an epigenetic therapeutic target. Furthermore, a bioinformatics analysis based on TCGA-HNSC tissue data identified significant differential methylation of SOX1 (involving eight DMRs), nominating it as a potential biomarker for plasma circulating free DNA (cfDNA) profiling [102].

SOX1 in cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC)

CESC is among the most common malignancies affecting women worldwide, with over 90% of cases associated with persistent infection by high-risk human papillomaviruses, particularly HPV16 and HPV18. In terms of treatment, surgical resection combined with adjuvant chemoradiotherapy remains the cornerstone of cervical cancer management, while recurrence and metastasis represent the primary causes of mortality. The World Health Organization (WHO) has set a global target to eliminate cervical cancer by 2030 through the implementation of universal HPV vaccination, enhanced population-based screening, and improved healthcare accessibility.SOX1 has been identified as a sensitive biomarker in both cervical cancer and early intraepithelial neoplasia [103, 104]. Multi-omics studies conducted in Asian populations have demonstrated that SOX1 undergoes hypermethylation in cervical cancer and precancerous lesions [105], with methylation levels showing a significant increase along with disease progression [106]. Hsu et al. further revealed that HPV infection interacts epigenetically with host genes, where SOX1 methylation levels display a positive correlation with HPV16/18 methylation [107]. Such genotype-specific associations highlight the differential epigenetic regulation imposed by HPV infection on host cells, providing molecular insights that may optimize cervical cancer screening strategies.SOX1 methylation exhibits robust discriminatory power for high-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions, and its superior diagnostic performance, either as a stand-alone or in combination with other biomarkers, supports its application as a triage tool in high-risk HPV–positive individuals [108]. Moreover, in cervical cancer cell lines lacking endogenous SOX1 expression, forced re-expression of SOX1 markedly suppresses cell proliferation, growth, and invasive capabilities.

Mechanistically, SOX1 overexpression reduces cellular β-catenin levels, competitively inhibits TCF transcriptional activity, and negatively regulates EMT phenotypes. Conversely, high-risk HPVs may induce SOX1 promoter hypermethylation by upregulating DNMTs, thereby releasing inhibitory control over the β-catenin/Wnt signaling pathway [109].SOX1 in cervical cancer functions not only as a sensitive biomarker but also exerts tumor-suppressive effects by inhibiting the β-catenin/Wnt signaling pathway. Its methylation status interacts with HPV infection, with HPV-driven epigenetic regulation leading to its inactivation, highlighting SOX1’s dual potential as both a diagnostic marker and a therapeutic target.

Cervical adenocarcinoma (CAC) is a less common subtype of cervical cancer; however, its incidence has gradually increased in recent years. CAC generally has a poorer prognosis than cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC), with a higher propensity for metastasis and lower treatment sensitivity, highlighting the urgent need for effective diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. In fact, studies have identified SOX1 as a CAC-specific methylated gene, with promoter hypermethylation significantly higher in CAC compared to normal tissues, demonstrating tissue specificity [110]. And SOX1 methylation can be stably detected in cervical cytology specimens, suggesting its potential as a novel molecular marker for CAC screening [111]. Additionally, Zhao et al. reported that SOX1 promoter hypermethylation serves as a potential diagnostic biomarker for CAC, and SOX1 positivity is associated with improved overall survival (OS) [112]. Although SOX1 has shown considerable promise as a clinical biomarker in cervical cancer, most studies are limited to cell lines or small patient cohorts, and in-depth in vivo validation remains necessary.

SOX1 in central nervous system tumors

Approximately 80% of malignant primary central nervous system tumors are gliomas, with a global age-standardized incidence rate ranging from 4.67 to 5.73 per 100,000 individuals. Although gliomas are relatively rare compared with other solid malignancies, a single tumor often harbors multiple molecular subclonal populations, and this high heterogeneity severely compromises therapeutic efficacy [113]. SOX1 plays a crucial role in neural development, driving neuronal differentiation and promoting oligodendrocyte maturation [114]. PR-LncRNA, a p53-regulated tumor suppressive non-coding RNA, exhibits significantly reduced expression as tumor malignancy progresses. Among SOX family members, SOX1 shows the most pronounced negative correlation with PR-LncRNA, and SOX1 knockdown can block the oncogenic phenotypes induced by PR-LncRNA silencing [35]. Notably, SOX1 appears to exhibit oncogenic properties in gliomas that contrast with its tumor-suppressive role in most other cancers. Loss of neuroprotective mechanisms is an initiating factor for various malignant CNS disorders, and SOX1 functions as a dual regulator of neuroprotection and glioma genesis. It promotes glioma stem cell (GSC) survival through metabolic reprogramming (e.g., enhanced glutamate conversion) and aberrant activation of oncogenic pathways, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR [115].

Furthermore, Garcia et al. reported that high SOX1 expression correlates with poor prognosis in glioblastoma (GBM) patients, with elevated SOX1 mRNA observed in both tumor samples and GSCs. SOX1 promotes tumor progression by maintaining GSC stemness and disrupting cell cycle regulation, as evidenced by aberrant Cyclin D1 expression [116, 117]. Holmberg et al. further identified SOX1 as a GSC marker with significant upregulation in high-grade gliomas (WHO grade IV) [118]. Given that SOX1 is highly expressed in neural progenitors but nearly absent in differentiated neurons, its expression level may serve as a metric for glioma malignancy, with higher SOX1 correlating with stronger tumor stemness. This pattern suggests that SOX1’s role in gliomas may be independent of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway, contrasting with its tumor-suppressive function in other cancers. In CNS tumors, SOX1-positive cells represent a distinct, highly proliferative subpopulation capable of maintaining cancer stem-like properties, and targeting SOX1 and its downstream pathways may slow glioma progression and recurrence. Interestingly, antidepressants, such as imipramine, have been found to significantly downregulate GSC markers, including SOX1 [119], potentially impairing glioma self-renewal and differentiation potential. The SOX1 locus also contains the overlapping transcript SOX1-OT. Analogous to bladder cancer, SOX1-OT variants may serve as biomarkers for CNS tumor subtyping [120]. Targeting SOX1-OT could indirectly regulate SOX1, and the complex structure and co-expression patterns in malignant tumors provide a novel mechanistic perspective for the development of SOX1-targeted therapies.

SOX1 in other malignant tumors

The stage at initial diagnosis is closely associated with prognosis in patients with endometrial carcinoma (EC), with abnormal vaginal bleeding often presenting as the first symptom. Wentzensen et al. identified SOX1 as a “top-tier methylation marker,” demonstrating significant value in distinguishing endometrial carcinoma from benign endometrial lesions. The probe SOX1_P294_F exhibited markedly elevated methylation levels in cancerous tissues, with a single-gene odds ratio of 17.76 (95% CI: 5.09–61.97) [121]. Given the high-frequency hypermethylation of SOX1 in EC, it is suggested that SOX1 plays a central role in the epigenetic dysregulation of EC [122], although the detailed mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Additionally, 23% of patients with atypical endometrial hyperplasia (AH) were found to harbor occult endometrial carcinoma upon postoperative pathological examination. Lai et al. reported that the SOX1 methylation index in the hidden EC group was significantly higher than in patients with isolated AH (P = 0.023) [123]. Taken together, methylation markers could be integrated into liquid-based cytology or endometrial biopsy, providing valuable adjunctive information for pathological diagnosis and aiding in the differentiation between healthy endometrium, AH, and EC.

Early diagnosis of ovarian carcinoma (OC) remains challenging, with over 70% of patients presenting at advanced stages, adversely affecting prognosis. In ovarian tumor tissues, SOX1 exhibits a hypermethylation rate of up to 58.7%, which increases the risk of patient mortality by 2.14-fold (95% CI: 1.05–4.36). Multivariate analysis confirmed its role as an independent prognostic factor. Furthermore, when combined with PAX1/SFRP1 in serological testing, SOX1 provides optimal screening performance (sensitivity 73.08%, specificity 75%), significantly outperforming plasma CA125 detection [124]. Kaur et al., in a retrospective study of Indian women, also reported that the SOX1 methylation rate was significantly higher in the ovarian cancer group compared to controls [125], suggesting that SOX1 hypermethylation could serve as a novel epigenetic biomarker for OC and a candidate target for blood-based cfDNA methylation detection, potentially enhancing early detection of ovarian carcinoma.

Mathews et al. employed Matrigel invasion assays to isolate an invasive subpopulation of prostate cancer (PCa) cells, which exhibited high tumorigenic potential. They found that the SOX1 promoter was highly methylated in non-invasive and parental cells, whereas it was hypomethylated in the invasive subpopulation. qRT-PCR and immunobiological analyses, including immunocytochemistry and co-immunoprecipitation, confirmed that SOX1 mRNA and protein were significantly upregulated in the invasive cells. Knockdown of SOX1 markedly reduced the invasive and differentiation capabilities of these cancer cells. Through promoter demethylation, SOX1 can directly bind STAT3 and activate the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby promoting prostate tumor invasion and maintaining cancer stem cell traits, with its expression level positively correlating with PCa progression [126].

In hematologic malignancies, SOX1 is significantly downregulated in the serum of patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Rescue experiments demonstrated that re-expression of SOX1 inhibited tumor cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis. The Nuclear Factor I (NFI) family consists of transcription factors sharing an N-terminal DNA-binding domain, among which NFIC can target and suppress SOX1, resulting in aberrant autophagy activation, inhibition of apoptosis, and driving AML progression [127]. However, these findings are limited to in vitro cell studies, and further mechanistic investigations are required to clarify its translational potential.

In mesenchymal malignancies, SOX1 predominantly exhibits oncogenic features. Tsz-Kwong Man et al. used array-based comparative genomic hybridization and identified frequent SOX1 amplification in osteosarcoma [128]. Such amplification may cooperate with other oncogenic genes and promote osteosarcoma genesis through abnormal downstream pathway activation, warranting further functional validation.

Discussions

SOX1, as one of the structurally conserved and functionally complex members of the SOX transcription factor family, has long been considered a key regulatory factor in embryonic development and neural differentiation. However, recent research has gradually revealed that SOX1 also plays special roles in the occurrence and development of various tumors. Broadly, in most solid tumors SOX1 tends to act as a tumor suppressor. It suppresses tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion via multiple mechanisms, including modulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, regulation of tumor stemness, inhibition of EMT, and epigenetic modulation. At the same time in solid tumors like lung [67], cervical [106], liver [84], and colorectal cancers [75]. SOX1 frequently loses its tumor-suppressive capacity owing to promoter hypermethylation. Its abnormal promoter methylation can be actively induced by cancer-related viruses such as HPV and HBV [73, 108], by treatment-associated stress such as cisplatin chemotherapy [66], or by harmful lifestyle factors including smoking or betel-quid chewing [100]. In some cancer types, it may also accumulate as a stochastic or age-related epigenetic event. In contrast, in CNS tumors SOX1 displays cancer-promoting features—maintaining cancer cell stemness and driving tumor progression. A comparable pro-invasive role has also been observed in prostate cancer, apparently independent of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway. This suggests that SOX1’s mode of action is multifaceted and its transcriptional network interactions are complex. The biological role of SOX1 is not static; its function is highly dependent on tissue context and the differentiation stage of target cells. SOX1 cannot simply be regarded as an oncogene or a tumor suppressor, its context-dependent duality warrants further investigation. For example, in gliomas and some neuroectoderm-derived tumors, SOX1 expression is actually correlated with increased tumor malignancy. This may be due to its inherent developmental function in the maintenance of neural precursor cells. While such stemness maintenance underpins neurogenesis during development, within the tumor microenvironment it may be co-opted to sustain tumor stem cells. Neuroendocrine (NE) transformation is an important mechanism of acquired resistance in some lung and prostate cancers; the loss of TP53/RB1 is the foundation of NE transformation, but it still requires cooperation from factors like SOX [129]. In fact, in non-small cell lung cancer—a tumor type with prominent neuroendocrine features—the SOX1 promoter becomes hypermethylated following prolonged cisplatin exposure, which abrogates its inhibitory influence on autophagy and enhances the cells’ ability to remove cisplatin-induced damage via autophagy, thereby promoting survival [66]. The seemingly contradictory findings regarding SOX1 as either a tumor promoter or inhibitor across different cancers may be explained by two factors. First, the distinct cellular context of different tissues is critical. In most epithelial-derived cancers, SOX1 appears to function within a tumor-suppressive circuit, primarily by antagonizing oncogenic signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin. In contrast, in CNS tumors and SCLC, SOX1's developmental role in maintaining the neural progenitor state may be co-opted to sustain cancer stemness, thereby driving tumor-promoting outcomes. Second, methodological disparities contribute to the divergent conclusions. Data obtained from in vitro cell line models, which often possess unique genetic backgrounds and lack a tumor microenvironment, may yield findings not fully replicable in vivo models.

The molecular interaction between SOX1 and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is relatively conserved, and its ability to competitively bind β-catenin represents a common mechanism for suppressing downstream oncogenic genes, particularly Cyclin D1 and c-MYC. Although the association between SOX1 dysregulation and cancer progression has been well documented, our understanding of its underlying mechanisms remains incomplete. Many studies report strong correlations but lack direct in vivo mechanistic validation, and the reliance on small or single-center cohorts limits generalizability. Future research should integrate analyses across multiple cancer types using well-annotated clinical cohorts. In addition, the systematic understanding of various SOX1 modifications remains insufficient, and integrative multi-omics analyses are warranted to validate their functional significance. While specific methylation sites within the SOX1 promoter have been identified in gynecologic cancers [104, 121] and linked to tumor initiation and progression, the existing evidence is unevenly distributed across cancer types. This uneven distribution raises several unresolved questions. It remains unclear whether specific SOX1 promoter methylation sites are conserved across different tumor types, and whether their biological significance is consistent. Therefore, research on SOX1 promoter methylation should be extended to a wider range of cancers.

In the development of precision medicine strategies, a “druggable” target often refers to a factor that can be specifically regulated by antibodies or other biologics. Targetability not only implies that its expression or function can be modulated, but also that the factor occupies a nodal position within key cellular pathways. As a member of the SOXB1 subfamily, SOX1 is a structurally stable transcriptional regulator, and its aberrant expression or epigenetic silencing has been widely reported in various tumors. However, SOX1 has not traditionally been considered a conventional “druggable” target. This is partly due to incomplete understanding of its functional mechanisms and the inherent structural challenges of targeting transcription factors, which often contain dynamic, intrinsically disordered regions. Furthermore, the drug-transcription factor-DNA interface possesses unfavorable physicochemical properties—such as convex, positively charged, and flat surfaces lacking deep pockets—which preclude the formation of stable binding sites for small molecules [130]. SOX1 functions as a natural antagonist of the Wnt signaling pathway. Unlike genetic mutations that typically arise during tumorigenesis, epigenetic inactivation of Wnt antagonists (e.g., promoter hypermethylation) often initiates in precancerous lesions and accumulates during malignant progression, suggesting that epigenetic changes may precede genetic alterations [131]. Given the reversibility of epigenetic modifications, combination therapies targeting epigenetic regulators (such as chromatin regulators) may have therapeutic potential in Wnt-dependent cancers. Beyond developing drugs that directly target the SOX1 transcription factor, modulating its upstream and downstream pathways (such as the SOX1/Wnt/β-catenin axis) could be a viable approach. The Porcupine (PORCN) enzyme catalyzes the palmitoleoylation of Wnt proteins, a modification necessary for their activity. WNT974 specifically inhibits PORCN to block aberrant Wnt signaling; this small-molecule inhibitor has shown preliminary clinical activity in metastatic colorectal cancer trials [132]. ICG-001 is a selective antagonist of β-catenin interaction; by blocking the binding of β-catenin to the transcriptional co-activator CBP, it inhibits Wnt/β-catenin pathway transcription. In preclinical studies, ICG-001 significantly inhibits endometrial cancer cell growth [133], and it has been reported to reverse tamoxifen resistance in triple-negative breast cancer [134]. However, various regulatory layers—including DNA methylation (epigenetic modification) and diverse post-translational modifications—significantly influence SOX1’s physiological and pathological roles. Regulating SOX1’s transcriptional activity, interfering with its DNA binding sites, or disrupting its nuclear localization may provide more feasible alternative strategies. Several preclinical studies have shown that both the classical DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-aza-2'-deoxycytidine [76] and the natural compound astaxanthin [101] can reverse SOX1 hypermethylation and restore its mRNA levels in different cancer models (such as colorectal cancer and oral premalignant lesions). These findings provide experimental support for indirectly targeting SOX1 function.

Comprehensive management remains a cornerstone of modern cancer therapy. High-sensitivity early diagnosis can significantly improve cancer cure rates and long-term survival, while effective efficacy assessment and prognostic modeling help maximize treatment outcomes. The clinical value of SOX1 depends not only on its alteration in a given cancer but on whether measurement provides actionable information for specific subgroups or disease stages. Clinical translation is limited by lack of standardized assays. In methylation testing, differences in targeted CpG sites and specimen type (tissue, serum, urine) can yield inconsistent results. Similarly, SOX1 antibody assays lack a universally accepted immunoassay format, which may produce inter-laboratory variability. A reproducible assay with predefined thresholds is essential for clinical adoption. Targeted therapeutic strategies pose additional challenges. Although upstream interventions, such as DNMT inhibitors, and downstream interventions, such as Wnt pathway inhibitors, provide potential alternatives, these approaches are not specific to SOX1 and may introduce off-target effects. Achieving selective modulation of SOX1 activity therefore remains an unresolved pharmacological challenge. Despite these obstacles, a clearer clinical role for SOX1 has begun to emerge. SOX1 promoter methylation shows potential for incorporation into next-generation cervical cancer screening, with a workflow involving HPV primary testing, SOX1-based triage alongside other markers, and colposcopy confirmation. This strategy may enhance screening precision, reduce unnecessary invasive procedures, and optimize resource allocation. Moreover, unlike its context-dependent behavior in solid tumors, the function of SOX1 in paraneoplastic syndromes is comparatively well defined. SOX1 antibody testing can serve as a valuable adjunct in neurological practice, and incorp orating it into routine diagnostic evaluation for patients with unexplained neurological symptoms may help identify underlying tumor risk at an earlier stage. Notably, some researchers are actively exploring the clinical application of this bridging factor. Promoter methylation of SOX1 has been identified as a common early event in several epithelial-derived tumors. For example, combined detection of SOX1 and PAX1 enhances the identification of high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL) in cervical liquid-based cytology (Identifier: ChiCTR2400092968, https://trialsearch.who.int), providing a more stratified screening tool for HPV-positive women. In the management of immune-related tumor complications, a prospective study on adverse events in patients receiving anti-PD-1 immunotherapy is currently recruiting (Identifier: NCT05669638, https://ClinicalTrials.gov), in which SOX1, as a key onconeural antibody, may provide early indicators for immune-related neurological events, highlighting its potential value in predicting treatment-related toxicities. Similarly, a registry project for pediatric multiple sclerosis and other inflammatory brain disorders (Identifier: NCT05017142, https://ClinicalTrials.gov) includes SOX1 among tumor neuron antibody detection markers. Regarding prognostic prediction, SOX1 also shows clinical potential. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, radiotherapy sensitivity studies (Identifier: ChiCTR2500104903, https://trialsearch.who.int) demonstrate that dual methylation of SOX1/PAX1 serves as an important epigenetic marker for predicting radiotherapy response, guiding individualized dose optimization. Taken together, these studies outline the potential clinical utility of SOX1, although they remain at early stages with limited sample sizes and follow-up durations. They preliminarily sketch a translational trajectory from “molecular marker” to “clinical tool.” Future validation through multi-center, large-cohort, prospective clinical trials is warranted to assess SOX1’s utility across cancer types and treatment strategies, as well as its integrative potential within pathology platforms, clinical adverse event monitoring systems, and molecular therapy centers.

Conclusions and perspectives

Originally recognized for its role in embryonic neural development, SOX1 has garnered significant attention due to its aberrant expression, promoter hypermethylation, and autoantibody generation across multiple malignant tumors. Emerging evidence indicates that SOX1 functions as a tumor suppressor in most malignancies; however, its elevated expression in certain neural-related tumors enhances cancer stem-like properties and promotes tumor progression—a phenomenon likely attributable to its context-dependent capacity to maintain cellular stemness in diverse tissue environments. SOX1 methylation abnormalities have been extensively reported in various cancers including lung, cervical, hepatic, and head and neck carcinomas. This epigenetic modification thereby ablates its tumor-suppressive biological function. Related detection methods have entered preliminary clinical investigation stages, demonstrating promising diagnostic and prognostic potential. At the same time, as a nuclear transcription factor, SOX1 is widely considered a non-druggable molecule. Therapeutic approaches should therefore target its upstream regulatory mechanisms—including DNA methylation, RNA modification, and non-coding RNA networks—as well as downstream effector pathways, such as Wnt/β-catenin–mediated transcription. Additionally, SOX1 antibodies serve as serological markers for paraneoplastic neurological syndromes, showing high specificity in neuroendocrine tumors like SCLC and providing a molecular basis for detecting tumor-associated complications. Nevertheless, the clinical translation of SOX1 faces challenges, including unclear functional mechanisms, lack of standardized detection protocols, and limited effective targeting strategies. Advancing the conversion of SOX1 from a mere pathological factor to an integral node in clinical diagnostic-therapeutic networks represents a critical research frontier.
