Mental health illnesses and university life satisfaction among medical students in post-conflict Syria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Monzer Keblawy, Alaa Alsarhan, Ali Taghi, Mais Alreem Mohaisen, Mohammad Ibrahim Atia, Ahmad Alhamid, Collaborators group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical students are a population highly vulnerable to psychological distress due to their demanding academic environment. In Syria, the prolonged conflict has further exacerbated educational and psychological challenges. This study evaluates University Life Satisfaction (ULS) among Syrian medical students and its association with depression, anxiety and stress.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted between March and May 2025 across public and private Syrian universities. A total of 700 medical, dental and pharmacy students completed an online questionnaire assessing demographic variables, ULS (The Campus Satisfaction Scale) and psychological distress (Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scales-21).ResultsModerate overall ULS was reported (mean=2.95±0.65). Severe-to-extremely severe levels of depression (35.0%), anxiety (29.4%) and stress (29.8%) were observed. ULS was significantly lower among females, students in later academic years and those with poor prior psychological diagnoses. Results revealed negative correlational relationships between ULS and depression (r=−0.333), anxiety (r=−0.171) and stress (r=−0.240) (p<0.001). Regression analysis indicated that grade point average, financial status and depressive symptoms were significantly associated with ULS.ConclusionSyrian medical students reported concerning levels of psychological distress, particularly those with lower ULS. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted mental health interventions and systemic improvements in Syrian educational environments. Enhancing student engagement, improving campus facilities and integrating psychological support services help reduce distress and improve ULS among medical students. These results reflect correlational relationships only and do not establish causality.

Introduction

University is one of the most critical milestones in students’ lives, bringing with it a mix of academic and social challenges that can significantly affect their psychological well-being.1 2 Medical students, in particular, face intense workloads, demanding curricula and long clinical rotations, all of which contribute to elevated stress and anxiety.2 3 University students are facing a demanding curriculum, long clinical rotations and continuous evaluations.3 4 However, the university environment—with its supportive instructors, peers and extracurricular activities—can play a protective role in alleviating these stressors and fostering positive mental health.3 5 Attachment to campus refers to the extent to which students identify with and feel part of the academic community, as well as their level of engagement in academic and social activities.4 6 Students who feel a strong sense of attachment to their university tend to experience lower levels of stress, thanks to the supportive social networks that help them cope with academic challenges.4 5 Conversely, a weak sense of attachment can lead to social isolation and make students more vulnerable to stress and anxiety.4 6 The results of previous studies showed that both average grade scores and academic efficiency were strongly related to satisfaction with the efficiency of studying and slightly related to some of the other subscales.7 8

In Syria, the prolonged conflict has disrupted university infrastructure and negatively impacted students’ well-being and mental health. In conflict-affected settings such as Syria, where higher education systems face resource constraints and instability, examining ULS provides critical insights into how institutional factors contribute to both student well-being and the resilience of the future health workforce.7 Thus, focusing on ULS allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the interplay between educational experiences and population-level health outcomes. In addition, there is a dearth of epidemiological data regarding psychological morbidities among undergraduate medical students in Arab nations.8 Therefore, this study will investigate the correlation of psychological distress with ULS in Syrian medical students. We hypothesise that higher levels of psychological distress are associated with lower ULS.

This study aims to address this gap in scientific literature by learning the correlational relationship between ULS and the mental health of medical students in Syria. This study’s results will help with highlighting the quality of the Syrian universities, developing more effective psychological support strategies for medical students and exploring the impact of ULS on mental health and well-being in conflict-affected settings.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional study aimed to assess ULS and its association with levels of stress, anxiety and depression among Syrian medical students.

Participants

We sampled on-campus students enrolled in medicine, dentistry and pharmacy programmes across both public and private universities in Syria. Data collection was conducted by a structured, self-administered and anonymous online survey built by Google Forms. Participants were recruited through universities’ official social-media groups and student organisations, using a convenience sampling strategy consistent with non-probability sampling. Participants accessed the survey using their email accounts. The sample collection period ranged from March to May 2025. Inclusion criteria included currently enrolled on-campus students in one of the Syrian universities in any of the medical specialties (medicine, dentistry and pharmacy). Exclusion criteria included those who declined to participate and first-year medical preparation students. All participants were informed of the study’s objectives and assured that their participation was voluntary and anonymous.

For the expected prevalence of 60%,7 8 the required sample size was 642 for the margin of error or absolute precision of ±4% in estimating the prevalence with 95% confidence. This sample size was calculated using the Scalex SP calculator.9

Materials and procedure

The survey comprised three main sections. The first section included demographic information such as participant’s age, sex, academic year, cumulative grade point average (GPA), social status, place of residence before university, type of accommodation during study, university name, extracurricular activities, working status, working hours, smoking status, financial status, field of study and psychological history. The second section assessed ULS using the Campus Satisfaction Scale (C-Sat). The C-Sat was translated into Arabic using a forward–backward translation method by a bilingual linguistic expert to ensure conceptual and cultural equivalence. The final section evaluated mental health using the Arabic version of the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scales (DASS-21).

The C-Sat scale

The C-Sat scale, previously referred to as CSS, is a newly developed tool which is a more succinct, multidimensional and complete indicator of ULS with strong psychometric properties.10 This new scale uses fewer items than the previous scales to evaluate several aspects of ULS.10 It evaluates five distinct ULS areas: usefulness for future professions, relationships with coworkers, appropriateness of decisions, effectiveness of study habits and quality of university services.10 The C-Sat scale encompasses four dimensions and 20 items, assessing satisfaction with career relevance, university services, study methods and peer relationships. This scale evaluates, using 20 items rated on a Likert scale (1=not at all satisfied and 5=completely satisfied). In the current study, the C-Sat scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.911.

The DASS-21 scale

The DASS is a widely used scale in research studies for the assessment of depression, anxiety and stress symptoms.11 The DASS-21 is a short version of the basic DASS questionnaire and is available with seven items per subscale, resulting in 21 items.11 The dimensions of stress, anxiety and depression can all be measured with validity using the DASS-21 subscales.11 The Arabic version of DASS-21 was modified and translated in accordance with generally recognised standards, consisting of four rigorous phases.12 The Arabic DASS-21 provides preliminary evidence of its psychometric qualities and supports the identification of stress, anxiety and depression.12 The raw scores from the DASS-21 can be interpreted across five severity levels: normal, mild, moderate, severe and extremely severe. Interpretations were following the DASS-21 manual.13

Statistical analysis

Data were collected using Google Forms and exported to Excel for processing and SPSS (V.25) for analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables, including means and SD. Independent-samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to evaluate associations between demographic variables and C-Sat (ULS) and DASS-21 subscale scores, with Tukey’s HSD post hoc tests applied to significant ANOVA results. Given the extensive univariable testing, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) procedure (q=0.05) separately for each outcome across the 15 demographic comparisons, and BH-FDR adjusted p-values are reported in online supplemental table S1. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the strength and direction of associations between C-Sat (ULS) and DASS-21 subscales (depression, anxiety and stress). Finally, multiple linear regression was conducted to examine associations between independent variables (demographic characteristics and DASS-21 components) and ULS scores (dependent variable). Because the oldest age categories were sparse, age was collapsed for multivariable modelling (≥26 years). Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factors, and regression assumptions were evaluated using a histogram and normal P–P plot of standardised residuals and a scatterplot of standardised residuals versus standardised predicted values. Independence of errors was assessed using the Durbin–Watson statistic. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at p<0.05, with FDR adjustment applied as described for univariable analyses.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Of the approximately 3000 students invited to participate, 700 medical students completed the survey from diverse academic levels and universities across Syria, yielding a response rate of 23.3%. The majority are aged between 20 and 25 years (81.0%). Females represented 67.6% of the sample, while males accounted for 32.4%. Most students were enrolled in medicine (72.7%). The sample consisted predominantly of single students (96.3%), with urban residents making up 62.0% of participants. Financial status was reported as bad by 27.7% of respondents. Lastly, 31.7% of participants reported a prior diagnosis of a psychological disorder (Table 1).

ULS scores and demographic associations

The C-Sat scores indicated a moderate overall level of ULS among students, with a mean score of 2.95 (SD ±0.65) out of 5. The highest levels of ULS were observed in areas related to personal interest in the field of study, perceived degree suitability and expectations of positive future outcomes, such as feeling that their studies will help them find employment and prepare for their future careers, all scoring around or above (mean: 3.4, SD: ±1.00–1.12). Moderate ULS was noted in domains such as peer relationships and academic motivation (with scores typically ranging between mean: 2.7, SD: ±1.08 and mean: 2.9, SD: ±1.26). Conversely, the lowest levels of ULS were related to institutional factors, including classroom comfort, staff helpfulness and the condition of university facilities, all of which scored close to or below (mean: 2.3, SD: ±1.00–1.05) (online supplemental table S1).

The results revealed several statistically significant correlations between students’ ULS and their demographic and academic characteristics. Male students demonstrated higher ULS compared with female students (p=0.004; mean=3.06 vs 2.90; Δ=0.16). Students who participated in extracurricular activities reported a higher ULS than those who did not (p=0.038; mean=3.01 vs 2.91; Δ=0.10). In addition, levels of ULS differed significantly according to age (p=0.004), with students under the age of 20 reporting a higher ULS than those aged 20–25 years (p=0.006; mean=3.16 vs 2.93; Δ=0.23) and 26–30 years (p=0.015; mean=2.77; Δ=0.39). A significant difference was also observed across academic years (p<0.001), as second-year students demonstrated greater ULS (mean=3.11) compared with those in the fifth year (p=0.046; mean=2.96; Δ=0.15) and sixth years (p<0.001; mean=2.74; Δ=0.37). The academic branch was also significantly associated with ULS (p=0.016), with dentistry students reporting higher ULS (mean=3.10) than those in medicine (p=0.042; mean=2.94; Δ=0.16) and pharmacy (p=0.023; mean=2.84; Δ=0.26). University affiliation showed a significant association as well (p<0.001), with students from Al-Hawash Private University (mean=3.21) and other private universities (mean=3.30) demonstrating a greater ULS compared with students from Damascus (mean=2.91) and Aleppo Universities (mean=2.85) (p<0.05). GPA was also significantly associated with ULS (p=0.002), with students scoring between 90% and 99% reporting a higher ULS (mean=3.16) compared with those with scores between 60% and 69% (p=0.004; mean=2.74; Δ=0.42) and 80%–89% (p=0.013; mean=3.00; Δ=0.16). Better financial status (p=0.001; mean=3.16 for ‘excellent’ vs 2.83 for ‘bad’; Δ=0.33) and being married (p=0.010; mean=2.46 vs 2.97 for singles; Δ=−0.51) were also linked to greater ULS.

Correlations between psychological distress and demographics

Several demographic variables showed statistically significant associations with components of psychological distress. Students with a history of psychological diagnosis reported markedly higher levels of depression (p<0.001; mean=21.95 vs 15.40; Δ=6.55), anxiety (p<0.001; mean=15.81 vs 9.52; Δ=6.29) and stress (p<0.001; mean=23.62 vs 16.65; Δ=6.97) compared with those with no such history. Female students exhibited higher anxiety (p<0.001; mean=12.69 vs 9.07; Δ=3.62) and stress (p=0.012; mean=19.58 vs 17.37; Δ=2.21) than male students. Regarding place of residence before university, students from the city reported higher depression (p=0.048; mean=18.12 vs 16.42; Δ=1.70) and stress (p=0.026; mean=19.58 vs 17.70; Δ=1.88) compared with those from the countryside. Financial status was also significantly related to mental health: students with bad financial status had higher levels of depression (p<0.001; mean=21.21 vs 16.59 for good status; Δ=4.62), anxiety (p<0.001; mean=13.65 vs 10.90; Δ=2.75) and stress (p<0.001; mean=21.33 vs 18.17; Δ=3.16). Marital status was significantly associated with both anxiety (p=0.003) and stress (p=0.002), with engaged students reporting notably higher anxiety (mean=20.62 vs 11.35 for single; Δ=9.27) and stress (mean=29.08 vs 18.64; Δ=10.44). Academic year was significantly related to depression (p=0.001) and stress (p=0.016); fifth-year students reported the lowest depression (mean=15.00), while second-year students had higher levels (mean=19.33; Δ=4.33). Stress was also elevated among sixth-year students (mean=21.13) compared with fifth-year students (mean=16.58; Δ=4.55). The academic branch was associated with anxiety levels (p=0.016), with dentistry students reporting higher anxiety (mean=13.33) compared with medical students (mean=10.87; Δ=2.46). Finally, smoking status was linked to higher anxiety (p=0.009; mean=13.71 vs 11.10; Δ=2.61) and stress (p=0.047; mean=20.73 vs 18.51; Δ=2.22) (online supplemental table S2).

Given the large number of univariable comparisons, we applied BH-FDR correction (q=0.05) separately for each outcome across the 15 demographic comparisons (online supplemental table S3). After adjustment, C-Sat (ULS) remained significantly associated with age, gender, academic branch, university, academic year, GPA, social status and financial status (all BH-FDR p≤0.05), whereas the unadjusted associations with extracurricular activities (raw p=0.038) and accommodation type (raw p=0.050) did not remain significant. For DASS-21 outcomes, depression remained significantly associated only with academic year, financial status and psychological history; anxiety remained significantly associated with gender, academic branch, social status, financial status, psychological history and smoking status; and stress remained significantly associated with gender, academic year, social status, financial status and psychological history (all BH-FDR p≤0.05) (online supplemental table S3).

Correlations between ULS and psychological distress

The Pearson correlation analysis revealed statistically significant negative relationships between medical students’ ULS and levels of depression, anxiety and stress. Specifically, students’ ULS exhibited a moderate negative correlation with depression (r=−0.333, p<0.001), indicating that higher depressive symptoms were associated with lower ULS levels. However, weaker negative correlations were observed between ULS and anxiety (r=−0.171, p<0.001) as well as stress (r=−0.240, p<0.001), suggesting that increased anxiety and stress levels similarly slightly corresponded to reduced medical student ULS. All correlations were statistically significant at the 0.01 level (two-tailed), highlighting consistent inverse relationships between students’ ULS and psychological distress factors (table 3).

Factors associated with ULS

In multivariable linear regression (n=700), the model was statistically significant (F(26,673)=8.986, p<0.001) and explained 25.8% of the variance in ULS (R²=0.258; adjusted R²=0.229). Age was entered as three categories (under 20 years (reference), 20–25 years and ≥26 years). Higher ULS was independently associated with higher GPA (70%–79%: B=0.291, 95% CI 0.132 to 0.450; 80%–89%: B=0.436, 95% CI 0.272 to 0.601; 90%–99%: B=0.595, 95% CI 0.361 to 0.829; all p<0.001) and private university affiliation (Al-Hawash: B=0.447, 95% CI 0.300 to 0.595; other private universities: B=0.531, 95% CI 0.287 to 0.775; both p<0.001). Being in the sixth academic year was associated with lower ULS (B=−0.285, 95% CI −0.465 to −0.105; p=0.002). Higher depression scores were independently associated with lower ULS (B=−0.023, 95% CI −0.030 to −0.016; p<0.001), whereas anxiety and stress were not significant in the fully adjusted model (p=0.137 and p=0.398, respectively) (table 4).

Discussion

Our sample included students from multiple universities, both public and private, which provided broader coverage of Syrian medical students. However, recruitment through social media may have introduced selection bias, excluding students with limited internet access or lower engagement. The sample demographics showed strong similarity with previous studies on Syrian university students.8 14 Also, the sample age ranged between 20 and 25, similar to previous studies on similar populations.15 16 The females (67.6%) were predominant in our sample, similar to a Sudanese study (64.38%) during conflict15 and in contrast to a study in Saudi Arabia (24%).16 Higher prevalence of smoking among Syrian medical students (16.0%) compared with Saudi Arabian medical students (11.6%).16

Medical students face high mental health risks because of their demanding education requirements.1 2 On a global scale, prevalence rates of depression and anxiety among medical students are estimated to be 27% and 34%, respectively.17 According to our study results, depression, anxiety and stress reached extreme levels in 35.0%, 29.4% and 29.8% of medical students, respectively. As expected, Syrian medical students reported significantly higher levels of psychological distress compared with a comparable cross-sectional study of Saudi medical students using the DASS-21 scale, which found extreme levels of stress, anxiety and depression at 12.7%, 25.3% and 14.7%, respectively.16 Whereas, in Sudanese medical students during conflict, 58.3% of participants experienced depression and 51.5% experienced anxiety.15 In Egypt, medical students using DASS-21 reported extreme levels of depression, anxiety and stress of 22.7%, 41% and 29%, respectively.18 Our findings showed that females, smoking, academic year, economic situation and previous psychological illness are correlated with higher psychological distress and are consistent with previous studies.8 15 16 18

To our knowledge, few studies have examined ULS and mental-health outcomes among Syrian medical students, and this study contributes to that emerging evidence base. One prior Syrian study examined the effect of psychological distress on GPA scores, without evaluating ULS.8 The results of our study demonstrated that various student characteristics were correlated with their levels of ULS. In addition, our results showed that Syrian public university students showed significantly lower ULS compared with private university students. More specifically, the lowest levels of ULS were related to institutional factors, including classroom comfort, staff helpfulness and the condition of university facilities, all of which scored close to or below (mean: 2.3). This reflects the poor services, underfunding in infrastructure and unwelcoming environment of Syrian public universities, which require intervention by the government.19 This underscores the economic strain on government funding for public universities, which translates into a poorer educational environment. On top of that, the Syrian conflict has contributed to the destruction of universities’ infrastructure and displaced more than 70% of the medical community.20 Moreover, the academic branch was also significantly associated with ULS, with dentistry students reporting higher ULS than those in medicine. This demonstrates the disparity in public resources at public universities, specifically during conflict.

Extracurricular activities play a positive part in the ULS of Syrian students. These findings are consistent with a previous study, which showed that academic engagement—through both curricular and extracurricular activities—allows students to take part in their education and apply what they learn to real-world situations.21 Moreover, students who join extracurricular activities build stronger bonds with their university, which helps them feel part of a community and fight feelings of isolation.2 21 Therefore, it is a significant factor that supports students’ healthy development.

Our results showed that GPA is strongly correlated with high ULS, consistent with a previous Syrian study.8 Surprisingly, married students demonstrated lower ULS than single students. This correlation rationale is unclear, which requires further investigation. In the Syrian context, families and children are not supported by the government, which creates an extra burden on the parents. This highlights the need to address the social determinants of health of Syrian university students in post-conflict settings. This theory is backed by the fact that students with excellent and good financial status reported significantly higher ULS compared with low financial status students, who reported low ULS. As expected, our results revealed negatively correlated relationships between students’ ULS and levels of depression, anxiety and stress. These findings are consistent with previous studies’ results, where students with psychological distress showed lower ULS and lower GPA.15 16 18 Therefore, urgent mental health interventions for Syrian medical students are important due to the context of long conflict in Syria and the high prevalence of psychological illnesses among Syrian medical students compared with peers in other conflict-affected countries.

Public health implications

The findings suggest universities should implement tailored mental health programmes for Syrian medical students, promote peer support and enhance faculty-student communication. Encourage extracurricular engagement and mentorship, which builds a positive community on campus.21 Improved classroom comfort and staff support boost ULS, reduce psychological distress and foster positive academic outcomes. Train and empower educational faculty members to practise open communication about mental health issues with their students.4 As ULS and student mental health are critical determinants of workforce sustainability, particularly in conflict-affected contexts where higher education institutions serve as pipelines for the future health and social service workforce.

The broader impact of the Syrian conflict likely operates through multiple pathways that influence both ULS and psychological distress. Conflict-related underfunding of higher education may directly result in deteriorating facilities, overcrowded classrooms and limited extracurricular opportunities, all of which diminish student ULS.21 At the same time, trauma exposure, widespread family displacement and economic hardship may further exacerbate psychological distress, compounding the negative effects on perceived ULS.15 Together, these pathways suggest that institutional challenges cannot be separated from the wider conflict environment, and future research should aim to test these mechanisms to better inform health system responses and education policy in post-conflict recovery. Addressing these challenges requires coordinated public-health interventions, including investment in student well-being programmes, integration of mental-health services within universities and policies that strengthen population resilience.

Limitations of the study

The research generates meaningful findings, although it faces certain constraints. A limitation of this study is that the C-Sat scale has not undergone prior validation in the Syrian context. Therefore, this may affect its reliability and validity in this population. In addition, it is important to acknowledge that unmeasured confounders may have influenced the observed associations. Factors such as trauma exposure, family displacement and concurrent life stressors are highly relevant in conflict-affected settings and could contribute both to psychological distress and to ULS. Without accounting for these variables, the relationships reported here should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies should incorporate measures of trauma history and socio-economic disruption to better disentangle the independent effects of academic and institutional factors from broader contextual stressors.

Additionally, causal mediation analysis was not performed, because mediation requires a defensible temporal ordering between exposures, mediators and outcomes, which cannot be established when all variables are measured at a single time point in a cross-sectional survey. Therefore, all reported relationships should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Further research using longitudinal methods should explore how these variables develop through time and may better evaluate potential mediating pathways under appropriate assumptions. The assessment of psychological distress through self-reported measures faces the risk of distortion because participants might hide or exaggerate their distress because of social desirability or stigma concerns. The survey method conducted online restricts participation from students who lack internet access or those who do not use social media platforms. In addition, a low response rate may limit the representativeness of the sample. Overall, this study focuses on a particular geographical area, which reduces its capacity to produce generalisable findings for other populations and regions.

Conclusion

This study underscores the heightened psychological vulnerability of Syrian medical students, who reported alarmingly high levels of depression, anxiety and stress. Importantly, their ULS was found to be moderately low and inversely associated with psychological distress. While some demographic factors such as gender, academic year and marital status impacted both mental health and ULS levels, the most consistent and meaningful factors were GPA, financial status, university affiliation and history of mental disorders.

These findings are particularly significant in the Syrian context, where the decade-long conflict has undermined university infrastructure, reduced access to mental health services and imposed economic hardship on students. Students in public universities reported significantly lower ULS compared with their peers in private universities, indicating a disparity in resources and support systems. The positive associations of GPA and extracurricular engagement with ULS indicate that academic achievement and campus involvement were linked to higher ULS, and these factors were correlated with lower levels of psychological distress.

Notably, female students and students with a history of psychological disorders reported both lower ULS and greater psychological distress, highlighting the need for early identification and support. The moderate inverse correlation between ULS and depression indicates that higher levels of ULS were associated with lower levels of depressive symptoms, highlighting an important correlational relationship between ULS and mental health.

In light of these results, universities in Syria must consider comprehensive interventions that extend beyond the academic curriculum. Efforts should include improving physical infrastructure (eg, classroom comfort), enhancing staff training and communication and promoting extracurricular programmes that foster peer connection and resilience. Furthermore, policies must support student well-being through accessible mental health services and financial aid, particularly for vulnerable groups.

This study adds to the growing body of evidence linking psychological outcomes with ULS and provides actionable insights for improving student welfare in conflict-affected settings. However, the findings represent correlational associations and cannot establish causality given the cross-sectional study design. This highlights the need for bigger longitudinal studies to further evaluate the causality of the relationship.
