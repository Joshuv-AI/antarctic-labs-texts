U-shaped association between estimated glomerular filtration rate and mortality in HSV-positive adults: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jun Wei, Yang Liu, Xinying Hu, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is a key indicator of kidney function, its association with mortality in herpes simplex virus (HSV)-seropositive individuals remains unclear. We analyzed 17,848 HSV-1/HSV-2 seropositive participants from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (1999–2016). The eGFR was calculated using standard equations, and mortality data were linked to the National Death Index through December 31, 2019. Kaplan–Meier survival curves, Cox regression models, generalized additive models, and stratified analyses were used to assess the association between eGFR and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. The mean age of participants was 33.85 years (standard deviation: 9.4), and the average eGFR was 110.4 mL/min/1.73 m2 (standard deviation: 18.7). During a median follow-up of 141 months, 598 participants (3.35%) died from all causes, and 139 (0.78%) died from cardiovascular causes. A U-shaped association was observed between eGFR and all-cause mortality. Below an inflection point of 85.22 mL/min/1.73 m2, each 10-unit increase in eGFR was associated with reduced risk of all-cause death (hazard ratio = 0.70; 95% confidence interval: 0.64–0.75; P < .0001). Above this threshold, higher eGFR was associated with increased mortality (hazard ratio = 1.12; 95% confidence interval: 1.05–1.20; P = .001). Similar trends were found for cardiovascular mortality, although the association with elevated eGFR was not statistically significant. These findings were consistent across stratified and sensitivity analyses. In HSV-infected individuals, eGFR showed a nonlinear relationship with mortality. Moderate eGFR levels were associated with the lowest mortality risk.

1. Introduction

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) serves as an essential instrument for assessing kidney function. Approximately 14% of adults in the United States are impacted by chronic kidney disease (CKD), which is defined by either a low eGFR or the presence of albuminuria.[1] According to the 2017 Global Burden of Disease Study, there were 697.5 million instances of all stages of CKD, indicating a global prevalence rate of 9.1%. Furthermore, that year, an estimated 1.2 million individuals (with a 95% uncertainty interval of 1.2–1.3) died as a result of CKD.[2] The eGFR values represent the filtration rate of blood by the kidneys and are measured in mL/min/1.73 m2. To estimate eGFR, equations incorporating variables such as serum creatinine, cystatin C, age, and sex are frequently utilized. Research has shown a connection between variability in eGFR and long-term mortality,[3] with abnormal eGFR levels linked to a range of conditions including sickle cell disease,[4] Parkinson disease,[5] cardiovascular disorders,[6] the severity of CKD,[7] sleep duration,[8] and liver cirrhosis.[9]

Herpes simplex viruses (HSV), encompassing type 1 (HSV-1) and type 2 (HSV-2), belong to the Herpesviridae family and possess a linear double-stranded DNA genome encased within an icosahedral capsid.[10] HSV-1 is primarily transmitted via oral contact, while HSV-2 is mainly spread through sexual transmission; however, both types are capable of infecting either anatomical site.[11] HSV infections are widespread globally. As of 2016, approximately 491.5 million individuals were estimated to be infected with HSV-2, representing around 13% of the global population.[12] HSV-1 is even more prevalent and is commonly acquired during early childhood. After initial infection, HSV establishes lifelong latency within the host and may periodically reactivate, resulting in clinical manifestations such as oral or genital lesions, herpetic keratitis, or, in rare instances, serious complications like encephalitis.[13,14]

Based on this background, we conducted an analysis using data from the nationally representative NHANES 1999–2016 cohort to investigate the association between eGFR and long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in HSV-seropositive adults. We hypothesized that this relationship would be nonlinear, with both low and high eGFR levels associated with increased mortality risk in this population.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and population

This study was a mortality-linked analysis based on data from HSV-seropositive adults who participated in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). NHANES is a nationally representative, cross-sectional survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the civilian, noninstitutionalized US population. The survey is conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) using a multistage, stratified, probability-based sampling method. At the time of enrollment, participants underwent standardized interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory testing. Although NHANES is cross-sectional in design, its linkage to the National Death Index (NDI) allows for passive follow-up of mortality outcomes. This enables evaluation of associations between baseline health measures and subsequent death. All survey protocols were approved by the NCHS Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.[15] For this analysis, we used publicly available, de-identified NHANES datasets and linked mortality files, which are exempt from additional institutional review. Full documentation of NHANES procedures is accessible at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

A total of 1,01,316 participants from the NHANES database (1999–2016) were initially screened. Individuals were excluded if they were younger than 18 years (n = 42,112) or lacked serological data for both HSV-1 and HSV-2 (n = 41,319), yielding 17,885 adults (≥18 years) with valid HSV-1 or HSV-2 test results. Among these, participants were further excluded if they had missing eGFR data (n = 13) or lacked mortality follow-up information (n = 24). After applying all exclusion criteria, a total of 17,848 HSV-seropositive adults were included in the final analytic cohort. This study employed a cohort design based on NHANES cross-sectional data with linked prospective mortality follow-up, allowing investigation of the association between eGFR and long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 1).

2.2. Calculation and grouping of eGFR

The calculation of the eGFR was conducted utilizing the CKD-EpI 2021 formula, which considers factors such as age, sex, and serum creatinine levels, as outlined in existing studies. The measurement of serum creatinine was executed through the Jaffe rate method on the DxC800 modular chemistry analyzer. During this process, a specific volume of the sample was placed into a reaction vessel containing an alkaline picrate solution. Absorbance readings were captured at 520 nm approximately 19 to 25 seconds post sample injection. The creatinine present in the sample interacted with the reagent, resulting in the formation of a red-colored complex. The variation in absorbance was directly related to the concentration of creatinine within the sample.[16] Based on the computed eGFR values, the participants were classified into 3 distinct groups: ≤80 mL/min/1.73 m2, >80 and ≤100 mL/min/1.73 m2, and >100 mL/min/1.73 m2. The ≤80 mL/min/1.73 m2 group was chosen as the reference group because this threshold is widely recognized as indicative of impaired kidney function and is commonly used in clinical practice to define CKD.[17,18]

2.3. Measurement of herpes simplex virus

Blood specimens were obtained through venipuncture at NHANES mobile examination centers and processed in accordance with standardized laboratory procedures. Serological testing for HSV-1 and HSV-2 was conducted at Emory University using a type-specific enzyme-linked immunoassay that utilizes purified glycoproteins gG-1 and gG-2. These antigens were fixed onto nitrocellulose membranes, allowing for the specific identification of HSV-1 and HSV-2 antibodies despite the known cross-reactivity among herpesviruses. Detection of bound serum IgG antibodies was performed using a peroxidase-labeled goat antihuman IgG reagent along with a colorimetric substrate. A blue-colored dot signified a positive result, with reactivity to gG-1 indicating HSV-1 seropositivity and reactivity to gG-2 indicating HSV-2 seropositivity. All participants had provided written informed consent for the use of their biological samples.[19]

2.4. Definitions of the outcome variables

The variables of interest regarding outcomes consisted of overall mortality and mortality caused by cardiovascular disease (CVD). Data pertaining to mortality for the population followed in the study were obtained from the NHANES Public-use link mortality files, with the last updates available until December 31, 2019. These records were linked to the NCHS and the NDI through the use of probabilistic algorithms. Mortality outcomes were classified following the standardized International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) framework. The period of observation was established as the time elapsed from the initial assessment (first interview) to the later event of either mortality or the conclusion of the study.[20,21] Overall mortality includes fatalities from all causes, such as heart disease, various malignant tumors, accidental injuries, cerebrovascular ailments, diabetes, and others. In contrast, cardiovascular mortality pertains solely to deaths that are a result of heart disease and cerebrovascular conditions.[22,23]

2.5. Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on their potential to confound the relationship between kidney function and mortality and were informed by prior literature. Demographic variables included age (continuous), sex (male or female), and race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, other race). Socioeconomic status was captured by marital status (married/living with partner, widowed/divorced/separated, never married), education level (less than high school, high school graduate, above high school), and poverty income ratio (categorized as poor, nearly poor, middle income, and high income). Lifestyle factors comprised smoking status (never, former, current), alcohol consumption (never, former, mild, moderate, or heavy), and total physical activity. In NHANES, physical activity was assessed using self-reported questionnaires. Specifically, total physical activity was quantified in metabolic equivalents (METs) per week. Physical activity was dichotomized based on the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations as follows: those with <600 MET-min/wk were classified as physically inactive, and those with ≥600 MET-min/wk were classified as meeting physical activity guidelines.[24] Clinical conditions included self-reported or measured diagnosis of hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Impaired fasting glucose (IFG) and impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) were also considered as separate categories based on fasting plasma glucose and 2-hour OGTT results, respectively. Missing values for covariates were treated as separate indicator categories where appropriate to retain the full analytic sample. All covariates were included in multivariable models to adjust for potential confounding in the relationship between eGFR and mortality. All covariates were selected based on prior literature and clinical relevance to both kidney function and mortality risk.

2.6. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses incorporated the complex, multistage sampling design of NHANES, including sampling weights, stratification, and clustering, to produce estimates representative of the US adult population. Continuous variables were summarized as weighted means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, while categorical variables were expressed as weighted percentages. Group comparisons across estimated eGFR categories (≤80, >80–100, and >100 mL/min/1.73 m2) were conducted using the Rao–Scott chi-square test for categorical variables and weighted linear regression for continuous variables.

To evaluate the association between eGFR and mortality, Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Two models were fitted: model 1 was adjusted for demographic and socioeconomic covariates (age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, and poverty income ratio), while model 2 further adjusted for behavioral and clinical risk factors (smoking status, alcohol use, physical activity, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus).

To explore potential nonlinear relationships between eGFR and mortality risk, smooth curve fitting based on generalized additive models was performed within the Cox regression framework. When nonlinearity was detected, a 2-piecewise linear regression model was applied to estimate threshold effects, with the inflection point selected using a likelihood-based approach to maximize model fit. The log-likelihood ratio test was used to assess whether the segmented model significantly improved fit compared to the linear model.

Additionally, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare survival across the 3 eGFR groups, with differences tested using log-rank statistics. Stratified analyses were conducted across predefined subgroups (age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, behavioral characteristics, and comorbidities), and interaction terms were tested in the Cox models.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and EmpowerStats (version 4.2; X&Y Solutions Inc., Boston). A 2-tailed P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Marital status and income distribution also differed: the lowest eGFR group had a higher proportion of married or cohabiting participants, while the highest tertile had more individuals who were never married and had lower poverty-income ratios. Participants in the lowest eGFR tertile also had a higher proportion of individuals with education above high school and a lower proportion of those with less than high school education compared to the other groups.

Behavioral risk factors, including current smoking and heavy alcohol use, were more frequent among those with lower eGFR. Although physical inactivity (<600 MET-min/wk) was slightly more common in the low and high tertiles than in the middle tertile (28.3% and 28.1% vs 26.2%), the differences were relatively modest. Clinically, participants in the lowest eGFR tertile had a higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, IFG, and IGT. The proportion of missing data was highest for physical activity (23.7%), followed by alcohol use (16.6%) and smoking status (8.0%). All between-group differences were statistically significant (P < .001).

3.2. Association of eGFR with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality

The association between eGFR categories and mortality outcomes was evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, with the ≤80 mL/min/1.73 m2 group serving as the reference. In the age- and sex-adjusted model (model I), participants with eGFR 80 to 100 mL/min/1.73 m2 had significantly lower all-cause mortality risk (HR = 0.63; 95% CI: 0.47–0.85; P = .0024), which remained consistent after full adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates in model II (HR = 0.62; 95% CI: 0.46–0.84; P = .0020). A similar inverse association was observed for those with eGFR > 100 mL/min/1.73 m2 (model I: HR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.54–0.94; P = .0161; model II: HR = 0.67; 95% CI: 0.51–0.89; P = .0051; Table 2).

3.3. Nonlinear association between eGFR and mortality outcomes

Figure 2 illustrates the nonlinear associations between eGFR and mortality risk in HSV-seropositive individuals, based on smooth curve fitting from Cox proportional hazards models. Figure 2A shows a U-shaped association between eGFR and all-cause mortality, with the lowest risk observed near an eGFR of 85 mL/min/1.73 m2. Mortality risk decreased with increasing eGFR up to this point and then gradually increased at higher levels. Figure 2B displays a similar curvilinear trend for cardiovascular mortality, with a flattening of the curve in the higher eGFR range and wider confidence intervals suggesting greater uncertainty.

To explore potential threshold effects, Cox regression models were constructed (Table 3). For all-cause mortality, the inflection point was identified at 85.22 mL/min/1.73 m2. Below this threshold, each 10-unit increase in eGFR was significantly associated with lower mortality risk (HR = 0.70; 95% CI: 0.64–0.75; P < .0001). In contrast, eGFR levels above 85.22 were associated with increased all-cause mortality (HR = 1.12; 95% CI: 1.05–1.20; P = .0011). The 2-piecewise model showed a superior fit compared to a standard linear model (log-likelihood ratio test, P < .001).

3.5. Stratified analyses

Stratified analyses were conducted to assess whether the association between eGFR and mortality varied across subgroups (Table 4). A 10 mL/min/1.73 m2 increase in eGFR was significantly associated with reduced all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in several strata. The association was stronger among females (HR = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.76–0.85; P < .0001) compared to males (HR = 0.90; 95% CI: 0.85–0.95; P < .0005). Significant associations were observed in the 40 to 49-year age group, but not among younger participants.

By ethnicity, associations remained significant in non-Hispanic Whites, non-Hispanic Blacks, and Mexican Americans. The protective effect was observed in participants with lower income, especially those classified as poor (HR = 0.77; 95% CI: 0.73–0.82; P < .0001), but not in those with high income. Similarly, the association was more pronounced among those with high school education or higher.

Among behavioral factors, the inverse association persisted in never smokers and current smokers, individuals with mild or heavy alcohol use, and those reporting ≥600 MET/wk of physical activity. Clinically, the association was observed both in participants with and without hypertension or diabetes mellitus, and in those with IFG, but not in those with IGT.

4. Discussion

In this nationally representative cohort of HSV-seropositive adults, we observed a nonlinear, U-shaped association between eGFR and both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. The lowest risk of mortality was found at moderate eGFR levels, with elevated risks associated with both lower and higher eGFR values. These findings remained robust after adjusting for a wide range of demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and clinical covariates, and were consistent across multiple stratified analyses. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate such nonlinear eGFR–mortality associations in an HSV-infected population.

The inverse association between reduced eGFR and mortality has been well-established in previous studies, particularly among individuals with CKD.[25] Our results align with this evidence, showing that eGFR values below approximately 85 mL/min/1.73 m2 were associated with significantly increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. This is biologically plausible, as decreased kidney function can contribute to cardiovascular events and systemic complications through mechanisms such as fluid overload, inflammation, and dysregulation of mineral metabolism.[2]

Interestingly, we also found that higher eGFR values (>85.22 mL/min/1.73 m2) were associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality, although the association with cardiovascular mortality in this range did not reach statistical significance. This finding is consistent with several prior population-based studies that have reported a U-shaped relationship between eGFR and mortality, particularly in specific subpopulations such as the elderly or those with comorbidities.[26] Elevated eGFR values may reflect glomerular hyperfiltration, which has been proposed as a potential early marker of vascular or metabolic stress, especially in younger or otherwise healthy individuals.[27,28] Alternatively, high eGFR estimates based on serum creatinine may be influenced by low muscle mass or frailty, which themselves are linked to poor outcomes.[29,30] However, the underlying mechanisms remain uncertain and warrant further investigation in longitudinal studies.

Subgroup analyses revealed that the association between eGFR and mortality varied across sociodemographic and clinical strata. The inverse relationship was more pronounced among females, individuals with lower socioeconomic status, and participants with hypertension or diabetes mellitus.[31,32] These differences may reflect variations in baseline risk profiles, access to care, or underlying pathophysiological processes. Notably, the protective association of moderate eGFR was observed across most ethnic subgroups, supporting the generalizability of the findings.[33] However, in participants with IGT, the association was not statistically significant, which may indicate heterogeneous effects in metabolic subgroups or insufficient statistical power.

In our primary analysis, we reported HRs per 10 mL/min/1.73 m2 increase in eGFR to enhance interpretability. Given the relatively high mean eGFR in our study population (110.47 mL/min/1.73 m2), the effect size associated with a 1-unit change was numerically small and less intuitive, especially when rounded to 2 decimal places. However, in response to the editor’s suggestion, we performed a supplementary analysis using 1 mL/min/1.73 m2 increments, and reported the corresponding HRs with 3-decimal precision. These results, presented in Supplementary Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R601, demonstrated consistent effect directions and further confirmed the robustness of our findings.

Our study has several strengths. First, it uses data from NHANES, a well-characterized, nationally representative sample with standardized measurements and comprehensive follow-up through linkage to the NDI. Second, the large sample size and long follow-up period enhance the statistical power and allow for robust subgroup analyses. Third, the use of generalized additive models and threshold analysis provided nuanced insights into the nonlinear relationship between eGFR and mortality.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. Due to the cross-sectional nature of NHANES, our exposure variable was measured at a single time point, and we could not account for changes in kidney function over time. Furthermore, although we adjusted for a wide range of potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, particularly regarding unmeasured variables such as medication use, inflammatory markers, or HIV coinfection status. Additionally, we relied on serum creatinine–based eGFR estimates, which may be affected by muscle mass and may not fully capture kidney function in certain subgroups. Finally, while our findings demonstrate associations, causality cannot be inferred from observational data.

5. Conclusion

This study identified a U-shaped association between eGFR and long-term mortality in HSV-seropositive adults. Moderate eGFR levels were associated with the lowest mortality risk, whereas both reduced and elevated eGFR values were linked to increased risk.
