Imaging and clinicopathological features of a high-SUV hibernoma mimicking liposarcoma on PET/CT, MRI, and CT: A case report
Authors: Francesco Saverio Papadia, Bruno Spina, Silvia Bozzano, Matteo Mascherini, Federico Zaottini, Danila Comandini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hibernomas are rare benign tumors of brown adipose tissue that can demonstrate remarkably high fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) avidity on positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT), often mimicking malignant sarcomas and leading to unnecessary aggressive management. A 65-year-old woman with peripheral arterial disease underwent CT angiography that incidentally revealed a large (9 × 8.5 cm) mixed-density lipomatous mass in the proximal left thigh. MRI confirmed a well-encapsulated lesion with signal intensity consistent with fat on T1-weighted imaging, strikingly prominent internal vascular channels, and a feeding vascular pedicle. PET/CT demonstrated intense hypermetabolism with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 34.8—among the highest reported for hibernoma—and a central photopenic area corresponding to vascular spaces. Core biopsy suggested hibernoma (positive for S100 and CD10, negative for MDM2), creating significant radiological-pathological discordance given the aggressive imaging appearance. Multidisciplinary tumor board review recommended marginal excision, which confirmed a completely resected hibernoma; the patient remained recurrence-free at 12-month follow-up. Hibernoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of any FDG-avid lipomatous soft tissue mass, particularly when imaging shows prominent internal vascularity and absence of thickened fibrous septa. An SUVmax exceeding 30 does not exclude benignity, and recognizing these characteristic findings can improve diagnostic accuracy and help avoid unnecessary aggressive treatment.

Introduction

Hibernomas are uncommon, benign neoplasms originating from brown adipose tissue, accounting for approximately 1.6% of all benign lipomatous tumors [1,2]. They typically present as slow-growing, painless masses in adults, with the thigh being the most common location [3]. The principal diagnostic challenge lies in their imaging characteristics, which can overlap with those of malignant lipomatous tumors, particularly atypical lipomatous tumor/well-differentiated liposarcoma (ALT/WDLS).

On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), hibernomas often demonstrate areas that do not suppress with fat-saturation techniques, reflecting their brown fat content, and show prominent internal vascularity after contrast administration [4,5]. Due to their high mitochondrial content, they frequently exhibit marked FDG avidity on PET/CT, with standardized uptake values (SUVs) that can equal or exceed those of malignant soft tissue sarcomas [6,7]. Well-differentiated liposarcomas, in contrast, rarely demonstrate such high metabolic activity and typically lack organized vascularity.

Histopathologically, hibernomas are characterized by a variable mixture of multivacuolated brown adipocytes with granular, eosinophilic cytoplasm and univacuolated white adipocytes [1,2]. Immunohistochemically, they are typically positive for S100 protein, and importantly, negative for MDM2 and CDK4, which helps to exclude ALT/WDLS [8].

Herein, we present a case of a large thigh hibernoma with an exceptionally high SUVmax of 34.8—one of the highest reported in the literature—which contributed to a strong initial suspicion of liposarcoma. This case is particularly instructive as it demonstrates the full spectrum of potential imaging findings in hibernoma, including striking hypervascularity and extreme metabolic activity. Our aim is to reinforce the importance of recognizing these characteristic features and the critical role of a multidisciplinary team in navigating such diagnostic dilemmas.

Case report

A 65-year-old Caucasian woman with a significant history of severe, diffuse peripheral arterial disease was referred for vascular surgical evaluation. The referral was not for a palpable mass; rather, a large lesion was incidentally discovered in the proximal left thigh during preoperative CT angiography. The patient subsequently recalled a longstanding, asymptomatic swelling in that area that had recently increased slightly in size.

Diagnostic assessment

Initial CT angiography revealed a 9 × 8.5 cm well-marginated mass within the left iliopsoas compartment. The lesion exhibited a predominantly fat-attenuation matrix with internal nodular and linear soft-tissue density components consistent with vascular channels (Fig. 1A). Subsequent MRI detailed a 9.9 × 8.7 × 15 cm intramuscular mass. On T1-weighted imaging, the mass was isointense to subcutaneous fat, confirming its lipomatous nature (Fig. 1B). T2-weighted fat-saturated imaging demonstrated heterogeneous high signal intensity with incomplete fat suppression, characterized by hyperintense foci and septa (Fig. 1C). Post-contrast imaging revealed diffuse enhancement with ectatic internal vascular structures and visible feeding vascular pedicles (Fig. 1D). The mass showed intimate contact with the femoral neurovascular bundle but maintained a well-defined capsule without true invasion. Mass effect was evident on the surrounding muscles, and the lesion reached the femoral diaphysis cortex without erosion or scalloping. In retrospect, the coronal T1-weighted images not only highlighted the prominent vascularity but also demonstrated areas of signal slightly lower than that of subcutaneous fat—a feature attributable to the presence of brown fat and suggestive of hibernoma.

FDG-PET/CT demonstrated intense hypermetabolism with an SUVmax of 34.8. A central photopenic area corresponded to vascular lakes seen on MRI and CT (Fig. 1E).

Based on the imaging, the primary differential diagnosis lay between a hibernoma, given its fat content and prominent vascularity, and an ALT/WDLS, given the large size and deep location. The absence of thickened septa, combined with the striking vascularity, favored hibernoma, but the extreme FDG avidity raised concern for a more aggressive process.

Multidisciplinary review and management

The case was presented to a dedicated sarcoma multidisciplinary team (MDT). Initial core biopsy revealed sheets of brown adipocytes with characteristic multivacuolated cytoplasm and no cytologic atypia, suggesting hibernoma. The absence of lipoblasts, atypical stromal cells, or MDM2 amplification by immunohistochemistry supported this diagnosis. However, this created significant radiological-pathological discordance given the aggressive imaging appearance. The MDT recommended a repeat biopsy, which again confirmed hibernoma.

After reviewing the concordant histology and characteristic imaging features—specifically the internal vascularity and absence of dedifferentiation—the MDT concluded that the lesion was most consistent with a hibernoma. However, due to the extreme SUV value and patient safety concerns, marginal excision was recommended for definitive diagnosis and treatment.

Histopathological findings

The patient underwent marginal resection. The specimen was a 530 g, well-encapsulated, polylobulated tumor measuring 15 × 8 × 7 cm, with a thin capsule and prominent feeding vessels (Fig. 2). Follow-up and patient perspective

The patient recovered well from surgery. At 12-month postoperative follow-up, she remained asymptomatic with no clinical or ultrasonographic evidence of local recurrence.

The patient was informed that her case would be submitted for publication, and she provided written informed consent. She reported satisfaction with the multidisciplinary approach to her care and was relieved that the final diagnosis confirmed a benign condition, avoiding unnecessary aggressive treatment. She remains asymptomatic and has no objections to sharing her clinical images and history for educational purposes.

Discussion

This case illustrates several critical learning points regarding the imaging diagnosis and management of hibernoma, particularly when it presents with extreme metabolic activity.

First, hibernomas often display prominent internal vascularity, including feeding vessels and vascular lakes, best visualized on contrast-enhanced MRI and CT [4,5]. In our case, the well-encapsulated nature, fat signal on T1-weighted MRI, and prominent vascular channels were key diagnostic clues arguing against ALT/WDLS, which typically lacks such organized vascularity and instead may demonstrate thickened fibrous septa. The vascularity in hibernoma, consisting of ectatic feeding vessels and internal vascular lakes, reflects the rich blood supply of brown adipose tissue—a distinctive feature radiologists should recognize.

Second, due to their high mitochondrial content, hibernomas can show intense FDG uptake. Our case, with an SUVmax of 34.8, represents the upper extreme of reported values but remains within the metabolic spectrum of brown fat tumors. To our knowledge, this is among the highest SUVmax values ever documented for a hibernoma. This finding is critically important because many clinicians assume that SUVmax values above 10-15 is highly suggestive of malignancy. Our case demonstrates that benign brown fat tumors can exhibit metabolic activity exceeding that of most sarcomas.

Third, the definitive exclusion of liposarcoma relied on MDM2 testing. ALT/WDLS and dedifferentiated liposarcomas are frequently associated with MDM2 amplification/overexpression, a genetic aberration not present in hibernomas [8,9]. Negative MDM2 staining, in the appropriate morphological context, effectively rules out ALT/WDLS.

Fourth, this case underscores the indispensable role of an experienced sarcoma MDT in reconciling discordant radiological and pathological findings. The MDT's systematic approach—insisting on a second biopsy and recommending definitive surgical excision—balanced the low probability of two false-negative biopsies against the severe consequences of misdiagnosing a sarcoma.

Finally, when faced with a suspicious soft tissue mass, early referral to a specialist with expertise in sarcoma is paramount before any biopsy is performed. This ensures appropriate procedure planning and avoids compromising future definitive surgery.

Conclusion

Hibernoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of any FDG-avid lipomatous soft tissue mass, particularly when imaging shows prominent internal vascularity and absence of thickened fibrous septa. SUVmax values exceeding 30 can occur in benign brown fat tumors and should not automatically trigger a sarcoma diagnosis. Recognizing these characteristic findings can improve diagnostic accuracy, guide appropriate management, and help avoid unnecessary aggressive treatment. Multidisciplinary evaluation and MDM2 immunohistochemistry remain essential in cases of diagnostic uncertainty.
