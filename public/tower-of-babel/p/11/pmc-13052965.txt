The causal relationship between Omega-3 fatty acids and Achilles Tendinitis risk: A two-sample Mendelian randomization study in European populations
Authors: Bo Wang, Zongyang Li, Rui Zhou, Jianye Chen, Mingda Liu, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Omega-3 fatty acids, found in fish oil, flaxseed oil, and walnuts, are widely known for their anti-inflammatory properties and are used in the treatment of various inflammatory diseases. While their benefits in cardiovascular diseases and arthritis are well-documented, their role in musculoskeletal conditions, particularly Achilles tendinitis, remains unclear. This study investigates the causal relationship between Omega-3 fatty acids and the risk of Achilles tendinitis using a two-sample Mendelian randomization approach. Genetic variants associated with Omega-3 levels were selected from publicly available genome-wide association study data. Nineteen independent single nucleotide polymorphisms were used as instrumental variables to predict Omega-3 levels. The causal relationship between genetically predicted Omega-3 levels and Achilles tendinitis risk was evaluated using inverse variance weighting, MR-Egger regression, and weighted median methods. Sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out analysis and funnel plots, were performed to assess robustness. The main analysis showed that for each 1 standard deviation increase in genetically predicted Omega-3 levels, the risk of Achilles tendinitis increased by 18% (odds ratio = 1.18, P = .020). Consistent results were observed across multiple robust methods, although only the inverse variance weighting method reached nominal significance. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the results remained stable even when individual single nucleotide polymorphisms were excluded, and there was no significant evidence of horizontal pleiotropy or heterogeneity. This study suggests a potential causal relationship between Omega-3 fatty acids and the risk of Achilles tendinitis. Despite the small effect size and lack of statistical significance after multiple testing corrections, these findings warrant further investigation. Larger sample sizes and analyses of different Omega-3 subtypes are needed to confirm these results and explore their potential clinical implications in tendon diseases.

1. Introduction

Omega-3 fatty acids are essential polyunsaturated fatty acids that are widely found in foods such as fish oil, flaxseed oil, and walnuts. They are considered to play a crucial role in human health. The main components of omega-3 fatty acids include eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), with EPA being particularly notable for its ability to reduce inflammation and improve cardiovascular health. For a long time, the anti-inflammatory properties of omega-3 fatty acids have made them a focus of research, especially in the context of chronic inflammatory diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, arthritis, and certain types of cancer.[1,2]

Despite the extensive literature on the role of omega-3 fatty acids in inflammatory diseases, their potential in musculoskeletal disorders remains underappreciated. Musculoskeletal diseases, particularly tendon injuries and joint diseases, cause significant pain and economic burden for patients. Achilles tendinitis is one of the most common sports-related injuries, often caused by overuse, repetitive mechanical loading, or external trauma. The primary characteristics of this condition include inflammation, swelling, and pain in the Achilles tendon, severely impacting the quality of life for athletes and other individuals engaged in high levels of physical activity.[3] Current treatment options include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, physical therapy, and surgical intervention. While these treatments can alleviate symptoms to some extent, their effectiveness is limited for chronic or recurrent Achilles tendinitis, and they may be associated with side effects. Increasing evidence suggests that omega-3 fatty acids, particularly EPA and DHA, can reduce inflammatory markers in joint tissues, thereby alleviating the degree of chronic inflammation. For example, studies have shown that omega-3 fatty acids significantly lower cytokine levels associated with inflammation and improve the health of cartilage and tendons.[3] Therefore, exploring whether omega-3 fatty acids can serve as an intervention for the prevention or treatment of Achilles tendinitis is of significant clinical importance. However, most of the existing literature is based on observational studies, lacking causal inference. Mendelian randomization (MR), a method that uses genetic instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships, can effectively avoid reverse causality and confounding bias typically present in traditional observational studies.[4]

This study aims to explore the causal relationship between omega-3 fatty acids and Achilles tendinitis using a two-sample Mendelian randomization (2SMR) approach. By analyzing genetic variants associated with omega-3 fatty acids and utilizing these genetic IVs to assess their impact on Achilles tendinitis, the study seeks to provide reliable evidence for the potential application of omega-3 fatty acids in the prevention and treatment of Achilles tendinitis. Furthermore, the MR method used in this study controls for confounding factors, providing a more rigorous causal inference. The MR analysis offers strong evidence regarding the causal relationship between omega-3 fatty acids and Achilles tendinitis, filling a gap in the existing literature. If the results support a protective effect of omega-3 fatty acids on Achilles tendinitis, it could provide new intervention strategies for its treatment and prevention. This finding would not only help clinicians select safer and more effective treatment strategies but also provide theoretical support for public health policies.[5] Moreover, the 2SMR design used in this study effectively avoids the common biases found in traditional epidemiological studies, particularly the effects of reverse causality and confounding factors, providing more reliable causal inference. Through further clinical trials and validation studies, omega-3 fatty acids may emerge as a potential new strategy for the treatment and prevention of Achilles tendinitis.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and data sources

This study employs a 2SMR approach to assess the potential causal relationship between genetically predicted omega-3 fatty acid levels and the risk of Achilles tendinitis. The biological and epidemiological evidence linking omega-3 fatty acids with inflammation and metabolism has been documented in previous reviews and studies.[6,7] Exposure data (Omega-3): Genetic variants were sourced from publicly available genome-wide association study data associated with serum or plasma omega-3 levels, specifically from the IEU exposure code met-c-855 (mainly including UK Biobank/MetaboChip, etc). The selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are significantly associated with omega-3 levels and meet the instrumental strength requirements (see Section 2.3). Data extraction and interface were based on the MR-Base/MRC-IEU platform.[8] Outcome data (Achilles Tendinitis): The Achilles tendinitis phenotype from FinnGen Release 11 was used, with the IEU outcome code finngen_R11_M13_ACHILLESTEND, and a sample size of n = 326,188. Population and Data Format: Both the exposure and outcome data are summary statistics from individuals of European ancestry, obtained from publicly available platforms such as MRC-IEU, Neale Lab, and FinnGen. Individual-level data were not required.[8] The exposure data (UK Biobank/MetaboChip) and outcome data (FinnGen) are independent at both the project and country levels. Through cohort metadata verification, ID cross-checking, and statistical diagnostic evaluation, no participants are shared between the 2 datasets (χ2 = 0.00, P = 1.0). This design minimizes the risk of bias due to sample overlap.[9]

The MR method uses genetic variants as IVs, and when the corresponding assumptions are met, it can reduce confounding and reverse causality effects, thereby inferring the causal relationship between exposure and outcome.[9,10] All SNPs and phenotype data underwent strict quality control, with missing data, mismatches, and extreme values being excluded to ensure the reliability of the analysis.

2.2. Basic assumptions of MR analysis

To ensure the validity and robustness of causal inference, 3 fundamental assumptions of MR are followed.[11] Correlation assumption: the IV is significantly associated with the exposure (omega-3) (P < 5 × 10−8). To increase the number of IVs and statistical power, a relaxed threshold of P < 5 × 10−6 was initially applied in this study, and an F-statistic >10 was used to further ensure the strength of the instrument[12]; independence assumption: the IV is independent of potential confounders (such as age, sex, lifestyle, etc); exclusivity assumption: the IV affects the outcome solely through the exposure and not via other pathways (no horizontal pleiotropy). In the presence of potential risks of average pleiotropy, MR-Egger provides a bias correction and diagnostic framework.[11]

2.3. Instrumental variable selection and MR analysis methods

The selection and quality control of IVs were carried out through rigorous screening and data processing procedures. First, an initial screening was performed based on the criterion of P-values <5 × 10−6, selecting SNPs significantly associated with serum/plasma omega-3 levels.[13] Subsequently, to avoid linkage disequilibrium (LD) effects, the clump_data function in two sampleMR was used (r2 < 0.001, window 10,000 kb, with reference to the European 1KGP data) to remove LD-related loci, ensuring that the selected 19 SNPs were nearly independent and could effectively represent omega-3 exposure. The cumulative explained variance (R2) for these SNPs was 5.78%, and the F-statistic range (Fmin = 22.50, Fmax = 420.46, Fmean = 92.74) met the standard for strong IVs (F > 10), indicating a low risk of weak instrument bias. During data processing, harmonization of SNPs between the exposure and outcome datasets was performed to ensure consistent effect allele directions. Additionally, palindromic SNPs were processed based on minor allele frequency, and when certain outcome SNPs were missing, high LD proxies (r2 > 0.8) were used to supplement the data, maximizing information.[14]

To assess the causal effect of omega-3 levels on the risk of Achilles tendinitis, the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary analysis method. The relationship between a 1 standard deviation increase in exposure and the risk of Achilles tendinitis was calculated, reporting β, standard error (SE), odds ratio (OR), 95% confidence interval (CI) (95% CI), and two-tailed P-values. Additionally, MR-Egger regression, weighted median, and weighted mode methods were used as robustness analyses to examine the impact of pleiotropy and outliers. In terms of heterogeneity diagnostics, Cochran Q test for IVW (Q = 19.754, df = 18, P = .3468) and the Q test for MR-Egger (Q = 18.900, df = 17, P = .3343) did not show significant heterogeneity. Furthermore, the MR-Egger intercept was −0.0166 (SE = 0.0190, P = .3928), and no significant horizontal pleiotropy was found. Based on these robustness checks, MR-PRESSO was not used for global testing in this study.[11] To control the risk of false positives due to multiple comparisons, the P-values of the primary analysis were further adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate (FDR) correction (target α = 0.05). The MR analysis in this study was conducted using the two sample MR package in R (version 4.2.0), with the analysis process personally carried out by the researcher to ensure the rigor of the methodology. With data obtained from the MR-Base and MRC-IEU platforms. By combining these methods and platforms, we ensured the reliability of the analysis and provided robust causal inference for this study.[15]

3. Results

3.1. Main effect analysis

3.1.1. Instrumental variable selection and model setup

At the exposure end, an initial screening was performed for loci associated with serum or plasma omega-3 levels, using a threshold of P < 5 × 10−6,[16] LD clumping was implemented using the European 1KGP reference with a threshold of i2 < 0.001 and a 10 Mb window, resulting in the identification of 19 SNPs that were nearly independent and significantly associated with the exposure as IVs. The total explained variance (R2) for these loci was 5.78%, and the first-stage strength was robust (Fmin = 22.50, Fmax = 420.46, Fmean = 92.74), meeting the strong instrument criterion (F > 10), indicating a low risk of weak instrument bias. The loci covered multiple gene regions, with the rs174546 SNP in the FADS gene cluster explaining the highest variance for the exposure (R2 = 1.14%, F = 155.93), although it did not dominate the overall estimate. Exposure and outcome data were then harmonized (harmonise_data), aligning effect alleles and directions; palindromic SNPs were processed based on minor allele frequency. When certain exposure SNPs were missing in the outcome data, high-LD proxies (r2 > 0.8) were preferentially used to preserve the information.[17,18] Based on this, IVW was pre-set as the primary model, while MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode were configured as robustness supplements.[19] The effect size is defined as the impact of a 1 standard deviation increase in genetically predicted omega-3 levels on the risk of Achilles tendinitis, with β, SE, OR, 95% CI, and two-tailed P-values reported uniformly. To control for multiple comparisons arising from the use of multiple methods, P-values from the primary method are also presented with the BH FDR as a supplementary measure,[20] The primary inference is still based on the original P-values from IVW and the consistency of directions across methods. 3.1.2. Main analysis results (IVW Method)

After incorporating 19 independent, sufficiently strong genetic instruments, the multiplicative random effects IVW method (which is more robust to residual heterogeneity) was used as the pre-specified primary model. The Wald ratios for each SNP were combined using inverse variance weighting, yielding the overall effect: β = 0.1676, SE = 0.0722; OR = 1.182, 95% CI: 1.026 to 1.362; P = .020. This suggests that under the assumption that the IVs meet the assumptions of correlation, independence, and exclusivity, each 1 standard deviation increase in genetically predicted omega-3 levels is associated with an average 18% increase in the risk of Achilles tendinitis. This finding provides preliminary evidence for the potential role of omega-3 fatty acids in the development of Achilles tendinitis. However, due to the small effect size and limited statistical significance of the current results, future research should consider larger sample sizes and more refined stratified analyses to further validate this relationship and explore the practical applications of omega-3 in tendinitis prevention and treatment. Since β is on the log-odds scale, OR = exp (β) reflects the proportional change in relative risk. In the absence of a unified baseline risk denominator and detailed case definitions (e.g., acute vs chronic or tendon body vs attachment point types), this study cautiously interprets the estimate as a nominally significant, directionally consistent, weak-to-moderate effect.[21–23]

Given the instrument strength of this cohort (cumulative R2 = 5.78%, Fmean = 92.7) and the outcome sample size (n = 326,188), the overall informational power supports the detection of small to moderate effects.[17] After applying the BH FDR correction to the P-values from multiple methods, the IVW Q-value was 0.458 (not reaching FDR < 0.05). Given that different MR methods are not independent tests and that IVW was pre-registered as the primary analysis, the nominal significance of IVW and the consistency of directions across models remain the primary basis for inference.[18] Additionally, based on subsequent visualization and sensitivity diagnostics (such as the consistent direction of scatter plot regression lines, positive β values after leave-one-out analysis excluding any individual SNP, a nearly symmetrical funnel plot, nonsignificant Egger intercept, and no heterogeneity observed in the Q test), it is evident that the main effect is neither driven by a single outlier instrument nor dominated by significant horizontal pleiotropy. This strengthens the internal validity and robustness of the IVW estimate.[23,24]

3.1.3. Consistency validation across multiple models

To test the dependence of the main conclusion on model specification, robustness was reexamined using MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode on the harmonized dataset.[25] The results show that the effect direction was positive for all 4 methods and consistent with IVW. However, due to differing tolerances for pleiotropy or outliers and lower statistical efficiency compared to IVW, none of the methods except IVW reached nominal significance. Specifically, MR-Egger yielded β = 0.3120 (SE = 0.1801), OR = 1.366 (95% CI: 0.960–1.945), P = .101 (q = 0.747), with a larger point estimate but a wider CI that crossed 1. This is consistent with Egger characteristic of sacrificing efficiency to allow for horizontal pleiotropy.[26,27] In summary, under the context of low heterogeneity and a nonsignificant intercept, the consistency of a positive effect direction across multiple models suggests that the main effect is not a mere artifact of a single model. The significant differences mainly reflect the reduced efficiency of non-IVW models in mitigating potential pleiotropy, combined with the smaller effect size in this study. Considering the primary analysis strategy and diagnostic evidence, the significance of IVW is still regarded as the main basis for inference, with its direction and magnitude consistently supported by multiple models.[28,29]

3.3. Sensitivity analysis

3.3.1. Leave-one-out analysis

In the leave-one-out procedure: sequentially excluding each IV and re-estimating the effect under the same settings (harmonized dataset; multiplicative random-effects IVW): the overall effect consistently remained positive. The pooled β ranged from 0.1277 to 0.2221 (corresponding OR = 1.14–1.25), with p-values between 0.0045 and 0.0709. Even when the statistical significance approached the margin, the direction of the effect never reversed.[32] As shown in the figure, excluding more informative loci: often from the FADS cluster (e.g., rs174546): or loci with particularly extreme Wald ratios leads to relatively larger shifts in β; however, all estimates remain on the same side and within the vicinity of the main analysis estimate (β = 0.1676; OR = 1.18). By contrast, excluding loci with smaller contributions yields effects that nearly coincide with the overall estimate, indicating a negligible influence on the pooled result.[33] Overall, the results of the leave-one-out analysis suggest that the combined effect is not driven by a single outlier tool, but rather by the cumulative effect of multiple loci in the same direction. This is consistent with the positive slopes of the IVW/Egger regression lines in the scatter plot, as well as the distribution characteristics of the single SNP forest plot, where most estimates are positive and the CIs are wide. These findings reinforce the robustness and internal consistency of the main effect estimate. See Figure 2 for details.[34]

In the plot, the x-axis represents the single SNP causal estimates (Wald ratio), and the y-axis represents 1/SE as a measure of precision. Additionally, no visual patterns corresponding to “small sample effects/selective reporting” were observed. It is important to emphasize that the funnel plot in MR primarily provides qualitative evidence and should not be used alone as a basis for determining bias. Together with Cochran Q (which is nonsignificant for both methods) and the robustness of the leave-one-out analysis, these results collectively indicate that the combined estimates in this study exhibit good internal consistency, with limited influence from outlier instruments or systematic pleiotropy.[31] See Figure 3 for details.

3.3.4. MR-Egger intercept test

The MR-Egger intercept test was used to assess directional horizontal pleiotropy. The results showed an intercept of −0.0166 (SE = 0.0190, P = .3928), with a 95% CI approximately ranging from (−0.0538, 0.0206) (insufficient evidence to exclude zero), indicating that the average exogenous effect of the IVs does not significantly deviate from zero, and no statistically significant directional pleiotropy was detected. This result is consistent with the near-symmetrical shape of the funnel plot, as well as the absence of heterogeneity in Cochran Q (IVW: Q = 19.754, P = .3468; Egger: Q = 18.900, P = .3343). It also aligns with the robustness observed in the leave-one-out analysis, collectively supporting the internal validity of the main effect estimate.[37,38] It should be noted that although the MR-Egger slope is in the same direction as the IVW estimate, it does not reach statistical significance; with only 19 instruments, the MR-Egger estimator is relatively inefficient, so its nonsignificance should be interpreted with caution.[39]

3.3.5. Cochran Q heterogeneity test

To evaluate consistency across instrument-specific (SNP) estimates, Cochran Q was computed under both the IVW and MR-Egger frameworks. Results were: IVW, Q = 19.754, df = 18, P = .3468; MR-Egger, Q = 18.900, df = 17, P = .3343: both indicating no significant heterogeneity. This suggests that the single SNP causal estimates show limited dispersion around a common effect, and random-effects pooling is not materially affected by overdispersion.[40] Likewise, the results do not support substantial inconsistency attributable to unbalanced (directional) horizontal pleiotropy.[29] Taken together with the near-symmetric funnel plot and the nonsignificant MR-Egger intercept, there is insufficient evidence of systematic bias; consistent with the leave-one-out analysis and the forest plot, this test further supports the internal consistency and robustness of the main-effect estimate.[34]

Synthesizing the main effect estimates across MR models, the visualizations, and the sensitivity analyses: under 19 strong instruments (F > 10), low heterogeneity, and a nonsignificant MR-Egger intercept, the primary IVW model indicates that each 1-SD increase in omega-3 is associated with −18% higher odds of Achilles tendinopathy (IVW OR = 1.18, P = .020). The robustness estimators (MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted/simple mode) are concordant in direction (all positive) but: apart from IVW: do not reach nominal significance, and none remain significant after BH FDR correction, suggesting that if a true causal effect exists, its magnitude is small and greater information would be needed to achieve higher statistical power. Consistent with this, the leave-one-out analysis shows unchanged direction with limited fluctuation in magnitude upon removal of any single SNP; the funnel plot is nearly symmetric; the forest plot shows a “mostly positive with wide intervals” pattern; and both Cochran Q and the Egger intercept are nonsignificant, providing no indication that the association is a false positive driven by a single outlying instrument, directional horizontal pleiotropy, or substantial heterogeneity. Overall, this study provides directionally consistent, internally robust but small-magnitude causal evidence, warranting further confirmation in larger samples and with finer exposure or outcome stratification.[41]

4. Discussion

In the 2SMR framework for European ancestry populations, the causal effect of Omega-3 (IEU: met-c-855) on Achilles tendinopathy (FinnGen R11: finngen_R11_M13_ACHILLESTEND) was evaluated based on 19 strong instruments (total R2 = 5.78%, Fmin = 22.50, Fmean = 92.7). The results suggest that for each 1 standard deviation increase in Omega-3, the probability of Achilles tendinopathy occurrence increases by approximately 18% (IVW OR = 1.18, P = .020).[42] In multiple models, including MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted and simple mode, the results consistently showed a positive direction, with no evidence of directional pleiotropy or significant heterogeneity under various diagnostics (Egger intercept nonsignificant, Cochran Q nonsignificant, funnel plot nearly symmetrical, Leave-one-out analysis not sensitive to individual points), suggesting the robustness of the conclusion. However, after multiple testing correction, the overall result was not significant (FDR), and the wide CIs of individual SNPs jointly indicated that, if a true causal effect exists, its magnitude is likely small. Therefore, this study provides a “directionally consistent, nominally significant, internally robust, but small effect size” evidence landscape. Considering that MR estimates reflect genetic differences in exposure rather than directly equivalent to short-term supplementation effects, and this study focused primarily on the total Omega-3 index without distinguishing between EPA/DHA/ALA subtypes, further replication in larger samples and updated cohorts is warranted. Additionally, prioritizing MR of fatty acid subtypes, MVMR (e.g., including Omega-6/inflammatory markers/lipids), and colocalization analysis in key regions (such as FADS) should be conducted to further clarify the sources of the signal and potential pathways, enhancing the causal understanding of tendon disease mechanisms.[13]

The validity of the inferences in this study is primarily supported by 3 aspects: First, the correlation assumption is met: instrument strength is sufficient (all F > 10), and the risk of weak instrument bias is low; second, the independence assumption is supported: the exposure and outcome are derived from independent platforms, both of European descent, with expected sample overlap negligible, and no bias patterns consistent with systematic confounding were observed in cross-methods and graphical diagnostics; third, evidence suggests that the exclusivity assumption is not violated: the Egger intercept is negative, the funnel plot is symmetrical, the Q test is nonsignificant, and the Leave-one-out analysis rules out “single outlier instrument-driven” effects, collectively suggesting that directional pleiotropy at the population level is not a dominant factor.[18,20] Building on this, the nominally significant result of the IVW can be interpreted as genetic evidence of a modest increase in risk, but it does not serve as a direct basis for making nutritional supplementation decisions in clinical populations. It is noteworthy that, although this study did not yield significant results after multiple testing correction, its findings still provide reliable genetic evidence for the potential role of Omega-3 fatty acids in musculoskeletal diseases, such as Achilles tendinopathy. This finding offers direction for further mechanistic studies and clinical interventions. Future research should consider larger sample sizes and more precise stratified analyses to further validate this potential causal relationship and explore the clinical application value of Omega-3 in tendon diseases.

At the genetic causal level, a modest, directionally consistent, and internally robust nominal positive correlation was observed between genetically elevated Omega-3 levels and the risk of Achilles tendinopathy. Given that this finding has not yet passed the multiple testing correction threshold and subtype and pathway decomposition have not been completed, it should be viewed as a credible but needing further validation causal clue. Its scientific value primarily lies in pointing to potential links between specific fatty acid subtypes, the inflammation-repair network, and tendon degeneration, providing a clear and actionable roadmap for subsequent mechanistic studies and intervention trials.

In summary, the main limitation of this study is that its results are only applicable to individuals of European ancestry and that potential confounding factors, such as lifestyle, physical activity, or injury history, which may influence tendinitis risk, were not adjusted for. At the level of genetic causality we observed a modest, directionally consistent, and internally robust nominal positive association between genetically elevated omega-3 levels and the risk of Achilles tendinopathy. Although the effect size was small and did not remain significant after correction for multiple testing, this finding outlines an actionable research roadmap for probing the potential causal links among fatty acid profiles, inflammatory/repair networks, and tendon degeneration, and it provides an important theoretical basis for subsequent mechanistic studies and intervention trials.

5. Conclusion

In individuals of European ancestry, we used 2SMR to evaluate the causal relationship between genetically proxied omega-3 levels (IEU: met-c-855) and Achilles tendinopathy (FinnGen R11: finngen_R11_M13_ACHILLESTEND). The primary multiplicative random-effects IVW analysis indicated that each 1-SD increase in genetically determined omega-3 was associated with a nominally increased risk of Achilles tendinopathy (OR = 1.18, P = .020). The positive direction of effect was consistent across robustness models: including MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode: and was supported by multiple diagnostics: a nonsignificant Egger intercept, no significant heterogeneity by Cochran Q, an approximately symmetrical funnel plot, and leave-one-out analyses showing that exclusion of any single variant did not alter the direction of effect.[43] Overall, the results were characterized by directionally consistent, internally robust, yet small effects. Meanwhile, BH FDR correction applied within the multi-model framework for the same exposure–outcome pair was negative (i.e., nonsignificant), suggesting that if a true causal effect exists, its magnitude is small and additional data are needed for further confirmation. In sum, at the level of genetic causality we observed a nominal positive association between genetically elevated omega-3 levels and the risk of Achilles tendinopathy that was modest in magnitude, directionally consistent, and internally robust. Given the lack of significance after multiple testing correction and the absence of subtype and pathway decomposition to date, this finding provides a testable causal hypothesis and a clear research roadmap linking the fatty acid profile, inflammation/repair networks, and tendon degeneration, which can be further clarified and strengthened through larger samples, subtype stratification, multivariable MR (MVMR), and colocalization analyses.[44]
