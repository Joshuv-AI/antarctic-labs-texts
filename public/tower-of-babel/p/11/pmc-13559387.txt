Determinants of intensive care unit-acquired acute brain injury in acute respiratory distress syndrome: a retrospective cohort study of ventilator settings and patient factors
Authors: Joonho Lee, Jinsong Park, Jeong-Am Ryu, Chi Ryang Chung, Jeong Hoon Yang, Ryoung-Eun Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is associated with neurological complications, but whether mechanical ventilation parameters or patient factors predominantly contribute to intensive care unit (ICU)-acquired acute brain injury (ABI) remains unclear. We aimed to determine whether ICU-acquired ABI in ARDS is independently associated with positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and driving pressure delivered early after intubation or, instead, with patient-level factors.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory, single-center retrospective cohort study of mechanically ventilated ARDS patients (January 2019 to June 2023). ICU-acquired ABI was defined as a new acute intracranial lesion on computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) obtained more than 72 hours after ICU admission. In a pre-specified analysis, PEEP and driving pressure measured in the first 24 hours after intubation were tested for independent association with ABI using Firth penalized logistic regression with multiple imputation, adjusting for pre-specified confounders [age, sex, arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ratio, vasopressor use, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)], with platelet count and C-reactive protein (CRP) analyzed as separate host-factor exposures. Robustness, subtype, exposure-window, and analyses accounting for the selective use of neuroimaging were performed.ResultsAmong 2,844 patients (median age, 66 years; 62% male), 54 (1.9%) developed ICU-acquired ABI. Neither PEEP [adjusted odds ratio (OR) =1.02; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.91 to 1.14] nor driving pressure (adjusted OR =1.01; 95% CI: 0.96 to 1.06) was independently associated with ABI, and this null result held across the pre-specified adjustment sets, both ischemic and hemorrhagic subtypes, the imaged subcohort, and inverse-probability weighting. Thrombocytopenia (platelet <100×103/µL) was independently associated with ABI (adjusted OR =3.22; 95% CI: 1.77 to 5.88), with CRP associated only with ischemic lesions. ABI was associated with a longer ICU stay (adjusted β =16.4 days) and higher in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR =2.92).ConclusionsIn ARDS, neither PEEP nor driving pressure was independently associated with ICU-acquired ABI once exposures were time-aligned, whereas thrombocytopenia was. At the airway pressures used in contemporary practice, brain injury appeared to be related more to the patient’s hematologic vulnerability than to ventilator settings, and these findings do not support restricting lung-protective ventilation out of concern for neurological harm. This exploratory analysis, with 54 events, cannot exclude smaller effects and requires prospective confirmation.Keywords: Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), acute brain injury (ABI), mechanical ventilation, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this exploratory cohort of 2,844 mechanically ventilated acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) patients, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and driving pressure measured early after intubation were not independently associated with intensive care unit (ICU)-acquired acute brain injury (ABI) after multivariable adjustment, and this null result was consistent across all sensitivity analyses. Thrombocytopenia (platelet <100×103/µL) was the one factor independently and reproducibly associated with ABI, with an additional association for systemic inflammation confined to ischemic injury.

What is known and what is new?

• Previous studies described ARDS-associated brain injury at the clinical and imaging levels, but it remained unconfirmed whether the ventilator settings delivered at the bedside are associated with the occurrence of clinically relevant ICU-acquired brain injury.

• Using a pre-specified analysis, this study found that neither PEEP nor driving pressure was independently associated with imaging-confirmed ICU-acquired brain injury, which was instead associated with patient-level factors, particularly thrombocytopenia.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• In ARDS patients without pre-existing brain injury, concern about neurological harm need not restrict standard lung-protective targets. Monitoring thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, and systemic inflammation may help identify those at higher risk. Prospective studies with protocolized neuroimaging are needed to confirm these observations.

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a frequent cause of acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, representing up to one-quarter of mechanically ventilated patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) and carrying a hospital mortality of approximately 35–48% (1). Beyond pulmonary dysfunction, there is growing recognition of the brain-lung interaction, whereby acute brain injury (ABI) may precipitate ARDS and, conversely, severe lung injury may contribute to neurological complications and long-term cognitive impairment (2-4).

Preclinical and translational work suggests that ARDS and ventilator-induced lung injury can trigger hippocampal neuroinflammation, neuronal apoptosis, endothelial activation, and cerebral microvascular injury (2). Clinical neuroimaging studies further support these findings, with structural brain lesions such as acute ischemia, hypoxic-ischemic injury, and cerebral microbleeds frequently observed in ARDS patients undergoing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (5,6). Critical illness-associated cerebral microbleeds, particularly with callosal or juxtacortical predominance, have been described in up to 30% of patients with respiratory failure or sepsis, a vulnerability linked to systemic hypoxemia, microangiopathy, and extracorporeal support (7,8).

In ARDS, invasive mechanical ventilation is the main supportive therapy. Lung-protective strategies reduced ventilator-induced lung injury and improved pulmonary outcomes in general ICU populations (9). In patients with ABI, such strategies may increase partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) and intracranial pressure and can influence cerebral venous return and cerebral perfusion pressure, raising concerns about impaired cerebral hemodynamics (10,11). Systematic reviews suggest that protective ventilation can often be applied safely in patients with ABI and may improve oxygenation (10), but firm evidence is lacking on whether specific ventilator settings directly contribute to ABI in ARDS.

Previous studies have mainly characterized ARDS-associated ABI at the clinical or imaging level (2-8), without quantifying how these lesions relate to the ventilator settings actually delivered at the bedside. Because ICU neuroimaging is obtained at the clinician’s discretion rather than by protocol, the apparent burden of ABI also depends on which patients are selected for imaging, a source of ascertainment bias that has rarely been addressed. It therefore remains unclear whether mechanical ventilation parameters are decisive drivers of ABI or whether baseline patient characteristics are the more important determinants of structural brain injury (2-4,8,12-16). In general ICU cohorts, ICU-onset stroke has been linked to global severity indices, mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), and coagulopathy, including abnormalities in prothrombin time (17).

This exploratory, single-center retrospective cohort study of ARDS patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation examined whether the ventilator pressures positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and driving pressure are independently associated with ICU-acquired ABI, and characterized the patient-level factors that accompany it. The analysis focused on imaging-confirmed acute structural brain lesions (ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, hypoxic-ischemic injury, and cerebral edema) as outcomes with immediate clinical implications for ICU practice. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0973/rc).

Methods

Patient population

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study of adult ICU patients with ARDS managed with invasive mechanical ventilation at Samsung Medical Center, a 1,900-bed tertiary referral hospital in Seoul, Republic of Korea between January 2019 and June 2023. ARDS was defined according to the Berlin definition (18). All consecutive ICU patients meeting ARDS criteria and receiving invasive ventilation were eligible. Patients were excluded if they: (I) were not intubated with invasive mechanical ventilation during the ICU stay; (II) had pre-existing structural brain lesions or acute stroke before ICU admission; (III) were admitted primarily under neurology or neurosurgery services; or (IV) lacked essential ventilator data required for analysis. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (IRB approval No. 2023-08-003). The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design and the use of deidentified data.

Neuroimaging indications and timing

Brain imaging [computed tomography (CT) or MRI] was obtained at the treating clinician’s discretion when new neurological findings arose. To focus on ICU-acquired brain injury, imaging studies performed within the first 72 hours of ICU admission were excluded. Any acute brain lesions identified within the first 72 hours were not counted as events. This rule was chosen to reduce contamination by pre-ICU events while retaining lesions plausibly attributable to the ICU ARDS course.

Definitions of ABI

The primary outcome was development of acute structural brain injury during ICU admission. ABI was defined as a new acute intracranial lesion (on CT or MRI) identified during the ICU stay, including ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage (intracerebral, subarachnoid, subdural, or epidural hemorrhage), diffuse hypoxic-ischemic injury, and acute cerebral edema. Chronic or non-acute findings and cerebral microbleeds were not counted as ABI.

Data collection and variables

Abstracted variables included demographic data (age, sex), pre-existing comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, prior vascular disease, atrial fibrillation), and illness severity, recorded as the first Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score during the ICU stay. Time-varying laboratory and ventilator variables, used both to describe the cohort and in the primary analyses, were drawn from a common window of the first 24 hours after intubation, applying the same window in patients with and without ABI. Within this window, the most abnormal value for each variable was used [the lowest platelet count and the highest C-reactive protein (CRP), prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, and lactate, together with the lowest arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ratio].

Ventilator variables

Ventilator variables were extracted from the electronic medical record within the same first 24-hour post-intubation window and comprised PEEP, plateau pressure, tidal volume, and the PaO2/FiO2 ratio. Driving pressure was calculated as plateau pressure minus PEEP. When a static plateau pressure was not recorded, the peak inspiratory pressure during pressure-controlled ventilation (PEEP plus the set pressure above PEEP) was used as a surrogate.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared with the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Missing values were infrequent (below 9% for every covariate) and were handled by multiple imputation using chained equations with 20 imputations, with estimates combined by Rubin’s rules. The primary analysis was a pre-specified test of whether PEEP and driving pressure were independently associated with ICU-acquired ABI. Rather than choosing covariates by statistical significance, a fixed set of confounders was pre-specified on clinical grounds. This set comprised age, sex, PaO2/FiO2 ratio, vasopressor use, and ECMO support, each a recognized risk factor for brain injury in critically ill patients with respiratory failure (2,17). PEEP and driving pressure were adjusted for this confounder set and entered simultaneously, regardless of univariable significance. Platelet count and CRP were not treated as confounders of the ventilator-pressure effect. Instead, they were analyzed as separate host-factor exposures adjusted for the same covariate set, and PaCO2 was examined separately as a potential mediator. SOFA score was not included because it incorporates platelet count, PaO2/FiO2 ratio, and vasopressor use. In a sensitivity analysis, adding the host-factor covariates to the ventilator model did not change the estimates (Table S1).

Associations were estimated with Firth’s penalized logistic regression, which limits small-sample bias when outcomes are rare, entering PEEP and driving pressure together and then separately. Odds ratios (ORs) are reported per 1 cmH2O, and platelet count was additionally dichotomized at 100×103/µL and CRP at 10 mg/dL to denote elevated systemic inflammation. Robustness to the choice of adjustment set was examined across pre-specified specifications, including models that added vascular and hematologic risk factors (atrial fibrillation, prior vascular disease, antithrombotic use, and coagulation markers) and a model that excluded patients supported with ECMO. These factors may influence ABI risk but do not plausibly determine ventilator settings, and were therefore not part of the pre-specified confounder set. Ischemic and hemorrhagic subtypes were analyzed separately as exploratory outcomes. Prespecified sensitivity analyses comprised a complete-case analysis, alternative exposure windows and anchors, and inverse-probability-of-imaging weighting to address selection by neuroimaging. Variance inflation factors were below two for all covariates. Secondary outcomes, namely ICU length of stay and in-hospital mortality, were analyzed by multivariable linear and Firth penalized logistic regression, respectively. All tests were two-sided at α=0.05, and analyses were performed in R (version 4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the logistf and mice packages.

All scientific content, data analysis, and interpretation were performed independently by the authors, who reviewed and edited all AI-assisted content and take full responsibility for the published article.

Results

Study population

Of 3,990 patients with ARDS screened during the study period, 2,917 received invasive mechanical ventilation and had ventilator data available for analysis (Figure 1). Brain imaging was performed in 557 patients during the ICU stay. Fifty-four patients (1.9%) met the criteria for ICU-acquired ABI, defined as a new acute lesion on imaging obtained more than 72 hours after ICU admission. A further 73 patients with acute lesions that did not meet this definition were classified as neither cases nor controls and were excluded from the comparison, leaving 2,790 patients without ABI as the reference group. Among the 54 patients with ABI, 39 had ischemic and 18 had hemorrhagic lesions (7 had both), and 4 had other patterns such as diffuse hypoxic-ischemic injury or cerebral edema.

Median age was 66 years (interquartile range, 56 to 75 years) and 62% were male, with no difference between patients with and without ABI (age 66 vs. 66 years, P=0.97, and male 67% vs. 62%, P=0.60). Illness severity was greater in the ABI group, reflected by a higher SOFA score (9 vs. 8, P=0.01). Vasopressor use within the first 24 hours after intubation was similar between groups (59% vs. 58%, P>0.99), as was the PaO2/FiO2 ratio (145 vs. 161, P=0.19). Patients with ABI had a markedly longer ICU stay (median, 21.5 vs. 5 days) and higher in-hospital mortality (50% vs. 26%, both P<0.001).

Baseline laboratory and ventilator parameters

On unadjusted comparison, the groups differed principally in platelet count (Table 1). Patients with ABI had a markedly lower platelet count within the first 24 hours after intubation [median, 53.5 vs. 110.0 (×103/µL), P<0.001], whereas prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, and lactate did not differ, and CRP showed only a trend toward higher values (10.8 vs. 7.1 mg/dL, P=0.058).

In contrast, the ventilator settings measured over the first 24 hours after intubation were comparable between groups. Neither PEEP (median, 5 vs. 5 cmH2O, P=0.25) nor driving pressure (13 vs. 12 cmH2O, P=0.27) differed, and prone positioning and ECMO were used at similar frequencies.

Ventilator pressures and host factors

In the pre-specified analysis, neither PEEP [adjusted OR =1.02 per cmH2O; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.91 to 1.14; P=0.74] nor driving pressure (adjusted OR =1.01; 95% CI: 0.96 to 1.06; P=0.66) was independently associated with ICU-acquired ABI, whether the two pressures were entered together (Table 2) or in separate models (Tables 3,4). The one host factor that remained independently associated with ABI was thrombocytopenia: a platelet count below 100×103/µL carried an adjusted OR of 3.22 (95% CI 1.77 to 5.88, P<0.001), an association already apparent on unadjusted analysis (OR =2.93; 95% CI: 1.62 to 5.30). Neither vasopressor use in the first 24 hours (adjusted OR =0.94; 95% CI: 0.55 to 1.61) nor elevated CRP (≥10 mg/dL; adjusted OR =1.53; 95% CI: 0.88 to 2.67; P=0.13) was independently associated with the outcome. With 54 events, the analysis had approximately 80% power to detect an OR of about 2.2 for a binary exposure and about 1.5 per standard deviation for a continuous exposure. The null association for the ventilator pressures was stable across the pre-specified adjustment sets, including models that added vascular and hematologic factors and a model that excluded ECMO-supported patients (Table S1).

Adjusted ORs were estimated by Firth’s penalized logistic regression with multiple imputation (m=20). PEEP and driving pressure were adjusted for the pre-specified confounders (age, sex, PaO2/FiO2, vasopressor within 24 h, and ECMO), defined a priori on clinical grounds, and entered together in the combined model (Table 2) or separately (Tables 3,4). Platelet count, thrombocytopenia, continuous CRP, and elevated CRP were each examined as separate host-factor exposures, every one adjusted for the same confounder set in its own model rather than entered together. No stepwise variable selection was used. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

When ischemic and hemorrhagic ABI were analyzed separately, thrombocytopenia remained associated with both subtypes (ischemic adjusted OR =3.41; 95% CI: 1.69 to 6.90; and hemorrhagic OR =3.53; 95% CI: 1.25 to 9.95), and CRP was associated only with ischemic injury (OR =1.43 per standard deviation; 95% CI: 1.10 to 1.86; P=0.008). PEEP and driving pressure were null in both subtypes (Table S2). The results were unchanged in a complete-case analysis (Table S3). The exposure-window analysis was informative: when the ventilator pressures were defined as the whole-stay worst value, as in earlier reports, PEEP became statistically associated with ABI (OR =1.13 per cmH2O; 95% CI: 1.04 to 1.22; P=0.004), but this association disappeared once the exposure was time-aligned to the first 24 hours after intubation (OR =1.02; 95% CI: 0.91 to 1.15), indicating that the apparent effect reflected temporal bias from measurements taken after neurological deterioration (Table S4).

Imaged subcohort analyses

Analyses restricted to the 484 patients who underwent neuroimaging showed the same pattern: PEEP (adjusted OR =1.00; 95% CI: 0.86 to 1.15) and driving pressure (adjusted OR =1.03; 95% CI: 0.98 to 1.09) were null, whereas thrombocytopenia (adjusted OR =2.88; 95% CI: 1.54 to 5.36, P<0.001) and elevated CRP (≥10 mg/dL; adjusted OR 2.14; 95% CI: 1.18 to 3.87, P=0.01) remained associated with ABI (Table 5). In the imaged cohort weighted by the inverse probability of undergoing neuroimaging, the estimates were concordant (PEEP OR =1.00; driving pressure OR =1.00; and thrombocytopenia OR =2.98, Table S5).

Secondary outcomes

ICU-acquired ABI was independently associated with worse secondary outcomes. After adjustment for clinical covariates, ABI was associated with a longer ICU stay (adjusted β =16.4 days; 95% CI: 9.2 to 23.7; P<0.001) and higher in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR =2.92; 95% CI: 1.63 to 5.23; P<0.001) (Table S6). Missing data were infrequent, below 9% for every analysis variable (Table S7).

Discussion

In this exploratory cohort of mechanically ventilated patients with ARDS, neither PEEP nor driving pressure was independently associated with ICU-acquired ABI in the primary analysis, and the null result was consistent across the pre-specified adjustment sets, in both ischemic and hemorrhagic subtypes, within the imaged subcohort, and after inverse-probability-of-imaging weighting. The factor most consistently associated with ABI was thrombocytopenia, with a weaker association for systemic inflammation that was limited to ischemic injury.

Because PEEP and driving pressure largely reflect the severity of the underlying lung injury, their lack of association with ABI indicates that the severity of the respiratory failure itself is not what relates to ICU-acquired brain injury in this cohort; patient-level vulnerability appears more relevant. This interpretation is consistent with the recent VENTIBRAIN cohort, in which ventilator settings were associated with mortality but not with neurological outcome (19). The modest airway pressures applied in this cohort (median PEEP 5 cmH2O), in keeping with contemporary lung-protective practice, may also have limited any detectable cerebral effect. A positive association between PEEP and ABI appeared only when pressures were summarized over the whole ICU stay. It disappeared once exposure was time-aligned, indicating temporal bias rather than a true effect (Table S4).

The pathways by which ventilation might injure the brain may operate mainly when cerebral autoregulation is already compromised. Raised intrathoracic pressure and permissive hypercapnia can impair autoregulation and elevate intracranial pressure, yet in patients with preserved autoregulation cerebral blood flow is maintained across the range of PEEP used clinically (11,20,21), and PEEP affects intracranial pressure appreciably chiefly when cerebrovascular reserve is exhausted (22-24). Although critical illness and sepsis can themselves impair autoregulation, patients with pre-existing brain injury or primary neurological disease were excluded, in whom reserve is most compromised. In keeping with this, PaCO2, the most potent physiological regulator of cerebral blood flow, showed no association with ABI, and adjusting for it left the null for PEEP and driving pressure unchanged, which argues against a clinically important lung-brain pathway in this population.

In contrast, thrombocytopenia showed a consistent, independent association with ABI in both subtypes and across the analyses in which it was evaluated. Because platelet count was measured before the index imaging and the association persisted after adjustment for markers of illness severity, it is unlikely to reflect reverse causation or severity alone. Platelets help maintain microvascular integrity, so their depletion favors hemorrhage and microbleeding, while the endotheliopathy and consumptive coagulopathy of critical illness simultaneously promote microthrombosis and ischemia and may increase blood-brain barrier permeability (8,25). This dual role may explain the association with both subtypes, whereas isolated coagulation-time abnormalities (PT and aPTT) were not associated, suggesting that a low platelet count marks systemic microvascular injury rather than a specific clotting-factor defect. The findings are concordant with critical-care data linking thrombocytopenia to both intracerebral hemorrhage and ischemic stroke (26,27), and the association with CRP, confined to ischemic injury, suggests an additional contribution from inflammation-driven endothelial injury.

Our observed incidence of ICU-acquired ABI (1.9%) is considerably lower than that reported by MRI-based studies of ARDS, which have described acute ischemic lesions, hypoxic-ischemic injury, and critical illness–associated microbleeds in a substantial proportion of systematically imaged patients, with microbleeds alone in up to 30% (5,6,8). Much of this difference is methodological and represents a limitation of our approach: imaging was clinically indicated and predominantly CT-based, which is less sensitive than MRI for small ischemic lesions and microbleeds, and our case definition deliberately required more than 72 hours after ICU admission and excluded microbleeds. Our estimate therefore reflects overt, ICU-acquired structural injury rather than the full burden of subclinical microvascular pathology, and direct comparison with prior cohorts is constrained by these differences in imaging modality and outcome definition. Even so, the host-factor pattern we observed is consistent with those studies, which have implicated microangiopathy and coagulopathy rather than ventilator mechanics.

Clinically, in ARDS patients without pre-existing brain injury, concern about neurological harm need not constrain standard lung-protective targets. Instead, thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, and systemic inflammation may help identify patients at higher risk. Whether treating them lowers that risk is unknown. These implications are hypothesis-generating and require prospective studies with protocolized neuroimaging.

This study has several strengths. Exposures were assessed within an identical early window in patients with and without ABI, removing the temporal asymmetry that has complicated previous analyses. Confounders were pre-specified on clinical grounds, and the primary result was robust to penalized estimation, multiple imputation, alternative adjustment sets, and subtype analysis. Because ascertainment of ABI depended on discretionary neuroimaging, selection bias was specifically addressed by repeating the analysis within the imaged subcohort and by inverse-probability-of-imaging weighting, both of which reproduced the null for ventilator pressures and the association with thrombocytopenia. Several limitations remain. The study is single-center and retrospective, and, as noted, reliance on clinically indicated and largely CT-based imaging with a strict acute-lesion definition may have underascertained smaller lesions and limits comparability with MRI-based cohorts. Imaging was hardest to obtain in the sickest patients on the highest settings. Because these patients were often deeply sedated, ABI in this group may have been underascertained. With 54 events, only moderate or larger effects were detectable. The CIs were narrow and close to the null, but this reflects a lack of association rather than proof of no effect. Vasopressor use and ECMO were recorded within the exposure window and may lie partly on the causal pathway, although estimates were unchanged when ECMO-supported patients were excluded (28). Finally, cerebral autoregulation was inferred from cohort composition rather than measured directly, and cerebral microbleeds were excluded by design.

Conclusions

In this ARDS cohort, ventilator pressures were not independently associated with imaging-confirmed ICU-acquired brain injury once exposures were time-aligned, whereas thrombocytopenia was associated with both ischemic and hemorrhagic lesions. At the airway pressures used in contemporary lung-protective practice, brain injury therefore appears to be related more to the patient’s hematologic vulnerability than to the ventilator settings applied. These findings do not support restricting lung-protective ventilation out of concern for neurological harm in ARDS patients without pre-existing brain injury, although prospective studies with protocolized neuroimaging are needed for confirmation.
