A Sensor based turning dataset for data-driven surface roughness estimation
Authors: N R Sakthivel, H Harigovind, Binoy B Nair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface roughness is a critical parameter in machining, as it affects the corrosion resistance and fatigue properties of the finished workpiece. For difficult-to-machine materials such as Inconel-625, ensuring desired levels of surface roughness is even more challenging. Accurate surface roughness estimation for such expensive alloys can help in reducing material wastage and enhancing machining efficiency. In this paper, machining data collected during the turning of a particularly difficult-to-machine alloy, Inconel-625, is presented. Inconel is widely used in aerospace applications and is difficult to machine, as unlike other materials, Inconel does not get softer with increasing temperature. This dataset comprises twenty-seven sets of vibration data collected using a triaxial accelerometer and corresponding force and moment data collected using a dynamometer, resulting in 382,189,197 samples in total, acquired during the dry turning of Inconel-625 on a Kirloskar Turnmaster 40 Lathe. A Mitutoyo Surface Roughness Tester was used to measure the surface roughness after each turning operation. This publicly available dataset will be of help to the scientific community in developing machine learning/deep learning based on-line surface roughness estimation models for turning processes.Subject terms: Engineering, Mechanical engineering

Background & Summary

Turning is a widely used machining process wherein the surface finish and the cylindricity of the workpiece are crucial parameters. Difficult-to-machine alloys like Inconel are extensively used in advanced engineering and aerospace applications. Machining such a material is always a challenge due to its hardness leading to the tool insert’s surface deterioration, chatter, and poor surface finish on the workpiece. Surface finish is an important metric in such applications and directly affects the functional properties such as wear resistance, corrosion resistance, and aesthetic appeal of the machined workpiece. Inconel-625, a difficult-to-cut material is widely used in advanced aerospace applications due to its hardness and corrosion resistance. Typically, surface roughness measurement for a workpiece is carried out offline post machining, resulting in significant wastage in terms of the workpiece material, tool wear and machining time in case surface roughness is not within the desired limits. Online estimation of surface roughness based on vibration/force/ moment data acquired in real time can help develop better Artificial Intelligence (AI) based predictive modelling frameworks to enable adaptive control of machining processes and advance the integration of intelligent manufacturing systems in an Industry 4.0 ecosystem.

Machine Learning/AI and big-data based techniques are now being increasingly employed for applications such as tool wear estimation1–8 and surface roughness estimation/prediction9–14 in operations such as milling4,5, turning9,11,12 and drilling6,7,13. High quality datasets are essential for building effective AI models for such applications. It is observed from the survey of datasets as summarized in Table 1, that such datasets for tool wear estimation and monitoring15–17 are indeed available. However, it is also observed that the available datasets for surface roughness estimation in turning, on the other hand are too small12,18 to be effectively used for building deep learning/ AI based online surface roughness estimation models. Large datasets for surface roughness estimation, especially with respect to dry turning of difficult-to-machine alloys like Inconel-625 are not widely available as open access. Another issue observed is that in developing economies, especially India, a large number of small and medium scale enterprises are still unable to afford expensive CNC machines and still rely on cheaper legacy machines. This makes it difficult for such users to make effective use of CNC internal signals logged in available datasets16,19,20. Hence, this study focuses on generating a dataset using a legacy lathe and external sensors only.

Dataset Comparison.

This paper presents an Inconel-625 turning dataset collected in a laboratory environment at Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham, Coimbatore-India. The experiments were performed using a legacy lathe (Kirloskar Turnmaster 40). In line with the available literature, this dataset includes the raw vibration4,11,15–18,21,22 data along the x, y and z axes collected using a triaxial accelerometer (Kistler IEPE Triaxial Accelerometer, Model 8763B100BB) as well as the corresponding raw force and moment data collected along the x, y and z axes using a dynamometer4,11,16–18 (Kistler Multicomponent Dynamometer, Model 9257B) collected during the dry turning of Inconel-625. All three process parameters in the turning process, namely, speed, feed and depth of cut have significant impact on the roughness of the machined surface22. Hence, in this study, three speeds, feeds and depths of cut are considered22,23, resulting in 382,189,197 samples in total acquired for these 27 combinations of process parameters. Available datasets log surface roughness values as either Ra11–13,22–24 or Rz10,18. In this study, we measure three surface roughness parameters Ra (µm), Rq (µm) and Rz (µm) for each set at equally spaced points24 on the finished workpiece using a Mitutoyo® Surftest SJ-410 surface roughness tester and record them separately. Since the work reported in this study aims to develop and report a quality experimental dataset to establish empirical relationships between machining parameters and surface roughness for data-driven modelling applications, detailed investigations into cutting mechanisms, characterization of the worn tool16, machined surface morphology, or wear debris analysis is considered beyond the scope of the current experimental design.

Methods

The dataset presented in this study is for measurement of surface roughness in dry turning of Inconel-625 turning dataset. To create this dataset, a four-step methodology, as reported in Abadia et.al.16, has been followed: design of experiments (DOE), experimental setup, data collection, and data synchronisation.

Design of Experiment

The DOE for the proposed dataset was based on the literature14,22,23 and recommended manufacturer settings of tools and workpiece materials. The DOE consists of three distinct combinations of spindle speed n: 400, 630 and 1000 RPM, feed f: 0.05, 0.1, 0.16 mm/rev and depth of cut d: 0.2, 0.4 and 0.6 mm, resulting in 27 sets of experiments, all carried out dry (without lubrication). Turning process

In turning process, a single-point cutting tool (CNMG120408-SR-YG401) was used to remove outer surface material from the rotating work piece to produce cylindrical parts. Workpiece, cutting tool, tool holder & tailstock, machine tool (lathe) are the main elements of the turning process. Three parameters are varied in the experiments to generate the dataset: spindle speed (n, rpm) – how fast the work piece rotates, feed (f, mm/rev) – distance the tool advances per revolution and depth of cut (d, mm) – thickness of material removed in one pass. The combination of low speed, low feed and small depth of cut results in low Material Removal Rate (MRR) and better surface finish (low surface roughness), whereas the combination of high speed, high feed and deeper depth of cut results in high MRR, at the cost of tool life and surface finish. Data acquisition

The data acquisition flow is illustrated in Fig. 7. Signals from the accelerometer and the dynamometer were acquired separately using two data acquisition systems. KiNOVA Lite Portable Data Acquisition system was used for vibration data acquisition along with KiNOVA Acquisition software. Kistler LabAmp Type 5167 A was used for dynamometer data acquisition along with DynoWare data acquisition software. For each set of speed, feed and depth of cut combination, the turning operation was repeated. The data acquired by sensors for each speed-feed-depth of cut combination was recorded and the corresponding surface roughness parameters for each case was measured and recorded separately using a Mitutoyo® SURFTEST SJ-410 surface roughness tester. The specifications of the surface roughness tester used, Mitutoyo® SURFTEST SJ-41027 are provided in the Table 3. Three surface roughness parameters are measured: average roughness (Ra), root mean square roughness (Rq) and Mean peak-to-valley height (Rz). All three parameters are measured in micrometers (µm). In order to ensure better synchronization between the logged data from the accelerometer and the dynamometer, the sampling rate for both sensors was fixed at 10000 samples/second. The readings from accelerometer and the dynamometer along with the respective time stamps were logged as separate comma-separated-value (CSV) files with one file for each speed-feed-depth of cut combination resulting in a total of 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 files each for accelerometer and dynamometer readings. Experiment-wise parameters and raw data collected is summarized in Table 5. Number of samples acquired is = ∑Accelerometer No. of Samples for each signal×3 (i.e. Acc_X, Acc_Y, Acc_Z) + ∑Dynamometer No. of Samples for each signal×6 (i.e. Fx, Fy, Fz, Mx, My, Mz) = 382,189,197 samples in total.

Data synchronization

As can be seen from data acquisition workflow illustrated in Fig. 7, the lathe machine is started first, followed by start of the data acquisition. Immediately afterwards, the turning process is started. The turning process is manually initiated and terminated by an experienced operator based on visual inspection when the turning operation is no longer considered to be safe to continue. The tool is also visually inspected for wear by the operator after each turning process termination, to ensure that the process is not being unduly influenced by cutting tool wear. The accelerometer data acquisition is then manually terminated while the dynamometer data is continued to be logged till the pre-set time limit of 200 seconds. Both accelerometer and dynamometer data are logged with timestamps corresponding to each sample, starting with a timestamp of 0.00 for each dataset. Since data logging for both the sensors are not started or terminated at the same time instant, the accelerometer and dynamometer datasets are not synchronised in time. Therefore, the data is synchronised using the identification of optimal changepoints28,29 and trimming the data accordingly to remove the datapoints outside the selected changepoint window. Force signals along x,y,and z (denoted as Fx, Fy and Fz in this study) axes are considered as references for identification of changepoints as the force signals exhibit significant visible variations in magnitude of the acquired data as the tool comes in contact/ breaks contact with the workpiece. For each speed-feed-depth of cut combination, two furthest changepoints each, are identified for Fx, Fy and Fz (the detailed synchronization algorithm is presented as a separate file along with the dataset30 in the dataset repository within the ‘Code’ folder as Data_Sync_Algorithm.pdf). Optimal changepoints returned for each speed, feed and depth of cut are applied to all the acquired time series resulting in trimmed and synchronized data, as shown in Fig. 8. The red dashed vertical line in Fig. 8 indicates the trimming points. Only the data between these two trimmed points is included in the ‘Synchronized’ folder.

Data Records

The dataset is available at Mendeley Data repository30 with this section being the primary source of information on the availability and content of the data being described.

This Inconel-625 turning dataset comprises data collected for combinations of three spindle speeds (400 rpm, 630 rpm, and 1000 rpm), three feeds (0.05 mm/rev, 0.1 mm/rev, and 0.16 mm/rev), and three depths of cut (0.2 mm, 0.4 mm, and 0.6 mm) resulting in 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 sets of data each, with: (a) a dynamometer: force and moment data logged along x,y and z axes, (b) an accelerometer: vibration data logged along x,y and z axes and (c) surface roughness tester: Ra, Rq and Rz. Accelerometer and dynamometer data are logged with a sampling frequency of 10 KHz. Three surface roughness values are measured (Ra, Rq and Rz) at three randomly selected points on the machined workpiece and recorded.

Data folder organization is presented in Fig. 9. The root folder named ‘Inconel-625 Dataset’ contains two subfolders: ‘Unsynchronized’ and ‘Synchronized’. Each of these subfolders has three subfolders labelled ‘Dynamometer’, ‘Accelerometer’ and ‘Roughness’. Each of these subfolders contain 27 data files logged in.csv file format. The.csv file format was chosen as it is easily readable by most data processing software as well as programming languages like MATLAB and Python.

Technical Validation

To verify the usability of data, an analysis of the relationship between the logged data and measured surface roughness was carried out. Initially, visual inspection of the logged accelerometer and dynamometer time series data was carried out. Since data logging is started (for all the experiments) before the tool makes contact with the workpiece and is stopped manually (for accelerometer data) or via software (for dynamometer data) after the tool breaks contact with the workpiece at the end of the experiments, as described in ‘Data Acquisition’ section above, data synchronization is needed to identify the experiment start and end points. This is carried out via the algorithm described in the ‘Data Synchronization’ section above. The algorithm has been implemented in MATLAB with the two most important change points representing the experiment start and end points respectively, identified using the findchangepts function in MATLAB (represented as function fnFindChangePoints() in the algorithm above). Once the data has been synchronised, following statistical features15,16,31 are extracted for each acquired signal for each experiment: Mean, Median, Mode, Standard Deviation (Std), Skewness (Skew), Range, Kurtosis, Maximum (Max), Minimum (Min) and Inter-Quartile Range (IQR). Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients (as employed in Abadia et.al.16) of these features with the measured roughness parameters Ra, Rq and Rz is then computed. Results are presented in Table 7. Non-zero positive and negative relationships between features and the measured roughness values were observed (ranging between −0.465 to 0.621), in line with the existing literature16,32. Visualization of the correlations is illustrated in Fig. 11 using radar plots. In Fig. 11, the subplots (a), (b) and (c) represent the Pearson correlation coefficients while the subplots (d), (e) and (f) represent the Spearman correlation coefficients for Ra Rq and Rz, respectively.
