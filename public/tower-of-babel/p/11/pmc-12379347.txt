Perceived organizational support on compassion fatigue among kindergarten teachers: a chain mediation model analysis of professional identity and emotional intelligence
Authors: Ge Cai, Liqiang Guo, Xiaoning Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKindergarten teachers are vulnerable to compassion fatigue, which threatens their physical and mental health. Drawing upon the compassion fatigue model, this study aims to explore the impact of perceived organizational support (POS) on compassion fatigue, while examining the mediating effects of professional identity and emotional intelligence in this relationship.MethodsA convenience sampling method was used to conduct a questionnaire survey with 1104 kindergarten teachers from Henan, Shaanxi, Hubei, and Anhui Provinces in China. This study utilized a general information questionnaire, a POS scale, a professional identity scale, an emotional intelligence scale, and a compassion fatigue scale to construct and test a chain mediation model.ResultsThe results showed that (1) POS, professional identity, emotional intelligence, and compassion fatigue, were significantly correlated in pairs, and (2) POS can not only directly affect compassion fatigue, but also affect compassion fatigue through three indirect effects, namely, the separate mediating effect of professional identity and emotional intelligence (mediating effect size − 0.16, -0.24, respectively), and the chain mediating effect of professional identity → emotional intelligence (mediating effect size − 0.09).ConclusionThis study reveals that POS is negatively associated with compassion fatigue, and professional identity and emotional intelligence play a chain mediating role in this relationship. Kindergarten administrators should focus on creating supportive organizational environments, enhancing teachers’ professional identity, and improve their emotional intelligence to alleviate compassion fatigue among kindergarten teachers.

Introduction

It is undeniable that kindergarten teachers play a pivotal role in early childhood education. As part of the efforts to implement the National Plan for Medium- and Long-Term Education Reform and Development (2010–2035), Chinese governments have been actively expanding the workforce of kindergarten teachers. For instance, the number of kindergarten teachers in China has grown from 2.43 million in 2017 to 3.19 million in 2021. However, during the same period, the population of age-eligible children (aged 3–6 years) surged from 25.49 million to 48.05 million [1]. This significant disparity has resulted in high child-to-teacher ratios, placing immense pressure on kindergarten teachers. Moreover, children often require constant empathy and support from teachers in their daily interactions [2]. Therefore, kindergarten teachers are vulnerable to compassion fatigue, which is defined as the physical and psychological fatigue and dysfunction caused by persistent empathic stress on the basis of empathy and exposure to traumatic situations [3]. A study by Li et al. [4] further revealed that kindergarten teachers in China to be under long-term pressure, with more than 50% of them experiencing burnout, which increases their risk of developing compassion fatigue. Compassion fatigue has been shown to be strongly associated with a range of physical symptoms (e.g. headaches, difficulty falling asleep and nightmares) [3], cognitive symptoms (e.g. intrusive thoughts, difficulty concentrating) [3], emotional symptoms (e.g., generalized anxiety, irritability, and lack of empathy) [5], and behavioral symptoms (e.g. drug abuse, absenteeism, and attrition) [6]. The adverse effects may not only harm teachers’ physical and mental health but also compromise the quality of education provided to young children. Therefore, uncovering the mechanisms underlying kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue and exploring potential intervention strategies hold significant importance for enhancing the professional mental health of preschool educators and elevating the overall quality of early childhood education.

Literature review and hypotheses development

Perceived organizational support and compassion fatigue

Previous studies have extensively explored the influencing factors of compassion fatigue [7, 8]. For example, Yang and Kim’s review of the relevant research among nurses indicated that the factors affecting compassion fatigue were personal factors such as age, education background, work-related factors such as caring for trauma patients, work hours, psychological factors such as work stress, burnout, and support/coping factors such as organizational support, and coping resources [9]. Although organizational support has been proven to be a protective factor against compassion fatigue, this relationship has not yet been explored among kindergarten teachers. Moreover, there are significant differences in the job characteristics between nurses and kindergarten teachers. These disparities limit our understanding of the multifaceted nature of kindergarten teacher compassion fatigue and hinders the development of effective interventions. Therefore, it is still worth exploring whether the organizational support perceived by kindergarten teachers would influence their empathy fatigue.

The compassion fatigue model [10] emphasizes that it is not empathy that puts the individual at risk of developing compassion fatigue, but rather the individual’s response to personal distress, inadequate positive feedback, and lack of resources. Accordingly, enriching kindergarten teachers’ resources could help enhance their empathy and humanistic care abilities, thereby alleviating compassion fatigue. The job demands-resources (JD-R) model elucidates the dualistic impact of work on employees through aspects: job demands and job resources. Specifically, job resources encompass protective factors that facilitate goal achievement and personal growth, such as social resources and psychological resources [11]. Perceived organizational support (POS) is regarded as an important job resource, which is characterized as employees’ perception of valuing their contributions and personal well-being [12]. When employees feel supported, trusted and recognized by the organization, they are motivated to work harder and experience less negative affect. Previous studies have demonstrated that POS serves as a significant and positive predictor of teachers’ well-being [13] and job satisfaction [14], while showing significant negative correlations with emotional exhaustion [15] and work stress [13]. A significant negative correlation was observed between the sense of organizational support and compassion fatigue in a survey on Chinese nurses [16]. Miller and Unruh’s [17] study indicated that increasing POS would increase public safety workers’ compassion satisfaction and decrease burnout and secondary traumatic stress. Moreover, adequate organizational support and resource allocation have been shown to effectively mitigate the risk of compassion fatigue among teachers [18]. Therefore, POS may also be a significant psychological resource for kindergarten teachers, which can alleviate their compassion fatigue. Taken together, we propose the hypothesis 1:

H1: POS is negatively related to kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue.

The mediating role of professional identity

Professional identity represents an individual’s internalized acceptance of their occupational role, encompassing positive affective perceptions and evaluations of their professional purpose and societal value, ultimately reflecting one’s cognitive and emotional alignment with their vocational role [19]. Drawing upon social identity theory (SIT), when organizations provide ample emotional care and material support, employees would experience a sense of recognition and respect, enabling them to perceive the emotional and meaningful values derived from their professional roles, thereby fostering employees’ strong professional identity [20]. Accordingly, a high level of POS may lead to a stronger professional identity for kindergarten teachers. Moreover, research has demonstrated that targeted professional identity development programs can significantly reduce employee burnout. Shuai et al. [21] surveyed 467 nursing interns and held that professional identity is negatively associated with compassion fatigue. Furthermore, Chen et al. [22] observed a significant and negative association between kindergarten teachers’ professional identity and compassion fatigue. Taken together, POS would positively influence kindergarten teachers’ professional identity, and the stronger the professional identity, the lower the level of compassion fatigue. Therefore, the hypothesis 2 is developed:

H2: Professional identity mediates the relationship between POS and kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue.

The mediating role of emotional intelligence

Mayer and Salovey [23] conceptualized emotional intelligence as “the ability to accurately appraise, and express emotions; the ability to access and/or generate feelings when they assist thought; the ability to comprehend emotions and emotional knowledge; and the ability to effectively regulate emotional to foster emotional and intellectual growth”. Several studies have shown a significant and positive correlation between POS and employees’ emotional intelligence. Akhtar et al. [24] found that POS is significantly and positively associated with emotional intelligence among employees in telecom industry. Similarly, a study of 279 Chinese hotel employees indicated a significant positive association between POS and emotional intelligence [25]. Although these studies were not directly targeted at kindergarten teachers, they still suggest that a satisfactory POS can help promote the emotional intelligence of employees. We will investigate this link among kindergarten teachers in the current study. In addition, previous research has widely examined the significance of emotional intelligence within teaching and educational contexts. For example, Karakuş [26] suggested that emotional intelligence can significantly reduce teachers’ burnout and depression, and the relieving effect on male teachers’ stress is greater than that on female teachers’ stress. A meta-analysis conducted by Mérida-López and Extremera [27] pointed out that emotional intelligence was significantly and negatively correlated with teacher burnout, suggesting that teachers with high-perceived emotional intelligence are likely to feel less emotional exhaustion, experience low levels of depersonalization and have a high sense of self-confidence and personal accomplishment. Hence, emotional intelligence may act as an essential protective factor against compassion fatigue. In sum, POS may exert a positive effect on kindergarten teachers’ emotional intelligence, which in turn influence their compassion fatigue, and the hypothesis 3 is developed as follows:

H3: Emotional intelligence mediates the relationship between POS and kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue.

The chain mediating effect of professional identity and emotional intelligence

In addition, scholarly consensus posits that emotional intelligence is an intrinsic ability that can be improved through targeted learning interventions and accumulating experience [28, 29]. The cognitive-behavioral theory emphasis that cognition, emotions, and behaviors interact and coordinate with one another, and cognition playing a pivotal coordinating role that significantly influences problem-solving and behavioral modification [30]. Accordingly, when individuals have a strong sense of identification with their profession, they are more likely to experience positive emotions, which can promote the development of emotional intelligence. In fact, a recent study has shown that a higher level of emotional intelligence could lead to a higher level of professional identity, and career construction played a mediating role in this association [31]. Therefore, kindergarten teachers’ professional identity may be positively correlated with emotional intelligence. In summary, we propose the exploratory hypothesis 4:

H4: Professional identity and emotional intelligence jointly mediate the relationship between POS and kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue in a chain mediation model.

The present study

Taking together the above theoretical foundations and research findings, higher levels of POS may enhance the professional identity of kindergarten teachers, thereby strengthening their emotional intelligence, ultimately mitigating their compassion fatigue. Consequently, the current study establishes a chain mediation model to explore the relationships among POS, professional identity, emotional intelligence, and compassion fatigue among kindergarten teachers. Methods

Participants

Using random sampling and convenience sampling methods, the survey of kindergarten teachers was conducted from September to November 2024 in several provinces in China, including Henan Province, Shaanxi Province, Hubei Province, and Anhui Province. An online survey was distributed to 1300 kindergarten teachers via a professional data collection software (https://www.wjx.cn/). The survey included the purpose of the study, informed consent, confidentiality of responses, individual demographic information, and questionnaires measuring POS, professional identity, emotional intelligence, and compassion fatigue.

We conducted a preliminary sorting of the questionnaire, eliminating a total of 196 invalid questionnaires with incomplete answers, regular responses, short or long response times. In the end, we obtained a total of 1104 valid responses, with a response rate of 84.92%. All participants had an average age of 28.73 years (SD = 8.67). Among them, 20 were male (1.81%) and 1,084 were female (98.19%). It should be noted that this seemingly extreme gender ratio for male and female is actually in line with the distribution characteristics of the gender ratio among kindergarten teachers in China. According to the latest educational statistics released by the Ministry of Education of the People’s Republic of China, the total number of staff in kindergartens is 5,514,369, among which there are 5,105,819 females, accounting for 92.59% [32]. Regarding teaching experience, 251 had 1–2 years (22.74%), 632 had 3–5 years (57.25%), 159 had 6–10 years (14.40%), and 62 had over 11 years (5.62%) of teaching experience. Concerning the areas, 698 teachers (63.22%) were from urban areas, 406 (36.78%) from rural areas. In terms of titles, 431 (39.04%) did not have titles, 327 (29.62%) had secondary titles, 264 (23.91%) had primary titles, 82 (7.43%) had senior titles.

The materials and research procedures performed in this study involving human participants conform to the guidelines of the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards, and they were approved by the Ethics Committee for Scientific Research at the authors’ institution (number LNU20240625074). Furthermore, before conducting the surveys, the informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. After completing the survey, the participants can take part in the lottery to obtain rewards.

Measures

All measures were firstly adapted to be more applicable to fit the job context of kindergarten teachers. Then, we invited several experts to assess all the items to ensure the content validity of these measures. Finally, the measures for the formal survey were formed.

Perceived organizational support

To measure kindergarten teachers’ POS, we adapted the nurses’ POS scale revised by Zuo et al. [33] to form the kindergarten teachers’ POS scale. This scale includes two dimensions: emotional support and instrumental support, with a total of 13 items. These items were assessed on a five-point Likert scale whose response choices ranged from “1 = completely inconsistent” to “5 = completely consistent”. The higher the total score, the higher the POS perceived by kindergarten teachers. The CFA showed good structural validity: χ2/df = 4.91, GFI = 0.90, CFI = 0.86, TLI = 0.89, RMSEA = 0.06 and SRMR = 0.05. The Cronbach’s α for emotional support and instrumental support were 0.86 and 0.91, respectively. The Cronbach’s α of the entire scale was 0.85.

Professional identity

To measure kindergarten teachers’ professional identity, we employed the professional identity scale developed by Wei et al. [34] and modified the items to be more applicable to kindergarten teachers. The scale consists of 18 items, including four dimensions: role values, occupational behavioral tendencies, occupational values and occupational belonging. The participants responded to the items on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from “1 = completely inconsistent” to “5 = completely consistent”. The higher the score, the higher the teacher’s professional identity. In this study, the scale fit was good: The results of CFA for the scale indicated a good model fit: χ2/df = 5.89, GFI = 0.85, CFI = 0.87, TLI = 0.83, RMSEA = 0.08 and SRMR = 0.07. The Cronbach’s α of the four dimensions ranged between 0.81 and 0.85. The Cronbach’s α of the entire scale was 0.83.

Emotional intelligence

A 16-item scale developed by Wong and Law [35] was used to measure kindergarten teachers’ emotional intelligence, and we adapted the queries to better match their job context. The scale is divided into four dimensions, namely self-emotional evaluation (4 items; e.g., “I know my emotions well”), emotional evaluation of others (4 items; e.g., “I have a strong ability to observe other people’s emotions”), self-emotional regulation (4 items; e.g., “I am very able to control my emotions”) and emotional use (4 items; e.g., “I am a person who can encourage myself”). This scale was scored on a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from “1 = strongly disagree” to “7 = strongly agree”. Higher scores indicated higher levels of emotional intelligence. The goodness of fit ( χ2/df = 4.67, GFI = 0.90, CFI = 0.88, TLI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.05 and SRMR = 0.04) of CFA in the present study demonstrated an adequate structural validity for this scale. The Cronbach’s α of the four dimensions ranged between 0.82 and 0.89. The Cronbach’s α of this scale was 0.86 in this study.

Compassion fatigue

To capture kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue, we used the Chinese version of the Compassion Fatigue Short Scale (C-CF Short Scale) revised by Sun et al. [36] based on the CF Short Scale [37]. This revised scale is widely used in Chinese cultural context. The scale consists of 13 questions that measure two aspects of compassion fatigue among kindergarten teachers: job burnout (8 items) and secondary stress (5 items). Participants responded to the items on a ten-point Likert scale ranging from “1 = rarely/never” to “10 = very often”, with higher scores indicating higher levels of safety attitude. Specifically, we modified the items to better apply to kindergarten teachers’ work context. A sample item is, “I frequently feel weak, tired, or rundown because of my work as a kindergarten teacher.” The results of CFA in this study showed this scale had a good structural validity : χ2/df = 4.89, GFI = 0.87, CFI = 0.91, TLI = 0.88, RMSEA = 0.07 and SRMR = 0.06. The Cronbach’s α for job burnout and secondary stress were 0.82 and 0.86, respectively. The Cronbach’s α of the entire scale was 0.83.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using AMOS 25.0 and SPSS 26.0 statistical software. The analysis process included descriptive statistics, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), correlation analysis, and mediation analysis. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to explore the relationships between POS, professional identity, emotional intelligence, and compassion among kindergarten teachers. Additionally, the chain mediation effects of professional identity and emotional intelligence within these relationships were examined.

To assess multicollinearity, we calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) for all the predictor variables. The results showed that all the VIF values were below 5 (the highest VIF = 4.6, emotional intelligence), indicating that there was no severe multicollinearity problem.

Results

Common method bias test

The reliance on single-source data in the current study may introduce the risk of common method bias (CMB), a methodological concern well-documented in organizational research [38]. To asses this, we employed two commonly reported methods [38, 39]. First, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted through exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to examine the potential presence of CMB. EFA was performed on the measurement items encompassing all four constructs under investigation: POS, professional identity, emotional intelligence, and compassion fatigue. The results showed that there are 10 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the variance explained by the first factor accounted for 18.62% of the total variance, which is lower than the critical threshold of 40%. Second, a latent CMB test was implemented using AMOS 26.0. We computed a model that contains an unmeasured latent method factor, where the paths of each item are constrained to unity to parcel out the error variance. The analysis yielded a method variance estimate of 4.68%, suggesting that CMB accounted for a small proportion of the total variance in the measurement model. Accordingly, there is no significant CMB effect in this study.

Preliminary descriptive analyses

Table 2 presents the means, standard deviation and intercorrelation coefficients for the major research variables, while controlling for gender, age, teaching experience, regions, and titles. As can be seen, POS was significantly and negatively correlated with compassion fatigue (r = -0.36, p < 0.001), and significantly and positively correlated with professional identity (r = 0.41, p < 0.001) and emotional intelligence (r = 0.28, p < 0.001). Both professional identity and emotional intelligence were significantly and negatively correlated with compassion fatigue (r = -0.35, p < 0.001; r = -0.24, p < 0.001). Professional identity was found to be significantly and positively correlated with emotional intelligence (r = 0.53, p < 0.001).

Mediation effect analysis

A structural equation model was constructed in AMOS 26.0 to examine the mediation effect of professional identity and emotional intelligence on the relationship between POS and compassion fatigue. First, we examined the relationship between POS and compassion fatigue, and the results showed that the model fits well: χ2/df = 3.57, CFI = 0.95, GFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.94, RMSEA = 0.07, SRMR = 0.04. POS was significantly and negatively associated with compassion fatigue (β = -0.78, t = 16.75, p < 0.001), H1 was supported. Then, we incorporated professional identity and emotional intelligence to constructed a chain mediation model. The results showed that the model has a good fitness: χ2/df = 3.98, CFI = 0.93, GFI = 0.91, TLI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.08, SRMR = 0.06. As shown in Fig. 2, POS was significantly and negatively related to compassion fatigue (β = -0.42, t = 13.48, p < 0.001); POS was significantly and positively related to professional identity (β = 0.51, t = 14.31, p < 0.001), and professional identity was significantly and negatively associated with compassion fatigue (β = -0.32, t = 11.85, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that professional identity plays a mediating role between POS and kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue, which supports H2. Furthermore, POS was found to be positively related to emotional intelligence (β = 0.63, t = 22.37, p < 0.001), which in turn was negatively related to compassion fatigue (β =-0.38, t = 15.62, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that emotional intelligence mediated the relationship between POS and kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue, thus H3 is supported. In addition, professional identity was significantly and positively associated with emotional intelligence (β = 0.46, t = 20.91, p < 0.001). In sum, these findings indicate that POS can not only affect compassion fatigue through professional identity and emotional intelligence respectively, but also through the chain mediation role of professional identity → emotional intelligence. The chain mediation model is established, thus supporting H4. The chain-mediated model with standardized pathway coefficients is shown in Fig. 2.

In order to further test the significance of the above mediation effects, the bias-corrected percentile bootstrapping method was used, and 95% confidence intervals were computed with 5000 random samples. If the 95% confidence intervals did not contain zero, the mediating effect was significant [41]. After incorporating the mediators of professional identity and emotional intelligence, there are three pathways through which POS affects compassion fatigue. Table 3 shows the mediation effect values and effect sizes for each pathway. As can be seen, professional identity (95% CI [-0.22, -0.05]) and emotional intelligence (95% CI [-0.25, -0.02]) play a significant mediating role between POS and compassion fatigue, respectively. Furthermore, professional identity → emotional intelligence plays a chain mediating role in the association between POS and compassion fatigue (95% CI [-0.16, -0.03]). The total effect of POS on compassion fatigue of kindergarten teachers was − 0.74, the direct effect was − 0.25, and the total indirect effect was − 0.49.

Discussion

The current study aims to explore the influence mechanism of POS on compassion fatigue of kindergarten teachers. Specifically, it empirically examines the chain mediation effects of professional identity and emotional intelligence within this framework. The results demonstrated that POS directly affects compassion fatigue among kindergarten teachers, and also exerts an indirect effect through professional identity and emotional intelligence. These factors contribute to the understanding of the underlying mechanisms of compassion fatigue in the context of the kindergarten setting. The following chapters provide a detailed discussion of the research findings and give the corresponding practical implications.

Perceived organizational support on compassion fatigue among kindergarten teachers

This study revealed that POS was negatively associated with kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue, which confirmed hypothesis 1. This finding is congruent with the previous studies [16, 17] suggesting that individuals with a higher level of organizational support perceive less compassion fatigue. Perceived organizational support refers to early childhood teachers’ perception of the extent to which kindergartens value and support them, which can significantly influence their attitudes and behaviors, especially in caring for young children. Rhoades and Eisenberger [42] highlight that employees who perceive high quality of organizational support are more likely to develop a heightened sense of trust toward the organization, which in turn leads to enhanced job performance. Furthermore, when an organization provides individuals with certain benefits and resources, it creates reciprocal obligations that motivate individuals to reciprocate with corresponding organizational rewards [43]. Therefore, when kindergarten teachers perceive organizational support, they are motivated to mobilize various resources to actively cope with work-related stress, thereby mitigating the occurrence and detrimental effects of compassion fatigue. Based on this finding, administrators should prioritize kindergarten teachers’ job needs and strive to cultivate a supportive organizational climate to enhance their sense of organizational support. For instance, trying a service-oriented leadership mechanism in kindergarten management, putting teachers’ individual interests and needs at the forefront, encouraging them to pursue their goals while providing necessary assistance. In addition, kindergartens should increase teachers’ participation in institutional decision-making processes by allowing them to contribute to policy decisions, voice their suggestions, and providing support based on their input.

The mediating role of professional identity and emotional intelligence in the relationship between POS and compassion fatigue among kindergarten teachers

In accordance with hypothesis 2, this study demonstrated that professional identity mediated the relationship between POS and compassion fatigue, accounting for 32.7% of the total indirect effect. That is, kindergarten teachers who experience a high level of POS are more likely to report high levels of professional identity, which in turn leads to a decrease in compassion fatigue. POS helps to foster the dependency and responsibility between employees and their organizations, and thus kindergarten teachers who feel well-supported by their kindergarten institutions are more likely to respond with a stronger professional identity [20]. Kindergarten teachers’ professional identity represents their recognition and understanding of job content, nature, personal value and social value, and it significantly influences teachers’ job satisfaction [44] and work passion [45]. Based on the social identity theory, individual emotions and thinking are affected by the identification degree with their group [20]. Hence, when kindergarten teachers develop strong professional identification and maintain positive attitudes toward their teaching career, they demonstrate greater emotional investment in their work with young children, consequently reducing their vulnerability to compassion fatigue. According to these research findings, kindergartens should also develop and implement training programs aimed at strengthening kindergarten teachers’ professional identity. For example, group psychological counseling can be adopted to organize teachers to write and share key educational event (e.g., the most touching teaching moments), thereby guiding them to recognize the professional meaning. In addition, digital career growth profiles for teachers can be developed, which can visually present the professional development process of teachers and enhance their sense of professional achievement. Finally, professional guidance from experienced teachers and education experts can be combined to conduct in-depth dialogues on teacher professional values and strengthen their sense of professional identity.

The current study also verified the mediating role of emotional intelligence in the association between POS and compassion fatigue (hypothesis 3), which accounted for 48.98% of the total indirect effects, suggesting that POS not only directly affect compassion fatigue, but also indirectly affect compassion fatigue by influencing emotional intelligence in kindergarten teachers. Highly supportive kindergarten teachers could feel the assistance and support from the organization in work, life and emotion domains, which will enrich their psychological resources and thereby improving their abilities to recognize and express emotions, understand and apply emotions, as well as regulate and utilize emotions [41]. In other words, high-quality organizational support can promote the emotional intelligence of kindergarten teachers. Individuals with higher emotional intelligence are better able to understand and control their own emotions, thereby effectively dealing with challenges and stress in their work [26, 27]. Accordingly, high-level of emotional intelligence will help alleviate kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue. In summary, high-level POS can enhance the emotional intelligence of kindergarten teachers, thereby reducing their compassion fatigue. Based on these findings, in addition to providing adequate organizational support, managers should also pay attention to cultivate kindergarten teachers’ emotional intelligence. Specifically, teachers can first be taught to identify stress signals through techniques such as body scanning, thereby enhancing their ability to perceive emotions. Secondly, simulation scenario training methods can be employed to conduct training on responding to children’s emotional demands, thereby enhancing teachers’ emotional understanding ability. Furthermore, cognitive reappraisal training for teachers can be carried out to strengthen their emotional regulation skills. Finally, role-playing training in high-demand kindergarten scenarios can be conducted to enhance teachers’ ability to apply emotions. Through these modular courses of training, it will help comprehensively improve the emotional intelligence of kindergarten teachers.

The present study found that professional identity and emotional intelligence play a chain mediating role between kindergarten teachers’ POS and compassion fatigue (confirming hypothesis 4), accounting for 18.37% of the total indirect effect, indicating that POS can directly influence compassion fatigue of kindergarten teachers, as well as indirectly affect compassion fatigue through professional identity and emotional intelligence. Dasan et al. [46] claimed that organizational support is the key to prevent and relieve work-related stress and compassion fatigue. A heightened perception of organizational support can enrich employees’ psychological resources, strengthen their job responsibility, and effectively mitigate negative emotions stemming from job demands, so that they can devote themselves to work in a more positive emotional state, thus reducing compassion fatigue. Specifically, high-quality organizational support can enhance kindergarten teachers’ professional identity, which encompasses a clear understanding and attitude disposition towards professional cognition, professional needs, professional emotions and professional will, and helps to cultivate high levels of emotional intelligence, enabling teachers to better navigate professional challenges and pressures. Furthermore, the high level of support from the organization also helps to cultivate teachers’ profound understanding for the significance of early childhood education, as well as their strong sense of self-motivation and professional dedication. Such teachers would demonstrate sustained vitality and focus, leading to more active engagement in their work and consequently reducing their vulnerability to compassion fatigue.

In addition, regarding the practical implications we proposed to alleviate compassion fatigue, namely creating supportive environments, conducting courses to enhance professional identity, and implementing emotional intelligence training. What we want to emphasize is that although implementing these interventions requires certain resource investment, in essence, it does not necessarily require extremely expensive or scarce resources. For instance, the establishment of a supportive environment can be gradually achieved through measures such as leadership advocacy, adjustment of communication mechanisms, and establishment of mutual support groups. Furthermore, the direct and indirect benefits of implementing the interventions may far outweigh the costs, which will help alleviate the compassion fatigue of kindergarten teachers, reduce their burnout and turnover rates, enhance their well-being and job satisfaction, and ultimately improve the quality of early childhood education. And these benefits are likely to be even more significant in the long run.

Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the research of compassion fatigue in the following aspects. Firstly, this research expands the application of compassion fatigue model [10]. Although this model has been widely used in the literature related to healthcare domain, it has not been fully utilized in the research on teachers’ mental health, especially for kindergarten teachers. Research in the field of healthcare typically considers trauma exposure as a key triggering factor for individual compassion fatigue, while our study emphasizes the significant influence of long-term emotional stressors in educational contexts, such as the daily empathy and support required to meet the needs of young children, in inducing compassion fatigue. Based on this, our study reveals that individual psychological resources, namely perceived organizational support, h was significantly and negatively related to compassion fatigue, which breaks through the limitations of traditional models that tend to focus on individual traits. Therefore, this study has opened up a new avenue for research on teachers’ mental health, using this model to explore various influencing factors related to compassion fatigue.

Secondly, although previous studies have linked organizational support to compassion fatigue, such as in nurses [9], we extend this linkage to kindergarten teachers and carry a unique contribution by exploring the chain mediating mechanism of POS on compassion fatigue. Our findings demonstrate that professional identity and emotional intelligence play a chain-mediating role between kindergarten teachers’ POS and compassion fatigue. Support from the organization is an important psychological resource for kindergarten teachers and is directly negatively correlated with compassion fatigue. Furthermore, our research also indicates that a high level of POS can enhance individuals’ sense of identity with their work, promote positive emotional states, and ultimately alleviate compassion fatigue. These findings suggest the potential of integrating relevant theories such as the compassion fatigue model, organizational support theory, and social identity theory to probe the influence mechanism of compassion fatigue, which also provides some new avenues for future research.

Limitations and future research direction

Several limitations need to be considered when interpreting the findings of our study in specific context, which also provide avenues for future research. First, the data collected in the study were cross-sectional in nature, and thus we cannot make causal inferences regarding the linkages between the research variables. Future longitudinal design studies with differing time frames could be conducted to further shed light on the dynamic nature of the relationships between POS, professional identity, emotional intelligence, and compassion fatigue. In addition, this study used a convenience random sampling method to investigate kindergarten teachers in four provinces of China, lacking control over the nested data structure. Therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing the results of the study. Future research can adopt more rigorous sampling methods, such as stratified random sampling and cluster random sampling, to conduct large-scale sampling from various kindergartens in different regions, in order to enhance the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Second, while our study found professional identity and emotional intelligence mediate the relationship between POS and compassion fatigue, some other potential mediators, such as resilience, job satisfaction, and professional commitment could be examined in future research. Moreover, some potential moderators, such as personality traits, and kindergarten climate, which could be also explored to uncover the boundary conditions of POS affecting compassion fatigue. Third, given that our study was conducted in Chinese kindergarten work context and women made up the majority of participants, it should be prudent about generalizing the findings to other job contexts. Future studies should extend the current research framework across diverse occupations, organizations, industries, and cultures to establish the generalizability of our findings. Fourth, this study used Likert scales with different points (5-point, 7-point, 10 point) to measure different constructs. Although we relied on standardized solutions for interpretation, this difference could still theoretically introduce subtle biases, especially when comparing the means or absolute variance of different latent variables. Future studies, could consider using a unified scoring method for all core constructs to further enhance the consistency of measurement and the comparability of results.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study has contributed much to deepen the understanding of the relationship between perceived organizational support, professional identity, emotional intelligence, and compassion fatigue, within the context of kindergarten teachers. The findings indicate that perceived organizational support can significantly mitigate compassion fatigue, and professional identity and emotional intelligence play a chain-mediating role in this relationship. In kindergarten management practices, administrators can help alleviate kindergarten teachers’ compassion fatigue by creating supportive organizational environments, developing targeted programs to enhance professional identity, and implementing interventions to improve emotional intelligence.
