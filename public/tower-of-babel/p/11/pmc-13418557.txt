Understanding the Role of Communication in Deprescribing Behavior Change
Authors: Terri R Fried, Danijela Gnjidic, Parag Goyal, Ariel R Green, Sarah N Hilmer, Sandra Ko, Marcia Mecca, Carmen E Reyes, Nancy L Schoenborn, Ranjit Singh, Richard Street, Justin Turner, Robert G Wahler, Jinjiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe use of behavior change models to conceptualize deprescribing provides an opportunity to explore the components of communication needed to overcome the many barriers to deprescribing.MethodsConsensus development working group composed of care partner stakeholders and international experts in geriatrics, nursing, pharmacology, communication, and community outreach. The goal of the working group was to create a framework for the communication required among patients, care partners, and clinicians in the ambulatory setting to achieve the behavior of shared decision making about medication appropriateness and deprescribing. The COM‐B (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation—Behavior) model provided an apt framework for characterizing deprescribing communication.ResultsEach component of the model requires specific communication skills, modes, and/or content. Capability requires clinician skills including elicitation of patient/care partner concerns and patient/care partner skills including self‐efficacy for raising medication questions and concerns. This facilitates a shared understanding of constructs that inform communication content, including medication benefits and harms and the “how‐to” of deprescribing. Opportunity requires the allocation of time during usual care or the creation of designated visits for communication. Motivation requires communication, such as audit and feedback directed at clinicians, and testimonials directed at patients and care partners, that encourages evaluation of medication appropriateness, increases awareness of potential problems, and overcomes clinical inertia.ConclusionsThe application of a behavioral health model to deprescribing communication highlights the importance of addressing capability, opportunity, and motivation in behavior change. Developing communication strategies that address these three components may enhance the effectiveness of deprescribing interventions.Keywords: communication, conceptual framework development, deprescribing, polypharmacy

Introduction

Medications are one of the geriatric 5 M's, which are five key areas in the care of older adults [1], with medication review and optimization as essential components of comprehensive care delivery to older persons [2]. Despite this emphasis, medication management in older adults is often complicated by polypharmacy. The prevalence of polypharmacy, commonly defined as taking at least five medications, increases with age [3]. Polypharmacy is associated with a range of adverse outcomes and places the older person at risk for the receipt of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) [3], which are medications that do not have benefit, have harms that outweigh benefits and/or are not aligned with an individual's treatment goals. Deprescribing is the supervised, patient‐centered evidence‐based strategy of identifying and removing PIMs [4]. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis of deprescribing trials conducted among community‐dwelling adults found that deprescribing interventions were associated with a small but significant decrease in the number of medications prescribed but no change in the proportion of patients prescribed one or more PIM [5].

Multiple barriers to deprescribing have been identified that pose substantial challenges to successful and sustainable deprescribing and may contribute to the limited overall effect of interventions. These barriers occur at the level of the clinician, including lack of awareness of medication problems and clinical inertia; at the level of patient/care partner, including inaccurate medication beliefs and concerns about worsening health with deprescribing, and at the level of the health system, with limited opportunities to conduct medication reviews and deprescribing [6, 7]. Triadic barriers, including disagreements among clinicians, patients, and care partners about the benefits and harms of specific medications [8], indicate the role for communication in overcoming barriers. These barriers are not unique to deprescribing, as similar barriers may affect other health behaviors. Viewed through the conceptual lens of behavior change, successful deprescribing shares additional features with other health behaviors, such as patient and clinician self‐efficacy and cues to action [9]. Because interventions based on behavior change models have proven effective in changing a range of health‐related behaviors [10], applying these concepts to deprescribing may present a promising avenue for designing more effective interventions.

Recognizing the importance of communication to the deprescribing process, the United States Deprescribing Network (USDeN) Communications Working Group (hereafter referred to as “the Working Group”) was assembled. The Working Group initially developed a framework describing the scope of deprescribing communication, including site and context in which communication occurs, communication methods, content, providers, and recipients [11] A final deprescribing step in the framework was the communication occurring among clinician, patient, and care partner resulting in shared decision making about the benefits and harms of medications in the context of the patient's treatment goals [12]. In the current project, the Working Group examined this communication in greater detail to inform future research on contributors to successful deprescribing communication and to guide the development and testing of interventions. As the work progressed, the relationship between these communication components and the components of health behavioral models emerged, and the Working Group created a sample framework for mapping the components of deprescribing communication onto behavior change models.

Methods

The Communications Working Group was composed of an international group of investigators in geriatrics, pharmacy and pharmacology, nursing, and stakeholders with expertise in community engagement and health behavior change drawn from the USDeN Stakeholder Engagement Council. The Working Group employed a consensus development method for framework construction [13], which was grounded in systematic re‐examination of the manuscripts identified in our prior literature review [11].

Through this iterative process, the Working Group created a framework to characterize the communication required in an ambulatory clinical interaction among clinicians, patients, and care partners. This process led to the insight that the initial goal of communication is to prepare patients and clinicians and to increase their readiness to engage in deprescribing. The Working Group reflected that this process of preparation leading to action was represented in models of behavior change, which posit that increasing knowledge, enhancing motivation, and building self‐efficacy are key determinants of readiness for behavior change [14, 15].

The group explored how to map deprescribing onto these behavior change models. After reviewing various models, the group selected the COM‐B (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation—Behavior) model for this mapping. The COM‐B was developed as a “behavior system” to guide development of interventions [15] The COM‐B system specifies that capability, motivation, and opportunity must all be present for a given behavior to occur. “Capability” refers to the knowledge, skills, and abilities to engage in a behavior. “Opportunity” refers to the external factors that make the execution of the behavior possible. “Motivation” refers to the internal processes that influence the desire to engage in the behavior.

Using the COM‐B as the organizing structure, the Working Group used the consensus process to map communication components required to achieve capability, opportunity, and motivation. The Working Group also used this process to develop a set of intermediate measures to facilitate the study of deprescribing interventions based on the COM‐B.

Results

Figure 1 presents a proposed framework for deprescribing communication in clinical encounters based on the COM‐B. The framework considers communication specific to the clinician, communication specific to patients and their care partners, and communication that is pertinent to both clinician and patient/care partner. The framework is focused on shared decision making related to medication deprescribing as a behavior among patient, care partner, and clinician. Individualized assessment of benefits and harms of the medication regimen in the context of patient/caregiver preferences and goals leads to a shared decision about whether to deprescribe.

Application of the COM‐B Model to deprescribing communication. The gray boxes on the right indicate the three components of the COM‐B model: Capability, opportunity, and motivation. The red boxes describe the type of communication necessary to achieve these components as related specifically to clinicians. The yellow boxes describe the type of communication necessary to achieve these components as related specifically to patients/care partners. The green boxes describe the type of communication necessary to achieve shared aspects of capability and opportunity. The gray boxes on the left present measures that can be used to assess each of the components.

The proposed framework illustrates both the communication components specific to clinicians and to patients and care partners and the shared components that are prerequisites to shared decision making. To promote capability, or the ability to engage in shared decision making about deprescribing, clinicians need a set of communication skills including words to use to discuss the rationale for deprescribing, the ability to do teach back, and the ability to elicit patient/care partner concerns about medications. Patients and care partners need self‐efficacy to communicate, which can be built by educating them about the importance of medication discussions and providing them with “questions to ask” their clinicians. These skills facilitate a shared understanding of key communication content, including medication benefits and harms, non‐pharmacological alternatives, and the “how‐to” of deprescribing, including what to expect.

Opportunity is achieved through different modes of communication that create the conditions for deprescribing. For clinicians, these modes include electronic alerts, feedback reports, and computerized decision supports. They also include interdisciplinary meetings and asynchronous medication reviews with clinical pharmacists and/or nurses. For patients/care partners, examples include brochures, advertisements, lectures, interactions with pharmacists, and public service announcements. These modes should then lead to a shared opportunity to consider deprescribing, either as part of usual care, in a visit designated for deprescribing, or in a specialized deprescribing clinic.

Motivational or empowering communication helps clinicians and patients/care partners to recognize when the patient's medication regimen does not alight with the patient's preferences and care priorities. Such communication can overcome the clinical inertia that serves as a barrier to deprescribing. Examples of this kind of communication for clinicians include audit and feedback, academic detailing, and comparison to peers. For patients and care partners, examples include peer testimonials or storytelling that can enhance and/or personalize the relevance of medication harms.

Each of the three constructs of the COM‐B can be measured as they relate to deprescribing. For Capability, these measures include knowledge about medication benefits and harms, understanding of the rationale for deprescribing, and familiarity of tapering strategies and deprescribing options. Opportunity can be assessed through implementation metrics, such as delivery and use of communication materials (e.g., clinician electronic prompts, patient brochures) and attendance at deprescribing‐focused appointments. Motivation can be assessed through assessment of attitudes and beliefs, such as willingness to make a medication change and trust in the deprescribing process.

Discussion

The USDeN Communications Working Group applied the COM‐B behavior change model to develop a framework to identify the key components of communication among clinicians, patients, and care partners needed to achieve the capability, opportunity, and motivation for deprescribing. The framework includes measures that can be used to evaluate the mechanisms and implementation of deprescribing interventions. This is highly relevant to deprescribing since clinicians and patients/care partners require specific communication content, modes, and skills to achieve a shared understanding of the “why” and “how” of deprescribing along with a shared commitment and willingness to change medications. Shared knowledge and intent facilitate shared decision making about medication appropriateness and plans to deprescribe when the potential benefits and harms of medications do not align with patients' and care partners' priorities.

Characterizing deprescribing communication through the lens of behavior change offers important insights into the design of deprescribing interventions to enhance their effectiveness. Behavior change models emphasize the key role of motivation in initiating change and overcoming barriers. They also include the concept of self‐efficacy, which is critical for behavior change. The models therefore emphasize the importance of preparing clinicians and patients/care partners for the clinical encounter by enhancing both motivation and self‐efficacy in addition to capability and opportunity.

Historically, motivational communication has rarely been an explicit target of deprescribing interventions. A notable exception is the EMPOWER trial, a randomized controlled trial of direct‐to‐consumer materials for older persons with chronic benzodiazepine use resulting in a 23% risk difference in the likelihood of discontinuing the medication [16]. It is noteworthy that, even in an environment with a high prevalence of benzodiazepine use, an educational approach designed to increase capability, motivation, and opportunity prompted patients to overcome barriers to deprescribing. An educational brochure, mailed to participants, was designed to elicit cognitive dissonance iby challenging beliefs about the safety of benzodiazepines, directly targeting motivation. The materials also provided information about tapering to increase self‐efficacy and prompted patients to communicate with their clinician [17]. A secondary analysis of a subset of the participants demonstrated that the intervention increased both motivation and capacity [18]. While only a single example, this trial provides a potentially important case study in understanding how the concepts of behavior change can be harnessed to promote deprescribing.

A second insight provided by the application of the COM‐B to deprescribing communication, also illustrated by the EMPOWER study, is the use of intermediate outcomes to understand the mechanisms by which deprescribing interventions work. Measures of medication and deprescribing knowledge (i.e., capability), motivation, and self‐efficacy allow for mediation analyses, which can shed insight on theory‐driven design and provide information about the key elements of behavior change required to promote deprescribing.

Finally, the application of behavior change models to deprescribing communication emphasizes the potential benefits of designing interventions composed of more than a single clinical interaction. This notion expands the concept of deprescribing as a process, composed of multiple steps. The established steps of deprescribing include comprehensive medication review; identification of potentially inappropriate medications; shared decision making around deprescribing; and creating, implementing, and monitoring the effects of a deprescribing plan [4, 19]. Health behavior models applied to deprescribing suggest that dedicated interactions are required to prepare both patients and clinicians by increasing knowledge and self‐efficacy and addressing attitudes and beliefs that serve as barriers to deprescribing. For example, pre‐visit educational materials mailed to patients, responsive to language and health literacy needs, combined with pre‐visit clinician audit reports could address knowledge, motivation, and self‐efficacy.

Our findings lay the groundwork for additional studies examining communication, behavior change, and deprescribing. This COM‐B‐based deprescribing framework was not created to be the definitive model to guide deprescribing communication in the clinical encounter. For example, elements from other behavior change models, such as readiness to change as assessed in the Trans‐Theoretical Model, further help to elucidate the effective components of deprescribing interventions. Deprescribing‐specific tools for many of the measures in the framework do not currently exist, representing an important area for future research. Accordingly, the framework is intended to stimulate additional efforts to understand how behavior change models can ground interventions in theory that encompasses the complexity and multifactorial nature of deprescribing.
