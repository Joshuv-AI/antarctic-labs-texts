Genomic Insights Into Carbapenem‐Resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae in Pediatric Bloodstream Infections in Zhejiang, China (2019–2024)
Authors: Mingming Zhou, Xiucai Zhang, Zhenjie Chen, Chao Fang, Shixing Liu, Jintao Xia, Yining Zhao, Chenmei Zhang, Hongqiang Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesBloodstream infections (BSIs) caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae are associated with a significant risk of mortality. While the majority of existing research has concentrated on adult populations, there is a paucity of studies addressing pediatric cases. This study aims to elucidate the mechanisms underlying carbapenem resistance in K. pneumoniae strains responsible for bloodstream infections in children.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted at Children's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, focusing on patients diagnosed with bloodstream infections due to carbapenem‐resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing and whole‐genome sequencing were subsequently performed.ResultsIn this study, all the CRKP‐BSI strains were detected during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The predominant mechanism of carbapenem resistance among the 10 CRKP‐BSI strains was the production of class B metallo‐beta‐lactamases, with bla NDM‐1 present in 50% of the strains, followed by bla NDM‐5 (30%) and bla IMP‐4 (20%). The ST25‐KL2 lineage was the most prevalent strain, with all isolates originating from the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). In strain 22WJ8105, hypervirulence‐associated genes, including iucABC and iutA, were identified. Additionally, a strain of ST17‐type K. pneumoniae harboring the bla NDM‐5 gene was identified.ConclusionsThis study determined that the production of class B metalloenzymes is the primary mechanism of carbapenem resistance in pediatric bloodstream infections caused by CRKP. Importantly, the ST25‐KL2 lineage emerged as the dominant clone, highlighting its potential for nosocomial transmission.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a prominent pathogen in nosocomial environments, capable of causing a wide spectrum of healthcare‐associated infections (HCAIs) [1]. A recent study on European HCAIs identified K. pneumoniae as one of the top three isolated microorganisms, underscoring its role as a leading cause of hospital‐acquired infections globally [2]. Bloodstream infections (BSIs) attributed to the Enterobacterales order have become increasingly life‐threatening, with mortality rates reaching as high as 61% [3]. In particular, carbapenem‐resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) infections are a major contributor to these infections. The rising antimicrobial resistance of K. pneumoniae , especially against β‐lactamase and carbapenemase agents, has been extensively documented and is acknowledged as a critical factor exacerbating the severity of healthcare‐associated infectious threats [4]. The global increase in CRKP bloodstream infections (CRKP‐BSI) is alarming due to its association with heightened morbidity and mortality, primarily because of the limited availability of effective antibiotic treatments [5]. The primary mechanism underlying CRKP resistance is the acquisition of multiple genes encoding β‐lactamases capable of degrading carbapenems. The most prevalent carbapenemases identified in K. pneumoniae include the KPC‐type, OXA‐48‐type, NDM‐, VIM‐, and IMP‐type enzymes [6]. In non‐carbapenemase‐producing CRKP, carbapenem resistance is typically mediated by both porin loss and high‐level production of ESBLs or AmpC enzymes [7, 8].

CRKP is increasingly being detected in pediatric bloodstream infections and is associated with a high mortality rate [9]. However, the majority of research has concentrated on adult populations, with limited studies addressing pediatric cases. This study analyzed the clinical and molecular characteristics of CRKP‐BSI clinical isolates from a Grade A tertiary pediatric hospital, aiming to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying carbapenem resistance in CRKP‐BSI strains isolated from pediatric patients through the application of whole‐genome sequencing (WGS).

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Isolates and CRKP‐BSI Screening

Between January 2019 and December 2024, a total of 5680 clinical isolates were obtained from blood specimens of pediatric patients at Children's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. In this study, a total of 156 K. pneumoniae strains were isolated from 119 patients with BSI. Of these, 137 strains were identified as carbapenem‐sensitive K. pneumoniae , affecting 107 patients, while 19 strains were classified as CRKP, isolated from 12 patients. Following the exclusion of duplicate isolates and two strains that were not preserved, a total of 10 CRKP strains were ultimately included in the analysis (Figure S1). This study excluded all strains from identical patient sources, ensuring that all isolates were non‐repetitive. Patient histories and clinical data, including age, sex, hospital department, length of hospital stay, microbiological data, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing results, were collected and analyzed. All isolates were identified using Matrix‐Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time‐of‐Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI‐TOF MS, Bruker, Bremen, Germany). The study protocols were approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee at Children's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, under approval number 2021‐IRB‐031.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of seven antimicrobial agents (ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, levofloxacin, amikacin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, meropenem, imipenem) were determined for ten CRKP isolates from the bloodstream using the standardized commercial microdilution system (VITEK 2 COMPACT, BioMérieux, France). The MIC of colistin was ascertained via the broth microdilution method. Additionally, the in vitro antibacterial activity of ceftazidime‐avibactam against carbapenem‐resistant K. pneumoniae was evaluated using the disk diffusion method (Oxoid). Tigecycline susceptibility was assessed using the E‐test method (BIO‐KONT). All antibiotic susceptibility results were interpreted in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (M100, 34rd edition) [10], with the exception of tigecycline, for which interpretation followed the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidelines due to the lack of established CLSI breakpoints for this agent. To ensure the precision and reliability of the tests, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was employed as the quality control strain for the drug susceptibility testing. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes.

WGS and Genome Assembly

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was employed to identify the CRKP‐BSI strain and to elucidate its genetic characteristics, including its sequence type (ST), with a particular focus on multilocus sequence typing (MLST), virulence genes, and antibiotic resistance gene carriage. Genomic DNA was extracted from bacterial samples using the QIAGEN‐QiaAmp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The purity and concentration of the extracted DNA were evaluated using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) [11]. The genomic DNA was subsequently sent to Hangzhou Digital Microbial Biotechnology Co. Ltd. for whole genome sequencing via the Illumina HiSeq XTen platform. De novo assembly of the sequencing data was accomplished using the Unicycler pipeline v0.5.0 (https://github.com/rrwick/Unicycler). Genomic analysis was performed at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (CGE) by uploading the contig files generated from the de novo assembly of WGS data [12]. Virulence factors were identified by comparison with the virulence factor database (VFDB) (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate). A systematic phylogenetic analysis was performed on all CRKP strains based on whole‐genome pan‐genome Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (pan‐SNPs) identification, with the pan‐SNPs extracted and filtered from the core genome alignments of the strains. Phylogenetic tree construction was conducted using the maximum‐likelihood (ML) method implemented in IQ‐TREE software (version 2.0.3), and the resulting tree was visualized via the iTOL platform (https://itol.embl.de/).

Results

CRKP Is the Predominant Carbapenem‐Resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) Species Among BSI Patients

During 2019–2024, a total of 156 K. pneumoniae isolates were collected from patients with BSI. Of these, 19 isolates were confirmed as CRKP, accounting for an isolation rate of 12.2%. After excluding duplicate and non‐preserved strains, 10 CRKP isolates were retained for further analysis. Among the antimicrobials evaluated, tigecycline and colistin showed the highest in vitro activity against this cohort of blood‐derived CRKP isolates. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed that all CRKP strains were resistant to ceftazidime‐avibactam. Furthermore, the findings indicated that all CRKP‐BSI isolates were MDR strains. Detailed MICs and antimicrobial resistance values are presented in Table 1. The primary sources of CRKP‐BSI in this study were the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) and neonatal surgery departments. The majority of the patients were infants younger than 5 months (Figure 1). Regarding the prognosis of CRKP BSI patients, among the 10 included patients, 2 cases died and 8 cases showed clinical improvement and were discharged.

Resistance Mechanism of Carbapenem Antimicrobials in CRKP‐BSI Strains

WGS revealed multiple acquired genes encoding β‐lactamases across the ten CRKP isolates. Further genomic analysis indicated that bla NDM‐1 (50%) was the most frequently identified carbapenem resistance gene among the CRKP‐BSI strains, followed by bla NDM‐5 (30%). Notably, except for the bla SHV‐12 gene, the strains 20IR1029, 20IR1034, 20IR1035, and 20IR1049 exhibited identical resistance characteristics and STs. Whole‐genome sequence comparison revealed that fewer than 10 SNPs separated 20IR1029, 20IR1034, and 20IR1035 from 20IR1049, suggestive of a shared clonal origin for these isolates. Genomic comparisons of 20IR1029, 20IR1034, 20IR1035, and 20IR1049 are illustrated in Figure 2. The discovery of the carbapenem resistance gene cluster bla NDM‐5 in the ST17 strain is particularly noteworthy, as it is an uncommon finding in previous literature. The detailed distribution of drug resistance genes is depicted in Figure 3. Additionally, all CRKP‐BSI strains were found to harbor one or more plasmids, including IncA/C2, IncFIB(K), IncFII, IncX3, ColRNAI, and Col8282.

Mechanisms of Resistance to Non‐β‐Lactam Antimicrobial Agents

Acquired resistance genes associated with non‐β‐lactam antibiotics were identified, including those conferring resistance to fosfomycin, aminoglycosides, sulphonamides, tetracyclines, quinolones, and macrolides. Specifically, aminoglycoside resistance genes such as aph(3″)‐Ib, aac(3)‐IIa, aac(6′)‐Ib‐cr, aph(6)‐Id, aac(6′)‐Ib‐cr, and aph(6)‐Id were detected. All strains exhibited resistance genes for fosfomycin and quinolones.

Virulence Genes of CRKP‐BSI Strains

The expression of virulence factors is more prevalent among specific genetically related subpopulations of K. pneumoniae , which have the potential to evolve into distinct virulent clones within the broader populations of this species. Among the ten K. pneumoniae strains analyzed, the most frequently identified virulence genes were ompA, entB, ykgK/ecpR, yagZ/ecpA, yagY/ecpB, yagX/ecpC, yagW/ecpD, and yagV/ecpE (Figure 4). Additional virulence‐associated genes, such as iucA, iucB, iucC, entA, fepC, irp1, irp2, and fyuA, were also detected in these isolates.

Sequence Typing and Phylogenetic Analysis

The ten CRKP strains were categorized into six distinct sequence types (STs): ST25 (n = 4), ST1224 (n = 2), ST2407 (n = 1), ST17 (n = 1), ST35 (n = 1), and ST3591 (n = 1). A detailed distribution of ST and KL types is provided in Table 2. Notably, the four strains from the NICU department were all of the ST25‐KL2 type, exhibiting a close phylogenetic relationship as determined by evolutionary tree analysis. Despite being isolated over 4 months apart, the strains 21IR1173 and 21IR1177 demonstrated a close genetic relationship, sharing identical resistance genes and plasmids.

Discussion

K. pneumoniae is a significant opportunistic nosocomial pathogen prevalent in healthcare settings worldwide, associated with a range of infections including ventilator‐associated pneumonia and BSI [12]. Over the past two decades, the rising incidence of septicemia attributed to CRKP‐BSI in pediatric populations has raised significant concern [9]. The emergence of acquired resistance to critical antibiotics, coupled with hypervirulence in this rapidly evolving pathogen, is likely to exacerbate the challenges in managing these infections [2]. Longitudinal data from China's Bacterial Resistance Surveillance Network (CHINET) have consistently demonstrated an annual increase in the resistance rates of K. pneumoniae to imipenem and meropenem.

Numerous studies have been published concerning the dissemination of CRKP, yet there is a paucity of data specifically addressing pediatric sepsis [13, 14]. In the present study, the overall detection rate of CRKP‐BSI was found to be relatively low, suggesting a lower prevalence of CRKP‐BSI within the region. Notably, all CRKP‐BSI isolates were identified during the COVID‐19 pandemic, suggesting that pandemic‐related factors, such as increased broad‐spectrum antibiotic use and heightened patient vulnerability, may have driven the emergence and spread of these pathogens, with the pandemic itself acting as a significant risk factor for CRKP bloodstream infections. In contrast to adults, who predominantly contract infections with bla KPC K. pneumoniae [15], this study identified extensive dissemination of bla NDM‐5 and bla NDM‐1 K. pneumoniae among pediatric patients with BSI. The NDM‐5 variant, an evolution of NDM‐1, has demonstrated enhanced carbapenemase activity and an increased ability to hydrolyze ceftazidime/avibactam. This variant is frequently associated with plasmids carrying multiple replicon types, including IncX3, IncC, IncX, and IncFII [16]. The efficacy of ceftazidime‐avibactam and imipenem‐relebactam was observed to be lower in pediatric patients compared to adults, potentially due to the higher prevalence of NDM genes in the pediatric cohort. The presence of metalloenzyme‐related genes often confers resistance to ceftazidime‐avibactam and imipenem‐relebactam [17]. Given the high prevalence of MBL‐producing isolates in this pediatric population, empirical antimicrobial regimens for suspected CRKP infections, especially in vulnerable neonates, may need to be adjusted to account for MBL‐mediated resistance [18].

ST11 is the predominant sequence type of K. pneumoniae in China, contrasting with its prevalence in other countries across South and Southeast Asia [19]. Numerous studies have documented the presence of NDM‐1‐producing K. pneumoniae strains belonging to various STs that are responsible for human infections [20]. The bla NDM‐1 genotype CRKP is the primary cause of neonatal infections in China [21]. A previous study reported an outbreak of NDM‐1‐ and OXA‐181‐producing CRKP‐BSI in a neonatal unit in South Africa [22]. However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report an outbreak of neonatal bloodstream infections caused by NDM‐1‐producing K. pneumoniae of ST25, underscoring the significant nosocomial infection threat posed by such strains in neonatal wards. Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated that the genetic diversity of CRKP in pediatric populations is greater than in adults, with polyclonal backgrounds [23]. Consequently, it is crucial to investigate the genomic and virulence characteristics of ST25 CRKP strains isolated from neonates with sepsis. The relatively low frequency of carbapenem resistance acquisition in hvKP lineages can be explained by the “seesaw effect” evolutionary trade‐off, where the adaptive cost of maintaining high virulence factors limits the capacity of hvKP strains to acquire carbapenem‐resistance determinants [24]. Additionally, a strain of ST17‐type K. pneumoniae carrying the bla NDM‐5 gene was identified, which is a rare finding in previous reports, highlighting the necessity for ongoing surveillance.

Four virulence factors have been identified: pili, capsules, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and iron carriers [25]. In our study, all 10 CRKP‐BSI strains exhibited multiple virulence factors. However, according to established criteria, only one strain was classified as hypervirulent. Despite this, ongoing vigilance is necessary regarding hypervirulent‐CRKP strains.

A limitation of this study is the exclusive use of short‐read sequencing to analyze resistance gene environments. Although this method successfully identified NDM‐1 in two distinct STs with similar Inc. types, indicating potential plasmid‐mediated transmission, it falls short in fully resolving plasmid structures, flanking mobile elements, and transfer loci. Consequently, comprehensive data on the plasmid genetic contexts within CRKP‐BSI isolates are absent. Additionally, the relatively small sample size presents an unavoidable limitation, attributable to the low incidence of CRKP‐BSI and the challenges associated with specimen collection in the pediatric population, potentially impacting the representativeness and generalizability of our findings. Future research should aim to enhance understanding of the transmission dynamics and molecular epidemiology of CRKP, as well as elucidate the mechanisms driving the spread of multidrug resistance in this pathogen.

Conclusions

In conclusion, CRKP‐BSI strains pose a significant public health threat globally. The primary mechanism of carbapenem resistance identified in this study was the production of Class B metalloenzymes. We report, for the first time, an outbreak of bloodstream infections in a neonatal ward caused by the dissemination of NDM‐1‐producing ST25 K. pneumoniae . Our findings indicate that this high‐risk strain represents an emerging threat in neonatal wards in China.
