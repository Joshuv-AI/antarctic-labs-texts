New antibacterial 2-bromopyrrole-2-aminoimidazolinone alkaloids, 10E-dispacamide B and (+)-10E-dispacamide D, from a sponge Agelas nakamurai (Hoshino, 1985)
Authors: Atallah F. Ahmed, You-Cheng Lin, Chung-Wei Fu, Shu-Fen Chiou, Hui-Chun Wang, Hao-Chun Hu, Meng-Chen Yu, Su-Ying Chien, Ping-Jyun Sung, Jyh-Horng Sheu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Continued chemical investigation of the sponge Agelas nakamurai led to the isolation of two new 2-bromopyrrole-2-aminoimidazoline alkaloids, 10E-dispacamide B (1) and (+)-10E-dispacamide D (2), together with their known 10Z-isomers, dispacamide B (3) and dispacamide D (4). The structures of the new metabolites were elucidated through comprehensive spectroscopic analyses, including 2D NMR experiments, along with the assistance of a single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD) analysis of compound 3. Biological evaluation revealed that the newly identified alkaloids, 10E-dispacamide B (1) and (+)-10E-dispacamide D (2), exhibited stronger antibacterial activity than their corresponding 10Z-isomers, 3 and 4. In addition, a plausible biosynthetic pathway for the formation of both the 10E and 10Z isomers 1–4 from laughine was proposed. The occurrence of both 10E- and 10Z-isomers highlights the remarkable biosynthetic versatility of this sponge and underscores its potential as a valuable source of structurally diverse and biologically active natural products.

Introduction

Marine sponges have long been recognized as important sources for discovery of structurally diverse secondary metabolites and the possessed biological activities useful for medicinal application.1–3 Until 2022, sponges of the genus Agelas have significantly contributed to the search for new bioactive marine natural products, with nearly 400 compounds having been reported since 1971 from Agelas species. Among them, metabolites of pyrrole-based alkaloids comprise more than 50% of the total metabolites.4–6 These compounds were proposed to be biosynthesized from proline and lysine, and further diversified through oxidation, cyclization, and dimerization to afford three primary structural categories: linear, fused cyclic, and dimeric pyrrole alkaloids.7 Our ongoing chemical investigation of a Taiwanese sponge Agelas nakamurai (phylum Porifera, class Demospongiae, subclass Heteroscleromorpha, order Agelasida, family Agelasidae) has led to the isolation of a series of secondary metabolites with distinctive molecular frameworks corresponding to sponges of Agelas genus and the associated bioactivities.8

In this study, two new 2-bromopyrrole-2-aminoimidazolinone alkaloids 10E-dispacamide B (1) and 10E-dispacamide D (2) were discovered and elucidated as the rare E-geometric isomers co-existing with their known Z-configured counterparts dispacamide B (3) and dispacamide D (4) from the same sponge. Starting from the confirmation of the structure of Z-geometric metabolite 3, using SC-XRD, 1D NMR data measured in CD3OD, NMR correlation analyses, and detailed analysis including comparison of NMR spectroscopic data for both E and Z isomers, the structure of the new E-configured isomers 1 and 2 could be fully confirmed. Bioassay of the compounds 1–4 further revealed that only alkaloids of this type exhibited a noticeable and stronger antibacterial activity than their cis isomers. Herein, we report the isolation, structure elucidation of 1 and 2, structure confirmation of 3 by X-ray diffraction, and a putative biosynthetic pathway and bioactivity of 1–4.

Results and discussion

Compound 3 was recrystallized from MeOH as colorless prisms. The structure of 3 was then elucidated by analysis of ESIMS and NMR data in comparison with those of dispacamide B5 and further confirmed the presence of Z-configured 10,11-double bond in pyrrole-2-aminoimidazolidine alkaloids for the first time by SC-XRD analysis (Fig. 1 and Tables S1–S5). Its 9-hydroxy derivative 4 was also characterized in this study utilizing ESIMS and NMR spectroscopic data as dispacamide D.6 The identification of the two new corresponding 10E-isomers 1 and 2 was subsequently accomplished through extensive analysis and the comparison of spectroscopic data of 1–4 (Fig. 2, S1–S24, Tables 1 and 2) as follows.

Compound 1 displayed two protonated ion peaks [M + H]+ in the HRESIMS spectrum at m/z 326.02463 and 328.02262 (1 : 1) suggesting the presence of one bromine atom in a molecule with a molecular formula C11H12BrN5O2 and eight degrees of unsaturation. The UV maxima at 271 nm and 254 nm, and IR absorptions at 1684–1647 cm−1 suggested conjugated systems, while IR absorptions at 1748 and 3420 cm−1 further substantiate the presence of amino and/or amide functionalities. The NMR data (Table 1) and HSQC spectra (Fig. S6), measured in CD3OD, revealed the presence 11 carbons of two sp3 methylenes, three sp2 methines, and six sp2 quaternary carbons (δC 97.5–168.8), including those of amide carbonyl carbons and an iminocarbon (δC > 162: C-6, C-12, and C-14) and the 3-bromopyrrole carbon (δC 97.5) (Table 1), similar to those of dispacamide B (3).5 The 1H–1H COSY correlations extending from the methylene protons at C-9 (δH 2.60 and 2.58) to both of H2-8 (δH 3.48, 2H) and the olefinic methine proton H-10 (δH 6.10), in addition to the HMBC correlations observed from H2-8 and H-10 to the carbonyl carbons at C-6 (δC 162.8) and C-12 (δC 165.9) (Fig. S7 and S8), connected the propenyl unit to the 3-monobromopyrrole carboxamide and 2-aminoimidazolinone, respectively. This and other HMBC correlations (Fig. 3 and S8) further confirmed the structure of 1 as an isomer of dispacamide B (3), as illustrated in Fig. 2. Comparative analysis of the NMR data of compounds 1 and 3 (Table 2) revealed substantial similarity, with the exception of a marked upfield shift of the carbonyl carbon C-12 (ΔδC −13.2 ppm) and a downfield shift of the 10-methine group (ΔδC +6.4 ppm; ΔδH +0.34 ppm), suggesting the E-geometry of the 10,11-double bond in compound 1, as compound 3 possesses the 10Z geometry confirmed by SC-XRD analysis, as mentioned above. The downfield shift of H-10 in compound 1 (δH 6.10) was found to be similar to that observed in E-dispacamide (δH 5.97),9 relative to that in compound 3 (δH 5.76) (Table 2). This 10,11-E-geometry of 1 was further supported by the analogous trend observed for E-dispacamide9 whose NMR data, measured in DMSO-d6, displayed similar trends of shifts for C-12, C-10, and H-10 (ΔδC −15.8, ΔδC +10.8, and ΔδH +0.21 ppm, respectively) when compared to its Z-isomer, dispacamide (2-bromodispacamide B), measured in CD3OD.5 Moreover, a series of synthesized E- and Z- furfurylideneimidazolinones10 showed similar upfield and downfieldshifts at the carbon atoms corresponding to C-12 and C-10 of compound 1. Thus, compound 1 is identified as 10E-dispacamide B.

Key 1H–1H COSY and HMBC correlations of 1 and 2 measured in CD3OD.

Compound 2 showed two sodiated two ion peaks in the HRESIMS at m/z 364.00182 and 365.99976 (1 : 1) [M + Na]+, establishing the molecular formula C11H12BrN5O3. The IR absorption at 3417 cm−1, the EISMS peaks appeared at m/z 324 and 326 (1 : 1) [M–H2O + H]+, and NMR signals displayed at δH 4.71/δC 70.2, CH, revealed the presence of hydroxy group in the molecule of 2. Comparison of 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopic data of this compound with those of compound 1 (Table 1) revealed complete similarity, except for the replacement of the methylene group at C-9 (δH 2.60 and 2.58/δC 28.7, CH2) in compound 1 with a hydroxy-bearing methine group (δH 4.71/δC 70.2, CH) in compound 2. Based on COSY and HMBC correlation analyses (Fig. 3), compound 2 was identified as the 9-hydroxy derivative of compound 1. Again, the noticeable upfield shift at C-12 (ΔδC −10.2 ppm) and the downfield shifts at 10-CH2 (ΔδC +5.6/ΔδH +0.28 ppm) relative to those of dispacamide D (4)6 specified the E-geometry of the 10,11-double bond in 2. Based on the above-mentioned findings and optical rotation [α]D25 [+24.6 (c 0.60, MeOH)], compound 2 was assigned as (+)-10E-dispacamide D.

To further confirm the absolute configuration of 2, a comprehensive conformational analysis coupled with specific optical rotation (SOR) calculations was performed using validated in silico protocols.11,12 The gross structure of 2 displayed two possible configurations, including 2-S and 2-R. The calculated SOR values of 2-S and 2-R were +132.9 and −132.9, respectively. Comparing the calculated with the experimental SOR value of 2 (+24.6), the absolute configuration of 2 could be assigned as 2-S (Table 3).

A plausible biosynthetic pathway for the isolated compounds 1–4 is proposed as shown in Scheme 1. The oxidation of laughine (5), co-existing with 1–4 in A. nakamurai,13 at C-11 would yields an oxoethylguanidine intermediate a. Subsequent intramolecular nucleophilic addition of the terminal amino group to 11-keto group could afford the ring closure product possessing a 2-imino- imidazolidin-4-ol moiety, designated as intermediate b, which upon oxidation at C-12, could be transformed to the intermediate c with a terminal 2-iminoimidazolidinone-4-ol functionality. Acid-catalyzed dehydration of c and its rotamer d, formed via rotation of 10,11-single bond of c, further leads to the formation of compound 3 and its 10E-isomer 1, respectively, probably arisen from the E2 elimination of the corresponding anti-periplanar geometry. Finally, the allylic oxidation of both 1 and 3 could generate the corresponding 9-hydroxy derivatives 2 and 4, respectively.

Plausible biosynthetic pathway of bromopyrrole-2-aminoimidazolinone alkaloids 1–5.

The antibacterial assay results indicated that the 10E-bromopyrrole-2-aminoimidazolinone alkaloids 1 and 2 exhibited antibacterial activity. Compound 1 inhibited the growth of Staphylococcus aureus, producing an inhibition zone of 3.0 mm at 25 µg per disk. Compound 2 demonstrated a broader antibacterial spectrum, inhibiting S. aureus (Gram-positive), Escherichia coli, and Salmonella typhimurium (Gram-negative) at the same concentration, with inhibition zones of 3.0, 3.0, and 3.2 mm, respectively. In contrast, compounds 3 and 4 showed no detectable activity against the tested bacterial strains. Compared with ampicillin (positive control), which produced inhibition zones of 14.0, 7.0, and 7.0 mm against S. aureus, E. coli, and S. typhimurium, respectively, at the same disk concentration, the antibacterial activities of compounds 1 and 2 were considerably weaker. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that bromopyrrole-2-aminoimidazolinone alkaloids possess antibacterial potential, warranting further investigation of their biological activities and possible medicinal applications. Thus, we suggest that the structural analogues afforded from future natural products investigations or prepared by synthetic studies, including the additional 9-oxygenated compounds and the derivatives lacking bromine substituents or bearing alternative substituents on the pyrrole ring, should be further evaluated for their antibacterial and other pharmacological activities, with the aim of discovering natural products or synthetic derivatives of this type with promising potential for new drug development.

Conclusions

The chemical exploration of Agelas nakamurai yielded two new 2-bromopyrrole-2-aminoimidazolinone alkaloids, 10E-dispacamide B and (+)-10E-dispacamide D. These two compounds demonstrated stronger antibacterial activity than their corresponding 10Z-isomers. The structural novelty and biosynthetic capacity of producing both geometric (10E and 10Z) isomers within a single marine organism highlight the sponge's role as a reservoir of structurally distinct metabolites. This study further suggests, for the first time, that both E- and Z-olefinic isomers of related dispacamides may be biosynthesized from laughine via the pathway illustrated in Scheme 1. These findings reinforce the value of marine sponges in natural products research and highlight their promising potential as sources for future drug discovery.

Experimental

General experimental procedure

Optical rotations, IR spectra, and UV spectra were measured using a JASCO P-1020 digital polarimeter, JASCO FT/IR-4100 spectrometer, and JASCO V-650 spectrophotometer (JASCO Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), respectively. ESIMS and HRESIMS data were acquired using a Bruker APEX II mass spectrometer (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) and a Thermo Orbitrap QE mass spectrometer equipped with an ESI source operating in positive-ion mode, respectively. NMR spectra were recorded on Varian Unity INOVA 500 and Varian MR 400 FT-NMR spectrometers (Varian Inc., CA, USA), operating at 500 and 400 MHz for 1H NMR and 125 and 100 MHz for 13C NMR measurements, respectively. Sephadex LH-20 (Pharmacia, Stockholm, Sweden) and silica gel (200–230 mesh; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) were used for column chromatography (CC), while analytical TLC was carried out on precoated silica gel plates (Kieselgel 60 F254, 0.2 mm; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) separations were performed using a Hitachi L-7100 system (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with either a Supelco C18 column (250 × 21.2 mm, 5 µm; Bellefonte, PA, USA) or a Hibar C18 column (250 × 10 mm, 5 µm; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany).

Animal material

The marine sponge Agelas nakamurai was collected by SCUBA diving from coral reefs off the coast of Orchid Island (Lanyu Island), Taiwan, at a depth of 10–15 m in August 2008 and was immediately frozen prior to extraction. A voucher specimen was deposited in the Department of Marine Biotechnology and Resources, National Sun Yat-sen University, Kaohsiung, Taiwan, under the accession number OISP-2008-01. The sponge was identified as Agelas nakamurai (Hoshino, 1985) by Dr Meng-Chen Yu of the Biodiversity Research Center, Academia Sinica, Taipei, Taiwan, based on morphological characteristics, including skeletal structure and spicule morphology, together with DNA barcoding analysis.

Extraction and isolation

The freeze-dried sponge material (294 g) was exhaustively extracted with EtOAc to afford 7 g of solvent-free extract. The extract was subjected to silica gel CC using a gradient elution starting with EtOAc in n-hexane (0–100%), followed by MeOH in acetone (0–100%), to yield 19 fractions. Fraction 14, eluted with MeOH–acetone (1 : 3), was fractionated using a Sephadex LH-20 CC with acetone, resulting in two subfractions 14-1 to 14-2. These two subfractions were separately subjected to RP-HPLC using CH3CN–H2O (1 : 4), acidified with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, to yield compounds 1, 3, and 4 from subfraction 14-1, and compounds 1 and 2 from subfraction 14-2, in total quantities of 13.5, 6.8, 6.0, and 6.8 mg for compounds 1–4, respectively.

In silico calculations

The conformational search and calculated SOR results were carried out with the same method published as ref. 11 and 12. The brief procedure was described as follows. First, we optimized the minimized energy of the structure in the MM2 level and outputted an xyz file. Then, we submitted the file into spartan'16 software (Wavefunction Inc.; Irvine, CA, USA) at MMFF94 to generate conformational search results. The output data were imported into the Gaussian 09 software (Gaussian Inc.; Wallingford, CT, USA) and optimized using the time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) methodology at the B3LYP/6-31G* level in the gas phase and the B3LYP/6-31(d) levels in the solvent phase for SOR calculation, and the GIAO-DFT at the PCM/mpw1pw91/6-311 + g(d,p) level in the solvent phase for GIAO-NMR DP4+ analysis. The results were averaged by the proportion of each conformer.

Antimicrobial activity assay

The antibacterial activities of the two newly isolated compounds 1 and 2 were evaluated using the disk diffusion method against the Gram-positive bacterium Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 9144) and the Gram-negative bacteria Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922) and Salmonella typhimurium (ATCC 14028), following previously described procedures.18
