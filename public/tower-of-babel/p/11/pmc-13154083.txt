Reversible Left Ventricular Hypertrabeculation and Hypermetabolism Following Bentall Procedure

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft ventricular hypertrabeculation may develop secondary to hemodynamic stress rather than represent primary cardiomyopathy. Hypertrabeculation of the left ventricle has been increasingly recognized not only as a primary cardiomyopathy phenotype but also as a secondary feature that can develop in response to hemodynamic stress.1 Recent expert consensus emphasizes that the term “left ventricular (LV) noncompaction” is a misnomer, as current insights from cardiac biology indicate that compact myocardium does not form through compaction of pre-existing trabeculations, but rather through independent, differential growth of the trabecular and compact layers. The characteristic trabeculated myocardial structure may regress after correction of the underlying hemodynamic abnormalities.2, 3, 4, 5 Herein, we report a case of prominent LV hypertrabeculation associated with severe aortic regurgitation secondary to aortic root dilatation and subsequently underwent a Bentall procedure. Both excessive hypertrabeculation and increased preoperative myocardial 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake on positron emission tomography (PET) showed marked regression on follow-up imaging after surgery.

History of Presentation

A 43-year-old woman initially presented to a local hospital with progressive dyspnea and was diagnosed with acute heart failure. She was subsequently referred to our institution for further evaluation and management. Physical examination revealed bilateral lower-leg edema and a regular cardiac rhythm with a grade III/VI to-and-fro murmur.

Investigations

Laboratory evaluation showed an elevated level of N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide of 1,043 pg/mL, whereas cardiac troponin T remained normal (<0.01 ng/mL). Electrocardiography demonstrated normal sinus rhythm with LV hypertrophy (Figure 1A). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed LV dilation (end-diastolic and end-systolic dimensions of 59 and 48 mm, respectively) with reduced systolic performance (ejection fraction, 39.0%) (Video 1). Severe aortic regurgitation was observed with dilation of the sinus of Valsalva (45 mm) (Figure 2A, Video 2). The left ventricle exhibited diffuse severe hypokinesis with prominent hypertrabeculation involving the anterior, lateral, and infero-posterior walls (Figures 2B to 2D). Color Doppler imaging demonstrated blood flow within deep intertrabecular recesses (Figure 2C). The ratio of trabecular to compact myocardium was 2.4 (20.0 mm/8.3 mm) at end-systole (Figure 2D), satisfying established criteria for hypertrabeculation. Chest radiography showed a cardiothoracic ratio of 53.2%, with sharp costophrenic angles and no evidence of pulmonary vascular congestion (Figure 1A). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated dilation of the sinus of Valsalva to 50 mm with a normal ascending aorta (27 mm) (Figure 3A). Coronary angiography revealed no significant stenosis. Left ventriculography confirmed impaired systolic function with prominent trabecular structures (Figure 3B, Video 3). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed diffuse LV systolic dysfunction with hypertrabeculation (Figure 3C) and no late gadolinium enhancement. FDG-PET/CT was performed following a high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet with 18-hour fasting to suppress physiological myocardial glucose uptake. The maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of the LV myocardium was elevated at baseline (SUVmax 4.20) in the presence of cardiomegaly (Figure 4A).

Management

Based on the clinical presentation of symptomatic severe aortic regurgitation with heart failure and LV dysfunction, our heart team decided on surgical management. Intraoperative findings revealed that valve-sparing repair was unsuitable due to shortening of all 3 aortic cusps. The sinus of Valsalva was markedly dilated with an irregular intimal surface (Figure 5A), and comparable irregularity extended to the region surrounding the right coronary artery, resulting in ostial stenosis. The left ventricle exhibited prominent trabeculations (Figure 5B, Video 4). A Bentall procedure using a mechanical valved conduit was performed. Following aortic cross-clamp release, ST-segment depression in leads II, III, and aVF indicated acute inferior wall ischemia due to ostial stenosis of the right coronary artery. Consequently, coronary artery bypass grafting using a saphenous vein graft to the right coronary artery was additionally performed. The postoperative course was uneventful. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated resolution of aortic regurgitation, with reduced LV dimensions of 56/44 mm (end-diastolic/end-systolic) and an ejection fraction of 43.0%. Histopathological examination of the resected sinus of Valsalva demonstrated cystic medial degeneration (Figure 6A). Although this finding may suggest an underlying connective tissue disorder, this remains speculative without formal genetic or systemic evaluation. Myocardial specimens from the left ventricle showed mild interstitial fibrosis without specific pathological abnormalities (Figure 6B).

Outcome and Follow-Up

Over a 4-year follow-up, prominent LV hypertrabeculation gradually regressed (Figure 7). In parallel, electrocardiography demonstrated an improvement in LV hypertrophy (Figure 1B). LV end-diastolic dimension decreased from 59 mm preoperatively to 56 mm early postoperatively and 43 mm at 4 years; end-systolic dimension decreased from 48 mm to 44 mm and 32 mm, and ejection fraction improved from 39.0% to 43.0% and 52.8%. Chest radiography showed normalization of the cardiothoracic ratio (47.1%) without pulmonary congestion (Figure 1B). Serial FDG-PET/CT demonstrated resolution of myocardial FDG uptake at the 4-year follow-up (SUVmax 1.81) (Figures 4B and 7). No postoperative complications or hospitalizations for heart failure were observed during the follow-up period.

Discussion

Advances in cardiac imaging and insights into cardiac biology have transformed our understanding of LV hypertrabeculation. Current evidence indicates that trabeculated myocardium does not develop into compact myocardium during cardiac development. The trabecular and compact layers develop independently, with the compact layer growing more rapidly than the trabecular layer.1 Therefore, the term “noncompaction” is considered a developmental misnomer, and the phenotype is more accurately described as hypertrabeculation. Hypertrabeculation may represent a normal anatomical variant or develop secondarily as an adaptive response to increased cardiac volume or pressure load. Reversible hypertrabeculation has been documented in pregnancy, athletic training, and various cardiac conditions.2, 3, 4, 5 Regression of hypertrabeculation after correction of hemodynamic stress supports the concept of adaptive myocardial remodeling rather than a fixed structural cardiomyopathy.

The association between hypertrabeculation and aortic regurgitation has been described, but limited cases have required aortic valve or aortic root replacement.6,7 Bicuspid aortic valves have been observed in reported cases, although a genetic link has not been clarified.6,7 In our case, the patient had a tricuspid aortic valve but exhibited marked intimal irregularity and cystic medial degeneration, suggesting possible underlying connective tissue disorder.8 Regression of LV hypertrabeculation after the Bentall procedure supports a secondary, adaptive response to hemodynamic stress rather than a primary cardiomyopathy.

The significance of myocardial FDG uptake in patients with hypertrabeculation remains incompletely understood. In our case, increased preoperative myocardial FDG uptake resolved after the Bentall procedure. Residual focal FDG uptake at the prosthetic valve and graft anastomosis sites likely reflects physiological inflammation rather than pathological myocardial activity. Previous studies have reported variable glucose uptake in hypertrabeculation.9 The disappearance of FDG uptake after surgical correction of aortic regurgitation suggests that preoperative FDG accumulation reflected increased myocardial glucose metabolism secondary to heart failure,10 rather than intrinsic metabolic abnormalities. Relief of severe aortic regurgitation reduced LV volume overload and improved systolic performance, thereby likely normalizing myocardial glucose metabolism. This reversible metabolic abnormality is consistent with the concept of secondary hypertrabeculation.2, 3, 4, 5

Given previous reports describing regression of hypertrabeculation after treatment of the underlying hemodynamic stress,2, 3, 4, 5 our findings suggest that FDG-PET may warrant further investigation as a marker for assessing disease activity and prognosis in patients with hypertrabeculation. The resolution of both trabecular morphology and metabolic activity in this case highlights the importance of distinguishing primary cardiomyopathy from secondary, adaptive responses to hemodynamic stress. Furthermore, the parallel time course of trabeculation regression and normalization of FDG uptake suggests that FDG-PET could potentially serve as a quantitative marker for monitoring therapeutic response and predicting long-term outcomes in patients with secondary hypertrabeculation. Serial FDG-PET imaging may help identify patients who are more likely to experience reverse remodeling after correction of underlying hemodynamic abnormalities, thereby potentially guiding treatment strategies and follow-up protocols. However, this requires validation in larger prospective studies before clinical application. It is important to acknowledge that regression of trabecular prominence in this case may, in part, reflect geometric remodeling secondary to LV volume unloading after correction of aortic regurgitation, rather than true reversal of hypertrabeculation. As LV cavity size decreases, trabecular prominence may diminish without intrinsic structural change. This distinction between geometric and structural remodeling warrants careful interpretation of serial imaging findings. Recent expert consensus emphasizes that clinical management should not be determined by trabecular pattern alone in adults with normal ventricular function and size.1 In patients with hypertrabeculation, management is guided by clinical presentation and the presence of ventricular dysfunction or myocardial fibrosis, rather than the extent of hypertrabeculation.

Conclusions

This case illustrates that LV hypertrabeculation can regress following correction of underlying hemodynamic stress, such as severe aortic regurgitation. FDG-PET/CT may warrant further investigation as a tool for evaluating disease activity and monitoring response to therapy in secondary hypertrabeculation. Distinguishing primary cardiomyopathy from secondary, adaptive hypertrabeculation is critical for appropriate clinical management.
