Effects of an early restrictive versus liberal fluid strategy on long-term patient-centered outcomes in sepsis-induced hypotension: an open-label, randomized controlled trial
Authors: Anselm Jorda, Georg Gelbenegger, Nathan I Shapiro, Ivor S Douglas, Casey Whitman, Zhe Chen, Joseph A Hippensteel, Lee A Kamphuis, Robert J Flick, Michael O Harhay, Ellen Caldwell, Markus Zeitlinger, Bernd Jilma, Theodore J Iwashyna, Catherine L Hough, Catherine L Auriemma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationaleThe impact of fluid resuscitation strategies on patient-centered outcomes of sepsis survivors is unknown.ObjectivesTo assess the effect of an early restrictive or liberal fluid resuscitation strategy on long-term functional outcomes in patients with sepsis-induced hypotension.MethodsSHAMROC (Sepsis-induced Hypotension: Assessing effect of Method of Resuscitation On Patient-centered Outcomes) prospectively assessed the impact of random assignment to a restrictive or liberal fluid resuscitation strategy for sepsis-induced hypotension outcomes at 6 and 12 months after randomization in the NIH NHLBI PETAL Network’s CLOVERS trial (NCT03434028). The pre-specified analyses used trimmed means at 50% to prevent informative censoring of deceased patients.Measurements and Main ResultsOf the 1563 participants included in the CLOVERS trial, 898 (57%) were included in the SHAMROC trial. As 196 were lost to follow-up, 702 participants were analyzed at 6 months (431 survivors and 271 non-survivors). Baseline characteristics were similar between the groups. At 6 months, no group differences were observed in cognitive function (restrictive versus liberal; trimmed mean difference in Montreal Cognitive Assessment-Blind Score, 0.11, 95% CI -1.44-1.70), executive function (trimmed mean difference in Hayling Sentence Completion Test, 0.38, 95% CI -0.97-1.76), disability status (trimmed mean difference in Activity of Daily Living Score, 0.03, 95% CI -0.84-0.90), mobility (trimmed mean difference in PROMIS Mobility Score, 0.72, 95% CI -2.20-3.64), and health-related quality of life (trimmed mean difference in EQ-5D-5L, -0.01, 95% CI -0.07-0.06). Outcomes also did not differ at 12 months.ConclusionsIn this long-term follow-up of a randomized controlled trial of patients with sepsis-induced hypotension, the restrictive fluid strategy used in this study resulted in similar cognitive and physical function at 6 and 12 months, compared to a liberal fluid strategy.

Scientific Knowledge on the Subject: Optimal fluid resuscitation strategies for sepsis-induced hypotension remain controversial. While the randomized CLOVERS trial found no difference in short-term mortality between a restrictive fluid strategy (prioritizing vasopressors and lower intravenous fluid volumes) or a liberal fluid strategy (prioritizing higher volumes of intravenous fluids before vasopressor use) among patients with sepsis-induced hypotension, their impact on long-term, patient-centered outcomes, including cognition, physical function, and quality of life, remains poorly understood. Observational and ancillary trial data have suggested that fluid strategies may differentially affect cognitive and physical recovery after critical illness, but high-quality randomized evidence addressing these outcomes were lacking.

What This Study Adds to the Field: In this multicenter, randomized long-term follow-up study of the CLOVERS trial, early restrictive versus liberal fluid resuscitation strategies resulted in similar cognitive function, physical disability, mobility, and health-related quality of life in sepsis survivors at 6 and 12 months. These findings suggest that the fluid resuscitation strategies used in this study did not meaningfully impact long-term patient-centered outcomes among survivors of sepsis-induced hypotension. Importantly, substantial long-term morbidity was observed across both groups, underscoring the persistent burden of post-sepsis impairment and highlighting the need for interventions beyond initial resuscitation to improve survivorship.

Introduction

Ideal fluid resuscitation strategies for patients with sepsis-induced hypotension have been debated for decades. The National Heart Lung and Blood Institute’s Prevention and Early Treatment of Acute Lung Injury (PETAL) Network’s Crystalloids Liberal Or Vasopressors Early Resuscitation in Sepsis (CLOVERS) trial was a large, nationwide, multicenter, randomized clinical trial to investigate whether an early restrictive fluid strategy, compared with a liberal fluid strategy, improves survival in patients with sepsis-induced hypotension.1 Most studies of critical care interventions, including CLOVERS, have been designed only to look at short-term clinical outcomes. However, because many patients and families value functional independence and cognitive outcomes more highly than survival-at-any-cost,2-4 it is important for interventional trials to investigate outcomes beyond mortality.5 In fact, surviving sepsis is frequently associated with a range of long-term impairments and a decline in functional capacity.6-8 Previous literature suggests that the type of post-sepsis impairment is potentially linked to the fluid resuscitation approach.9-11 A secondary analysis of the FACTT trial suggested that a fluid-restrictive strategy was associated with cognitive impairment.9,10 Conversely, volume overload at ICU discharge in patients with septic shock has been associated with physical impairment.11 Further, liberal fluid strategies are associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation, which, in turn, are associated with weakness and worse functional outcome.9,12 While the CLOVERS trial found no significant difference in 90-day in-hospital mortality between treatment groups, the effect of fluid resuscitation approaches on long-term, patient-centered outcomes is unknown. The Sepsis-induced Hypotension: Assessing effect of Method of Resuscitation On patient Centered outcomes (SHAMROC) study investigates whether the initial fluid resuscitation strategy impacts cognitive outcomes and functional disability at 6 and 12 months in patients with sepsis-induced hypotension.

Methods

Study design and participants

The SHAMROC study was developed as an ancillary study that was subsequently incorporated into the parent prospective, randomized, controlled, multicenter CLOVERS trial following CLOVERS initiation. The SHAMROC study extended the CLOVERS trial follow-up period to compare the effect of an early restrictive fluid treatment strategy (prioritizing vasopressors and lower intravenous fluid volumes) with a liberal fluid treatment strategy (­prioritizing higher volumes of intravenous fluids before vasopressor use) for sepsis-induced-hypotension on 6- and 12-month patient-centered outcomes. The trial enrolled adults with suspected or confirmed infection and sepsis-induced hypotension (systolic blood pressure <100 mmHg after ≥1000 mL of intravenous fluid). Exclusion criteria were >4 hours since onset of refractory hypotension, >24 hours since hospital presentation, >3000 mL of fluid received during the episode, fluid overload, or severe non-sepsis-related volume depletion. The design and results of the original CLOVERS trial were published previously.1,13 The trial protocol was reviewed and approved by a central institutional review board and monitored by an NHLBI-appointed independent data and safety monitoring board. Written informed consent for study participation was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives. Data for this analysis were obtained from the NHLBI Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center. The present analysis was approved by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (BioLINCC ID 16945).14,15

Patients were potentially eligible for participation in SHAMROC if they were previously included in the original CLOVERS trial. Upon enrollment in the CLOVERS trial, baseline cognitive screening (Ascertain Dementia 8-Item Informant Questionnaire), baseline disability status (Activities of Daily Living [ADL]), the current living situation, contact information, and language spoken by the patient were collected from patients or their proxies. Exclusion criteria were severe premorbid cognitive impairment or disability (determined by screening Ascertain Dementia 8 [AD8] Score > 4 or ADL Score ≥ 4), ­fluency in neither English nor Spanish, and homelessness.

Trial procedures

The detailed treatment protocol of the CLOVERS trial has been described previously.13 In brief, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either a restrictive fluid strategy with early vasopressor use or a liberal fluid strategy. The restrictive fluid protocol prioritized vasopressors and permitted “rescue fluids” in case of severe volume depletion. The liberal fluid protocol recommended an initial 2 L intravenous infusion with consecutive boluses administered based on clinical triggers (eg, tachycardia). Clinical teams guided by a trial team and study protocol determined the use of vasopressors and fluids for 24 hours. Fluid ­management was not protocolized after the initial 24 hours of randomization.

Assessments

Data on demographics, comorbidities, acute illness variables, data describing hospital course, and hospital outcomes were obtained as part of the CLOVERS trial.1 SHAMROC-specific baseline data (AD8 cognitive screen, ADL assessment, living situation, primary language spoken) were obtained from patients or their proxies at the time of enrollment in the CLOVERS trial. After participants completed the CLOVERS trial, data collection continued to capture the remaining hospital course, demographic data, hospital outcome, destination of discharge, and contact information. All participants were contacted by the University of Michigan, University of Washington, or Oregon Health & Science University for participation in a telephone assessment of cognitive function and disability after 6 and 12 months. Surveys were conducted in either English or Spanish (the Hayling Sentence Completion Test [HSCT] was only available in English). Subjects completed 7 instruments, for an estimated total call duration of 20-30 minutes.

Outcomes

Physical function was assessed using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Mobility Score. The PROMIS Mobility assessment is scored from 10-90 with higher scores indicating better physical function.16 A PROMIS Mobility Score of <40 was considered indicative of moderate impairment, while values <30 denoted severe impairment.17,18 A score of 10 indicates an inability to do any of the queried tasks.

Disability was assessed using the Katz ADL and Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (i/ADL) instrument. The ADL instrument is scored from 0 to 13 with a score of 13 indicating disability in all items.19 An ADL score of ≥ 1 indicated some disability in at least one domain.20 Cognition was assessed using the telephone Montreal Cognitive Assessment-Blind Score (MoCA-Blind Score). The MoCA-Blind score ranges from 0 to 22, with scores <19 indicating cognitive impairment.21-23 Table S1 provides test details. Executive function assessment, a specific domain of cognitive function, was assessed using the HSCT, ranging from 2-23 with higher scores indicating better executive function. The exact individual items and calculation algorithm of the HSCT is provided in Table S2. As no established thresholds for impairment exist, we derived data-driven thresholds by converting the normative mean and 95% confidence interval of the 60-year-old reference group reported by Bielak et al to our scoring range, using the lower bound of the interval (12 points) to define impaired executive function. Health-related quality of life was assessed using the EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D-5L). EQ-5D-5L responses are converted to index scores that are validated for the U.S. population.24 An index value of 1.0 indicates perfect health, a value of 0.0 indicates death. Because the mean EQ-5D-5L index score for U.S. adults aged 55-64 years is 0.82 (95% CI 0.78-0.85), a score below the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval was interpreted as impaired quality of life.25 Importantly, these thresholds are derived from published reference data but lack universal validation; thus, the resulting impairment categories should be interpreted carefully.

If participants were unable to complete the assessments themselves, proxies were permitted to complete the PROMIS Mobility and ADL surveys, as well as the AD8 cognitive assessment, which served as a substitute for the MoCA-Blind Score. The AD8 is an 8-item informant-based questionnaire used to screen for dementia and cognitive impairment and is completed by proxies rather than patients themselves.26 The primary analysis of cognitive impairment used the MoCA-Blind Score but not the AD8 Score. The HSCT could be completed only by patients and not proxies.

Statistical analysis and sample size considerations

Categorical variables are presented as counts with percentages, whereas continuous variables are summarized as means with standard deviations for normally distributed data or as medians with interquartile ranges for non-normally distributed data. Differences between baseline characteristics between the restrictive fluid group and liberal fluid group were tested using Fisher’s exact test or independent t test (or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed data).

Differences in short-term mortality can interfere with the assessment of patient-centered, long-term outcomes of treatment interventions, a problem commonly referred to as “informative censoring,” or “censoring of the extreme phenotype.” While no solution to this problem is universally accepted,27 the predefined analysis plan stipulated the use of the approach of Permutt and Li to mitigate bias introduced by potential differences in short-term survival.28 This approach equates death with extremely poor cognitive function, which cannot be assessed on the same numeric scale as the MoCA-Blind Score or HSCT. In accordance with the prespecified analysis plan, deceased participants were assigned the worst possible outcome score for each patient-centered endpoint prior to application of the trimming procedure. Using the method suggested by Permutt and Li, we calculated an empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) for the fraction of participants with cognitive impairment, considering all possible dichotomizations of the cognition scores (including informative non-response due to poor cognition and death as the most extreme forms of poor cognition). The mean outcome scores among the better-performing participants from each study arm (“trimmed means”) are efficient statistics for reporting the between-group differences using the intention-to-treat principle. Because over 38% of the analysis cohort of this study died before follow-up, the lower 50% of participants for each patient-centered outcome analysis were trimmed. Under this trimming, the test statistic can be interpreted as a mean difference in cognitive function caused by randomization to restrictive versus liberal fluid resuscitation, assessed in the better-off half of participants. We used a permutation test proposed by Permutt and Li to calculate confidence intervals under no assumptions other than randomization. We report both the test statistics (with 95% CI) and graphically present the ECDF for each randomized group. To evaluate the robustness of our results, we performed sensitivity analyses with trimming of 1/3 (33.3%) of the participants, 2/3 (66.7%) of the participants, and no trimming. As exploratory analysis, we investigated long-term outcomes and individual test items of participants who were alive at 6 and 12 months, respectively. Missing data were not imputed. Only complete and interpretable test results were included.

In contrast to the anticipated sample size of 2320 participants, the original CLOVERS trial included only 1563 participants. The trial was halted as recommended by the data and safety monitoring board for futility at the second interim analysis. Sample size considerations for SHAMROC were based on the originally estimated 2320 participants. It was assumed that 20% of participants in CLOVERS would be ineligible for SHAMROC due to severe pre-existing cognitive impairment or disability, languages other than English or Spanish, or lack of telephone or permanent address, leaving 1856 potentially eligible for SHAMROC enrollment. Statistical analysis and visualization were performed using R (version 4.1.2, 2021, Vienna, Austria) and RStudio (RStudio Team, 2024).

In the overall cohort, irrespective of treatment allocation, impaired cognitive function (MoCA-Blind < 19) was present in 199 (65.5%) of 304 survivors with available 6-month data (Table S10). Functional disability (ADL ≥ 1) was reported by 298 (69.3%) of 430 survivors at 6 months. Based on PROMIS Mobility Scores, 114 survivors (26.5%) had moderate impairment (score < 40), and 80 (18.6%) had severe impairment (score < 30). Among 421 survivors, 212 (50.4%) had impaired quality of life at 6 months (EQ-5D-5L index score < 0.78). None of these proportions differed significantly between the two groups. Corresponding 12-month outcomes are shown in Table S11.

Among survivors with repeated assessments, the mean ADL score increased from 0.67 (SD 0.99) at baseline to 3.24 (SD 3.67) at 6 months (mean difference, 2.57 points, 95% CI, 2.22-2.92). The proportion of survivors with at least one ADL disability increased from 162 (37.7%) to 298 (69.3%) of 430 participants at 6 months (mean difference, 31.6%, 95% CI, 25.2-38.0).

Discussion

The SHAMROC trial revealed no significant differences in long-term patient-centered outcomes between a restrictive fluid strategy or a liberal fluid strategy for sepsis-induced hypotension. At 6 and 12 months, SHAMROC participants had similar cognitive functions, executive functions, health-related quality of life, disability status, and mobility, irrespective of group assignment. These findings parallel the neutral outcome of the main CLOVERS trial and suggest the overall equivalence of restrictive and liberal fluid strategies in terms of both short-term and long-term efficacy.

Evidence on the comparison of restrictive versus liberal fluid resuscitation strategies on patient-centered, long-term outcomes is scarce.29 As an ancillary study to the FACTT trial, which compared a restrictive and a liberal fluid strategy applied for 7 days in 1000 patients with acute lung injury, the ARDS Cognitive Outcomes Study (ACOS) was a prospective, multicenter cohort study of a subset of survivors from FACTT that investigated neuropsychological function in 122 subjects at 2 and 12 months.10 The parent FACTT trial found no significant difference in the primary outcome of 60-day mortality between treatment groups; yet the restrictive fluid strategy improved lung function and shortened the duration of mechanical ventilation. These treatment benefits were counterbalanced in the ACOS study, which found that randomization to a restrictive ﬂuid strategy was associated with cognitive impairment as measured by a 45-60-minute neuropsychological test battery.10 In contrast, long-term results from the CLASSIC trial were unable to confirm an association between a restrictive fluid strategy and cognitive impairment in patients with septic shock.30 In the CLASSIC trial, ICU patients with septic shock who had received at least 1 L of intravenous fluid were randomly assigned to receive restricted or standard intravenous fluid therapy. No difference in 90-day mortality was found between treatment groups. A pre-specified, long-term analysis of CLASSIC, including a total of 1326 patients, observed similar all-cause mortality, health-related quality of life and cognitive function at 1 year. Compared with SHAMROC and CLASSIC, which exclusively included patients with sepsis, the FACTT trial included patients with acute lung injury, of which most had sepsis or pneumonia. Moreover, unlike SHAMROC and CLASSIC, the conservative strategy in the FACTT trial combined restriction of fluid administration with active facilitation of urine output through diuretic use to achieve lower intravascular filling pressures, a distinction that may partly explain differences in observed long-term cognitive outcomes.10

Notably, participants enrolled in ACOS were younger than those in SHAMROC and CLASSIC. This raises the notion that younger patients may be more susceptible to harm from a restrictive fluid strategy, a possibility that warrants further investigation. In our subgroup analyses, both in the prespecified primary analysis using 50% trimming (Figure 4) and in the survivor-only analysis (Figure S5), results were consistent with a potential association between the restrictive fluid strategy and poorer cognitive outcomes among patients younger than 60 years.

Our findings do not support the hypothesis that treatment targeting a fluid-liberal/vasopressor-restrictive approach might preserve cognitive function by enhancing early cerebral perfusion, and, conversely, that a fluid-restrictive/vasopressor-intensive approach might improve physical outcomes by minimizing fluid overload. Two contrasting mechanisms might explain how resuscitation strategy might differentially impact long-term outcomes. A liberal fluid strategy could improve cognitive outcomes by optimizing cardiac output and cerebral perfusion in the initial hours of sepsis, thereby preventing brain injury responsible for long-term cognitive impairment. A restrictive fluid strategy could improve physical outcomes by mitigating fluid overload, resulting in less interstitial edema that might translate to earlier mobilization, less ventilator dependence, and preservation of neuromuscular function. However, our findings suggest that these theoretical mechanisms either did not occur to a meaningful extent or were offset by other factors. Furthermore, these results suggest that the mechanisms responsible for long-term cognitive and physical impairment after sepsis may be independent of early hemodynamic effects caused by either a liberal or restrictive fluid resuscitation strategy. Additional factors associated with long-term functional outcomes may include systemic and neuroinflammation, direct vascular injury, and circulating neurotoxic amyloid.31-34 In addition, the parent CLOVERS trial reported no increase in acute organ failures with the restrictive strategy and no significant difference in shock reversal or need for dialysis between groups, which also supports the notion that both strategies result in similar organ and tissue perfusion. Notably, the CLOVERS trial protocol only dictated the resuscitation approach for 24 hours; thereafter, fluid management was left to the provider. In contrast to FACTT, SHAMROC only evaluated the impact of early resuscitation, suggesting that cumulative fluid balance may be more impactful than early fluid management on patient-centered outcomes at 6 and 12 months.

Importantly, SHAMROC observed substantial overall long-term morbidity and mortality in both groups, underscoring the considerable long-term burden of sepsis and reinforcing prior literature on the challenges associated with critical illness survivorship.7,12,35 At 6 months, 431 (48.0%) of the 898 participants included in SHAMROC were alive and provided outcome data, 271 (30.2%) were dead, and 196 (21.8%) were lost to follow-up. Approximately two thirds (65.5%) of survivors in the SHAMROC trial exhibited some degree of cognitive impairment at 6 months. In addition, half (50.4%) of survivors reported a reduced health-related quality of life, and 69.3% experienced difficulties in ≥ 1 domain of ADL at 6 months, underscoring the substantial burden of long-term functional and cognitive sequelae in this population. Compared with baseline, survivors experienced a substantial increase in functional disability over time, with the mean ADL score increasing by 2.6 points and the proportion reporting ≥ 1 ADL limitation rising by 32 percentage points at 6 months. These findings tie into those from other studies showing that a significant proportion of ICU survivors suffer from new physical, mental, and/or cognitive problems.8,36-38 Similarly, recent data showed high rates of post-intensive care syndrome in sepsis survivors, which is characterized by cognitive decline, mental health disorders, and physical disability.39

Limitations

This trial should be interpreted in the context of its limitations. First, the intended sample size for this study was 2320 participants. Due to premature termination of the parent CLOVERS trial driven by futility and a higher loss to follow-up than expected, the final analysis sample size of 702 may have been insufficient to detect more subtle differences. Although exploratory analyses of participants lost to follow-up did not suggest meaningful between-group imbalances, residual attrition bias cannot be fully excluded. Considering the consistent absence of any effect signal, a substantial group difference seems unlikely. Furthermore, while previous analyses suggested that particular subgroups may respond differently to liberal or restrictive fluid strategies,40 the sample size of this study was too small to perform meaningful subgroup analyses. Ultimately, the possibility of a false negative result cannot be excluded. Second, the exclusion of homeless participants, participants unable to respond in English or Spanish, and those with severe premorbid cognitive impairment or disability may have introduced the risk of selection bias. Third, the open-label study design of the parent CLOVERS trial could potentially have influenced medical care or perception of patients. Fourth, although the cognitive assessments were administered and interpreted by trained investigators with prior experience using these instruments, a trained neuropsychologist was not directly involved in the study, which may have further strengthened the assessment and interpretation of the neurocognitive outcomes. Moreover, this study used the telephone-based MoCA-Blind, which provides a less comprehensive cognitive assessment compared with more detailed telephone-based instruments used in prior studies.22,41,42 Fifth, this analysis estimated an average treatment effect based on randomization to one of the two fluid resuscitation strategies rather than actual treatment received. Overlap in treatment exposures between groups may have attenuated observed differences. Lastly, the trial population largely included patients with sepsis-induced hypotension without shock and extensive organ failure and only 20% of whom required mechanical ventilation. Whether our findings translate to long-term patient-centered outcomes of patients with more severe illness remains unknown.

Conclusion

In this long-term follow-up study of a randomized controlled trial of patients with sepsis-induced hypotension, the restrictive fluid strategy used in this study resulted in similar cognitive and physical function at 6 and 12 months, compared to a liberal fluid strategy.
