MultiECGNet: Evaluation of a Deep-Learning Ensemble Method for Image-Based 12-Lead Electrocardiographic Detection of Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: George Parker, Justin Phan, Nigel H. Lovell, Rajesh N. Subbiah, Jamie I. Vandenberg, Adam P. Hill, Ahmadreza Argha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence holds significant promise for analysis and interpretation of electrocardiograms (ECGs) in cardiovascular disease. However, many ECGs are still stored as paper records or static images, necessitating deep-learning models that can operate on image-based ECG formats. This study aims to evaluate the performance of an ensemble classifier, MultiECGNet, using multi-format ECG images for the diagnosis of atrial fibrillation (AF).MethodsAn ensemble classifier was developed using 4 models derived by truncating the pretrained EfficientNet B3 model at different feature extraction layers. Transfer learning was employed to train the ensemble on the publicly available PTB-XL dataset for AF detection. External validation was performed using the Chinese Physiological Society Signal Challenge dataset. ECG samples were converted into 2 image formats (2 x 6 and 4 x 3), and performance was evaluated across same-format, cross-format, and mixed-format classification tasks.ResultsThe classifier detected AF in the validation dataset with an accuracy of 0.95 with an F1-score of 0.87, comparable to a signal-based model (F1-score: 0.87 vs 0.83) and better than a single EfficientNet-B3 model (F1-score: 0.87 vs 0.71). Training on one ECG format and testing on a different format resulted in reduced performance (F1-score: 0.66-0.71). However, training on a dataset containing a mix of both formats closely matched the performance of the original same-format training (F1-score: 0.86).ConclusionsThe proposed image-based ensemble classifier demonstrated comparable performance to a strong signal-based model for AF detection. Although cross-format generalization posed a challenge, incorporating multiple ECG formats during training mitigated this limitation and improved model robustness.

Electrocardiography (ECG) is a widely used, noninvasive diagnostic tool that records the electrical activity of the heart. ECG analysis is a critical diagnostic method for detecting a range of cardiovascular conditions, such as arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, and other heart abnormalities.1 One important use of ECG analysis is in the detection of atrial fibrillation (AF). AF-related ischemic strokes and transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) are leading contributors to recurrent stroke risk, which can be reduced significantly through appropriate oral anticoagulant therapy. Traditionally, ECG analysis has relied on expert interpretation and rule-based algorithms. However, the emergence of deep learning has introduced a paradigm shift in the analysis of ECG data.

Two model inputs can be utilized when training neural networks on ECG data—the raw digital signals exported from the ECG recording device, or the ECG traces in the form of a paper scan or electronic image file. Although many successful deep-learning applications in ECG analysis have utilized one-dimensional convolution neural networks (1-D CNNs) applied to raw digital signals, such data are not always readily available, particularly in retrospective studies.2 An image-based approach is particularly promising for application to large repositories of historical ECGs that are often stored in paper or digital scan formats instead of as raw digital signals. Additionally, models trained using image-based inputs can be implemented into smartphone applications usable in clinical settings. Therefore, methods using 2-dimensional CNNs (2-D CNNs) to analyze ECG images are gaining traction.

Recent work has shown that 2-D CNNs can be trained to predict a range of diseases. For example, Sangha et al. showed that a 2-D CNN trained on ECG images could predict echocardiographic left ventricular dysfunction with a sensitivity of 89% and a specificity of 77%.2 Also, 2D-CNNs have proven to be capable of distinguishing different rhythm abnormalities from ECG images. A study by Li et al. trained a 2D-CNN—Inception-V3—to predict 7 different kinds of arrhythmia from a singular cropped ECG lead with balanced recall of 95.43% and a specificity of 96.75% among the classes.3 Prior work also has demonstrated that image-based 2D-CNNs can perform comparably to signal-based 1D-CNNs, particularly when applied to smaller datasets. Ao and He achieved a sensitivity of 0.992 with their image-based model in predicting AF, which was comparable to the range of sensitivities reported by Jo et al. from their high-performing digital signal classifier (0.985-0.999).4,5

The success of application of 2D-CNNs in small data sets is partly due to the use of transfer learning, in which 2D-CNNs pretrained on large image datasets (such as ImageNet6) are fine-tuned on smaller ECG image datasets. This approach enables the model to leverage previously learned visual features, improving performance and reducing the risk of overfitting when labelled ECG data are limited. For example, Wu et al. used AlexNet, a 2D-CNN architecture, to classify normal vs abnormal QRS complexes based on ECG images, achieving an accuracy of 98% in detecting abnormalities.7 2-D CNNs also have been trained to classify ECG images with multiple complex diagnostic labels (eg, left bundle branch block, right bundle branch block, and AF) across different ECG formats (2 x 6 and 4 x 3 layouts).2 Sangha et al. also have compared the performance of a signal-based classifier to an image-based classifier using Google’s EfficientNetB3 (Google, Mountain View, CA) with pretrained weights from ImageNet (Google, Mountain View, CA). Given a complex multi-label classification task detecting conduction pathologies, the image-based classifier achieved a greater weighted F1 score, of 0.770, across all labels, compared the to the signal-based classifier (F1 score of 0.653).8 Other implementations of deep learning have processed ECG image formats to obtain the original signal data. For example, Policastro et al. retrieved digital signal data from ECG images using PMcardio digitization technology (Powerful Medical, New York, NY) before training their model to detect occlusion myocardial infarction, achieving performance similar to that of ECG experts, with a sensitivity of 80.6% and a specificity of 93.7%.9

The studies highlighted here have striven to improve the performance of both digital signal- and image-based classification of ECGs, particularly on multilabel classification tasks in the domain of rhythm-based heart disease. Aside from Sangha et al.,8 many of these studies fail to acknowledge the impact of ECG formatting on image-based classification. Training methods need to be developed further for ECG-image classifiers to perform robust predictions on new data that have been collected in a variety of layouts.

Using ECG images for machine learning, however, also presents unique challenges compared to digital signal-based approaches. Notably, neural networks may inadvertently learn from nonphysiological features, such as the layout of ECGs, which must be considered in model design and evaluation. To our knowledge, only one study has evaluated the performance of an image-based deep-learning model trained to classify ECG images with multiple diagnostic labels (eg, left bundle branch block, right bundle branch block, and AF) across different ECG formats (2 x 6 and 4 x 3 layouts). Another issue when using deep learning on small datasets is the risk of overfitting. Ensemble learning, a technique that combines predictions from multiple models, has been developed as a method to mitigate the risk of overfitting and has been shown to improve performance compared to using a single network.10

Accordingly, in this study, we aimed to test the hypothesis that ensemble learning can enhance the performance of a moderately sized ECG image–based deep-learning model (trained on thousands of ECGs) for the identification of AF, regardless of the format in which the ECG data are presented. We developed an ensemble of truncated networks based on the publicly available EfficientNet B3 architecture,11 and compared its performance to that of a single EfficientNet B3 model, as well as to a signal-based deep-learning approach for classifying ECGs with and without AF. Using our ensemble of models, called MultiECGNet, we investigate the impact of this ensemble technique on generalizability to external datasets and study the performance of the individual ensemble members on cross-format classification.

Methods

Data acquisition and pre-processing

We used data from the publicly available PTB-XL dataset,12 accessible via PhysioNet (physionet.org/content/ptb-xl/1.0.3/). The dataset comprises 21,799 12-lead ECGs, collected from 18,869 patients, that were annotated by up to 2 cardiologists. The ECG and patient data had been deidentified before public release, with only clinical data relating to the cardiologist’s diagnosis preserved. Within the dataset, 5071 ECGs were annotated as showing AF. The ECGs in the PTB-XL dataset were collected with a sampling frequency of 500 Hz.

The raw ECG signal data were converted into electrocardiographic images using the Python ecg-plot function.13 By default, this function renders the ECG in a 2 x 6 lead format, displaying 5 seconds of data per lead. We modified the function to also generate images in a 4 x 3 lead layout, where each lead displays 2.5 seconds of data. Examples of both ECG formats are presented in Figure 1. All images were resized to 300 x 300 pixels for compatibility with the EfficientNet B3 CNN.Figure 1Sample 12-lead electrocardiogram images generated using the ecg-plot function: (A) the original output; and (B) 2 x 6 format (left) and 4 x 3 format (right) reduced to 300 x 300 pixels to match the input required for the EfficientNetB3 algorithm.

Sample 12-lead electrocardiogram images generated using the ecg-plot function: (A) the original output; and (B) 2 x 6 format (left) and 4 x 3 format (right) reduced to 300 x 300 pixels to match the input required for the EfficientNetB3 algorithm.

The labels “atrial fibrillation” (AF) and “not atrial fibrillation” (not AF) were used for binary classification. The PTB-XL dataset was separated into 3 subsets using a stratified split to preserve class distribution—60% for training, 20% for validation, and 20% as a holdout set for model testing. External validation was subsequently performed using the publicly available Chinese Physiological Signal Challenge (CPSC) dataset, which contains 6877 12-lead ECG recordings, of which 1094 were annotated as AF.14 The ECGs in the CPSC dataset were collected with a sampling frequency of 500 Hz.

Model architecture

EfficientNet

EfficientNet is a family of 2-D CNNs, developed by Google Research, designed to achieve high accuracy while optimizing computational efficiency.11 EfficientNet B3, the fourth model in this family, offers a strong balance between performance and computing resource consumption (see Supplemental Table S1). The model requires input images of 300 x 300 pixels, includes 388 layers, and contains over 10 million trainable parameters. Initially trained on the ImageNet dataset, which includes over 1000 object categories, EfficientNet B3 can be fine-tuned for downstream tasks using transfer learning,15 making it well-suited for applications such as ECG image classification.

Model variants

Four ensemble-based classification strategies were developed and compared.

Snapshot ensemble

The first approach used a snapshot ensemble (Fig. 2A), in which a single model is saved at different stages of training. A single EfficientNet B3 model was fine-tuned for 500 epochs using cosine annealing, as described by Huang et al.,16 with the learning rate undergoing 50 annealing cycles. At the end of each cycle, a snapshot of the model was saved, resulting in 50 distinct models.Figure 2The 4 different training strategies employed to create the model ensembles: (A) the Snapshot Ensemble; (B) the FreezNet strategy using pretrained ImageNet weights (https://www.image-net.org/); (C) the RetrainNet strategy; and (D) the DiverseNet strategy using the retrained weights from RetrainNet. Blue signifies the frozen layers of the network, and orange signifies unfrozen layers that are retrained.

The 4 different training strategies employed to create the model ensembles: (A) the Snapshot Ensemble; (B) the FreezNet strategy using pretrained ImageNet weights (https://www.image-net.org/); (C) the RetrainNet strategy; and (D) the DiverseNet strategy using the retrained weights from RetrainNet. Blue signifies the frozen layers of the network, and orange signifies unfrozen layers that are retrained.

Partially frozen fine-tuning (FreezNETs)

This fine-tuning method was adapted from the soft cut approach described by Hansun et al.17 In this approach, EfficientNet B3 models were initialized with pretrained weights. All layers prior to a selected point in the network were frozen (ie, weights were not updated during training), whereas the subsequent layers remained trainable (Fig. 2B). This approach enabled the fine-tuning of deeper features without altering the lower-level representations learned during pretraining. The pretrained classification layers were removed, and new classifiers were attached to accommodate the classification task. These classifiers consisted of an average pooling layer, 2 fully connected layers (with randomly selected sizes from [32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024]), a dropout layer, and a final classification layer. The selected points for freezing were at layers 125, 198, 272, or 385—corresponding to the end of key squeeze-and-excitation blocks, which play a central role in the network’s feature-extraction hierarchy.

Full fine-tuning (RetrainNETs)

In this setup, 4 truncated variations of the pretrained EfficientNet B3 were created by extracting features from the same 4 layers chosen in the FreezNets approach—125, 198, 272, or 385—but here, all layers after the truncation were removed (Fig. 2C). Remaining layers were unfrozen (ie, weights were allowed to update during training), and a new classifier with similar randomized architecture to the classifiers described in the FreezNets was attached to each of the models. Jo et al. proposed technique for creating an ensemble using a handful of different 2-D CNN feature extractors and demonstrated an improvement in performance over the individual models.5 By using an ensemble of these truncated efficient models, we similarly aim to leverage the diverse predictive capabilities of varying depth models while reducing the computational strain of training and implementing multiple full-sized models.

Feature-based ensemble with diverse classifiers (DiverseNETs)

Based on the fully fine-tuned RetrainNETs, classification heads were removed, and the remaining networks were frozen to serve as fixed-feature extractors (Fig. 2D). Hansun et al. demonstrated an improvement to generalizability and performance using 10 distinct networks to generate a probability distribution for each class output.17 Adapting this technique for each of our frozen feature extractors, 10 separate classifiers were trained with similar architectures and randomized sizes to those of the classifiers described in the FreezNet approach. The resulting output of the ensemble consisted of 40 probability scores for the classification of AF. This setup introduced diversity into the ensemble, enhancing its robustness and generalizability.

Soft voting for final classification

A soft voting system was used to generate the final classification output from the ensemble models, with the predicted class determined by averaging the probability scores from each model and selecting the class with the highest average probability.

DigitalNet

For benchmarking purposes, our imaged-based classifier was compared to a model trained on raw ECG signal data. We used the neural network developed by Cai et al.18 Their model was originally trained on 16,557 samples from the CPSC and local 12-lead ECG datasets, achieving high performance over 99% accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score for binary classification between AF and normal rhythm. In our study, this model was retrained from scratch using the PTB-XL dataset, following the original training pipeline provided by the authors. ECG signals were trimmed to 10-second lengths, and z-score was normalized according to the methods described by Cai et al.18 Samples that were not at least 10 seconds long were rejected from training and inference.

Training parameters and hardware

We employed transfer learning by initializing model weights with pretrained EfficientNet B3 weights. The image-based models were trained using the Adam optimizer,19 with a learning rate of 1 x 10–5 and a mini-batch size of 32. Early stopping was applied, terminating training if the validation loss did not improve over 10 consecutive epochs. The digital-based classifier was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 x 10–4 and a mini-batch size of 32, with 10-epoch early stopping also applied. Training was performed using an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU (NVIDIA, Santa Clara, CA) with 48 GB of RAM. All code was developed in Python 3.11.9 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE), using Keras 2.9.0 (https://keras.io/) as the front end, with a TensorFlow 2.9.1 (https://www.tensorflow.org/) back end.

Assessing the impact of ECG lead formats on image-based classification

First, to assess the effect of cross-format classification, all image-based models were trained using either 2 x 6 or 4 x 3 image formats from the PTB-XL dataset, with external validation performed using the opposite format from the CPSC dataset. Second, to evaluate the effect of combining lead formats, a training set was created by including both 2 x 6 and 4 x 3 images for each ECG from the PTB-XL dataset, effectively duplicating each ECG in 2 formats. External validation was then performed on a similarly combined CPSC dataset. Finally, to investigate the effect of training on a mixture of lead formats without duplication, a training set was generated by including an equal proportion of 2 x 6 and 4 x 3 images from the PTB-XL dataset, with each ECG represented only once. External validation was again performed on a similarly mixed-format CPSC dataset.

A class activation mapping technique, called gradient-weighted class activation map (GradCAM) was used to visualize the regions of the ECG images that most influenced the classification decisions.2,4 The GradCAM heatmaps were created using a weighted average of the probability scores across the models in the 40-classifier RetrainNets ensemble.

Excluded samples due to signal artefacts

A total of 12 samples were excluded from the datasets (1 from PTB-XL and 11 from CPSC) as they caused errors when processed by the DigitalNet. Each of these samples was reviewed manually by a cardiac electrophysiologist (J.P.), who confirmed that in every case, one or more leads demonstrated artefact consistent with poor electrode contact.

Performance metrics

Each model’s performance was evaluated using the metrics of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Confidence intervals (Cis) for performance metrics were calculated using the method described by Takahashi et al.,20 available at https://github.com/jacobgil/confidenceinterval.

TRIPOD AI guidelines checklist

A completed TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis) AI guidelines checklist is included in Supplemental Table S2, with page references to relevant sections of the study. As the datasets used for training and validation are publicly available and de-identified, items that are not relevant to this study have been marked not applicable (N/A).

PTB-XL can be accessed from physionet.org/content/ptb-xl/1.0.3/, while CPSC 2018 can be accessed from https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/physionet/china-physiological-signal-challenge-in-2018.

Patient consent

No patient consent was required for this study, as all data used have been collected, de-identified, and released as publicly available databases.

Mobile scan dataset performance

One key limitation of the dataset used for model training is the uniformity of the synthetic ECG plots. In a clinical implementation, input images are likely to be scans or digital photographs of paper ECG traces. To determine the impact of a photo-based dataset on the predictive performance of our trained model ensemble, a dataset comprising 100 AF ECGs and 100 ECGs without AF from PTB-XL was printed to A4-size sheets. ECGs used in this dataset were selected from the PTB-XL hold-out subset used for model testing and evaluation, to guarantee no data leakage occurred. The dataset included 4 variations of ECG layouts (see Fig. 6), with each ECG sample being represented only once. The printed ECGs were photographed using 3 different smartphone models—Samsung Galaxy S23 FE using the notes app (Samsung, Seoul, South Korea), iPhone 12 Pro Max (Apple, Cupertino, CA) using the cam scanner app, and an iPhone 15 Pro (Apple, Cupertino, CA) using the scanner function. The phones and applications used for collection performed automatic contrast adjustment and cropping on the mobile scanned dataset. All samples were photographed in the same room with adequate lighting. The extent of automatic image processing and contrast correction varied among smartphone models, introducing a variety of potential image artefacts into the dataset, including shadowing and overcropping. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that a novel ensembling approach can enhance the performance of image-based ECG analysis to a level comparable to that of a 1-D CNN trained on digital signals. For AF classification, our ensembles of neural networks, including RetrainNETs, Snapshot Ensemble, and DiverseNETs, achieved performance that was comparable to that of a high-performing digital signal–based model, and clearly outperformed a single EfficientNet B3 model. Previous work by Alsayat et al. showed that ensemble learning using different CNN architectures can improve ECG classification performance.10 However, our proposed method streamlines the input data processing, as the ensemble members share similar architectures, unlike traditional ensembles, which typically combine models with different structures and input requirements. By training the largest efficient net ensemble member first, weights were able to be transferred to the next member by performing layer truncation on the previously trained model. This method reduces the computational strain of training multiple different full-sized models from scratch.

Our study demonstrates that the format of ECG images used for training and classification is a critical factor in image-based deep-learning approaches. When models were trained on either 4 x 3 or 2 x 6 images and tested on a different format (cross-format classification), a notable decline in performance occurred, compared to same-format classification. However, incorporating both formats into the training dataset, either by duplicating ECGs in both formats or using a balanced mix, substantially mitigated this performance drop. As shown, classification performance using either the combined or mixed-format training datasets was comparable to that of same-format training and testing. We refer to the model trained on the multiformat dataset as MultiECGNet. MultiECGNet demonstrated robust performance across both ECG formats, achieving results similar to those with same-format classification, while avoiding the performance degradation seen in cross-format testing. Overall, these findings suggest that training with a mixed-format ECG dataset, as done in MultiECGNet, effectively reduces the adverse effects of cross-format classification and supports more-robust model generalization.

Of note, the full EfficientNet B3 model (model 385 in Fig. 4) consistently demonstrated poor and low-confidence predictions during cross-format classification. This result suggests that it may be more sensitive to layout-specific features in ECG images. However, removing this model from the ensemble led to an overall decline in performance, as indicated by the reduced F1-score. Therefore, despite its suboptimal performance in isolation, this model contributes complementary information that enhances the ensemble's overall effectiveness.

Our models achieved performance in AF classification comparable to the results reported by Sangha et al.,2 despite being trained on a much smaller dataset—21,000 ECGs from the PTB-XL dataset, compared to 2.7 million ECGs used in the Sangha et al. study. This result suggests that our approach may be particularly valuable for studying less-common conditions in which large historical datasets are not available. An additional advantage of the ensemble learning approach is its flexibility: different models within the ensemble may exhibit varying sensitivity and specificity. This variation can be leveraged in clinical applications in which specific performance characteristics are prioritized. For example, in scenarios requiring high sensitivity, such as ruling out myocardial infarction, a model favouring sensitivity over specificity may be more appropriate.

The classification threshold indicated by the maximum Youden Index in Figure 3 represents the optimal threshold at which false negatives and false positives are equally minimized. However, relying solely on this classification threshold would likely hold negative implications in producing a small but significant false-positive prediction rate, with potentially healthy patients receiving unnecessary rhythm correction treatment. Da’Costa et al. discuss the role and impact of AI use in patient triage, particularly in improving resource allocation and providing faster care for patients at greater risk.21 An important point that they highlight is the need for “human in the loop” systems, wherein the clinician makes an informed decision based on the magnitude of the model prediction scores, along with sensitivity and sensitivity calibration data.

We also confirmed that the heart rate of the patient did not impact the performance of the model in terms of misclassifying AF. Supplemental Figure S2 displays the frequency histogram and probability density of the heart rates (in beats per minute) of the correct and incorrect AF classification. Although determining specifically what features are used by the model in classifying AF is difficult, the Grad-CAM technique in Figure 5 highlights regions of higher activation from the excitation layers, suggesting that features in these areas hold greater weight/influence in classifying the sample as AF.

A key advantage of our technique is that an image-based approach can leverage extensive historical ECG databases, which traditionally have been archived as image files in medical records. Although more recent ECG data increasingly are stored prospectively as digital signals, collecting a similarly large volume of high-quality digital ECG data for training deep-learning models would take considerable time and resources. In addition, image-based ECG analysis offers practical benefits in certain clinical settings. For example, remote and regional health services may rely on smartphone camera-based ECG transmission.22 Given this context, a robust image-based analysis pipeline remains highly relevant, even as digital signal acquisition becomes more widespread in prospective data collection.

Finally, an additional benefit of our approach is its potential applicability to other areas of medicine that rely on diagnostic imaging, such as pathology and radiology. For example, in a previous study, a single EfficientNet B3 model was trained on 149,130 histologic images of various deep myxoid soft tissue lesions, achieving an accuracy of over 97%, compared to 69.7% accuracy by pathologists.23 Our ensemble-based approach could further improve performance in such contexts, particularly when only limited datasets are available.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the ensemble model approach is computationally more demanding than a single model, which may pose challenges when scaling to larger datasets. Such scaling requires increased computational resources and longer training times. Our ensemble also is computationally constrained to a 300 x 300 resolution image input. In preliminary testing, the performance increase between EfficientNet-B3 (300 x 300) and EfficientNet-B7 (600 x 600) did not justify the computational cost and time required by the larger model (Supplemental Table S1). However, the process of down-sampling image size can be considered an additional layer of dataset normalization, given the varying photo scan quality among mobile devices (Fig. 6). Future advancements in hardware and computing infrastructure may help mitigate this limitation.

Second, when evaluating the impact of ECG format on classification performance, we used only 2 formats, and both were represented equally in the training dataset. In real-world clinical settings, more than 2 formats may exist, often with an imbalance in their distribution, which may lead to a change in performance. In addition, we did not account for minor image rotations that are frequently present in clinical ECG images, such as those addressed in the Sangha et al. study.2 Other additional artefacts also are likely present in clinical scenarios, such as photos from a monitor, stained printouts, and creased paper. Although the presence of such rotations or image artefacts in both training and validation datasets may help mitigate this issue, it still represents a limitation in terms of real-world generalizability.

Finally, a key challenge that remains is the domain shift—that is, the reduced performance of models when applied to external or unseen datasets. This issue is common in machine learning and remains a major hurdle for the deployment of deep-learning models in clinical practice. Developing methods to address domain shift would significantly enhance the generalizability and clinical utility of our ensemble approach.

Conclusion

Our novel ensembling approach, which combines fine-tuned truncated variants of the EfficientNet B3 model, demonstrates improved performance over a single EfficientNet B3 model and achieves accuracy comparable to that of signal-based deep-learning methods. An important finding is that incorporating multiple ECG image formats during training enhances classification performance, underscoring the value of format diversity in image-based ECG analysis. This strategy supports broader applicability in clinical environments in which ECGs are often stored or transmitted as images.
