Armed conflict and child vaccination in Nigeria: the deprioritised preventive care
Authors: Dongqin Wang, Aidan Hollis, Bryan Mercurio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates how armed conflict influenced paediatric vaccination uptake in Nigeria. Combining Demographic and Health Survey data with detailed conflict event information, we examine the relationship between the intensity of reported conflicts near children’s residences and their vaccination probabilities. Our findings reveal that a 1 SD increase in conflict intensity reduced vaccination rates by 0.73–1.80 percentage points (between 2.10% and 4.60% of the mean). The results suggest that beyond disrupting healthcare infrastructure, conflict may affect vaccination rates through demand-side effects, where heightened security risks lead parents to deprioritise preventative health investments. These findings contribute to understanding vaccination challenges in conflict zones and inform targeted immunisation strategies.

Introduction

States affected by armed conflicts are burdened by insecurity and inadequate access to healthcare services, leading to lower healthcare utilisation and vaccination rates. This is particularly evident in sub-Saharan Africa, where more than half of the countries have witnessed conflicts since 1989 and still have active conflicts in 2020.1 2 The region also has the world’s lowest vaccination rates, with only 75% of children receiving basic immunisations, compared with a global average of 86%.3 Vaccination plays a crucial role in protecting against severe illness and death, with immunisation preventing an estimated 4.4 million deaths annually.4 Low vaccination rates lead to frequent vaccine-preventable disease outbreaks and also pose significant health risks to children,57 with vaccine-preventable diseases accounting for approximately 1.5 million childhood deaths annually in developing regions, particularly sub-Saharan Africa.8

Nigeria, as sub-Saharan Africa’s most populous nation, has experienced a dramatic surge in conflict, with nearly a 20-fold increase in recorded violent events in the last two decades,9 while having frequent endemic outbreaks and maintaining one of the world’s lowest vaccination coverage rates. According to UNICEF’s global child vaccination report, Nigeria has the highest number of children (approximately 30% of the child population) who have not received any vaccines among African countries, placing it as the country with the second highest number of zero-dose children in the world, following India.10 In 2024, the vaccination rate remains at a relatively low level: with an annual birth cohort of more than 8 million, delivering paediatric vaccination is a huge challenge in Nigeria.11

The combination of escalating conflict and persistently low vaccination coverage raises the critical research questions: to what extent and how does armed conflict affect child vaccination in Nigeria?

Whereas scholars and policy-makers have recognised that conflict is a key driver that impedes paediatric vaccination coverage,1214 there is limited evidence on how conflict affects parental vaccination decisions beyond the disruption of vaccine-related logistics and supply chains. Armed conflicts can influence parents’ risk perceptions and healthcare priorities through a behavioural mechanism. As conflict increases background risk and uncertainty, parents may develop heightened present-bias and consequently deprioritise preventive care like vaccination. Our study examines these demand-side behavioural responses to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how conflict affects vaccination beyond supply constraints. Our study could inform policy-makers about targeted immunisation strategies in conflict zones, where around 40% of the world’s unvaccinated and undervaccinated children reside.15

Challenges to vaccination in Nigeria

In this section, we present a targeted review of literature on vaccination challenges in Nigeria, building on existing systematic reviews while focusing specifically on factors affecting immunisation coverage in conflict settings.16 This approach allows us to contextualise our research questions and empirical findings effectively.

Despite free provision of tuberculosis, measles and polio vaccines in Nigeria, vaccination rates remain low due to various challenges including culture, religious beliefs, weak healthcare access and, as we demonstrate in this paper, armed conflict.

Culture, myths and misconceptions and religious beliefs are barriers to uptake of vaccines. Anecdotal evidence shows that people in some regions in Nigeria have poor knowledge of vaccine preventable diseases.17 In addition, the 1996 Pfizer drug trials in Nigeria, which resulted in deaths and disabilities among Muslim children, triggered widespread vaccine hesitancy that persisted for over a decade.18

Access—Access to vaccination is another important obstacle for many children. While vaccines are free, access may be challenging for various reasons, including difficulty in travelling to clinics, vaccine stockouts, staff absences and long queues.19 20

Armed conflict—Another major challenge to vaccination coverage is the prevalence of armed conflicts in Nigeria. The country has faced numerous security challenges, and these violent conflicts have persisted for decades. Conflicts arise from multiple motivations, including resource competition, religious tensions, historical ethnic grievances and governance failures that have created regional security vacuums. For example, armed groups in the Niger Delta compete for oil and those in central regions fight for arable land. One of the major sources of conflict is the Islamic terrorist group Boko Haram, which is fighting for the replacement of the secular Nigerian state with an Islamic one based on Sharia law throughout the country.21

The existing literature has documented the profound impacts that exposure to conflict can have on access to children’s vaccination. The pathways could be categorised as supply-side and demand-side channels.

The supply-side channel is straightforward: conflict can disrupt the availability and accessibility of childhood vaccination services. Infrastructure is destroyed and public services are disrupted. Health facilities may be damaged, destroyed or rendered inaccessible due to violence.22 23 Breakdowns in public financing for health could also undermine the local government’s ability to procure, store and distribute vaccines.24

On the demand side, which remains relatively unexplored, the sense of insecurity and heightened uncertainty in conflict-intensive zones may directly undermine parents’ motivation to invest in children’s vaccination as a form of long-term preventative healthcare. The insecurity comes from the trip to health facilities, which could expose their children and themselves to significant dangers like crossfire, roadblocks and other violence-related risks. Parents may choose to postpone vaccination visits in hopes of ‘safer times’. Beyond these physical risks, the perceived threat to their children’s survival and well-being could lead parents to have a diminished outlook on their children’s (and their own) life expectancy, resulting in reluctance to seek out vaccinations.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

The study does not involve patients and, therefore, there is no personal medical information reported in the article.

Data source

Demographic and health survey in Nigeria

We use Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data in Nigeria, which are repeated cross-sectional data and are nationally representative survey of Nigerian households.25 We use the 2003, 2008, 2013 and 2018 waves of DHS data and mainly draw data from the women’s survey, the under-5 children survey and the household survey.

Armed conflict location and event data

We use conflict data drawn from the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data (ACLED), which records real-time conflicts around the world since 1997 to the present.26 The ACLED project was designed to capture various violent and non-violent actions by political agents including governments, political parties, militias, rebel groups, rioters, protesters and civilians.26 The data contain exact event geolocation with latitude and longitude, date and the number of fatalities. Information on conflict events is derived from various sources, including local, regional, national media, reports from war zones, human rights organisations and governments.27

WorldPop

We use the population data provided by WorldPop, which offers gridded population estimates at a 100 m resolution.28 We then use the ArcGIS tool to calculate the total population within a 20 km radius buffer of each DHS cluster in a given year (see ‘Armed conflict’ section for reasons behind this distance).

Variables

Measure for vaccination

Our main outcome variables are childhood vaccination against three infectious diseases. We examine three types of vaccines, including the BCG vaccine for tuberculosis, polio 0, polio 1, polio 2, polio 3 and Measles. We also construct a ‘full’ vaccination index, which is 1 for children who received all six vaccines and 0 otherwise. The information on vaccination in DHS data is reported by the child’s mother, either based on her recall or on the records on a health card. We incorporate the age of vaccine administration recommended by WHO when measuring conflict exposure for each child to precisely match the timing of potential conflict exposure with the recommended vaccination schedule. WHO recommends that the BCG vaccine for tuberculosis be given at birth, the polio vaccine be given at birth, 6 weeks, 10 weeks and 14 weeks, and the measles vaccine be given at 9 months of age. In addition, we examine the impacts of conflict events on other health-seeking behaviours, including mothers’ behaviours on how they react to children’s fever or diarrhoea.29

Control variables

We include a set of control variables that may be associated with parents’ decisions on children’s vaccination, including child-level, month-level and household-level controls. Child-level controls include indicators for female children and multiple births, a discrete variable for birth order, and child’s age in months. Mother-level characteristics include mother’s age, total years of education and Muslim affiliation. Household-level controls include a household wealth index and indicators for electricity access and solid fuel usage as proxies for household assets not accounted for by the household wealth index. The demographic characteristics (eg, age, Muslim affiliation, birth order) to account for potential individual-level variations in cultural and social norms that affect vaccination; socioeconomic indicators (total years of education, household wealth index, household assets) to address the possibility that economic and educational status may influence mothers’ decision on children’s vaccination.

Measure for conflict intensity

In our analysis, we construct a conflict intensity index that captures localised exposure to conflict:

This index measures the total number of conflict events per 10 000 inhabitants within a 20 km radius of the child’s current residence within the 12 months prior to the child’s scheduled age for vaccination. Using the number of events per 10 000 inhabitants accounts for differences in population density. Therefore, we assess the impact of recent, proximate, population-adjusted conflict on vaccination behaviours and other healthcare-seeking behaviours. Figure 1 illustrates the time frame for accumulated conflict events prior to the scheduled age for each vaccine. For example, for polio 2, which is administered at 10 weeks of age, we measure conflict exposure by looking back 1 year prior to this vaccination age. This time frame spans from 42 weeks before the child’s birth up to 10 weeks after birth. The same approach is applied to other vaccines. For the full vaccination measure, we calculate the cumulative duration across all vaccines, from 52 weeks before birth to 36 weeks after birth. The conflict intensity, therefore, measures the frequency of conflict events that a child’s family would likely be aware of or affected by when making vaccination decisions. Specifically, it estimates how many conflict events occurred in their local area (within 1 day walking distance), relative to the local population size, during the year leading up to when their child was supposed to receive each vaccine.

The dependent variable Yiymis a dummy variable that equals one if the child i who was born in year y and month m was vaccinated with a given vaccine or set of vaccines. ConflictIntensityiym is the measure of regional conflict intensity which we defined in the previous section. Xiym are control variables at child, mother and household levels. We further include the child’s birth-year fixed effects, BirthYeariy, and birth-month fixed effects, BirthYearim, controlling for factors related to the timing of the child’s birth that may influence vaccination. This captures: (1) seasonal effects and yearly specific effects, considering that vaccination rates may fluctuate seasonally or across years due to seasonal health campaigns, availability of healthcare resources or variations in healthcare access in Nigeria; (2) epidemiological variations, considering that certain birth cohorts might experience different health risks or responses to disease outbreaks, which could impact vaccine demand or supply in those specific months or years. For instance, if a measles outbreak occurs during a certain month or year, vaccination rates may temporarily increase for children born shortly before or during the outbreak, as healthcare providers and parents prioritise immunisations to control the spread of disease; (3) economic or sociopolitical shifts, considering that economic conditions or political shifts could also vary over time and influence mothers’ decisions on vaccination for their kids. In addition, although we control for maternal religion (specifically, whether the mother is Muslim), we also include a regional fixed effect, γr, whether the child is located in the northern area or the southern area to control for the historical Muslim-Christian divide in Nigeria. This regional categorisation naturally aligns with the religious composition of the country, where the north is predominantly Muslim, while the south is predominantly Christian.30 31 This regional fixed effect thus captures systematic differences in social norms, institutional frameworks and healthcare-seeking behaviours that have evolved along religious lines but may not be fully captured by individual-level religious affiliation alone. The parameter of interest, β in the regression model, therefore, measures the impact of the intensity of conflict events on a child’s probability of vaccination conditional on a set of control variables and fixed effects. SEs are clustered at the DHS cluster level to take into account any within-cluster correlation of the error term, ϵiym.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Vaccination

Table 1 reports summary statistics for our analysis on vaccination and other health seeking behaviours. The vaccination rate among children under five in Nigeria is notably low, ranging from 38% for Measles to 52% for BCG, with fully vaccinated children showing the lowest rate at only 16%. The vaccination rates exhibit several distinct patterns. First, the vaccination rate for polio 0 (38%) is significantly lower than that for polio 1 (65%). This pattern could reflect administrative recording practices: children who miss polio 0 at birth but receive their first polio vaccine later are recorded as receiving polio 1, even though it is their first polio vaccination. Second, we observe a declining pattern across sequential polio doses, from polio 1 (65%) to polio 2 (56%) to polio 3 (38%). This decline could stem from a cumulative effect where only children who receive earlier doses proceed to later ones. When children miss earlier doses, it may reduce parents’ intention or willingness to pursue subsequent vaccinations, creating a cascade of missed immunisations.

Armed conflicts

Figure 2 illustrates the spatial distribution of conflict intensity across Nigeria in our analysis sample using the conflict intensity measure for full vaccination. Black dots represent DHS cluster locations, covering 1126 clusters for all 11 800 children in our analysis. Each circle represents a 20 km radius buffer zone around a DHS cluster. We use a 20 km buffer zone for two reasons. First, it is the distance that a typical person carrying a small child can walk within 1 day. Second, we take into account the random displacement of DHS clusters. The DHS survey intentionally displaces the actual Global Positioning System coordinates of the clusters to protect the confidentiality of respondents. For urban clusters, the coordinates are displaced by up to 5 km, and for 99% of rural clusters, by up to 5 km as well, while 1% of rural clusters may be displaced by up to 10 km. Since we use a 20 km radius of the DHS cluster, this does not systematically affect the measurement for conflict intensity. For children residing in the same buffer zone, we calculated their average conflict intensity for this diagram. (For our formal analysis, we calculate a child-specific intensity.) The shaded circles show these intensities in quintiles, with darker blue indicating higher intensity. As shown, conflict events are not uniformly distributed across the country, offering enough variation to support our empirical analysis.

Impacts on paediatric vaccination

Panel A in table 2 presents the results from our regression model. As we define the conflict intensity to be the number of conflict events within 12 months of the scheduled vaccine date for a given vaccine per 10 000 population, the coefficient in each cell is derived from a separate regression. We find that mothers’ exposure to conflict in the 12 months prior to their child’s scheduled vaccination decreases the probability of vaccination, and the effects are consistent across all vaccines. Specifically, we find that a 1 SD increase in conflict intensity reduces vaccination rates by 1.44 percentage points for BCG, representing a 2.76% decline relative to its sample mean, 1.43 percentage points for Polio0 (3.78% of mean). Both birth-dose vaccines, BCG and polio, demonstrate an effect of similar magnitude. Given that these vaccines should be administered at birth, this pattern suggests immediate disruption to healthcare access during childbirth, indicating the supply of vaccines or vaccine-related logistics might be disrupted. The effect could also be explained through the demand-side, where conflict exposure during pregnancy affects mothers’ decisions either to seek medical care at the time of birth or to bring their child for immediate postnatal vaccination.

For vaccinations scheduled after birth, we find that a 1 SD increase in conflict intensity reduces polio 1 rates by 2.79% relative to its mean, followed by reductions of 2.10%, 2.24% and 3.39% relative to mean rates for polio 2, polio 3 and measles, respectively. Unlike birth doses (BCG, polio), these vaccinations’ exposure windows capture post-birth conflict exposure. The persistent negative effects here provide strong evidence that post-birth conflicts also affect vaccination access and decisions.

Finally, these patterns point to a broader systemic issue in vaccination coverage, as evidenced by the largest relative decline of 4.60% in full vaccination status compared with its mean. This disproportionate effect on full vaccination suggests that both prenatal and postnatal conflict exposure substantially increase the risk of incomplete immunisation, highlighting the persistent and cumulative nature of conflict’s impact on child vaccination. The fact that complete vaccination, which already has the lowest base rate of 16%, experiences the largest relative decline, particularly underscores how conflict creates compounding barriers to achieving complete immunisation coverage.

It is also worth emphasising that our estimates represent a lower bound for the true effect. As parents may delay their children’s vaccinations beyond scheduled dates, creating a longer window during which conflict could affect vaccination timing, our conflict intensity measure only captures exposure up to the scheduled vaccination date, potentially missing conflict events that occurred during the delay period. This systematic undermeasurement of conflict exposure for delayed cases implies that our estimates should be interpreted as the minimum impact of conflict on vaccination, with actual effects potentially being larger.

Supply-side or demand-side?

In this section, we aim to investigate whether the impacts could be explained by the supply-side or demand-side channel. We first consider supply-side effects. Ideally, we would use data on measures for vaccination-related supplies, such as vaccine volumes, healthcare staff numbers per facility, or transportation disruption measures to examine whether armed conflicts affect them. However, such data are unavailable. Instead, we assess supply-side effects indirectly by examining whether the effect of conflict on vaccination differs between babies born in healthcare facilities versus those born at home. This comparison allows us to distinguish between supply and demand factors, as children born in healthcare facilities are already connected to the formal healthcare system and may face different barriers to vaccination than those born at home.

Home delivery in Nigeria is very common and it is usually affected by household and parents’ characteristics, such as mothers’ education level, religious beliefs and household wealth, which we have already controlled for in our model, rather than conflict.32 Similarly, children born at home commonly receive their vaccinations at local health centres or during outreach vaccination campaigns, making vaccination access an important consideration for all families regardless of birth location.33

Furthermore, our regression analysis reveals no significant relationship between conflict intensity and delivery place choice, indicating that conflict itself does not change mothers’ decisions to deliver at home versus in a healthcare facility. Nevertheless, we find that conflict significantly reduces vaccination rates among children delivered at home, while having no significant effect on children delivered in healthcare facilities, as shown in Panels B and C in table 2. This differential pattern, therefore, provides supportive evidence on the potential mechanisms (supply vs demand side) through which conflict affects vaccination.

If supply-side constraints (such as vaccine shortages or damaged health facilities) were the primary barriers in child vaccination, we would expect similar effects on vaccination rates for all children regardless of delivery location. Instead, the differential impact we observe suggests that demand-side factors might play a dominant role. Specifically, conflict appears to change preventive healthcare decision-making behaviours among specific groups (families of home-delivered children) rather than equally restricting vaccine supply for all.

We provide one of the plausible explanations for the significant effects on the home delivery population. Mothers who deliver at home may lack knowledge about vaccine importance, as they typically have lower educational attainment and are from poorer families.32 The increased uncertainty and risk perception in conflict environments may make these families more inclined to postpone non-urgent medical activities like vaccination. In contrast, mothers who deliver at facilities, who are usually more educated and wealthier, are more likely to adhere to child vaccination schedules they consider necessary.32

Taken together, our findings suggest that the observed negative effects of conflicts on children’s vaccination work plausibly occur through demand-side channels, disproportionately affecting different populations, rather than through universal supply-side constraints that would affect all children equally.

Maternal health-seeking behaviours for children

Having established that demand-side effects, rather than supply constraints, might be principally responsible for the impacts of conflicts on children’s vaccination, we now explore the specific behavioural mechanisms underlying this pattern. We analyse how parents in conflict zones make healthcare decisions that vary based on the nature of the health need. Specifically, we examine whether parents’ responses to immediate health concerns (such as childhood illnesses) differ from their approach to preventive care (such as vaccination). This comparison will help to understand the demand-side effect with an economic framework that we will discuss in the discussion section.

We examine how conflicts affect parents’ health-seeking behaviours when their children are sick, focusing on two common childhood illnesses: fever/cough and diarrhoea. Thus, here we estimate the impact of the frequency of conflict events that occurred in their local area (within 20 km radius) relative to the local population size, during the year leading up to their child’s birth, on parents’ healthcare decisions for their sick children.

We find that, across both types of illnesses and all specifications, there are no statistically significant negative effects of conflict on the propensity to seek medical care, indicating two key points. First, the absence of any effect of conflict intensity on the probability of a child being treated in a medical facility suggests that parents remain capable of accessing healthcare facilities when their child is sick, which further reinforces our earlier conclusion that supply-side constraints are not the primary barrier. Note that although healthcare facilities and services do face disruption during conflict, the degree varies by location and conflict intensity, with some access to them still remaining possible, though often becoming more inequitable.33 Second, it is particularly informative about the mechanism underlying parents’ healthcare decisions. Unlike vaccination, which is a preventive service with longer-term contingent benefits, childhood illnesses present relatively immediate and visible health risks. When children are sick, parents maintain their usual care seeking patterns even under conflict conditions, suggesting that the negative impact on vaccination is not driven by a general reluctance to access healthcare facilities during conflicts.

Taken together, our analysis points to a more nuanced pattern of decision-making: in conflict environments, even when facing similar challenges in healthcare service disruption, parents prioritise resources and risk-taking for immediate health threats (such as acute illness) while deprioritising long-term preventive interventions (such as vaccination). This differential response supports our inference that demand-side factors can drive healthcare utilisation patterns during conflicts. If supply-side constraints were the only barrier, we would expect to see similar negative effects on both vaccination and sickness treatment. Instead, the observed pattern suggests that parents make distinct healthcare decisions based on the immediacy and visibility of health risks and the nature of the healthcare needs, with a particular tendency to prioritise acute care over preventive services when operating under conflict conditions.

Boko Haram insurgency

Having demonstrated distinct patterns in health-seeking behaviours between immediate health needs and preventive care, with evidence suggesting demand-side factors drive parents’ responses to conflict-related risks, we now examine whether the type of conflict matters for vaccination decisions. Specifically, we investigate whether parents respond differently to conflicts based on the perpetrators’ characteristics. This analysis is policy-relevant: if certain conflict actors disproportionately affect vaccination rates, designed policies should be tailored to specific conflict types. Alternatively, if parents respond similarly to different conflict sources, policies should focus on addressing the general insecurity and uncertainty that arise from conflict events regardless of their origin. To this end, we examine the effect of Boko Haram insurgency.

Boko Haram is a highly active terrorist group that has carried out assassinations and large-scale acts of violence in Nigeria since 2009.34 35 Following intensification of the conflict after 2013, the insurgency has resulted in at least 20 000 deaths and induced an estimated 2.1 million people to flee their homes both within Nigeria and across international borders by 2015.36

Discussion

The heterogeneous impacts of conflict on parents’ access to different types of healthcare services offer important insights about household decision-making under uncertainty. Our findings suggest that conflicts fundamentally alter how parents evaluate and respond to different types of health risks.

Several mechanisms could explain the patterns we observe in our data. From an economic perspective, our findings can be explained using the concept of present-biased preference, where individuals discount future benefits in favour of immediate needs, and risk aversion, where individuals prefer outcomes with low uncertainty to those with high uncertainty. Heightened background risks, such as those uncertainties created by conflict in Nigeria, result in different responses to different healthcare needs.

Vaccination is a preventive service. It represents an investment in future health, where the benefits, such as protection from illness or death, are delayed, while the costs, especially the higher costs and risks in conflict zones, are immediate. In contrast, treatment for acute illnesses, such as fever/cough or diarrhoea, is a reactive response to immediate and visible health needs and the costs and benefits are realised immediately. If children do not receive adequate treatment, the perceived risks, such as discomfort or even death, outweigh the costs and risks of seeking care. Therefore, one of the plausible inferences is that heightened background risks resulting from conflict intensify present-biased preference, leading mothers to deprioritise long-term health investments in their children’s health such as vaccination, without affecting their willingness to address their children’s short-term sickness treatment needs. This is consistent with other evidence that exposure to violence leads to increased preference for certainty.37 38 This behaviour difference could also be attributed to another distinction between vaccination and illness: parents may feel that they can delay children’s vaccination until later, although the delay may result in vaccination never happening at all. Healthcare treatment for emergent illnesses, however, cannot be delayed.

Besides the economic framework, other factors, like health literacy (although we have controlled for mother’s educational attainment, it may not fully capture parents’ health literacy) and information challenges may also play a crucial role in explaining our findings. During conflict, access to reliable health information can be severely disrupted, potentially affecting parents’ understanding of vaccination benefits more than their recognition of acute illness symptoms. Some parents may question vaccine efficacy or have misconceptions. Unlike acute illnesses where symptoms are clearly visible and the need for treatment is obvious, the benefits of vaccination are preventive and, therefore, less immediately apparent, requiring greater health literacy to fully understand it.

Our differential findings between home-delivered and facility-delivered children further support these multiple explanations. Families already connected to the formal healthcare system through facility delivery are likely to have greater exposure to health information, potentially being less likely to respond to conflict-induced preventive care behaviours changes.

Our findings have important implications for health policy in conflict-affected regions. First, the potential evidence that present-bias and risk aversion particularly affect preventive care, while having less impact on acute care-seeking behaviour, together with other potential multiple explanatory frameworks, suggests that traditional approaches to healthcare service delivery in conflict zones need to be reconsidered. Our results indicate that the lower vaccination rates in conflict-ridden areas of Nigeria mainly stemmed from changes in parental decision-making rather than supply-side constraints. To address these behavioural barriers, policy-makers could consider implementing incentive systems that help offset present-biased preferences in vaccination decisions. For instance, combining vaccination services with immediate benefits, such as conditional cash transfers or food supplements, could help counter the tendency to postpone or decline preventive care. Such interventions directly address both behavioural mechanisms we identified, where immediate costs and heightened uncertainties discourage preventive healthcare investments in conflict settings. Moreover, there may be value in investing in vaccination promotion campaigns, where these have been proven to be effective.39 40

Second, the greater vulnerability of complete vaccination coverage to conflict disruption suggests that traditional vaccination campaigns, which often focus on increasing individual vaccine uptake, may be insufficient in conflict zones. One solution to this challenge is to simplify vaccination schedules, such as providing combination vaccines that reduce the number of required vaccination visits and integrating vaccination services with other essential healthcare needs.

Last, our investigation of the Boko Haram insurgency demonstrates that the specific nature or perpetrator of conflict appears less important than the general security environment it creates. Parents respond similarly to different conflict types, suggesting that interventions should focus on addressing the fundamental uncertainty and risk created by conflict rather than tailoring policies to specific conflict actors.

Notably, the results derived from our study are not consistent with previous research which has documented the positive effects of armed conflict on vaccination. Previous studies have implied that the positive effect is related to the humanitarian assistance in conflict regions, which increases healthcare accessibility and utilisation. However, the context in Nigeria is different. The decade-long conflict has led to diminishing international attention despite persistent needs. This chronic underassistance has been exacerbated by current conditions with 7.1 million people now requiring urgent humanitarian assistance and 1.8 million internally displaced, overwhelming the available resources.41

Conclusions

This paper examines the relationship between conflict and paediatric vaccination in Nigeria and finds that increased conflict intensity significantly reduces childhood vaccination. We further uncover the underlying mechanism, primarily through the demand-side channel, which is the changes in parental decision-making in health-seeking behaviours. In conflict zones, heightened background risks appear to intensify risk aversion, leading parents to deprioritise preventive healthcare investments like vaccination.

Our findings from Nigeria offer important policy implications for improving vaccination coverage in conflict-affected regions. Interventions in conflict zones should address demand-side effects on parental decisions regarding children’s vaccination uptake, not just supply-side constraints. Conflict-induced vaccination hesitancy differs from traditional forms of vaccine resistance, public health strategies in these regions may require distinct approaches that account for the unique psychological and behavioural impacts of persistent insecurity in conflict zones, where approximately 40% of the world’s undervaccinated children reside.10

Our study has several limitations. First, we lack direct measures of both risk perception and present-biased preferences, which constrains our ability to precisely quantify the channel. While we hypothesise that conflict may intensify parents’ present bias, leading them to overweight immediate security concerns relative to future health benefits, we cannot directly test this mechanism without measures of time preferences. Similarly, our conclusions about risk attitudes and health-seeking behaviours are necessarily indirect, inferred from observed vaccination choices rather than scientific measures of risk valuation and time discounting. Second, our data sources, ACLED and DHS, may suffer from measurement errors. Conflicts in remote areas might be underreported in the ACLED database due to limited media coverage and reporting difficulties in highly isolated regions, and households in the most severely conflict-affected areas might be underrepresented in DHS surveys due to security challenges during data collection. Third, in terms of our methodology, although we employ fixed effects and include many control variables to address endogeneity concerns, we cannot completely rule out all potential confounding factors that might simultaneously affect conflict intensity and vaccination decisions. Therefore, we cannot claim that the findings about the relationship between conflict and vaccination in our study represent causality but rather an association.

Given the recurring outbreaks and low vaccination rates and the potential high return to the investment in immunisation, it is essential for the government and relevant stakeholders to maintain and strengthen childhood vaccination efforts. Sustained progress in vaccination coverage is crucial to achieving long-term immunity and preventing further re-emergence of preventable diseases.
