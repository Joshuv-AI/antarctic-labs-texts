Mapping the evidence on factors influencing postpartum contraception among sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women in the United States: a scoping review
Authors: Comfort Z Olorunsaiye, Augustus Osborne, Hannah M Degge, Ucheoma Nwaozuru, Sanni Yaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe postpartum period is a critical window for addressing women’s contraceptive needs. Sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women living in the United State face unique barriers to postpartum contraception due to cultural norms, religious beliefs, migration-related challenges, and systemic inequalities, yet there is limited evidence on their postpartum contraceptive preferences and use. This scoping review mapped the existing literature on postpartum contraception among this population and identified key factors influencing uptake.MethodsFollowing Joanna Briggs Institute guidelines, we conducted a scoping review of studies published in English from January 2000-June 2024. Eligible studies focused on sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women aged 15–49 living in the US, and examined postpartum contraception, including predisposing factors, facilitators, and barriers. A systematic search of five databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, PsychInfo, CINAHL, EMBASE, Global Health Database) identified 410 studies; 10 studies were included. Data were coded and synthesized thematically using the PEN-3 Model as an analytic framework; findings were interpreted through an intersectionality lens.ResultsPostpartum contraceptive behaviors were shaped by predisposing factors, facilitators, and barriers. Cultural and religious norms favored natural methods like lactational amenorrhea, due to religious support for birth spacing but not limiting. Migration experiences were influential barriers, with challenges including language barriers, limited health literacy, and loss of insurance, postpartum. Facilitators were inclusive Medicaid policies, long-acting methods availability, and culturally sensitive counseling.ConclusionsCulturally responsive, equity-focused policies are essential to meet the postpartum contraceptive needs of sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women. Background

The postpartum period is a time of both heightened fertility and significant life transitions and represents a critical window for addressing the contraceptive needs of women [1, 2]. Effective contraceptive use during this period not only helps women achieve their desired family size and birth spacing but also contributes to improved maternal and child health outcomes [3–5]. Access to and utilization of postpartum contraception are influenced by a complex interplay of individual, cultural, systemic, and structural factors [6, 7]. Among immigrant and refugee women in the United States, particularly those originating from sub-Saharan Africa, these factors may be further compounded by migration-related challenges, cultural norms, and barriers to healthcare access [8, 9]. Despite the growing presence of sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee populations in the US, there remains a paucity of comprehensive evidence on their postpartum contraceptive behaviors, preferences, and needs.

Sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women represent a diverse group with unique reproductive health experiences shaped by experiences in their countries of origin, migration trajectories, and resettlement contexts [10]. Cultural beliefs surrounding fertility, contraception, and motherhood often play a central role in shaping reproductive decision-making within these communities [11]. For example, traditional pro-natalist norms, religious teachings, and gender dynamics may influence attitudes toward family planning and contraceptive uptake, including during the postpartum period [12, 13]. Additionally, the migration experience itself, which may be characterized by displacement, trauma, and adaptation to a new sociocultural environment, can further impact reproductive health behaviors [14]. In the US, immigrant women encounter a healthcare system that may differ significantly from those in their home countries, leading to challenges in navigating services, understanding available options, and establishing trust with providers [15]. Language barriers, limited health literacy, financial constraints, and systemic inequalities such as access to health insurance coverage, further exacerbate these difficulties, leaving many women underserved in terms of postpartum contraceptive care [16–18].

The importance of addressing postpartum contraception among sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women cannot be overstated. Unmet contraceptive need during the postpartum period can result in unintended pregnancies, closely spaced births, and associated health risks for both mothers and infants [19, 20]. Furthermore, understanding the factors that influence contraceptive uptake and preferences in this population is essential for designing culturally appropriate interventions that promote reproductive autonomy and equity. While studies have explored postpartum contraception in various settings and populations, there is a notable gap in research specific to sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women, especially because this group has the highest birth rate among all immigrant groups living in the US [21–23]. This gap is particularly significant given the increasing representation of sub-Saharan African immigrants within immigrant populations in the US [23].

To address this knowledge gap, this paper reports on a scoping review aimed at mapping the existing evidence on postpartum contraception among sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women living in the United States. Scoping reviews are an invaluable tool for synthesizing evidence on broad topics where literature may be heterogeneous or fragmented. By systematically identifying and analyzing available studies, this review seeks to provide a comprehensive overview of what is known about postpartum contraceptive practices, barriers, facilitators, and interventions within this population. The findings may not only highlight key trends and knowledge gaps but also inform future research priorities and policy recommendations.

Contributions of this scoping review

We advance the literature in three ways. First, this is the first review to synthesize evidence on postpartum contraception, specifically among sub‑Saharan African immigrant and refugee women living in the United States, disaggregating cultural, and migration‑related, and structural influences on postpartum contraception in this population. Second, by applying the PEN‑3 Model as the analytical framework, we explicitly synthesize and interpret identified cultural constructs and structural barriers from an intersectionality lens [24]. Third, we translate the findings into prioritized, evidence‑based recommendations for policy, clinical practice, and future research.

Methods

The protocol for this scoping review was registered in Open Science Framework [25] and has been published [22]. With the assistance of a professional librarian, we developed the review strategy based on methodological guidelines of the Joana Briggs Institute [26]. The methodology for this review has been detailed in the published review protocol [22]. Briefly, the primary question guiding the review was: “What is the evidence on postpartum contraception among sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women living in the United States?” Three other sub-questions were also considered in conducting the review, namely: (a) What sub-Saharan African immigrant populations have been studied in the literature on postpartum contraception among sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women living in the US? (b) What are the barriers to, and facilitators of, postpartum contraception from the perspectives of sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women and health care providers in the US? (c) What is the evidence linking postpartum contraceptive counseling, access, and use to the prevention of short-interval births or unintended pregnancy among sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee populations in the US?

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were based on three key elements of the JBI guidelines – population, concept, and context [26] (Table 1). Population. Sources were eligible for inclusion in this scoping review if they included sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women aged 15–49 years, who had at least one live birth, and lived in the US. Studies focused on individuals providing services (e.g., nurses, midwives, physicians, doulas, community health workers, and social workers/case managers, serving sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee populations were also eligible for inclusion. Concept. The main concept explored in this review, postpartum contraception, was based on definitions of the World Health Organization (WHO) and American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), which is the use of services that prevent close-spaced and unintended pregnancy through the first 12 months after childbirth [27, 28]. Thus, the concept included family planning and contraceptive counseling, contraceptive methods, and patterns of use reported in eligible studies. Professional guidelines recommend an interval of 18–24 months before conception after childbirth [27, 28]. The concept included all methods of contraception [29], as well as factors related to postpartum contraception (e.g., barriers, facilitators, and pre-disposing factors [21, 30, 31]. Context. Studies were eligible for inclusion in this review if their samples involved sub-Saharan African immigrants living in the US. Moreover, eligible studies included peer-reviewed and grey literature, including preprints, with full-text articles, published in English, and between January 1, 2000, and June 30, 2024. All study designs were considered eligible for inclusion in the review.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria applied in this review were if studies did not include immigrant or refugee women born in sub-Saharan Africa, or those involving women younger than 15 or older than 49 years. Additionally, studies conducted outside the US or with populations living outside the US were ineligible for inclusion. Lastly, due to resource constraints, we excluded studies that were published in a language other than English, and those that were published prior to the year 2000 or after June 30, 2024.

Search strategy and study selection

The search strategy included key terms from JBI’s PCC framework (i.e., population, concept, and context) described earlier (Table 1). We searched the following databases, which were selected because they were accessible through the first author’s institution, to identify relevant studies meeting the review inclusion criteria: PubMed/MEDLINE, PsychInfo, CINAHL, EMBASE, and the Global Health Database. Searches were conducted between July 15th 2024, and August 4th 2024. Exact search dates by database were: PubMed/MEDLINE (July 15th 2024), PsycINFO (July 16th 2024), CINAHL (July 18th 2024), EMBASE (July 22nd 2024), and Global Health Database (July 30th 2024). We conducted a targeted grey literature search from July 31st to August 4th, 2024, to minimize publication bias and capture community‑based and non‑indexed sources relevant to immigrant and refugee reproductive health. Sources searched included Google Scholar, organizational websites (e.g., refugee health and immigrant service organizations), conference abstract lists, and citation search of included studies. Full search strings for each database, including Boolean operators and any filters applied, are available upon reasonable request. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts. Disagreements at screening were resolved by discussion and consensus and, when needed, arbitrated by a third reviewer. Records were managed in Covidence and references in Zotero.

Data extraction and charting

Two authors independently extracted data from the included studies; discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus and, if unresolved, adjudicated by a third reviewer. The final set of included full texts (n = 10) was shared with all authors for confirmation. We used a data extraction form, adapted from the JBI manual guide [26], piloted on 20% (n = 2/10) of included studies to extract study characteristics and relevant variables (Fig. 1). Two reviewers independently piloted and refined the form, mostly to expand the categories for study design. The two reviewers re-extracted the piloted studies; the remaining eight studies were split for extraction. Each extracted dataset was independently checked by a third reviewer. Discrepancies were resolved through structured consensus meetings arbitrated by the third reviewer.

Data Extraction Form

Extracted data were exported to Microsoft Excel for verification and coding. Extracted items included: title, author and year of publication, aims/objectives, study design, sample size, and methods; population, concept, context; factors related to postpartum contraception (predisposing factors [knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs], facilitating factors, and barriers). We used thematic analysis to identify relevant themes, grouped as predisposing factors, facilitating factors, and barriers to postpartum contraception. When reviewers disagreed on thematic mapping, they documented the rationale and final decision in a mapping log; disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus, arbitrated by the third reviewer. We recorded the source of each extracted item and the final thematic assignment.

Data synthesis and analysis

Synthesis

We conducted a narrative synthesis [32, 33] using the PEN-3 Model [34] as an analytic framework. The PEN-3 Model offers a strength-based, culturally grounded lens for mapping the identified themes related to health behavior [34, 35]. The model has three main domains, each with three constructs that make up the acronym ‘PEN’: Cultural Identity (Persons, Extended Family, and Neighborhood): identifies entry points for health education and behavior change interventions; Relationships and Expectations (Perceptions, Enablers, and Nurturers): identifies perceptions about health behavior (e.g., postpartum contraception) and factors that support and reinforce the behavior; and Cultural Empowerment (Positive, Existential, and Negative): identifies positive, existential (neutral), and negative, dimensions of culture that can serve as levers of behavior change and empowerment. The model’s domains highlight culturally affirming pathways, relational influences, and modifiable factors for health promotion, making it well suited to immigrant research that adopts a strength‑based rather than deficit‑based view of culture [34, 36, 37].

Analysis

We applied the PEN-3 Model as a deductive, analytic framework and mapped themes, using a matrix approach, to the Cultural Identity (Person, Extended Family, Neighborhood), Relationships and Expectations (Perceptions, Enablers, Nurturers), and Cultural Empowerment (Positive, Existential, Negative) domains of the PEN-3 Model. For each domain/construct we recorded whether a study provided direct evidence (explicit discussion or participant quote), or indirect evidence (inferred from results). We summarized the evidence tags, noting direct (D) versus indirect (I) support for themes from each included study and to indicate construct support. For example, the theme ‘breastfeeding for spacing’ was mapped to the Cultural Empowerment: Positive construct based on participant quotes endorsing its cultural acceptability. The matrix informed cross‑study comparisons, identification of heterogeneity (e.g., by study design or population), and the narrative synthesis presented in the Results. An intersectionality lens [24] guided the mapping and interpretation of structural factors across cultural domains. Consistent with intersectionality scholarship we examined how overlapping social positions (e.g., migration pathway [immigrant/refugee], duration in the US, insurance status, language proficiency, gendered power relations, and socioeconomic status) interact with cultural constructs to shape postpartum contraceptive experiences [24, 38].

Quality assessment and risk of bias

Although a formal critical appraisal is not required for scoping reviews [26], we took steps to minimize bias. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts and independently extracted data; disagreements were resolved by discussion and, when needed, adjudication by a third reviewer. We extracted study‑level variables relevant to heterogeneity (refugee versus immigrant status, duration of residence in the United States, study design, and sample characteristics) and used these variables to assess methodological differences during synthesis. We also extracted study funding and conflict‑of‑interest statements and found no instances of declared conflicts of interest; funding sources were considered when interpreting study limitations but did not alter inclusion. Reporting follows the PRISMA-ScR checklist for scoping reviews. Although we excluded studies published before January 1, 2000, this cutoff is unlikely to bias results given the rapid increase in sub‑Saharan African migration to the US since the 2000s.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

The database and grey literature searches identified n = 410 records (404 database; 6 hand‑search/grey literature) (see Fig. 2, PRISMA‑ScR flow diagram). After removal of 134 duplicates, 276 sources were screened by title/abstract, of which 141 were excluded. We assessed 135 full texts for eligibility and excluded 125 (most common reasons: wrong population n = 24; wrong concepts n = 22; study population not stratified by nativity n = 55; content not relevant n = 16; review articles n = 5). Ten studies met inclusion criteria and were retained for data extraction.

PRISMA-ScR Flow Diagram of Data Search and Results

Of the 10 studies, six were qualitative and four were quantitative. Among the quantitative studies, one was a cohort study (n = 1) [39], retrospective chart review (n = 1) [40], secondary data analysis (n = 1) [41], and cross-sectional study (n = 1) [42] (Table 2). Data collection approaches for the qualitative studies included focus group discussions and individual in-depth interviews [43–48]. The earliest year of publication was 2012 and the most recent was 2023. Sample sizes of the included studies ranged from 5 to 2,249. Two studies compared African immigrant women with US-born women.

Key findings related to study Population, Concept, and context (PCC)

Population

Participants included women, men, and health-care providers; ages ranged from 18 to 68 years, with most studies focused on adult women [39–43, 46–48] (Table 2). Participants were born in Somalia, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Kenya, Eritrea, Sudan, and Djibouti; a few studies grouped all sub-Saharan African-born participants [40] or included US-born comparison groups [39, 41]. A few studies included Somali men, community/religious leaders, or a small number of health-care providers [43–45]. Reporting on parity and residence was inconsistent: five studies omitted parity, while those that included it reported between 1 and 15 children. Where length of US residence was reported most women had lived in the US ≥ 10 years (range 1–26) [41, 44, 46, 47].

Concept

The primary concept across studies was postpartum contraception (defined as any pregnancy prevention method use within 12 months after birth) [39, 40]; and closely related concepts, such as postpartum counseling, family planning, birth/child spacing, fertility preferences, and timing of contraceptive adoption [40, 42, 44, 45, 47, 48]. Preferred methods reported included sterilization [39], LARC [39], non-LARC hormonal methods [39, 42–44], barrier methods [40, 42, 43], natural family planning (e.g., lactational amenorrhea, withdrawal, fertility awareness) [39, 44, 46, 47], and abstinence [42]. Compared with US-born or non-sub-Saharan African groups, sub-Saharan African immigrants more often reported less-effective methods (barrier or natural methods); for example, sterilization uptake was 0.5% vs. 13.1% and natural method use 40% vs. 21% among Somali versus non-Somali participants [38, 39]. Traditional methods, especially breastfeeding for spacing, were widely acceptable in cases of maternal health risk or severe economic hardship, and contraceptive counseling was more acceptable when framed as birth spacing rather than limiting childbearing because of cultural and religious beliefs about non-interference with God’s will [39, 41, 43, 47, 48].

Context

All included studies were US-based, conducted in New York [40, 41, 46], Minnesota [39, 42–44], Washington [47], Utah [48]; one study spanned New York City, Washington DC, and Georgia [45]. Six studies were qualitative and four quantitative study designs. Where findings diverged we attributed differences to design and sampling (small qualitative studies provided rich contextual quotes; larger quantitative studies offered population-level patterns), and we prioritized constructs supported by both qualitative and quantitative direct evidence. A few studies disaggregated refugees from other immigrants; when disaggregation occurred, refugee samples highlighted pre-resettlement and camp-based contraceptive experiences, whereas longer-term immigrants described acculturation-linked shifts in service navigation and preferences [43, 44, 48]. Across contexts, structural constraints, e.g., postpartum insurance loss, language barriers, and limited clinic capacity, frequently prevented culturally acceptable practices (e.g., faith-aligned acceptance of birth spacing) from translating into contraceptive uptake, even where Medicaid and LARC availability were present.

We analyzed and interpreted themes through the lens of intersectionality; mapping findings through intersectionality showed that structural constraints (e.g., lack of insurance, postpartum Medicaid loss) interact with relational mediators/extended networks (intimate partners, faith leaders) and cultural practices (e.g., lactational amenorrhea) in ways that differ by socioeconomic position and nativity background and migration pathway. Evidence support for each theme is indicated by evidence tags: D (direct) and I (indirect). This pattern echoes critiques that culture-only explanations obscure structural drivers of immigrant health [49, 50]. Predisposing factors

At the individual level, breastfeeding/LAM and parity shape method choice and timing, with prior contraceptive exposure and postpartum visit attendance increasing likelihood of contraceptive uptake [39, 42, 48]. Interpersonally, partners, family members, faith leaders, and community elders exert strong normative influence that can either facilitate or hinder postpartum contraceptive use depending on local expectations [43, 44, 48]. Community norms and storytelling traditions sustain preferences for natural methods of pregnancy spacing and high‑fertility values, which interact with individual perceptions [43, 47].

Facilitators

Service-level facilitators center on access and culturally tailored care, including Medicaid or other insurance coverage, hospital counseling policies, availability of LARC (including same-day placement), multilingual staff, and community outreach, improve postpartum contraceptive acceptability and uptake [39, 40, 42, 45]. Organizational features, such as clinic capacity and trust-building models, support service delivery while culturally tailored counseling and peer education increase engagement [45, 48]. Policy context shapes perceptions of postpartum care and therefore influence whether these facilitators translate into postpartum contraceptive use [41].

Barriers

Barriers operate across multiple levels - individual fears (of side effects and infertility), embarrassment, and communication difficulties reduce postpartum contraceptive seeking [44, 47]; interpersonal constraints include male dominance in fertility decision making, partner refusal of contraception, and clan-driven pressures for high fertility [43, 44]. At the structural level, resettlement context, structural racism, limited awareness of available services, and unstable supports (e.g., lack/loss of insurance, interpreter gaps, funding shortages, and high clinic volumes) frequently convert potential facilitators into active barriers [40, 41, 45]. Cultural resistance and stigma further amplify reluctance to adopt modern contraceptive methods unless counseling and community engagement explicitly address these concerns [43, 47].

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes evidence on postpartum contraception among sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women in the United States. The findings are framed within the Cultural Identity, Relationships and Expectations, and Cultural Empowerment domains of the PEN-3 Model and provide a culturally grounded understanding of intersecting sociodemographic, cultural, and structural factors influencing postpartum contraception in this population [24, 34].

Demographic and resettlement characteristics, such as age at migration, employment, and parity were identified as consistent predisposing influences on postpartum contraceptive uptake [42, 44]. Younger age at migration and employment during pregnancy were associated with greater postpartum contraceptive uptake, suggesting that earlier integration and economic participation can improve attitudes toward family planning, and uptake. Conversely, older maternal age, socioeconomic instability, and experiences of racial discrimination were linked to delayed or reduced contraceptive use [41, 43], consistent with literature showing that socioeconomic adversity and systemic discrimination undermine reproductive autonomy and access to care [51, 52].

Cultural meanings and norms shaped postpartum contraceptive acceptability in complex ways. Existential and positive cultural factors, most notably religious endorsement of birth spacing, support interventions that align family planning with prevailing cultural values [44, 47]. At the same time, negative cultural norms (stigmatization of birth limiting, high‑fertility ideals, and religious fatalism) act as barriers to uptake [47, 48]. These patterns support culturally congruent messaging that frames contraception in terms of birth spacing rather than limiting fertility to enhance acceptability among more conservative sub-Saharan African immigrant communities [21, 52, 53].

Predisposing exposures and health‑system contacts also matter; pre-migration contraceptive experience and postpartum visits were positive influences [39, 48], whereas low pre‑pregnancy contraceptive use and misconceptions about side effects reduced uptake [40, 47]. The existential role of natural family planning, especially extended breastfeeding, reflects long-standing cultural practices that can be leveraged for healthy birth spacing [43, 46]. Social dynamics after resettlement, including shifts toward more equitable gender roles and community activism, served as facilitators in some contexts [45, 48]; however, persistent reliance on social networks over clinical advice and male partner dominance in fertility decisions remained important constraints [43, 44, 47], underscoring the need to engage both women and men and broader community interest holders as entry points for family planning interventions [54].

System‑level facilitators and barriers were equally influential factors. Inclusive public insurance and tailored hospital counseling policies enhanced contraceptive access [39, 40]. Likewise, culturally and linguistically appropriate services, such as community liaisons and multilingual staff, further supported uptake of postpartum contraception [45]. However, pervasive structural barriers, including provider cultural insensitivity, language and interpreter gaps, postpartum insurance loss, high clinic volumes, and broader anti‑immigrant rhetoric, hindered continuity and quality of care, undermining service delivery [39–41, 45–47]. These findings underscore the necessity of combining culturally grounded care with stable structural supports to translate contraceptive acceptability into sustained access and use [55–59].

Taken together, this evidence synthesis shows that cultural meanings, interpersonal expectations, and structural conditions interact, often conditionally. As a result, the same facilitating factor (for example, culturally tailored counseling) may succeed for some subgroups but fail where intersecting disadvantages (e.g., recent migration, limited English proficiency, unstable insurance, constrained clinic capacity) persist. For instance, culturally tailored counseling and same‑day LARC placement increase postpartum contraceptive uptake only when foundational supports, such as continuous insurance and funded interpreter services, protected counseling time, and sufficient clinic capacity, are also present [40–42, 45]. Thus, framing these results with intersectionality clarifies that culturally congruent counseling alone is insufficient for population groups experiencing multiple dimensions of marginalization; instead, combined policy, service delivery, and community interventions are needed; an approach consistent with intersectionality‑based policy analysis and public health applications of the framework [49, 60–62].

Implications for policy, programs and research

Policy

Applying an intersectional lens to the evidence synthesis clarifies that perceived barriers and facilitators of postpartum contraceptive access do not affect all women equally. Their impact is conditional on intersecting positions such as migration length, insurance and legal status, language proficiency, and gendered power relations within households [40, 41, 45]. Policy responses should therefore ensure stable insurance coverage (for example, Medicaid expansion that reduces insurance loss from pregnancy to postpartum) and funded interpreter services as foundational supports so that clinic‑level facilitators are not undermined by structural instability.

Program and service delivery

Programmatic strategies can translate policy into practice by combining culturally respectful counseling, community and faith-leader engagement, and partner-inclusive outreach to address normative and perceived barriers [39, 43, 47]. Clinics should invest in expanded capacity for same-day LARC placement, multilingual staffing, and protected counseling time, and create targeted navigation services (e.g., for insurance enrollment assistance, language support, appointment reminders) for recent arrivals and other high-need subgroups [42, 45].

Research

To track equity and refine interventions, research and routine monitoring data must report disaggregated outcomes by key social factors (migration length, insurance and legal status, language proficiency, parity) and include process measures (interpreter use, counseling duration, navigator contacts) and cost/sustainability data. Mixed methods and equity-focused evaluations should test whether interventions reduce disparities or unintentionally widen them, and findings should feed back into policy and program design [40, 41, 61].

Summary

It is expedient that policy stability (service coverage and interpreter funding), programmatic translation (clinic capacity, culturally grounded counseling, navigation), and rigorous disaggregated research are conducted concurrently so that facilitators reliably become effective supports for those experiencing overlapping disadvantages.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

To our knowledge this scoping review is the first to synthesize evidence on postpartum contraception, specifically for sub‑Saharan African immigrant and refugee women in the United States, and fills an important gap in the literature. The application of the PEN‑3 Model enabled us to identify cultural, interpersonal, and structural drivers of postpartum contraception in this population [34, 35]. Applying an intersectionality lens to the extracted study characteristics and themes highlighted how migration length, insurance status, language proficiency, parity, and gendered power relations shape the distribution and meaning of facilitators and barriers. Dual coding and reconciliation of themes across studies improved reliability of the synthesis and produced actionable program, policy, and research implications.

Limitations

First, the review was restricted to studies conducted in the United States and to English‑language publications, which may limit transferability to other national or linguistic contexts. Second, heterogeneity in study designs, samples, and outcome measures precluded meta‑analysis and limited inferences about effect magnitude across studies. Third, many primary studies did not disaggregate findings by the key social factors we prioritized (migration length, insurance status, language), constraining the depth of intersectional assumptions and making the absence of disaggregated reporting itself an important evidence gap. Fourth, publication bias, incomplete indexing may have caused exclusion of grey literature, which may have omitted community‑driven or programmatic innovations not formally evaluated. However, tailored searches for grey literature and hand-searching the references of included studies mitigated this bias. Finally, most included studies relied on self‑report and qualitative recall, which are subject to social desirability and recall biases for sensitive topics such as contraception and fertility [39–44, 46].

Gaps and future directions

This scoping review highlights several empirical gaps that should guide future work. Primary studies rarely disaggregate findings by refugee versus immigrant status or by duration of residence, limiting inference about acculturation effects. Neighborhood‑level and structural data in studies involving immigrant and refugee populations (e.g., local clinic capacity) are sparse, constraining multi‑level causal inference. Future research should prioritize intersectional, disaggregated, and implementation‑focused studies that move beyond descriptive accounts to test what works, for whom, and under what conditions. Specifically, rigorous evaluations of culturally tailored, community‑based interventions (community health workers, health navigators, peer educators, and engagement of faith leaders) that include process measures (e.g., interpreter use, counseling duration, navigator contacts), and equity‑focused effect estimates to inform scale‑up are warranted [42, 45, 57]. Studies should also develop and use standardized measures for perceived barriers to contraceptive uptake (infertility fears, stigma) and culturally meaningful existential practices (breastfeeding/LAM acceptance) so that findings are comparable across settings [21, 53]. Future studies should also test digital and telehealth strategies to overcome language and access barriers in the post‑COVID pandemic era, and intentionally include men, community leaders, and providers in evaluation designs to capture interpersonal dynamics that shape postpartum contraceptive decision‑making [55]. Finally, routine monitoring systems should require disaggregated reporting and equity indicators so that policymakers and implementers can track whether interventions reduce disparities or inadvertently widen them in contexts of insurance turnover, limited language access, and constrained clinic capacity [39–41, 45].

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that postpartum contraceptive behavior among sub-Saharan African immigrant and refugee women in the US is shaped by intersecting cultural meanings, interpersonal expectations, and structural conditions. The PEN‑3 narrative synthesis, interpreted through an intersectionality lens, shows that positive, existential, and negative cultural factors operate conditionally across social positions, and to be effective, culturally responsive counseling and community engagement must be paired with stable policy and service delivery. To promote reproductive autonomy and equity, interest holders must combine targeted program delivery (e.g., multilingual staffing, navigation, protected counseling time, same‑day LARC capacity) with policy actions that secure insurance coverage and language access, and researchers should report disaggregated outcomes to ensure interventions reduce rather than reproduce disparities.
