Expanding the Clinical Spectrum of Mycobacterium haemophilum Infection: Absence of Cutaneous Manifestations in Immunocompromised Hosts
Authors: Haitham Alaithan, Michael T Deavers, Masayuki Nigo, Satish Mocherla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Mycobacterium haemophilum infection typically presents with cutaneous manifestations. Absence of cutaneous involvement is rare and diagnostically challenging. We report 2 immunocompromised adults, a liver transplant recipient and a patient receiving tumor necrosis factor-α inhibitor, without cutaneous manifestations. These cases expand the recognized clinical spectrum and underscore the importance of considering this pathogen in immunocompromised hosts even in absence of cutaneous involvement.

Mycobacterium haemophilum is a nontuberculous mycobacterium (NTM) found frequently in the environment, similar to other NTM species, and is capable of causing infections in both animals and humans, particularly in people with immunocompromising conditions [1]. It is a slow-growing, acid-fast bacilli (AFB) with distinctive microbiological characteristics including a unique requirement of iron supplementation in culture media to grow and an optimal growth temperature of 30° to 32° C, the latter being similar to Mycobacterium ulcerans and Mycobacterium marinum [1].

The most frequently reported clinical manifestations of M haemophilum infection include cervicofacial lymphadenitis, typically observed in reportedly healthy children [2], and skin lesions, observed in immunocompromised patients [1, 3]. The predilection for skin and peripheral lymph nodes likely related to the species growth preference in lower than core body temperature. Less common presentations including perihilar lymphadenitis, pulmonary involvement, pneumonia or pulmonary nodules, osteomyelitis, myositis, and conjunctivitis [4].

In immunocompromised adults, cutaneous involvement represents the most common presentation or, at minimum, a component of the constellation of presenting symptoms [1, 5]. Lesions may appear as painless papule, pustule, nodules, plaques, cysts, cellulitis, erythema, or even deep painful ulceration lesions [6]. Therefore, the development of evolving skin lesions in an immunocompromised host prompt the consideration of M haemophilum as a possible cause. However, the cases without cutaneous manifestations are rarely reported and can be diagnostic challenges, as AFB cultures outside the skin are often not routinely incubated in a lower temperature.

Herein, we report 2 cases of M haemophilum infection in immunocompromised hosts in the absence of cutaneous manifestation highlighting the importance of including M haemophilum in the differential diagnosis of infection in immunocompromised patients, even in the absence of skin involvement.

CASE PRESENTATION

Case 1

A 67-year-old male with a history of hepatitis C virus infection and hepatocellular carcinoma managed with orthotopic liver transplant 15 months before presentation presented with 2 weeks of intermittent fever associated with night sweats, productive cough with white thick sputum, exertional dyspnea, fatigue, and dysgeusia leading to anorexia.

He was employed as a safety coordinator and reported performing his own yard maintenance, including removal of leaf detritus. He also frequently chased stray cats from his property. His immunosuppression regimen at presentation included tacrolimus and everolimus; mycophenolate mofetil was held for 5 months following an episode of severe pneumonia.

On admission, he was febrile with a temperature of 38.4° C and hypoxemic requiring 6 L of oxygen flow rate through a nasal cannula to maintain oxygen saturation above 95%. His respiratory rate was 22 breath per minute and blood pressure of 114/75 mm Hg. Physical examination was unremarkable, with no skin or mucosal lesions nor palpable lymphadenopathy.

Basic laboratory data are summarized in Table 1. Computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis without contrast, obtained because of baseline chronic kidney disease, revealed a 5-mm left lower lobe pulmonary nodule and cluster of nodules in the right lower lobe, diffuse ground-glass opacities, 1.2-cm left paratracheal lymph node, and evidence of gastroduodenitis. Subsequent esophagogastroduodenoscopy demonstrated mild mucosal erythema of the prepyloric region and duodenal bulb, consistent with gastritis and duodenitis. Histopathological examination of the endoscopic biopsy confirmed these gross findings, with negative viral staining.

An extensive infectious workup was negative, including bronchoscopy, bone marrow biopsy, bartonella serologies, rickettsial serologies, endemic fungal antigens including cryptococcus and histoplasmosis, coccidiomycosis antibodies, blood cultures, and QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus. Blood AFB cultures were not obtained. Empiric antibiotics including meropenem and doxycycline yielded no clinical improvement.

After 20 days of persistent fever and unclear diagnosis, a positron emission tomography (PET) scan was obtained, revealing an avid uptake of the known 1.2-cm left paratracheal lymph node. An endoscopic lymph node biopsy demonstrated necrotizing granulomas with presence of abundant AFB (Figure 1), which later were identified as M haemophilum by both broad-range polymerase chain reaction testing and conventional AFB cultures (liquid media at temperature of 37° C).

Mycophenolate mofetil was kept on hold. Tacrolimus was also held. Everolimus was continued. Antimicrobials with azithromycin 500 mg orally daily, ciprofloxacin 500 mg orally every 12 hours, and rifabutin 300 mg orally daily were initiated, resulting in symptomatic improvement and defervescence in 48 hours. This was attributed to immune reconstitution syndrome (IRIS) in the setting of reducing immunosuppression. Given continued clinical improvement, absence of recurrent fever, and lack of treatment adverse events, no changes were made to the therapeutic regimen. The patient was advised to continue follow-up in the infectious diseases clinic, with planned treatment duration of at least 12 months from the time of diagnosis.

Case 2

A 74-year-old-female with a history of rheumatoid arthritis presented with 1 week of intermittent fever associated with night sweats, nonproductive cough, and substernal chest pain. She was not employed at the time of presentation. She lived with her husband and occasionally visited his rice farm. She reported no recent travel. Her maintenance therapy is methotrexate and adalimumab, a tumor necrosis factor-α inhibitor.

She was evaluated at an urgent-care clinic where a chest radiograph demonstrated findings suggestive of community acquired pneumonia. She was prescribed azithromycin and amoxicillin without clinical improvement and subsequently presented to our institution.

On admission, her temperature was 36.7° C. Blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation were normal. Physical examination was unremarkable, with no cutaneous or mucosal lesions and no palpable lymphadenopathy.

Basic laboratory data are summarized in Table 1. CT angiography of the chest revealed a left mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy with a 3-cm hilar mass encasing the left hilar structures and an associated postobstructive opacity of the lingula; findings concerning for bronchogenic neoplasm and postobstructive pneumonia (Figure 2). The patient started empirically on ampicillin/sulbactam 3 g intravenously every 6 hours in addition to doxycycline 100 mg orally every 12 hours.

An extensive workup including bronchoscopy with endobronchial ultrasound-guided biopsy of the left hilar mass, demonstrated necrotizing granulomatous inflammation without malignant cells and negative stains for AFB and fungal organisms. Blood AFB cultures were not obtained. Adalimumab was held pending further diagnostic clarification. The patient was clinically stable following the bronchoscopy and was discharged on levofloxacin 750 mg orally once daily to complete a course of 7 days for community acquired pneumonia, with symptomatic improvement.

Three weeks later, because of persistent diagnostic uncertainty, a repeat bronchoscopy with endobronchial ultrasound-guided biopsy was performed. Around the same time, AFB cultures from the initial biopsy grew M haemophilum, which was subsequently confirmed by broad-range polymerase chain reaction from the second biopsy specimen.

The patient was started on azithromycin 500 mg orally once daily, rifampin 600 mg orally once daily, and ciprofloxacin 500 mg orally every 12 hours. Ciprofloxacin was later replaced with sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (double-strand strength 1 tablet twice daily) based on antimicrobial susceptibility results; susceptibility testing is shown in Table 2. Adalimumab remained on hold. A follow-up chest CT scan obtained 1 month later showed a significant reduction in the size of the left hilar mass from 3 cm to 1.4 cm. As the patient has tolerated the antimicrobial regimen without significant adverse events, no changes were made to the current therapy. Ongoing clinical and radiologic follow-up is planned, with continuation of treatment for at least 9 months from the time of diagnosis.

DISCUSSION

M haemophilum is an uncommon NTM that primarily affects immunocompromised hosts. Classically, it presents with cutaneous manifestations alone or in combination of other organ involvements or lymphadenitis, a distribution likely related to its preference for cooler temperature (30° to 32° C) [1]. In this report, we present 2 cases of an unusual presentation of M haemophilum infection in immunocompromised hosts without cutaneous involvement. This observation broadens the recognized clinical spectrum and highlights the importance of considering M haemophilum in immunocompromised hosts, even in the absence of skin manifestations.

The diagnosis of M haemophilum infection poses significant challenges. Conventional mycobacterial culture techniques may fail to isolate the organism because its growth requires iron supplementation and incubation at lower temperatures [1]. In some microbiology laboratories, including ours, AFB cultures from cutaneous samples are routinely incubated at lower temperature to facilitate M haemophilum, M ulcerans, and M marinum recovery. Consequently, infections may go unrecognized when skin lesions are absent and standard 37° C incubation protocol is applied to noncutaneous specimens. In both our cases, the organism was successfully isolated from liquid media at 37° C. This finding might not be reproducible in most patients [3]. These diagnostic challenges reinforce the importance of histopathological evaluation and highlight the role of next-generation sequencing in achieving rapid identification, particularly when successful culture growth is uncertain.

Clinical suspicion for M haemophilum infection rises when cutaneous manifestations are present. Previous reports have linked M haemophilum infection with cervicofacial lymphadenitis or cutaneous disease [1–3], and to lesser extent pulmonary, bone or joint, and conjunctival infections [4]. Determining the incidence of absent cutaneous manifestations remains challenging because of limitations in the published literature, particularly inconsistent reporting regarding the presence of absence of skin involvement when noncutaneous disease predominates.

In a review by Saubolle et al., 8 of 47 adults (17%) had noncutaneous infections. Within this cohort, 10 patients were solid organ transplant recipients (kidney); 9 had cutaneous manifestations. Additionally, 1 patient with rheumatoid arthritis manifested cutaneous lesions [1]. Among the entire cohort, 4 of 47 adults (9%) had pulmonary infection, yet only 1 was reported to have associated cutaneous manifestations [1]. Shah et al. similarly reported 23 patients with M haemophilum infection, of whom 6 (26%) had pulmonary involvement but only 1 exhibited cutaneous manifestations [5]. Nookeu et al. reported 21 nonpulmonary cases, with 7 patients (33%) presenting with noncutaneous disease [7].

Collectively, these findings indicate that cutaneous disease remains the most common presentation of M haemophilum infection in immunocompromised hosts; however, patients presenting with primary pulmonary involvement appear less likely to exhibit concurrent cutaneous manifestations, suggesting that thoracic disease may represent a distinct and underrecognized clinical entity.

M haemophilum is typically susceptible to macrolides, fluoroquinolones, and rifamycin, and resistant to isoniazid and ethambutol [5]. Both our patients responded favorably to combination therapy with macrolide, fluoroquinolones, and rifamycin, with resolution of symptoms. In the first case, paradoxical radiological progression was noted in the first month despite clinical improvement, a finding attributed to IRIS following reduction of immunosuppression, a phenomenon seen with mycobacterial and fungal diseases after immune reconstitution [8]. Corticosteroids were not initiated, as the patient was asymptomatic, and he was managed with close clinical and radiological follow-up.

In conclusion, although M haemophilum infection most commonly presents with cutaneous manifestations, the absence of cutaneous manifestations, although uncommon, represents an atypical presentation that may obscure clinical recognition and contribute to delayed diagnosis and initiation of appropriate therapy. These cases underscore the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for M haemophilum infection in immunocompromised hosts, even in the absence of classically described cutaneous lesions. Increased awareness of this atypical presentation is critical to facilitate timely diagnosis and optimize clinical outcomes in this vulnerable population.
