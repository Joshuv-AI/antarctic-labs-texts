The Impact of Thermocycling on the Marginal Discrepancy of Zirconia CAD/CAM Generation on Central Incisors: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Abdulkarim H Alshehri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundZirconia ceramic materials have become a preferred choice for anterior teeth, but their marginal performance under thermocycling has not been sufficiently investigated. Thus, this in vitro study aims to compare the impact of thermocycling on the marginal discrepancy of zirconia computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) generation on maxillary central incisors.Materials and methodsA total of 36 zirconia central incisor crowns were fabricated using three different CAD/CAM systems (ZirCAD Prime, Cercon ht ML, Zolid Bion; 12 per group) and cemented onto models made from resin. After aging for 48 hours in artificial saline, the crowns underwent thermocycling for 10,000 cycles. Marginal discrepancies were measured at two points on four sides per specimen under a digital microscope at 40× magnification. Data were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and t-tests, and a p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.ResultsBefore thermocycling, the ZirCAD Prime group demonstrated the lowest overall mean and standard deviation (SD) marginal discrepancy values (55.76 ± 16.41 µm), followed by Zolid Bion (60.95 ± 16.64 µm) and Cercon ht ML (62.93 ± 20.79 µm). After thermocycling, the ZirCAD Prime group demonstrated the lowest discrepancy in overall mean and standard deviation (57.18 ± 16.43 µm), followed by Zolid Bion (62.92 ± 16.31 µm) and Cercon ht ML (63.83 ± 20.92 µm). ANOVA revealed no significant difference in the zirconia groups before (p ≥ 0.688) and after (p ≥ 0.681) thermocycling. However, paired t-tests indicated significant differences in discrepancies before and after thermocycling for all materials (p < 0.001).ConclusionAfter thermocycling, all types of CAD/CAM zirconia crowns showed an increase in marginal discrepancies, but these differences remained within clinically acceptable limits.How to cite this articleAlshehri AH. Introduction

The increased demand for esthetically natural dental restorations has driven considerable advancements in material innovation and fabrication processes, positioning all-ceramic systems as durable alternatives to conventional metal–ceramic prostheses.1 These systems and the integration of the computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technology have revolutionized restorative dentistry, enabling the precise and efficient use of dental restorations. This technological evolution, combined with the excellent esthetics and long lifetimes of monolithic zirconia systems, has led to the widespread application of these systems.2

Dental zirconia is categorized by yttria content (3–6 mol%), which influences its crystalline structure and clinical performance. Earlier generations (1st/2nd) have lower yttria content (3Y-TZP) and exhibit high mechanical strength (>1000 MPa) but lower translucency because of the dominant tetragonal phase.3 To improve esthetics, third-generation zirconia ceramics, such as ZirCAD Prime and Zolid Bion, were developed. They are fabricated by increasing the cubic phase concentration with stabilizing oxides; resulting zirconia has 5 mol% yttria.1 Although this method enhances translucency, it reduces strength and fracture toughness because cubic grains cannot undergo stress-induced phase transformation.3,4 Fourth-generation materials, such as Cercon ht ML, represent hybrid zirconia multilayers designed to combine high strength with natural esthetics. These materials are highly translucent and made from yttrium oxide–stabilized ZrO₂, offering flexural strength ranging from 750 to 1200 MPa.5 However, mechanical properties alone do not fully account for the remarkable durability of dental restorations.

The longevity of zirconia restorations is adversely influenced by several factors, including improper crown or tooth preparation design, uneven occlusal forces, fabrication errors, and marginal discrepancies.6,7 Marginal integrity is crucial for clinical success because gaps exceeding 100–120 μm can lead to microleakage, secondary caries, plaque buildup, and pulpal or periodontal inflammation.8 Failure mechanisms are often multifactorial, and marginal precision is a key factor determining the survival of prosthetic restorations.6

No clear consensus on the upper limit for permissible discrepancy has been reached in studies examining marginal fit accuracy. Clinical success was previously defined by McLean and Von Fraunhofer as a small discrepancy of less than 120 μm, with notable variations depending on the production procedure.9 Recent investigations into CAD/CAM-fabricated full zirconia crowns have documented marginal discrepancies ranging from 11 to 58 μm.10,11 Comparative studies between zirconia copings and other ceramic restorations suggest that zirconia exhibits high accuracy in marginal fit.12,13 However, the precision of CAD/CAM single prostheses is affected by numerous factors, including marginal design, die spacer thickness, choice of cement, and cementation process.14 Additionally, scanning, software, and machining processes may adversely affect the fit of CAD/CAM restorations.15

Evaluating the performance of dental materials under conditions that replicate the temperature variations of the oral cavity is important. A widely adopted method for simulating thermal or artificial aging is thermocycling, which replicates the natural aging of dental materials by subjecting and exposing them to repeated and constant temperature fluctuations between 5 and 55°C; these fluctuations mirror thermal changes that occur in the mouth.16 Through thermal aging, materials undergo repeated temperature cycles, mimicking thermal conditions in the oral cavity environment and setting.17 Approximately, 20,000 cycles of thermal aging are equivalent to 24 months of clinical use in the oral cavity.17

Recent research has explored the impact of thermocycling aging on the translucency of various generations of zirconia, revealing that thermocycling considerably affects the translucency of third- and fourth-generation zirconia.18 However, limited laboratory studies have examined marginal discrepancies among different zirconia generation systems for anterior teeth. Thus, the aim of this in vitro study was to measure and compare the effect of thermocycling on the marginal discrepancies of different zirconia CAD/CAM systems with maxillary central incisor tooth interfaces. The null hypothesis is that thermocycling does not lead to considerable differences between the marginal discrepancy values of the cemented crowns and tested surfaces individually and among the overall values of various zirconia CAD/CAM systems.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This laboratory study was carried out in the Department of Prosthetic Dental Sciences, College of Dentistry, Jazan University, to measure marginal discrepancies in cementation among different types of zirconia crowns before and after thermocycling.

Sample Size Calculation

G*Power (version 3.1.9.7, 2020, Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) was used in calculating specimen size while assuming a three-group assessment. The α coefficient was set at 0.05 with a power of 90%, and an effect size of 0.40 was used in calculating specimen size. A total sample size of 36 samples was considered sufficient for the study.

Teeth Mounting and Zirconia Crown Preparation

Ivory tooth (Frasaco An-4 Puk, Pok) #11 was mounted in a Frasaco typodont model fixed on a table Frasaco teaching and demonstration phantom heads (AnA-4 ZE100, Verkaufs partner/agent, GMBH, Postfach 1244, 88061 Tettnang, Germany).19 The tooth was prepared to receive a full-coverage 1.5 ± 0.25 mm zirconia crown, 1.0 ± 0.25 mm incisal and axial wall reductions, and 1.0 ± 0.25 mm-wide shoulder finish line and to have rounded line angles, as recommended by the manufacturer's instructions and by literature.20,21 A digital impression was applied using an intraoral scanner (MEDIT, Seoul, South Korea) to obtain a 3D digital representation of the prepared teeth.

Model Designing and Fabrication

Using CAD software (exocad Dental DB 2–3 Matera, exocad Co., Germany) and a Formlabs 3D printer (Form 2 3D printer, USA), a 3D model of the prepared tooth #11 was created in order to generate 36 samples. The 3D Printer OS program and a photopolymer resin made in Shenzhen, China, by ELEGOO-CA-EL-3D-005, were utilized (Fig. 1A). The designed dies were randomly distributed into three groups comprising 12 dies each.

(A) Buccal, proximal side, palatal side of resin dyes; (B) Different types of zirconia crowns; (C) Zirconia crowns fitted on resin model dies

Zirconia Crown Construction

A total of 36 zirconia crowns were constructed by using three CAD/CAM systems and zirconia generations (ZirCAD Prime, Cercon ht ML, Zolid Bion; Table 1). For each zirconia type, a scan was obtained with a corresponding CAM scanner. To design and assemble 12 zirconia crowns with a 35 µm luting spacer, the data and figures were converted into ordinary tessellation language format using the CAD software of each system. The five-axis milling machine was used to construct the crowns in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendation. The final crowns are shown with buccal, proximal, and palatal sides (Fig. 1B). All crowns were adjusted and fitted in their dies, as shown in Figure 1C.

Fitting Surface Preparation and Zirconia Prostheses Cementation

The fitting surfaces of each zirconia prosthesis or intaglio were suspended in the air and comprised 50 µm aluminum oxide particles from Polidental Indústria e Comércio in Cotia, SP. The particles were subjected to a 3.5 bar pressure, uniform spacing, and a fixed duration of immersion in an ultrasonic bath of 99% isopropanol for 3 minutes before being air-dried. After gradually air-drying, the tooth surface was microbrushed to remove any excess 3M Primer 94 (3M, St. Paul, MN, USA), and the mixture was applied. Zirconia-treated surfaces were cemented with self-adhesive resin cement under a constant load for the recommended times (4 minutes; Fig. 2A). The last step was to use an LED polymerization light (Ultradent Products) to light-polymerize each zirconia prosthesis for 60 seconds after removing excess cement. The procedure for zirconia prosthesis luting was executed in strict accordance with the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. The cemented zirconia crowns with the VITA resin model were exposed to 24 hours of aging at 37°C after they were stored in normal saline before mechanical testing.

(A) Specimens during and under customized holder for cementation; (B) Under microscope for marginal discrepancy measurements

Thermocycling of the Cemented Zirconia Crowns

Using a thermocycling machine (Thermocycler, SD Mechatronik, Feldkirchen-Westerham, Germany) that continuously cycles between cold (5°C) and hot (55°C) water with a dwell time of 30 seconds, the cemented crowns were evaluated and measured for marginal discrepancy both before and after the thermocycling process.16,17,22,23

Marginal Discrepancy Measurements

Cemented specimens were loaded in a customized holder to standardize scanning under a microscope during marginal discrepancy measurements (Fig. 2B). To find the margin of discrepancy, we assessed the vertical distance between the crown margins of several zirconia models and the die models with finish line interfaces.16 A digital microscope (Digital Microscope, KH-7700, Hirox-USA, Inc., USA) was used to analyze all of the samples at 40× magnification after they were sealed in a locking device. A light microscope incorporated a numerical camera, light foundation, liquid crystal display screen, computer, and software. Two sites at equally spaced landmarks on each side (buccal, palatal, mesial, and distal) were evaluated for marginal discrepancies three times each; the average results were computed and recorded in micrometers for each side independently. Measurements were obtained after 48 hours from cementation (baseline; Fig. 3A) and after thermocycling cycle (Fig. 3B).

(A) Marginal discrepancy measurements before thermocycling; (B) After thermocycling

Data Analysis

Statistics for Social Sciences for Windows (version 28.0), Armonk, NY: IBM Corp., was used for data entry and analysis. Marginal inconsistencies were described by calculating the mean and standard deviation (SD). When comparing groups, both within and between, we employed analysis of variance and t-tests. A statistically significant result was defined as a p-value of less than or equal to 0.05, and the confidence interval (CI) was 95%.

Results

Table 2 presents the mean ± SD of marginal discrepancies for three zirconia materials (ZirCAD Prime, Cercon ht ML, and Zolid Bion) before and after thermocycling. The discrepancies were measured at buccal, palatal, mesial, distal, and overall sites. Before thermocycling, Cercon ht ML exhibited the highest discrepancies at buccal (65.86 ± 20.96), palatal (65.75 ± 21.79), mesial (63.39 ± 17.67), and overall (62.93 ± 20.79) sites, whereas Zolid Bion showed the highest distal value (60.85 ± 22.77). ZirCAD Prime consistently displayed the lowest values in all regions: buccal (56.83 ± 15.90), palatal (54.20 ± 14.45), mesial (58.81 ± 17.26), distal (53.57 ± 19.25), and overall (55.76 ± 16.41).

After thermocycling, discrepancies increased slightly for all materials. Cercon ht ML remained the highest in buccal (67.03 ± 20.77), palatal (66.36 ± 22.35), mesial (64.43 ± 17.67), and overall (63.83 ± 20.92), whereas Zolid Bion surpassed it in the distal region (64.32 ± 21.44). Discussion

The principal factors of the success of prostheses are esthetics, resistance to fracture, and marginal discrepancies.24 Achieving optimal marginal discrepancies in ceramic restorations is influenced by various factors, including fabrication technique, material selection, and clinical procedure.6 Vertical marginal discrepancies have high clinical value, and measuring them is the critical step in the assessment of the prosthesis margin.14 This study compared the marginal discrepancies of various zirconia CAD/CAM crowns on maxillary central incisors and assessed the impact of thermocycling on these discrepancies.

The overall marginal discrepancies before thermocycling ranged from 55.76 μm (lowest, ZirCAD Prime) to 62.93 μm (highest, Cercon ht ML). After thermocycling, values increased slightly, and ZirCAD Prime maintained the lowest value (57.18 μm), whereas Cercon ht ML had the highest overall (63.83 μm). Zolid Bion exceeded Cercon ht ML distally (64.32 μm). These measurements were within clinically acceptable limits (<120 μm) and similar to Chouksey et al.'s finding of a marginal gap of 56.21 ± 15.07 μm for zirconia CAD/CAM material.25 Ferrini et al. found a lower value (21.45 ± 12.58 μm) in the same sample. Lithium disilicate glass ceramics produced comparable results (62.28 ± 51.8 μm).26 Meirowitz et al. found a greater disparity (133 ± 4 μm) for monolithic zirconia crowns, exceeding permissible limits.27 This variation may be attributed to differences in fabrication workflows, sintering parameters, and measurement techniques, including software algorithms and milling tool precision.

The marginal discrepancies observed in the study showed no significant differences among the tested materials (ZirCAD Prime, Cercon ht ML, and Zolid Bion) before and after thermocycling. This result supports the first part of the null hypothesis, which states that the difference in marginal discrepancy among various zirconia CAD/CAM crowns is nonsignificant. These results align with existing literature, which indicates that factors, including preparation design, have a more substantial impact on marginal discrepancies than the material itself.7 Vertical preparations generally produce smaller marginal gaps than chamfer designs.8 Almahdy et al. found that feather-edge preparations exhibited the highest level of marginal discrepancy.28 This phenomenon can be clarified by the proposed concept that sharp angles at the restoration margin reduce marginal gaps. This concept was introduced by Shillingburg et al.21

The marginal fit of CAD/CAM ceramic restorations can be influenced by several aspects, including scanning process, software design, fire equipment, milling tool, and the ceramic material's tendency to shrink after milling and sintering.6 During the sintering process, zirconia experiences a linear shrinkage of about 20% to 25%.29 It is probable that the lack of substantial changes between the groups is due to the fact that all materials were treated using the same parameters, fabrication procedures, CAD/CAM systems, scanners, and cement spacing as instructed by the manufacturer. These results are consistent with the findings of Al Hamad et al., who underlined that manufacturing process plays a critical role in determining the marginal accuracy of CAD/CAM prostheses.29 A CAD/CAM system greatly impacts the marginal discrepancy of all-ceramic crowns created with this technology.8 Furthermore, another investigation showed large variability in accuracy among different intraoral scanners.15

The mean values of marginal gaps in both groups of monolithic zirconia crowns (Zolid Bion and ZirCAD Prime) demonstrated vertical marginal discrepancies comparable to those of multilayer zirconia crowns (Cercon ht ML). Del Piñal et al. found that both veneered and monolithic zirconia CAD/CAM crowns had similar vertical gap discrepancies that were within clinically acceptable limits (approximately 50 μm).30 By contrast, Rayyan observed that monolithic high-translucency zirconia crowns had better marginal discrepancies than porcelain-veneered crowns.31 Temizkan et al. further supported this finding, noting larger marginal gaps in layered zirconia crowns compared with monolithic zirconia crowns.12

Many authors defined 120 μm as clinically acceptable for marginal discrepancies for zirconia and provisional restorations.9,32,33 In the current investigation, all restorative materials maintained marginal discrepancies below this limit, even after thermocycling. However, thermocycling caused statistically significant changes in discrepancies among the materials, leading to the rejection of the second part of the null hypothesis. These changes are attributed to degradation and material deterioration caused by mechanical stresses.16 These findings support the work of Salama et al., who demonstrated that hydrothermal aging alters zirconia's monoclinic phase composition and surface characteristics.18 Similarly, Lopez et al. reported increased marginal discrepancies in CAD/CAM ceramic crowns after thermocycling, though values remained clinically acceptable.17

Further research should include different generations of zirconia and comparisons with various CAD/CAM prosthetic systems, investigate longer thermocycling periods, and incorporate additional mechanical tests, such as fracture forces, tensile strength, and modes and types of failure.

The present study has notable limitations. It is an in vitro design, and the use of typodont models fails to replicate dynamic oral conditions (saliva, pH changes, and masticatory forces) or account for biological responses (e.g., inflammation). Static marginal discrepancy measurements overlooked dynamic occlusal stress, and standardized fabrication protocols and reliance on a single cement type limit clinical relevance. Focus on maxillary incisors and predefined measurement points restricts generalizability to other teeth or margin variability. Thermocycling simulated only 1 year of clinical aging, omitting long-term effects. Future research should prioritize clinical studies to validate findings, incorporate extended thermocycling (e.g., simulating >5 years), and employ dynamic and longitudinal monitoring of marginal discrepancy under functional stress.
