Pretransplant Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Treatment for Intermediate-advanced Hepatocellular Carcinoma in Living Donor Liver Transplantation
Authors: Chih-Hsien Cheng, Hao-Wen Shi, Yin Lai, Hao-Chien Hung, Jin-Chiao Lee, Yu-Chao Wang, Tsung-Han Wu, Chen-Fang Lee, Ting-Jung Wu, Hong-Shiue Chou, Tse-Ching Chen, Kun-Ming Chan, Wei-Chen Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background.The use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) as a downstaging or bridging therapy for liver transplantation (LT) in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is gaining momentum. However, evidence regarding the feasibility and safety of pre-LT ICI therapy remains limited and controversial. This study presents our experience with patients with intermediate-advanced HCC who were downstaged using regimens incorporating ICIs and subsequently underwent living donor LT.Methods.We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 10 patients with Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer stage B or C HCC treated with locoregional therapies (LRTs), ICIs, and living donor LT at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital at Linkou between 2021 and 2024.Results.In total, 10 recipients received pre-LT ICI-based downstaging therapy, with 8 also undergoing concomitant LRTs. Two patients experienced grade III immune-related hepatitis. The median ICI washout period before LT was 149 d. Posttransplantation, 5 patients developed allograft rejection, which was managed effectively with routine steroid therapy. Notably, 4 patients demonstrated a complete pathological response. After a median post-LT follow-up of 15.5 mo (interquartile range, 8–25 mo), 2 patients experienced tumor recurrence. The 12- and 24-mo overall survival rates post-LT were 75%.Conclusions.Selected patients with advanced, initially untransplantable HCC can benefit from a multimodal approach involving LRTs combined with ICIs, enabling successful LT. These findings suggest a potential paradigm shift in the treatment of intermediate-advanced HCC, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts and longer-term studies.

INTRODUCTION

Liver transplantation (LT) offers the best long-term outcomes for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), particularly in patients with portal hypertension, by addressing both tumor and underlying liver disease. In the 1980s, LT was attempted for advanced, unresectable HCC, but high recurrence rates led to poor outcomes and its decline. The introduction of the Milan criteria marked a pivotal advancement by providing a standardized and prognostically validated selection framework, which has since then become the benchmark for LT eligibility in patients with HCC.1 To broaden transplant eligibility while maintaining acceptable posttransplant outcomes, various expanded criteria have been proposed. These include modifications to pretransplant imaging thresholds and the incorporation of serum biomarkers such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and des-γ-carboxy prothrombin.2 The goal of these expanded models is to preserve a 5-y overall survival (OS) rate of 70%–75%. Although the “metro ticket” concept—where increased tumor burden correlates with decreased survival—remains relevant, emerging evidence suggests that selected patients, particularly those who respond favorably to neoadjuvant or downstaging therapies, may still derive substantial benefit from LT.3

For LT candidates with intermediate to advanced HCC, many societies recommend the use of neoadjuvant locoregional therapy (LRT) to prevent disease progression during the waiting period.4,5 The development of multimodal treatment strategies and the evolution of systemic therapies—particularly immune-based approaches—have opened new avenues for downstaging advanced HCC before LT. Among these, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have demonstrated notable efficacy in advanced-stage disease, significantly influencing treatment paradigms. Although nivolumab monotherapy yields a 14% response rate, the atezolizumab–bevacizumab combination exceeds 30% in Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage C disease.6,7 These results have sparked interest in the use of ICIs before LT.

However, the use of ICIs before LT raises concerns regarding the risk of graft rejection. Although ICIs are not routinely recommended as bridging therapy, their neoadjuvant use may be feasible if an adequate washout period is observed. Nonetheless, current evidence is limited, and the impact on posttransplant outcomes remains unclear. In this report, we describe our institutional experience with living donor LT (LDLT) in patients who received ICI therapy as part of their treatment for HCC.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Patients

This study included patients with HCC who underwent ICI therapy and LDLT. The ICI regimens included nivolumab (3 mg/kg q2w), pembrolizumab (200 mg q3w), relatlimab (1 mg/kg q3w), atezolizumab (1200 mg q3w), and bevacizumab (15 mg/kg q3w). Radiological response was assessed every 3 doses of therapy. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) used were sorafenib (400 mg/d) and lenvatinib (8 mg/d). Drug-related adverse events were managed according to established institutional protocols.

The planned washout period between ICI treatment and LT was a minimum of 3 mo whenever feasible. LRTs, including radiofrequency ablation, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), stereotactic body radiation therapy, or proton radiation therapy (specifically for portal vein tumor thrombosis), were performed before or after initiating ICI therapy as clinically indicated. Patients who achieved successful downstaging and met eligibility criteria were considered for LT.

The clinical profiles of donors and recipients were collected. This study conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 2000 Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Linkou Main Branch (No.202300278BO).

Pretransplant Assessment of HCC

In our institute, the acceptable indications for LDLT are based on the University of California San Francisco (UCSF) criteria, as determined through imaging studies, with AFP of <1000 ng/mL. Before transplantation, comprehensive preoperative assessments were conducted, including whole-body computed tomography (CT) or MRI, to evaluate the extent of HCC. Additionally, positron emission tomography was used to exclude the presence of extrahepatic metastases. LT was performed only if the tumors met UCSF criteria, and no evidence of extrahepatic metastasis was identified.

Posttransplant Immunosuppressive Regimen

After LT, the immunosuppressive regimen consisted of steroids, tacrolimus, and everolimus. Intraoperatively, 500 mg of methylprednisolone was administered. Postoperatively, methylprednisolone was tapered from 200 to 40 mg/d during a 5-d period and discontinued within 3 mo of the procedure. Tacrolimus was initiated orally on postoperative day 1 or 2, once renal function had stabilized. The therapeutic target trough levels of tacrolimus were maintained at 8–10 ng/mL in the early postoperative period and adjusted to approximately 5 ng/mL for long-term management. Everolimus (1 mg/d) was introduced orally 1 mo after transplantation to complement the immunosuppressive protocol.

Clinical Follow-up

Posttransplant tumor recurrence was screened for every patient. Liver ultrasonography was performed, and AFP was measured every 3 mo. CT was performed regularly every 3 mo in the first year and every 6 mo in the second year. However, CT would be performed anytime if it were indeed needed. Tumor recurrence was defined as tumors in the liver with a typical vascular pattern or extra-liver metastasis.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median values with interquartile ranges, whereas categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. OS was defined as the time from LT to death. Patients who died from causes unrelated to HCC were censored at the time of their death. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to illustrate time-to-event outcomes. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL).

RESULTS

Recipients

From 2021 to 2024, a total of 10 LT recipients (9 men and 1 woman) who had received ICI treatment for HCC were included in the study. The patients’ ages ranged from 41 to 67 y. Six patients were diagnosed with hepatitis B, 2 with hepatitis C, 1 with both hepatitis B and C, and 1 without any viral hepatitis. At diagnosis, 3 patients were classified as BCLC stage B, whereas the remaining 7 were classified as BCLC stage C. Among the BCLC stage C patients, 5 presented with portal vein tumor thrombus, and 1 exhibited tumor involvement in both the left hepatic vein and left portal vein.

Regarding ICI therapy before transplantation, the majority of patients received atezolizumab and bevacizumab, whereas 1 patient was treated with nivolumab, 3 with pembrolizumab, and 1 with a combination of relatlimab and nivolumab. Two patients experienced grade III immune-related hepatitis, which was managed with steroids but precluded further immunotherapy treatment. Most patients underwent combination therapies, TACE and TKIs. Additionally, 2 patients received radiofrequency ablation, and 3 underwent proton therapy. The median number of ICI cycles was 5.5 (range, 1–12). The median ICI washout period before transplantation was 149 d (range, 43–471 d). Notably, the patient with the shortest washout period experienced an acute hepatitis B flare-up, whereas all other patients had a washout period exceeding 90 d. Two patients received concurrent radiotherapy for local tumor control.

All patients underwent LDLT with right lobe grafts. The median graft-to-recipient weight ratio was 1.1% (range, 0.75%–1.32%). Detailed patient characteristics and treatment outcomes are summarized in Table 1 and Table S1 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A786).

Posttransplant Clinical Events

The immunosuppression regimen followed standard protocols for LT. During the first month posttransplant, the trough level of tacrolimus was maintained between 5 and 10 ng/mL. Five patients exhibited clinical signs suggestive of acute cell-mediated rejection and were managed with steroid therapy. All cases of rejection were resolved successfully.

During posttransplant follow-up, 5 patients developed biliary complications, including biliary stenosis in 2 patients and biliary leakage in 3 patients. These complications were effectively managed with endoscopic retrograde cholangiography, involving balloon dilatation and stenting.

Patients Survival

At the time of the data cutoff (April 30, 2025), the median follow-up duration for the 10 patients was 15.5 mo (interquartile range, 8–25 mo). Two patients succumbed to tumor recurrence at 8 and 25 mo posttransplant, respectively, whereas 1 patient died from COVID-19 pneumonia 7 mo after transplantation. The OS rates at 12 and 24 mo posttransplant were both 75% (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

HCC is an aggressive malignancy with a generally poor prognosis. For patients with intermediate to advanced HCC, treatment options are typically limited to noncurative approaches such as TACE or systemic therapies. LT is generally not considered for these advanced stages due to the high incidence of posttransplant tumor recurrence. However, recent advances in ICI and the combination of ICIs with TKIs have shown promising results in the treatment of advanced HCC, with objective response rates approaching 30%.8 In patients who respond to these treatments, tumors can be downstaged to meet LT criteria, enabling conversion to a curative approach through transplantation. In this small series, 5 patients with BCLC stage C HCC and vascular tumor thrombus were successfully downstaged to meet LT criteria after responding to ICI/TKI therapy. Additionally, 2 patients with late-stage BCLC B HCC were downstaged to meet the UCSF criteria, allowing for successful LDLT. These findings suggest that the scope of LT for HCC has expanded in the era of ICIs, as advanced HCC can be effectively treated and downstaged to meet transplant criteria, offering a potential curative option.

The indications for LT in HCC are often constrained by concerns over posttransplant tumor recurrence. To address this, various transplant centers have developed institution-specific criteria aimed at selecting candidates who are likely to achieve survival outcomes comparable with those of patients transplanted for nonmalignant liver diseases. Furthermore, the allocation of deceased donor liver grafts is tightly regulated to optimize utility and ensure equitable access, particularly in regions with organ shortages. However, the long-term outcomes of LT in patients with downstaged advanced HCC remain incompletely defined. These cases often involve greater tumor burden and multiple pretransplant treatments, which can lead to discrepancies between imaging-based staging and final explant pathology. Such discordance may negatively influence posttransplant prognosis by underestimating residual tumor aggressiveness or biological behavior. Therefore, accurate pre-LT assessment and rigorous patient selection remain critical for optimizing outcomes in this challenging population.9 In this study, all LTs were performed using LDLT, thereby avoiding competition with deceased donor organs that are prioritized for patients with benign liver diseases or those with HCC within standard criteria. Notably, a significant proportion of patients in our cohort were found to exceed UCSF criteria on pathological examination, which could increase the risk of recurrence and raise ethical concerns regarding donor safety and recipient benefit. However, in regions where access to deceased donor LT is limited because of prolonged waiting times, LDLT often remains the only viable option. In carefully selected patients, LT after pretransplant ICI-based downstaging may still offer acceptable long-term outcomes. Supporting this approach, a multicenter study reported that 47% of patients exceeded UCSF criteria,10 and in a pooled analysis of 91 patients from case reports and case series, 81% were beyond Milan criteria. Despite this, the overall posttransplant recurrence rate was only 9.9%,11 highlighting the potential efficacy of ICI-based downstaging strategies in expanding transplant eligibility without substantially compromising oncological outcomes.

The use of immunotherapy before LT is a recognized risk factor for acute rejection, which in severe cases may result in graft loss. Nordness et al12 reported a fatal case involving a patient who received nivolumab before LT and subsequently developed acute hepatic necrosis in the immediate postoperative period. Histological analysis revealed extensive hepatic necrosis with marked lymphocytic infiltration in the portal tracts, underscoring the potential for life-threatening immune-mediated graft injury after perioperative ICI exposure.12 Given this risk, a sufficient washout period is generally recommended to reduce the likelihood of acute rejection and graft failure.13 In our study, the median washout period was 152 d—exceeding 5 half-lives of the administered ICIs. Despite this prolonged interval, 50% of patients still experienced acute cellular rejection, although all responded well to corticosteroid pulse therapy. These findings align with previous reports indicating that even washout periods of >3 mo do not fully eliminate the risk of immune-mediated complications.14 The duration of the washout period appears to be inversely correlated with the risk of posttransplant rejection. In an individual patient meta-analysis, Rezaee-Zavareh et al11 reported that a median washout period of 72 d was associated with a 30% probability of rejection, which decreased to 20% when the washout period was extended to 94 d. Notably, in 4 reported cases of graft loss, the washout intervals were only 0, 7, 8, and 42 d.11 In addition to rejection, biliary complications occurred in approximately 50% of our patients. Although often attributed to ischemia or surgical technique, such complications may also involve immunologic injury at the anastomotic site, potentially exacerbated by prior ICI exposure. Further studies are needed to elucidate the immunopathological mechanisms underlying ICI-associated complications in LT and to define optimal strategies—including timing, immunosuppressive regimens, and patient selection—to improve safety and outcomes in this high-risk setting.

Four patients in our cohort demonstrated complete pathological response, with no viable tumor identified in the liver explants at the time of transplantation. Previous data from Guo et al10 showed that the risk of posttransplant HCC recurrence correlates with the response to downstaging therapy: recurrence occurred in 0%, 19.5%, 37.0%, and 33.3% of patients who achieved complete response (CR), partial response, stable disease, and progressive disease, respectively. However, whether a CR to ICIs reliably predicts negligible recurrence risk after LT remains uncertain. It also remains unclear whether LT offers superior survival compared with continued nontransplant therapies in patients who achieve CR after immunotherapy. In patients undergoing ICI-based conversion therapy followed by hepatectomy, some studies have shown that those achieving CR have comparable survival outcomes regardless of whether surgery is performed. Unlike resection, LT is not limited by residual liver function, making technically all patients eligible for conversion strategies. If ICIs are capable of eliminating micrometastatic disease and are followed by total hepatectomy during LT, this combined approach may lead to better long-term oncologic outcomes. Further studies are needed to validate this hypothesis and define which patients with CR derive maximal benefit from LT.

Conversely, 2 patients in our cohort developed tumor recurrence after LT. Both had initially presented with BCLC stage C disease and exceeded UCSF criteria but were successfully downstaged to within Milan criteria after treatment with ICIs and LRTs. Despite this radiological response, explant pathology revealed no evidence of tumor necrosis and confirmed the presence of microvascular invasion in both cases. These adverse features are well-established predictors of posttransplant recurrence.15 Unfortunately, despite aggressive postrecurrence management—including cell therapy and targeted agents—both patients died within 6 mo of recurrence. This discrepancy highlights a critical limitation of current pretransplant imaging modalities. Although tumor size and number can be estimated, key biological factors such as microvascular invasion, tumor grade, microsatellite lesions, and molecular mutations remain largely undetectable before transplantation. These findings underscore the need for improved diagnostic tools to assess tumor biology preoperatively. Future studies should explore the integration of advanced imaging technologies, such as radiomics and artificial intelligence–based predictive models, to enhance tumor characterization and refine patient selection for LT.16,17 Adjuvant strategies to prevent recurrence, including the use of mTOR inhibitors and targeted therapies, have been investigated, although outcomes remain variable and inconclusive. Furthermore, the role of immunotherapy in the posttransplant setting is complex. Although isolated reports have documented success with agents such as pembrolizumab for treating metastatic recurrence, these instances remain exceptional. Due to the high risk of severe, potentially fatal graft rejection and unpredictable therapeutic responses, posttransplant immunotherapy should be approached with extreme caution and is not currently recommended as standard practice.18,19

This study is limited by its retrospective design and small sample size, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, most patients received multimodal treatment before transplantation, including TKIs and LRTs. The overlapping use of these modalities introduces substantial confounding and limits our ability to isolate the specific contribution of each intervention to downstaging success or posttransplant outcomes. Although prior studies have suggested synergistic benefits from combination therapies, our limited cohort size precluded meaningful stratified or multivariable analyses. In addition, due to the short follow-up, survival estimates beyond the observed period should still be interpreted with caution, given the limited number of patients at risk beyond 24 mo. Despite these limitations, our findings support the feasibility of using ICI-based strategies to downstage patients with advanced HCC to within transplantable criteria. Given that LT remains the only curative option for appropriately selected patients, these preliminary results are encouraging. With longer-term follow-up and validation in larger, prospective studies, this approach may represent a paradigm shift in the management of advanced and previously unresectable HCC.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study highlights the potential of incorporating ICIs into downstaging regimens for LT in patients with HCC. This approach is particularly valuable for patients with advanced disease, where conventional downstaging strategies are often associated with high recurrence rates. With careful adherence to an appropriate washout period, posttransplant rejection appears to be manageable, paving the way for broader application of this promising therapeutic strategy.
