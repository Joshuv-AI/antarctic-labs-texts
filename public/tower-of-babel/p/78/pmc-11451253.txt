Corneal stromal lenticule transplantation for the treatment of congenital optic disc pit maculopathy : a case report and review
Authors: Xiaojuan Zhang, Gang Qiao, Yu Quan, Yukun He, Hui Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCongenital optic disc pit (ODP) is a relatively uncommon congenital anomaly of the optic disc, which seriously affects the patient’s vision when combined with optic disc pit maculopathy(ODP-M). Currently, the treatment of ODP-M remains a clinical challenge and a focus of research.Case presentationA boy had a pit in the inferotemporal segment of the optic disc with ODP-M. Optical Coherence Tomography(OCT) showed ODP and serous retinal detachment. He was treated with pars plana vitrectomy(PPV), followed by Corneal Stromal Lenticule (CSL) sealing and C3F8 tamponade. In the end, significant anatomical improvement was achieved, and the Best Corrected Visual Acuity(BCVA) was improved.ConclusionsThe CSL transplantation may be a viable therapeutic option for improving ODP-M with stable anatomical and functional result. However, more cases and longer follow-up are needed to confirm the safety and effectiveness of the technology.Keywords: Optic disc pit maculopathy, Corneal Stromal Lenticule, Pars plana vitrectomy, C3F8, Retina

Background

ODP-M can lead to severe visual impairment and require active treatment. The treatment of ODP-M is still controversial and in the exploratory stage. PPV combined with various adjuvant treatment options is the preferred method for ODP-M [1].

The closure of the ODP has always been a hot topic of innovation. Previous studies have shown that the sealing materials for the ODP include the inner limiting membrane (ILM) [2], amniotic membrane(AM) [3–5], sclera tissue [6, 7], neurosensory retinal transplant [8], silicone punctal plug [9], anterior lens capsule [10], evicel fibrin sealant [11], and autologous platelet rich fibrin [12].

But there have been no reports of CSL sealing the ODP. The CSL, as a transparent biomaterial, has been used for autologous and allogeneic transplantation to treat ocular surface and refractive problems. Now, it is innovatively used for sealing the ODP and is expected to become a new treatment direction for the ODP.

Case presentation

This was a prospective clinical study. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Mianyang Wanjiang Eye Hospital (approval number: WJYK-04). Written informed consent was obtained from the parent.

Patient: An 8-year-old boy presented to our hospital with the complaint of decreased vision in the right eye for over two years. He was previously diagnosed with “amblyopia of the right eye” at a local hospital and underwent treatment with glasses and amblyopia training, but no improvement was noted. Ophthalmological Examination: The BCVA in the right eye was 0.7. No significant changes were found in the anterior segment examination of both eyes. The right eye revealed distinct optic disc borders with a cup-to-disc ratio of 0.3. A grayish, quasi-circular defect about one -half disc diameter in size was observed inferotemporal to the optic disc, with extensive posterior retinal detachment (Fig. 1A and B). Fundus examination of the left eye showed retina in place, no abnormalities in the optic disc and macular with normal foveal reflex. Past health and developmental history were normal. Eye Ultrasound (A-scan): Axial length of the right eye was 23.60 mm. OCT(Heidelberg Engineering, Germany) revealed neuroepithelial detachment in the right eye with numerous scattered hyperreflective spots; optic disc OCT showed a linear, small, hyporeflective dark area, indicating a small range of lamina cribrosa tissue signal absence (Fig. 2A and B).Fundus Fluorescein Angiography (FFA) Examination: In the early phase, the temporal side of the optic disc showed notably delayed filling, with low fluorescence at the disc margin. The ODP exhibited relatively weak fluorescence, and the posterior pole of the retina displayed diffuse granular strong fluorescence, sharply demarcated from the surrounding retina, possibly indicative of atrophic retinal pigment epithelium(RPE) cells, interspersed with dot-like obstructions of fluorescence, likely normal RPE cells or proliferative pigment. In the late phase, accumulation of fluorescein at the ODP showed relatively strong fluorescence (Fig. 3A and B). Visual field showed an arcuate defect connected to the physiological blind spot, but poor cooperation was noted, significantly increasing the likelihood of error in the result. B-scan ultrasonography of both eyes and adnexa (Philips A70, USA) indicated retinal detachment in the right eye.

Under local anesthesia, a standard three-port, 25-gauge vitrectomy through the flat part of the ciliary body was performed, followed by posterior vitreous detachment(PVD). Regular gas-liquid exchange was performed. The CSL that had undergone physical decellularization was stained with indocyanine green. One part was cut into small strips for insertion into the pit, while the other part was trimmed to a size larger than the pit to cover the surface of the pit. One to two drops of autologous platelet concentrate (APC) cover the surface of the CSL. 12% C3F8 was finally used. Postoperatively, the patient was instructed to remain in a face-down or left side-lying position for at least 2 weeks. The processing and placement of the CSL during the surgery was shown in Fig. 4A and B.

A 1.5 × 1.5 mm wide and 60 μm thick CSL is then placed over its surface (white arrow indicates the CSL stained with indocyanine green)

The BCVA was 0.9 at one month follow-up, a small amount of gas remained in the vitreous cavity, ODP was closed, CSL was in place and the macular detachment largely regressed (Fig. 5A and B). At the end of follow-up, the BCVA improved to 0.22, and the patient showed complete C3F8 reabsorption, with retinal attachment and the OCT examination confirmed that the optic pit was sealed by CSL (Figs. 6A-E). There were no postoperative complications and recurrence.

Discussion and conclusions

Congenital cavitary optic disk anomalies are a rare ocular condition, encompassing a range of abnormalities such as optic disc coloboma(ODC), ODP, peripapillary staphyloma, and morning glory syndrome(MGS) [13].

ODPs was first reported by Wiethe in 1882 [14]. These anomalies typically occur unilaterally and sporadically, with no apparent genetic predisposition, no gender or racial differences [15], and a low incidence rate of about 1/11,000 [16]. However, Some families have autosomal inheritance [17, 18]. They are most commonly found in the temporal segment of the optic disc (approximately 70% of cases) but can also be central (around 20%). The pits are well-defined, usually oval or circular in shape, and vary in size and depth. Generally, there is only one pit, but occasionally two to three can be present. These pits represent atypical congenital defects within the substance of the optic disc, appearing as small black, gray, or yellow-white depressions, sometimes covered with greyish-white glial tissue [19–21]. The affected optic disc is often larger than its counterpart. In rare instances, ODPs may coexist with defects in the iris, choroid, and retina [13]. Congenital ODP may also be associated with various systemic diseases, such as Alagille syndrome, Aicardi, neurological developmental malformations, and with specific gene mutations [22]. When not accompanied by retinal pathology, the condition is often asymptomatic and presents atypically in fundus examinations, making diagnosis challenging and leading to potential misdiagnosis. However, when associated with macular disease, it can cause severe central visual impairment, micropsia, metamorphopsia, floaters, and central scotoma [23]. In this case, the patient was initially misdiagnosed with “amblyopia” and underwent ineffective patching and other amblyopia treatments before being referred to our hospital.

The potential pathogenesis of ODP is still not very clearis. One theory is believed to be histopathologically linked to the herniation of underdeveloped primitive retinal tissue into a collagen-rich orifice, extending through a defective lamina cribrosa to the subarachnoid space. This results in the formation of pits, which are not actual holes but are filled with fibrous tissue. Another theory posits that these are congenital defects within the optic nerve substance, caused by incomplete closure or disruption during the closure of the neuroectodermal embryonic fissure [24–26].

Pits located at the center of the optic disc often lead to pallor of the temporal optic disc, while those at the temporal side are more likely to cause ODP-M [27, 28]. ODP-M includes retinoschisis in the macular region, serous retinal detachment of the macular, and cystoid macular edema. Prolonged macular retinal detachment can result in macular lamellar or full-thickness holes, RPE atrophy, and malnutrition [29–31]. Clinical presentations of serous retinal detachment commonly include detachment of the outer and inner retinal layers. Detachment of the outer retinal layer occurs in the macular area, more likely leading to macular holes(MHs), whereas the inner retinal layer detachment occurs between the optic disc and macular, i.e., retinoschisis.

Potential sources of intra-/sub-retinal fluid include liquefied vitreous [32, 33], leaky blood vessels at the base of the ODP [34], cerebrospinal fluid(CSF) from the subarachnoid space [35], peripapillary atrophy, and the choroid through the Bruch’s menbrane [36]. Extensive research has been conducted in this area, but the exact type and mechanism of fluid origin have not been fully confirmed. In any case, these fluids enter various layers of the retina, including the ganglion cell layer, inner nuclear layer, beneath the ILM, outer nuclear layer, and subretinal space, with different studies showing varying proportions of involvement. However, the outer nuclear layer is likely the first and most affected due to its loose structure. Long-term fluid accumulation between retinal layers can lead to lamellar or MHs and degeneration at various retinal layers, and excessive fluid can result in retinal detachment [37, 38].

The vitreous traction plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of ODP-M, with vitreous cortical traction exacerbating the splitting of the inner retinal layer and the occurrence of serous retinal detachment [39–41]. ODP-M usually occurs in adulthood, mostly after the age of 20, with a higher incidence in the 30–50 age group [42, 43]. However, the patient in this case was a child, which is relatively uncommon.

The ODP-M poses a particularly challenging clinical problem, with no entirely satisfactory treatment options currently available. Previous exploration of strategies has mostly been reported through case reports, lacking large sample studies.25% of cases with serous retinal detachment may heal spontaneously due to proliferative tissue closure of the communication between the pit and the subretinal space [39]. In the early stages of the disease, for patients with stable ODP, mild ODP-M, and mild visual symptoms, a careful follow-up is essential. For ODP-M patients with persistent subretinal fluid (SRF) or poor vision, active intervention may be reasonable [13].

The common intervention measures are PPV combined with PVD induction and gas filling, with or without removal of the ILM, and with or without endolaser. Of course, there are still many innovative technologies emerging. Macular buckling [44] has been used for ODP-M with a scleral sponge fixed at the posterior pole of the eye ball. However, this method is technically complex and has a long learning curve. Inner retinal fenestration [45] is performed radially in the ODP, which can cause iatrogenic damage. Moreover, this technique will be ineffective in the presence of a direct conduit beneath the retina. Autologous retinal transplantation has also been used to treat refractory ODP-M, but it may still cause complications such as retinal detachment and vitreous hemorrhage [8]. ILM related surgeries include removal of ILM and inverted ILM Flap, which expose the nerve fiber layer, affecting visual function recovery and increasing the likelihood of MH [46]. Laser photocoagulation applied laterally to the optic disc act as a fluid barrier, but it may damage the macular mastoid bundle and cause significant visual field defects [47]. Some other adjuvant strategies are used for ODP-M, but they cannot truly seal this congenital pit. Ooto et al. described the treatment of ODP-M with PPV combined with Glial Tissue peeling [48]. Valdés-Lara et al. reported a case of retinal fluid malabsorption in an ODP-M patient after PPV, which was successfully treated with micro pulse laser [49]. Systematic spironolactone and topical dorzolamide 2%, as adjuvant therapy to PPV, can also have a certain effect in the treatment of ODP-M [50, 51].

The ultimate goal of the above methods is to eliminate the sub- and intraretinal fluid and to seal the hypo thetical connection between the subretinal space and the vitreous cavity. In recent years, autologous and allogeneic transplant materials have appeared to close the communication.

The transparent CSL, procured from our hospital’s small incision lenticule extraction(SMILE) surgery, was confirmed to be free of infectious agents through rigorous testing. The CSL was cryopreserved in anhydrous glycerol at -70℃ after decellularization, subsequently thawed prior to use, rinsed with balanced salt solution, and stained with diluted indocyanine green for visualization.

The corneal stroma constitutes 90% of the cornea’s thickness, serving as its principal structural component. The CSL, extracted during SMILE surgery, comprises the anterior segment of the central corneal stroma, sourced from the corneal tissue of young, healthy humans. There is an increasing focus on both basic and clinical research into CSL transplantation, with its applications in clinical treatments and explorations expanding. The corneal stroma is composed of neatly arranged collagen fibers of Types I, III, and IV, which form long fibrils measuring approximately 35 nm, embedded in intercellular fluid and proteoglycans. Its avascularity-lacking both lymphatic tissue and blood vessel-significantly reduces the risk of immune rejection [52, 53]. Swelling in the corneal stroma arises with the dysfunction of endothelial cell pump activity. However, the anterior corneal stroma is less susceptible to swelling due to its densely interwoven fibers [54]. Corneal materials preserved in glycerol, which cause early and extensive cellular disruption, eliminate potentially antigen-presenting viable cells, and are thus widely acknowledged for their use in treating various corneal diseases. Research indicates that cryopreserved CSL exhibits outstanding tissue compatibility with rabbit and macaque corneas and possesses favorable refractive properties. These patches are increasingly recognized for their potential clinical value in human corneal transplantation, correction of hyperopia and presbyopia, and the treatment of keratoconus [55], earning them a preferred status among clinicians.

This patient was presented to our hospital with a chronic decrease in visual acuity and a prolonged case of macular retinal detachment. Following thorough discussions with his parents, who provided written informed consent, surgical intervention was agreed upon. PPV was the primary step in relieving vitreous traction and then an innovative method was utilized, involving the application of a CSL to fill and overlay the ODP. Firstly, the CSL can be used as a plug to fill the pit, effectively sealing it and providing a physical and blockade between the subarachnoid and subretinal space. Secondly, The CSL flap on the ODP serves as a physiologic physical barrier to prevent vitreous migration. The APC as an adhesive makes this coverage more secure. Given the potential risk of retinal laser barrage near the ODP exacerbating damage to the nerve fiber layer and the consequential loss of visual function, the surgical approach was meticulously designed to address the ODP directly, minimizing collateral damage to ensure optimal healing and visual improvement. After extensive follow-up, the subretinal fluid was effectively absorbed, the ODP was successfully sealed by the CSL, and a significant enhancement in BCVA was observed.

The materials previously studied, such as ILM [56], sclera tissue [57], neurosensory retinal transplant [8], and anterior lens capsule [10], all have additional damage. In addition, AM, APC [58] and fibrin glue are gradually absorbed over time, and cannot be regarded as a permanent solution [11, 59]. The CSL, which is transparent, does not affect the patient’s visual function, and does not degrade, theoretically closing ODP for a longer time. The CSL, as human eye tissue, has stronger biocompatibility. The CSL was once discarded as medical waste, but now If it can be used for ODP-M, it may open a new field for treatment of retinal diseases.

Currently, the patient has demonstrated substantial functional and anatomical restoration. However, ongoing monitoring is necessary moving forward. Should the SRF remain unabsorbed over an extended period, potentially impairing RPE function, there is a risk of deteriorating vision for the child. Increasing SRF or the emergence of other complications related to the ODP would necessitate additional interventions. The understanding and management of ODP-M are still evolving. The applicability of this treatment as a clinical strategy for addressing ODP conditions requires validation through further research involving larger sample sizes.
