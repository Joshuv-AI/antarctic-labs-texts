A Bayesian network meta-analysis of peripheral nerve blocks for postoperative analgesia in adults following hip fracture surgery
Authors: Wenzhu Wang, Changxiu Yuan, Fei Wang, Ang Liu, Haolin Fang, Jian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeripheral nerve blocks are an important component of postoperative pain management after hip fracture surgery. However, the optimal peripheral nerve block remains uncertain. This Bayesian network meta-analysis aimed to compare the analgesic effectiveness of commonly used peripheral nerve blocks and identify the most effective approach for postoperative pain control.MethodsThe Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, CNKI, Wanfang, and Sinomed databases were searched from inception to October 2024. Randomized controlled trials involving adult patients undergoing hip fracture surgery and reporting 24-h postoperative opioid consumption, pain scores within 24 h, or time to first rescue analgesia were included. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2.0, and the certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework.ResultsForty-four randomized controlled trials involving 3,292 patients and ten peripheral nerve blocks were included. Compared with control groups, most peripheral nerve blocks reduced opioid consumption within 24 h postoperatively. Quadratus lumborum block and erector spinae plane block reached the minimum clinically important difference (mean difference (MD), -37.51 [95% confidence interval (CI), -49.74 to -25.28]; MD, -31.28 [95% CI, -43.26 to -19.21], respectively). Paravertebral block, erector spinae plane block, and quadratus lumborum block significantly reduced 24-h pain scores (MD − 2.12, 95% CI − 3.84 to − 0.39; MD − 1.56, 95% CI − 2.49 to − 0.65; MD − 1.22, 95% CI − 2.08 to − 0.35, respectively), with paravertebral block and erector spinae plane block achieving the minimum clinically important difference. Quadratus lumborum block provided significant analgesia up to 12 h. Most peripheral nerve blocks prolonged the time to first rescue analgesia. Meta-regression and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings. No evidence of publication bias or small-study effects was identified.ConclusionsErector spinae plane block, quadratus lumborum block, and paravertebral block may offer clinically meaningful postoperative analgesia for patients undergoing hip fracture surgery. However, the certainty of the evidence ranged from very low to low. Use of peripheral nerve blocks should be individualized within a multimodal analgesia approach, and further high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings.Trial registrationPROSPERO register: CRD42024527147.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03609-5.

Introduction

Hip fractures are increasingly recognized as a major public health challenge, particularly among older adults, as global populations continue to age [1]. These injuries primarily include femoral neck, intertrochanteric, and subtrochanteric fractures, with femoral neck fractures being the most prevalent [2]. A recent network meta-analysis (NMA) of 33 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 5,703 patients reported that arthroplasty has become the preferred treatment for displaced femoral neck fractures [3]. Effective postoperative pain management is essential following hip fracture surgery, as inadequate analgesia may be associated with complications such as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, and myocardial infarction. Poor pain control may also limit early mobilization, prolong hospital stay, delay rehabilitation, and adversely affect long-term functional recovery [4–6].

Peripheral nerve blocks (PNBs) are widely used for postoperative analgesia and have been associated with improved perioperative outcomes [7–10]. In hip fracture surgery, commonly applied PNBs include paravertebral block (PVB), erector spinae plane block (ESPB), quadratus lumborum block (QLB), fascia iliaca compartment block (FICB), pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block, lumbar plexus block (LPB), and femoral nerve block (FNB). Despite the availability of multiple techniques, the optimal PNB strategy for patients undergoing hip fracture surgery remains uncertain [11].

Previous traditional meta-analyses have evaluated the analgesic effects of PNBs in this population; however, most comparisons have focused on individual PNBs versus placebo or no block, with limited direct head-to-head evidence among different techniques [12–15]. NMA enables the integration of both direct and indirect evidence, allowing for the estimation of relative treatment effects in the absence of direct comparisons and providing a more comprehensive evaluation of available interventions [16]. Bayesian NMA offers further advantages by characterizing uncertainty through probability distributions, incorporating prior information to improve precision, and providing greater flexibility in modeling multi-arm studies, sources of heterogeneity, small sample sizes, and missing data [17].

This study aims to conduct a comprehensive Bayesian NMA to compare commonly used PNBs for hip fracture surgery. By evaluating outcomes including postoperative opioid consumption, pain scores, and time to first analgesic request, this analysis seeks to provide comparative evidence to inform postoperative analgesic decision-making in patients undergoing hip fracture surgery.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (registration number CRD42024527147), and no deviations from the registered protocol were made. Additional details of the protocol are available on the PROSPERO website (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024527147).

Eligibility criteria

The following inclusion criteria were applied. Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) undergoing surgery for hip fractures. PNB could be administered as a single injection or via continuous catheter infusion and could be performed in the preoperative area, operating room, or post-anesthesia care unit. Comparator groups included either a sham block or no block. Eligible studies were required to report at least one of the following outcomes: cumulative analgesic consumption within 24 h, pain scores at 6, 12, or 24 h postoperatively, or time to first request for rescue analgesia. Only RCTs were included.

Hip surgeries performed for arthritis or avascular necrosis were excluded. Studies were also excluded if PNB was performed in the emergency department or ward, or if the procedure involved repeat surgery. In addition, studies were excluded if the full text or key data could not be obtained after contacting the authors or if duplicate publications were identified.

Search methods

We searched the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Embase, PubMed, CNKI, Wanfang, and Sinomed databases from inception to October 2024. Detailed search strategies for the English-language databases are provided in Supplemental Appendix 1. For non-English articles (excluding Chinese), professional translation support was used. When full-text articles were unavailable, assistance was sought from a literature search specialist.

Data extraction

Two pairs of reviewers (WZW, CXY, FW, and AL) independently extracted data using EndNote X9. Extracted variables included publication year, patient demographics (age and sex), sample size, intervention characteristics, type of local anesthetic (LA), timing of nerve block administration, type of surgical anesthesia, postoperative analgesic regimen, publication language, funding, fracture type, surgical procedure, and reported outcomes. Data were primarily extracted from study tables. When relevant information was available only in graphical form, the corresponding authors were contacted. If no response was received, numerical data were extracted using GetData Graph Digitizer 2.26. Disagreements regarding study inclusion were resolved by a fifth reviewer (JL).

Definition of outcomes

The primary endpoints were cumulative opioid consumption and pain scores at 24 h postoperatively. Secondary endpoints included pain scores at 6 and 12 h and time to first rescue analgesic. All opioid doses were converted to oral morphine equivalents (OME, mg) using the conversion tables described by Nielsen et al. and Pereira et al. [18, 19]. Postoperative pain was assessed using either the 11-point Numeric Rating Scale (0 = no pain, 10 = worst pain imaginable) or a 10-cm Visual Analog Scale (VAS). As these instruments demonstrate comparable validity, reliability, and responsiveness, pain scores were pooled without further transformation [20]. When pain scores were reported both at rest and during movement, only scores at rest were extracted for analysis.

The minimum clinically important difference (MCID) was defined as the smallest improvement that patients would consider meaningful after weighing potential risks and costs [21]. In this review, the MCID was operationalized as a reduction of ≥ 30 mg in 24-h OME consumption and a change of ≥ 1.86 cm on the 0–10 cm VAS at any assessed time point [22]. Time to first rescue analgesia was recorded in hours. Safety outcomes included adverse events related to PNBs, such as hematoma, infection, nerve injury, and LA systemic toxicity.

Quality assessment

To assess study quality and the certainty of evidence, the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) tool [23] and the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) framework [24] were applied. Risk-of-bias assessment focused on key domains, including the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. Initial RoB 2 judgments and CINeMA ratings were performed independently by WZW and CXY. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (HLF) adjudicated.

Data synthesis and analysis

The NMA was performed using STATA version 14.0 and the R packages gemtc and rjags (R version 4.43). For continuous outcomes reported on different scales, standardized metrics were applied, and effect sizes were expressed as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For three-arm trials, a covariance matrix was used to account for correlations among intervention groups and to avoid double-counting of shared comparators. Network plots were generated in STATA, and subsequent analyses were conducted in R. Global inconsistency was assessed by comparing the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) between consistency and inconsistency models, with a ΔDIC < 5 considered indicative of acceptable consistency. The consistency model was implemented in JAGS with 50,000 burn-in iterations followed by 100,000 sampling iterations across four Markov chains, using a thinning interval of 10, yielding 20,000 iterations for inference. Model convergence was evaluated using trace plots, density plots, and the potential scale reduction factor (PSRF), with convergence defined by stable chains, narrow bandwidths, and a PSRF close to 1. Transitivity was examined by assessing potential effect modifiers, including publication year, proportion of male participants, and sample size. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using τ2 and I2 statistics. Local inconsistency was evaluated using the node-splitting method, with a p-value < 0.05 indicating statistically significant inconsistency. A random-effects model was applied for all pooled estimates. The Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking curve (SUCRA) was calculated to rank interventions, with higher SUCRA values indicating more favorable rankings. Meta-regression analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the primary outcomes, considering study quality, publication year, participant age, gender distribution, sample size, type of LA, type of surgical anesthesia, timing of intervention, and use of continuous infusion. Sensitivity analyses for the primary outcomes included excluding non-English studies and reassessing pain scores using the standardized mean difference (SMD). Comparison-adjusted funnel plots and Egger’s regression test were generated in STATA to evaluate potential small-study effects.

Results

A total of 3,849 records were identified through database searches, including 1,695 from the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, 1,105 from Embase, 443 from PubMed, 159 from CNKI, 219 from Wanfang, and 228 from Sinomed. After removal of 1,413 duplicate records, 2,436 unique records remained for screening. Title and abstract screening yielded 79 studies for full-text review. Of these, 44 RCTs [25–68] met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. Study characteristics and quality assessment

A total of 44 RCTs comprising 3,292 patients with a mean age of 66.9 years were included. Among these, 2,273 patients received PNBs, and 1,019 were assigned to control groups. One study used a three-arm design [64], while all others were two-arm trials. Ten types of PNBs were evaluated: ESPB in four studies [33, 52, 53, 64] (9.1%); FICB in 29 studies [25, 27–32, 34, 38–47, 51, 55, 57–60, 62, 63, 65–67] (65.9%); FNB in three studies [49, 50, 63] (6.8%); FNB combined with lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) block in two studies [54, 56]; LPB in two studies [33, 39]; LPB combined with sacral plexus block (SPB) in one study [36]; PENG block in 19 studies [26, 28, 30, 34, 35, 37, 38, 41, 42, 44–49, 55, 56, 60, 61] (43.2%); PENG combined with LFCN in one study [62]; PVB in one study [53]; and QLB in three studies [35, 64, 68]. Control interventions included sham blocks in three studies [32, 43, 68] and no intervention in 21 studies [26, 27, 29, 31, 35–37, 40, 48, 50–52, 54, 56, 58, 59, 61, 64–67]. Sample sizes ranged from 10 to 116 participants. LAs included ropivacaine, bupivacaine, and levobupivacaine. Most blocks (72.7%) were administered preoperatively, whereas ten studies [25, 27, 30, 33, 38, 40, 53, 60, 63, 64] performed them postoperatively; one study applied blocks either pre- or postoperatively [59]. Spinal anesthesia (SA) was used in 35 studies (79.5%), general anesthesia (GA) in eight [29, 36, 39, 47, 59, 62, 65, 67], and one study [49] did not report the anesthesia type. Most trials adopted multimodal analgesia, although five did not clearly describe the regimen [35–37, 49, 65]. Five studies were published in non-English languages [39, 50, 51, 61, 62]. No study reported industry funding. Detailed study characteristics are provided in Supplementary Appendix 2.

Risk of bias within studies

In the risk of bias assessment, 22 studies were judged to have a low risk in terms of random sequence generation. Twenty studies were rated as having “some concerns,” primarily due to insufficient reporting of allocation concealment. Two trials were classified as high risk: one did not report allocation concealment or baseline comparability, and the other employed a non-random assignment method based on medical record numbers [57, 59]. Approximately half of the trials raised concerns regarding deviations from intended interventions, primarily because blinding was absent or incomplete, and several studies used unblinded saline controls. All studies were judged to be at low risk of bias due to missing outcome data. In contrast, 14 studies were rated as high risk with respect to outcome measurement, as procedures to minimize detection bias were inadequate [35–37, 40, 43, 48, 50–52, 56, 58, 61, 63, 67]. Given the subjective nature of pain assessment, awareness of group allocation may have influenced outcome scoring. One study raised concerns regarding selective reporting, as the final sample size did not meet the prespecified minimum [29]. Overall, 14 studies (31.8%) were rated as low risk of bias [25, 27, 28, 30, 32–34, 41, 42, 44, 46, 47, 64, 66], 14 (31.8%) as having some concerns [26, 29, 31, 38, 39, 45, 49, 53–55, 60, 62, 65, 68], and 16 (36.4%) as high risk. Convergence diagnostics and transitivity

Convergence of the MCMC chains was confirmed using the Gelman–Rubin diagnostic (PSRF), and visual inspection of trace plots indicated stable chains (Supplementary Appendix 3). Assessment of potential effect modifiers, including publication year, proportion of male participants, and sample size, suggested that the transitivity assumption was acceptable (Supplementary Appendix 4).

Primary outcomes

Twenty studies (45.5%), including 1,420 participants (43.1%), reported 24-h postoperative analgesic consumption. These trials assessed seven types of PNBs: ESPB, FICB, FNB, FNB + LFCN, LPB, PENG block, and QLB. The network plot of comparisons is shown in Fig. 2A. Compared with the control group, all PNBs except LPB significantly reduced 24-h analgesic consumption. However, only QLB and ESPB reached the predefined MCID, with MDs of –37.51 mg (95% CI –49.74 to –25.28) and –31.28 mg (95% CI –43.26 to –19.21), respectively (Fig. 3A). Twenty-seven studies (61.2%), comprising 2,087 participants (63.4%), reported pain scores at 24 h postoperatively. Six PNBs were evaluated: ESPB, FICB, LPB, PENG block, PVB, and QLB (Fig. 2B). Compared with the control group, PVB, ESPB, and QLB were associated with significantly lower 24-h pain scores, with MDs of –2.12 cm (95% CI, –3.84 to –0.39), –1.56 cm (95% CI, –2.49 to –0.65), and –1.22 cm (95% CI, –2.08 to –0.35), respectively. Of these interventions, only PVB achieved the predefined MCID threshold (Fig. 3B). Pairwise comparisons demonstrated that PVB resulted in greater pain reduction than the PENG block (MD, –1.79 cm; 95% CI, –3.60 to –0.01) and FICB (MD, –1.80 cm; 95% CI, –3.57 to –0.07). ESPB was also associated with lower pain scores compared with the PENG block (MD, –1.23 cm; 95% CI, –2.28 to –0.18) and FICB (MD, –1.25 cm; 95% CI, –2.24 to –0.38). QLB demonstrated a modest analgesic advantage over FICB (MD, –0.90 cm; 95% CI, –1.79 to –0.02). Complete pairwise comparisons are provided in Supplementary Appendix 5.2.

Secondary outcomes

Twenty-two studies (50.0%), comprising 1,802 participants (54.7%), reported pain scores at 6 h postoperatively. Six PNBs were evaluated: ESPB, FICB, LPB + SPB, PENG block, PVB, and QLB (Supplementary Appendix 6.1). QLB, PENG block, and FICB were associated with lower 6-h pain scores, with MDs of − 2.30 cm (95% CI − 3.79 to − 0.81), − 1.39 cm (95% CI − 2.52 to − 0.26), and − 1.23 cm (95% CI − 2.15 to − 0.32), respectively. Among these interventions, only QLB appeared to reach the predefined MCID threshold. No statistically significant pairwise differences were observed among the PNBs (Supplementary Appendix 5.3).

Seventeen studies (38.6%), comprising 1,122 participants (34.1%), reported pain scores at 12 h postoperatively. Five PNBs were assessed: ESPB, FICB, LPB, PENG block, and QLB (Supplementary Appendix 6.2). ESPB, QLB, and PENG block were associated with lower 12-h pain scores, with MDs of − 1.89 cm (95% CI − 2.87 to − 0.91), − 1.48 cm (95% CI − 2.24 to − 0.73), and − 1.09 cm (95% CI − 1.64 to − 0.53), respectively. Among these interventions, only ESPB appeared to reach the MCID threshold. Pairwise comparisons indicated that ESPB, QLB, and PENG block were associated with lower pain scores than FICB (MD − 1.44 cm; 95% CI − 2.54 to − 0.35; MD − 1.03 cm; 95% CI − 1.85 to − 0.22; and MD − 0.65 cm; 95% CI − 1.08 to − 0.20, respectively) (Supplementary Appendix 5.4).

Twenty-two studies (50.0%), comprising 1,646 participants (50.0%), reported time to first rescue analgesia. Seven PNBs were evaluated: ESPB, FICB, FNB, FNB + LFCN, PENG block, PENG block + LFCN, and QLB (Supplementary Appendix 6.3). Except for PENG block + LFCN and FNB, all evaluated blocks were associated with a longer time to first rescue analgesia, with MDs ranging from 2.70 to 10.83 h. Pairwise comparisons indicated that FNB + LFCN was associated with a longer time to first rescue analgesia compared with other PNBs. QLB was also associated with a longer duration than FICB (MD, 3.59 h; 95% CI, 0.94 to 6.36) (Supplementary Appendix 5.5).

Safety outcomes

No nerve block–related complications were reported in the included studies.

Heterogeneity, inconsistency analysis

High heterogeneity was observed across all five outcomes (Supplementary Appendix 7). However, neither the global inconsistency test nor the node-splitting analysis indicated evidence of inconsistency, as shown in Supplementary Appendix 8.

Meta-regression analyses and sensitivity analyses

Meta-regression analyses for the primary outcomes indicated that study quality, publication year, mean age, proportion of male participants, sample size, type of LA, type of surgical anesthesia, timing of intervention, and use of continuous infusion did not significantly influence the results (Supplementary Appendix 9). As all studies reporting 24-h postoperative analgesic consumption used single-shot injections, meta-regression for continuous infusion was not applicable for this outcome. In sensitivity analyses, non-English studies were excluded. For the 24-h analgesic consumption outcome, exclusion of two studies resulted in the absence of LPB-related data but did not alter the ranking of interventions (Supplementary Appendix 10.1). As all studies reporting 24-h pain scores were published in English, no additional sensitivity analyses were conducted for this outcome. To further assess robustness, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed using SMDs to harmonize NRS and VAS data, with results consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Appendix 10.2).

Small study effects, publication bias, and certainty of the evidence

Comparison-adjusted funnel plots and Egger’s tests for all five outcomes indicated no evidence of publication bias or small-study effects (Supplementary Appendix 11). According to the CINeMA framework, the certainty of evidence for 24-h postoperative analgesic consumption and time to first rescue analgesia was predominantly rated as very low. The certainty of evidence for pain scores at 24 and 6 h postoperatively was generally rated as low to very low, whereas the evidence for pain scores at 12 h postoperatively was primarily rated as low. The main reasons for downgrading the certainty of evidence were within-study bias and imprecision. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first NMA comparing the analgesic effects of different PNBs in patients undergoing hip fracture surgery. The results indicated that QLB and ESPB appeared to be associated with reduced opioid consumption within 24 h postoperatively and may have reached the MCID. PVB appeared to be the only technique that may have reached the MCID for pain scores at 24 h postoperatively. In addition, QLB appeared to be associated with analgesic effects up to 12 h after surgery, although this finding is based on limited and uncertain evidence. Most PNBs were also associated with a longer time to first rescue analgesia, suggesting a possible analgesic benefit; however, no definitive conclusions can be drawn. Given that the CINeMA assessment indicated low to very low certainty of evidence, all comparative findings should be interpreted with caution.

The sensory innervation of the hip joint is primarily derived from the anterior branches of the lower lumbar plexus (L2–L4) and the upper sacral plexus (L4–S1). The anterior capsule is predominantly innervated by the femoral nerve (FN) and obturator nerve (ON), whereas the posterior capsule receives sensory input from the sciatic nerve, superior gluteal nerve, and the nerve to the quadratus femoris muscle [69–72]. Gerhardt et al. [73] suggested that the anterior region of the hip capsule represents a key target for analgesic interventions in hip surgery.

The application of ESPB has expanded from the thoracic to the lumbar region. In selected high-risk elderly patients, a single L4-level lumbar ESPB combined with mild sedation has been reported to facilitate surgical completion without the use of GA or incision infiltration anesthesia [74]. However, these findings are derived from limited evidence and should be interpreted with caution. Similarly, in pediatric patients undergoing open reduction surgery for developmental dysplasia of the hip, lumbar ESPB has been reported to be associated with lower pain scores within the first 24 h postoperatively and a possible reduction in opioid consumption [75]. Nevertheless, given the small sample sizes and study-specific contexts, these findings should be interpreted as indicating possible analgesic benefits rather than definitive effects.

At present, there is no clear consensus regarding the analgesic role of QLB following total hip arthroplasty (THA) [76–78]. In contrast, among patients with hip fractures, QLB has been increasingly reported to demonstrate potential clinical benefits, although the certainty of evidence remains limited. The apparent discrepancy in analgesic effects between these two populations may be related to differences in underlying pathology. Patients undergoing THA frequently present with advanced osteoarthritis, and long-standing chronic pain may contribute to central sensitization, which could potentially amplify postoperative pain responses [79]. Consequently, compared with individuals with hip fractures, patients undergoing THA may require more comprehensive and diversified multimodal analgesic strategies to achieve adequate postoperative pain control [80, 81].

PVB has been reported to be associated with analgesic effects that appear comparable to those of neuraxial techniques, with a potentially lower incidence of adverse events such as hypotension and urinary retention [82]. When performed at the L1–L2 vertebral level, PVB has been reported to be associated with effective postoperative analgesia in procedures such as inguinal hernia repair, open appendectomy, and laparoscopic cholecystectomy. However, its application in hip fracture surgery remains exploratory, and the current evidence is limited.

FICB and PENG blocks are among the most commonly used PNBs for analgesia in the management of hip fractures. FICB primarily targets the FN and the LFCN, providing sensory coverage of the anterolateral thigh with partial analgesia of the hip joint [83]. The PENG block targets the anterior hip capsule by anesthetizing the articular branches of the femoral, obturator, and accessory obturator nerves [84, 85]. In our analysis, both techniques were associated with statistically significant reductions in postoperative pain scores. However, neither appeared to reach the MCID threshold, indicating that the clinical relevance of these reductions may be limited, despite favorable statistical results.

PNB plays an important role in clinical analgesia; however, its effectiveness may be influenced by substantial procedural and pharmacological variability, which likely contributes to the heterogeneity observed in the present analysis. These sources of heterogeneity include the type, dose, and concentration of LA, the choice between GA and SA, and the timing of block administration. At comparable concentrations and volumes, levobupivacaine has been reported to be associated with a longer duration of analgesia than ropivacaine [86]. Increasing the volume of LA while maintaining the same total dose has been reported to be associated with greater sensory spread and may be associated with improved analgesic efficacy [87, 88]. The relative merits of GA and SA remain an active area of investigation. SA offers superior short-term postoperative analgesia and facilitates earlier mobilization and discharge compared with GA [89, 90], largely due to its ability to reliably block nociceptive transmission in the targeted spinal segments. In addition, preoperative rather than postoperative block administration has been shown to reduce intraoperative opioid requirements, attenuate inflammatory responses, lower postoperative pain scores, and prolong the time to first rescue analgesia [91, 92]. Such heterogeneity should be acknowledged as a key limitation, as it may weaken the strength of clinical recommendations and limit the generalizability of the present findings.

Although none of the included studies reported complications related to nerve block techniques, adverse events were not primary outcomes and therefore may not have been systematically assessed or consistently reported. As a result, this review is unable to draw definitive conclusions regarding the safety of these techniques. In this context, existing case reports suggest that potential complications should not be overlooked [93, 94].

This study has several limitations. First, although the NMA satisfied the transitivity assumption at the statistical level, substantial variability in intervention protocols and baseline characteristics across centers may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, most included trials did not implement allocation concealment and frequently adopted blank controls rather than sham procedures. When combined with the inherently subjective nature of pain assessment, these methodological limitations contributed to a considerable proportion of studies being judged as having “some concerns” or “high risk” of bias. Incorporation of both allocation concealment and sham blocks in future trials may help strengthen blinding and improve internal validity [95]. Third, substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity was observed, despite the use of meta-regression analyses to explore potential effect modifiers. Fourth, although the MCID threshold was applied to aid interpretation, equating statistical significance with clinical relevance may represent an oversimplification, as the optimal level of analgesia can vary across patients and healthcare settings. Finally, based on the CINeMA framework, the overall certainty of evidence was rated as low, indicating that although the present findings represent the best available synthesis, they should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Conclusion

PVB, ESPB, and QLB may be associated with clinically meaningful postoperative analgesia in hip fracture surgery, although the overall certainty of evidence remains limited. Their apparent effectiveness may vary according to patient characteristics, anesthesiologist expertise, and institutional analgesic practices. Given the low certainty of the available evidence, clinical decisions regarding the use of PNBs for hip fracture surgery should be individualized, taking into account patient-specific factors, provider experience, and principles of multimodal analgesia. While these findings offer preliminary insights, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn, underscoring the need for high-quality RCTs to further evaluate these associations.
