Multidisciplinary diagnosis and treatment of thyroid diffuse large B cell lymphoma with tracheal invasion: a case report
Authors: Zhiqiang Hu, Zhiling Liu, Jinjie Pan, Qingyang Liu, Yong Ying, Xiangtai Zeng, Yang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary thyroid lymphoma (PTL) is a rare malignant tumor that grows rapidly, with tracheal compression being a common symptom. Surgical removal of the tumor to relieve tracheal compression plays a crucial role in improving the patient’s quality of life.Case presentationWe report a case of a 66-year-old female patient of Han ethnicity (China) with primary thyroid diffuse large B cell lymphoma accompanied by tracheal invasion. The patient underwent total thyroidectomy followed by a rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone chemotherapy regimen, achieving complete remission of tracheal invasion.ConclusionThis case demonstrates the significant value of multidisciplinary collaboration in managing locally advanced primary thyroid lymphoma, which successfully avoided resecting the patient’s trachea and greatly improved their quality of life.

Introduction

Primary thyroid lymphoma (PTL) accounts for 1–5% of all thyroid cancers [1], mainly affecting middle-aged and elderly women [2]. Women are significantly more susceptible than men, and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) is a well-known risk factor for developing PTL [3]. The disease typically manifests as a rapidly enlarging neck mass. When the mass compresses or invades the trachea—a critical complication that can lead to airway obstruction—it often causes symptoms such as dyspnea, hoarseness, or dysphagia [4]. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common histological subtype. Moreover, tracheal invasion further complicates management of these cases. The optimal treatment strategy remains debated. This report describes a patient that was successfully managed with surgical resection and chemotherapy, thereby preserving tracheal integrity; it provides valuable insights for clinical decision-making.

Case report

Clinical presentation

A 66-year-old Han Chinese woman presented with a 3-month history of a painless neck mass that gradually increased in size, along with exertional dyspnea for 1 week. She had a history of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and hypothyroidism. Physical examination revealed grade III thyroid enlargement, with the left lobe measuring 8.5 × 5.0 × 4.0 cm and the right lobe measuring 6.0 × 4.0 × 3.0 cm. The thyroid was firm and less mobile. No palpable lymphadenopathy was observed.

Diagnostic investigations

Laboratory findings on the second day of hospitalization showed the following: thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH): 51.70 μIU/L (reference range: 0.27–4.20 μIU/mL); free T3: 3.30 Pmol/L (reference range: 3.10–6.80 Pmol/L); free T4: 15.00 Pmol/L (reference range: 12.00–22.00 Pmol/L); thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb): 955.00 IU/mL (reference range: ≤ 115.00 IU/mL); and thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb): 64.9 IU/mL (reference range: ≤ 34.00 IU/mL).

Postoperative surveillance CT (after surgery and chemotherapy) (Fig. 2) of the neck demonstrated no residual normal thyroid tissue in the thyroid bed, consistent with total thyroidectomy changes, with no residual thyroid tissue at the site of prior tracheal tumor invasion, preserved structural integrity of the tracheal wall without focal thickening or soft tissue nodules, a patent tracheal lumen without stenosis, and an intact smooth mucosal surface in the previously involved region.

Treatment course

The patient underwent total thyroidectomy followed by an adjuvant rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP) chemotherapy regimen administered every 3 weeks. Critically, to address the tracheal invasion, the tumor tissue adherent to the trachea was meticulously dissected and scraped off, with the specific goal of preserving the structural integrity of the tracheal cartilage; no tracheal resection was required. The management of residual disease was consolidated with an adjuvant R-CHOP chemotherapy regimen administered every 3 weeks for a total of eight cycles. The patient received the first cycle of chemotherapy on the 10th day after the surgery (Fig. 3). The expression of PAX-5, CD20, and CD45 (LCA) confirmed the diagnosis of diffuse large B cell lymphoma. The high Ki-67 index (80% +) indicated its highly aggressive nature. The immunophenotypic profile of CD10 (−), Bcl-6 (weak+), and MUM-1 (−) classified it as a germinal center B cell-like (GCB) subtype. Furthermore, the negative results for the other biomarkers helped exclude other types of lymphoma.

Discussion and conclusion

PTL is a rare malignant thyroid tumor with an annual incidence of approximately two cases per million people [1], with a two- to threefold higher incidence in female than in male individuals [5, 6]. Patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) have a relative risk of developing PTL that is 67–80 times higher [7]. Meta-analysis data reveal that 78.9% of PTL cases coexist with HT, while approximately one-third of patients with PTL present with hypothyroidism. This case report describes a 66-year-old female patient with preexisting HT and hypothyroidism.

Diagnosing PTL can be challenging. Its clinical manifestations lack specificity, and ultrasound often reveals hypoechoic masses with abundant blood flow signals, which can easily be confused with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis [8, 9]. In this study, ultrasound was used to diagnose diffuse thyroid lesions. CT examination is valuable for assessing tumor relationships with surrounding tissues, detecting compressive symptoms, and evaluating the extent of local invasion [10–12]. In the current case, CT was used to identify a malignant thyroid tumor invading the tracheal wall and causing luminal stenosis. Thyroid biopsy has become increasingly important in the diagnosis of thyroid disease. For thyroid masses that are suspected to be PTL, a preoperative biopsy should be performed whenever possible; a core needle biopsy is more appropriate than a fine needle biopsy [13]. Open surgical biopsy provides complete lesion sections for pathological diagnosis with the highest accuracy [14, 15]. Owing to the rarity and heterogeneity of PTL, there is limited high-level evidence from large-scale, prospective studies. Current PTL treatment follows the approach used for extranodal lymphomas, combining chemotherapy and radiotherapy with local therapy [16]. Some studies demonstrate that radiotherapy improves the prognosis of PTL [17–19]. Additionally, a Japanese retrospective study of PTL [20] showed favorable outcomes in patients who underwent radical thyroidectomy, indicating the significant prognostic value of surgical resection.

Preoperative cervical CT in this patient suggested thyroid malignancy with tracheal wall invasion and intraluminal protrusion, confirming the diagnosis of malignant thyroid tumor accompanied by tracheal stenosis-induced dyspnea that warranted surgical intervention. The planned procedure was total thyroidectomy with tracheal lesion resection and tracheostomy, with intraoperative frozen section pathology prepared. Frozen pathology confirmed PTL, which encompasses multiple pathological subtypes: DLBCL being most frequent (> 50%), followed by extranodal marginal zone mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma (10–23%), follicular lymphoma (~10%), and rarer forms, including classical Hodgkin lymphoma and T cell lymphomas [21]. Given PTL’s rarity and heterogeneity, high-level evidence from prospective large-scale studies remains limited, and current treatment follows extranodal lymphoma protocols combining chemoradiotherapy with local therapy. Relevant studies demonstrate that rituximab induces thyroid lymphoma cell lysis through both direct (apoptosis induction) and indirect (complement-mediated cytolysis) mechanisms, and its addition to CD20-positive lymphoma treatment significantly improves complete response rates while reducing treatment failure and relapse rates [16]. On the basis of the excellent response of PTL to R-CHOP chemotherapy, and to avoid the physical and psychological trauma associated with post-resection speech loss, accelerate recovery, and improve the patient’s quality of life, the surgical strategy was intraoperatively adjusted to total thyroidectomy alone, without tracheal resection or tracheostomy and preserved tracheal integrity. The residual tumor tissue on the trachea was managed with postoperative R-CHOP chemotherapy. Follow-up neck CT scans showed no evidence of recurrence in the surgical area or the primary involved tracheal region, a patent tracheal lumen, and complete resolution of the previously invaded area. The patient described experiencing mild fatigue for 1–2 days after each of the five subsequent chemotherapy sessions administered at our hospital, but no other specific discomfort was reported. This indicates that the R-CHOP regimen provided effective tumor control, and the patient also achieved an excellent recovery. In this case of PTL with tracheal invasion, treatment was not limited to surgical resection alone; instead, the combination of surgery and chemotherapy achieved complete tracheal preservation and significantly enhanced the patient’s quality of life. This underscores the critical importance of multidisciplinary collaboration in developing individualized treatment plans to optimize patient outcomes.

Limitations of the study were the following: First, whether preoperative chemotherapy could have been attempted to reduce the tumor volume was not explored. Although this surgery was successful, performing the operation after tumor shrinkage could have made it safer and simpler. Second, the current follow-up for the patient is nonstandard and requires improvement. Future long-term follow-up should adopt a more comprehensive approach to obtain standardized and complete clinical data and records.

Conclusion

PTL is a rare malignant thyroid tumor with nonspecific clinical symptoms and imaging findings, requiring pathological confirmation for a definitive diagnosis. Current treatment primarily involves a combination of chemotherapy, surgery, and radiotherapy. For patients with PTL with tracheal invasion, we adopted a strategy of surgery followed by chemotherapy, in which tracheal resection was avoided to preserve the integrity of the tracheal rings. This approach achieved therapeutic efficacy while maintaining the patient’s quality of life. The patient will not subsequently suffer from the adverse effects associated with tracheal resection, can return to normal life to the greatest extent, and has a good overall prognosis. This case provides a valuable reference for clinicians in selecting surgical approaches when facing such rare situations.
