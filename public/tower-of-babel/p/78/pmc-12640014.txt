Uric Acid Trajectories and CKD Progression in the African American Study of Kidney Disease and Hypertension
Authors: Yoojin Lee, Michael S. Lipkowitz, Jeffrey B. Kopp, Cheryl A. Winkler, Sung Kweon Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSerum uric acid levels increase with progressive kidney disease, and chronic elevations may contribute to chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression. We analyzed data from the African American Study of Kidney Disease and Hypertension (AASK) to examine the hypothesis that individuals with declining serum urate levels during follow-up would experience slower loss of kidney function.MethodsThe AASK recruited 1094 African-American individuals with reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and followed up with them for up to 12 years. The eGFR and defined end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) composite score were used to establish CKD stages. We employed a Cox proportional hazards model to investigate the association between uric acid trajectories and the composite ESKD outcome.ResultsCompared with the normo-increasing uric acid group (i.e., those who had the lowest overall serum uric acid levels), the high-stable (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.28, P = 0.012), the high-increasing (HR = 1.95, P = 0.042), and extremely high-stable (HR = 3.21, P < 0.001) uric acid groups had higher risk of the composite ESKD outcome. The extremely high-stable group also had a greater ESKD risk than the high-decreasing group (HR = 1.98, P = 0.046). Adjustment for APOL1 risk allele, antihypertensive drug type, and mean arterial pressure treatment target did not alter the relationship between uric acid trajectories and the composite ESKD outcome.ConclusionUric acid trajectories are associated with ESKD incidence in African Americans with CKD. Tracking temporal changes in uric acid improves the assessment of ESKD risk in CKD progression.

Uric acid is the end-product of purine nucleotide metabolism. Humans and other great apes lack the major catabolic enzyme that metabolizes uric acid and therefore have higher plasma uric acid levels than other species.1 Plasma and tissue levels of uric acid reflect the balance between production and excretion. This equilibrium is affected by diet,2 kidney function,3 metabolic syndrome,4 and some medications.5 Uric acid transporters in the renal proximal tubule have roles in reabsorption and excretion.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Hyperuricemia is associated with gout8 and increased risk of hypertension,12 type 2 diabetes,13,14 and cardiovascular diseases.15,16

CKD stage is defined based on eGFR; the 5 stages range from normal kidney function to ESKD defined by eGFR < 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2, dialysis, or transplantation.17 Even with well-controlled blood pressure and therapies to reduce proteinuria, CKD often progresses to ESKD. Hyperuricemia is associated with CKD incidence and progression, though a causal relationship between hyperuricemia and progressive CKD has been proposed but remains unproven.18, 19, 20, 21 Hyperuricemia activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, which in turn tends to promote high blood pressure,22 inflammation,23 and oxidative stress.24 However, uric acid–lowering treatment is not associated with renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system deactivation,25 which indicates that understanding of the mechanism and direction of the association between the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and uric acid levels remains incomplete. Studies involving lowering uric acid levels using medications,26,27 diet,28,29 and lifestyle changes30, 31, 32 alone or combined have generated conflicting results for slowing CKD progression.33, 34, 35, 36

Despite plausible biological links, recent randomized controlled trials involving uric acid–lowering therapies using the xanthine oxidase inhibitor, such as allopurinol, have not demonstrated a benefit in slowing CKD progression.4,27 Notably, these studies mainly enrolled European-American participants and did not account for variation in response to allopurinol, which can be influenced by both biological sex and ethnicity.37 Allopurinol carries a risk of severe cutaneous adverse reactions, particularly in certain ethnic groups, and this risk may affect both study design and generalizability. Furthermore, these trials assessed uric acid at single time points, potentially overlooking the clinical relevance of uric acid trajectories over time. Owing to their design, these randomized controlled studies were limited to account for individual variability in response to allopurinol, instead evaluating treatment effects at discrete intervals rather than as dynamic processes.38 Such limitations may confound the observed association between allopurinol use and uric acid–lowering at individual level. For example, Badve et al.27 reported changes in the mean eGFR levels and geometric mean albumin-to-creatine ratio scores over 104 weeks, which hinted at a potential nonlinear relationship between the serum uric acid level and kidney function decline over time. To address these shortcomings, we applied a latent trajectory model to estimate uric acid trajectory class membership and examined its association with longitudinal changes in eGFR and clinical outcomes among an African-American population. We hypothesized that participants with a declining uric acid trajectory would be less likely to experience CKD progression or ESKD-related composite outcomes, than other trajectory groups.

Methods

Study Design and Population

The AASK recruited 1094 individuals who self-identified as African American, were aged 18 to 70 years, and had reduced eGFR (25–65 ml/min per 1.73 m2) and lacked other identified causes of renal insufficiency. The study was performed between February 1995 and September 2001.39 The primary objective was to determine the long-term course of CKD attributed to hypertension and risk factors for CKD progression. Using a 3 × 2 factorial design, each participant was randomly assigned to 1 of 3 medication groups (an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, a sustained release β-blocker, or a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker) and 1 of 2 blood pressure control targets (mean arterial pressure ≤ 92 mm Hg for the intensive control group and 102–107 mm Hg for the standard control group). After the clinical trial phase was completed, subjects who had not progressed to ESKD were invited to enroll in the cohort follow-up phase, which started in April 2002 and ended in June 2007.39 Systolic and diastolic blood pressure were measured at each visit using validated standardized techniques.

Laboratory Measurements

The Central Biochemistry Laboratory at the Cleveland Clinic (Cleveland, OH) measured serum and urinary chemistry levels at the baseline visit and at follow-up visits every 6 months for 6 years.40 Serum measurements included glucose, creatinine, albumin, and uric acid levels. Fasting lipid profiles included total cholesterol, high-density lipid cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipid cholesterol levels; these components were measured annually. Urine measurements (over a 24-hour period) included urinary protein and creatinine levels. Serum creatinine values were used to generate an eGFR using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration equation, with adjustment for self-reported African ancestry.41 eGFR values were used to define the ESKD composite score and to establish CKD stages.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome variable for the present study was the ESKD composite score. Previous analyses of AASK data found that 22.3% of individuals developed ESKD. ESKD was defined as: (i) a 50% decline from baseline in the eGFR,1 or (ii) eGFR ≤ 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2, or (iii) initiation of kidney replacement therapy (chronic dialysis or renal transplant), or death because of kidney failure.

Statistical Analyses

After data cleaning, we investigated the relationships among 22 factors (Supplementary Table S1) of trajectories of the composite ESKD outcome. We performed semiparametric group-based trajectory modeling to test whether some participants manifested decreased uric acid levels.42 We used the centered norm model in the Traj package in Stata software (StataCorp, College Station, TX) to estimate serum uric acid group levels across sample times. We entered uric acid level as a dependent variable and time from baseline as an independent variable. We compared models while differentiating the number of groups and nonlinear terms. We determined the best model among 5 (3–7) groups × 3 (linear, quadratic, and cubic terms) models, using fit indices, such as the Bayesian information criterion and measure of the entropy, and published findings for uric acid trajectories.43

Using the best-fitting uric acid trajectory model, we investigated the association between serum uric acid levels and composite ESKD outcomes. We first used a chi-square test to evaluate whether group, defined by uric acid changes over the course of the AASK study, was related to composite ESKD incidence. Next, using a Cox proportional hazards model, we estimated differences in ESKD outcome among the groups having different uric acid trajectories during the study period. Model 1 was minimally adjusted for biological sex and age. For model 2, we added the lifestyle-related covariate variables, including alcohol consumption, smoking status, household income, and body mass index. We coded annual household income as previously described44 as follows: 1 for < $5000, 2 for $5000 to $9999, 3 for $10,000 to $19,999, 4 for $20,000 to $29,999, 5 for $30,000 – $39,999, and 6 for ≥ $40,000. We treated data from individuals who declined to report household income as missing data. For model 3, we added baseline eGFR levels and baseline urine protein-to-creatinine ratios. For model 4, we added baseline glucose levels, systolic blood pressure, and triglycerides levels. For skewed covariates, we defined outliers as values outside 2 standard deviations from the mean. During the analyses, the sample size was reduced if any covariate variables in the model were missing. To investigate the association between uric acid group and eGFR levels further, we conducted the mixed effect modeling using age and biological sex as covariates. We also adjusted the null model (model 1) for potential confounders, including APOL1 risk allele status, antihypertensive drug type, and mean arterial pressure treatment target, to assess whether these variables altered the association between uric acid trajectories and the composite ESKD outcome. We did not perform imputations for missing data.

Trajectory Analysis Using Uric Acid Levels

We examined serum uric acid trajectories using uric acid levels across follow-up visits over a 6.5-year period. For the trajectory analysis, we entered the number of visits as an independent variable and uric acid levels as a dependent variable. We evaluated the linear trajectory of uric acid level changes at the first stage using the semiparametric group-based trajectory model (Supplementary Table S2). As we increased the numbers of groups to specify the best model, a unique group was identified. This group showed decreased uric acid levels across visits (termed the high-decreasing group) and included about 14% of the sample population (5- and 6-group models, Supplementary Figure S1).

We chose the 5-group model as the primary model because it was economical and identified the group with individuals who had substantial decreases in uric acid levels (group 4 in the 5-group model), consistent with previous findings.43 We also evaluated quadratic and cubic equations to model uric acid level changes using the 5-group model. The higher-order term models (quadratic and cubic models) had slightly better fits than the linear model for the Bayesian information criterion and entropy methods (Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Figure S2). This result indicated that the higher-order models better identified true group membership.45 Finally, we used the cubic 5-group model to evaluate associations between uric acid trajectories and the ESKD composite outcomes. The 5 groups were labeled based on uric acid level trajectory across the visits: normo-increasing, mean = 6.02, SD = 1.18; high-stable, mean = 7.74, SD = 1.10; high-increasing, mean = 9.22, SD = 1.26; high-decreasing, mean = 8.02, SD = 1.68; and extremely high-stable, mean = 10.41, SD = 2.02 (Figure 2). The normo-increasing group was the only group with normal uric acid levels at enrollment, defined as ≤ 6.0 mg/dl for females and ≤ 7.0 mg/dl for males).46Figure 2Trajectory analyses using uric acid levels.

Incidence of Composite ESKD

We next determined whether the 5 uric acid trajectories were associated with time to the ESKD composite outcome, using a Cox proportional hazards model (n = 576; model 4), while controlling for the potential covariates (Table 2 and Figure 3). Compared with the normo-increasing group, which had the lowest overall uric acid levels, the high-stable group (HR = 2.28, standard error [SE] = 0.75, P = 0.012), the high-increasing group, (HR = 1.95, SE = 0.64, P = 0.042), and the extremely high-stable group (HR = 3.21, SE = 1.05, P < 0.001), all had higher incidence for the ESKD composite outcome. However, the high-decreasing group failed to show a higher ESKD composite outcome incidence, compared with the normo-increasing group (HR = 1.62, SE = 0.69, P = 0.250). These results indicated that in model 4 (i.e., the fully adjusted model), the normo-increasing group had lower ESKD composite outcome incidence than the other groups, except the high-decreasing group. To validate our trajectory analyses results, we conducted a multilevel mixed effects linear regression modeling of uric acid level changes in prediction of the eGFR levels across time. In the model 4 (i.e., fully adjusted model), the high-stable group, the high-increasing group, and the extreme high-stable group showed a marginal decrease in eGFR, compared with the normo-increasing group (Supplementary Table S7). In comparison to the normo-increasing group, the high-decreasing group failed to show a significant difference (β = −2.00, SE = 1.95, P = 0.305). Those findings from this model support our findings about the relationship between the group membership of uric acid level changes and the composite ESKD incidence.

In addition, we adjusted for potential confounding variables in the analysis of the relationship between uric acid trajectory groups and the composite ESKD outcome. APOL1 risk variants are strongly associated with CKD and hypertension in African-American individuals,47 suggesting that APOL1-mediated kidney injury may contribute to secondary hypertension. Although elevated uric acid is recognized as a risk factor for kidney dysfunction, the direct relationship between APOL1 risk variants and uric acid levels remains unclear. To account for the potential confounding effect of APOL1 in the association between uric acid trajectory groups and the composite ESKD outcome, we performed a Cox proportional hazards analysis (n = 584; model 1), adjusting for age, biological sex, and APOL1-additive risk variants. Results indicated that individuals with 2 APOL1 risk alleles had a significantly increased risk of ESKD (HR = 1.60, SE = 0.32, P = 0.020), whereas those with 1 risk allele did not (HR = 1.07, SE = 0.20, P = 0.727), highlighting that only the high-risk genotype is associated with ESKD in this population. After adjusting for APOL1 risk, the extremely high-stable uric acid trajectory group remained at elevated risk for ESKD compared with the high-decreasing group (HR = 1.87, SE = 0.60, P = 0.049). The high-stable and high-increasing groups showed no significant difference in ESKD risk compared with the high-decreasing group (HR = 1.17, SE = 0.36, P = 0.612; and HR = 1.19, SE = 0.36, P = 0.572, respectively). The normo-increasing group exhibited a nonsignificant trend toward lower ESKD incidence relative to the high-decreasing group (HR = 0.49, SE = 0.19, P = 0.067).

In this cohort, participants were treated with 1 of 3 antihypertensive agents: an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (enalapril), a calcium channel blocker (amlodipine), or a beta blocker (atenolol) to achieve a target mean arterial blood pressure of either 102–107 mm Hg or ≤ 92 mm Hg.44 Given that uric acid may contribute to the development and progression of hypertension through mechanisms involving oxidative stress and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system,48 we conducted a Cox proportional hazards analysis (n = 713; model 1) to examine the potential impact of antihypertensive treatment on the association between uric acid trajectories and the composite ESKD outcome, adjusting for age, biological sex, and type of antihypertensive agent. Compared with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, neither beta blocker nor calcium channel blocker use predicted the composite ESKD outcome (HR = 1.11, SE = 0.26, P = 0.653; and HR = 0.83, SE = 0.24, P = .531, respectively). This indicates that antihypertensive drug types are not associated with the composite ESKD outcome. After adjusting for antihypertensive drug type, the extremely high-stable uric acid trajectory group remained at significantly higher risk for ESKD compared with the high-decreasing group (HR = 3.34, SE = 1.51, P = 0.005). The high-stable and high-increasing groups did not differ significantly in ESKD risk relative to the high-decreasing group (HR = 1.80, SE = 0.80, P = 0.190; and HR = 1.96, SE = 0.87, P = 0.129, respectively). The normo-increasing group also showed no difference in the ESKD incidence compared with the high-decreasing group (HR = 0.74, SE = 0.41, P = 0.588).

Lastly, we investigated whether the treatment goal—either maintaining mean arterial pressure within the usual range (102–107 mm Hg) or lowering it to ≤ 92 mm Hg—affected the relationship between uric acid trajectories and the composite ESKD outcome. Using a Cox proportional hazards analysis (n = 713; model 1) , adjusting for age, biological sex, and treatment target, we found that lowering mean arterial pressure (≤ 92 mm Hg) did not significantly differ from maintaining the usual range (102–107 mm Hg) in predicting the composite ESKD outcome (HR = 1.05, SE = 0.22, P = 0.806), suggesting that treatment goal is not related to the composite ESKD outcome. After adjusting for treatment goal, the extremely high-stable uric acid trajectory group remained at significantly higher risk for ESKD than the high-decreasing group (HR = 3.51, SE = 1.55, P = 0.004). The high-stable and high-increasing groups did not differ significantly in ESKD risk relative to the high-decreasing group (HR = 1.80, SE = 0.80, P = 0.185; and HR = 2.00, SE = 0.89, P = 0.120, respectively). The normo-increasing group also showed no significant difference in ESKD incidence compared with the high-decreasing group (HR = 0.72, SE = 0.39, P = 0.546). In summary, the high-risk APOL1 allele was associated with the composite ESKD outcome compared with the low-risk allele, whereas neither antihypertensive drug type nor mean arterial pressure treatment target showed such an association. Adjustment for these potential confounders did not alter the association between uric acid trajectories and the composite ESKD outcome.

Discussion

We hypothesized that participants with decreasing uric acid levels over time would be less likely to develop the composite ESKD outcome. We identified a group of individuals with marked decreases in uric acid levels during the study. Overall, individuals in the high-decreasing uric acid group were less likely to progress to the composite ESKD outcome, compared with the extremely high-stable uric acid group, even after adjusting for APOL1 risk variants and antihypertensive medications. We classified the subjects according to changes in uric acid levels over a 6.5-year period and identified 5 distinct trajectory patterns in uric acid levels over time. A previous general population study that investigated associations between the 5 uric acid level trajectories and hypertension incidence in a Chinese population of 7062 patients found similar trajectories.43 Thus, similar trajectory profiles were found using 2 distinct ethnic populations. This result supports the validity of this approach. These findings suggest that uric acid trajectory may serve as a potential biomarker of renal impairment. However, it is important to note that it is not possible to definitively separate uric acid as cause of CKD progression from uric acid as a consequence of CKD progression.

The conclusions of other studies differ from the present study. These studies mainly recruited samples from subjects with type 1 diabetes (PERL study)4,27 or advanced CKD (CKD-FIX study).33,49 The present study involved an African-American population with hypertension but not diabetes. Thus, population differences and disease differences might have contributed to the differences in results. Because hyperuricemia is associated with renovascular hypertension,36,50, 51, 52 future research might examine this association as a risk for ESKD across different ethnic populations.

The finding of the nonlinear relationship between uric acid and ESKD progression differed from previous findings. One explanation for this difference is that the use of multiple serum uric acid levels as measurements in the analysis contributed to the complexity. A Mendelian randomization study examined the effect of serum uric acid on CKD incidence in 4 cohorts in a large medical record dataset from the UK Biobank53; the study findings did not support a causal relationship. Genetic risk scores using the 26 previously identified serum urate−related single nucleotide variants, and rs12498742, a gene related to the GLUT9 transporter, were associated with uric acid level but were not related to eGFR and CKD incidence. Such results are consistent with the results of 2 randomized controlled drug studies.4,27 However, it is important to note that these studies measure serum urate—the concentration of uric acid in the blood—rather than uric acid itself. A meta-analysis study also did not find a predictive role for serum uric acid in progression to ESKD.54 These studies used baseline uric acid level as a predictor of ESKD.18,34,35,55, 56, 57, 58, 59 However, the baseline serum uric acid measurement may be insufficient to fully capture the effect of hyperuricemia, particularly when in renal function and serum uric acid levels vary over time. Moreover, a recent study found that older patients with gout who received urate-lowering therapy during the CARES trial experienced slower CKD progression,60 suggesting that the relationship between changes in uric acid levels and CKD may be more nuanced in gout patients. Further studies are needed to validate these findings.

We accounted for the temporal issue of the 1-time uric acid measurement in individuals by including multiple serum uric acid measurements over time. Some studies have used uric acid level trajectory to estimate uric acid exposure over time. In these studies, use of this approach demonstrated that a high uric acid level trajectory is positively associated with diabetes,61 nonalcoholic fatty liver disease,62 CKD progression, and all-cause mortality.42 These findings suggest that uric acid trajectory may be a robust approach to analyze the effects of uric acid over time, including prediction of ESKD progression.

The strengths of the present study include the long total follow-up duration (12 years) in the AASK study and examination of diverse covariate variables linked with both uric acid levels and ESKD. Adjustment for these variables would tend to minimize the confounding effects of the variables on the relationship between uric acid levels and ESKD. Because this study was performed as part of a clinical trial instead of in the context of natural history study, there were more robust data available.

This study had important limitations. Because it was an observational study and relied in part on participants’ retrospective reports, conclusions about causality are not possible. The study population was limited to middle-aged African Americans without diabetes, and caution should be used in extrapolating the results to other populations. A longitudinal study design is warranted for future research. Although some participants manifested a sustained uric acid decrease and experienced fewer composite renal outcomes, we have limited understanding about the role of uric acid in progression to ESKD. Lastly, the finding of the present study was marginally significant (α = 0.05) and would become less so after multiple comparison corrections. Future studies are needed to validate these findings using longitudinal study designs with larger sample sizes.

In conclusion, uric acid levels decreased over time in a subset of the AASK study population. Those subjects were less likely to develop the composite ESKD outcome than those with persistently elevated uric acid levels. Further study is needed to determine whether lowering serum uric acid levels slows progression of nondiabetic CKD among African Americans and others.
