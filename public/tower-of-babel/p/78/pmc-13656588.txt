Circulation of arctic canine distemper virus in diarrheic dogs in Iran: molecular detection and phylogenetic characterization
Authors: Hamid Staji, Sara Kashi, Iradj Ashrafi Tamai, Seyedeh Fatemeh Angoshtan, Atrin Behmanesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCanine distemper virus (CDV) is a pathogen that causes systemic infection in dogs and wildlife. Among the highly variable regions in the genome of the virus, the H gene is commonly sequenced for phylogenetic studies. The aim of the present study was to screen for the presence of CDV infection in dogs in Iran and to identify the genotype of the virus. Seventy-five dogs with gastrointestinal symptoms were screened via an immunochromatographic test. Fifty of them were positive. CDV was isolated from rectal swabs, and PCR was performed. The top 10 positive cases were subsequently selected for sequencing. Phylogenetic analyses were subsequently conducted. Among the 75 dogs analyzed, 50 (66.7%) dogs were found to be infected with CDV via rapid tests. All 10 sequenced isolates were from the Arctic lineage, forming a monophyletic cluster. Our sequences closely clustered with some European strains. Some nucleotide variations were observed. The present study reports the presence of Arctic-like CDV strains in diarrheic dogs in Tehran. Ongoing molecular surveillance will help in assessing any alterations in genetic diversity and vaccine efficacy. Introduction

Canine Morbillivirus, although known as canine distemper virus (CDV), is a highly infectious virus. CDV causes major health concerns not only for dogs but also for various types of wildlife [1, 2]. CDV belongs to the genus Morbillivirus of the family Paramyxoviridae, subfamily Paramyxovirinae [3, 4]. It has a worldwide distribution, and its incidence is increasing among different animals [1, 5–8].

Clinical symptoms often involve the respiratory, digestive, and nervous systems simultaneously [9]. The vast majority of commercial vaccines available on the market include modified live virus vaccines based on the America 1 strain (such as Onderstepoort, Lederle, Snyder Hill, Convac, and CDV3) [10]. On the other hand, such live virus vaccines present several safety problems regarding various endangered animals, necessitating the use of recombinant vaccines, which offer protection without any species-specific risks. However, recombinant vaccines need revaccinations [11, 12].

The virus has a single-stranded, nonsegmented, negative-sense RNA genome. The nucleotides encode eight proteins, including six structural and two nonstructural proteins [13, 14]. The genomic structure of the virus leads to mutation at a very high rate, leading to high genetic variation in the virus. Hemagglutinin (H), a surface glycoprotein of CDV, is the most variable component within the Morbillivirus genome [1].

Although many studies have been conducted in Iran regarding the presence of CDV, very little is known about the molecular aspects and genetic variability of the circulating strains, especially among dogs with gastroenteritis, in recent years. The continuous evaluation of CDV genetic diversity is crucial for its evolution, spread, and protection. The main objective of the current research was to detect CDV in the feces of diarrheic dogs and characterize the circulating strains by sequencing and phylogenetic analysis. The present study is one of the few recent phylogenetic investigations of CDV in Iran, focusing on fecal samples from dogs with gastroenteritis in recent years.

Methods

This molecular epidemiology study was conducted among dogs in Tehran, Iran, from January to July 2025. The study was performed according to the standard guidelines of Semnan University regarding animal welfare. In addition, no invasive sampling methods were used, nor were any experiments conducted on the dogs. Therefore, according to the guidelines of Semnan University, no ethical approval code was required for this study.

Initially, a population of 75 dogs with gastrointestinal symptoms was selected from 15 veterinary clinics across various districts of Tehran. The study population comprised dogs of either sex between 3 months and years old (74.66% were less than 2 years old). All dogs had diarrhea as their primary symptom while vomiting, lethargy, and anorexia were present in 40% of cases and mild fever in 15% of cases. No dogs had any prior vaccinations.

Within this population, 50 dogs that were positive for being infected with canine distemper virus (CDV) via a commercially available rapid immunochromatographic assay (Labtelligent Diagnostics, kit number SG/KTA/302/030001) were recruited. The viral RNA was isolated via the SinaPure RNA Extraction Kit (Sinaclon, Iran; product no. EX6031) following the instructions provided by the manufacturer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was then synthesized via the Yekta Tajhiz cDNA Synthesis Kit (Iran).

Primers targeting the CDV hemagglutinin (H) gene, including CDVHF (5′-TGGTTCACAAGATGGTATTC-3′) and CDVHR (5′-CAACACCACTAAATTGGACT-3′), were used [15]. Polymerase chain reaction was performed via a reaction mixture of 25 µL, including 12.5 µL of Ampliqon Master Mix, 1 µL of each primer, 7.5 µL of DNase-free water, and 3 µL of cDNA template. The thermal cycle program was as follows: denaturation for 5 min at 94 °C; 40 cycles of 30 s at 94 °C, 30 s at 56 °C, and 30 s at 72 °C; and an extension period of 5 min at 72 °C. Distilled water served as the negative control, while the positive control used was a previously known CDV-positive sample provided by the collection of the Tehran Faculty of Veterinary Medicine. The amplification products obtained from the PCR were analyzed by electrophoretic separation through a 1.5% agarose gel. The expected amplicon size for the CDV H gene was 613 bp.

From the positive PCR products, ten samples with the highest band intensity were selected from 10 different clinics located in various regions of Tehran, distributed across the sampling time frame. The products were sent to Genetic Codon Company (Iran) for Sanger sequencing. Multiple alignment of the sequences was performed to recognize conserved and different nucleotides via ClustalW implemented in MEGA11. Reference sequences were selected from GenBank to represent all major CDV lineages and included geographically diverse strains. Importantly, vaccine strains belonging to the America-1 lineage were included in the analysis. Phylogenetic tree construction was carried out via the neighbor‒joining approach with 1,000 bootstraps via MEGA11 [16]. To evaluate whether nucleotide changes resulted in amino acid substitutions, a codon-based Z-test of selection was performed using the Nei-Gojobori method in MEGA11.

Results

Fifty out of 75 (66.7%) suspected samples were positive. The results of gel electrophoresis revealed specific amplification of the anticipated 613-bp H gene product in positive dogs. Owing to their specificity and intensity, ten of the fifty amplicons were chosen for sequencing and phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 1).

The figure shows electrophoretic analysis of RT‒PCR products targeting the hemagglutinin (H) gene of canine distemper virus (CDV). Positive samples produced the expected 613-bp amplicon. The positive samples with strong and specific bands were subjected to sequence analysis and phylogenetic construction

The partial H genes of the ten selected CDV isolates were successfully sequenced via the Sanger sequencing method and deposited into the GenBank database under accession numbers, as indicated in Table 1. A neighbor-joining (NJ) tree was constructed with the MEGA11 program. The evolutionary distances were calculated via the Tamura‒Nei method. Tree reliability was calculated via bootstrap testing, which involved 1,000 replicates (Fig. 2). The overall mean pairwise distance among the Iranian sequences was 0.01 (99% nucleotide identity). The complete pairwise identity matrix with all numerical values has been included in Supplementary File 1. The Z-test results, including pairwise dN/dS values and significance levels, have been included in Supplementary File 2. The amino acid sequence data are presented in Fig. 3.

The phylogenetic tree revealed that all ten examined isolates from Iran were classified into the Arctic lineage, thus forming a monophyletic cluster. The clustering of our isolates into one cluster implies genetic relatedness between the strains sequenced, but considering the small number of sequenced strains and the single-city sampling, the result cannot be generalized to all circulating strains in Iran. Moreover, the Iranian isolates grouped with some Arctic strains found in Europe, especially the Hungarian strains (DQ889183 and DQ889186) and Italian strains (DQ226088.1, DQ226087, KF914669). Although the sequences of the isolates were rather homogeneous, some nucleotide differences were detected, indicating the possible evolution of viruses in the region. Arctic strains continue to circulate in the region, adjusting to local conditions. More extensive studies with larger sample sizes and broader geographic coverage should be carried out to evaluate the dominant strain in Iran.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the molecular properties and phylogenetics of CDV strains isolated from diarrheic dogs in Tehran, Iran. RT‒PCR and phylogenetic analysis were subsequently performed. The diagnosis of CDV requires the presence of clinical and hematological findings along with serologic or molecular evidence of the disease. Advanced diagnostic tools such as reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT‒PCR), nested RT‒PCR, real-time polymerase chain reaction (real-time PCR), insulated isothermal PCR (iiPCR), recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), reverse transcription loop-mediated isothermal amplification (RT‒LAMP), recombinase-aided amplification (recombinase‒Cas13a) lateral flow detection, enzyme‒linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), indirect immunofluorescent antibody assay (IFA), virus neutralization (VN), and rapid immunochromatographic (IC) antigen test kits aid in diagnosing CDV [17–21]. Nevertheless, antibody testing can be problematic because of the presence of antibodies due to vaccination or previous infection with CDV. In this case, antigen testing or DNA-based testing is recommended. The rapid IC kit and RT‒PCR tests are the most applicable [17].

The hemagglutinin (H) protein is an important constituent of the spikes of the virion envelope [22]. The H gene shows the greatest genetic variation. Currently, CDV is classified into several phylogenetic lineages, namely, America 1 to America 5, Canada 1 and 2, Asia 1 to Asia 6, Europe Wildlife, Arctic-like, Africa 1 and 2, Southern Africa, Europe 1/South America 1, South America 2, South America 3, and Rockborn-like [23]. The number of CDV lineages varies depending on the genetic region used for classification purposes. Studies that utilized the complete coding sequence of the H gene detected 18 CDV lineages. However, other researchers who used partial H gene sequences reported that there were up to 22 lineages of CDV [7, 24]. Genetic modifications allow the virus to explore a new antigenic landscape while adjusting to different receptors of diverse carnivores. Consequently, understanding the dynamics of evolution is crucial for tracing its spread and possible cross-transmission among species [23].

Canine distemper virus likely originated from mutations in human measles virus following the European colonization period in the New World during the fifteenth century. The first cases of canine distemper disease occurred in South America. The virus then spread to Europe, with cases first recorded in Spain (1760), followed by Great Britain and Italy (1764) and Russia (1770) [25]. Among the different CDV lineages, the Europe 3/Arctic lineage is one of the most common in Europe. While it is associated mainly with Europe, it has been found in dogs in the United States (2003, 2012), foxes in China (2004), and Amur tigers in Russia (2001–2010). Its geographical range is expanding [26]. Considering the worldwide spread of the Arctic lineage, its circulation in Iran warrants special attention. Several studies have revealed evidence of the Arctic lineage in the dog population of Iran. Phylogenetic analyses conducted via the nucleoprotein (NP) gene by Namroodi et al. revealed that among domestic dogs, three isolates from the northeastern region were part of the Arctic lineage, whereas one isolate from the central region fell into the category of the European lineage [26]. In another study, the findings suggested that there were two different lineages circulating in Iran: a European lineage isolate (JN941238) and an Arctic-like isolate (JN941239). The European lineage was similar to a Turkish isolate, whereas the Arctic-like lineage was similar to Italian Arctic strains [27]. The isolates from this study, including DQ226088.1, DQ226087, and KF914669, presented close phylogenetic relationships with Arctic lineage strains previously reported from Italy. In more recent studies, Mojtahedzadeh et al. reported that all CDV strains obtained from dogs suffering from either neurologic or nonneurologic variants of CDV infection in Iran belong to the Arctic-like lineage, which also closely clusters with strains from Russia [28]. In 2025, Khosravi et al. investigated CDV isolates obtained from vaccinated dogs in Iran and determined that all were Arctic-like strains, unlike the American-1 vaccine strains. On the basis of these results, the differences in the antigenicity of the Arctic-like strains and America-1 vaccine strains might lead to vaccine breakthroughs. Thus, the development of new vaccine strains that match the Arctic-like lineage in Iran is warranted [29]. Furthermore, the discovery of an Arctic-like CDV lineage in domestic dogs was also made in Turkey. The strains were closely related to those from Italy [30]. This lineage has been observed among Apennine wolves in Italy [31]. It is evident that Arctic lineages also coexist in several countries outside Europe [22]. This lineage has been detected in America, Australia, and Asia [22, 30, 32, 33]. In this study, some of the European Arctic strains used as references, such as the Hungarian strains (DQ889183 and DQ889186) and Italian strains (DQ226088.1, DQ226087, and KF914669), appeared to be closely related phylogenetically to our Iranian strains. Such close clustering could be indicative of the continuity of Arctic CDVs between Iran and European countries, although this interpretation is limited by the partial H-gene sequences and the small sample size. The observed relatedness could also reflect shared ancestral strains rather than recent direct transmission events.

Vaccines from the America 1 genotype have been extensively employed in Iran [29]. The results from this study, together with previously published literature, indicate that there is an established genotypic difference between Arctic lineage isolates and vaccine strains [4, 32]. Nonetheless, the significance of this genetic variation concerning the effectiveness of vaccination needs further examination. There is also the need for further molecular surveillance to see if there is a need to update the vaccination program.

Limitations

The current investigation has several limitations. First, a small sample size of infected dogs with diarrhea from only one city in Iran was used for the study. Thus, the strains found during this work could be partially representative of the genetic variability of circulating CDV in other regions of Iran or in dogs with other symptoms of disease. Second, the analysis of viral isolates was performed using a partial sequence of the H gene of CDV; therefore, more detailed studies of viral evolution and mutations can provide more details. Moreover, the ten samples selected for sequencing were chosen based on PCR band intensity, sampling period, and geographic distribution, rather than clinical features. Nonetheless, the study confirms the ongoing presence of Arctic strains in isolated samples in Iran. High nucleotide homology between isolates from Iran and Europe may indicate the spread of the virus geographically, although this conclusion is limited by the partial H-gene sequences and small sample size. This work demonstrates the necessity of regular molecular surveillance and assessment of the protective efficiency of modern America-1 vaccines against Arctic strains.
