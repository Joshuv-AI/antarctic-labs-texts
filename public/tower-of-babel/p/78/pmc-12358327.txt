Exploring Facial Nucleus-Centered Connectivity in Hemifacial Spasm: Novel Insights into Pathogenesis and Surgical Impact
Authors: Yu Duan, Kuan Lv, Chao Zhao, Liangbo Han, Jianke Wang, Chuanpeng Zhang, Ziyi Zhang, Hanlin Liu, Ke Yang, Zhen Yuan, Li Zhu, Yuli Wang, Jixin Luan, Guolin Ma, Jiang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hemifacial spasm (HFS) is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by involuntary muscle contractions of the face, significantly impacting patients’ quality of life. Although the facial nerve nucleus has been implicated in HFS pathogenesis, specific research on its functional connectivity within whole-brain networks remains limited. This study aimed to investigate alterations in whole-brain functional connectivity with the facial nerve nucleus as the region of interest (ROI) in HFS patients, before and after microvascular decompression (MVD), to uncover potential mechanisms underlying the disorder and the impact of surgical intervention. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) was conducted on 30 HFS patients and 30 matched healthy controls. Functional connectivity (FC) was analyzed using the facial nerve nucleus as the seed ROI. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected, including spasm severity, anxiety and depression scores, and preoperative biomarkers. Statistical analyses assessed differences in FC and its correlation with clinical parameters. HFS patients demonstrated significantly increased FC between the left facial nucleus and the right parahippocampal gyrus, as well as between the right facial nucleus and the right fusiform gyrus, compared to healthy controls. These patterns persisted postoperatively, with additional increased FC observed between the right facial nucleus and bilateral superior temporal gyri. Correlation analyses revealed that left facial nucleus-right parahippocampal gyrus FC was positively associated with spasm severity and fibrinogen levels, while right facial nucleus-right fusiform gyrus FC was negatively correlated with monoamine oxidase (MAO) levels. ReHo of both facial nucleus showed significant differences between preoperative HFS patients and healthy controls, whereas ALFF/fALFF and lateralisation of facial nucleus did not show significant between-group differences. This study highlights the role of altered FC between the facial nucleus and brain regions involved in memory, emotion, and visual processing in HFS pathogenesis. While MVD provides symptomatic relief, its short-term effects on FC appear limited, suggesting that functional connectivity changes are chronic and may serve as biomarkers for disease monitoring. These findings provide novel insights into the neural mechanisms of HFS and emphasize the need for further research on long-term brain network adaptations post-surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10548-025-01133-y.

Introduction

Hemifacial spasm (HFS) is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by involuntary facial muscle contractions, typically starting with the orbicularis oculi muscle and gradually spreading to other facial muscles (Aktan and Depierreux 2024). The prevalence of HFS is 12–14 per 100,000 people, predominantly affecting middle-aged and elderly individuals, with a slightly higher incidence in women than men (Chaudhry et al. 2015). However, there is a trend towards younger onset (Tan and Chan 2006). HFS typically follows a chronic course and rarely resolves spontaneously. While not life-threatening, the recurrent spasms significantly impact patients’ quality of life and can lead to psychological issues such as anxiety and depression (E.-K. Tan et al. 2005; Wang et al. 2024a, b). Recent advancements in neurophysiology and neuroimaging have highlighted the key role of the facial nerve nucleus in the pathogenesis of HFS(Szmyd et al. 2022; Terasaka et al. 2016).

Studies utilizing resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) have provided insights into the brain’s functional connectivity in HFS patients (Tu et al. 2015). Abnormal activity within the facial nerve nucleus is thought to cause disrupted facial motor signal transmission, leading to involuntary muscle contractions (Zhimin 2021). In addition, research has shown altered functional connectivity between brain regions involved in facial movement and emotional regulation, such as the amygdala, thalamus, and striatum (Gao et al. 2021; Niu et al. 2020). While these studies have provided important clues about the role of central mechanisms in HFS, they primarily focus on regions associated with facial movement and emotional circuits(Han-feng*, 2024; Jung et al. 2023). Despite evidence suggesting that the facial nerve nucleus plays a crucial role in initiating abnormal discharges that lead to muscle spasms(Wilkinson et al. 2017), specific research targeting the functional connectivity of the facial nerve nucleus remains scarce. Understanding its exact involvement could be key to uncovering the underlying mechanisms of HFS and improving therapeutic strategies.

Thus, this article aims to address these gaps by investigating whether changes in whole-brain functional connectivity with the facial nerve nucleus as the region of interest (ROI) are involved in the pathogenesis of HFS. We hypothesize that altered functional connectivity between the facial nerve nucleus and brain regions involved in sensory-emotional integration underlies the pathophysiology of HFS.This research aims to deepen the understanding of the disease’s pathogenesis, particularly its basic of neuroimaging, and to provide imaging-based evidence for early diagnosis and personalized treatment of HFS.

Methods

Participants

This study enrolled 30 patients with HFS who underwent microvascular decompression (MVD) at the Department of Neurosurgery of China-Japan Friendship Hospital and met the following criteria: (1) primary unilateral HFS, (2) no history of neurological diseases, and (3) right-handed. To ensure appropriate comparisons, 30 healthy controls were also recruited who were matched for gender and age and had no previous history of neuropsychiatric disorders. Any participant with contraindications for MRI or with artifacts in the acquired images were excluded. Demographic data were collected for all subjects, including age, gender and education level. For HFS patients, clinical data encompassed disease severity (assessed using the Cohen Spasm Scale), time of symptom onset, previous treatments, anxiety and depression levels, imaging findings, surgical duration, intraoperative bleeding, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, and postoperative outcomes. The severity of the condition was defined by Cohen spasm scale (Cohen et al. 1986), which classify symptoms from 0 to 4: Level 0: No spasms; Level 1: Increased blinking or mild tremors of facial muscles triggered by external stimuli; Level 2: Spontaneous mild tremors of eyelids and facial muscles without functional impairment; Level 3: Noticeable spasms with slight functional impairment; Level 4: Severe spasms and significant functional impairment, such as the inability to keep the eyes open long enough to read or difficulties walking independently. Before MRI scanning, all HFS patients completed the Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS), Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), and Self-Rating Scale of Sleep (SRSS) to evaluate their affective states. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the China-Japan Friendship Hospital (approval No.2022-KY-181), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Image Data Acquisition

All MR images were acquired using a 3.0T MRI scanner (Discovery MR750, General Electric Medical Systems, USA) equipped with a standard 8-channel head coil. Resting-state fMRI data were collected using a single-shot gradient-recalled echo-planar imaging (EPI) pulse sequence, with 34 axial slices, slice thickness/gap of 3.5/0 mm, a matrix size of 64 × 64, TR of 2000 ms, TE of 30 ms, flip angle of 90°, and a field of view (FOV) of 224 × 224 mm. A total of 240 time points were acquired over 8 min. Additionally, a three-dimensional fast spoiled gradient-recalled (3D FSPGR) sequence was used to acquire T1-weighted anatomical images with the following parameters: TR = 6.7 ms, TE = 2.9 ms, FOV = 256 mm × 256 mm, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, flip angle = 12°, bandwidth = 31.25 Hz/pixel, and a scan time of 4 min 10 s.

Laboratory Parameters

A comprehensive panel of preoperative laboratory indices, commonly assessed in HFS patients, was collected (Chen et al. 2019). These included a complete blood count (CBC), which measured peripheral white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte (LYMPH) count, and monocyte (MONO) count. Liver and kidney function tests assessed biomarkers such as monoamine oxidase (MAO), leucine aminopeptidase (LAP), and adenosine deaminase (ADA). A coagulation profile included prothrombin time (PT), prothrombin time activity (PTA), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), fibrinogen levels, and thrombin time (TT). Finally, electrolyte analysis measured serum concentrations of potassium, sodium, chloride, inorganic phosphate, and calcium.

Surgical Approach

All patients underwent MVD and had a follow-up MRI one week postoperatively. All surgeries were performed by chief surgeons with significant experience in microvascular decompression. The brief process was as follows (Kaufmann 2024): After the patient was intubated and administered intravenous anesthesia, they were positioned laterally with the affected mastoid elevated to the highest point. A postauricular incision was made parallel to the hairline. A grinding drill was used to create a bone window, with the anterior edge reaching the sigmoid sinus. After sealing the mastoid air cells with bone wax, the dura was opened, and cerebrospinal fluid was released by opening the subarachnoid space. The root exit zone of facial nerve was carefully examined, and after isolating the responsible blood vessel, a Teflon pad was placed to separate it from the brainstem. The area was irrigated with warm saline, the dura was closed, the bone defect was repaired, and the incision was closed layer by layer.

Data Processing

Preprocessing was conducted using SPM12 and the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF). To standardize the affected side among patients, the T1-weighted and fMRI data from the 11 cases with left-sided hemifacial spasm (HFS) were flipped from left to right (Tu et al. 2016). The preprocessing steps included: (1) Conversion of DICOM images into NIFTI format. (2) The first 10 time points for each file were removed. (3) The timing correction and realignment were carried out, the T1WI to the mean functional image was co-registered, and the DARTEL tool to compute transformations from individual native space to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space was used. The subjects with head motion exceeding 2 mm or 2◦ were excluded. (4) The Friston 24-parameter model was used to regress out head motion effects. (5) The spatial smoothing using a 4-mm Gaussian kernel was performed and the low-frequency drift and the band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) was used to remove low-frequency drift and high-frequency noise. In participants’ rs-fMRI analysis, we used the left and right facial nucleus as regions of interest (ROI). According to the previous study, the MNI spatial coordinates of facial nucleus are defined as (−6, −34, −38) and (6, −34, −38), and the radius of each ROI was set to 5 mm (Sato et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2021). Subsequently, ALFF/fALFF, ReHo, and functional connectivity were calculated, which were extracted to explore the relationship between the severity of HFS and altered connectivity.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics) and SPM (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). Demographic and radiographic data were compared between patient groups (both preoperative and postoperative) and healthy controls using the Student’s t-test, while gender differences were analyzed using the chi-square test. Differences within groups were compared by paired t-tests. A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. P-values were adjusted using the Holm-Bonferroni method to control for multiple comparisons, with a significance threshold set at α = 0.05.

For functional connectivity (FC) analysis of regions of interest (ROIs), the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to control for false positives in multiple comparisons, with a statistical threshold set at P < 0.05. This analysis was performed using xjview (https://www.alivelearn.net/xjview/), with a cluster size threshold of 10 voxels.

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (https://www.graphpad.com/) to assess the relationship between FC and spasm severity. Age, sex, education level, and disease duration were included as covariates in the regression analysis. Additionally, the associations between preoperative laboratory indices and FC were assessed using Pearson correlation with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction to control for family-wise error. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

The demographic and clinical characteristics of all participants are presented in Table 1. There were no significant differences in age, sex, or years of education between the HFS patients and healthy controls (p > 0.05). For the HFS patient group, the average disease duration was 4.56 ± 4.36 years. The mean Cohen spasm score was 2.37 ± 0.61, with 21 patients (70%) classified at Level 2, 7 patients (23.3%) at Level 3, and 2 patients (6.67%) at Level 4. The mean duration of surgery was 1.30 ± 0.31 h, with an average blood loss of 18.83 ± 4.49 ml. The mean ASA score for the HFS group was 1.83 ± 0.38.

Between- and within-group Differences in ReHo and ALFF/fALFF

There are significant differences in ReHo across each ROI between preoperative patients and healthy controls, as shown in Fig. 1. After correction for multiple comparisons, for Reho values in the region of the left facial nucleus, the q-value between the preoperative and healthy control groups was 0.02, and for Reho values in the right facial nucleus, the q-value comparing the two groups was 0.002. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in ALFF/fALFF values between the two groups. Similarly, when comparing postoperative results to healthy controls, no statistically significant changes were found, as well in the preoperative and postoperative comparisons. We also did not find the difference between left and right facial nucleus.

Between- and within-group Differences in Bilateral facial-nucleus-seeded FC

The inter-group differences in bilateral facial nucleus-seeded functional connectivity (FC) are presented in Fig. 2; Table 2. Compared with control subjects, patients with HFS exhibited increased inter-network FC between the left facial nucleus and right parahippocampal gyrus, as well as between the right facial nucleus and right fusiform gyrus. Post-surgery, the results showed slight variations: in addition to the increase between right facial nucleus and right fusiform gyrus, HFS patients also demonstrated increased inter-network FC between the right facial nucleus and right parahippocampal gyrus, along with the bilateral superior temporal gyrus. All differences remained statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons using FDR. No significant differences were observed between the preoperative and postoperative groups and between the right and left sides within each group (P > 0.05, FDR-corrected).

Correlation between Functional Connectivity and Clinical Characteristics

We investigated the relationship between the strength of facial nucleus-seeded FC, which demonstrated significant between-group differences, and spasm severity ratings, neuropsychological test scores as well as clinical parameters in patients with HFS. The FC between the left facial nucleus and the right parahippocampal gyrus showed a positive correlation with Cohen spasm scores (r = 0.481, P = 0.006) and fibrinogen levels (r = 0.370, P = 0.024). Conversely, a negative correlation was observed between the FC of the right facial nucleus and the right fusiform gyrus with MAO levels (r = −0.382, P = 0.037), which showed in Fig. 3. However, no significant associations were identified between this FC and other clinical characteristics. Additionally, no significant correlations were detected between other abnormal FCs and clinical characteristics (Fig. 4). After correction for multiple comparisons, none of these results remained statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study investigated FC and ReHo differences in patients with HFS compared to healthy controls, along with pre- and post-operative changes. While demographic factors such as age, sex, and years of education did not differ significantly between groups, the observed increased ReHo across various ROIs in HFS patients versus controls suggests altered local functional synchrony in regions associated with the facial nucleus. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in ALFF or fALFF values between the two groups, indicating that these metrics may be less sensitive to the functional changes associated with HFS. Furthermore, no significant differences were identified between the left and right facial nucleus in any of the metrics examined. This lack of lateralized findings indicates that both facial nucleus may exhibit similar functional characteristics, regardless of the side affected by the spasm.

Our findings also reveal distinctive inter-network FC patterns in HFS patients. Specifically, increased FC between the left facial nucleus and the right parahippocampal gyrus, as well as between the right facial nucleus and the right fusiform gyrus, differentiates HFS patients from controls. These findings align with previous studies suggesting abnormal connectivity in brain regions associated with sensory, motor, and emotional regulation in HFS patients. For example, Niu (Niu et al. 2020) found enhanced FC between the amygdala and prefrontal regions, implicating the role of emotional dysregulation in HFS, while Gao (Gao et al. 2021) highlighted abnormal FC within motor-related regions, supporting the hypothesis of impaired motor signal transmission in HFS. This altered connectivity, which persisted post-operatively, reflects the relative stability of these functional network adaptations in the short term despite symptomatic interventions. Interestingly, after surgery, while some variations in FC patterns were observed, such as increased FC between the right facial nucleus and the right parahippocampal gyrus, as well as bilateral superior temporal gyri, no significant differences emerged between preoperative and postoperative groups. Li (Li et al. 2024) revealed that HFS is associated with specific surface-based morphological changes in the contralateral precentral gyrus, left orbitofrontal cortex, left orbitofrontal cortex, right insula and superior temporal cortex. Tu (Tu et al. 2015) and Wei (Ye 2015) reported decreased ReHo in areas such as the medial frontal gyrus, cingulate gyrus, and superior temporal gyrus, alongside increased ReHo in motor-related regions such as the precentral gyrus and brainstem. The increased FC observed between facial nuclei and limbic structures such as the parahippocampal and fusiform gyri further implicates emotional and memory-related networks in the pathophysiology of HFS. These findings align with previous reports of enhanced FC between the thalamus and sensory-motor regions (Wang et al. 2024a, b), reflecting maladaptive plasticity in response to chronic motor dysfunction. These alterations are consistent with the notion that HFS involves a complex interplay between motor dysfunction and compensatory plasticity in sensory and motor networks. This suggests that surgery may partially modulate FC but does not entirely normalize connectivity patterns, underscoring the chronic nature of neural network alterations in HFS, though further research is needed to clarify this aspect.

Interestingly, the positive correlation between the left facial nucleus-right parahippocampal gyrus FC and Cohen spasm scores suggests that this specific FC abnormality may relate to spasm severity. The lack of correlation with other clinical factors could imply that the relationship between brain connectivity and clinical features in HFS is selective or region-specific, potentially influenced by the distinct neurobiological mechanisms underlying this condition. The relationship with fibrinogen levels also raises interesting questions about the potential role of inflammatory markers in HFS. Fibrinogen is known to be involved in both inflammation and coagulation processes(Hartmannsberger et al. 2024), and its association with FC may indicate that inflammatory or vascular changes contribute to the altered brain connectivity observed in these patients. The negative correlation with MAO levels potentially points to a relationship between reduced connectivity and altered neurotransmitter metabolism.

In our study, we found that the right parahippocampal gyrus and right fusiform gyrus may have potential impact on HFS for their functions in higher cognitive and sensory processing. The right parahippocampal gyrus is primarily associated with memory encoding and retrieval, particularly in spatial and contextual memory (Engstrom et al. 2024; Schnellbächer et al. 2024). It plays a role in scene recognition and navigation, integrating sensory input to form a cohesive memory of an environment. Bao (Bao et al. 2015) found a gray matter volume decrease in the parahippocampal gyrus of HFS patients relative to those in normal controls. The increased FC we found between the facial nucleus and parahippocampal gyrus in HFS patients may indicate heightened neural communication, possibly as the brain integrates sensory information from involuntary facial movements with memory-related processing areas. This association may contribute to the cognitive strain reported by some HFS patients (Tambasco et al. 2019; Wabbels and Yaqubi 2021), as increased sensory processing demands could interfere with the parahippocampal gyrus’s typical memory functions. The right fusiform gyrus, located on the ventral surface of the temporal and occipital lobes, is essential for visual processing, particularly for face and object recognition (Vockert et al. 2024). It is part of the “ventral visual stream(Martin and Barense 2023),” which aids in identifying objects and discerning facial expressions. Altered connectivity between the facial nucleus and the fusiform gyrus in HFS patients could suggest an over-engagement of facial-recognition circuits (Rodríguez-Antigüedad et al. 2024), potentially due to the presence of involuntary facial spasms that demand heightened visual attention and processing. This could affect facial recognition efficiency and visual processing in social contexts(Lischka et al. 2023), which may contribute to difficulties in interpersonal interactions often reported in HFS. Together, these connectivity patterns underscore the potential widespread effects of HFS on memory, spatial orientation, and social visual processing. Further research could clarify whether these changes reflect compensatory mechanisms or maladaptive reorganization within these brain networks.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. A larger sample would allow for better matching on variables such as symptom laterality, duration of illness, and severity, helping to isolate the effects of HFS on functional connectivity more precisely. Second, although this study includes pre- and post-operative comparisons, the post-operative data were collected only one week after surgery. This short follow-up period may not fully capture the long-term effects of surgery on functional connectivity. Changes in brain connectivity may evolve over time as the brain adapts to the altered facial muscle activity post-surgery, so a longer follow-up would be beneficial for understanding the true impact of surgical intervention on FC in HFS patients. Furthermore, although fMRI data offer valuable information on connectivity changes, they do not capture underlying structural changes. Combining fMRI with structural imaging techniques, such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), could help elucidate the anatomical pathways contributing to these functional differences.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence of altered FC in HFS patients, highlighting increased FC between the facial nucleus and regions such as the right parahippocampal and fusiform gyri. These findings suggest that HFS may impact neural circuits related to memory, spatial processing, and social visual functions. While certain connectivity patterns correlate with spasm severity, surgical intervention showed limited immediate impact on FC, indicating that longer-term follow-up may be needed to observe potential adaptive changes post-surgery. Future research with larger sample sizes, longitudinal data, and multi-modal imaging techniques will be essential to deepen our understanding of HFS’s effects on brain function and to explore FC changes as potential biomarkers for symptom monitoring and treatment efficacy.
