The upregulation of GDF15 is controlled epigenetically by oncogenic TCF19 signaling in human hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yongqian Liu, Caiyu Yong, Mengyu Yang, Haoxiang Qi, Sijie Zheng, Menglan Wang, Xu Huang, Shujuan Zhao, Qiyi Zhang, Jianwei Han, Jie Ying, Heng Dong, Jianxiang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryGrowth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) plays an important role in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and chronic liver disease, but its regulatory mechanisms are still unclear. Here, we found that the histone methylation reader protein transcription factor 19 (TCF19) was significantly positively correlated with and regulated GDF15 expression. RNA sequencing and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) revealed that TCF19 regulated GDF15 expression by directly binding to its promoter through the plant homeodomain (PHD) finger, recognizing H3K4me3. Coculture analysis suggests that TCF19 promoted the proliferation of HCC cells and the activation of hepatic stellate cell line LX2 by increasing the extracellular expression level of GDF15. We also found that Ras-related nuclear protein (RAN) might mediate the nuclear localization of TCF19, thereby affecting GDF15 expression and its pro-proliferative function. In summary, we demonstrated that GDF15 is positively regulated by TCF19, promoting the development of HCC.Subject areas: Epigenetics, Molecular network, Cancer

Introduction

Primary liver carcinoma is the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounting for approximately 75%–85% cases.1 In recent years, the epidemiological profile of HCC has shifted substantially from virus-related etiologies to non-viral causes, including alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).2 Hepatokines are secretory proteins originating from the liver. The dysregulation of hepatokines contributes to metabolic dysfunction, which in turn promotes the development of metabolic disorders such as MASLD, and ultimately leads to HCC.3,4,5

Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) has emerged as a prominent hepatokine in recent years, and plays important roles in tumorigenesis, metabolic disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases.6,7,8,9,10 Recently, GDF15 has garnered attention as an emerging key target for cachexia, cancer, and obesity, with several promising agents advancing to phase II clinical trials.11,12,13,14,15 The expression of GDF15 is abnormally elevated in the serum of chronic liver diseases, such as patients with MASLD16,17,18 and HCC.19,20,21,22 In chronic liver disease, GDF15 binds to its receptor, GDNF family receptor alpha-like (GFRAL), exerting a protective role in the early stages of MASLD and obesity.6,23,24 However, sustained high expression of GDF15 promotes disease progression and the development of HCC,19,25 and mediates cellular communication by facilitating interactions between hepatic stellate cells and HCC cells.26

The expression of GDF15 is regulated by transcription factors such as p53,27 Sp1,28 and ATF3,29 but its epigenetic regulation remains poorly understood. Studies on renal and bladder cancer have shown that the histone lysine demethylases (HKDMs) JARID1C and JMJD1A regulate GDF15 expression by removing the trimethylation of histone 3 lysine 4 (H3K4me3) from its promoter.30,31,32 These findings suggest that histone lysine methylation is crucial in GDF15 regulation. Histone lysine methylation is the most common and complex form of histone modification, and is established by histone lysine methyltransferases (HKMTs) and removed by HKDMs.33 These modifications are recognized by reader proteins, which recruit downstream effectors to perform biological functions.34 Compared with HKMTs and HKDMs, reader proteins exhibit greater diversity and serve as direct effectors that determine the expression of downstream genes. Exploring the reader proteins that recognize H3K4me3 signals of the GDF15 promoter may provide key insights into its epigenetic regulatory mechanisms.

The plant homeodomain (PHD) finger gene family, one of the most numerous and important reader families, encodes zinc finger transcription factors that primarily recognize H3K4 methylation.35 PHD finger proteins, such as UHRF2 and UBR7, have been confirmed to play roles in HCC development,36,37,38 and further exploration of additional readers is warranted. Transcription factor 19 (TCF19), a member of the PHD finger protein family,39 is abnormally upregulated in various cancers, including lung cancer,40 kidney cancer,41 and HCC.39 TCF19 promotes cancer progression39 and regulates metabolic processes, including gluconeogenesis,42 mitochondrial energy metabolism, and stress adaptation.43 However, the mechanisms underlying its downstream targets require further elucidation.

Here, we found that the H3K4me3 signal on the GDF15 promoter was recognized by the reader protein TCF19, thereby upregulating GDF15 expression. This subsequently promoted HCC cell proliferation and activated the hepatic stellate cell line LX2. Additionally, we found that ras-related nuclear protein (RAN) might facilitate the nuclear translocation of TCF19 and promote its function. Deciphering the regulatory mechanisms of GDF15 in HCC is beneficial for explaining this phenomenon and optimizing GDF15-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Growth differentiation factor 15 expression is significantly positively correlated with and regulated by transcription factor 19 in hepatocellular carcinoma

In the TCGA-LIHC cohort, a significant correlation was identified between GDF15 expression and 64 of the 96 PHD finger genes, suggesting the potential regulation of GDF15 by PHD finger genes. Further analysis revealed that 8 genes were differentially expressed between HCC and adjacent non-tumor tissues, with TCF19 being the most significant one (Figure 1A).

Analyses of the GSE64041 dataset (Figure 1B) and 33 paired HCC samples (Figure 1C) revealed a significant positive correlation between TCF19 and GDF15 expression in HCC. The mIHC analysis of 15 paired HCC tissues also confirmed this significant positive correlation (Figure 1D). Analysis of single-cell sequencing data from the Human Life Browser database revealed notable similarities in the expression patterns of TCF19 and GDF15 across hepatocytes, carcinoma and proliferation cells (Figure 1E).

Further experimental results revealed that the expression of GDF15 mRNA (Figure 1F) and protein (Figures 1G and 1H) was positively regulated by TCF19 in HCC cell lines Huh1 and HCCLM3. These findings suggested that TCF19 might serve as a key PHD finger gene regulating GDF15 expression in HCC.

Transcription factor 19 is upregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma and promotes its growth and tumorigenesis

Data from the TCGA (Figure 2A) and GSE64041 databases (Figure 2B) revealed that TCF19 expression was significantly elevated in tumors compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues. qRT-PCR (Figure 2C) and mIHC (Figure 2D) further confirmed this upregulation of TCF19 in HCC. Survival analysis revealed that high TCF19 expression was significantly associated with poorer overall survival (Figure 2E) and disease-free survival (Figure 2F).

Compared with normal human liver cell (MIHA), TCF19 expression was significantly upregulated in multiple HCC cell lines, including Huh1, Huh7, HCCLM3, MAHLAVU, SUN449, SUN739, SUN886 and Hep3B (Figures 2G and 2H). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) revealed that knocking down TCF19 (Figures S1A and S1B) inhibited HCC cell proliferation (Figures 2I and 2J). Conversely, overexpressing TCF19 (Figures S1C and S1D) promoted proliferation (Figures 2K and 2L). Colony formation assay also confirmed that knocking down TCF19 inhibited HCC cell proliferation (Figures 2M and 2N) but overexpressing TCF19 promoted proliferation (Figures 2O and 2P). Further analysis revealed that, compared with the full-length TCF19 overexpression group, the TCF19-ΔPHD overexpression group exhibited a diminished proliferative effect (Figures 2L and 2P). This finding underscored the critical role of the PHD finer in TCF19-mediated cellular proliferation.

Similarly, in a subcutaneous tumor mouse model (Figure 2Q), knocking down TCF19 significantly suppressed tumor growth without affecting the body weight of the mouse (Figures 2R–2T). Additionally, knocking down TCF19 reduced the expression of GDF15 (Figures 2U and 2V) in vivo. These results suggest that TCF19 may function as an oncogene in HCC.

These findings suggest that TCF19 functions mainly as a transcription factor.

RNA-seq was conducted in TCF19-downregulated (siTCF19-1 and siTCF19-2) and control Huh1 cells. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that TCF19 was most significantly associated with the membrane transport pathway. Additionally, it confirmed the previously reported associations with the cell cycle, p53, and regulatory chromatin pathways (Figure 3C).

The ChIP-seq target genes (fold enrichment > 4, p value <0.05) were further compared with the RNA-seq differentially expressed genes (|log2FoldChange| > 1, p value <0.05) (Figures 3D and 3E), and a total of 9 overlapping genes were obtained. Analysis of the TCGA-LIHC cohort revealed that there was no significant difference in the expression levels of 7 genes (GPR180, SGK3, UNC5A, MDGA1, KANK3, LINC00324, and PTPN6) between tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues. In contrast, GDF15 and INHBB were found to be significantly overexpressed in tumor tissues (Table 1). The ChIP-seq results revealed that TCF19 might bind 230 bp upstream of the TSS of the GDF15 promoter (Figure 3F) and 1300 bp upstream of the transcription start site of the INHBB promoter (Figure S2B). Further qRT-PCR validation revealed that the regulation of INHBB by TCF19 was cell-specific (Figures S2C–S2F). These results suggest that TCF19 might directly regulate GDF15.

IP-WB revealed an interaction between TCF19 and H3K4me3 (Figure 3G). ChIP-qPCR revealed significant enrichment of H3K4me3 signaling in the GDF15 promoter region (Figures 3H and S2G). Growth differentiation factor 15 promotes the growth of hepatocellular carcinoma cells

By analyzing the TCGA (Figure 4A), GSE64041 and GSE14520 databases (Figure 4B), we confirmed that GDF15 was indeed significantly highly expressed in HCC and that the overall survival of patients was negatively correlated with the expression of GDF15 (Figure 4C). CCK-8 (Figures 4D–4G) and 2D cloning (Figures 4H–4K) in Huh1 and HCCLM3 cells demonstrated that the knockdown of GDF15 inhibited the proliferation of HCC cells (Figures S3A and S3B), whereas the overexpression of GDF15 promoted the proliferation of HCC cells (Figures S3C and S3D). Coculturing revealed that the overexpression of GDF15 in the upper chamber significantly promoted the proliferation of lower-chamber HCC cells (Figure 4L). The above results demonstrated that GDF15 promoted the growth of Huh1 and HCCLM3 cell lines, which would be utilized in the subsequent analysis.

To investigate the impact of the extracellular level of GDF15 regulated by TCF19, we constructed HCC cells with different TCF19 expression levels as upper chamber cells to conduct coculture experiments. Given the extended duration of this experiment, HCCLM3 cells were chosen for the upper chamber due to their lower TCF19 expression and their slower growth rate. Compared with the control group, the full-length TCF19-overexpressing group presented significantly increased the expression of extracellular GDF15 (Figure 5D) and significantly promoted the proliferation of HCC cells in the lower chamber, whereas the TCF19-knockdown group presented the opposite effect. Notably, in the TCF19-ΔPHD overexpression group, the expression level of extracellular GDF15 did not change significantly, and the proliferation of HCC cells was not promoted (Figures 5E and 5F). Moreover, the overexpression of GDF15 reversed the inhibitory effect of TCF19 knockdown on the proliferation of HCC cells (Figure 5G).

Studies show that α-SMA can be used as a marker to determine whether LX2 cells are activated.44 Western blot analysis revealed a significant increase in α-SMA expression in LX2 cells in the upper chamber of the full-length TCF19-overexpressing group. In contrast, no significant change in α-SMA expression was observed in LX2 cells in the TCF19-ΔPHD group. Additionally, the TCF19 knockdown group exhibited a tendency toward decreased α-SMA expression in LX2 cells (Figure 5H). These findings suggested that only full-length TCF19 promoted the activation of LX2 cells in the lower chamber. Western blot analysis revealed that the overexpression of GDF15 similarly increased α-SMA expression in LX2 cells, thereby promoting their activation (Figure 5I). A rescue assay further demonstrated that GDF15 overexpression rescued the inhibitory effect of TCF19 knockdown on LX2 activation (Figure 5J).

Rescue experiments indicated that GDF15 promoted the proliferation of HCC cells via the GFRAL receptor (Figures 5K, S4B, and S4C).

RAN, a member of the GTPase family, is significantly highly expressed in malignant tissues and has a critical impact on tumor cell proliferation.45,46 RAN mainly regulates the nucleocytoplasmic transport of proteins and RNA.47,48,49 Immunofluorescence analysis in cell lines revealed that TCF19 was predominantly localized in the nucleus (Figure 6C), a finding consistent with the observations in tissue samples (Figure 1D). Western blot analysis revealed that RAN knockdown did not significantly alter total TCF19 expression, but led to a significant reduction in its nuclear localization (Figure 6D). Immunofluorescence further confirmed a significant increase in TCF19 distribution outside the nucleus following RAN knockdown (Figure 6E).

TCGA data revealed a significant positive correlation between the expression levels of RAN and GDF15 (Figure 6F). Simultaneous knockdown of RAN and TCF19 resulted in a more pronounced reduction in GDF15 expression (Figures 6G and 6H) and more substantial inhibition of cell proliferation (Figures 6I–6K) compared to groups where only RAN or TCF19 was knocked down. The above results demonstrated that RAN affects the protumor function and regulates GDF15 expression by mediating the nuclear translocation of TCF19.

Discussion

In recent years, GDF15 has emerged as a star hepatokine and a key regulator in the development of various diseases.15 It improves MASLD by promoting weight loss,50 increasing insulin sensitivity,6 reducing inflammation,23 and enhancing lipolysis.51 The serum level of GDF15 increases progressively during liver disease and reaches its peak in patients with HCC.17,52,53,54,55 Elevated level of GDF15 serves as an independent risk factor for HCC development, and is associated with higher incidence and poorer prognosis.56 In addition, the expression of GDF15 is also upregulated in other cancers such as cervical cancer,21 colon cancer,20 and prostate cancer.22 This suggests that GDF15 also plays a significant role in cancers other than HCC.

The mechanism studies on GDF15 mainly focus on its downstream targets, with limited studies suggesting it is regulated by histone methylation.31,32 Our study indicated that GDF15 was positively regulated by TCF19 through PHD finger, thereby affecting the proliferation of HCC cells and the activation of LX2 (Figure 7). TCF19, a H3K4me2/3 reader protein, can regulate metabolism42,43 and promote the development of various cancers,39,40,41 such as non-small cell lung cancer, renal cancer and HCC. Previous studies have confirmed that TCF19 promotes the transition of cancer cells from the G1 phase to the S phase,40 interacts with p53,43 and can recognize H3K4me3 through the PHD finger.39,42 These findings are consistent with our KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the downstream targets identified through ChIP-Seq and RNA-seq (Figures 3B and S2I), suggesting the function of TCF19 is relatively conserved in different cancers. Reader proteins are important epigenetic regulators in various cancers.57 PHD finger reader proteins, such as UHRF158 and PHF20,59 have been found to be crucial for tumor growth and treatment resistance. It has been reported that patients with HCC with high expression levels of TCF19 have increased resistance to trametinib.60 Drugs targeting reader proteins, including calixarenes61 and benzimidazole,62 are still in the preclinical stage. So there is still more potential value with regarding to the clinical application of reading proteins to be explored, and they have the potential to be diagnostic and therapeutic targets.

The proposed model of TCF19 regulates GDF15 in HCC

The regulation of TCF19 and GDF15 appears to be significantly associated with processes beyond oncology. TCF19 is considered an insulin response and glucose factor that is widely involved in the progression of type 2 diabetes.43,49,63 The absence of TCF19 can lead to significantly reduced insulin secretion, apoptosis of pancreatic β-cells, DNA damage,64,65 and impaired glucose tolerance.66 GDF15 is also involved in insulin secretion, protects pancreatic β-cells,67,68 and improves glucose tolerance.69 Additionally, GDF15 has been identified as a tumor-derived immunosuppressive molecule and is considered a potential target for immunotherapy.70,71 Among the GDF15-targeted drugs, visugromab combined with nivolumab (an anti-PD-1 antibody) for the treatment of recurrent/refractory metastatic solid tumors has entered phase II clinical trials.12 TCF19 is also associated with immunotherapy. The elevated TCF19 expression lead to lower levels of immune infiltration and indicates greater resistance to HCC drugs.60 Research on endometrial cancer has revealed that inhibiting TCF19 can increase the antitumor efficacy of anti-PD-1 drugs.72 The regulatory effect of TCF19 on GDF15 suggests that TCF19 may be involved in the insulin response, glucose tolerance, and immune suppression of GDF15, but there is currently a lack of clinical data to verify.

Additionally, our study has demonstrated that the nuclear translocation of TCF19 mayregulate by RAN, although the specific mechanism still needs to be elucidated. RAN can mediate the development of tumors and drug resistance through its nucleocytoplasmic transport function.46,73,74 Research indicates that the FHA domain of TCF19 can act as its nuclear localization signal.75 Exploring whether the regulation of RAN-related nuclear translocation of TCF19 is assisted by FHA and the directly binding sites, will help further elucidate the oncogenic mechanism of TCF19.

Limitations of the study

Our study still has some limitations. Firstly, GDF15 binds to the GFRAL receptor and transmits signals through RET, further activating downstream signal transduction involving ERK, AKT, and PLC-γ.16,24 Current studies suggest that GFRAL primarily distributed in the brainstem.24 Our study, along with previous research,26 shows that GFRAL is also expressed in HCC cells and hepatic stellate cells, expanding the known distribution of GFRAL receptors. However, whether the expression of GFRAL indirectly affects the proliferation of TCF19 still needs further verification.

Secondly, based on the single-cell sequencing data, we found that the expression patterns of TCF19 and GDF15 are different in cell types other than hepatocytes, carcinoma, and proliferation cells. This suggests other mechanisms are involved in GDF15 expression in these cells, which need further studies.

Conclusion

Our research revealed that the TCF19/GDF15 axis drives the progression of HCC. The expression level of GDF15 is regulated by TCF19, affecting the proliferation of surrounding tumor cells and the activation of hepatic stellate cells, with the RAN protein may play a crucial role in the nuclear translocation and functional activity of TCF19. This key discovery not only deepens our understanding of HCC progression but also clarifies the mechanism underlying GDF15-targeted therapy and identifies TCF19 as a therapeutic target.

Experimental model and study participant details

HCC tissues were obtained from the Hospital Affiliated of Hangzhou Normal University, and the study was approved by its medical ethics committee (2020(E02)-KS-014). The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The cell lines Huh1 and Huh7 were obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) Cell Bank; SNU449, SNU739 and SNU886 were obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB); Hep3B, MAHLAVU, HCCLM3, MIHA and LX2 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), the Cell Lines Service (CLS), the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC), the Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and the Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd., respectively. The cells were cultured at a constant temperature and pressure incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2, 10% fetal bovine serum (FSP500, ExCell Bio, China), and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (S110JV, BasalMedia, China). MIHA and SUN-739 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (L210KJ, BasalMedia, China), while the other cells were cultured in DMEM (L110KJ, BasalMedia, China). The cells used in the experiments were in the logarithmic growth phase. The cell lines used in this work have have been tested, and have no mycoplasma contamination.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

The tissue paraffin blocks were cut into 4 μm sections. These sections were incubated at 65°C for 2 hours, dewaxed twice with xylene (10 minutes each time), dehydrated with a gradient of ethanol (100%, 95%, 80%, and 70%; 5 minutes each time), and then hydrated with distilled water. The sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 minutes and then rinsed with distilled water. The sections were stained with eosin for 2 minutes and then rinsed with distilled water. The samples were then dehydrated with graded ethanol and xylene. A scanning electron microscope (VS200, Olympus, USA) was used to scan and capture the images.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue blocks were sectioned at 4 μm. After baking at 65°C for 2 h, sections underwent two xylene dewaxing steps (10 min each), followed by graded ethanol dehydration (100%, 95%, 80%, 70%, 5 min each) and hydration with distilled water. The sections were placed in a microwave and boiled in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval. They were then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4°C After that, a DAB chromogenic solution was prepared, and the sections were incubated at room temperature in the dark. Once the color developed, the reaction was stopped by rinsing with tap water for 10 minutes. Subsequently, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 minutes and treated with 0.1% hydrochloric acid - ethanol solution for 30 seconds for bluesing. Finally, after dehydration with graded ethanol and xylene, the sections were mounted with neutral resin. A scanning electron microscope (VS200, Olympus, USA) was used to scan and capture images. The antibodies used were presented in Table S1.

Multiplexed immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

The tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval at high temperature and pressure (EDTA, pH 9.0). The samples were stained according to the TSA staining method by Wuhan Changyan Pathology Technology Co., Ltd. A scanning electron microscope (VS200, Olympus, USA) was used to scan and capture images. The measure tool in ImageJ (version 1.52) was used to perform the statistical analysis of the positive signal area ratio (%area). The antibodies used were presented in Table S1.

siRNA and transient transfection

siRNAs and negative control (siNC) were purchased from Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. and were transfected with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (13778150, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The siRNAs were presented in Table S1.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR

Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol (9109, Takara, Japan). RT SuperMix (R323, Vazyme, China) was used to reverse transcribe total RNA. A CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA) and ChamQ Universal qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Q711, Vazyme, China) were used for real-time fluorescence quantification. The sequences of the primers used were presented in Table S1.

RNA-seq

RNA sequencing was completed by Tianjin Ringpu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Sequencing was completed on the NovaSeq-PE150 platform. The sequencing data were quality controlled using Fastq. HISAT2 was selected for alignment with the reference genome (GRCh38). Gene expression levels were calculated by edgeR and analyzed using R 4.3.1. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. ggplot2, ggpubr and ggthemes were used to visualize the results.

Western blot

The cells were lysed on ice for 30 minutes with RIPA lysis buffer (89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 30 minutes, the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration was determined via a BCA protein assay kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Proteins were separated by 12% SDS‒PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Immobilon-P, Millipore, USA). The membrane was incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4°C and with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 hour. Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL-0100, SmartBuffers, China) and a chemiluminescence device were used to expose and capture the images. The primary antibodies were presented in Table S1.

Construction of plasmids and stable cell lines

The human TCF19 sequence with a 3∗FLAG tag at the C-terminus, the TCF19-ΔPHD sequence with a 3∗FLAG tag at the C-terminus, and the human GDF15 sequence with a HA tag at the C-terminus were amplified by PCR and ligated into the lentiviral vector pLVX-IRES-puro. The shRNA sequences were denatured at 95°C for 10 minutes, annealed at room temperature, and then ligated into the pLKO.1-TRC vector. Lentiviruses were prepared by transfecting packaging plasmids into HEK293T cells for infection. HCCLM3 cells were selected with 3 μg/ml puromycin (ST551, Beyotime, China) in the medium, and Huh1 cells were selected with 0.8 μg/ml puromycin to obtain stably transduced resistant cells. qRT‒PCR was used to verify the overexpression and knockdown efficiency. The primer sequences were presented in Table S1.

Xenograft experiments in nude mice

This experiment was approved by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Hangzhou Normal University (HSD-20231218-01). Male BALB/c nude mice aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from Jiangsu GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. A stable HCCLM3 cell suspension was implanted subcutaneously in the left abdominal area of each mouse (1 × 107 cells). The length and width of the tumors and the weights of the mice were recorded every two days. The tumor volume was calculated according to the formula volume = 0.5 × length × width2. In accordance with the regulations of the IACUC at Hangzhou Normal University, the mice were euthanized when the tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3. The paraffin embedding of the tumor tissue was completed by Hangzhou Haoke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry

The cells were lysed on ice for 30 minutes in prepared lysis buffer (0.1% Triton X-100, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol and 0.5% NP-40). After sonication, the lysate was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 30 minutes, after which the supernatant was collected. A BCA protein assay kit was used to determine the protein concentration in the supernatant. The supernatant was incubated with Pierce™ Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (88803, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 4°C for 1 hour to remove nonspecific proteins. The antibodies were incubated with the blocked beads at 4°C for 8 hours. Then, the supernatant was incubated with the bead‒antibody complex at 4°C overnight. Western blotting or mass spectrometry was used to verify or analyze the results. The primary antibodies used were presented in Table S1.

ChlP assay

The experiments were conducted with a ChIP kit (RK20258, ABclonal, China). The mixture was crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 minutes, after which the reaction was quenched with 1× glycine. After washing with PBS three times, the cells were collected. The mixture was then lysed and sonicated. The membrane was incubated with the blocked Pierce™ Protein A/G Magnetic Beads for 6 hours. The antibody beads were incubated with the chromatin‒protein complex overnight. After treatment with RNase A and proteinase K, the cross-links were reversed. DNA was extracted via a FastPure Gel DNA Extraction Mini Kit (DC301-01, Vazyme, China) for sequencing and qPCR detection. The sequencing was completed by Wuhan SeqHealth Technology Co., Ltd. Quality control was performed using fastq (Version 0.23.1). In the background, peaks were called in the IP-FLAG group using Macs2, and peak calling was performed in narrow peak mode. The primers used were presented in Table S1.

CCK-8 and 2D cloning

The treated cells were seeded in 96-well plates. A Cell Counting Kit-8 (MA0218, Meilunbio, China) was used, and the samples were incubated at 37°C for 2 hours. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm and 620 nm. The treated cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until colonies formed. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes and stained with crystal violet. After the samples were washed and air dried, images were captured.

Coculture

The treated HCCLM3 cells were inoculated into the upper chamber (3412, Corning, USA), and the untreated cells were inoculated into the lower chamber. After the cells adhered, the upper and lower chambers were combined. After 96 hours, the upper chamber was discarded. After the formation of cell clones in the lower chamber, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The samples were stained with crystal violet, and images of the clones were captured.

ELISA

The processed Huh1 or HCCLM3 cells were inoculated into a six-well plate. The cell culture medium was collected at 72 hours and 96 hours. After centrifugation at 2,500 rpm for 5 minutes, the supernatant was collected from the cell culture medium. After the samples were diluted 10-fold and 5-fold, an ELISA kit (EH0150, FineTest, China) was used to detect the secretion levels of GDF15. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm and 620 nm.

Immunofluorescence

The cells were inoculated onto the plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per plate. After the cells had fully adhered, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes. The samples were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 (diluted in PBS) for 15 minutes. Five percent BSA (A7030, Millipore, USA) was used to block the samples for 1 hour. The samples were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C. The cells were then washed with PBS three times for 5 minutes each. The samples were incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 hour. The nuclei were stained with DAPI (62248, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 10 minutes. The cells were then washed with PBS three times for 5 minutes each. After the fluorescent quencher (P36934, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added, the slides were covered. Images were captured using a laser scanning confocal fluorescence microscope (AX, Nikon, Japan) and processed with NIS-Elements AR (version 5.4). The Plot Profile tool in ImageJ (version 1.52) was used to complete the radial fluorescence intensity analysis. The primary antibodies used were presented in Table S1.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0). Survival analysis was performed via Kaplan-Meier analysis to plot the curves, and the log rank test was used to assess survival differences. Student's t test was used to evaluate the statistical significance of the difference between two groups with a two-tailed p value. A paired t test was used to assess the statistical significance of the differences between paired samples in two groups. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to determine the relationship between two variables. The error bars represent the means ± SDs of three independent experiments. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, where ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001. All of the statistical details of experiments, including the sample number and the statistical tests used have been listed in the figure legends, and figures.
