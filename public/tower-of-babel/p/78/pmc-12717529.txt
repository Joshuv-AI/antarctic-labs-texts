Nanobody-based gene therapy targeting complement component C3 reduces choroidal neovascularization in mice
Authors: Emilie Grarup Jensen, Thomas Stax Jakobsen, Gloriane Schnabolk, Kyrie Wilson, Mathias Rask-Pedersen, Nanna Jensen, Gregers Rom Andersen, Steffen Thiel, Lars Aagaard, Bärbel Rohrer, Anne Louise Askou, Thomas J Corydon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWet age-related macular degeneration (wAMD) is a leading cause of vision loss and is characterized by choroidal neovascularization (CNV). Current CNV management requires multiple treatments and lacks long-term efficiency, creating a need for better therapeutics. wAMD pathogenesis is associated with excessive activation of the complement system, contributing to retinal damage. Therefore, we generated a vector expressing the small alternative pathway-targeting nanobody, hC3Nb1, to treat wAMD. We demonstrate that hC3Nb1 is efficiently expressed and secreted by mammalian cells and shows full alternative pathway and partial classical pathway inhibition in vitro. A dual-promoter approach was used to generate a lentiviral-based vector for co-expression of hC3Nb1 and marker protein eGFP. Profound and safe hC3Nb1-expression, along with its secretion from the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), was confirmed following subretinal injection of nanobody expressing-vector in mice. The therapeutic potential of vector-encoded hC3Nb1 was demonstrated in vitro by protecting RPE from complement-mediated stress, and in vivo by reducing laser-induced CNV sizes in a mouse model consistent with complement inhibition. For the first time, nanobodies expressed in the eye are used therapeutically, and our findings suggest that hC3Nb1-based gene therapy may be a safe and long-acting treatment for wAMD and other chorioretinal diseases with dysregulated complement activation.Keywords: nanobody, complement-inhibition, age-related macular degeneration, laser-induced CNV, gene therapy, retina, viral vector, choroidal neovascularization, complement system, lentivirus

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a major cause of central vision loss in individuals older than 55 years and constitutes approximately 9% of legal blindness worldwide.1 Severe AMD-related vision loss is caused by the development of either of the two advanced stages: (1) geographic atrophy with areas of extensive photoreceptor and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) atrophy or (2) choroidal neovascularization (CNV) with the formation of new vessels from the choroid. The unstable vessels involved in CNV are associated with hemorrhage and exudation. Hence, the condition is also termed wet AMD (wAMD).

CNVs are currently managed with intravitreal injections of agents targeting the main angiogenic driver, i.e., vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). Such anti-VEGF therapeutics can effectively reduce leakage and substantially diminish AMD-related visual impairment and legal blindness.2,3,4,5,6 However, functional and anatomical outcomes are suboptimal—visual gains tend to be lost in the years following treatment initiation due to the development of subretinal fibrosis and outer retinal atrophy.7 The injections require high compliance and are needed repetitively, accumulating the risk of complications and constituting a considerable burden for the individual patient and the health care systems. Furthermore, 10%–35% of treated patients either fail to respond to anti-VEGF agents or develop resistance over time.8,9 Consequently, there is an unmet need for improved and more durable wAMD therapies based on alternative modes of action.

The complement system, an established contributor to AMD development when overactive, has emerged as an attractive therapeutic target.10,11,12,13 As part of the innate immune system, the complement system aims to recognize, tag, and eliminate microorganisms or diseased host cells. Among the three complement activation pathways: The alternative, the classical, and the lectin pathway, the alternative pathway seems of particular relevance to wAMD. Strong AMD genetic risk variants are located in alternative pathway-relevant complement genes,14,15,16 increased ocular17 and systemic levels18 of essential alternative pathway components are found in AMD patients, and a reduction of the negative alternative pathway regulator, complement factor H (CFH), is observed in AMD retinas.19 Extensive studies in the laser-induced CNV model mimicking wAMD pathology have also established alternative pathway amplification as a key anti-angiogenic target,13,20,21 while subretinal fibrosis development has been associated with complement activation.22,23,24 Pathological amplification of the alternative pathway may lead to inflammation, recruitment of VEGF-secreting macrophages, and the assembly of the membrane-attack complex (MAC). Sublytic levels of MAC on the RPE stimulate a pro-inflammatory and VEGF-secreting profile,25,26 while lytic levels of MAC in the outer retina may be involved in its degeneration. Collectively, this data underscores the potential for a complement inhibitor as a therapeutic capable of preventing wAMD-related vision loss.

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of two complement inhibitors pegcetacoplan (Syfovre, Apellis Pharmaceuticals, MA, USA) and Avacincaptad pegol (Izerway, IVERIC bio, NY, USA) for the treatment of geographic atrophy in 2023 underscored the therapeutic significance of targeting complement system components in AMD. Both the drugs reduced the geographic atrophy growth rate27,28; however, no visual improvement was measured in patients within the 12 months for Avacincaptad pegol and the 24 months for Pegcetacoplan. These and many other complement inhibitors are being developed for both advanced AMD stages: At present (as of June 2025), approximately 40 clinical trials have been performed, testing 15 complement inhibitors for geographic atrophy and six for wAMD. Clinical trials for wAMD have evaluated inhibitors of C3, C5, and C3b/C4b, as well as strategies promoting CD59 overexpression.13 These strategies have generally been well-tolerated, but additive reduction of exudation in combination with anti-VEGF therapies has not been readily demonstrated, and further development has mainly shifted focus toward geographic atrophy. The only therapeutic in current clinical development for wAMD is Efdamrofusp alfa (IBI302, Innovent Biologics, London, UK). This is a bispecific fusion protein targeting VEGF and complement factor C3b and C4b, which has showed favorable safety and efficacy profiles in early-phase studies.29 Notably, Efdamrofusp alfa combined the effects of VEGF antagonism and complement inhibition to suppress laser-induced CNV. Phase 1 evaluation demonstrated comparable visual acuity and central subfield thickness outcomes to Aflibercept.30 Long-term outcomes and potential proactive effects against treatment emergent atrophy and fibrosis are being evaluated in Phase 2 trials (NCT05403749). In parallel, a phase 2 trial of the complement factor B inhibitor Iptacopan (NCT05230537) explores its potential to prevent or delay progression of early-to-intermediate AMD in high-risk patients defined by having wAMD in the contralateral eye.

The therapeutic efficacy of complement inhibitors for AMD may depend on several factors including disease stage and subtype, the specific complement target, and the ability of the drug to reach the target tissue. Complement inhibitors evaluated in clinical trials for AMD to date typically range in molecular weight from ∼40 to 200 kDa—sizes that may limit their ability to diffuse across the RPE and Bruch’s membrane following intravitreal injection. Given the anatomical and pathological relevance of these layers, especially in the context of chorioretinal complement overactivation,31 an inhibitor capable of efficient trans-tissue diffusion is highly desirable.32,33 Nanobodies, derived from the variable domain (VHH) of camelid single-domain antibodies,34 are markedly smaller (∼15 kDa) and represent the smallest known antigen-binding fragment. This molecular compactness facilitates enhanced tissue penetration.35 This is particularly important in the context of complement inhibition for AMD as the choriocapillaris underneath the retina is affected by complement overactivation.31 Nanobodies such as the human C3/C3b-targeting nanobody 1, hC3Nb1, exhibit high antigen specificity, and affinity when compared to conventional antibodies, remarkable thermal and chemical stability, and reduced immunogenicity—traits that are advantageous for sustained ocular expression and therapeutic safety.35,36,37,38 The hC3Nb1 nanobody is a well-studied alternative pathway inhibitor hindering mainly alternative pathway C3-convertase assembly.39 Furthermore, nanobodies are relatively simple to engineer, circumventing challenges associated with proper folding of single-chain antibodies.

The favorable features of nanobodies have prompted their development for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Encouragingly, the bivalent nanobody Caplacizumab (Cablivi, Sanofi, Paris, France) was FDA-approved in 2019 to treat pathologic coagulation,40 and at least 10 additional nanobody-based therapeutics are being tested in clinical trials mainly for cancer and inflammatory diseases.35 Thus, the nanobody has indeed made its way into the clinic. Currently, recombinant nanobodies, i.e., nanobodies in their purified protein form, targeting complement have been investigated preclinically for indications such as anemia, rheumatoid arthritis, and cancer,41,42,43,44,45,46,47,48,49 however, not for treating complement dysregulation in AMD or other ocular diseases. The small size and absence of an Fc region offer notable advantages for ocular use but may also reduce therapeutic retinal bioavailability when the monomeric nanobody forms are delivered directly. Hence, to extend intraocular half-life, multimeric nanobodies such as BI 836880, a trivalent construct targeting VEGF, ANG2, and albumin, have been investigated.50,51 Preclinical studies further support this approach, demonstrating the potential of anti-VEGF nanobodies52 and Fc-nanobody fusion proteins53 for optimal binding affinity as well as superior stability and solution viscosity compared with conventional antibodies. However, these approaches limit the inherent advantages related to increased permeability.

An alternative strategy to achieve stable therapeutic concentrations of nanobodies in ocular tissue is vector delivery. With such gene therapy approaches, providing continuous production of the transgene, persistent nanobody levels can be achieved locally despite their potentially high tissue penetration. Vector-delivery of antibody fragments and Fc-fusion proteins is an established principle for AMD gene therapy with promising candidates in phase 3 trials (NCT05407636 and NCT06856577). Notably, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated delivery of single-chain variable fragments neutralizing VEGF effectively reduces choriodal54 and retinal55 neovascularization in preclinical models, and AAV delivery of a dual component inhibiting single-chain fragment antigen-binding is under development. Advantages of these smaller fragments include more efficient expression, intracellular stability, and ability to be readily accommodated within the 4.7 kB packaging capacity of the standard AAV platform. The advantages are particularly true for vector-delivery of nanobodies. However, the safety of sustained intraocular nanobody expression remains to be fully established, underscoring the importance of careful vector design and thorough preclinical evaluation.

AAV delivery of nanobodies has been explored for extra-ocular diseases38 and nanobody expression from a plasmid-based vector and the ability to bind intracellular targets has been demonstrated in the retina.56 However, therapeutic effect of vector-delivered nanobodies has not been demonstrated in the eye, particularly through viral-based delivery systems, which offer sustained expression and durability compared to plasmid-based approaches. Similarly, vectorization of complement-inhibiting nanobodies has not previously been attempted to obtain long-term therapeutic effects. Hence, we here evaluate a gene therapy-based strategy for treating wAMD with a small and readily secreted alternative pathway inhibitor. A nanobody expression cassette was analyzed in vitro to assess hC3Nb1 secretion and functionality following expression in mammalian cells. Then, an hC3Nb1-encoding lentiviral vector (LV) was used for subretinal injections to validate the expression of hC3Nb1 in the murine retina. Lastly, the ability of hC3Nb1 to safely be produced in the retina for extensive alternative pathway inhibition and to reduce laser-induced CNV was assessed. This is the first study to demonstrate therapeutic application of LV-delivered nanobodies targeting complement component C3 in the eye.

Results

Functional complement-inhibiting nanobodies are efficiently expressed and secreted by mammalian cells

Our goal was to explore nanobody expression by mammalian cells as a step toward vector-mediated delivery to the mouse retina in vivo. pcDNA-based expression vectors were constructed encoding four different nanobodies driven by the cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter and tagged with hemagglutinin (HA) (Figure 1A). Along with hC3Nb1, inhibiting the alternative pathway, additional well-characterized nanobodies were tested for pathway-specific comparison; hC3Nb1-W102A as a mutated and negative control for hC3Nb1,39 hC3Nb2 inhibiting all complement pathways,57 and C4Nb inhibiting classical and lectin pathway activity. The HA-tag was chosen for nanobody detection based on an evaluation comparing antibody-based detection of the HA-tag with the His- and Myc-tag (Figure S1). In our hands, all three tags were efficiently detected by western blot and immunofluorescence analysis with the HA-tag delivering the overall best performance. Following transfection of nanobody-encoded expression vectors into HEK293 cells, analysis using immunofluorescence revealed prominent expression of hC3Nb1, hC3Nb1-W102A, hC3Nb2, and C4Nb with granular localization patterns in the cytoplasm as expected for secreted proteins (Figure 1B). Nanobody expression and secretion were confirmed by western blot analysis of cell lysates and cell culture media (Figure 1C).

The secreted nanobodies demonstrated the expected ability to inhibit the deposition of C3 fragments onto cell surfaces that initiate the alternative pathway and the classical pathway. Specifically, compared with its inactive counterpart, hC3Nb1-W102A, we found that supernatants from hC3Nb1-expressing cells efficiently inhibited the alternative pathway and partially the classical pathway (Figure 1D). Media containing the secreted broad inhibitor hC3Nb2, inhibited both the alternative pathway and classical pathway, while C4Nb-containing media inhibited the classical pathway only. Thus, complement-inhibiting nanobodies targeting C3 and C4 expressed and secreted by a mammalian cell line, can exhibit their predicted inhibitory abilities.

Dual-promoter LVs are optimal for combined expression of nanobody and eGFP

To obtain stable in vivo expression of nanobodies, the genome-integrating LVs were used. Third generation self-inactivating (SIN) LVs pseudotyped with vesicular stomatitis virus G (VSV-G) were assembled as these effectively transduce RPE cells. Two different LV configurations were tested to allow for the combined expression of both the HA-tagged nanobody and a fluorescent transduction marker, eGFP: an IRES-based system and a back-to-back dual-promoter system using the vitelliform macular dystrophy 2 (VMD2) and phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK) promoters for transgene expression was assembled (Figure 2A). Initially, the IRES-based system was tested with pcDNA-based expression vectors in HEK293 cells (Figure S2), and robust expression of nanobodies and eGFP was observed. Then, co-expression of hC3Nb1 and eGFP delivered by the IRES- or the dual-promoter LV systems was assessed and compared in melanoma cells (Figures 2B and 2C), as these cells have been shown to allow activity of the RPE-specific VMD2 promoter.58,59 Both systems delivered expression of hC3Nb1 within the cells as well as secretion into the media, with the dual-promoter system delivering highest levels into the media. Importantly, eGFP expression was only evident from cells transduced with the dual promoter LVs compared with the IRES counterpart. Based on these findings, the dual-promoter LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 vector was selected for further analysis.

hC3Nb1 prevents complement-mediated ARPE-19 monolayer disruption

Next, the protective capacity of vector-based hC3Nb1 to maintain monolayer integrity of ARPE-19 cells exposed to stress was assessed. Oxidative stress combined with normal human serum (NHS) leads to the disruption of ARPE-19 monolayers as a result of the reduction of membrane-bound complement inhibitors and ensuing complement activation.60 Thus, the complement-inhibitory capacity of the hC3Nb1 was tested by measuring trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) on LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1-transduced and untransduced ARPE-19 monolayers exposed to H2O2 and NHS. To allow the cells to fully differentiate prior to the oxidative stress exposure, NHS were lowered to 1% for 2 weeks until the monolayer stability was established as confirmed by TEER. Untransduced ARPE-19 monolayers exposed to oxidative and complement stress demonstrated a TEER reduction of 17.2% ± 4.4 (mean ± standard deviation, [SD]) (p = 0.07) after 4 h and further decreased after 24 h resulting in a 30.5% ± 3.5 change compared with unstressed ARPE-19 monolayers (p = 0.002) (Figure 2D). LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1-transduced ARPE-19 cells (ARPE-19-hC3Nb1) experienced a reduced level of TEER 4 h following stress (9.1% ± 1.2, p = 0.004) compared with the unstressed ARPE-19-hC3Nb1, but completely recovered after 24 h (2% ± 1.7, p = 0.87).

Efficient and long-term nanobody expression following subretinal delivery in mice

Having demonstrated efficient LV-based expression of hC3Nb1 and eGFP in vitro, as well as a functional effect of the LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 using TEER, nanobody expression was assessed in vivo. Hence, LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 was tested along with the hC3Nb1 control vector, LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1-W102A, delivered subretinally in mice at 1–2 × 104 infectious units (IU). We selected the inactive hC3Nb1-W102A as the most relevant negative control, as it would report any potential effects of gene delivery. Following injections, transduction success was validated through eGFP expression with fluorescence fundus imaging acquired 3 weeks post-injection (Figures 3A and S3). The co-expressed eGFP strongly indicates that the vector was delivered correctly to the subretinal space. Co-expression of hC3Nb1 and eGFP in RPE cells in murine eyes injected with LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 was confirmed 3 weeks post-injection on anti-HA-stained RPE/choroidal flatmounts (Figure 3B). Here, the nanobody signal exhibited a granular cytoplasmic profile, supporting its presence in the secretory system. Furthermore, western blot analysis on neuroretina/RPE/choroid lysates confirmed hC3Nb1, hC3Nb1-W102A, and eGFP expression 3- and 8-weeks post-injection (Figure 3C). By anti-HA and anti-GFP staining of retinal sections, nanobodies and eGFP were detected in the RPE layer (Figure 3D). Thus, subretinal injections of LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 offered an efficient, long-term expression of hC3Nb1 in murine RPE cells.

Electroretinography (ERG) analysis demonstrated the general safety of retinal LV-based hC3Nb1-expression and alternative pathway inhibition 5 weeks post-injection, as a-, b-, and c-wave amplitudes were comparable between eyes injected subretinally with the active complement inhibitor LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 and its inactive counterpart LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-W102A. Furthermore, similar amplitudes observed in transduced eyes and HBSS-injected eyes demonstrated the overall safety of LV delivery and nanobody production in the retina (Figure 4C). ERG amplitude data are supported by ERG latencies in Figure S5, indicating safety of LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 when compared to eyes transduced with LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1-W102A. To investigate whether long-term expression of hC3Nb1 in the retina induces an immunological response, serum from mice injected with LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 was assessed for the presence of anti-nanobody antibodies (Figure S6). No detectable antibodies were identified.

Efficacy of hC3Nb1 was evaluated based on its ability to reduce the size of the laser-induced CNV area and to reduce complement activation. CNV size was assessed by optical coherence tomography (OCT) 7 days after laser, measuring the area of the hyporeflective spot in the fundus image. hC3Nb1 was found to reduce CNV lesion size by 49% (18.8%; 78.5%) in LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1-transduced eyes compared with eyes transduced with LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1-W102A (p = 0.0005) (Figure 4D). CNV lesion sizes were comparable for the hC3Nb1-W102A and HBSS-treated groups (Figure 4D). To assess the extent of complement inhibition following laser-induced CNV in the different treatment groups, C3d staining was performed on RPE/choroidal flatmounts. hC3Nb1-expressing retinas demonstrated a tendency toward less C3d intensity and a significantly reduced C3d positive area compared with hC3Nb1-W102A-expressing eyes (Figure 4E). These data demonstrate that hC3Nb1-mediated complement inhibition is associated with the reduction in CNV size in hC3Nb1-expressing retinas, and suggest that retinal expression of hC3Nb1 holds therapeutic potential for the treatment of wAMD-related pathology.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the use of a nanobody-based complement-inhibiting gene therapy. We demonstrated that (1) vector-based secreted nanobodies inhibit complement pathways; (2) the alternative pathway inhibitor hC3Nb1 reduces complement activation and is associated with a reduction of laser-induced CNV after subretinal injection in mice; and (3) safe, persistent, and efficient nanobody expression and secretion are possible from the RPE cells in vivo.

The successful in vitro and in vivo expression of nanobodies following LV transductions in this study demonstrates the potential of complement-inhibiting nanobodies, within a vector-based gene therapeutic approach. The complement inhibitory ability of the alternative pathway inhibitor hC3Nb1 was assessed in the C3 fragment deposition assay, and unexpectedly, it was found to also partially inhibit the classical pathway. This finding may be explained by its binding to C3, blocking the C3-cleavage by the classical pathway C3-convertase. However, compared with full classical pathway inhibition executed by hC3Nb2 and C4Nb, hC3Nb1 allows for substantial classical pathway activation, important for regulation of retinal homeostasis.61,62

After subretinal injection of these nanobody-encoding LVs in mice, we demonstrated highly efficient nanobody expression in the RPE cells and nanobody secretion from the RPE. Small proteins like nanobodies can diffuse extracellularly, which may limit the precision of localization by immunofluorescence. The seemingly modest levels detected in the eye cup by western blotting likely reflect this diffusibility and the dilution effect across tissue compartments. Moreover, unbound nanobodies may exit in the retinal tissue altogether contributing to their lower detection by both western blot and immunofluorescence. Even though our data suggest increasing levels of nanobody in the eye cup over time, nanobody was only detected in the RPE cells following immunostaining of the retina. One obvious explanation for this discrepancy is that the sensitivity in western blotting is higher compared to immunofluorescent staining. Importantly, immunostaining reveals high local concentrations in the RPE—the principal site of AMD pathology—and western blotting of samples collected from the neuroretina further demonstrates detectable nanobody signal within the retina. Together, the two target tissues, the retina and the RPE-choroid, both receive measurable amount of the alternative pathway inhibitor.

The functional effect of LV-encoded hC3Nb1 was demonstrated in vitro, protecting ARPE-19 cells against complement-mediated H2O2-induced stress, assessed by monolayer integrity using TEER, and in vivo, inhibiting outer retinal alternative pathway activation stimulated in the laser-induced CNV model. To treat AMD, affected by increased complement activation in the choroid and outer retina, penetration of the outer blood-retina barrier including Bruch’s membrane is necessary, favoring the use of smaller therapeutics, such as the nanobody, as a single-domain antibody. The detection of hC3Nb1 in the neuroretina as well as the apical and basal secretion of hC3Nb1 from hC3Nb1-ARPE-19 cells grown on transwells, support secretion of nanobodies from the RPE into nearby tissues for widespread alternative pathway inhibition. A Bruch’s membrane penetration assay could assess the potential diffusion of hC3Nb1 across this barrier,33 as penetration depends not only on molecular size, but also charge, post-translational modifications, and shape of the inhibitor, as well as the hydrostatic pressure and the concentration gradient on both sides of the membrane.32

The laser-induced CNV mouse model is the most commonly used model to mimic wAMD63,64,65 and assess the therapeutic potential of antiangiogenic drugs. The acute, angiogenic lesions induced in this model, peaked approximately 7 days after laser treatment,64,65 after which the phenotype switches to a predominantly fibrotic state. Interestingly, both phases are susceptible to complement inhibition.24,66 Here, the early CNV lesion sizes were assessed using a reconstruction tool of the InVivoVue software, identifying the center of the lesion in en-face images and then measuring the corresponding area of the hyporeflective spot produced in the fundus image, as previously described.67,68 With this approach, the total area above the RPE affected by the CNV is measured, making it comparable to the most commonly used method for quantifying CNV areas on RPE/choroidal flatmounts.64 With this method, we demonstrate a significant reduction in CNV size due to hC3Nb1-mediated complement inhibition. Furthermore, our ERG data demonstrate the safety of LV-based hC3Nb1 therapy. Limited ERG data points were included for the HBSS control group (n = 2–4) due to the exclusion of cataract-affected eyes and technical problems during ERG measurement, which complicates proper comparisons with this control group. Nevertheless, as no significant differences in ERG amplitudes were observed between hC3Nb1-expressing eyes and either hC3Nb1-W102A-expressing or untransduced eyes, the safety of retinal hC3Nb1-expression and alternative pathway inhibition were strongly indicated.

Therapeutic nanobodies have been investigated preclinically encoded in viral vectors, targeting organs such as the brain, the heart, and the liver.69,70,71 In the research field of AMD, a few recombinant nanobodies have been investigated, targeting angiogenesis.51,52,53 However, no previous study has assessed complement-inhibiting nanobodies for AMD, vectorized complement-inhibiting nanobodies, or applied vector-encoded nanobodies for ocular gene therapy.

Gene therapies tested in clinical trials for wAMD have mainly targeted angiogenesis72,73,74 using AAV-delivery of transgenes encoding antibodies, antibody fragments, or Fc-fusion proteins similar to approved wAMD therapeutics. Nanobodies are a compelling alternative due to their stability, low immunogenicity, and comparatively more efficient expression from a vector, potentially due to reduced dependence on post-transcriptional assembly. In contrast, the smallest human antibody fragment, the single-chain variable fragment, is difficult to engineer and often unstable in physiological environments.75 As an example, the anti-VEGF-A single-chain antibodies brolucizumab showed increased rates of intraocular inflammation following intravitreal injection. This was related to the non-natural surfaces of the single-chain antibodies format and their instability.76 Furthermore, nanobodies expand the range of targetable epitopes and can be engineered to provide a range of effector functions. Thus, this study provides an important proof-of-concept of vector-encoded nanobodies as a safe, efficient, and versatile strategy for ocular gene therapy.

The only complement-inhibiting gene therapy investigated clinically for wAMD is JNJ-1887 (formerly AAVCAGsCD59) (Hemera Biosciences/Janssen Pharmaceuticals). This subretinally delivered type 2 AAV vector encoding a soluble form of the endogenous MAC inhibitor CD59 was tested in a phase 1 trial (NCT03585556) completed in 2022, but no results have yet been reported. With a size of ∼18 kDa, sCD59 is expected to have a similar retinal diffusion profile as hC3Nb1 (∼15 kDa). The effects of these inhibitors are, however, distinct, as sCD59 inhibits MAC formation, while hC3Nb1 inhibits alternative pathway amplification, MAC formation, immune cell recruitment, and inflammation. Similar effects to hC3Nb1 were obtained with the larger-sized, targeted alternative pathway inhibitor, and fusion protein CR2-fH that directs CFH-mediated complement inhibition to locations of excessive complement activation via its CR2 entity.66 CR2-fH has been investigated preclinically and was found to reduce complement activation and CNV size in the laser-induced CNV mouse model when delivered as an AAV5-delivered VMD2-encoded gene therapy.68 A plethora of additional complement inhibitors including multitargeting therapies have been investigated preclinically for wAMD. For instance, combined inhibition of angiogenesis and complement has been attempted.77,78,79 A multitargeting treatment may result in more efficient clinical outcomes for wAMD patients as the pathology is multifactorial and highly complex.80 For instance, PEDF may be a promising anti-angiogenic agent in a multitargeting strategy.78,79,81 Notably, nanobodies are optimally suited for such multitargeting gene therapies as multiple nanobodies easily can be simultaneously accommodated within the standard AAV platform for clinical application.

The potential for hC3Nb1 as a relevant complement inhibitor to treat wAMD, geographic atrophy, or other complement-mediated diseases either as a monotherapy or as part of a multitargeting therapy requires further investigation. Overall, the appropriateness of employing a complement inhibitor for AMD treatment is supported by a vast amount of research and the recent successes of Pegcetacoplan (Syfovre) and Avacincaptad (Izerway) in reducing the geographic atrophy growth rate.27,28 Especially, the alternative pathway is indicated to play a key role in AMD based on human specimens and genetics,14,15,16,17,18,19 and in vivo experiments with knock-out models21,66 or knockdown of complement factor B.20,82 Interestingly, a study by Rohrer et al.21 showed that a simultaneous knockout of the classical pathway and lectin pathway resulted in similar protection of CNV progression in the laser-induced mouse model compared with mice with a complement factor B knockout preventing alternative pathway activation. These results support the hypothesis that the classical pathway or lectin pathway are involved in the initiation of the complement activation that the alternative pathway subsequently amplifies.

Using the subretinal injection route to deliver a gene therapy facilitates efficient gene transfer as the therapy is placed in direct apposition to the target cell. Furthermore, the gene therapy product is compartmentalized with limited systemic egress ensuring low immunogenicity. However, disadvantages include a relatively localized transduction, and the potential adverse effects related to the transient retinal detachment. The latter may be a particular concern in patients with pre-existing degeneration. Clinical trial data, however, suggest that subretinal injections are generally well tolerated in patients with neovascular AMD. In the phase 1/2A trial of RGX-314 (NCT03066258), subretinal delivery in patients with neovascular AMD was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events attributed to the delivery procedure.83 Similarly, in the AAV2-sFLT01 trial (NCT01494805), subretinal blebs were successfully created, retinal reattachment occurred rapidly, and no persistent detachments or serious complications were observed.84 Taken together, these clinical experiences indicate that, while a mechanical detachment of the retina is an inherent procedural step, subretinal delivery can be safely executed even in the context of ongoing macular pathology, when performed carefully by experienced surgeons. Notably, no detectable neutralizing antibodies were identified in the serum from mice expressing hC3Nb1, suggesting that long-term expression of nanobodies in the retina has low immunogenicity.

To confirm that subretinal injections and nanobody expression did not negatively impact retinal function, ERG recordings were performed after injections. ERG recordings were not performed at the final time point after CNV induction, although the Rohrer lab has shown that the size of the CNV lesion correlated with the loss of retinal function.66 Another limitation of this study is that only a structural marker for outcomes post-CNV induction was used. Molecular or cellular indicators—such as markers of complement activation, angiogenesis, or inflammation—could provide complementary insights that would help disentangle true complement-mediated effects from effects on angiogenesis pathways independent of complement inhibition. Localization of hC3Nb1 within the retina, to the choriocapillaris, or within the choroid could provide further insights on the biodistribution and the fate of nanobodies secreted by the RPE.

In this proof-of-concept study, a gene therapy-based strategy encoding the alternative pathway inhibitor hC3Nb1 was validated as a potential therapeutic option for treating CNV as seen in wAMD. With this article, we are the first to show the therapeutic use of vector-based nanobodies expressed in the eye. Retinal expression of the nanobody was efficient, sustained, and well-tolerated. The secreted hC3Nb1 effectively inhibited alternative pathway amplification, supporting its role in complement pathway suppression and its association with the reduction in laser-induced CNV. These findings suggest that vector-mediated delivery of hC3Nb1, which specifically targets the alternative pathway, holds promise as a therapeutic approach for the treatment of wAMD. More broadly, this strategy highlights the potential of nanobody-based gene therapy as a versatile platform for managing ocular diseases.

Materials and methods

Vector construction

For the generation of nanobody-encoding vectors, synthetic DNAs were ordered (Genscript, Piscataway, NJ, USA) encoding an upstream Kozak sequence, an amino (N)-terminal signal peptide, followed by a nanobody gene (hC3Nb1,39 hC3Nb1-W102A, hC3Nb2,57 or C4Nb-270 [C4Nb] [patent no. US 12,110,321 B2 - Oct. 8, 2024, Laursen et al.]) and flanking restriction sites; BamHI (5′) and NheI (3′). We used the commonly used murine Ig kappa (Igk) signal peptide for mammalian cells; METDTLLLWVLLLWVPGSTGD. HA-, His-, or Myc-tag-encoding oligos were ordered (Eurofins, Luxembourg) with EcoRI (5′) and BshTI (3′) overhangs for C-terminal addition to the nanobody genes (Table S1). The gene cassettes and tags were inserted into pcDNA3.1+ expression vectors using BamHI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), NheI (Thermo Fisher Scientific), EcoRI-HF (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), and BshTI (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For IRES-based expression vectors, the IRES-eGFP sequence was PCR-amplified from a pHIV7-IRES-eGFP plasmid85 with XmaI-tagged primers (Table S1) using the Phusion hot start Flex (New England Biolabs) and cloned into pcDNA3.1-Igk-Nb-HA plasmids (pcDNA-CMV-Igk-Nb-HA-IRES-eGFP) or an empty pcDNA plasmid (pcDNA-CMV-IRES-eGFP), using the XmaI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and BshTI restrictions sites.

For the generation of IRES-based LVs, the gene cassettes (Igk-Nb-HA-IRES-eGFP) were transferred into the third-generation pCCL-based lentiviral transfer vector pLV-PGK-MCS (multiple cloning site),86 using the restriction sites of XbaI (Thermo Fisher Scientific), XmaI, NheI, and BshTI.

Dual-promoter LVs were cloned using the three following back-to-back promoter variants: pLV-CMV-intron+PGK-eGFP, pLV-EF1a-intron+PGK-eGFP, or pLV-VMD2-intron+PGK-eGFP.87 The Igk-hC3Nb1-HA gene cassette was PCR-amplified with BgIII- and Xho-tagged primers (Table S1) and replaced eGFP downstream of the PGK promoter using BgIII (New England Biolabs) and XhoI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) restriction sites, while introns were replaced with eGFP, using restrictions sites of XmaI and SalI.

Cell cultures

HEK293 (CRL-1573, American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA), Lenti X (#632180, Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan), and human melanoma cells59,88 were cultivated at 37°C and 5%, v/v, CO2 in 5%–10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) Glutamax (31966-021, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 1% 100× Penicillin-Streptomycin (ECB3001D, EuroClone, Pero, Italy). ARPE-19 (CRL-2302, ATCC) were cultivated at 37°C and 5%, v/v, CO2 in 10% FBS in DMEM (11995073) 1% Antibiotic-Antimycotic (15240062, Gibco).

Detection of nanobody and eGFP in transfected or transduced HEK293 cells using western blot analysis

HEK293 cells were seeded at 20%–25% confluency in 6-well plates and transfected with 1.5 μg DNA (in a 3:1 ratio of μL X-tremeGENE 9 [Merck, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA] to μg DNA), or melanoma cells were seeded at 30% confluency and transduced with LVs at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 3.6. Two days after transfections or 5 days after transductions, fluorescence microscopy was performed on live cells, and the medium was harvested and passed through 0.2 μm filters (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany), while cells were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) Lysis and extraction buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with cOmplete, Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, Basel, Schweiz) (RIPA+). Protein concentrations were determined, and cell lysates were loaded (10 μg total protein per lane) in 4%–15% Criterion TGX Stain-Free Protein Gels (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Gels were run for ∼30 min at 200 V and proteins were blotted onto 0.2 μm polyvinylidene difluoride membranes for 5 min (2.5 A, 25 V) using a Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer system (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature (RT) in 5% skim milk powder (VWR Chemicals, Radnor, PA, USA) (w/v) TBS-T (TBS with 0.1% Tween 20). Overnight (ON) incubations were performed at 4°C with either 1:1,000 anti-HA (mouse, ab130275, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) (Figures 1, S1, and S2), 1:10,000 anti-HA (rabbit, C29F4, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) (Figures 2, 3, 4, and S4), or 1:10,000 anti-GFP (rabbit, ab290-50, 5 mg/mL, Abcam) primary antibody. After washing, the membranes were subsequently incubated for 1 h at RT with a secondary antibody, either horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse (P0447, 1 mg/mL, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) or goat anti-rabbit antibody (P0448, 0.25 mg/mL, Dako), diluted 1:10,000 in TBS-T. Clarity Western ECL Substrate (179–5060, Bio-Rad) was used for detection, and imaging was performed with the ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad). Western blots in Figures 1, 3, and 4 are shown in full size in Figure S7.

Dot blot analysis detecting secreted nanobodies from ARPE-19 monolayers

Media were gathered from the apical and basal transwell compartments 1 and 5 weeks following the seeding of ARPE-19 cells. 50 μL of media was loaded into a 96-well plate using the Bio-Dot Microfiltration Apparatus (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and a 0.45 μm nitrocellulose blotting membrane (Amersham Protran, 10600016, Cytiva, Amersham, Great Britain). Vacuum was applied to pull the samples through, and suction was left on for 45 min. After this step, the membrane was blocked, washed, and stained with antibodies as described in the western blot analysis section, using 1:10,000 anti-HA primary antibody (rabbit, C29F4, Cell Signaling Technology) and an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody. Signal was detected using chemiluminescence and captured on film.

Detection of nanobodies in transfected HEK293 cells using immunostaining

HEK293 cells were seeded at 5%–25% confluency in slide flasks and transfected with XG9 and 1.5 μg DNA on Day 2. After 48 h, cells were fixed for 30 min in 10% formalin buffer (Hounisen, Skanderborg, Denmark) and incubated for 30 min in cold ethanol. Slides were incubated for 1 h with 1:400 anti-HA (mouse, ab130275, Abcam) in 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (422351S, VWR Chemicals) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (diluted from a 10× solution X0515-500, Dulbecco’s PBS containing 1.37M NaCl, 27mM KCl, 43mM Na2HP04, and 14.7 mM KH2PO4, Biowest, Nuaillé, France), followed by 1 h of incubation with 1:400 goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (488, A-11029, 2 mg/mL, Alexa Flour, Invitrogen, Thermo Fischer) in 0.5% BSA in PBS protected from light. Slides were stained with DAPI (Diamino-2-phenylindol, D9564, Sigma-Aldrich), washed, dipped in 70% ethanol, dried, and mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Complement-depositions assay

Complement activation was assessed as deposited C3 fragments in a time-resolved immunofluorometric assay. NHS was added to zymosan or IgG-coated 96-well maxisorp plates (#446612, Nunc, ThermoFisher), initiating the alternative or classical pathway on the two surfaces, as described earlier.39,57 Briefly, NHS was mixed 1:1 (v/v) with potential complement-inhibiting nanobodies before being loaded on plates. Either recombinant control nanobodies (diluted in TBS) or cell-secreted nanobodies in DMEM harvested 48 h after transfection of HEK293 cells (see above) were mixed with NHS. Recombinant hC3Nb1 and recombinant c4Nb were used as positive controls for the alternative pathway or classical pathway, respectively, and recombinant hC3Nb1-W102A was the negative control in both assays. Two-fold dilutions series were prepared for each nanobody using pathway-specific 10 mM HEPES buffers. The alternative pathway buffer contained 10 mM EGTA (0732-50G, VWR Chemicals), 5 mM Mg2+, and 140 mM NaCl, and the classical pathway buffer included 2 mM Ca2+, 1 mM Mg2+, and 140 mM NaCl. The alternative pathway buffer contained Mg2+ required by complement factor B, and EGTA was included to bind Ca2+, which is vital for classical pathway and lectin pathway recognition.89 Ca2+ and Mg2+ were included in the classical pathway buffer as it is crucial for C2 and C4 cleavage. The buffers were furthermore used to dilute NHS to obtain a ratio of 1/18 in the alternative pathway samples, and 1/500 in the classical pathway samples. The NHS-nanobody mix was incubated for 30 min on ice. Dilution series were loaded in technical duplicates in their respective 96-well plates, and plates were incubated for 1.5 h at 37°C for complement activation. Between each of the following incubations, three TBS-T (TBS with 0.5% Tween 20) washes were performed. For the classical pathway plate, TBS-T was supplemented with Ca2+. After incubation and wash, the anti-C3d-biotin primary antibody (rabbit, A00063, Dako) was added at 0.5 μg/mL and incubated ON at 4°C. EU3+-labeled streptavidin (1244-360, DELFIA, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) was subsequently loaded 1:1000 in 25 μM EDTA TBS-T and incubated 1 h at RT. Enhancement solution (ampq99800-100, Ampliqon, Odense, Denmark) was added to allow efficient Eu3+ signal, and time-resolved fluorescence was measured after 5 min incubation.

Each assay was repeated thrice, and for each assay run, an NHS dilution series was included to ensure proper performance of the assay. Averages for each data point were normalized to the equal point of dilution for the hC3Nb1-W102A medium group.

Lentiviral production and titer measurement

Lenti-X cells were used for the production of 3rd generation LVs. Crude LV preps were produced for the in vitro study and ultracentrifuged LV preps were prepared for the in vivo studies essentially as earlier described.86 Crude LVs were produced by seeding 3.5 × 106 Lenti X cells per p10 dish. CaCl2 co-transfections were performed on day 2, adding 10.8 μg pMDLg/pRRE (gag/pol), 2.5 μg pRSV-Rev (rev), 3.1 μg pMD.2G (VSV-G), 10.8 μg transfer vector, 62.5 μL 2 M CaCl2 (21115-100mL, Sigma Aldrich), 500 μL 2× Hepes-buffered saline (HBS) (#J62623, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and H2O in a total volume of 1000 μL per dish. On day 3, medium was changed to 2% FBS and 1 mM sodium butyrate DMEM, and harvest was performed 1 day later. The supernatant was gathered and filtered through 0.45 μm filters (83.1826, Sarstedt), and aliquots were stored at −80°C.

For each ultracentrifuged LV prep, 1 × 107 Lenti X cells were seeded in 18× p15 plates. On day 2, CaCl2 co-transfections were performed with 29.3 μg pMDLg/pRRE, 6.8 μg pRSV-Rev, 8.4 μg pMD.2G (VSV-G), 29.3 μg transfer vector, 140.6 μL 2M CaCl2, 1125.3 μL 2× HBS, and H2O in a total volume of 2,250 μL per dish. After 20 h of incubation at 37°C, the medium was changed to 2% FBS and 1 mM sodium butyrate DMEM. On day 4, supernatants were harvested from the first 9 plates, filtered through 0.45 μm filters (Sarstedt), and 29 mL was added to 6× Ramcon centrifuge tubes (358126, Beckman coulter, Brea, CA, USA). A 5 mL 20% sucrose (84100, Fluka Analytical – Honeywell, Charlotte, NC, USA) cushion was carefully placed on the bottom. Tubes were ultracentrifuged at 25,000 rpm and 4°C for 2 h (Sorvall WX+, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The process was repeated with the remaining 9× p15 supernatants reusing the Ramcon tubes. 80 μL HBSS (14175-046, GibcoTM, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added and stored ON at 4°C. LV preps were resuspended, pooled, and spun at max speed for 5 min. The supernatant was aliquoted in stocks and placed at −80°C.

The qPCR-derived LV titer was determined by quantifying the number of integrated proviruses (vector copy number [VCN]), following transduction with a fixed volume in a known number of cells. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was analyzed with qPCR relating the number of WPRE copies to the albumin gene.

HEK293 cells were seeded in 6-well plates; at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well, analyzing three wells per LV prep analyzed, and at least two control wells. On day 2, the cell number of the control wells was counted to determine the number of cells at transduction. Then, 20 μL, 100 μL, and 500 μL of each crude LV or 150× diluted ultracentrifuged LV prep were added to each well for transduction. On day 3, medium was changed, and on day 6, cells were harvested and gDNA was isolated following the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit protocol (#69504, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). qPCR samples were prepared in triplicates in 96-well plates following the Manufacturer’s protocol for Maxima Probe qPCR Master mix (#K0261, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

qPCR standard curves for albumin and WPRE (encoded in pCCL-based vectors) were prepared for the copy number analysis with the same template DNA dilution series using pCCL+Alb; this plasmid was generated from pCCL-PGK-MCS86 by removing the PGK promoter using flanking BsiWI and XhoI sites and replacing it with the 150 bp human albumin PCR amplicon using pAlb as template (Addgene #22037).

Albumin and WPRE primers and light-sensitive probes were added to the samples, and qPCR was run on the LightCycler 480 instrument (Roche). Mean numbers of WPRE and albumin copies were determined for each sample therefore the VCN per cell. Based on the number of cells counted at the time of transduction and the three VCNs obtained for each LV volume, the number of infectious units (IU) present at transduction could be calculated for the three volumes. The resulting numbers of IU were divided by the respective volume added, and the obtained titers were averaged.

Complement-mediated stress assay–TEER

ARPE-19 cells (passage 28) were seeded 2 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific). When the cells were approximately 70%–80% confluent, they were transduced with the LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 (MOI of 25) diluted in 8 μg/mL polybrene, 10% FBS DMEM. The medium was changed after 24 h, and eventually, the cells were split and frozen in aliquots as a new stable cell line (ARPE-19-hC3Nb1).

Untransduced ARPE-19 or ARPE-19-hC3Nb1 were seeded 1 × 105 cells/well on 0.4 μm pore polyester Transwell membranes (CLS3460, Corning, Sigma-Aldrich) in 12-well plates. Monolayers were grown for 4 weeks, to form a stable, polarized monolayer. Initially, cells were seeded and expanded in 500 μL 10% FBS DMEM medium apically and 1000 μL of 2% FBS medium basally (10%/2%). Medium was changed twice a week. After the cells reached confluency, the serum amount was decreased each week; from 10%/2% to 2%/2%, followed by 2 weeks of 1%/1% until monolayer stability was established. Expressed and secreted nanobodies were allowed to build up in the medium for 5 days until baseline TEER measurements (0 h) and the subsequent induction of stress. Values were measured in ohm/cm2 with an Epithelial Voltohmmeter (EVOM, World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA) and a TEER chopstick electrode (STX2, World Precision Instruments). Before every measurement, the electrode was stabilized in RT medium, and the 12-well plate stabilized in RT for 5 min with lid, 5 min without lid, and placed on a heating pad at 37°C, keeping the temperature stable as the TEER measurement is temperature-sensitive. The background TEER value was measured on an empty transwell without cells and subtracted from all measurements (107 ohm/cm2). For each well, triplicate measurements were obtained and averaged to obtain the overall well TEER. Complement-dependent H2O2 stress90 was induced in half of the wells by adding 500 μM H2O2 (216763, Sigma-Aldrich) and 25% NHS (AB, A13003).60 Non-stressed wells had an equal volume of DMEM added. Each treatment group consisted of 6 wells. Following the induction of complement stress, TEER was remeasured at 4 and 24 h after stress. The 4- and 24-h value for each well was normalized to the 0-h value to provide a % of baseline TEER, and the unstressed ARPE-19 group was set to 100%.

Animals

Mice were housed in facilities with a 12/12-h light/dark cycle, with unrestricted access to water and food, and were handled in accordance with the Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research from the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO).

Assessment of retinal nanobody expression following subretinal LV injections

Eight-to nine-week-old male C57BL/6JRj (Janvier, Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France) mice were kept at the Animal Facility of the Department of Biomedicine, Aarhus University, Denmark, with approval from The Danish Animal Inspectorate (case# 2020-15-0201–00745). Mice were anesthetized intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 60–80 mg/kg ketamine (Ketador, Richter Pharma AG, Wels, Austria) and 1 mg/kg medetomidine hydrochloride (Domitor, ScanVet Animal Health A/S, Fredensborg, Denmark). To prevent possible pain after subretinal injections, 5 mg/kg carprofen (Rimadyl Bovis Vet, Zoetis Finland Oy, Helsinki, Finland) was injected subcutaneously. Eyes were dilated with 1% tropicamide solution (Mydriacyl, Alcon Nordic A/S, Copenhagen, Denmark), and lubricated with carbomer eye gel (Viscotears, 2 mg/g, Alcon Nordic). Using an OPMI 1 FR PRO Surgical microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany), a small incision was made posterior to the limbus with a 30-gauge (G) needle for anterior transscleral subretinal injections91; a blunt 35G needle affixed to a 10 μL microinjection syringe with Siliflex tubing (World Precision Instruments) was inserted through the vitreous cavity and perforating the retina, whereafter 1.6 μL LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1, LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1-W102A, or HBSS were slowly delivered. Anesthesia was reversed with 0.6–0.8 mg/kg atipamezole hydrochloride (Anti-Sedan, Orion Pharma, Copenhagen, Denmark). LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 was injected in 13 mice (10 eyes with 1 × 104 IU and 16 eyes with 2 × 104 IU); LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1-W102A was injected in 10 mice (10 eyes with 1 × 104 IU and 10 eyes with 2 × 104 IU); while 3 mice (6 eyes) were injected with HBSS.

Normal and fluorescence fundoscopic assessments, in addition to OCT evaluations, were conducted using the Micron IV (Phoenix Research Laboratories, Pleasanton, CA, USA) to validate retinal reattachment following subretinal injection and transduction efficiency.

Detection of nanobodies and eGFP in neuroretina/RPE/choroidal lysates using western blot analysis

Mice were anesthetized and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Eyes were enucleated and neuroretina/RPE/choroids or neuroretinal tissues were isolated from the eye cups and placed in 100 μL RIPA+ lysis buffer with a small bead in 1.5 mL safe-lock Eppendorf tubes (0030121.589, Eppendorf) on ice. The tissues were homogenized in the Bullet Blender (Next Advance, Inc., Troy, NY, USA); for 3 × 30 s using the setting 6 with 2 min ice breaks in between. The homogenates were centrifuged for 10 min at 13,000 g at 6°C, and supernatants were saved for total protein concentration analysis. Western blotting was performed as earlier described, detecting HA-tagged nanobodies and eGFP, loading 22.5 μg neuroretina/RPE/choroid total protein or 45 μg neuroretina total protein.

Immunostaining of nanobody and eGFP in RPE/choroidal flatmounts

Eyes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (9713.1000, VWR Chemicals) for 1 h at RT or ON at 4°C, followed by numerous washes in PBS. As previously described,92 the eye cup were isolated, neuroretina removed, and 8 relaxing cuts were made to flatten the tissue. Flatmounts were blocked ON in permeabilization-blocking buffer (PBB); 0.5% Triton X-100 (93443, Sigma-Aldrich), 4% BSA in PBS, at 4°C with gentle rocking. Anti-HA (rabbit, C29F4, Cell Signaling Technology) was diluted 1:800 in PBB and incubated ON at 4°C while rocking gently. Next, flatmounts were washed in 0.5% Triton X-100 PBB, and 1:400 goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 568, A11011, 2 mg/mL, Invitrogen) was added for incubation 2 h at RT with gently rocking. After extensive washing, tissues were mounted on slides with Fluoromount-G (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and cover slipped. Images were obtained using an Olympus BX63 Fluorescence microscope with the CellSens Software.

Immunostaining of nanobody and eGFP in murine retinal sections

Mice were sacrificed 3- and 8-weeks after subretinal injections of LVs or HBSS. As previously described,93 eyes were enucleated and immediately placed in 4% PFA (9713.1000, VWR Chemicals) for ON fixation at 4°C. The Department of Pathology, Aarhus University Hospital (Aarhus, Denmark) performed paraffin embedding and sectioning. Sections of 2 μm were placed on Super Frost Plus object glasses, dried at a minimum of 60 min at 60°C or ON at 37°C, and kept at 4°C. Before staining, sections were incubated ON in xylene, followed by washes in 99%, 96%, 70% ethanol, and distilled H2O. Antigen retrieval was performed by incubating slides in citrate (pH 6.0) for nanobody detection or Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) for eGFP detection for 10 min while boiling. Sections were washed and blocked in 3% BSA, 1% Triton X-100 PBS for 1 h at RT. Primary antibody incubations were performed with 1:800 Anti-HA (rabbit, C29F4, Cell Signaling Technology) or 1:100 anti-GFP (G10362, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 1% BSA 0.5% Triton X-100 PBS ON at 4°C. Slides were washed in PBS and incubated for 30 min at RT with 1:400 secondary antibody mixes: Goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated with either Alexa Fluor 568 (red) for nanobody detection or Alexa Fluor 488 (green) (A11034, Invitrogen) for eGFP detection. DAPI staining was performed for 3 min followed by washing. TrueBlack (Cell Signaling Technology) diluted 1/20 in 70% ethanol was added for 60 s to quench autofluorescence. Slides were carefully dipped in PBS and left to dry before mounting with Fluoromount-G (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Detection of anti-nanobody antibodies

To examine if antibodies against hC3NB1 were present, the hC3Nb194 was coated in MaxiSorp microtiter wells at 1 μg/mL, diluted in PBS. After overnight incubation, the wells were emptied, and 1 mg/mL of HSA was added to block residual binding sites. After 1 h of incubation, the wells were washed with TBS/Tween (TBS with 0.05% Tween 20). Mouse serum samples obtained 7 weeks post-injection were diluted in TBS/Tween at 1/25, 1/100, and 1/400 and incubated in the wells overnight. After washing with TBS/Tween biotin anti-mouse IgG (cat. no. 553441, BD Bioscience, Herlev, Denmark) at 0.5 μg/mL in TBS/Tween with 50 μg/mL of normal rabbit IgG (to reduce background) were added to the wells. Positive control wells were instead incubated with biotinylated anti-alpaca VHH antibody (A01995, Genscript), diluted 1/1000. After 2 h of incubation, the wells were washed, and europium-labeled streptavidin (1244–360, PerkinElmer) was added. After 1 h of incubation and washing, enhancement buffer was added (AMPLIQON, AMPQ99800.1000), and the signals read by time-resolved fluorometry using a VICTOR5 Plate Reader (PerkinElmer).

Laser-induced CNV mouse model

Nine-week-old C57BL/6J (Strain#000664, Jackson, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) mice were integrated into the animal facility at the Medical University of South Carolina, Charleston, South Carolina, USA, to obtain similar microenvironments to previously published experiments. The experiment was approved by the Medical University of South Carolina Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. For subretinal injections, anesthesia was performed i.p. with ketamine and xylazine (80 mg/kg ketamine [NDC 0143-9509-10; Hikima Pharmaceuticals, Berkeley Heights, NJ, USA], and 10 mg/kg xylazine [NDC 46066-750-02; Pivetal, Liberty, MO, USA]) and pupils were dilated using 2.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride (NDC 17478-201-15, Akorn, Lake Forest, IL, USA) and 1% atropine sulfate (NDC 17478-215-15; Akorn), as previously described.68 The ocular surface was anesthetized with Proparacaine hydrochloride ophthalmic solution (0.5% sterile, NDC24208-730-06, Bausch and Lomb, Laval, Canada), and lubricated with GenTeal Tears gel (787130-1218, Alcon laboratories, TX, USA). Transcorneal subretinal injections95 of 1 μL of HBSS, LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1, or LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1-W102A (9.1 × 104 IU) were performed using a 30½G disposable needle through the superior cornea to create a guide hole and a blunt 33G needle (7803-05, style point 3, 12 mm, Hamilton) on a 5 μL Hamilton syringe (7634-01, Hamilton, NV, USA) for injection. Neomycin polymyxin B sulfates and Dexamethasone ointment were applied to prevent infection (NDC 61314-631-36; Sandoz, Princeton, NJ, USA). Anesthesia was reversed with Anti-Sedan and GenTeal night-time ointment (281960-1218, Alcon laboratories) was added to eyes for lubrication. LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1 and LV/VMD2-eGFP+PGK-hC3Nb1-W102A were injected in 10 mice (20 eyes) each, 5 mice (10 eyes) were injected with HBSS, and 5 mice (10 eyes) were left uninjected. Fundoscopic assessments were performed 4 and 6 weeks post-injection with the Micron III (Phoenix Research Laboratories) to validate injection success and stable eGFP expression. The Envisu OCT system (R2210, Bioptigen, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) was used to confirm retinal reattachments 4 weeks post-injection and for CNV evaluation 7 weeks post-injection. Eyes with cataracts, prior retinal defects, significant injection damage, lack of eGFP detection in transduced eyes, or failure of retinal reattachment were excluded from all analyses.

Six weeks following subretinal injections, mice were anesthetized, pupils were dilated, and the image-guided laser photocoagulation system (Micron III, Phoenix Research Laboratories) was used to generate laser spots (frequency-doubled Nd:YAG laser, 532 nm, 100 μm spot size, 70 ms duration, 200 mW). Four CNVs were laser-induced per eye surrounding the optic nerve. Bubble formation in the laser spot indicated a successful rupture of Bruch’s membrane. Seven days following laser-induced CNV, spectral-domain OCT images were obtained before mice were sacrificed for tissue collection. The lesion size was assessed by acquiring rectangular volume scan images at 1.8 × 1.8 mm consisting of 11 OCT b-scans (each consisting of 1000 a-scans), averaged by five replicates (1000 × 11 × 5 × 1, 3 s). The InVivoVue OCT software (Leica) was used to generate b-scan images from a series of a-scans to determine the center of the lesion, as described earlier.68 The en-face reconstruction tool is then used to produce volume projections, enabling measurements of the area of the hyperreflective spot produced on the fundus image using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). CNVs with a lack of proper bubble formation following laser-induction and/or with excessive damage to the RPE and choroid were excluded.

Electroretinography

Five weeks post-injection, mice were dark-adapted ON for scotopic full-field ERG recordings to assess the safety of LV transduction, nanobody expression, and alternative pathway inhibition. Mice were anesthetized and eyes were dilated and kept lubricated with Refresh celluvisc (NDC0023-4554-30) to allow for electrical contact between the electrode and the cornea. A ground electrode was placed in the tail, a reference needle in the cheek, and corneal loop electrodes on each eye. A-, b-, and c-waves were recorded using the UTAS E−4000 system (LKC Technologies, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). A- and b-waves were obtained following 10 μs single-flashes of white light with varying light intensities (exposures: 0.4, −0.6, −1.6, −2.6 log[cd∗s∗m−2]). Amplitudes were determined by the automatic placement of cursors in the EMWIN software provided with the UTAS system; the peak a-wave amplitude was measured from the baseline to the initial negative-going voltage, while the peak b-wave amplitude was measured from the bottom of the a-wave to the peak of the positive b-wave. C-waves were obtained for electrical RPE response assessment as an average of five 4-s light exposures of 100 cd∗s∗m−2 (2 log[cd∗s∗m−2]), with 60 s between flashes and measured from baseline to peak.96

C3d detection on RPE/choroidal flatmounts

Enucleated eyes were fixed in 4% PFA ON at 4°C, washed, and cut flat as described above. Eyes were blocked ON in Tris-B buffer: 5% donkey serum (D9663, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.1% Triton X-100 PBS. Flatmounts were incubated with 1:150 anti-C3 primary antibody (ab11862, 11H9, Abcam) in Tris-B ON at 4°C. Washes were performed with 0.1% Triton X-100 PBS, and 1:200 donkey anti-rat secondary antibody in PBS was used for 1 h of incubation at RT. Following washes, slides were mounted and covered. Overviews of flatmounts were stitched together by images obtained at 10× magnification with the Keyence BZ-X710 microscope (Keyence Corporation of America, Itasca, IL, USA). C3d positive CNV areas and intensities were determined with ImageJ. The total C3d intensity was assessed inside a marked area corresponding to the CNV lesion (as seen in representative dotted lines in Figure 4). The C3d-positive area inside each CNV lesion was determined by (black and white) binarization of images at a set threshold, and the area of the subsequent lesions was measured.

Statistics

Statistics were performed in GraphPad (Prism 10). Normal distribution was tested with QQ plots and several build-in tests (D’Agostino & Pearson, Anderson-Darling, Shapiro-Wilk, Kolmogorov-Smirnov), and no data were non-parametric. To compare data in multiple treatment groups, the ordinary one-way ANOVA was performed with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test if the variance (SD) of data was similar. If the data were normally distributed and had significantly different SDs, the Welch and Brown-Forsythe ANOVA test was performed, followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test. Furthermore, the two-way ANOVA and Šídák’s multiple comparisons test were applied for the TEER measurements. Significance is depicted; ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
