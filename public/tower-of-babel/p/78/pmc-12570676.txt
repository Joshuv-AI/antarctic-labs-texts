Prophage landscape in Enterococcus faecium: diversity, resistance genes, virulence factors, and endolysin profiling
Authors: Zhiqian Wang, Shuo Gao, Yan Zhang, Han Shen, Xiaoli Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnterococcus faecium is a major opportunistic pathogen associated with healthcare-associated infections and increasing antimicrobial resistance. Prophages play critical roles in bacterial evolution by mediating horizontal gene transfer, but a comprehensive analysis of prophages in E. faecium has not been performed.MethodsA total of 495 complete E. faecium genomes were retrieved from the NCBI database. Prophages were identified and classified using PHASTEST and PhaGCN2.3 software. The interactions between prophages within host genomes were analyzed using logistic regression. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR), virulence factor (VF), and endolysin genes within intact prophages were characterized using Abricate, the VFDB, and the CARD databases. Comparative analyses were conducted between human- and animal-origin strains.ResultsA total of 2119 prophages were detected, of which 1628 were intact. Nearly all E. faecium strains (99.4%) harbored at least one prophage, with an average of 4.2 prophages per genome. Staphy_SPbeta_like (26.4%) and Lister_2389 (22.1%) were the main types of prophages. Exclusion was most prevalent pattern, and specific prophages exhibiting varying interaction profiles. Classification revealed that most prophages belonged to the Bronfenbrennervirinae subfamily and the Herelleviridae family. Among intact prophages, 20.4% carried AMR genes and 1.4% harbored VF gene, primarily the adhesin-encoding gene ecbA. Endolysin genes, detected in 59.6% of prophages, exhibited high sequence diversity. Prophage distribution and types varied significantly among different ST strains, with prophage types showing distinct patterns in carrying AMR, VF, and endolysin-encoding genes. No significant differences in AMR, VF, or endolysin genes were observed between prophages of human and animal origins.ConclusionsThis study provides the first comprehensive genomic characterization of prophages in E. faecium, revealing their abundance, diversity, and potential roles in resistance, virulence, and evolution. These findings highlight the importance of prophages in shaping the pathogenicity and adaptability of E. faecium and underscore the need for further functional investigations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12172-x.

Introduction

Enterococcus faecium is a Gram-positive bacterium widely distributed in the intestinal microbiota of humans and animals, and is recognized as an opportunistic pathogen [1, 2]. E. faecium can cause various infections, including urinary tract infections, intra-abdominal infections, bacteremia, and meningitis, particularly in immunocompromised hosts or under other predisposing conditions [3, 4]. In recent years, E. faecium, especially vancomycin-resistant E. faecium (VREfm), has gained increasing attention and is becoming a significant global public health concern. VREfm is typically resistant to multiple antibiotics and has a strong ability to acquire new antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes through horizontal gene transfer, posing major challenges to clinical treatment and infection control [5, 6].

Prophages are viruses that infect bacteria. Based on their life cycles, phages are classified into virulent and temperate phages [7, 8]. Upon infection, virulent phages enter the lytic cycle and eventually lyse the host cell to release progeny phages [8, 9]. In contrast, temperate phages can integrate into the host genome, establishing a lysogenic cycle during which the phage genome, now called a prophage, replicates alongside the host genome [9, 10]. Under certain conditions, prophages may excise from the host genome and re-enter the lytic cycle, resulting in bacterial lysis and the release of viral progeny [8]. Due to their ability to target and lyse bacteria, phages have unique advantages in antimicrobial therapy [11]. Notably, phages encode endolysins, peptidoglycan hydrolases that disrupt bacterial cell walls, offering a promising alternative to traditional antibiotics [12].

In addition to their therapeutic potential, phages contribute to horizontal gene transfer, promoting bacterial genetic diversity and evolution [13, 14]. Studies have shown that prophage-like elements or remnants are integral components of bacterial genomes, profoundly influencing bacterial adaptability and evolutionary trajectories [15, 16]. Understanding the complex relationship between phages and host bacteria is crucial for elucidating bacterial evolution and developing novel infection control strategies. However, studies on prophages in E. faecium are still limited. Existing research has shown that phage therapy and phage-derived endolysins can be effective against E. faecium and VREfm infections [17–19]. Several studies have explored prophages in E. faecium from genomic perspectives, including characterization of prophage genomes and prophage-encoded functional gene [20–22]. Nonetheless, comprehensive large-scale analyses of prophage diversity in E. faecium remain limited.

This study aimed to characterize the diversity of prophages in E. faecium, investigate the distribution of AMR genes, virulence factor (VF) genes, and endolysin sequences within prophages, and compare differences between animal- and human-origin strains. Our findings provide new insights into the role of prophages in the adaptability, pathogenicity, and evolution of E. faecium, and may inform novel approaches for the prevention and treatment of antibiotic-resistant infections.

Methods

Collection of E. faecium genomes

A total of 495 complete genomes of E. faecium were retrieved from the NCBI database, of which 70.9% (351/495) were of human origin, 6.7% (33/495) were of animal origin, and the remaining genomes had unknown origins (as of October 2024). The accession numbers of all E. faecium genomes, along with background information for each strain obtained using a Python script, are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Sequence types (STs) were determined using MLST version 2.22.0, which scans genome sequences against the traditional PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/) typing schemes. The mlst database was updated using PubMLST allelic profiles and sequences (https://pubmlst.org/data) accessed on February 9, 2025. Results are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Prophage prediction

Prophages were predicted using the PHASTEST web server (https://phastest.ca/, accessed October 2024) [23]. The PHASTEST ‘score’ represents an overall measure of prophage completeness, which is calculated based on the presence of essential structural and functional modules such as integrase, capsid, head, tail, and lysis-related genes. Based on scoring criteria, PHASTEST classifies prophages as incomplete (score < 70), questionable (70 ≤ score ≤ 90), or intact (score >90). Based on PHASTEST analysis, each prophage type was named according to the phage showing the highest number of matching proteins in the database. Only intact prophages were included in subsequent analyses.

Correlations and anticorrelations between the presence of prophages within a single host genome

To analyze the co-occurrence and exclusion patterns of prophages within a single host genome, we followed the approach of Stefanic et al. by constructing a presence-absence matrix for each prophage type [24]. Logistic regression was used, modeling the presence of one prophage as the dependent variable and the presence of another prophage as the independent variable. The β coefficient from the regression analysis indicates the direction and strength of the relationship: a positive β (β >0) and P-value < 0.05 indicate significant co-occurrence, while a negative β (β < 0) and P-value < 0.05 suggest significant mutual exclusion. When P ≥ 0.05, no significant relationship is considered, and the prophages are regarded as independent.

Identification of VF and AMR genes

VF genes and AMR genes within prophages were identified using Abricate version 1.0.1 with the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB, https://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/download.htm, accessed and updated December 2024) and Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD, version 4.0.0, https://card.mcmaster.ca/download, accessed and updated December 2024) [25, 26]. The search parameters were set with the following cutoffs: E-value ≤ 1 × 10⁻²⁰, identity ≥ 80%, and coverage ≥ 80%.

Prophage annotation and classification

Prophages were annotated using Pharokka version 1.7.3, which utilizes PHANOTATE to predict coding sequences (CDS) and the PHROGs database for standardized functional annotation (E-value cutoff: 1 × 10⁻⁵) [27–29]. Prophages were classified with PhaGCN2 version 2.3.0 based on the latest ICTV taxonomy (MSL39_v1, https://ictv.global/vmr) [30].

Endolysin identification and phylogenetic analysis

Endolysin genes were identified from annotated prophages using custom Python scripts. The search for “endolysin” in our study was specifically limited to this term, which encompasses a broad range of peptidoglycan hydrolases, including muramidases, amidases, N-acetylmuramoyl-L-alanine amidases, and N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidases. These enzymes share functional similarities and are categorized under the common label of “endolysin” in the PHROG database, which provides standardized nomenclature for protein functions, grouping similar proteins under unified categories [29]. To ensure consistency, we used the PHROG database’s functional categories for endolysins, which integrate various peptidoglycan hydrolases into one group. The scripts for endolysin identification, which search for “endolysin” in the “product” field of the annotation files, have been uploaded as supplementary materials. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT version 7.526, and a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree version 2.1.11 [31, 32]. Trees were visualized with iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/) [33].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, while continuous variables were analyzed using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. When Chi-square tests showed statistical significance, residual analysis was used to detect significant individual associations between variables. Residuals with absolute values of 3 or greater were considered to be contributing a significant amount to the χ² value [34]. Statistical analyses and graphical visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 9, SPSS 26.0, and R version 4.4.3. Graph construction, data manipulation, plotting, and network visualization were performed using the R packages igraph version 2.1.4, dplyr version 1.1.4, ggplot2 version 3.5.2, and ggraph version 2.2.2, respectively [35–38]. Heatmap visualization and data processing were conducted using reshape2 version 1.4.4, readxl version 1.4.5, scales version 1.4.0, and extrafont version 0.19 [39–42].

Results

Prevalence of prophages in the genomes of E. faecium

A total of 2119 prophages were identified across 495 E. faecium genomes, comprising 1628 intact prophages, 429 questionable prophages, and 62 incomplete prophages (Fig. 1A). The number of prophages per strain ranged from 0 to 13, with an average of 4.2 prophages per strain. Notably, 99.4% (492/495) of the E. faecium strains harbored at least one prophage, while only three strains lacked prophages entirely. Most strains carried between 3 and 6 prophages, with strains carrying 4 prophages being the most common (23.2%, 115/495), followed by those carrying 5 (20.2%, 100/495), 3 (13.9%, 69/495), and 6 prophages (13.7%, 68/495) (Fig. 1B). In this study, we identified 68 different prophage types in all 1628 intact prophages (Supplementary Table S2 and Fig. 1C). Staphy_SPbeta_like and Lister_2389 were the main types of prophage, accounting for 26.4% (430/1628) and 22.1% (360/1628), respectively (gene organization in Figure S1-S2). These were followed by Entero_phiFL1A, Bacill_phBC6A52, and Lister_LP_101, which accounted for 7.8% (127/1628), 6.3% (102/1628), and 4.5% (74/1628), respectively. More details are shown in Fig. 1C.

Prevalence and diversity of intact prophages across E. faecium sequence types

To explore the association between strain genetic backgrounds and prophage carriage, we compared the number of prophages across different STs. Among the 1628 intact prophages, ST17, ST80, and ST1421 strains accounted for the highest proportions, contributing 13.0% (212/1628), 11.4% (186/1628), and 10.9% (177/1628) of the total prophages, respectively. They were followed by ST117 (7.0%, 114/1628), ST203 (5.9%, 96/1628), and ST18 (5.8%, 95/1628).

We further analyzed the relationship between prophage types and bacterial STs (Fig. 2B). Staphy_SPbeta_like prophages were present in almost all ST strains with relatively high proportions (18.6%−39.6%), while Entero_vB_IME197 prophage was only identified in ST117, ST192, and ST17 strains (0.5%−7.9%). Prophage types exhibit both co-occurrence and exclusion relationships within E. faecium genomes

To analyze the relationships among prophages within the same host genome, we constructed an interaction network diagram (Supplementary Table S3, Fig. 3). Our analysis revealed that prophage types exhibited both co-occurrence and exclusion relationships. Among all detected interactions, exclusion was the most prevalent. Prophages such as Bacill_phBC6A52, Lister_LP_101, and Lister_2389 exhibited the highest number of interactions, involving both co-occurrence and exclusion. In contrast, Escher_RCS47 and Bacter_Diva showed fewer interactions, each establishing co-occurrence relationships with only one other prophage type. Staphy_SPbeta_like exclusively exhibited co-occurrence relationships, while Entero_vB_IME197 showed only exclusion interactions.

Classification of E. faecium prophages

To characterize the diversity of prophages, intact prophages were classified using PhaGCN2. While most prophages could be assigned at the family level, some were classified at the order, subfamily, or genus levels due to incomplete ICTV taxonomy.

Overall, 75.2% (1224/1628) of intact prophages were successfully classified (Fig. 4 and S3). Specifically, 6.9% (112/1628) of prophages were classified at the order level (three orders), 17.0% (277/1628) at the family level (seven families), 37.3% (608/1628) at the subfamily level (six subfamilies), and 13.9% (227/1628) at the genus level (five genus). The most abundant category was the subfamily Bronfenbrennervirinae (29.2%, 475/1628), followed by the family Herelleviridae (10.7%, 174/1628) and the genus Fernvirus (8.1%, 132/1628). Notably, 24.8% of prophages remained unclassified, indicating that phages associated with E. faecium require further exploration.

Prevalence of AMR and VF Genes in prophages from animal- and human-origin E. faecium

Prophages play critical roles in shaping the genome plasticity of E. faecium. Among the intact prophages, 20.4% (332/1628) carried AMR genes, whereas only 1.4% (22/1628) harbored VF gene (Supplementary Table S4 and S5). Importantly, no prophages were found to simultaneously carry both AMR and VF genes. We identified 23 prophage types capable of harboring AMR genes, whereas only 5 prophage types were found to carry VF gene. Staphy_SPbeta_like was the predominant prophage type carrying AMR genes, accounting for 74.0% (245/332) of the total, followed by Escher_RCS47 and Bacill_phBC6A52, which accounted for 6.0% (20/332) and 4.2% (14/332), respectively (Fig. 5A). Among all prophages capable of carrying VF gene, Lister_LP_101 was the predominant prophage type, accounting for 68.2% (15/22), followed by Lister_2389 at 18.2% (4/22). Further analysis identified 44 distinct AMRs and their variants (Supplementary Table S5, Fig. 5C). The ermB gene (conferring resistance to lincosamides and streptogramin B, and certain macrolides such as erythromycin) was the most prevalent (6.6%, 107/1628), followed by aph(3’)-IIIa (conferring resistance to kanamycin and neomycin, 4.1%, 66/1628), vanA (vancomycin resistance, 4.3%, 70/1628), and tet(M) gene (tetracycline resistance, 0.7%, 12/1628). Additional AMR genes such as sat-4, aad(6), and mef(H) were detected at lower frequencies. The only VF gene identified was ecbA (1.4%, 22/1628), encoding an adhesin associated with enhanced colonization.

We further analyzed the relationship between AMR genes and prophage types, and found that AMR genes were significantly associated with specific prophage types (Fig. 5D). Staphy_SPbeta_like prophages were capable of carrying almost all AMR genes, while Bacill_phBC6A52 prophages only carried the mef(H) gene. The vanM, poxtA, and fexB genes were more prone to integrate into Escher_RCS47 prophages, with prophages carrying these genes accounting for 5.0%, 30.0%, and 30.0% of this prophage type, respectively, while they were rarely detected in other types of prophages. In contrast, the aad(6) and sat-4 genes were more likely to integrate into Entero_EFAP_1 prophages, with prophages carrying these genes accounting for 71.4% in this type, far exceeding the 6.5% to 11.8% found in other types of phages. Interestingly, the mef(H) gene was more inclined to integrate into Bacill_phBC6A52 and Lister_2389 prophages (accounting for 100.0% and 80%, respectively), but was not detected in Staphy_SPbeta_like prophages (accounting for 0.0%). Additionally, the vanB gene was also more likely to integrate into Lister_2389 prophages.

Comparative analyses between animal- and human-origin strains revealed that, although some AMRs (e.g., sat-4, aad(6), fexB, lnuB, lsaE, optrA, poxtA, tet(L), and tet(M)) differed significantly in prevalence, most AMR and VF genes showed no significant differences (Figs. 6A). Additionally, the median number of AMR and VF genes per prophage was zero in both groups (Fig. 6B and C). The proportion of prophages carrying AMR genes was slightly higher among animal-origin strains (29.1%) than human-origin strains (20.4%), but the difference was not statistically significant (Figs. 6D).

Identification and phylogenetic analysis of endolysins in E. faecium prophages

Among the 1628 intact prophages, 59.6% (971/1628) harbored endolysin-encoding genes. Among these, 94.1% (914/971) carried one endolysin sequence, 5.6% (54/971) carried two, and only three prophages carried three endolysin sequences. A total of 39 prophage types harboring endolysin-encoding genes were identified in this study (Supplementary Table S6, Fig. 7). Among all prophages harboring endolysin-encoding genes, Lister_2389 was the predominant prophage type, accounting for 35.8% (348/971), followed by Entero_phiFL1A, Bacill_phBC6A52, and Lister_LP_101, which accounted for 13.1% (127/971), 10.4% (101/971), and 7.6% (74/971), respectively. We further analyzed the proportion of prophages carrying endolysin-encoding genes among the total prophages to better understand the relationship between prophage types and endolysin-encoding capacity. In most prophage types, the proportion carrying endolysin-encoding genes was generally high, ranging from 86.8% to 100%, demonstrating high conservation. Specifically, 100% of Entero_phiFL1A, Lister_LP_101, Entero_IME_EFm5, and Lister_B025 prophages carried endolysin-encoding genes. Notably, Staphy_SPbeta_like prophages exhibited distinctly different characteristics, with only 4.9% of prophages carrying endolysin-encoding genes.

Phylogenetic analysis based on the 971 endolysin sequences revealed extensive diversity, clustering into multiple distinct branches (Fig. 8). Endolysin genes from different types of prophages exhibited a mixed distribution across various branches, indicating that the distribution of endolysin genes does not strictly follow the taxonomic classification of prophages. Host origin analysis revealed that endolysin genes of human origin (marked in orange, 77.1%, 749/971) predominated and were widely distributed throughout the phylogenetic tree, while endolysin genes of animal origin (marked in green, 4.2%, 41/971), were similarly scattered across multiple branches. These results suggest that extensive horizontal gene transfer of endolysin genes may have occurred among prophages of different types and different host origins, demonstrating characteristics of ubiquitous presence and diversification.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically analyzed the prevalence, diversity, and genetic features of prophages in E. faecium, revealing their potential roles in bacterial evolution, AMR, and VF. Below, we contextualize our findings and their implications.

The near-ubiquitous presence of prophages in E. faecium (99.4% of strains) underscores their importance in shaping genomic plasticity. We also found that E. faecium strains typically harbored 3–6 prophages, with an average of 4.2 per strain. This was notably lower than Klebsiella pneumoniae (average 11.1) and Acinetobacter baumannii (average 6.53), but higher than Staphylococcus aureus (average 3.5) [43–45]. Previous studies have shown a positive correlation between bacterial genome size and prophage abundance [46]. Considering that E. faecium has a smaller genome size (average 2.8–2.9 Mbp) compared to K. pneumoniae (5–6 Mbp) and A. baumannii (3.87 Mbp), and a genome size comparable to S. aureus (2.7–2.8 Mbp) [1, 47–49], our results are consistent with this relationship. Furthermore, the complete prophages we identified were mainly Staphy_SPbeta_like and Lister_2389 prophages. They are typically associated with Staphylococcus and Listeria genera, respectively [50, 51], suggesting that these lineages have evolved stable integration mechanisms or confer selective advantage. Notably, previous study has shown that Staphy_SPbeta_like prophages exhibit low genetic similarity to other staphylococcal phages (less than 10%) and lack genes required for the stable maintenance of lysogeny [52]. Whereas, the discovery of diverse prophages in E. faecium indicates that prophages may serve as important drivers of interspecies horizontal gene transfer and make significant contributions to the genetic diversity of their hosts. Further taxonomic analysis revealed that a considerable proportion of prophages belonged to the Herelleviridae family, which is consistent with previous phage studies in E. faecium [53]. Notably, despite prophage classification efforts, approximately 25% of prophages remained unclassified, highlighting the necessity of expanding phage taxonomic frameworks.

Prophage diversity was substantial across different STs, with ST117 strains harboring the most prophages and ST17 strains the fewest. Interestingly, both ST117 and ST17 are major VREfm clones [54, 55], highlighting the need for further investigation into the relationship between prophage load and clonal success [21]. Our study revealed a significant correlation between prophage types and STs. In this study, Staphy_SPbeta_like prophages were present in almost all different ST strains with considerable proportions, which may indicate their broad host range. In contrast, Entero_vB_IME197 prophages were found almost exclusively in ST117 and ST192 strains, suggesting its narrow host range. These findings are similar to studies in A. baumannii and Clostridioides difficile [56, 57], where significant associations existed between prophages and strain STs, with different prophage types exhibiting broad or specific differences in host integration range. This prophage preference for specific ST strains may significantly influence the pathogenic characteristics of different ST strains.

The interactions observed between prophages within E. faecium genomes reveal a complex network of relationships, including both co-occurrence and exclusion patterns. Notably, exclusion was the most prevalent relationship type, suggesting that many prophage pairs within E. faecium are mutually exclusive, likely due to competition for genomic space or shared regulatory mechanisms [58]. This is consistent with previous studies showing that prophages within the same host often compete for similar genomic regions, leading to the exclusion of certain types [59, 60]. Among the prophages with the highest number of interactions, Bacill_phBC6A52, Lister_LP_101, and Lister_2389 exhibited both co-occurrence and exclusion relationships, indicating their ability to coexist with some prophages while excluding others. These prophages may possess the capacity to establish stable relationships with certain prophages, while limiting interactions with others through specific molecular mechanisms [60, 61]. Interestingly, the Staphy_SPbeta_like and Entero_vB_IME197 prophages exhibited exclusively co-occurrence and exclusion relationships with other prophages, respectively, highlighting the need for further investigation. The dominance of exclusion relationships within the prophage network of E. faecium has significant implications for understanding phage dynamics within the genome. These findings suggest that the genomic landscape of E. faecium could be shaped by complex interactions between prophages, with some actively excluding others to maintain dominance or enhance fitness. This could be crucial for the development of phage therapy strategies or antimicrobial resistance interventions, as certain prophages may have the ability to outcompete or inhibit others, influencing bacterial behavior and resistance profiles [60, 62–64]. Future studies should focus on understanding the mechanistic basis of these interactions, particularly the molecular factors driving co-occurrence versus exclusion.

Horizontal gene transfer mediated by prophages plays an important role in bacterial evolution. In line with previous reports [43, 65], we detected AMR genes conferring resistance to vancomycin, tetracycline, and kanamycin within E. faecium prophages. Such prophages may also act as reservoirs and vectors for resistance dissemination, posing significant clinical challenges [8, 65, 66]. Importantly, our study demonstrated that the distribution of AMR genes within prophages was not random but followed prophage type-specific patterns. For instance, vanM, poxtA, and fexB genes were more prone to integration into Escher_RCS47 prophages, while aad(6) and SAT-4 genes were more likely to integrate into Entero_EFAP_1 prophages. In contrast, Staphy_SPbeta_like prophages served as integration carriers for most AMR genes. These findings are similar to previous studies [66–68], which have demonstrated that EGRM195, EFRM31, and ΦFL4A prophages may specifically transfer tetracycline (tetM), gentamicin (ant2-I), and macrolide resistance genes, respectively. This specificity may facilitate the dissemination of specific resistance phenotypes. Moreover, we also identified ecbA, an adhesin-encoding gene, within prophages [69]. This finding suggests that prophages contribute not only to antimicrobial resistance but also to the virulence potential of E. faecium. Consistent with this observation, prophages have also been shown to influence biofilm formation in E. faecalis [70].

Endolysins, known for their potent bacteriolytic activities, were prevalent among E. faecium prophages. Given their effectiveness against Gram-positive bacteria, endolysins offer promising prospects for developing novel antimicrobials [71, 72]. Although endolysin prevalence (59.6%) in E. faecium was lower than both that reported by Alrafaie et al. in enterococci (70%) and that reported for K. pneumoniae (79.0%), their widespread distribution across prophage types suggests broad evolutionary conservation [73, 74]. In addition, the near-universal presence of endolysins in Entero_phiFL1A and Lister_LP_101 prophages (100.0%) contrasts sharply with their scarcity in Staphy_SPbeta_like (4.9%), suggesting lineage-specific lysis strategies. The scattered distribution of endolysin genes across phylogenetic branches implies extensive horizontal gene transfer as reported in Streptococcus phages [75].

Importantly, no major differences were found in the functional composition of prophages between human- and animal -derived E. faecium strains. This observation aligns with previous studies indicating that clinical and animal isolates of E. faecium share close evolutionary relationships within Clade A [76, 77]. The functional convergence of prophages across host origins may be driven by shared environmental pressures, such as widespread antibiotic exposure.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, only complete genomes of E. faecium were analyzed, potentially limiting the diversity and representativeness of the prophage pool. Second, the analysis focused solely on intact prophages (identified by the PHASTEST web server, though these may not all contain complete gene sets for the phage life cycle), possibly overlooking fragmented elements that may contribute to horizontal gene transfer and evolution. Third, the study was based entirely on in silico genomic data without experimental validation, leaving the functionality of identified genes unconfirmed. Additionally, an imbalance between human- and animal-derived strains may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle differences, and the exclusion of environmental strains may have underestimated the broader ecological diversity of prophages. Future studies with larger, more diverse datasets and experimental approaches are needed to better elucidate the roles of prophages in E. faecium evolution and pathogenicity.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that prophages are integral to E. faecium’s genomic architecture, facilitating AMR spread and strain diversification. Prophages within the same host exhibit complex co-occurrence and exclusion relationships. The strong association between specific prophage types (e.g., Staphy_SPbeta_like) and AMR genes highlights targets for surveillance and phage therapy development. Future studies should explore prophage induction dynamics and their impact on E. faecium pathogenicity in vivo.
