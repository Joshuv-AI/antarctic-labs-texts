Dissecting the shared genetic architecture between migraine subtypes and cardiovascular diseases: a multi-layered genomic analysis
Authors: Yiming Liu, You Ma, Yiwei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpidemiological studies have linked migraine to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD); however, the shared genetic basis and putative causal relationships between migraine subtypes and cardiovascular traits remain poorly understood.MethodsLeveraging large-scale GWAS summary statistics for migraine phenotypes (overall migraine, migraine with aura [MA], and migraine without aura [MO]) from FinnGen R12, along with seven cardiovascular diseases from publicly available consortia, we conducted a multi-layered genetic analysis. This integrative framework encompassed genetic correlation [linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) and high-definition likelihood (HDL)], cross-trait meta-analysis (CPASSOC and PLACO), Bayesian colocalization, summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) using GTEx v8 eQTL data, and bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR).ResultsSignificant genetic correlations were identified between migraine and multiple cardiovascular traits, with hypertension and coronary artery disease (CAD) showing the most robust associations. MA exhibited broader genetic overlap with cardiovascular diseases than MO, including a notably stronger correlation with ischemic stroke, whereas MO demonstrated a stronger correlation with hypertension. Cross-trait meta-analysis identified 160 pleiotropic loci across 17 of 21 trait pairs. Colocalization analysis confirmed 32 loci harboring shared causal variants, mapped to 13 candidate genes, of which 7 (PHACTR1, LRP1, SOX7, ABO, FHOD3, MEI1, XKR6) were further validated by SMR as exhibiting tissue-specific regulatory effects. Among these, PHACTR1 displayed the broadest pleiotropic profile across migraine phenotypes and vascular diseases. After MR-PRESSO outlier removal, bidirectional MR identified 10 MR-supported associations, two of which (genetic liability to hypertension on overall migraine, and CAD on MA) survived Bonferroni correction, all free of detectable horizontal pleiotropy. Genetic liability to hypertension was associated with increased migraine risk (OR = 1.90, 95% CI 1.25–2.90, P = 2.64 × 10⁻³), atherosclerotic diseases showed subtype-specific effects (inverse for MO, positive for MA), and, in the reverse direction, migraine was associated with increased ischemic stroke risk.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive and systematic characterization of the shared genetic architecture between migraine subtypes and cardiovascular diseases. By identifying pleiotropic genes and bidirectional putative causal relationships with subtype-specific patterns, our findings carry implications for the development of targeted therapeutics and subtype-specific cardiovascular risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-026-02433-9.

Introduction

Migraine represents a highly prevalent and disabling neurological condition marked by recurring bouts of moderate-to-severe headache, frequently accompanied by autonomic manifestations such as nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia [1]. From a clinical standpoint, migraine is broadly categorized into two principal subtypes: migraine with aura (MA) and migraine without aura (MO) [2]. MO refers to these recurrent headache attacks occurring without aura. MA involves the same attacks but is distinguished by aura: transient, fully reversible neurological symptoms—most commonly visual, such as flickering lights, zigzag lines, or temporary blind spots, and occasionally sensory or speech-related—that usually precede or accompany the headache. The defining clinical distinction between the two subtypes is therefore the presence (MA) or absence (MO) of aura [1, 2]. The global lifetime prevalence of migraine ranges from 10% to 20%, with a marked female predominance, and the condition ranks among the leading causes of years lived with disability worldwide, imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden [3, 4]. Migraine has a strong genetic basis, with heritability estimates from twin studies ranging between 40% and 60% [5]. The largest genome-wide association study (GWAS) to date, comprising 102,084 cases and 771,257 controls, identified 123 risk loci and revealed an enrichment of migraine-associated signals within cardiovascular and central nervous system tissues, further reinforcing the conceptualization of migraine as a neurovascular disorder [6].

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality and morbidity globally [7]. The comorbidity between migraine and CVD has garnered increasing recognition. Numerous cohort studies and meta-analyses have established robust associations between migraine and various cardiovascular events, including stroke, coronary artery disease (CAD), myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, and cardiovascular mortality [8–11]. These associations are particularly pronounced among patients with MA. Accordingly, the 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on CVD prevention recommend incorporating MA into cardiovascular risk assessment [12]. Nevertheless, the biological mechanisms underlying migraine–CVD comorbidity remain incompletely understood. Although several pathophysiological pathways have been proposed—including endothelial dysfunction, hypercoagulability, cortical spreading depression, and shared cardiovascular risk profiles [13, 14]—none fully account for the observed epidemiological associations.

Growing evidence from genetic studies suggests that the migraine–CVD relationship may be partly underpinned by a shared genetic architecture. Using a conditional false discovery rate approach, Winsvold et al. identified three shared risk loci (PHACTR1, KCNK5, and AS3MT) between migraine and CAD. Notably, the index single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) at two of these three loci exhibited opposing effect directions between the two diseases [15, 16]. Mendelian randomization (MR) studies have further contributed to elucidating this causal landscape. Duan et al. reported a protective causal effect of genetically predicted migraine on CAD and ischemic stroke—a finding seemingly paradoxical given the epidemiological evidence indicating elevated cardiovascular risk among individuals with migraine [17]. However, these prior studies were limited in scope: the genetic analyses primarily focused on individual cardiovascular traits, lacked subtype-specific resolution for migraine, and did not incorporate gene-level evidence derived from expression data. Consequently, the full extent of shared genetic etiology between migraine subtypes and a broad spectrum of cardiovascular diseases, as well as the specific pleiotropic genes and biological pathways mediating this shared susceptibility, remains largely uncharacterized.

Genome-wide cross-trait analysis offers a powerful framework for comprehensively characterizing shared genetic architecture across traits, advancing beyond epidemiological associations to yield novel insights into underlying biological mechanisms [18, 19]. Furthermore, summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR), which integrates GWAS summary statistics with expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data, provides a robust approach for identifying genes whose expression levels are pleiotropically associated with complex traits [20]. To address these gaps, we conducted a comprehensive multi-layered genetic analysis to dissect the shared genetic architecture between migraine (including overall migraine, MA, and MO) and seven cardiovascular diseases (hypertension, atrial fibrillation, ischemic stroke, CAD, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and peripheral artery disease). Specifically, we first assessed genome-wide genetic correlations between migraine subtypes and cardiovascular traits using linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) and high-definition likelihood (HDL). We then performed cross-trait meta-analysis to identify pleiotropic loci, followed by Bayesian colocalization to distinguish shared from distinct causal variants. SMR analysis was subsequently applied to identify pleiotropic genes with tissue-specific regulatory effects. Finally, bidirectional two-sample MR was conducted to evaluate causal relationships and their directionality with subtype-specific resolution. Building on this prior work, our study integrates genetic correlation, cross-trait meta-analysis, Bayesian colocalization, SMR, and bidirectional MR within a single framework to characterize the shared genetic architecture between migraine subtypes and seven cardiovascular diseases, resolving shared signals and putative causal effects at the subtype level (MA versus MO) (Fig. 1). Clarifying this shared genetic basis carries both biological and clinical significance: it can help explain the long-recognized epidemiological comorbidity between migraine and cardiovascular disease, pinpoint pleiotropic genes and pathways of potential therapeutic interest, and inform subtype-specific cardiovascular risk stratification in individuals with migraine. By resolving these links at the gene and subtype level, our study provides a basis for mechanistic follow-up and for more biologically informed management of migraine–cardiovascular comorbidity.

Method

Data sources

GWAS summary-level data corresponding to three migraine phenotypes — overall migraine, migraine with aura, and migraine without aura — were retrieved from Release 12 of the FinnGen consortium. For seven cardiovascular traits, including hypertension, atrial fibrillation, ischemic stroke, coronary artery disease, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and peripheral artery disease, summary statistics were drawn from large-scale, publicly accessible GWAS datasets (Supplementary Table 1). All included datasets comprised individuals of European ancestry. The non-overlapping sample requirement was applied specifically to Mendelian randomization analyses, where sample overlap may bias causal effect estimates toward the observational association [21]; exposure and outcome datasets were therefore drawn from non-overlapping study populations whenever feasible. For LDSC, HDL, CPASSOC, and PLACO, sample overlap does not bias the parameters of interest, as these methods either explicitly model or are inherently robust to such overlap. Because all analyses relied exclusively on publicly available summary-level data, no further ethical approval was necessary.

Global genetic correlation analysis

As a preliminary step before our primary analyses, we examined genetic correlations between migraine phenotypes (including migraine with aura and migraine without aura) and seven cardiovascular traits using two well-established approaches: linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) [22] and high-definition likelihood (HDL) [23]. LDSC was employed to estimate genome-wide genetic correlations from GWAS summary-level data. We selected this approach for its demonstrated robustness in distinguishing genuine polygenic signals from confounders, including population stratification. All analyses were performed using rigorously quality-controlled GWAS data of European descent [24], with LD scores calculated from the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 reference panel [25]. Genetic correlation estimates were generated by integrating heritability and covariance values, and standard errors were computed via the block jackknife procedure [26]. Additionally, we applied HDL, a likelihood-based framework for estimating genetic correlations from GWAS summary data. By reducing the variance of genetic association estimates by approximately 60% compared with LDSC, HDL yields complementary and more precise correlation estimates. To account for multiple testing across the seven cardiovascular traits, a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of P < 0.05/7 = 7.14 × 10⁻³ was applied to both LDSC and HDL analyses. Trait pairs with P < 0.05 that did not survive Bonferroni correction were considered suggestive.

Cross-trait meta-analysis

This step aims to identify individual genetic loci that influence both migraine and cardiovascular disease. Subsequently, cross-trait meta-analysis was carried out using two complementary approaches to detect potentially pleiotropic loci: CPASSOC [27] and PLACO [28]. CPASSOC produces two test statistics — SHom and SHet — that combine trait-level summary data while adjusting for population structure and trait correlations. SHom performs optimally when effect sizes are homogeneous across traits; however, this condition is often unmet in multi-phenotype meta-analyses. To address this limitation, SHet assigns greater weight to larger trait-specific effects, thereby maintaining statistical power in the presence of effect-size heterogeneity. Accordingly, we adopted the SHet statistic and performed LD-based clumping in PLINK with the following thresholds: –clump-p1 5e-8, –clump-p2 1e-5, –clump-r2 0.2, and –clump-kb 500 [29]. For each trait pair, shared SNPs reaching genome-wide significance were defined as index variants with CPASSOC P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ and individual-trait P < 1 × 10⁻³ for both phenotypes. To qualify as novel shared loci, significant pleiotropic SNPs were required to satisfy additional criteria: [1] no single trait dominates the association signal (5 × 10⁻⁸ < P_single_trait < 1 × 10⁻³); [2] the SNP is not in linkage disequilibrium (r² < 0.1) with any previously reported genome-wide significant variant for either trait, and no neighboring SNP (± 500 kb) achieves P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ in any individual-trait GWAS. PLACO is a recently introduced method that leverages summary-level genotype–phenotype association data to detect pleiotropic loci shared between complex traits. Z-scores for each variant were squared, and SNPs exhibiting unusually large Z² values (> 80) were removed. To address potential inter-trait correlations among complex diseases, a correlation matrix of Z-scores was computed. The intersection-union test (IUT) was then applied to evaluate the composite null hypothesis of no pleiotropic association. The composite P-value from the IUT was calculated as the larger of the P-values derived under the null (H₀) and alternative (H₁) hypotheses.

Pleiotropic SNPs jointly identified by both methods were intersected and subsequently clumped using PLINK (v1.9) with the parameters: –clump-p1 5e-8, –clump-p2 1e-5, –clump-r2 0.2, –clump-kb 500. The most significantly phenotype-associated independent SNPs were retained for downstream analysis. Novel loci were defined as independent SNPs with LD r² ≤ 0.2 within a 1,000 kb window that were not in LD with genome-wide significant variants from the cross-trait meta-analysis.

To evaluate candidate pleiotropic loci, Bayesian colocalization analysis was performed to test two specific hypotheses: H₃, which posits that both traits are associated but through distinct causal variants, and H₄, which posits a single shared causal variant underlying both trait associations. The coloc.abf function was applied using default prior settings (p1 = p2 = 1 × 10⁻⁴, p12 = 1 × 10⁻⁵). Here, p1 and p2 denote the prior probability that any given SNP is associated with trait 1 or trait 2, respectively, and p12 represents the prior probability that the same SNP influences both traits. Colocalization was inferred when the posterior probability for H₄ (PP.H4) surpassed 0.7 [30, 31]. Functional characterization of the identified pleiotropic SNPs was performed using the dbSNP database [32].

Summary-based Mendelian randomization

Summary-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) [33] integrates GWAS summary-level data with expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) information to identify genes exhibiting pleiotropic associations between their expression levels and complex trait outcomes. eQTLs represent genetic variants with significant effects on transcript abundance, thereby elucidating inter-individual differences in transcriptional regulation [20]. Through the mapping of SNP–gene expression relationships, eQTL analyses reveal regulatory variants that may concurrently affect both gene expression and complex phenotypic outcomes.

SMR employs the most strongly associated cis-eQTL SNPs as instrumental variables to evaluate whether the influence of a genetic variant on a complex trait operates through gene expression, and has been broadly applied to conditions such as obesity and autoimmune diseases. This approach is routinely paired with the Heterogeneity in Dependent Instruments (HEIDI) test to discriminate genuine pleiotropy from spurious associations attributable to linkage disequilibrium. A non-significant HEIDI result (P_HEIDI > 0.05) supports a single shared causal variant. A significant result (P_HEIDI ≤ 0.05) instead points to two distinct variants in linkage disequilibrium; we interpreted such signals with caution. Collectively, SMR combined with HEIDI offers a rigorous framework for prioritizing candidate genes and elucidating the regulatory architecture of complex traits.

MR

A bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed to evaluate potential causal associations between migraine phenotypes and cardiovascular outcomes (hypertension, atrial fibrillation, ischemic stroke, coronary artery disease, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and peripheral artery disease). Genome-wide significant, independent SNPs associated with each exposure (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were extracted through LD-based clumping in PLINK v1.9, with an r² threshold of < 0.001 and a physical distance window of 10,000 kb [25]. LD estimates were derived from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel. The strength of each instrument was assessed using the proportion of variance explained (PVE) and the F-statistic; instruments with F > 10 were deemed sufficiently strong [34]. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) approach served as the primary analysis owing to its maximal statistical efficiency when all instruments satisfy validity assumptions [35]. Between-instrument heterogeneity was examined using Cochran’s Q statistic [36, 37], and directional horizontal pleiotropy was tested via the MR-Egger intercept [38]. To further identify and adjust for horizontal pleiotropy, MR-PRESSO global and outlier tests were conducted with 1,000 simulated distributions. Outlier SNPs flagged by MR-PRESSO were excluded, and the MR analysis was re-performed with the refined instrument set to obtain robust causal effect estimates. To account for multiple testing, Bonferroni correction was applied within the MR analytical family. Because the three migraine phenotypes (overall migraine, MA, and MO) are strongly genetically correlated rather than independent, they were treated as a single phenotypic family, consistent with the genetic correlation analyses. The correction therefore accounted for the seven cardiovascular traits tested in both directions (forward and reverse), giving an adjusted threshold of α = 0.05/14 = 3.57 × 10⁻³. Associations with P < 3.57 × 10⁻³ were considered significant after correction; those with 3.57 × 10⁻³ ≤ P < 0.05 were considered nominally significant and interpreted as exploratory.

Results

Genetic correlations between migraine and cardiovascular diseases

First, we assessed the genetic correlations between migraine (including its subtypes) and seven cardiovascular diseases (Table 1; Fig. 2), applying a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of P < 0.05/7 = 7.14 × 10⁻³ and prioritizing findings convergent across LDSC and HDL. Overall migraine showed Bonferroni-significant correlations with CAD (LDSC: rg = 0.304, P = 8.04 × 10⁻⁶; HDL: rg = 0.116, P = 1.60 × 10⁻³) and hypertension (LDSC: rg = 0.273, P = 4.66 × 10⁻⁵; HDL: rg = 0.112, P = 5.88 × 10⁻³) in both methods, with heart failure additionally significant under HDL only (rg = 0.149, P = 4.59 × 10⁻³; method-specific discrepancy addressed below). Migraine without aura (MO) showed four convergent correlations across both methods: hypertension (LDSC: rg = 0.286, P = 8.97 × 10⁻¹⁴; HDL: rg = 0.341, P = 1.23 × 10⁻¹⁴), CAD (LDSC: rg = 0.175, P = 5.11 × 10⁻⁶; HDL: rg = 0.242, P = 1.33 × 10⁻⁷), PAD (LDSC: rg = 0.317, P = 1.5 × 10⁻³; HDL: rg = 0.338, P = 4.57 × 10⁻³), and MI (LDSC: rg = 0.153, P = 2.3 × 10⁻³; HDL: rg = 0.245, P = 6.55 × 10⁻³), with HF again significant under HDL only (rg = 0.263, P = 1.70 × 10⁻³). Migraine with aura (MA) exhibited the broadest overlap: five of seven cardiovascular traits reached Bonferroni significance and were convergent across both methods — hypertension (LDSC: rg = 0.247, P = 4.91 × 10⁻¹⁰; HDL: rg = 0.261, P = 1.50 × 10⁻⁹), CAD (LDSC: rg = 0.206, P = 1.92 × 10⁻⁸; HDL: rg = 0.249, P = 2.14 × 10⁻⁸), ischemic stroke (LDSC: rg = 0.319, P = 3.55 × 10⁻⁷; HDL: rg = 0.367, P = 2.11 × 10⁻⁵), MI (LDSC: rg = 0.207, P = 2.42 × 10⁻⁶; HDL: rg = 0.298, P = 5.19 × 10⁻⁴), and PAD (LDSC: rg = 0.306, P = 3.5 × 10⁻³; HDL: rg = 0.303, P = 4.81 × 10⁻³). Heart failure was Bonferroni-significant under HDL only (rg = 0.221, P = 5.67 × 10⁻⁴; LDSC P = 0.0496, nominal), and atrial fibrillation reached only nominal significance in both methods. Integrating across phenotypes, CAD and hypertension were the only cardiovascular traits Bonferroni-significant with all three migraine phenotypes in both methods, representing the most robust shared genetic architecture. Two subtype-specific patterns further emerged: only MA — not MO — showed a Bonferroni-significant correlation with ischemic stroke, suggesting that the cerebrovascular genetic component of migraine is largely confined to the aura subtype; and the hypertension correlation was notably stronger for MO than for MA (MO: rg = 0.286/0.341; MA: rg = 0.247/0.261), pointing to distinct vascular genetic pathways between subtypes.

Cross-trait meta-analysis identifies pleiotropic loci shared between migraine and cardiovascular diseases

To identify specific genetic loci with pleiotropic effects on both migraine and cardiovascular diseases, we performed cross-trait meta-analysis using CPASSOC and PLACO methods, followed by intersection of the results from both approaches. Across the 21 trait pairs tested (3 migraine phenotypes × 7 cardiovascular diseases), we identified 160 independent pleiotropic loci reaching genome-wide significance in 17 trait pairs (Supplementary Table 2). No shared loci were detected for four trait pairs: overall migraine with heart failure, overall migraine with peripheral artery disease, MO with heart failure, and MO with peripheral artery disease, consistent with the weaker genetic correlations observed for these pairs in the LDSC and HDL analyses.

The largest number of pleiotropic loci was identified between overall migraine and CAD (43 loci), followed by MO and CAD (21 loci), MA and myocardial infarction (20 loci), and MA and hypertension (19 loci). For MA, shared loci were identified with all seven cardiovascular traits, further supporting the broader genetic overlap of MA with cardiovascular diseases compared to MO. Notably, MA shared loci with heart failure (2 loci) and peripheral artery disease (4 loci), whereas MO did not, highlighting subtype-specific pleiotropic architecture.

Bayesian colocalization confirms shared causal variants at pleiotropic loci

Bayesian colocalization analysis was performed for all 160 pleiotropic loci to assess whether the observed associations were driven by shared or distinct causal variants (Supplementary Table 3). A total of 32 loci (20.0%) demonstrated strong evidence of a shared causal variant (PP.H4 > 0.7), while 52 loci (32.5%) were supported by distinct causal variants in close proximity (PP.H3 > 0.5). Functional annotation using dbSNP mapped these 32 colocalized loci to 13 unique candidate genes, with 8 loci located in intergenic regions (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 4).

Bidirectional Mendelian randomization reveals putative causal effects between migraine and cardiovascular diseases

Bidirectional two-sample MR analyses were performed across 42 exposure–outcome pairs (Supplementary Table 6). For each pair, the inverse variance weighted (IVW) estimate is reported after exclusion of outlier SNPs identified by the MR-PRESSO outlier test (1,000 simulations), ensuring robustness to the most influential pleiotropic instruments. At P < 0.05, 10 MR-supported associations were identified (Fig. 5). Two of these associations (the effect of genetic liability to hypertension on overall migraine, P = 2.64 × 10⁻³, and of CAD on MA, P = 2.88 × 10⁻³) survived Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.05/14 = 3.57 × 10⁻³); the remaining eight were nominally significant and are interpreted as hypothesis-generating findings.

In the cardiovascular-to-migraine direction, genetic liability to hypertension was associated with increased risk of overall migraine (OR = 1.90, 95% CI 1.25–2.90, P = 2.64 × 10⁻³) and MA (OR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.13–3.33, P = 0.017). Heart failure showed MR-supported associations with increased risk of overall migraine (OR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.01–1.29, P = 0.040) and MA (OR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.02–1.37, P = 0.025). Genetic liability to CAD was associated with increased risk of MA (OR = 1.09, 95% CI 1.03–1.15, P = 2.88 × 10⁻³). Genetic liability to MI was inversely associated with MO (OR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.86–0.99, P = 0.023).

In the migraine-to-cardiovascular direction, overall migraine was associated with increased risk of ischemic stroke (OR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.01–1.24, P = 0.026) and heart failure (OR = 1.07, 95% CI 1.00–1.14, P = 0.039), and showed an inverse association with MI (OR = 0.85, 95% CI 0.72–0.99, P = 0.041). MO showed an inverse association with peripheral artery disease (OR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.62–0.96, P = 0.022). No significant associations were observed between any migraine phenotype and atrial fibrillation in either direction.

Sensitivity analyses showed no evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy for any of the 10 MR-supported associations after MR-PRESSO outlier removal (MR-Egger intercept P > 0.05 for all; Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

In this study, we performed a comprehensive, multi-layered genetic analysis to dissect the shared genetic architecture between migraine and cardiovascular diseases. By integrating genetic correlation analysis, cross-trait meta-analysis, Bayesian colocalization, SMR, and bidirectional MR, we identified 160 pleiotropic loci, 13 colocalized genes, and 7 SMR-validated pleiotropic genes, while revealing complex, bidirectional putative causal relationships with subtype-specific patterns. Our study extends previous cross-trait and MR analyses of migraine and cardiovascular disease [15–17] by jointly integrating five complementary genomic approaches within a unified framework, expanding the scope to seven cardiovascular traits, and resolving shared signals and bidirectional causal estimates at the level of migraine subtypes.

Our genetic correlation analyses revealed significant positive correlations between migraine and multiple cardiovascular traits, with hypertension and CAD exhibiting the most robust associations. Notably, MA demonstrated the broadest genetic overlap, with five cardiovascular traits convergent across both LDSC and HDL and a sixth (heart failure) supported by HDL alone, whereas MO showed a stronger correlation with hypertension and MA — but not MO — showed a Bonferroni-significant correlation with ischemic stroke. These subtype-specific patterns align with the ESC guidelines recommending that MA be incorporated into cardiovascular risk assessment [12], and with previous findings demonstrating enrichment of migraine GWAS signals in both cardiovascular and central nervous system tissues [6]. The migraine–heart failure correlation reached Bonferroni significance under HDL but not LDSC across all three migraine phenotypes. This likely reflects a genuine but modest correlation detectable by the more statistically efficient HDL framework yet underpowered under LDSC; for overall migraine, an inflated LDSC standard error (SE = 0.137, versus 0.07–0.08 for MO and MA) further indicates an unstable LDSC estimate. We therefore treat this as a method-specific, suggestive finding rather than a robust two-method-convergent result. Our cross-trait meta-analysis substantially extends the work of Winsvold et al. [15], who identified only three shared loci (PHACTR1, KCNK5, and AS3MT) between migraine and CAD, by expanding the analytical scope to encompass seven cardiovascular traits and resolving 160 pleiotropic loci to 13 candidate genes through colocalization.

Among the 13 colocalized genes, 7 were further validated by SMR analysis, representing high-confidence pleiotropic genes supported by convergent evidence from cross-trait meta-analysis, colocalization, and gene expression–mediated effects. These genes can be functionally categorized into vascular (PHACTR1, LRP1, SOX7), coagulation/endothelial (ABO), cardiac structural (FHOD3), and less well-characterized (MEI1, XKR6) groups. Importantly, these groups differ substantially in the strength of their supporting evidence. PHACTR1, LRP1, SOX7, ABO, and FHOD3 each have independently documented roles in vascular or cardiac biology, and we consider their pleiotropic interpretations to be reasonably well supported by prior functional and genetic evidence. In contrast, MEI1 and XKR6 have no previously established role in either migraine or cardiovascular disease; the mechanistic interpretations proposed for these two genes below are therefore speculative and are presented as hypothesis-generating observations that require dedicated experimental validation.

PHACTR1 emerged as the most prominent pleiotropic gene, demonstrating near-perfect colocalization (PP.H4 ≈ 1.0) and SMR associations across all three migraine phenotypes and two cardiovascular diseases (CAD and MI) in arterial tissues. The lead SNP rs9349379 at the PHACTR1/EDN1 locus has been associated with at least five vascular disorders [39, 40], functioning as a distal regulator of endothelin-1 expression [41], with opposing effects attributable to differential endothelin receptor subtype actions in coronary versus systemic vascular beds [42]. PHACTR1 itself ranks second among prioritized causal genes for CAD through its regulation of endothelial survival and nitric oxide production [43]. ABO colocalized between MA and heart failure (PP.H4 = 0.93), with SMR signals across 12 tissues. Several mechanisms may underlie this link: higher von Willebrand factor and factor VIII in non-O blood groups [44, 45], elevated inflammatory markers [46], and microembolic pathways connecting cardiac dysfunction with migraine [14]. These are consistent with the broad pleiotropy of ABO, which spans 24 of 35 phenotypes tested in exome-wide studies [47]. LRP1 exhibited convergent arterial tissue signals between overall migraine and CAD (PP.H4 = 0.79), supporting a shared pathway through vascular remodeling and lipid clearance. This is further substantiated by its independent genome-wide significance for both migraine and CAD [48] and suggestive association with cervical artery dissection [49]. SOX7 was the only gene colocalized across three trait pairs (MA–hypertension, migraine–CAD, and MA–CAD), with brain-enriched SMR signals. As a SOXF transcription factor regulating VEGF signaling and vascular development [50, 51], genetic variants affecting SOX7-mediated vascular patterning may confer shared predisposition to both conditions. FHOD3 linked MA with ischemic stroke exclusively in heart atrial appendage tissue. Given its essential role in sarcomere organization [52] and established causality for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [53], together with evidence that FHOD3 deficiency leads to CaMKII activation and impaired calcium handling [54], this tissue-specific pattern suggests that FHOD3-mediated atrial remodeling may serve as a cardioembolic pathway connecting MA to stroke risk [55]. MEI1 exhibited the most extensive tissue associations (24 tissues), linking MO and MA with atrial fibrillation; however, its canonical role in meiotic chromosome synapsis [56] renders this association unexpected. The observed signal may instead reflect cis-regulatory effects on neighboring genes within the gene-dense 22q13 region rather than MEI1 itself. Because MEI1 has no established role in cardiovascular or neurovascular biology, this association should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating observation rather than evidence of a direct biological role, and it requires dedicated functional validation before any mechanistic conclusion can be drawn. XKR6 linked MA with hypertension (PP.H4 = 0.89) in testis and thyroid tissues. Although not previously implicated in either condition, XKR family members have been associated with metabolic traits [57, 58]. We tentatively note that the thyroid tissue enrichment could be consistent with hormone-mediated effects on vascular tone; however, this mechanistic interpretation is entirely speculative. XKR6 has not been functionally implicated in blood-pressure regulation or vascular biology, and this proposed pathway should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating observation requiring direct experimental testing rather than as an established mechanism. Collectively, these seven genes implicate vascular tone, coagulation, and cardiac structure as the principal shared pathways, though the strength of evidence varies by gene.

Our MR analysis revealed that genetic liability to hypertension was associated with increased risk of overall migraine (OR = 1.90, P = 2.64 × 10⁻³) and MA (OR = 1.94, P = 0.017), and that heart failure showed MR-supported associations with elevated risk of overall migraine (OR = 1.14, P = 0.040) and MA (OR = 1.18, P = 0.025). These effects are biologically plausible, as hypertension-induced endothelial dysfunction may lower the threshold for cortical spreading depression [59]. Inverse associations consistent with a protective effect of atherosclerotic diseases on migraine were also observed, most notably for the effect of MI on MO (OR = 0.92, P = 0.023). These directionally protective signals are consistent with findings reported by Duan et al. [17], who described inverse MR-supported associations between migraine and CAD (OR = 0.881) and ischemic stroke (OR = 0.912). This apparent paradox—whereby epidemiological evidence suggests increased risk while genetic analyses indicate protection—may be explained by CGRP-mediated cardiovascular protection through vasodilation and nitric oxide production [60, 61], a hypothesis supported by our identification of NOS3 as a colocalized gene between MO and MI (PP.H4 = 0.90). Notably, the protective signals were predominantly observed for MO, whereas MA showed a positive association with CAD (OR = 1.09, P = 2.88 × 10⁻³), further reinforcing the necessity of subtype-specific cardiovascular risk stratification. Among these MR associations, the effects of genetic liability to hypertension on overall migraine and of CAD on MA survived Bonferroni correction, whereas the remainder reached only nominal significance; even the corrected-significant associations lay only modestly above the correction threshold, and all should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrant replication in independent cohorts.

Our findings suggest several translational implications. First, the PHACTR1/EDN1 axis and NOS3 represent potential therapeutic targets capable of simultaneously modulating migraine susceptibility and cardiovascular risk. Second, the robust MR-supported association between genetic liability to hypertension and migraine supports the use of antihypertensive agents—particularly beta-blockers and angiotensin receptor blockers already employed in migraine prophylaxis—as dual-purpose therapies. Third, given the potentially protective cardiovascular role of CGRP identified in our analysis, long-term cardiovascular monitoring is warranted for patients receiving anti-CGRP therapies [62, 63], as antagonism of this endogenous protective mechanism could theoretically exacerbate cardiovascular risk. Finally, the distinct genetic profiles of MA and MO argue for subtype-specific risk management strategies, with MA patients potentially requiring more intensive cardiovascular surveillance.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our analyses were restricted to European-ancestry populations. Because allele frequencies, linkage disequilibrium, and effect sizes differ across ancestral groups, the genetic correlations, pleiotropic loci, and causal estimates reported here may not generalize to non-European populations. Multi-ancestry studies, particularly trans-ancestry fine-mapping, will be needed to confirm whether this shared genetic architecture is conserved across populations and to support clinical translation. Second, limited availability of MR instrumental variables for certain migraine subtypes may have reduced statistical power in specific analyses. Third, only two of the 10 MR-supported associations (the effects of genetic liability to hypertension on overall migraine and of CAD on MA) survived Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.05/14 = 3.57 × 10⁻³, treating the three correlated migraine phenotypes as a single family); the remaining eight reached only nominal significance. Moreover, the two corrected-significant associations lay only modestly above the correction threshold, so all MR findings should be interpreted with caution as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence of causality, and require replication in independent cohorts. Fourth, the functional roles of MEI1 and XKR6 in the migraine–cardiovascular axis remain unclear; the mechanistic interpretations we propose for these two genes are speculative and are presented solely as hypotheses to be tested, not as established biology, and they require dedicated experimental validation. Fifth, we were unable to perform sex- and age-stratified analyses owing to the unavailability of stratified GWAS summary statistics, a limitation of particular relevance given the well-established sex differences in both migraine prevalence and cardiovascular risk profiles. In addition, SMR alone cannot prove causality, so each SMR signal was corroborated with the HEIDI test and colocalization. Phenotype-level causal relationships between migraine and cardiovascular disease were assessed separately by bidirectional MR, and the SMR-based gene findings are treated as supportive, hypothesis-generating evidence rather than proof of causality. Future studies incorporating sex-stratified GWAS data, multi-ancestry cohorts, and experimental functional validation will be essential to address these limitations.

In summary, this study systematically dissected the shared genetic architecture between migraine subtypes and cardiovascular diseases through an integrated, multi-layered genomic framework. Our findings revealed subtype-specific genetic overlap, identified seven high-confidence pleiotropic genes, and uncovered complex bidirectional putative causal relationships, with notable divergence between MA and MO in their cardiovascular genetic risk profiles. These results advance our understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying migraine–cardiovascular comorbidity and carry implications for subtype-specific risk stratification and targeted therapeutic development.
