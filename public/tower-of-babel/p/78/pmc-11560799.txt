3‐year quality of life, functional performance, and long‐term survival after acute pulmonary embolism; A prospective study
Authors: Kasra Mehdizadeh, Sepideh Kalantari, Mahsa Mohammadi, Melody Farrashi, Raheleh Kaviani, Danial Farmani, Mona Naghshbandi, Jamal Moosavi, Bahram Mohebbi, Hooman Bakhshandeh, Hamid Reza Pouraliakbar, Stefano Barco, Frederikus A Klok, Parham Sadeghipour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur prospective study investigates the 3‐year trajectory of disease‐specific quality of life (QoL) using the PEmb‐QoL questionnaire, functional performance via 6‐min walk tests, and the 5‐year survival following acute pulmonary embolism (PE) and explores their association with patient demographics and clinical characteristics. We highlight that PE‐specific QoL improves over time despite no significant changes in cardiopulmonary performance.Keywords: exercise test, post pulmonary embolism syndrome, prognosis, pulmonary embolism, quality of Life

Persistent dyspnea and diminished functional capacity are common symptoms after acute pulmonary embolism (PE), 1 yet only a minority of these patients meet the criteria of chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH). Consequently, post‐pulmonary embolism syndrome (PPES) has been proposed as a more inclusive diagnostic rubric for describing persistent dyspnea and functional limitation following PE. 2 Notably, complications of PE often transcend physical capacity, and may even lead to depressive, anxiety, and even posttraumatic stress disorders, necessitating a broader evaluation framework in which clinical outcomes such as PPES are accompanied by patient‐reported outcome measures such as quality of life, offering a more comprehensive insight into patient's condition and long‐term prognosis. 3 For this purpose, a PE‐specific quality of life (PEmb‐QoL) questionnaire was developed. 4 However, few studies have incorporated PEmb‐QoL questionnaire into their longitudinal assessments. 5 In our prospective cohort, we aimed to investigate (i) the 3‐year evolution of self‐reported quality of life throughout the course of PE, (ii) the association of quality of life with clinically measured indices of disease severity, and (iii) prognostic utility of quality of life for long‐term survival in Iranian patients.

Design, objectives, outcomes and 6‐month results of the Pulmonary Emboli Registry of Rajaie Cardiovascular Medical and Research Center (RHC‐PE) were previously published. 6 , 7 Briefly, RHC‐PE registry is an all‐comers cohort of patients with a confirmed diagnosis of PE between September 2015 and August 2018, in which surviving patients were invited via phone calls for both 6‐month and 3‐year structured follow‐ups, which consisted of detailed transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), 6‐min walk test (6MWT) and quality of life assessment through pulmonary embolism quality of life (PEmb‐QoL), all these examinations were conducted in the same day for each patient. The PEmb‐QoL questionnaire was previously validated in the Persian language. 8 Post‐pulmonary embolism syndrome was defined as a combination of CTEPH, CTEPD without PH, or post‐PE cardiac impairment (composed of incomplete RV‐recovery and a 6‐min walk test distance less than 350 m).

To explore the association of demographic and clinical characteristics with QoL, we initially conducted time‐adjusted mixed‐effect regression analyses. Time adjustment allowed us to pool the data from all participants— including those who missed one of the two follow up sessions— while controlling for the factor of time at which the questionnaire was filled. Subsequently, variables yielding significant association in at least 2 dimensions of QoL were included in multivariable mixed effect regressions to further explore their independent effects on quality of life. Finally, unadjusted and age adjusted Cox regressions were performed to investigate the association of age, sex, QoL score, 6MWT distance, and PPES with 5‐year survival. R (version 4.3.2) statistical language was used for statistical analysis.

The time‐adjusted regression revealed that older age, female sex, cardiopulmonary comorbidities, shorter 6‐min walk distances, and the presence of post‐PE syndrome were associated with lower QoL in at least two dimensions (Table S1). Subsequent multivariable regression analysis, aimed at isolating the independent effects of these factors, showed that a shorter duration since the acute PE event (6 months vs. 3 years) and reduced distances in the 6‐min walk test were independently asscociated with poorer QoL across all dimensions. Additionally, younger age was associated with poorer scores in the ‘frequency of complaints’ dimension, female sex with more ‘emotional complaints’, and established post‐PE syndrome were significantly associated with ‘activities of daily living limitations’, ‘work‐related problems’, and ‘intensity of complaints’ (Table S3).

No notable changes were observed in 6‐min walk test results or in the number of patients with echocardiographic evidence of complete right ventricular recovery between the 6‐month and 3‐year follow‐ups. However, we observed a marked reduction in the number of patients meeting the post PE syndrome criteria (OR: 0.25; 95%CI: 0.003–0.93).

We obtained the 5‐year survival data of participants in initial QoL assessment. 14 (14.6%) deaths occurred during this period (median = 1180 days, range: 350–1991 days). Poorer 6‐min walk test results and worse PEmb‐QoL scores, especially in “activities of daily living limitations”, “work‐related problems”, and “emotional complaints” dimensions, were linked to higher long‐term mortality rates. Notably, post‐PE syndrome established within 6‐months of PE were associated with six‐fold (95% CI: 1.96–18.41, p‐value = 0.002) hazard rate for mortality (Table S4). However, this association was highly confounded by age and 6MWT results. ROC curves were produced for 6MWT distance and each PEmb‐QoL score to determine the optimal score threshold for predicting long‐term mortality (Figure S2). Dimensions “activities of daily living limitations” (threshold = 69), “work‐related problems” (threshold = 88) along 6‐min walk test distance (threshold = 310 m) yielded acceptable area under the curve.

PE has been shown to disturb quality of life. Yet, long‐term trajectory of PE‐specific quality of life and its association with demographic and clinical characteristics of PE survivors are scarcely studied. We demonstrated that the negative impact of PE on quality of life is mitigated over time, a finding supported by the only two other longitudinal studies focused on PE‐specific QoL. 5 , 9 Our multivariable analysis identified several predictors of better QoL outcomes: longer time elapsed since the acute PE event, and greater distances covered in the 6‐min walk test. Conversely, female sex was associated with poorer scores in emotional complaints dimension, and established post‐PE syndrome were linked to worse scores in dimensions related to physical activity and symptom intensity.

In our study, 6 months to 3 years improvements in quality‐of‐life occurred despite no corresponding changes in 6MWT distance, these results indicate that while cardiopulmonary performance is indeed a predictor of QoL at any given time point, improvements in QoL are not necessarily accompanied with improved cardiopulmonary performance.

Our analysis also highlights the association of PEmb‐QoL scores with long‐term complications of PE, particularly their value in predicting long‐term mortality which aligns with the findings of the study by Keller et al. 9 Additional factors predicting long‐term mortality included older age at the onset of acute PE, presence of post‐PE syndrome and diminished 6‐min walk test performance at the 6‐months milestone. Interestingly, clinical and patient reported measures of functional limitations—namely, 6MWT distance and “activities of daily living limitation” score—emerged as the most reliable predictors of 5‐year mortality of acute PE.

Our study has several limitations, primarily its observational nature and small sample size. We were unable to reliably determine the cause of death for patients who died outside our center. As a result, we used all‐cause mortality, rather than PE‐related mortality, as the end‐point. Another limitation relates to the lack of measurement of residual pulmonary vascular obstruction (RPVO), which might have impacted functional capacity and QoL. Unfortunately, due to resource constraints, we were unable to assess RPVO in our study.

Furthermore, a potential limitation rests in the PEmb‐QoL questionnaire itself. PEmb‐QoL has proven to be a valuable diseases‐specific patient reported outcome measure for assessing the long‐term complications of PE. Nonetheless, there is still room for improvement, particularly by mitigating the problem of floor effects among the subscales, a phenomenon observed across all studies involving PEmb‐QoL questionnaire. Floor effect occurs when a disproportionate number of scores cluster at the bottom of the scale creating a skewed response distribution. This potentially undermines scale's ability to distinguish nuances in the quality of life of patients with better health status. To mitigate this issue, improve the scale's sensitivity, and effectively boost the true variance of the PEmb‐QoL subscales, a viable solution is to introduce items that elicit responses closer to the scale's midpoint. Similarly, the description of response options could be modified; for example, from ‘not limited at all ‐ limited a little ‐ limited a lot’ to ‘not limited at all ‐ fairly limited ‐ limited a lot’. 10 Another effective strategy involves adopting composite scores, an approach that has enhanced the SF‐36 questionnaire and is supported by the reliability assessment and factor analysis of our previous study. 8

Our study found no association between the severity of PE and quality‐of‐life outcomes. Although this may initially appear controversial, it has likely stemmed from the fact that nearly all patients with high‐risk PE received advanced reperfusion therapies making it impossible to isolate the potentially opposing effects of initial treatment and disease severity and initial treatment on quality of life.
