Effectiveness of a mobile respiratory trainer (AiroFit PRO™) in ankylosing spondylitis: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: İffet Merve Uçar Baytaroğlu, Ali Yavuz Karahan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of Airofit PRO™, a mobile respiratory trainer device, on pulmonary function, chest expansion, and functional status in patients with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) compared to a conventional pursed-lip breathing (PLB) exercise program.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted with 70 patients with AS who were allocated into two groups: the mobile device exercise (MDE) group (n = 36), using Airofit PRO™, and the PLB control group (n = 34). The participants underwent a 12-week exercise regimen consisting of three 6-minute daily sessions. Pulmonary function tests (PFTs), including forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), forced expiratory flow between 25–75% (FEF25–75), peak expiratory flow (PEF), chest expansion, and the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI) scores, were measured pre- and post-intervention.ResultsSignificant improvements were observed in the MDE group compared to the PLB group. Male patients in the MDE group exhibited statistically significant improvements in FEV1, FVC, FEF25–75, and PEF (p < 0.05). Female patients in the MDE group demonstrated significant improvements in PFT parameters and chest expansion (p < 0.05). No significant improvement was observed in the PLB group. The compliance rates were similar between the groups, and no significant adverse effects were reported.ConclusionThe Airofit PRO™ respiratory trainer significantly improved pulmonary function parameters and chest expansion in AS patients , demonstrating greater effectiveness compared to conventional PLB exercises. These findings suggest that mobile respiratory trainers may provide a promising adjunctive therapeutic option for the pulmonary rehabilitation of patients with AS.Keywords: Ankylosing Spondylitis, breathing exercises, pulmonary rehabilitation, respiratory function tests, muscle strength

Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS), characterized by chronic inflammatory changes in the axial skeleton, affects both the thoracic spine and the chest wall, leading to pulmonary complications with a restrictive pattern involvement [1,2]. In addition, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary fibrosis (especially in the upper lobes), and sleep apnea are frequently observed during the progression of the disease. All these conditions are reflected in the clinical picture of patients with marked dyspnea and exercise intolerance [2,3]. Although nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and biologics represent an effective and crucial therapeutic step in the treatment of AS, controversial reports on their effects on cardiopulmonary systems are available literature [4]. Although few randomized controlled trials have examined the effects of various pulmonary rehabilitation methods on patients with AS, the existing literature shows that an 8-week pulmonary exercise program focusing on inspiratory muscles contributes to pulmonary function and improves patients’ quality of life, regardless of the rehabilitation methodology [5–8].

The Airofit PRO™ 2.0 (Copenhagen, Denmark) breathing trainer device, designed for inspiratory muscle training (IMT) for athletes or individuals seeking to improve their respiratory capacity, has also shown promise in pulmonary rehabilitation, particularly for patients with chronic respiratory conditions such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). The increase in VO2 max and maximal respiratory power in athletes after an exercise program with AiroFit PRO™ demonstrated that this mobile device is efficient and successful in enhancing respiratory performance through telerehabilitation [9]. Vázquez-Gandullo et al. also highlighted that devices such as Airofit PRO™ improve exercise tolerance and quality of life among COPD patients undergoing respiratory therapy rehabilitation [10]. This is particularly relevant for patients with AS, as enhanced inspiratory muscle strength can lead to better management of dyspnea and improved exercise capacity, which are critical components of their rehabilitation. Integrating such devices into rehabilitation programs can improve respiratory function and overall patient outcomes. The use of such devices improves lung volumes and oxygen saturation while enhancing patient compliance with rehabilitation protocols [11]. This is crucial because adherence to rehabilitation programs often poses a barrier to achieving optimal outcomes in chronic respiratory diseases [12]. Include exercise, education, and support, which are essential for improving the overall well-being of individuals with chronic respiratory conditions [13]. beneficial for patients with AS who may experience significant limitations in physical activity due to their condition. However, many of these studies have focused on obstructive pulmonary diseases. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the impact of this novel device, AiroFit PRO™, on the pulmonary rehabilitation of AS patients.

The objective of this study is to assess the efficacy of the AiroFit PRO™ mobile respiratory trainer in enhancing pulmonary function, thoracic expansion, and functional status in individuals diagnosed with ankylosing spondylitis.

Patients and methods

Participants and design

This study was conducted at the Rheumatology Outpatient Clinic of Usak University as a prospective, randomized controlled trial with parallel groups. It conformed to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials. Local ethics committee approval (decision number 272-272-04) was obtained on 11/01/2024. All procedures adhered to the ethical standards of the National Research Committee of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration ID: NCT06652984).

Individuals were recruited through newsletters from local associations for people with ankylosing spondylitis and counselors at outpatient clinics. Participants were recruited and enrolled between November 2024 and February 2025, based on the following inclusion criteria: they volunteered for the study, were over 18 years of age, were under regular follow-up at our rheumatology outpatient clinic, and had disease activity controlled with the same medical treatment for at least 6 months. The exclusion criteria included the presence of diseases affecting the function of the respiratory system (such as pneumonia, atelectasis, pulmonary edema, pulmonary hypertension, emphysema, and lung cancer), ongoing regular exercise habits (defined as exercising 3 days a week for at least six months), and significant physical disability or impairment. Additionally, contraindications specified in the device user manual were included as exclusion criteria: history of traumatic or spontaneous pneumothorax, known history of eardrum perforation, and unstable asthma with an abnormally low perception of dyspnea. Data collection for all the primary outcome measures is completed in May 2025.

Participants were randomized into either a mobile device exercise (MDE) group or a pursed lip breathing (PLB) exercise group after pre-intervention assessments. A statistician not affiliated with the trial generated a computerized random allocation sequence using varying block sizes. To ensure a comparable representation of age and sex, random assignment to treatment groups was stratified based on these variables. The study was single-blinded, with outcome assessors (the physiotherapist for chest expansion and the spirometry technicians for PFTs) blinded to group allocation. The nature of the interventions made blinding of participants and investigators impossible.

Interventions

Patients in both groups underwent 84 exercise sessions (6 min each), three times a day, for four consecutive weeks. Each session included a 30-second warm-up (inhaling and exhaling as deeply as possible). At the end of the exercise, there was a 15-second cool-down (breathing in and out as deeply as possible).

The subjects in the MDE group performed their training using Airofit PRO™, a mobile respiratory training device (Figure 1). The devices used by the patients for exercise were provided by the study’s sponsors and were free of charge for the participants to use in their home environment. The participants received individual training on how to use the device and the mobile application developed specifically for it. The inhalation air resistance parameters of the device were divided into six categories (A–F), ranging from 20 to 140 cm H2O/(L/s), while the exhalation air resistance parameter was categorized into six levels (1–6), ranging from 30 to 200 cm H2O/(L/s). The exercise protocol was consistent for all patients, with three exercises scheduled at 6 min each (morning/afternoon/evening). The first four weeks began with inhalation (B) and exhalation (2), the second four weeks with C-3, and the final four weeks with D-4.

AiroFit-PRO device and user interface.

Patients in the control group were taught Pursed Lip Breathing by a physiotherapist during face-to-face training sessions. Exercises were defined according to the recommendations of the American Lung Association (ALA). PLB is a well-established, fundamental technique commonly taught in respiratory rehabilitation and is widely recommended for patients with various lung conditions by authoritative bodies like the ALA. It represents a minimal, readily available, and low-cost intervention that a patient might receive as general advice. Practical exercises were performed until it was ensured that the patients correctly followed these instructions. The patients received a handheld poster that described and demonstrated the exercises. They were also provided with additional videos and information from the American Lung Association website (www.lung.org/lung-health-diseases/wellness/breathing-exercises). The exercise protocol was consistent for all patients, with all exercises scheduled for 3 × 6 min/day (morning, afternoon, and evening).

Patients in both groups were verbally informed that they could be excluded from the study if they did not adhere to the exercise plan, and their participation rate fell below 80%. Compliance was monitored through the mobile application for the MDE group, which automatically logged completed sessions. For the PLB group, compliance was assessed via self-reported daily logs submitted weekly.

Outcome measures

To provide a comprehensive evaluation, outcome measures were selected to document changes in functionality and lung capacity at various levels of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF), including activity and body structure/function. The primary outcome of this study was the change in Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) from baseline to week 12. Secondary outcomes included changes in other pulmonary function parameters (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced expiratory flow at 25%–75% of FVC (FEF25–75), and peak expiratory flow (PEF)), chest expansion, and the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI) score.

Pulmonary function tests were conducted by spirometry technicians in the hospital’s pulmonary function unit where the study took place. During the test, patients were first asked to inhale and exhale normally three times. They were then instructed to inhale as deeply as possible and, after a brief pause, exhale as quickly as they could. Consequently, the FEV1, FVC, FEF25–75, and PEF values were calculated based on these maneuvers.

Chest expansion was measured circumferentially using a centimeter measuring tape. Measurements were taken in one plane at the 4th intercostal space. The difference between deep expiration and deep inspiration was measured three times, and the best measurement was obtained with the patient seated. Chest expansion was assessed by a physiotherapist who was blinded to the study methodology.

The Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI) is widely used to assess functional impairment in patients with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) [14]. Therefore, in this study, we measured the functional effects of respiratory exercises using the BASFI.

The participants were assessed both at baseline and after the intervention. Their demographic information, including age, sex, body mass index, years of education, disease duration, and current medication, was also recorded.

Sample size

The Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Metrology Index (BASMI) data from a previous study were used to determine the number of people included in the present study. According to the results of Salbas and Karahan, the mean BASMI score in Group 1 was 2.0, with a standard deviation of 1.2 according to the results of Salbas and Karahan [15]. The pre-intervention mean BASMI score was 2.8 in both groups in their study [15].

The required sample size of forty patients in each group was determined based on a power of 80% (beta 0.2), a 15% dropout rate, and a statistical significance (alpha 0.05) of 95% (p = 0.05).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 15.0 for Windows was employed for statistical analysis. When the normal distribution condition was met, descriptive statistics were presented as numbers and percentages for categorical variables and mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and median interquartile range for numerical variables. The proportions in the groups were compared using the chi-squared test. Comparisons of numerical variables in two independent groups were made using the Student t-test when the normal distribution condition was met and the Mann-Whitney U test when the normal distribution condition was not met. Logistic Regression Analysis was used to analyze predictive factors. The alpha significance level was accepted at p ˂ 0.05. To account for potential confounders, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to adjust for height, BMI, and smoking status in the comparison of post-intervention outcomes. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were made as the study was exploratory with a predefined primary outcome. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for key outcomes.

Results

Eighty participants were randomized to participate in this prospective study. A total of ten patients were excluded from the study: nine because they were unable to attend training sessions (n = 9) and one because he lost his training device. Finally, the study was completed with 70 AS patients (Figure 2). The average age of the total cohort was 34.38 years, and 75.8% of the cohort were male. The groups were similar (Table 1). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of baseline characteristics such as age, sex, BMI, education, duration of symptoms, use of biological therapy, and smoking (p > 0.05) or in terms of baseline measurements of chest wall mobility, finger-to-floor distance, and CRP levels (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Compliance with the mobile device exercise program was 89.2% for the MDE group (mean 75 out of 84 possible sessions, minimum 60, maximum 84) and 87.5% for the PLB group (mean 73.5 out of 84 possible sessions, minimum 60, maximum 84). The compliance rates were not significantly different between the two groups (p = 0.312). No severe or mild adverse effects were observed in either group. Only one patient experienced lightheadedness and dizziness after the first two sessions of exercise using a mobile device. However, the patient tolerated the exercise well and did not require medical intervention. At baseline, pulmonary function tests, BASFI, and chest expansion scores were similar between the two groups (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

From baseline to week 4, significant differences were observed for measurements including FEV1, FVC, FEF 25–75, and PEF in the MDE group under exercise conditions (p < 0.05). The PLB exercise group did not exhibit any statistically significant improvements in the study parameters at the end of week 4 (p > 0.05) (Table 2). Comparisons of the Δ values (post-treatment minus baseline) between the groups indicated that significant differences were present in the FEV1, FVC, FEF 25–75, and PEF test results (Table 3).

For the female cohort:

In addition to FEV1, FVC, FEF 25–75, and PEF measurements, the chest expansion measurements of the female participants in the MDE group also improved significantly after 4 weeks of exercise compared to the baseline measurements (Table 2). Similar to the men, the PLB exercise group showed no statistically significant improvement in the study parameters at the end of the fourth week (p > 0,05) (Table 2). Comparing the Δ values (post-treatment minus baseline) between the groups for the female cohort, significant differences were found for FEV1, FVC, FEF 25– 75, PEF test results, and chest expansion (Table 3).

After adjusting for height, BMI, and smoking status using ANCOVA, the significant improvements in the MDE group for FVC, FEV1, FEF25–75, PEF, and chest expansion remained (all p < 0.05), confirming that the results were not confounded by these factors.

Discussion

AS is a chronic inflammatory disease that primarily affects the axial skeleton and can lead to significant pulmonary complications, including restrictive lung disease. Among the various parameters used to assess respiratory function in patients with AS, Forced Expiratory Flow (FEF) at 25%–75% of the forced vital capacity (FVC) and Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF) are particularly relevant. FEF25–75 values, which are often found to be low in patients with AS, serve as a valuable measure of small airway compromise caused by thoracic deformities and decreased chest wall mobility, eventually leading to abnormal breathing patterns [1].

The application of mobile breathing trainers in managing rheumatological and pulmonary diseases is gaining increasing attention, reflecting the essential role of respiratory function in overall health outcomes. Such devices offer innovative solutions for patients experiencing impaired respiratory mechanics or dysfunctional breathing patterns, commonly associated with conditions such as AS and COPD. Breathing trainers serve as practical tools to enhance inspiratory muscle strength, crucial for optimal lung function. Notably, Hollebeke et al. demonstrated that an electronic inspiratory training device provides valid, automatically processed information on various breathing characteristics, including tidal volume and inspiratory pressure, which are integral to monitoring patient progress in inspiratory muscle training sessions [16]. Similarly, Wang et al. observed significant improvements in respiratory function among COPD patients utilizing device-guided rehabilitation, where visual feedback motivated adherence to the training protocols [11].

This correlation underscores the capacity of mobile devices to bolster patient engagement and enhance benefits derived from rehabilitation initiatives. The unique design of these devices allows for tailored training regimens that can adapt to the individual respiratory needs and capabilities of the user. For example, Parisi et al. employed the SpiroTiger device for respiratory training in post-COVID-19 patients, emphasizing its role in respiratory muscle conditioning and recovery [17]. Their findings support the broader applicability of such devices in rehabilitation protocols for various populations that experience respiratory compromise. Moreover, studies have indicated that improved inspiratory muscle function through training has downstream effects on functional capacity among individuals with chronic conditions. In a randomized controlled trial, Charususin et al. evaluated the efficacy of adjunctive inspiratory muscle training in COPD patients. Contrary to our findings, they found that physical endurance significantly increased, correlating well with improvements in quality of life [18]. Despite statistically significant increases of 15%–25% in pulmonary function test parameters in our study, only 3%–4% increase in the BASFI index could be achieved. This may be associated with the fact that the patients included in our study were in the mild-moderate group in terms of lung involvement when all cases were considered. Similarly, different studies in the literature support that respiratory muscle strength is not directly observed as an improvement in BASFI scores [19,20]. The modest improvement in the BASFI, despite significant gains in pulmonary physiology, warrants discussion. This dissociation may be attributed to two primary factors. First, our cohort comprised patients with mild-moderate functional limitation at baseline (mean BASFI ∼3.7-4.1), potentially limiting the scope for a large absolute functional improvement. Second, the BASFI is a validated tool for assessing global function and mobility in AS but is not specifically designed to quantify the impact of respiratory impairment or improvements in dyspnea. Our intervention targeted a specific organ system (respiratory) whose primary deficit—exercise intolerance due to restrictive lung disease—may not be fully captured by a questionnaire focused on activities of daily living reliant on axial mobility. Future studies may therefore benefit from supplementing the BASFI with respiratory-specific patient-reported outcomes to more sensitively gauge the perceived benefits of such interventions. Although there are not enough studies evaluating PLB as a primary treatment modality in AS patients, it has been shown that PLB efficiently increases inspiratory lung capacity in COPD patients [21–23]. In AS, reduced chest wall compliance and costovertebral joint involvement directly affect inspiration by preventing adequate lung expansion, resulting in a restrictive ventilatory defect and reduced inspiratory rather than expiratory capacity. In our study, although FVC and chest expansion values should be prioritized as indicators of inspiratory capacity in patients with AS, we believe that the significant increase in expiratory capacity and indices evaluating small airways should be interpreted as a general increase in lung capacity. PEF values can also be diminished by the same factors affecting FEF25–75, such as thoracic rigidity and impaired respiratory muscle strength. Studies have demonstrated that patients with AS often present with lower PEF values, correlating with their overall disease activity and functional impairment as assessed by indices such as BASFI [1,24]. We interpret the significant improvement in all parameters in the MDE group compared with the PLB group as a sign that mobile breath training devices will become a standard of care in the future. Although Ortancil et al. previously found an increase in both expiratory and inspiratory capacity, a significant decrease in BASFI scores, and an increase in chest expansion after 6 weeks of simple home breathing exercises in their study, we have demonstrated that this effect is very limited with PLB, and much more effective results can be achieved with an individually adjustable mobile exercise device [25].

Another advantage of this new device is its unique applications. This application provides users with a personalized training program (based on the results of the tests within the app), feedback on their progress, and the ability to share results with a healthcare professional. Videos are included within the app to help users perform the exercises correctly. The device manufacturer recommends at least one daily session of 3–6 min for healthy athletes. However, since our study involved a group with limited lung capacity, we aimed for 18 min of daily use. One of the most apparent benefits of such devices is that they facilitate self-management by enabling patients to regularly monitor their pulmonary function. This will allow for the tracking of symptoms, medication adherence, and lung capacity, which can assist in the early detection of exacerbations and better disease management.

It is imperative to acknowledge the limitations of the present study when interpreting the results. Firstly, the single-center design and recruitment strategy (which involved local patient associations to aid in participant identification) may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader AS population and introduce selection bias. Secondly, the exclusion of individuals with regular exercise habits or significant pulmonary comorbidities was necessary to isolate the effect of the respiratory trainer from confounding variables; however, this also means that the results may not be applicable to more active AS patients or those with complex cardiopulmonary conditions. Thirdly, the male predominance (∼75%) observed in this cohort is indicative of the higher prevalence of AS in men; however, this finding should be extrapolated to female patients with caution. Finally, whilst the results of our study indicate considerable short-term enhancements, it is important to acknowledge the restriction imposed by the 12-week duration of the study. The long-term sustainability of these benefits after the cessation of formal training is unknown. The necessity of a maintenance program to preserve respiratory function gains remains to be ascertained. It is imperative that future studies incorporating prolonged follow-up assessments are conducted to ascertain the longevity of the observed responses and to formulate optimal long-term training strategies for patients suffering from AS.

In conclusion, by presenting the first clinical data showing the use of Airofit PRO™, a mobile breathing training device that has demonstrated significant improvement in pulmonary function tests and chest expansion for pulmonary rehabilitation in AS patients, we believe that more personalized treatment modalities can be developed with the widespread adoption of such devices in the future.
