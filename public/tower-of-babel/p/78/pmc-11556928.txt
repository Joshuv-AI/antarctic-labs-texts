Robotic Ultrasound and Novel Collagen Analyses for Polycystic Kidney Disease Research Using Mice
Authors: Caroline R. Sussman, Heather L. Holmes, Alison Stiller, Ka Thao, Adriana V. Gregory, Deema Anaam, Ryan Meloche, Yaman Mkhaimer, Harrison H. Wells, Luiz D. Vasconcelos, Matthew W. Urban, Slobodan I. Macura, Peter C. Harris, Timothy L. Kline, Michael F. Romero
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key Points Robotic ultrasound performed favorably compared with magnetic resonance imaging for evaluating total kidney volume.Collagen evaluation by two novel methods of picrosirius red imaging were more informative than the standard method by brightfield imaging.Findings can improve research by increasing speed and access to total kidney volume determination and sensitivity of collagen assessment. Background3D imaging and histology are critical tools for assessing polycystic kidney disease (PKD) in patients and animal models. Magnetic resonance (MR) imaging provides micron resolution but is time consuming and expensive, and access to equipment and expertise is limited. Robotic ultrasound (US) imaging has lower spatial resolution but is faster, more cost-effective, and accessible. Similarly, picrosirius red (PSR) staining and brightfield microscopy are commonly used to assess fibrosis; however, alternative methods have been shown in non-kidney tissues to provide greater sensitivity and more detailed structural characterization.MethodsIn this study, we evaluated the utility of robotic US and alternative methods of quantifying PSR staining for PKD research. We compared longitudinal total kidney volume measurements using US and MR imaging. We additionally compared PSR imaging and quantification using standard brightfield microscopy with that by circularly polarized light with hue analysis and fluorescence imaging analyzed using curvelet transform fiber extraction software for automatic detection of individual collagen fibers.ResultsIncreased total kidney volume was detected by US in Pkd1RC/RC versus wild-type (WT) at time points spanning from early to established disease. US interobserver variability was greater but allowed scanning in 2–5 minutes/mouse, whereas MR imaging required 20–30 minutes/mouse. While no change in fibrotic index was detected in this cohort of relatively mild disease using brightfield microscopy, polarized light showed fibers skewed thinner in Pkd1RC/RC versus WT. Fluorescence imaging showed a higher density of collagen fibers in Pkd1RC/RC versus WT, and fibers were thinner and curvier with no change in length. In addition, fiber density was higher in both glomeruli and tubules in Pkd1RC/RC, and glomeruli had a higher fiber density than tubules in Pkd1RC/RC and trended higher in WT.ConclusionsThese studies show robotic US is a rigorous imaging tool for preclinical PKD research. In addition, they demonstrate the increased sensitivity of polarized and fluorescence analysis of PSR-stained collagen.

Introduction

Polycystic kidney disease (PKD) is the single most common genetic cause of kidney failure and the fourth leading cause of kidney failure overall.1,2 Autosomal dominant PKD (ADPKD) is the most common form, and most cases have mutations in the PKD1 gene, encoding Polycystin-1, or the PKD2 gene, encoding Polycystin-2. Polycystin-1 and Polycystin-2 comprise a subfamily of transient receptor potential channels, that is, TRPP1 and TRPP2, respectively, located in various cellular membranes, for example, plasma membrane, cilia, endoplasmic reticulum, and Golgi membranes. The predominant ADPKD phenotype is the development and growth of innumerable kidney cysts. Many proteins and processes are involved in PKD, including abnormal cellular cAMP signaling. This pathway has led to the development of the only PKD therapeutic approved to date, tolvaptan, targeting cAMP production by vasopressin receptor antagonism. Although helpful in slowing PKD progression, tolvaptan is not curative nor is it tolerated by all patients, indicating the need for continued research and development of new therapies.

Medical imaging is a critical tool for assessing PKD severity and progression. It allows the determination of total kidney volume (TKV) measured by magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, computed tomography, or ultrasound (US) imaging. TKV is a prognostic biomarker for ADPKD qualified by the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency, and is the primary or secondary end point in many clinical trials.3–7 Similarly, imaging is valuable to characterize PKD rodent models and to assess preclinical trials.8–10 MR imaging has the advantages of high resolution and available tools for automated analysis of images.11–13 However, MR imaging is expensive, access is limited, and image acquisition is time consuming. By contrast, US is more accessible, less expensive, and faster. Factors limiting the widespread use of US in the assessment of TKV include difficulties in obtaining accurate 3D images and reproducibility using handheld scanners.

To address these limitations, a robotic US scanner has been recently developed and used for initial assessment in healthy and cystic mice.14,15 These previous studies demonstrate the promise of robotic US imaging for determining TKV at a single time point.14 They additionally show the ability of robotic US to detect a difference in TKV between wild-type (WT) and PKD model mice with severe disease at a single time point.15 However, longitudinal analysis of TKV is an important component of PKD research, including detection of mild disease. In this study, we directly compared robotic US with MR imaging in the same cohort using the slowly progressive Pkd1RC/RC mouse model.16,17 Longitudinal imaging was conducted at three time points spanning mild to established disease.

In addition to cysts, progressive fibrosis characterizes ADPKD and provides another tool for assessing disease severity.18 The most commonly used stains for detecting fibrosis are trichrome and picrosirius red (PSR).19,20 Brightfield imaging is used to determine whether staining is present or absent to calculate the percent fibrotic area (fibrotic index). Both approaches have limited sensitivity and high variability because of specimen handling and analysis.20 As a result, alternative methods for more reproducible and higher resolution analysis have been developed for imaging PSR staining, yet these have not been evaluated in PKD.

PSR is anionic and has an elongated conformation, which results in binding parallel to cationic, basic collagen fibers, enhancing the birefringence of collagen and allowing specific detection of collagen.19,21,22 When detected with circularly polarized light, the signal is more sensitive than trichrome, and collagen thickness can be distinguished on the basis of hue. This has been demonstrated in rat myocardial scarring,23 mouse dermal wound healing,24 and human squamous cell carcinoma.25 Applying color threshold filters (red, orange, yellow, and green) to the data allows quantification of the proportion of collagen by thickness, as shown in studies of rat skin and myocardium and rabbit artery.26

PSR also emits a red fluorescence signal, which is detected with greater sensitivity than brightfield imaging, and at least equal sensitivity as polarized light or second harmonic generation imaging.27–30 Software (curvelet transform fiber extraction [CT-FIRE]) automatically detects and quantifies individual collagen fibers and their characteristics (length, width, and straightness) from second harmonic imaging or the fluorescence signal.30–32 When combined with measurement of tissue area, absolute fiber density can be calculated. This approach has been used to gain insight into collagen dynamics with disease, for example, increase in fiber curviness with time in mouse mammary tumors,33 increase in fiber length near beta-catenin–overexpressing cells in mouse prostate,30 and fiber remodeling by human non–small cell lung carcinoma cell lines.34

In this study, we compare longitudinal kidney imaging using robotic US and MR imaging, as well as PSR analysis using hues from polarized light, and fluorescence for PKD. We compare the distribution of fiber thickness and analysis of individual collagen fibers with standard analysis of fibrotic index using brightfield imaging. Overall, data show robotic US compared favorably with MR imaging, and imaging of PSR staining using polarized and fluorescent light allowed more sensitive, objective, and consistent quantification than brightfield imaging.

Methods

Mice

All mouse protocols were approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Mice homozygous for the PKD1 hypomorphic allele, p.Arg3277Cys, in the 129S6/SvEvTac (129) background were bred to inbred C57BL/6J Pkd1RC/RC to generate F1 progeny, F1(129/B6).17 WT C57BL/6J animals were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and 129S6/SvEvTac animals from Taconic Biosciences (Rensselaer, NY). Mice were maintained on the 5053-PicoLab Rodent Diet 20 (LabDiet, St. Louis, MO). Breeding mice were fed the high-protein 5058-PicoLab Mouse Diet 20 (LabDiet, St. Louis, MO), and experimental animals had access to this feed until weaned at approximately 4 weeks of age. Mouse colonies were kept at a constant 22.8°C with 34% humidity and a strict 12-hour light/dark cycle.

In Vivo Scanning

Scanning protocols were approved by the Mayo Clinic IACUC. MR scans were performed using Bruker Avance III 300 MHz (7 T) and Bruker DRX-700 (16.4 T) spectrometers.35 TKV was calculated from manually traced kidney regions on axial slices using Analyze 12.0 software. US imaging was performed using a SonoVol Vega system (Revvity, Inc., Durham, NC) and the accompanying software.15 TKV was calculated using an in-house–developed semiautomated program built in Matrix Laboratory and Statistics Toolbox Release 2019b (MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA) (Supplemental Figure 1). Cardiac parameters were calculated from M (motion)-mode US measurements using SonoVol software. Time points at 3, 13, and 17 weeks are referred to as 1, 3, and 4 months, respectively. TKV was determined by two observers for US and two different observers for magnetic resonance imaging to maintain independence and avoid bias that could be caused by information crossover from one approach to the other.

Histological Analysis

Animals anesthetized with isoflurane were euthanized by cervical dislocation in accordance with Mayo Clinic IACUC. Body and organ weight and kidney volume measured by fluid displacement were recorded before fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde. Fixed tissue was infiltrated with 20% sucrose, embedded in ocular coherence tomography, cryosectioned, and stained with PSR for histological analysis. Tissue area; quantification of stained tissue; and distribution in green, yellow, orange, and red bins from circularly polarized light micrographs were determined using ImageJ as described.26 PSR fluorescence was detected using a Zeiss Observer 7 system, 40× dry objective (Plan Apochromat 40×/0.95 Korr M27-Air) with a mCherry filter setting (Ex: bandpass 591/27; Em: 649/65). Automated detection and characterization of individual collagen fibers from red fluorescence images were performed as described using CT-FIRE, software developed by and freely available from the Eliceiri laboratory (University of Wisconsin, Madison).30–32 Tissue area was determined using ImageJ.

BUN and cAMP Measurements

Blood was collected by cheek bleed into heparinized tubes and centrifuged at 2000×g for 8 minutes to collect plasma. Diluted plasma was used to determine BUN using the colorimetric BUN assay (BUN-Urea, BioAssay Systems). cAMP levels were performed using 30–70 mg of pulverized, flash-frozen kidney tissue (Direct cAMP enzyme immunoassay kit, Enzo Life Sciences). Both assays were performed according to the manufacturers' protocols.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Comparisons involving TKV measurements over time were evaluated by fitting linear mixed models using a compound symmetry covariance matrix fit using restricted maximum likelihood. The Geisser-Greenhouse correction was used to account for nonsphericity in the models. To assess whether the association between genotype and TKV differed by sex, a linear mixed model was also fit including an interaction term between genotype and sex. Cardiac parameters measured over time were analyzed using the same approach. Comparisons involving a single time point were analyzed by a two-sample equal variance t test or a one-way ANOVA. Analyses using a single time point were tested for sex differences (one-way ANOVA), and data are shown separately when significantly different. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The WT group had three each male and female. The Pkd1RC/RC group started with ten each male and female. By 3 months, one Pkd1RC/RC female died, and at 3 months, four remaining females and five males were euthanized for tissue collection. No other deaths occurred doing the study, and postmortem analyses are shown on 4-month kidneys.

To assess the reproducibility and sensitivity of robotic US imaging, we conducted longitudinal analysis of TKV using US and MR imaging from the same Pkd1RC/RC and WT mice at 1, 3, and 4 months with two observers (Figure 2). MR images with 7- and 16- T magnets were comparable and had similar interobserver variation by Bland–Altman analysis. Although US was slightly less sensitive and had greater interobserver variation, significant differences were still detected between WT and Pkd1RC/RC at all time points using averaged measurements of observers 1 and 2 (Figure 3). None of the interaction terms for genotype and sex were significant in models for any of the three imaging modalities (P = 0.67, 16 T; P = 0.66, 7 T; P = 0.90, US). On the basis of this, data from males and females were combined for analysis of genotype and TKV over time. MR and US determination of TKV were not different from that by water displacement (Supplemental Figure 2). Using M-mode US images, several parameters of cardiac function did not differ between genotypes or were lower or trended lower only at the 1-month time point (Supplemental Figure 3). None of the interaction terms for genotype and sex were significant in models for any of the parameters (P = 0.13, heart rate; P = 0.24, stroke volume; P = 0.07, cardiac output; P = 0.59, fractional shortening; P = 0.62, ejection fraction).

For analysis of collagen, we compared three different approaches for quantifying PSR staining in the same mouse cohort. Standard brightfield imaging showed no significant differences and a high level of variability (Figure 4, A and B). To determine whether polarized light imaging improved collagen analysis, we imaged PSR using circularly polarized light to allow quantification of percentage of tissue in red, orange, yellow, and green hue bins corresponding to thick through thin collagen, as previously described22–24,26 (Figure 4, C–E). Polarized light analysis showed Pkd1RC/RC mice had a greater percentage of collagen in the green bin (thin filaments) and lower percentages in the yellow and orange bins, indicating a shift toward thinner collagen.

To compare individual collagen fibers between WT and Pkd1RC/RC, we used CT-FIRE for automated detection and quantification of fibers from red fluorescence images (Figure 5). CT-FIRE is a software developed for this purpose and available as a free download.30–32 For this analysis, four fields of view (FOVs) per mouse with only tubules (no glomeruli) were analyzed. The density of collagen fibers was higher in Pkd1RC/RC versus WT (Figure 5B). For comparison of fiber width, straightness, and length, measurements are shown as frequency histograms of percentage of fibers in bins of increasing magnitude. Compared with WT, female Pkd1RC/RC had more fibers distributed in the lowest width bin (centered at 4 µm) and fewer in higher bins (6, 8 µm), indicating a greater prevalence of narrower fibers. In addition, female Pkd1RC/RC had a greater percentage of fibers in an intermediate bin for fiber straightness and less in the highest (straight) bin than WT, indicating a greater prevalence of curvy fibers. Similar trends were observed in males. No differences were seen in fiber length. To compare glomeruli and tubules, images were collected of FOVs containing both glomeruli and tubules in the same image. Glomeruli were analyzed separately from tubules by selecting regions of interest in CT-FIRE containing only glomeruli or only tubules (Figure 6A). Six FOVs containing at least one glomerulus were analyzed per mouse, except one Pkd1RC/RC male that did not have any visible glomeruli and was excluded from this analysis. Glomeruli had a higher density of collagen fibers than tubules in Pkd1RC/RC and trended higher in WT (Figure 6B). In addition, fiber density was higher in Pkd1RC/RC than WT for both glomeruli and tubules.

Discussion

To improve methodologies for PKD research, we report here on validation testing of robotic US for measuring TKV and imaging of PSR staining using circularly polarized and fluorescent light. We report similar findings for TKV using robotic US and MR imaging, but higher interobserver variation using US. Detecting and quantifying PSR staining using circularly polarized and fluorescent light provided greater sensitivity and resolution of collagen changes in PKD compared with the established method using brightfield imaging.

This study is consistent with previous observations comparing robotic US measurement of TKV to MR and Vernier calipers after necropsy.14,15 However, measurements are extended to include comparison between WT and Pkd1RC/RC mice, including longitudinal analysis spanning early to established disease. Overall, US compared reasonably with MR imaging regarding detection of differences in TKV despite greater observer bias by Bland–Altman analysis. We concluded that US is likely to be useful for estimating disease severity and possibly randomization for preclinical trials. Improved training and coordination of observers in the future as well as applying machine learning to training and datasets are likely to reduce this variability. The use of four unique observers (two for magnetic resonance imaging and two for US) helped maintain the integrity of two independent analyses in this study, but also likely contributed to the variability. In addition, the decreased sensitivity of US was offset by its greater speed and accessibility, that is, >20 mice/h with US versus 4–5 mice/h with MR imaging.

Robotic US can also measure renal vascular density and cardiovascular parameters as demonstrated previously and shown here.14,15 While kidney vascularity inversely correlates with kidney size, neither our data nor a previous study demonstrated a change in cardiovascular function.15 However, the previous study using more advanced disease showed a trend toward a lower cardiac output in Pkd1RC/RC mice, and another study of these mice with advanced disease showed increased heart weight and thickening of the left ventricular myocardium.17

In addition to TKV, histological analysis is another standard indicator of PKD severity.2,18 PSR and Masson's trichrome stains for collagen are evaluated using brightfield imaging. However, the results are notoriously variable, and as a result, quantitation is generally arbitrary on the basis of user selection of a reference staining intensity considered positive. In addition, evaluation is limited to determination of fibrotic tissue area.

To allow more objective evaluation, consistency, and detailed characterization, alternative quantitative approaches have been developed, but not previously applied to PKD research. PSR-stained tissue can be imaged using circularly polarized light and divided into green, yellow, orange, and red bins indicating increasing collagen thickness. Distribution of pixels in hue bins can be quantified using ImageJ.22–24,26 Using this approach, we observed an increased prevalence of thin (green) collagen with a decreased prevalence of thicker (yellow and orange) collagen in Pkd1RC/RC versus WT with no difference in fibrotic index determined using brightfield imaging. The molecular characteristics of thick versus thin collagen as distinguished by hues are unclear. First, the influence of fiber density on hues is unknown. In addition, while Collagen I fibers are generally thicker than Collagen III fibers, fiber thickness is also affected by the stage of injury progressing from thin to thick.24 It is possible that during relatively mild ADPKD, as used here, collagen is in a state comparable with that of early injury when fibers might be undergoing active degradation and remodeling before fibrosis is established with accumulation of thick fibers. This could explain the lack of change in fibrotic index from brightfield analysis observed here, as is consistent with a previous study for this genetic background and time point.16

An additional powerful approach on the basis of PSR imaging allows automated detection and quantification of several characteristics of individual collagen fibers. The algorithm, CT-FIRE, was developed for analysis of second harmonic generation images and has been validated and used to document fiber shape changes in a mouse model of breast cancer.31 Subsequent studies showed even greater sensitivity of CT-FIRE when applied to fluorescence images of PSR-stained tissue.30 This approach was used to detect increased fiber length in mouse prostate, near transgenic β-catenin–overexpressing cells. In this study, we applied this approach to analyze fluorescence images of PSR-stained kidneys and observed increased collagen density, decreased fiber width, and increased fiber curviness in Pkd1RC/RC mice compared with WT (Figure 5). Decreased fiber width could explain the increased prevalence of thin collagen (green hue). Comparison of glomeruli and tubules showed fiber density was higher around tubules of Pkd1RC/RC mice versus WT and trended similarly in glomeruli (Figure 6). This is consistent with a greater effect of PKD on tubular than glomerular collagen, as expected. These data suggest that analysis of tubules separate from glomeruli will facilitate detection of changes associated with PKD and/or potential treatments.

Overall, the current data indicate the promise of robotic US and automated collagen fiber detection to improve tracking of PKD in animal models. Robotic US is more widely accessible than MR imaging and, therefore, can allow more widespread incorporation of longitudinal TKV measurements in PKD research. The higher resolution information provided by polarized light with hue analysis of collagen thickness or fluorescence and automated fiber detection showed greater sensitivity than standard PSR quantitation using brightfield imaging. Thus, these approaches demonstrate potential to provide novel insight into collagen changes throughout the course of PKD, particularly during early disease.
