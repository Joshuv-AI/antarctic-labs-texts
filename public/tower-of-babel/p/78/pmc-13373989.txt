Train Rural, Work Rural: The Potential for Advanced Fellowships in Addressing Rural VA Healthcare Workforce Shortages
Authors: Kathleen L. Carluzzo, Linda M. Kinney, Bradley V. Watts, Karen E. Schifferdecker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe United States faces a looming nationwide shortage of healthcare workers, especially in rural areas. This is particularly concerning for the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), the US’ largest integrated healthcare system, which is experiencing high vacancy rates. Professional training programs can increase recruitment and retention in rural areas. We examined the 22 Advanced Fellowships offered by the VHA's Office of Academic Affiliations to assess their potential for retaining trainees in rural areas and at the VA.MethodsFellow surveys were administered from June 2018 through August 2023 in this cross‐sectional study. Key variables included fellowship characteristics (location, topic area, length), and post‐fellowship employment, including location (rural or non‐rural), and type (clinical or non‐clinical, VA or non‐VA). We followed STROBE guidelines in reporting.FindingsSix (13%) of the 47 training sites are at facilities serving primarily rural veteran populations; six of the 22 programs are offered at these sites. Of the 843 fellows trained during the study period, 55 (7%) were at rural‐serving VAMCs. Fellows who trained in rural‐serving areas versus non‐rural areas were significantly more likely to be employed in rural areas post‐fellowship (63% vs. 2%, p < 0.01), and tended to remain employed at or affiliated with the VA post‐fellowship at higher rates.ConclusionsThe Advanced Fellowship program is effective at retaining trainees in rural areas and should be considered as part of the VA's strategy to address rural workforce shortages. Additional research is needed to assess longitudinal retention and the potential for targeted program expansion in rural sites.

Background

The United States faces a looming nationwide shortage of healthcare workers, including physicians [1, 2, 3, 4], nurse practitioners [5], and mental health providers [6]. The projected physician shortage is more severe in rural areas, predicted to be 56% less than needed in those areas compared to 6% in metropolitan areas by 2036 [7]. Specialists are lacking in general [8], more so in rural areas [9], and across a broad range of fields [6].

Projected shortages in healthcare workers are particularly concerning for the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), the largest integrated healthcare system in the United States, which is already experiencing a high vacancy rate at many facilities [10].

A number of initiatives within the VA aim to address these shortages [11, 12], including in rural areas, but many of them have not been evaluated [12]. There is evidence that training programs can be effective at increasing retention in rural areas through residencies and other post‐degree approaches [13, 14], and they may do this by addressing aspects of Cosgrave's Whole‐of‐Person Retention Improvement Framework [15] which provides guidance for addressing rural health workforce challenges. The Framework posits that three components are essential for retention: role/career (access to professional development networks and pathways), workplace/organizational (characteristics of the workplace setting that support a positive, inclusive environment), and community/place (characteristics of the rural community setting that foster a sense of integration and belonging). Training programs, such as the VHA's Office of Academic Affiliations’ (OAA) Advanced Fellowships, may address the role/career domain of Cosgrave's framework by providing trainees with opportunities to further develop skills and may also offer access to career opportunities at the VA. Since the OAA oversees the nation's largest healthcare training system, it is poised to play an important role in addressing the growing healthcare workforce shortage [16, 17].

During the study period (AY2018 to AY2022) the OAA offered 1‐ or 2‐year Advanced Fellowship programs in 22 topic areas at 47 VA medical centers across the United States. These programs were designed to address clinical priority areas and training needs at the VA and in the larger US healthcare system, and offered novel training opportunities. Trainees were eligible if they had a Master's degree or higher, and included physicians, nurses, dentists, psychologists, social workers, and pharmacists. In these interdisciplinary fellowships, trainees follow a national curriculum while receiving local mentorship and gaining applied experience. Fellowships are classified as research, education, and/or clinical work. While some have been evaluated for their effectiveness and retention in the field [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23] there has been no comprehensive assessment of the role these programs play in addressing the national or VA‐specific healthcare workforce shortages, particularly in rural areas.

Given workforce shortages in the VA (particularly in rural areas), the significant role the VA plays in healthcare workforce training, and evidence that training location may influence subsequent practice location, this study aims to assess the potential of the Advanced Fellowship programs to alleviate rural VA workforce shortages by (1) describing the characteristics of Advanced Fellowship programs and trainees in rural versus non‐rural VA facilities and (2) comparing rural and non‐rural trainees post‐fellowship by practice setting (rural vs. non‐rural) and by VA affiliation.

Methods

Design

This study is part of a larger prospective cohort study initiated by the OAA's Advanced Fellowship Curriculum Work Group. We followed the STROBE guidelines for reporting cohort studies.

Target Population

All OAA's Advanced Fellowship program participants during the study period (AY 2018‐AY2022) were eligible to participate. No written consent was obtained as this study posed no more than minimal risk, and consent was implied by their submission of the survey.

Variables and Instruments

In collaboration with the OAA's Advanced Fellowship Curriculum Work Group, we developed participant questionnaires. The questionnaire includes demographic questions to describe participant characteristics, and questions about post‐fellowship employment to describe practice setting (location), type of work (clinical, research, educational, administrative), and employer type (VA, VA‐affiliated, or non‐VA affiliated). To describe the characteristics of the Advanced Fellowship programs, the OAA provided administrative program data, including name, location, focus (research, education, or clinical), and length (1 or 2 years). Fellowship program sites were designated as being “rural‐serving” or “not rural‐serving” based on whether the VAMC serves a majority of patients who live in rural areas. We used a hybrid approach to identify those who continued to serve rural patients. Fellows who continued to work at VA facilities that were designated as “rural‐serving” retained the “rural” designation post‐fellowship. For fellows who worked in other VA locations or non‐VA facilities, we used RUCA codes to identify those that were located in rural areas, with RUCA codes ≥3 designated as “rural,” and RUCA codes 1–2 designated as “non‐rural” using the 2020 zip code to RUCA crosswalk and definitions [24].

Data Collection

Online questionnaires were sent via email to fellows in multiple waves based on each program's start and end dates at baseline (June through November), mid‐program for 2‐year fellowships (May through June), and endpoint (June through December) annually throughout the study period. The survey communication plan included an introductory email from the OAA Fellowship Director, an initial survey invitation and two follow‐ups from the survey administrator, and a final email from the OAA Fellowship Director. End‐point surveys were administered prior to the intended program end date to reduce attrition bias. Demographic and education questions were included at baseline, and repeated in subsequent surveys until completed. We contacted all fellows with a valid VA email address and considered them eligible for participation and included them in the analysis of administrative data (Table 1) regardless of their survey participation. Individuals who reported that they did not start their fellowship within the survey eligibility window were removed from the sample. Individuals who indicated that they left their fellowship early were asked to complete a shortened version of the end‐point questionnaire, which included post‐fellowship outcomes; their surveys were included in the analyses. For fellows who completed the endpoint survey but did not report post‐fellowship career plans at that time, we included their reports of post‐fellowship career outcomes publicly available online (e.g., online resumes and employer websites). Study data were collected using the Qualtrics [25] online survey platform.

Data Analysis

We used Stata [26] for merging and cleaning the data, quality assurance checks, and analysis.

Fisher's exact test (chosen to accommodate small cell sizes) was used for comparisons with significance level (alpha) set at 0.05. The relevant population for analysis varies by table: 1. all fellows, 2. all respondents, 3. all endpoint respondents.

Results

During the 5‐year study period, there were 22 fellowship programs with 843 fellows enrolled and confirmed as having a valid VA email address (Table 1). Of the 47 participating VAMC training sites, we designated six (13%) as “rural‐serving” as they primarily serve rural veterans. The six programs offered at rural‐serving VAMCs included Advanced Geriatrics (GRECC), Health Professions Education Evaluation and Research (HPEER), Mental Health Illness and Treatment (MIRECC), Patient Safety, Psycho‐Social Rehabilitation, and Quality Scholars, addressing priority areas among the VAs clinical and research needs. Five of the six offer a research component, four are clinical, and one is education‐focused. Of the 843 fellows, 55 (7%) were trained at rural‐serving VAMCs with the greatest participation in the MIRECC (n = 13), Patient Safety (n = 12), Quality Scholars (n = 12), and Psycho‐Social Rehabilitation (n = 11) programs.

A total of 738 fellows (88% overall, 96% of rural fellows, and 87% of non‐rural fellows) answered at least one survey (Table 2). Overall, respondents’ mean age was 33.0 years (SD = 6.3), and the majority of respondents (72%) were between the ages of 23–34. Rural respondents were significantly more likely to fall in the youngest age category (23–29 years) than non‐rural respondents (42% vs. 24%, p < 0.01), indicating that rural trainees are participating earlier in their careers. Most respondents were White (74%) and the majority were female (67%), with similar rates in rural and non‐rural areas. While an advanced degree is a requirement for participation, the proportion who held a terminal degree was significantly lower among rural participants (68%) than non‐rural participants (90%, p < 0.01), indicating differences in training coming into the fellowship. Among those with an MD degree specifically, the program‐wide average was 33%, with higher rates among non‐rural fellows (34%) than rural fellows (18%, p = 0.04).

We collected data about jobs post‐fellowship in the end‐point survey, to which 514 (61%) of all fellows replied (Table 3). Of these, 329 (64%) provided job‐related outcomes. Fellows who trained in rural‐serving areas were significantly more likely to be employed in rural areas post‐fellowship than those who trained in non‐rural areas (63% vs. 2%, p < 0.01). However, those who trained in non‐rural areas remain in non‐rural areas at a higher rate (98% vs. 63%) comparatively. The majority of fellows remained at or affiliated with the VA post‐fellowship (n = 223, 68%), with higher rates in rural areas (n = 19, 83%) compared to non‐rural fellows (n = 204, 67%), but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.16). Most of the 20 fellows trained and retained in rural areas were in clinical training programs: Mental Health Illness and Treatment (MIRECC) program (n = 6), Quality Scholars (n = 5), and Psychosocial Rehab (n = 3); a number were also retained in research‐based and clinically relevant Patient Safety program (n = 4).

Discussion

In this study examining VA Advanced Fellowship programs related to rural workforce retention, we found that only 6 of the 22 programs were offered in rural areas, that the majority of fellows who train in rural areas remain in rural areas, and that fellows who trained in rural areas were significantly more likely to be employed in rural areas post‐fellowship than those who trained in non‐rural areas. Further, the majority of fellows remained at or affiliated with the VA post‐fellowship, irrespective of training in rural versus non‐rural areas.

Broadly, these programs address important clinical, research, and training needs identified by the VA. Although the programs offered in rural areas are not strategically chosen to meet rural‐specific needs, they do address broad clinical priorities, such as mental health, geriatrics, and the quality and safety of care, that are important across settings but the need is especially acute in rural areas [6, 8, 9]. The rural retention rate for the VA Advanced Fellowships (63%) falls within the range of rural residency programs in the literature [14, 27, 28, 29]. Our study has limitations, including not having a control group to test outcomes, a relatively small number of rural participants, and a lower response rate at endpoint to assess post‐fellowship careers. However, this study provides evidence that these Fellowships hold promise as a strategy for the VA to leverage training programs to increase recruitment, training, and retention in rural areas and at the VA, which may play a role in alleviating high vacancy rates at rural facilities [10]. For instance, since only six of the 22 Advanced Fellowship programs were offered at rural‐serving VAMCs, there is opportunity to expand program offerings in rural locations. In addition, the VA could create more specific goals within existing programs or in new programs to emphasize rural‐focused topics and benefits. One newer program started in 2018, the 2‐year Rural Scholars Fellowship program, holds promise in this regard.

While this training program offers experience in a rural setting, and addresses the “role/career” aspect of Cosgrave's retention framework, there are important elements of the Framework that the program is not designed to address and were not assessed in this study. Opportunities for advanced training in rural areas are important for retention and building a skilled workforce, but should be paired with other interventions that more holistically meet long‐term workforce needs. This may include a workplace which supports the rural clinician and programs to integrate individuals into the rural community to foster a sense of belonging [15].

In conclusion, the Advanced Fellowship program is effective at retaining trainees at the VA and in rural areas, and should be considered as part of the VA's strategy to address rural workforce shortages. Ongoing research to assess its effectiveness for longitudinal retention, and barriers and facilitators to program expansion in rural areas, will assist in understanding future prospects for the program to address critical gaps in the rural workforce.
