A simple hand-sectioning approach for cellular-resolution imaging of anatomy and gene expression in small developing organs and boundary regions in Arabidopsis
Authors: Jae-Ung Hwang, Seonghwan Kim, Heejeong Son, June M. Kwak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrecise characterization of gene expression patterns across temporal, cellular, and tissue-specific contexts is fundamental to understanding plant development and function. Recent advances in ClearSee-based tissue clearing have enabled high-resolution visualization of internal structures and fluorescent reporter signals in plant tissues. Although hand sectioning can provide optical access to tissues that are not amenable to whole-mount clearing, its application to submillimeter-scale and fragile Arabidopsis organs and tissues, including developing inflorescence apices, flowers, fruits, and organ boundaries, has remained limited. Consequently, analysis of these tissues has largely depended on specialized microdissection techniques and labor-intensive histological workflows, such as wax- or resin-embedded microtomy, which restrict throughput, accessibility, and routine use.ResultsWe developed and optimized a simple hand-sectioning and imaging method that enables routine visualization of anatomical organization and gene expression patterns at cellular resolution in small, fragile Arabidopsis tissues. This method relies only on gentle manual tissue processing under a stereomicroscope and readily available reagents, allowing reproducible preparation of delicate tissues without the need for embedding or specialized equipment. Combined with ClearSee-based clearing and fluorescent reporters, the approach enables high-resolution imaging of internal tissue architecture and gene expression, while preserving tissue integrity and fluorescence signals that are often compromised during conventional embedding and microtomy procedures.ConclusionsOur method substantially reduces technical complexity, costs, preparation time, and labor associated with cellular-resolution imaging of small, fragile plant tissues. By providing a simple, scalable, and accessible alternative to conventional histological workflows, this approach facilitates routine analysis of internal developmental processes across diverse plant species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13007-026-01567-w.

Introduction

Plant development depends on the coordinated division and differentiation of cells into distinct types, each with unique structures and functions, governed by tightly regulated spatiotemporal gene expression programs. Fluorescent protein (FP) reporters, deployed as transcriptional or translational fusions, are indispensable tools for visualizing these expression patterns [1–3], enabling noninvasive, quantitative monitoring of gene expression dynamics and multiplexed imaging with spectrally distinct FPs. However, their utility diminishes with increasing imaging depth: light scattering, absorption, and autofluorescence degrade signal quality in deeper cell layers, even in partially transparent tissues [4, 5]. Consequently, single-cell resolution FP imaging has been largely restricted to surface-proximal cell layers, a constraint that broadly limits the analysis of developing tissues in which key regulatory cells occupy interior positions.

Overcoming these depth-resolution barriers is essential for elucidating gene regulatory networks in complex plant organs, yet current approaches each carry significant limitations. Chemical clearing techniques substantially improve tissue transparency [6], and ClearSee, in particular, is widely used to render plant tissues transparent while preserving diverse fluorescent proteins and dyes [7, 8]. However, whole-mount clearing of compact structures can take months to achieve adequate transparency [9], and imaging deep internal regions remains constrained by microscopy limitations. Physical sectioning circumvents this constraint by directly exposing interior cells and, when combined with clearing approaches, can further reduce the time required for clearing. However, the embedding-based microtomy, generally required for mechanically fragile tissues, is not only technically laborious and time-consuming but also incompatible with FP imaging. This limitation highlights the need for alternative approaches for visualizing interior cells.

A range of developmentally critical structures, including the inflorescence apex, the floral abscission zone, leaf nodes, and the silique dehiscence zone, mediate key processes such as meristem maintenance and termination, organ abscission, fruit opening, and represent important sites of cell type-specific gene regulation. These structures differ substantially in their optical and mechanical properties. Thin and partially translucent tissues such as inflorescence apex and pistil are amenable to whole-mount confocal imaging; however, fluorescent signals deteriorate with depth, limiting cellular resolution to surface-proximal layers and leaving the interior layers with insufficient resolution for reliable gene expression analysis [4, 10]. By contrast, abscission zones, siliques, and leaf nodes are optically dense, rendering whole-mount imaging ineffective for resolving internal cell layers. These structures are also minute and mechanically fragile, making them difficult to physically section without structural support. They have therefore been analyzed primarily using wax- or resin-embedded sectioning, which involves labor-intensive and time-consuming steps, including dehydration, infiltration, and microtomy, followed by mounting and imaging of tens to hundreds of individual microsections [11–13]. Gene expression in such preparations is typically examined using GUS reporter assays or in situ hybridization [12–14] rather than FP reporters, as the embedding process is generally incompatible with FP imaging. A method that can deliver high-resolution FP imaging across the full depth of these structures, without the burden of embedding or prolonged clearing, would therefore substantially expand the experimental toolkit available for studying plant development.

Here, we present an accessible hand-sectioning and imaging method for visualizing FP reporter expression in deep cell layers across diverse Arabidopsis tissues, including submillimeter-sized and mechanically fragile structures that were not previously amenable to this approach. Without the need for laborious embedding and using only basic laboratory equipment, the method delivers high-quality confocal imaging of interior cell layers that have been largely inaccessible to conventional fluorescence microscopy. We provide practical guidance for hand-sectioning even highly delicate tissues with minimal damage, and describe an integrated optimization of tissue clearing, cell wall counterstaining, and slide mounting to preserve specimen integrity while improving optical accessibility. Together, these advances substantially reduce preparation time compared with embedding-based workflows and open new opportunities to investigate the spatiotemporal gene expression programs that underlie plant development.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Arabidopsis thaliana plants (ecotype Columbia-0, Col-0) were cultivated in soil in a growth chamber at 22 °C ± 2 °C under a 16-h photoperiod with a light intensity of 110 µmol m− 2 s− 1.

Plasmid construction and generation of transgenic plants

The promoter regions (~ 1.5 kb upstream of the start codon) were cloned to generate transcriptional reporter constructs fused to nls-GFP, nls-mCherry, or H2B-TdTomato using the Gateway cloning system (Invitrogen) as described in [15]. The resulting constructs were introduced into Arabidopsis thaliana via the floral dip method [16].

Solutions

ClearSee solution was prepared following Kurihara et al. [7]. Briefly, xylitol 50 g (10% w/v), urea 125 g (25% w/v), and sodium deoxycholate 75 g (15% w/v) were added sequentially to 200 mL of distilled water, each fully dissolved by stirring at 37–50 °C before the next reagent. The final volume was adjusted to 500 mL. Calcofluor White (CW) solution (0.01%) was prepared fresh by diluting Fluorescent Brightener 28 solution (Sigma, 910090-20ML, 25%) into ClearSee. Auramine O (0.1%) and basic fuchsin (0.2%) solutions were prepared fresh by dissolving auramine O powder (Sigma, 861030–25 g, dye content 85%) and basic fuchsin powder (Sigma, 47860–25 g) in ClearSee solution respectively, protected from light, and stirred at room temperature until complete dissolution. Auramine O and basic fuchsin solutions were filtered (0.2-µm) before use.

Hand-sectioning procedures of Arabidopsis organs and boundary regions

Hand-sectioning was performed under a Leica S8APO stereomicroscope (Figure S2). Flowers and siliques with intact pedicels were harvested and stabilized on a moistened glass slide or filter paper using forceps or fingers (Fig. S2a–c). For improved stability, samples were mounted using carrot blocks, low-adhesion tape, or high-adhesion tape (Fig. S2b, d, e). Standard 0.1-mm-thick double-edged razor blades (Dorco ST300) were snapped in half and used for manual sectioning (Fig. S2f), held perpendicular to the tissue surface to minimize tissue deformation (Fig. S2g), and replaced after 2–3 cuts to maintain sharpness.

For longitudinal sectioning, tissues were bisected along the longitudinal axis and trimmed to retain ~ 5 mm around the region of interest (Figs. S3, S4). Sectioned tissues were immediately transferred to a prechilled multi-well plate containing 4% PFA on ice. For transverse sectioning, samples were sectioned as thinly as possible across the longitudinal axis at defined positions (Figs. S3, S4). Sections were immediately identified under the stereomicroscope and transferred into 4% PFA for fixation (Figs S3, S4).

Fixation, tissue clearing, and cell wall counterstaining

Immediately after sectioning, the tissues were transferred into prechilled 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (T&I, BPP-9004). Longitudinal sections were handled carefully by holding the distal portion of the region of interest with fine forceps (Fig. S4a). Transverse sections were first treated with a droplet of 1× PBS (pH 7.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific 70011044) and then transferred using a wide-bore pipette tip (Fig. S3c) or lifted adhering to a syringe needle tip (Fig. S4b). Samples were vacuum-infiltrated (25 mmHg, 15 min on ice) to enhance fixative penetration, sealed, and incubated overnight at 4 °C.

For tissue clearing, the PFA solution was carefully aspirated and replaced with ClearSee solution (2 mL per well). Plates were sealed, protected from light, and incubated at RT with shaking at 80 rpm for 2–7 days until tissues became transparent. The solution was refreshed if needed. In most cases, 2 days was sufficient for sufficient transparency, and shorter clearing (1 day) could be used when combined with subsequent staining.

For cell wall staining, the ClearSee solution was carefully aspirated and replaced with 0.01% CW solution. Plates were sealed, protected from light, and incubated at RT with shaking at 80 rpm for 16–24 h. For samples subjected to CW staining only, the staining solution was aspirated after incubation, and samples were washed with fresh ClearSee solution for an additional 16 h to 2 days, with the solution refreshed once.

For subsequent staining, the CW solution was carefully replaced with the respective staining solution without intermediate washing. For auramine O staining, samples were incubated in 0.1% auramine O solution overnight at RT with shaking in the dark, followed by washing with fresh ClearSee (30–60 min, at least twice). For basic fuchsin staining, samples were incubated in 0.2% basic fuchsin overnight at RT with shaking in the dark, after which the solution was replaced with fresh ClearSee and incubated overnight with one solution change. All samples were handled carefully under a stereomicroscope to prevent physical damage or accidental loss of the cleared, fragile sections.

Tissue mounting for CLSM imaging

Cleared sections are highly transparent and mechanically fragile, so they require careful mounting to prevent deformation or loss. To maintain appropriate spacing between cover glasses, strips of paper label tape combined with double-sided tape or small dabs of vacuum grease were applied to create spacers (Fig. S5a, b). The tape-based method was suitable for relatively rigid samples such as longitudinal receptacle or inflorescence apex sections, whereas vacuum grease was preferred for more fragile samples, including whole or transversely sectioned pistils or siliques. To facilitate efficient localization of transparent samples during CLSM imaging, droplets of ClearSee solution (2–5 µL) were placed on the bottom cover glass (Fig. S5e). Individual sections were transferred into the droplets, and excess solution was carefully removed to ensure the droplets remained isolated and did not merge. When mounting multiple samples, section positions were identified and marked under a stereomicroscope after mounting (Fig. S5f).

Proper section orientation was essential, as the effective imaging depth of CLSM is limited to a few cell layers. Sections were oriented prior to final mounting by gently manipulating them with fine syringe needles (Fig. S5d). For longitudinal receptacle sections, the internal cut surface was oriented toward the bottom cover glass for internal imaging, whereas the epidermal surface was oriented toward the glass for external imaging. After positioning the samples, a top cover glass was gently added and lightly tapped to promote contact between the sample and the glass. For extremely fragile samples, the top cover glass was omitted or positioned without contact, and imaging was performed from the bottom side (Fig. S5c). In such cases, the volume of ClearSee solution was minimized to prevent sample drift. The mounted slides were kept in a horizontal position to prevent tissue displacement and imaged on the same day whenever possible.

Results

To improve optical accessibility to internal regions of submillimeter-sized organs and boundary regions, we integrated hand-sectioning with ClearSee-based clearing and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) to establish a robust, reproducible protocol for the preparation, clearing, and mounting of fragile tissues. The method performs reliably across a broad range of delicate samples (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4; Fig. S1) and, compared with conventional histological approaches, markedly reduces processing time and labor while enabling high-resolution imaging of internal anatomy and spatially resolved fluorescent reporter expression (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Development of a workflow for hand-sectioning, clearing, and mounting small, fragile plant tissues

We first evaluated several conventional approaches for analyzing Arabidopsis receptacles, which are approximately 300–500 μm in diameter and 100–200 μm in height at stages 13–17 (Fig. S1a). However, each conventional approach proved unsuitable for the routine analysis of floral abscission zones within the receptacles. Vibratome sectioning failed to produce clean cuts, and sections were difficult to recover. Microtomy of fixed and embedded samples yielded high-quality sections but was excessively time-consuming and labor-intensive for routine use (Table 1). Whole-mount imaging of cleared samples by light-sheet fluorescence microscopy (LSFM) or CLSM was similarly ineffective, as limited light penetration prevented visualization of inner cell layers even after prolonged clearing (Fig. 1). We therefore developed and optimized a hand-sectioning workflow, with critical technical considerations outlined below.

First, because the receptacles were submillimeter in size, sectioning was performed under a stereomicroscope. As obtaining thin sections from such small and fragile organs was technically impractical, the samples for longitudinal imaging were simply bisected along the longitudinal axis, whereas samples for transverse imaging were sliced transversely as thinly as possible. Standard 0.1 mm-thick double-edged razor blades snapped in half were used and replaced frequently to maintain maximal sharpness (Fig. S2). Sections were generated with a gentle oscillatory slicing motion rather than direct downward pressure, thereby minimizing tissue deformation and mechanical damage. Although the outermost cell layer at the cut surface was inevitably disrupted, an inherent artifact of sectioning without wax or resin embedding, confocal optical sectioning readily penetrated beyond the damaged region, enabling high-resolution visualization of intact underlying cell layers with high signal-to-noise ratios.

Second, samples were stabilized primarily by gently holding them with fine forceps or a fingertip (Figs. S2b, c), supplemented where necessary with adhesive tape of varying adhesion strength or a small carrot block (Figs. S2, S3, S4). Low-adhesion tape, such as SEM carbon tape, provided mild anchoring while permitting easy specimen recovery (Figs. 3a, b), whereas high-adhesion tape offered stronger stabilization but required leaving the region of interest untouched for subsequent retrieval (Figs. S2e, S4b). In the carrot-based method, samples were inserted into a narrow slit, enabling simultaneous sectioning of both the tissue and the supporting block (Fig. S2d).

Third, all specimen transfers to fixative or mounting media were performed carefully under a stereomicroscope, minimizing mechanical damage and accidental loss. For longitudinal sections, extra-tissue regions were intentionally retained during trimming to serve as forceps handles (Fig. S4a). For transverse sections lacking such support, specimens were transferred either by gentle pipetting or by lifting with a syringe needle (Figs. S3c, S4b).

Fourth, all solution exchanges were performed under a stereomicroscope to prevent accidental loss of specimens. During each exchange, the previous solution was only partially aspirated before fresh solution was gently added, avoiding complete liquid removal at any step.

Fifth, to facilitate specimen localization during CLSM imaging, each specimen was mounted individually within a discrete ClearSee droplet, which served as a positional marker once sandwiched between cover glasses (Fig. S5e).

Sixth, the top cover glass was placed gently without applying pressure to avoid crushing fragile specimens. For extremely delicate samples, the top cover glass was either suspended above the specimen or omitted entirely, with imaging performed exclusively from below (Fig. S5c). In these cases, the volume of mounting medium (ClearSee) was minimized to just cover the bottom focal plane, as excess medium promoted specimen drift and compromised image quality.

Using this workflow, we successfully mapped the spatial expression of key regulatory genes during post-abscission residuum cell differentiation [15] and further demonstrated its utility for analyzing a wide range of developing organs and boundary tissues (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4).

Longitudinal sections of inflorescence apices and stem nodes resolve the spatial expression of boundary genes at cellular resolution

The inflorescence apex, which contains the meristem, flower primordia, and floral buds, has traditionally been analyzed by whole-mount imaging of live or cleared tissues, or by sectioning following wax or resin embedding, typically in combination with in situ hybridization or GUS assays. Whole-mount imaging permits spatiotemporal analysis of gene expression near the tissue surface but has limited capacity to resolve deeper cell layers. Embedding and microtomy provide high internal resolution yet are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and often incompatible with fluorescent reporters. Our workflow overcomes both limitations, enabling high-resolution imaging of internal cell layers while preserving fluorescent signals.

To demonstrate this, we examined the spatiotemporal expression of BLADE-ON-PETIOLE 2 (BOP2) in longitudinal sections of inflorescence apices from pBOP2:nls-GFP plants. BOP2 expression was absent from the inflorescence meristem but clearly detected in flower primordia and at sepal axils of young buds (Fig. 2a). By stage 7, the signal at the sepal axil had expanded to the abaxial side of the sepal base, forming a circumferential band, with additional expression at the bases of stamens and stamen stalks. This pattern is consistent with previous reports [17, 18], but is here resolved at substantially higher spatial resolution.

We further applied this approach to stem-cauline leaf nodes, where axillary meristems form, to examine the spatial expression of boundary genes (Fig. 2b). Both CUP-SHAPED COTYLEDON 1 (CUC1) and LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARIES (LOB) were expressed at this node. CUC1 signal was detected in a cluster of adaxial cells, particularly at the base of the lateral branch emerging from the leaf axil, while LOB displayed a similar but more spatially restricted pattern, confined to the adaxial base of the branch and accessory bud.

Our approach enables single-cell-resolution visualization of gene expression in deeply positioned cell layers and supports quantitative comparison based on FP signal intensity. Unlike GUS assays, it avoids artifacts arising from variable substrate penetration and diffusion of the reaction product. Together, these attributes make our method a more precise and informative alternative to conventional histological approaches.

Hand-sectioning enables cellular-resolution mapping of spatial gene expression during silique development

To extend the application of FP reporters to Arabidopsis silique development, we applied our hand-sectioning workflow to pistils and siliques at floral stages 10–17 (Fig. 3). Transverse sectioning was the primary approach, as longitudinal bisection often compromised tissue integrity; whole-mount, cleared pistils were reserved for longitudinal imaging.

We first examined the epidermis-specific pATML1:nls-GFP reporter [19]. GFP fluorescence was consistently detected in epidermal cell layers across developing tissues, including the exocarp epidermis, endocarp a layer, septum and funiculus epidermis, and outer integument 2 layer and endothelium of the ovule and seed (Fig. 3a, b), with signal intensity gradually diminishing as silique development progressed (Fig. 3a). When combined with basic fuchsin staining, red fluorescence from the cuticle of the pod, ovule, and seed, and from lignified cell walls in the dehiscence zone, vascular tissues, and endocarp b layer, was detected alongside GFP, enabling simultaneous visualization of epidermal identity and cell wall architecture.

We next applied this approach to resolve the spatial expression patterns of KNOTTED1-LIKE IN ARABIDOPSIS THALIANA 1 (KNAT1) and KNAT2, key regulators of dehiscence zone formation [20]. Previous studies suggested that KNAT1 is expressed in the replum while KNAT2 is localized to the valve margin, with KNAT2 repressing KNAT1 to regulate replum size [21–23]. Whole-mount imaging, however, provided limited spatial resolution: pKNAT1:nls-GFP signal was detected broadly in the replum, while the precise expression domain of KNAT2 remained ambiguous (Fig. 3c, d, right panels). Transverse sections resolved both domains at cellular resolution: pKNAT1:nls-GFP was confined to a subset of replum cells, likely parenchyma associated with xylem vessels (Fig. 3c), whereas pKNAT2:nls-GFP was detected in replum cells outside the KNAT1-expressing domain, with stronger signal in the adaxial part close to funiculi and little or no expression in the valve margin itself (yellow arrowheads, Fig. 3d).

These observations suggest that KNAT1 and KNAT2 may occupy spatially distinct, largely non-overlapping domains within the replum itself, rather than being segregated across the replum–valve margin boundary as previously inferred. This intra-replum partitioning suggests a refined model in which their complementary expression patterns contribute to replum patterning and size regulation. Together, these results demonstrate that the hand-sectioning workflow enables robust, high-resolution mapping of gene expression in developing pistils and siliques, resolving cellular-scale patterns that are difficult to distinguish using whole-mount imaging.

High-resolution spatial mapping of abscission-related genes in floral abscission zones

We applied our workflow to resolve the spatial distribution of key abscission regulators, including BOP2, HAESA (HAE), INFLORESCENCE DEFICIENT IN ABSCISSION (IDA), QRT2, EXORDIUM-LIKE 1 (EXL1), HAESA-LIKE 2 (HSL2), and MYB74 (Figs. 4 and S6); [15, 24, 25]. Consistent with previous reports, all seven genes were expressed at boundaries of the receptacle and floral organs (Fig. 4), yet higher-resolution imaging revealed markedly distinct spatial patterns. pBOP2:nls-GFP expression was broadly distributed across the organ junction (Fig. 4a), whereas pHAE:nls-GFP and pHSL2:nls-GFP signals were concentrated in AZ cells on the receptacle side (Fig. 4b and S6d). pIDA:nlsGFP signal, by contrast, was confined to the shedding organs, with little or no signal in receptacle AZ cells (Fig. 4c).

To further refine these spatial relationships, we performed multiplexed imaging of transgenic lines co-expressing spectrally distinct dual reporters: pMYB74:nls-GFP with pQRT2:nls-mCherry (Fig. S6a), pMYB74:nls-GFP with pEXL1:nls-mCherry (Fig. S6b), pMYB74:nls-GFP with pHAE:nls-mCherry (Fig. S6c), and pMYB74:nls-GFP with pHSL2:nls-mCherry (Fig. S6d). QRT2, encoding a polygalacturonase required for cell wall degradation during abscission [26], and pQRT2:nls-mCherry signal showed confined expression to a small number of AZ cell layers, likely corresponding to the residuum and secession cells directly involved in separation. pEXL1:nls-mCherry displayed a similarly restricted pattern. Transcriptional reporters of QRT2 and EXL1 have been used to define these cell types in our transcriptomic analyses [15, 24].

MYB74, a key regulator of post-abscission residuum transdifferentiation [15], and pMYB74:nls-GFP was expressed across a broader range of AZ cell layers, including cells not directly involved in separation (Fig. S6). pHAE:nls-mCherry and pHSL2:nls-mCherry signals similarly spanned multiple AZ cell layers (Fig. S6c, d), but remained restricted to the receptacle side. Together, these results reveal a clear spatial hierarchy among abscission regulators: from highly restricted expression in separation-layer cells (QRT2 and EXL1) to broader domains encompassing multiple AZ layers (MYB74, HAE, and HSL2). Our workflow resolves these distinctions at single-cell resolution within intact tissue architecture and, compared with conventional resin microsectioning, is more cost-effective and time-efficient while maintaining comparable structural resolution (Table 1) and additionally supporting simultaneous visualization of multiple fluorescent reporters.

Discussion

A practical and accessible alternative to embedding- and microtomy-based histology for small developing organs and boundary regions

Our optimized hand-sectioning and CLSM-based workflow provides a rapid, practical, and high-resolution alternative to traditional histological methods for analysis of small, fragile organs and boundary regions (Table 1). Unlike paraffin-, Technovit-, or cryo-embedding protocols, which demand labor-intensive embedding or cryoprotection procedures over multiple days, our approach enables rapid sample preparation with minimal technical complexity. This substantially reduces experimental turnaround time and hands-on effort, facilitating routine anatomical and gene expression analyses in internal tissues. Although embedding-based approaches remain indispensable for submicron sectioning precision, the present workflow offers a highly practical alternative for routine FP-based imaging of fragile developing organs and boundaries.

A further advantage is the robust preservation of native FP signals. Standard paraffin- and Technovit-embedding procedures typically quench FP fluorescence and may require expensive post-embedding reactivation steps, whereas our method is inherently compatible with FP reporters [27], enabling simultaneous visualization of multiple gene expression patterns at cellular resolution. This represents a marked improvement over GUS-based assays, which are limited to a single reporter and provide lower spatial resolution.

Our workflow also substantially reduces the labor associated with sectioning and imaging. Physical sectioning is limited to rapid hand-sectioning, after which automated optical Z-sectioning by CLSM yields full volumetric information, eliminating the need for extensive microtomy, serial section mounting, and sequential imaging of large numbers of sections. This not only simplifies the workflow but also facilitates three-dimensional reconstruction of internal tissue architecture. In addition, the method is cost-effective and broadly accessible, requiring neither specialized embedding reagents nor expensive equipment such as microtomes or cryostats, and avoiding recurring consumables costs associated with precision sectioning blades. A standard stereomicroscope and CLSM are sufficient for high-resolution internal tissue imaging, even in laboratories without dedicated histology infrastructure.

Complementarity with whole-mount imaging approaches

Whole-mount CLSM and LSFM imaging have greatly advanced visualization of plant development, particularly in optically accessible tissues such as meristems and thin organs [28–31], yet they remain limited in dense, structurally complex tissues where light penetration is insufficient and spatial resolution is compromised, as demonstrated by the whole-mount LSFM or CLSM analyses shown in Fig. 1. Our method directly addresses this limitation by combining rapid hand-sectioning with tissue clearing and CLSM, enabling high-resolution imaging of internal cell layers with high signal-to-noise ratios in tissues including inflorescence apices, floral receptacles, leaf nodes, pistils, and siliques. Reduced tissue thickness also substantially shortens clearing time compared with whole-mount protocols, which can take weeks to months for dense tissues. Importantly, the approach relies entirely on widely available CLSM infrastructure and does not require specialized LSFM systems or advanced deep-imaging platforms. As a result, it provides a scalable and broadly accessible solution for high-resolution imaging of internal tissues across diverse plant organs.

Technical considerations and practical scope

Despite technical advantages, our method carries practical limitations. Because it relies on hand-sectioning, it is not suitable for tissues that cannot be reliably sectioned under a stereomicroscope, and section quality may vary depending on operator experience. To facilitate reproducibility and broader adoption, we provide detailed step-by-step guidance (Supplementary Methods) together with representative sectioning outcomes for each tissue type we examined (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). Tissue-specific properties, including cell wall composition, rigidity, and tissue density, also differ across organs. Consequently, sectioning thickness, clearing duration, and counterstaining conditions may need to be optimized when adapting the workflow to new tissue types. Nevertheless, the method achieves a favorable balance of resolution, speed, cost, and accessibility.

The FP reporters used in this study are transcriptional reporters driven by native promoters. This design was selected to validate the spatiotemporal gene expression patterns identified from RNA-seq analyses of floral abscission zones [15, 24, 32]. Because such reporters provide a readout of promoter activity, their expression patterns can be more directly compared with transcriptomic data, making them suitable for this purpose. However, transcriptional reporters do not always fully represent endogenous transcript or protein distribution. This can occur when regulatory elements lie outside the cloned promoter region or when post-transcriptional and post-translational regulations influence their expression patterns. Nuclear-localized FPs, as used in this study, enhance detection sensitivity by concentrating fluorescence in the nucleus, allowing weak promoter activity to be detected. The resulting fluorescence signal, however, reflects accumulated promoter activity over time rather than instantaneous gene expression. Therefore, reporter selection should be guided by the specific biological question, and the resulting expression patterns should be interpreted in consideration of the inherent properties and limitations of each reporter system. Our method is not restricted to transcriptional reporters and can be readily extended to translational fusions and endogenous tagging strategies when direct analysis of protein localization or dynamics is required.

By preserving native FP fluorescence and enabling high-resolution imaging of internal cell layers, this workflow provides a practical approach for analyzing spatiotemporal gene expression patterns in delicate, structurally complex plant tissues. We anticipate that this method will complement existing imaging and histological approaches by facilitating cell-resolution analysis of gene expression in developing organs and boundary regions that are challenging to examine using conventional approaches.
