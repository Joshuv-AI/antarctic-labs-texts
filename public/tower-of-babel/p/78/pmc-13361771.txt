Efficacy and Safety of 0.3% Preservative-Free Hyaluronic Acid-Based Eye Drops for the Management of Moderate to Severe Dry Eye Disease: A Prospective, Multicenter, Clinical Investigation
Authors: Rita Mencucci, Matilde Buzzi, Carlotta Bernasconi, Claudia Desiderio, Silvia Sonego, Alfonso Strianese, Raffaella Gioia, Giovanna Carnovale-Scalzo, Vincenzo Scorcia, Paolo Fogagnolo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeDry eye disease (DED) is a prevalent and chronic condition with multifactorial etiology. This study evaluated the efficacy and safety of a isotonic, preservative-free 0.3% hyaluronic acid (HA)-based ophthalmic solution (BLUgel A free), in patients with moderate-to-severe DED.Patients and MethodsThis multicenter, prospective, post-market investigation enrolled 51 patients receiving HA eye drops. Most participants had comorbidities and were on polytherapy. Outcomes included changes in NEI grading scale, tear-film break-up time (TFBUT), Schirmer’s test I, IDEEL questionnaire scores, non-invasive Break-Up Time (niBUT), tear meniscus height, conjunctival hyperaemia and meibography, assessed at 30 and 60 days.ResultsA significant improvement in ocular surface integrity was observed throughout the study period. The NEI score decreased markedly from 8.38 ± 2.17 at baseline to 4.04 ± 2.93 after 30 days and further to 3.60 ± 3.30 at 60 days. Tear-film stability also improved, with TFBUT increasing significantly from 3.65 seconds at baseline to 6.51 seconds on day 30, and 7.26 seconds on day 60. Tear production, assessed by Schirmer’s test, increased from 5.70 ± 2.82 mm to 8.74 ± 3.94 mm and then to 9.62 ± 4.57 mm over the same period. Patient-reported outcomes reflected these clinical improvements: IDEEL symptoms-bother scores dropped from 58.82 ± 10.57 to 40.99 ± 11.09, surpassing the clinically meaningful threshold of 12 points. Safety was confirmed, with only one minor adverse event (blurred vision) reported.Conclusion0.30% HA-based eye drops proved to be an effective and well-tolerated treatment for moderate-to-severe DED, offering clinical benefits even in patients receiving concomitant systemic therapies.

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a common chronic ocular condition, affecting 5–50% of the global population.1,2 The Tear Film & Ocular Surface Society (TFOS) recently defined it as “multifactorial, symptomatic disease characterized by a loss of homeostasis of the tear film and/or ocular surface, in which tear film instability and hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation and damage, and neurosensory abnormalities are etiological factors”.3

DED arises from increased tear evaporation and/or reduced aqueous tear film resulting in evaporative dry eye (EDE) or aqueous tear-deficient dry eye (ADDE), respectively, although overlap is common.4 The subsequent hyperosmolarity triggers epithelial stress and a cascade of morphological changes, including apoptosis of conjunctival and corneal cells. This process, compounded by inflammation and the loss of mucin-producing goblet cells, perpetuates tear-film instability and sustains the vicious cycle of the disease.1,3,5–7

Its multifactorial nature and mixed presentation have long challenged clinicians in diagnosis and management. DED significantly impairs visual function, daily activities, and overall quality of life, with substantial personal and societal costs.2,8

Risk factors include increasing age, female sex, contact lens wear, systemic medications (eg., antihistamines, antidepressants), and environmental stressors such as pollution and low humidity.1,7,9 Environmental exposure plays an important role in both the development and severity of symptoms.10 Common manifestations include blurred vision, itching, pain, ocular fatigue, eyelid redness, light sensitivity, foreign body sensation, and watery eyes in response to irritation.11

Understanding disease severity is essential when considering therapeutic options.11 Several grading schemes and classification systems have been proposed, although no universal consensus exists.12 The first DEWS (2007) introduced a four-level severity scale, ranging from grade 1 (mild) to grade 4 (very severe), based on clinical thresholds.12,13 Subsequent updates in DEWS II and DEWS III underscored the limited value of rigid, threshold-based grading, considering the limited correlation between signs and symptoms, and promoted a more comprehensive, mechanism-driven framework that integrates etiological factors and symptomatic burden.3,14

Management of DED aims to relieve symptoms, stabilize the tear film, and protect the ocular surface.5,15 Given the complexity and multifactorial nature of the disease, a wide range of therapeutic strategies has been developed, ranging from education and environmental modification to tear substitutes, punctal plugs and topical and/or systemic anti-inflammatory medications, up to surgery where appropriate.14,15 Moreover, DED often coexists with systemic inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Stevens–Johnson syndrome, and Sjögren’s disease, where immune-mediated damage to the lacrimal glands leads to reduced tear production and altered tear composition.7,16 The presence of comorbidities further complicate the management of the underlying disease, with patients frequently being prescribed multiple medications and requiring a more coordinated approach to improve compliance and optimize treatment outcome.17 Findings from a population-based study revealed that hypertension was the most prevalent comorbidity in the DED group compared with the non-DED group. The authors further noted that patients with hypertension and cardiovascular disease are more likely to be prescribed anticholinergic medications, which may interfere with ocular homeostasis and exacerbate DED symptoms.17 Moreover, evidence suggests a correlation between ocular manifestations and systemic comorbidities, particularly with respect to the deterioration of ocular surface parameters.18

Tear substitutes are the mainstay of DED care. Among these, hyaluronic acid (HA)-based eye drops have shown efficacy in alleviating symptoms and improving tear-film stability, contributing to the restoration of ocular surface homeostasis.19–22 Despite the availability of numerous ophthalmic solutions for DED, the presence of concomitant pathological conditions may limit their effectiveness or applicability.

The concentration of HA in commercially available eye solutions can vary substantially, ranging from 0.1% to 0.4%,23 with higher concentrations typically used to treat more severe cases.23,24

Moreover, increasing attention has been directed towards the use of preservatives in topical medications, due to their potential association with more severe symptomatology and ocular surface inflammation, particularly in patients with a complex clinical profile.14,25–28

This prospective, multicenter clinical study was therefore designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a 0.3% preservative-free HA solution in the treatment of moderate-to-severe DED, including patients receiving systemic medications.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Population

This prospective, post-market, multicenter clinical investigation aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of 0.30% HA-based ophthalmic solution in patients with moderate to severe DED. The study was conducted across three Italian university hospitals, located in Florence, Catanzaro, and Milan.

This investigation followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Comitato Etico Regionale per la Sperimentazione Clinica della Regione Toscana - Area Vasta Centro (protocol number 23197_spe).

The investigation included adults (≥18 years), of either sex, with a confirmed diagnosis of moderate to severe DED, provided they had given written informed consent.

Diagnosis of DED was established through at least the following exams: slit lamp examination (SLE), tear (lacrimal) meniscus exam, Schirmer’s test I, TFBUT, cornea and conjunctiva staining, IDEEL questionnaire (“Dry Eye Symptom-Bother” module).

Eligible participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: tear film break-up time (TFBUT) < 7 sec; Schirmer’s test I < 7 mm; fluorescein staining (NEI scale value) ≥7; IDEEL questionnaire (Dry Eye Symptom-Bother module) score >50. Patients with comorbidities (eg. rheumatological diseases) that were under systemic control were not excluded.

The main exclusion criteria were: the use of gel-based treatments for DED within 15 days prior to enrollment; current treatment with topical corticosteroids, antibiotics, hypotony-inducing agents, or any therapy potentially interfering with efficacy or safety assessments; presence of systemic or ocular conditions (eg., malignancies, neuro-oncological diseases); diagnosis of glaucoma; use of therapeutic or refractive contact lenses; history of ocular surgery within 90 days prior to enrollment (excluding corneal refractive or cataract procedures); current use of medications such as antidepressants, antiepileptics, antihistamines, or anticholinergics. Full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Table S1.

Patients with moderate to severe DED underwent a 60-day treatment period with HA-based solution (2 drops per eye in the conjunctival sac, 3 times a day). The assessment was conducted at baseline (time of the diagnosis, T0), T1 (Day 15), T2 (Day 30) and T3 (Day 60). In case of bilateral DED, both eyes were treated but only the most severe eye was considered for assessments. In the case of bilateral DED with both eyes having the same level of impairment, the right eye was considered for assessments by convention. Evaluations of both the eyes were collected but only target eye evaluations were used for statistical analysis.

Study Objectives

The primary objective was to assess the efficacy of 0.3% HA-based eye drops in improving ocular surface health. To achieve this, the primary endpoint was defined as the change in the overall score of corneal and conjunctival fluorescein staining, as measured by the National Eye Institute (NEI) grading scale,29 from baseline to T2.

Secondary objectives included a broader evaluation of efficacy, specifically: promoting qualitative normalization of the tear film; improving the condition of the corneal and conjunctival surfaces; and enhancing patient-reported ocular symptoms and their impact on vision.

Secondary endpoints included the change from baseline to T3 in NEI score, as well as changes from baseline to T2 and T3 in TFBUT, Schirmer’s Test I, non-invasive Break-Up Time (niBUT), tear meniscus height, conjunctival hyperaemia as per the CCRLU (Cornea and Contact Lens Research Unit) score and meibography. Changes in patient-reported symptoms were evaluated at both time points through the IDEEL questionnaire.

The safety profile of the device was monitored throughout the investigation through patient reporting and direct observation by the investigator at each visit. Specific parameters included: incidence and nature of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), serious TEAEs, adverse device effects (ADEs), serious ADEs, and any potential investigational medical device deficiencies (IMDDs).

The summary of the endpoint assessment schedule across study visits is provided in Table S2.

Assessment Tools

Corneal and conjunctival damage was graded according to NEI/Industry Workshop guidelines. The cornea was divided into five sectors, each scored from 0 (no damage) to 3 (severe damage), for a maximum score of 15. The nasal and temporal conjunctiva were subdivided into three areas each, also scored 0–3, yielding a maximum conjunctival score of 18. The combined NEI total score ranged from 0 to 33.

Tear film stability refers to the ability of the tear layer on the eye to remain smooth and continuous between blinks and was assessed using the TFBUT test, with values below 10 seconds considered indicative of instability. Tear production was measured with Schirmer’s test I, performed without anesthesia, and expressed in millimeters of moistened strip after 5 minutes.

The Tearcheck® - an innovative device with an integrated screen that analyzes the quality and quantity of the tear film - was used to evaluate niBUT, tear meniscus height, conjunctival hyperaemia (graded using the CCLRU score from 1 to 4), and meibography.30

Dry eye symptom burden was assessed using the Symptom-Bother module of the IDEEL questionnaire.31 The score, ranging from 0 to 100, was calculated if at least 50% of the items were completed, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity.

Description of the Device

The device administered is BLUgel A free (Fidia farmaceutici, Italy), an isotonic sterile ophthalmic solution containing Hyaluronic Acid Sodium Salt 0.3% in a formulation enriched with amino acids. It presents an intrinsic viscosity of 17.7 ± 0.2 dL/g; zero shear viscosity of 24.4 mPa; molecular weight of 1070.10 ± 10 kDa; and hydrodynamic radius of 64 ± 1 nm.

The device is supplied with an OSD (Ophthalmic Squeeze Dispenser) dropper equipped with absolute filter and valve system that guarantees sterility without the need of preservatives.

Statistical Methods

Four analysis populations were defined for this clinical investigation. The enrolled set comprised all patients who provided informed consent. The safety set (SAF) included all enrolled patients who received at least one dose of the investigational product. The intent‑to‑treat (ITT) population consisted of all patients who received at least one dose and had both a baseline assessment and at least one post‑baseline assessment recorded. The per‑protocol (PP) population included patients who completed the treatment period without any major or otherwise relevant protocol deviations.

Safety analyses were conducted using the SAF population, whereas the primary and secondary performance endpoints were analyzed using the ITT population. A sensitivity analysis of the primary performance endpoint was additionally performed using the PP population.

Only results from the ITT and SAF populations are presented.

Only the target eye was considered in the statistical analyses. All p-values were rounded to four decimal places, and statistical significance was declared if the rounded p-value was less than 0.05. Two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated unless otherwise specified.

Each primary performance endpoint was summarized at the scheduled visits using standard descriptive statistics, along with relative changes from baseline within each patient cohort. The change in NEI overall score from baseline to Day 30 was assessed using a paired t-test. Secondary performance endpoints were summarized using standard descriptive statistics at each clinical visit, according to the nature of the variable. The change in NEI overall score from baseline to Day 60 was also evaluated using a paired t-test.

Changes from baseline at each visit (Day 30 and Day 60) for all secondary endpoints were analyzed using a repeated measures ANCOVA model. The model included baseline (pre-treatment) values as covariates and visit and center as fixed effects. Adjusted means at each timepoint were reported along with 95% confidence intervals and corresponding p-values.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was calculated for the primary endpoint, defined as the change from baseline in the overall corneal and conjunctival fluorescein staining score assessed using the NEI grading scale. A total of 44 patients was estimated to provide 90% power to detect a non-zero mean change using a two-sided paired t-test, assuming a medium standardized mean change (Cohen’s dz = 0.5) and a significance level of 0.05. To account for an anticipated 10% drop-out rate, 50 patients were planned for enrollment.

Sample size determination and all statistical analyses were performed using SAS® software version 9.4.

Results

Characteristics of the Cohort

Out of the 51 patients enrolled, 50 represented the ITT population and 46 represented the PP. All patients (N=51) received at least one dose of study treatment and were therefore included in the SAF. A detailed diagram of the populations analyzed is available in Figure S1. The mean age was 67.7 ± 13.8 years, 74.5% were women (N=38), 36 of whom were post-menopausal (70.6%). All patients had bilateral DED. Ten subjects had history of DED, while the remaining were diagnosed at baseline. Six patients (11.8%) reported a history of at least one previous disease, predominantly related to surgical interventions (intraocular lens implantation N=2, myopia correction N=1, retinal laser coagulation N=1, thyroidectomy N=1). Systemic concomitant conditions were also common: 47 patients (92.2%) had at least one, including cardiac disorders (N=4; 7.8%), eye disorders (N=13; 25.5%), hypercholesterolemia (N=12; 23.5%), hypertension (N=23; 45.1%), and musculoskeletal/connective-tissue disorders (N=13; 25.5%). Among the latter, Sjögren’s syndrome was present in 9 patients (17.6%) and rheumatoid arthritis in 3 (5.9%). Concomitant systemic medications were used by 36 patients (70.6%). At baseline, mean TFBUT was 3.65 ± 1.59 s (95% CI: 2.00–5.00 s). Figure 1B reveals a sharp increase at T2 (6.51 ± 2.58 s; 95% CI: 4.00–8.00 s; p<0.0001) and T3 (7.26 ± 2.34 s; 95% CI: 5.00–9.00 s; p<0.0001), with values increasing almost twofold over time. Mean change from baseline at both time points were statistically significant (Table 1).

Schirmer’s test scores also showed improvement over time, with a mean value of 5.70 ± 2.82 mm at T0, which is in line with the severe DED cutoff (<5 mm). The greatest improvement was seen at 30 days of treatment (T2), with a mean value of 8.74 ± 3.94 mm, followed by a moderately higher increase at T3 to 9.62 ± 4.57 mm. Changes from baseline were found to be statistically significant at both time points (3.15± 4.56 mm; 95% CI: 1.00–4.00 mm; p = 0.0008 at T2; 3.92 ± 4.80 mm; 95% CI: 1.00–7.00 mm; p<0.0001 at T3) (Figure 1C and Table 1).

Analysis of the additional performance parameters revealed a mixed pattern. While there was a slight increase in niBUT, this increase did not reach statistical significance. Tear meniscus height demonstrated stability at T2, subsequently exhibiting a significant increase at T3 (p<0.05). Conjunctival hyperaemia was also evaluated. CCRLU scores demonstrated a marked enhancement over the course of the investigation, exhibiting a substantial decline at T3 (p = 0.0022), yet not at T2. As shown in Table S4, gland loss worsened slightly over time, as indicated by meibography, although always remaining within a low-grade range.

There was a consistent improvement in patient-reported symptoms. The IDEEL Symptom-Bother score demonstrated a significant decrease from 58.82 ± 10.57 to 45.08 ± 9.96 at T2 (p<0.001) and to 40.99 ± 11.09 at T3 (p<0.001), demonstrating an overall change of 17 points (Figure 1D and Table 1).

Safety Outcomes

One patient (2%) experienced a non-serious TEAE (blurred vision) considered related to the device; no treatment discontinuation or dose reduction was considered necessary.

Discussion

This prospective, multicenter clinical investigation demonstrated the clinical efficacy and safety of 0.3% HA-based ophthalmic solution in patients with moderate to severe DED. Notably, 90% of participants had at least one concomitant condition, including eye disorders (25.5%) and musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders (25.5%), and 70% were receiving systemic medications. Despite this, treatment with preservative-free eye drops containing 0.3% HA resulted in significant improvements in both corneal and conjunctival staining, as well as in tear-film break-up time. These clinical benefits were observed both after 30 days of treatment and maintained through 60 days, with a favorable safety profile.

The population was predominantly female and of relatively advanced age (mean age 67.7 ± 13.85), consistent with the well-established association between aging and increased risk of DED.18 A substantial proportion of patients presented with at least one systemic comorbidity, most commonly hypertension (45.1%), hypercholesterolemia (23.5%), and Sjögren’s syndrome (17.6%), and most of the population was undergoing concomitant medication. The presence of systemic conditions is known to exacerbate DED severity by compromising the ocular surface and negatively impacting quality of life. These findings align with previous evidence from a large retrospective case-control study conducted in the United States, which reported significantly higher rates of comorbidities - including hypertension, cataracts, thyroid disease, type 2 diabetes, and glaucoma - among patients with DED compared to matched controls.17

The high burden of multimorbidity and concurrent medications observed in this cohort underscores the need for a multidisciplinary and tailored approach. In this context, HA-based eye drops could represent a useful option, potentially offering symptom relief without interfering with systemic therapies. They may also help delay more invasive interventions and limit disease escalation. Also, a gradual therapeutic plan might be preferable, especially in patients with multiple comorbidities.32

In this study, the NEI score improved significantly at both time points, with the greatest change observed at day 30. The mean difference was −4.09 points between baseline and day 30, and −4.78 points between baseline and day 60. TFBUT also showed a significant and progressive improvement, increasing from 3.65 seconds at baseline to 6.51 seconds at day 30 and 7.26 seconds at day 60. Among the secondary endpoints, changes in Schirmer’s test I at day-30 and 60 were evaluated. Tear production showed a progressive increase over time, with a statistically significant mean change from baseline to day-60 of 3.92 ± 4.8 mm, indicating an improvement in lacrimal gland function and overall tear secretion.

The remaining performance parameters yielded mixed results. While mean non-invasive break-up time showed slight improvements at both time points, these changes did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, conjunctival hyperaemia, measured via the CCRLU score, significantly decreased at the end of the treatment period. This delayed response may be interpreted as consistent with a cohort of patients diagnosed with moderate-to-severe DED. In fact, considering the severity of the condition and the prevalence of systemic diseases and concomitant medication within the population, a treatment duration exceeding 30 days may be necessary to achieve a statistically significant improvement in this parameter. Conjunctival hyperaemia is a recognized hallmark of ocular surface inflammation, often triggered by tear hyperosmolarity and commonly associated with the progression to chronic DED.33 The observed reduction may be attributed to the moisturizing properties of HA,34–36 which contribute to ocular surface stabilization and symptom relief.

HA is considered an essential component of tear substitutes, as its elevated viscosity improves retention time and optimizes ocular surface hydration and lubrication.34 The treatment device exhibits pseudoplastic behavior, with an intrinsic viscosity of 17.07 ± 0.2 dL/g and a zero-shear viscosity of 24.4 mPa s. The latter is responsible for its prolonged retention on the ocular surface and its interaction with mucins, thereby protecting the corneal epithelium against dehydration.37 In addition, non-preserved tear substitutes are typically advised for regular use, particularly in cases of moderate-to-severe DED, to avoid the potential adverse effects of preservatives on the ocular surface.3,14,26

Meibography findings remained almost stable, with no substantial change in the percentage of gland loss. This result indicates the preservation of meibomian gland structure and suggests that the treatment did not exacerbate the progression of atrophy. Tear meniscus height showed no relevant change at day-30 but exhibited a slight increase by day-60, reflecting a gradual improvement in tear volume over time, consistent with the expected response in patients with moderate-to-severe disease.

Only one patient in this cohort experienced blurred vision after treatment, and no other adverse events were reported, which confirms the product’s optimal safety. This is consistent with the marked improvement in the IDEEL Symptom-Bother score, which increased by almost 20 points from baseline to day 60, well above the clinically meaningful threshold of 12 points.38 DED is known to have a substantial impact on patients’ quality of life, and improvement in symptom perception is of primary importance, particularly given the high prevalence of concomitant conditions in this population. IDEEL questionnaire, developed by Abetz et al,31,39 is a validated tool designed to assess changes in DED-related burden. Although dry eye symptoms are heterogeneous, they represent the primary reason for seeking care and are a key indicator of disease severity. Therefore, evaluating the impact of DED on daily life is considered essential,40,41 as poor quality of life is closely linked to limitations in daily activities, emotional wellbeing, and work productivity.42

These preliminary results should be considered in light of the limitations of the investigation. The absence of a control group may have limited the causality of the results, while the absence of subgroup analysis may have limited the interpretability and generalisability of the results. No masking was applied, which may have introduced assessment bias. Nevertheless, the relatively large sample size and the evaluation at multiple time points over a period of up to 60 days, strengthen the robustness of the results and provide valuable insights into the mid-term effects of the treatment over time. However, further studies with longer follow-up periods are warranted to better assess the durability of the effects and long-term safety. This study confirmed the favourable efficacy and safety profile of preservative-free HA-based eye drops, even in a more vulnerable population under polytherapy and comorbid systemic conditions.

Conclusion

The findings of this study support the use of 0.3% HA-based tear substitutes in patients with moderate-to-severe DED, including those with multimorbidity and concomitant systemic medications. Significant improvements were observed in ocular surface integrity (NEI score), tear-film stability (TFBUT), and tear production (Schirmer’s Test I) at both 30 and 60 days. Importantly, the treatment led to a marked and statistically significant reduction in patient-reported symptom burden (IDEEL questionnaire), with no major adverse effects reported. These results suggest that 0.3% ophthalmic solution with HA may represent a valuable option for managing symptoms in patients with moderate-to-severe DED, even in the presence of concomitant systemic therapies. Longer follow-up may be important to evaluate the sustainability of the results over time and the long-term safety profile.
