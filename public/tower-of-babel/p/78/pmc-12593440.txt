Introduction

Fibrotic interstitial lung disease

Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) represent a heterogeneous group of diseases characterised by inflammation or fibrosis of the pulmonary alveolar units due to various causes.1 Inflammation and fibrosis are common pathologic features of ILDs, but their extent and distribution vary among different ILD types and disease stages. For example, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) exemplifies a typical progressive fibrotic interstitial lung disease (FILD), displaying signs of fibrosis from its early stages.2 Conversely, some ILDs, such as hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) and connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease (CTD-ILD), are characterised by inflammation in the early stages of the disease, and as the disease progresses, fibrosis develops and worsens, leading to FILD.3 Irreversible fibrosis leads to destruction of lung structure and loss of function, potentially resulting in respiratory failure and death. FILD imposes a significant health and socioeconomic burden, emerging as a major public health concern. Pulmonary fibrosis is an essential factor affecting treatment response and prognosis of ILDs.4,6 Therefore, early identification of fibrosis and effective intervention are important for delaying the progression of FILD and improving the prognosis.

Obstacles

Europe and the USA began initiating IPF registry studies about 10 years ago, and some results have been published.7,10 Our team has been leading multiple centres nationwide to conduct IPF registry studies in China since 2017.11 As pulmonologists delve deeper into ILDs, the focus is gradually shifting from IPF to FILDs such as progressive fibrosing interstitial lung disease (PF-ILD) or progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF), a newly defined subgroup of ILDs in recent years that exhibit a progressive pattern of pulmonary fibrosis, even after adequate medical intervention.2 12 However, there are still many medical issues in the field of FILD that have not been elucidated, such as which types of ILDs are susceptible to conversion to FILD, the timing of conversion of non-fibrotic ILDs to FILD and how to recognise FILD early. Therefore, it is urgent and vital to establish a large multicentre FILD cohort and rely on the cohort to initiate histopathological, imaging and biomarker-related studies on FILD.

Methods and analysis

Overview

The FIRST study is a prospective, multicentre cohort study of FILD (NCT06655090). The trial was registered on 28 June 2022, and patient enrolment was completed on 30 November 2024, with a follow-up period of 3 years. 40 centres across 23 provinces in mainland China obtained ethics approval and were responsible for patient enrolment and follow-up (online supplemental table S1). During the enrolment period, a total of 14 582 patients with ILD were included. The study included both prevalent and incident patients, with follow-up time calculated from the date of enrolment. The participating sites were selected according to the following criteria: (1) a medical college teaching hospital or an upper first-class hospital certified by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China with relatively high ILD diagnosis rates and the infrastructure to support ILD diagnosis and treatment; (2) have a multidisciplinary team, including respiratory disease clinical specialists, and imaging and pathology specialists to support comprehensive diagnosis and care for patients with ILD; (3) have the basic facility to properly store and process biospecimens, including blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF).

Sample size

The sample size was determined according to the primary research objective of this study, which is the development of prediction models. The primary endpoint of the prediction models was the observation of fibrosis progression within 12 months in FILD. We calculated the sample size using the method proposed by Riley et al.13 The assumptions involved in this method were as follows: (1) the number of candidate predictors; (2) the proportion of fibrosis progression in patients with FILD; (3) criterion value for root mean square percentage error, which is generally recommended not to exceed 0.05. Based on previous studies and clinical professional judgement, we expected the prediction model to include 12 predictors. According to an earlier study, 50% of fibrotic ILD met PF-ILD criteria in the first 24 months of follow-up.14 Based on these assumptions, the calculated sample size of FILD was 1170 for each ILD subclass. We will do subgroup analysis for several major ILD subclasses in this study, such as IPF, non-IPF idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (IIPs), CTD-ILD, HP, sarcoidosis and pneumoconiosis. Consider that there will be a 10% loss during the follow-up. The final sample size required in this study will be greater than 10 000 patients with FILD.

Patients’ evaluation and enrolment

This study enrolled patients who were already diagnosed with FILD at baseline, as well as those who transitioned from a non-FILD status to an FILD diagnosis over the course of the follow-up period. By systematically collecting clinical information, laboratory test results, lung function data, imaging records and biological samples, a specialised cohort focusing on FILD was established, encompassing over 10 000 patients. Newly enrolled patients must meet the following criteria:

Inclusion criteria: satisfies one of the following conditions: (1) radiological manifestations based on high-resolution CT (HRCT) included reticular opacities, honeycombing, interlobular septal thickening and traction bronchiectasis/bronchiolectasis. All images were interpreted by experienced chest radiologists; (2) pathological evaluation of lung tissue by experienced pathologists revealed interstitial fibrosis and honeycombing; (3) through multidisciplinary discussion, diagnosed with IPF, fibrotic NSIP, fibrosing CTD-ILD, stage IV sarcoidosis, fibrosing pneumoconiosis and other types of ILDs with pulmonary fibrosis.

Secondary outcomes include descriptions of the natural history, mortality and related causes of death, as well as the causes and incidence of acute exacerbations in Chinese FILD patients.

Other outcomes include the following: (1) risk factors and related prediction models for the occurrence and poor prognosis of FILD; (2) FILD-related comorbidities and complications (such as pulmonary hypertension, lung cancer, coronary heart disease, diabetes, gastro-oesophageal reflux, anxiety and depression); (3) serum inflammatory indicators and changes in related biomarkers; (4) quality of life assessed by the St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ); (5) treatment conditions for FILD in China, including prevention and treatment plans (such as influenza, SARS-CoV-2, pneumococcal vaccination, drug treatment, traditional Chinese medicine, oxygen therapy, mechanical ventilation and rehabilitation therapy); (6) costs related to FILD treatment (such as hospitalisation, outpatient and emergency fees); and (7) the establishment of a FILD biobank, including blood, BALF and lung biopsy samples, for further research on specific biomarkers for FILD diagnosis, thus monitoring the progression of FILD and determining individual prognosis.

Data collection

Data collection will occur through an Electronic Data Capture (EDC) system that incorporates logic checks to verify the information. Each participating centre is expected to appoint one or two experienced clinical research coordinators responsible for accurately and completely entering patient information into the EDC system. These coordinators will undergo consistent training and evaluations to ensure uniform data entry standards are met. Continuous monitoring of the data entry process will be in place, with the contract research organisation providing regular, timely feedback to maintain data quality. Additionally, all research documents will be provided in Chinese, and the original files will be stored at each participating research centre.

Follow-up

The follow-up of patients was conducted according to rigorous follow-up plan developed during the study design period. After the enrolment of patients, the follow-up schedule has been structured to take place every 3 months for the first two sessions following the initial diagnosis. After these sessions, the follow-ups will be conducted semi-annually until the study concludes, as detailed in table 1. Table 1 also lists all the baseline and subsequent follow-up measurements. Patient-reported outcomes will be evaluated using the SGRQ for quality of life and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale for assessing levels of anxiety and depression. Support staff are available to aid patients in completing these evaluations if necessary. All data and findings from patients will be securely stored in the EDC database in their unmodified form, serving as reference material.

Statistical analysis

Before the study began, we developed a statistical analysis plan in which the specified groups for analysis and the statistical methods were described. For descriptive analysis, continuous variables will be expressed as mean±SD or median (interquartile) according to their distribution and compared by Student’s t-test, analysis of variance (ANOVA) analysis or non-parametric test, wherever appropriate. Categorical variables will be expressed as number (percentages) and compared by χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. Pulmonary function parameters will be analysed as continuous variables in terms of their absolute changes. Categorical outcomes derived from changes in pulmonary function parameters (particularly FVC and DLCO), such as a relative decline of 10% in FVC over 12–24 months, will also be analysed. Crude incidence rates will be determined as incident cases per 1000 person-years, together with 95% CIs. The annual incidence of fibrosis will be calculated in the entire population, while progression of fibrosis and acute exacerbation of fibrosis will be calculated within a population of patients with fibrosis. We will conduct subgroup analyses for specific ILD subtypes. The other outcomes include disease progression, acute exacerbation, death, lung transplantation, hospitalisation and quality of life. The rate of these outcomes among subgroups will be compared. Age, gender, baseline ILD severity and other variables identified on screening analysis will be considered potential confounding factors.

One of the major purposes of this study is to construct prediction models. We will do data cleaning before data analysis. Parameters with more than 35% missing will be excluded from the analysis while missing values below this proportion will be multi-imputed. The overall study population will be separated into training and validation datasets. Take disease progression as an example. Univariate analysis will assess differences in various parameters between patients with and without progression to determine which variable to include in developing the prediction model. We will use several machine learning methods to build prediction models and validate them in the validation dataset, including logistic regression, K-nearest neighbours, random forest, gradient boosting classifier, eXtreme Gradient Boosting, adaptive boosting, Gaussian naïve Bayes and support vector machine. In this study, the SHapley Additive exPlanations score was used to determine the importance of predictors. We will measure the predictive effect of the models in terms of differentiation, calibration and decision curve analysis. The optimal cut-off for prediction models will be estimated by the Youden index. Then, the models’ performances will be assessed by sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value. Finally, the prediction model with the best predictive results will be visualised by computer technology. SAS software, V.9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) and R software (V.4.0.2) were conducted to do all statistical analyses. P less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Biospecimens

This study collected multiple specimen types, including blood, BALF, lung tissue and primary lung fibroblasts. For blood, at least two tubes (10 mL each; one plain and one ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tube) were obtained from each patient, separated within 2–6 hours and uniformly labelled. BALF and lung tissue were first used for clinical diagnosis, and residual samples, if available, were collected for research. Fresh lung tissue was rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline and used directly for storage or for isolating primary fibroblasts, which were cultured and purified after two passages.

Sample processing included centrifugation of plain and EDTA tubes to obtain serum and plasma (≥3 mL each), preparation of leucocyte pellets for DNA extraction and Ficoll density gradient separation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), ensuring viability ≥90% before single-cell RNA sequencing or assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq). BALF was filtered and centrifuged, and lung tissue was cut into small aliquots.

Specimens were stored under standardised conditions: serum, plasma, leucocyte pellets and BALF at −80°C (minimum −20°C); DNA at −20°C short-term or −80°C long-term; PBMCs in 90% fetal bovine serum + 10% dimethyl sulfoxide before transfer to liquid nitrogen; and lung tissue and fibroblasts uniformly in liquid nitrogen. All samples were transported to the central laboratory: centres with −80°C freezers shipped quarterly, those with only −20°C freezers shipped monthly and centres without separation capacity sent specimens within 2 hours. Serum, plasma, leukocytes and BALF were transported by cold chain (−80°C, minimum −20°C); DNA and RNA on dry ice; and PBMCs, lung tissue and fibroblasts using liquid nitrogen dry shippers to ensure integrity.

Ethics and dissemination

This research is an observational, non-interventional study committed to safeguarding patient rights and interests, adhering to the established research protocols, medical guidelines and diagnostic standards within China. The patient’s primary physician must ensure the delivery of standard, appropriate clinical care covering diagnosis, prevention and treatment strategies.

The study’s oversight, design and management responsibilities lie with the sponsor, China-Japan Friendship Hospital, and the principal investigator, Dr. Huaping Dai, through the guidance of the steering committee. All research activities will align with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and adhere to both national and local ethical standards, as well as regulations safeguarding patient data privacy. Written informed consent has to be obtained from either the patients or their family members. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the ethics committees of all participating sites. The full names of the respective ethics committees are listed in online supplemental table S1.

Analysis of patient data will proceed in an anonymised fashion. Access to the study’s data and outcomes will be granted to the lead investigator, authorised physicians within the same centre, sponsor delegates, ethics committees and regulatory bodies. Patient health information will be treated as confidential and not disclosed to external parties without explicit permission from the sponsor, China-Japan Friendship Hospital, and the principal investigator, Dr. Huaping Dai, except as required by national regulatory authorities. Participating centres are obliged to retain clinical research data pertinent to this study for a minimum of 5 years following the study’s conclusion and the completion of data collection. Upon conclusion, the findings of the study will be shared with the broader scientific community through publication in peer-reviewed journals.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public did not participate in any phase of the study’s design, nor were they involved in the recruitment process or the actual conduct of the study.

Study limitations

Our study programme had several potential limitations that are worth noting. First, although we enrolled over 10 000 patients, it is still uncertain if we managed to represent the full diversity of the Chinese FILD population in terms of geographic locations, ages and genders. Second, like many long-term studies, we faced challenges keeping track of participants and ensuring the completeness of follow-up data. There are various reasons participants might leave the study, such as losing contact, which could introduce bias in our data. Third, our study collected vast clinical information, including lab results, lung function tests, imaging data and biological samples. However, maintaining consistency and quality across this broad dataset is challenging in such a large study. Differences in data collection standards and methods across various centres require a strict quality control process to guarantee that data is comparable and accurate. Fourth, the sample size calculation and follow-up schedule of this study were primarily based on the currently accepted definition of fibrotic progression. However, for some more slowly progressive ILDs, such as sarcoidosis and early-stage pneumoconiosis, the sample size and follow-up duration in this study may still be insufficient, and the data generated may not provide conclusions with adequate power. This also limits the practical significance of the study for these specific diseases. Finally, as our study is observational and not intervention based, it limits our ability to determine how specific treatments affect FILD progression directly. While we can identify factors that seem to influence the disease’s progression, proving a cause-and-effect relationship between these factors and the progression is more complex.

Implications

The study aims to enrol patients with FILD and conduct systematic follow-ups to monitor the progression of fibrosis across various FILD subtypes, including fibrotic NSIP, chronic HP/fibrotic HP, CTD-ILD, pneumoconiosis and other related conditions. Through regular patient follow-ups, the study intends to gather comprehensive data, including demographic information, laboratory test results, imaging findings, pathology reports and potential biomarker assessments. The goal is to delineate distinct phenotypes of FILD, particularly those linked with the onset, progression or poor outcomes of fibrosis. Moreover, the study aims to integrate new analytical techniques, such as machine learning, with diverse clinical phenotypes to enhance the identification of fibrosis or predict its progression.

During the cohort follow-up, biological samples, including peripheral blood and BALF from patients, are collected for the identification of potential biomarkers using multi-omics technologies. These biomarkers are then validated against prognostic data to assess their application value in clinical settings. Pulmonary function tests and imaging studies are employed to evaluate the extent of fibrosis in patients, who are subsequently categorised into either a stable or progressive group based on their disease trajectory.

The study uses fibrosis progression as an outcome measure to identify predictors associated with clinical events such as disease advancement or mortality. These predictors are screened from clinical data, imaging characteristics and biomarkers to develop an early warning model for fibrosis progression. The model’s predictive performance is assessed in terms of its discrimination and calibration abilities. It is further refined through both internal and external cross-validation processes. After optimisation, intervention strategies are proposed, and relevant guidelines or expert consensus is established. The ultimate goal is to promote the model nationally, offering a tool for early identification and management of patients at risk of rapid fibrosis progression.
