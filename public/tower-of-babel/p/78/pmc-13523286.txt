Comparative mass spectrometry analysis of high and low centrifugation extracellular vesicle (EV) pellets from healthy urine following Tamm-Horsfall protein removal
Authors: Dana Bielopolski, Luca Musante, Henrik Molina, Douglas Barrows, Samantha Upson, Lifang Yang, Thomas Carrol, Jonathan N. Tobin, Rhonda G. Kost, U. Erdbrügger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesUrinary extracellular vesicles (uEVs) are potential biomarkers for renal physiology and disease. Their isolation is challenged by Tamm-Horsfall protein (THP) contamination, particularly in high-speed ultracentrifugation (P100) fractions. Low-speed (P20) fractions contain biologically relevant uEVs, yet their proteomic composition is underexplored. This study evaluates strategies for THP removal to optimize mass spectrometry analysis and characterizes P20 and P100 uEV proteomes and its combination in healthy urine. MethodsUrine from a healthy volunteer was processed via sequential centrifugation at 21,200 g (P20) and 164,244 g (P100). THP removal was performed using low ionic strength buffer (P20) and size-exclusion chromatography (P100). Western blotting, nanoparticle tracking, and mass spectrometry were used to characterize protein content. Functional analysis was performed to assess biological significance.ResultsMass spectrometry identified 1,103 unique proteins in P100, 929 in P20, and 1,676 in the combined pellet, with 90% similarity between fractions. P20 was enriched in proteasome components (PSMC2, PSMD2, PSMD5, PSMD6, PSMD8, PSMD11, PSMD13, PSME1), while P100 had no advantage in terms of expression profile in any of the proteins examined. Functional analysis indicated immune activation in P100 and protein degradation in P20, suggesting complementary biological roles.ConclusionThis study reveals proteasome enrichment in P20 uEVs and supports a combined P20 + P100 approach to improve biomarker discovery. Optimized THP removal strategies enhance mass spectrometry resolution, facilitating uEV analysis for clinical and research applications.Institutional Review Board for Health Sciences Research (IRB-HSR) at UVAHSR220053.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12014-026-09615-2.

Introduction

Urinary extracellular vesicles (uEVs) denote minuscule membranous entities generated by cells lining the urinary tract and adjoining tissues [1]. The examination of uEVs as an exploratory instrument has garnered substantial focus owing to their prospective utility as non-invasive biomarkers for diagnosing and monitoring kidney disorders, like hypertension, diabetes, immune-related ailments, and malignancies [1, 2] The protein payload of uEVs reflects both the protein composition of tissues [2, 3] as well as physiologic changes within the kidney [4].

As a result of technological advancements in mass spectrometry the identification of proteins has become more meticulous, expeditious, and capable of detecting subtle variations in protein quantities [5, 3]. Thus far urinary EV proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses have identified more than 4000 unique proteins [4, 6].

High speed ultracentrifugation is the most widely used method for the isolation of EVs. Differential ultracentrifugation is commonly used to capture the whole population of vesicles at the size range 40–200 nm and larger [5, 7]. Recently, investigations into the low-speed pellet resulting from centrifugation have shown its potential as a valuable source of EVs as well [8, 9, 10]which was previously mostly discarded.

Ultracentrifugation can remove the vast majority of contaminant protein, Tamm-Horsfall protein (THP; also known as uromodulin), which tends to co sediment with EV’s [6–8]. THP polymerization gives rise to multiple isoforms, including high molecular weight networks [9, 10] that precipitate at low speeds, forming urinary casts, along with short oligomers that co-segregate with EVs, acting as impurities [11, 12]. This situation can complicate downstream analysis by either masking low-abundance proteins during mass spectrometry [13] or interfering with glycan analysis due to THP’s extensive glycosylation [12, 14].

To overcome residual contamination, THP depolymerization by dithiothreitol (DTT) treatment [15] or uEV isolation by sucrose gradient centrifugation [15, 16]have been routinely used, but both approaches have disadvantages [17]. The use of DTT doesn’t entirely eliminate THP from uEVs [18], potentially affecting the folding and biological functionality of specific proteins in uEV preparations [19]. Incorporating chemical agents like iodixanol to counter viscous contaminants complicates proteomic analysis due to mass spectrometry sensitivity. On the other hand, separation via sucrose gradients or cushions [20, 21, 16]is time-intensive, rendering uEV preparations derived from these methods less suitable for functional investigations(22) [11].

The utilization of filters to remove THP has been extensively explored for uEV isolation. Combining filtration with other techniques like differential ultracentrifugation as an enrichment step to enhance EV yield can be used, but maybe impractical for large-scale studies; [22, 23, 24]however, it might be an alternative when the proteomic analysis is focused on the uEV high centrifugation pellet and an ultracentrifuge is available [25]. Density gradient could enhance EV fraction purity by eliminating contaminants such as THP, but this method leads to EV loss and isn’t feasible in clinical settings [12, 26].

The absence of standardized protocols for EV isolation techniques has prompted the development of innovative methods to optimize EV extraction from diverse biological fluids [13, 8] Currently, the optimal method for EV isolation is chosen based on factors like the type and quantity of bodily fluid being analyzed, availability of specialized equipment, intended therapeutic application, administration route, and desired vesicle fraction and type.

As a result, the literature abounds with numerous protocols for EV isolation and strategies to counter THP interference [27]. Previously, we demonstrated consistent retrieval of uEVs through centrifugation at 21,200 g [16] following unfolding of THP filaments by reducing agent (TCEP). In the current study we explore simplified methods for EV preparation for large scale analysis, in particular for the low centrifugation pellet (21,200 g) and describe a strategy to analyze a combined pellet of the low and high centrifugation pellet (164,244 g) in pursuit of identifying biomarkers for kidney and urogenital tract diseases.

We report a comparative mass spectrometry analysis of high centrifugation vs. low centrifugation uEV pellets following THP removal and compare the results to the combination of both high and low centrifugation uEV pellets.

Methods

uEV fractionation

One healthy volunteer collected the first morning void urine (50 ml). This sample was aliquoted and three of these portions were processed within 3 h without adding any protease inhibitors (Workflow summatory is shown in supplementary Fig. 1S).

Each urine aliquot (15 ml) was centrifuged at 4600 g for 30 min to remove cell debris. The pellet was discarded, and the supernatant was then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm the resulting pellet, termed P20. The supernatant of P20 was further centrifuged in Beckman Coulter XL-80 ultracentrifuge using the 70Ti fix angle rotor (Beckman) for 2 h at 164,244 g at 4 °C, using polycarbonate tubes (Beckman catalogue number 35563). The resulting pellet is termed P100.

Tamm-Horsfall protein (THP) removal

P20 pellets were depleted using low ionic strength buffer (10 mM HEPES pH 7.4) supplemented with EDTA (2.5 mM pH 8.0) [6, 7, 16, 17] and centrifuged at 21,200 g; ) for 30 min at 4 °C in an Eppendorf microcentrifuge 5424 fix angle rotor (FA-45-24-11) (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). This step was repeated twice for a total of 3 solubilization-centrifugation cycles. Pellets were stored at -80 °C degrees and solubilized after thawing in 0.1 μm filtered (Minisart® PES syringe filter code 16553-K, Sartorious, Göttingen, Germany) phosphate buffered saline solution (PBS) pH 7.4 no Ca2 + and Mg2+ (Gibco®, life technology™, Thermo Fisher Scientific) (PBS0.1µM) or electrophoresis solubilisation buffer (ESB).

P100 pellet was cleaned by size exclusion chromatography (SEC). The P100 pellet was resolubilized in guanidine 6 M in acidic condition (PH = 3.5) and mixed for 20 min in end-over-end wheel before being applied on top of SEC 70 nm original column(29). Columns were washed with guanidine buffer before and after samples loading. The collection of the fraction was carried out with the aid of automatic fraction collector. One mL fraction was collected and precipitated by Trichoroacetic Acid and Sodium Deoxycholate (TCA-DOC) Presence of uEV’s was validated by Western blotting for TSG101.

De-lipidation

We used chloroform and methanol to precipitate P20 and P100 pellets for proteomic analysis. In addition to preparing the P20 and P100 pellet separately for Mass Spectrometry we also combined the P20 and P100 pellets after uromodulin removal and extracted uEVs using Chloroform methanol and acetone for the combined pellet.

Proteomic analysis: Mass spectrometry of samples enriched for EVs was performed at The Rockefeller University Proteomics Resource Center, as described(30). Briefly, proteins were precipitated and re-dissolved in 50uL 8 M Urea/50mM ammonium bicarbonate/20mM Dithiothreitol followed by alkylation (iodoacetamide). Proteins were first digested with Endoproteinase Lys-C at less than 4 M Urea and then diluted to less than 2 M Urea for trypsinization. Digestions were halted by adding neat trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and peptides were subjected to Solid Phase Extraction (C18-like Empore). Peptides were separated using a 12 cm packed-in-emitter column (inner diameter of 75 μm) and data were acquired on a Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data was searched against a UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/ )Human database, using Proteome/Discoverer+Mascot as well as MaxQuant (version 2.0.3.0) using a False Discovery Rate of 1% for proteins and 2% for peptides. Only proteins quantified in at least two of three replicates in at least one group were retained, GENE-E software was used for heatmap generation and data display(31) [18].

Statistical analyses were performed using R. Log transformed IBAQ values were quantile normalized using the Bioconductor Limma package(32) [28] (version 3.50.1) following imputation of missing values using the fitdistr function within the MASS R package(33) [22] (version 7.3–54). The topTable functions implemented within Limma were used to identify all proteins detected by mass spectrometry that changed across the time course (p < 0.05) as well as between individual timepoints. Adjustment for patient effects for visualization was performed using the removeBatchEffect function in Limma. The abundance of differential proteins was visualized using the heatmap R package (1.0.12) with a heatmap showing the z-scores of each protein across all samples (row scaled). Z-score is calculated as (value – row mean)/(row standard deviation).

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using GOBP for biological processes and GOCC for cellular compartment localization. Input was the combination of uEV enriched proteins (iBAQ count in uEV of each fraction and replicate), and the background was all identified proteins in the pool (defined by at least one iBAQ value). We uploaded protein lists with enrichment fold > 1, passing the false discovery rate of 5% threshold. GO functional analysis was performed based on log2 fold-change in uEV > 1 or less than − 1. (all annotations updated on February 11, 2021). Evidence level was set at “All_without_IEA”; “use GO Term fusion” function was enabled; and only pathways with P < 0.05 were shown. Full gene lists are available.

Results

Separation and characterization of uEVs from urine

Differential centrifugation to enrich uEVs followed 3 centrifugation steps: The first pellet containing mainly THP (4,600 g) was discarded (Supplementary Fig. S2). The second pellet, P20, and the ultracentrifugation pellet, P100, enriched in uEVs and THP (Supplementary Fig. S2) along with associated markers (Supplementary Fig. S3) were used in a further process to eliminate the majority of THP. According to Coomassie blue stain protein content between raw urine and following the first spin were similar, this amount reduced following in P20, but increased in the EV fraction, P100, and compared to its supernatant. According to traditional markers of EVs, despite the differences in total protein the P20 fraction is enriched for EVs, and the protein patterns were similar to P100 when examining TSG101, ALIX, CD9, CD81, CD63, CR1, PODXL, MME, and Synthetin-1.

Removal of THP from P20: THP was released into the supernatant following washing with a low ionic strength buffer supplemented with a divalent cation chelator (EDTA). Three washes were sufficient to remove the prevalence of THP from P20 pellet regardless of the starting amount of THP collected in the crude P20 pellet (Supplementary Fig. S4 A, B and C), but not from P100 pellet (Supplementary Fig. S4D). EV-associated markers like TSG101 and aquaporin-2 showed a good recovery with minimal loss in the first two supernatants. (Supplementary Fig. S5 A-C).

Particle size distribution did not change from the first to the last washings (Supplementary Fig. S5 D) of the 20,00 g centrifugation. However, during the washing steps the particle concentration in the pellets slightly decreased (from 1.10 × 1011 to 9.45 × 1010 particles/mL) which is in line with the EV markers detection in WB. The particle count in the supernatant was reduced by 1.5 orders of magnitude compared to the final pellet (P20-4) and SN_1 which is a mix of THP monomers/oligomers and uEVs (TSG101 and AQP2positive) (Supplementary Fig. S5 E and F).

Removal of THP from P100: A washing with low ionic strength buffer supplemented with a divalent cation chelator did not remove THP from P100 (Supplementary Fig. S4D). Thus, size exclusion chromatography (SEC) was implemented without any chaotropic reagent, using TCEP as reducing agent with 6 M guanidine at acidic pH (Supplementary Fig. S6), for 20 min which effectively removed THP filaments. We performed a SEC separation of P20 to compare the elution time of the EVs for the two pellets. Interestingly, P100 guanidine treated and P20 are eluted in the same first 2 fractions after the column void volume. SEC protocol was reproducible (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Finally, EV presence in the SEC fractions was confirmed by western blot analysis for known markers (CD9, TSG101 and syntenin-1 Supplementary Fig. S8 B, C and D respectively) in the first 2 fractions while validating the removal of THP (Supplementary Fig. S8 A). Of note, these markers were also detected in the following fractions. Further validation of the whole protocol was conducted by using urine at different concentrations according to creatinine concentration. Three healthy donors collected their urine at different times of the day that reflected different concentrations. For each time point, we tested the performance of the enrichment method using 10 mL of urine. Based on the result of the Coomassie staining of the final fraction, 10–15 mL of urine is the minimum volume needed to begin enrichment (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Morphological analysis of EVs was conducted using cryo-electron microscopy and particle counting with nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (Zetaview by Particle Metrix). NTA showed a median EV size of 166.1 nm (84.0–323.3 nm min/max) (Supplementary Fig. 5). The absence of THP polymers is further confirmed in our Coomassie blue images indicates its successful removal by low ionic strength buffer (Fig. 1A) from P20 and fractions 1 + 2 by SEC from P100 (Fig. 1C). The presence EV after removal of THP was verified according to TSG101 by western blot analysis (Fig. 1B, D).

Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of uEV’s fractionation

MS analysis was utilized for detailed characterization of the pellets and to explore optimal coverage of the uEV proteome. Each fraction was analyzed in triplicate, and an additional fraction was created pooling together P20 and P100 before reduction and alkylation. P20 and P100 pellets obtained from low ionic strength buffer and SEC respectively, were loaded to SDS-PAGE after chloroform methanol lipid extraction as quality control before MS (Supplementary Fig 10, panel A,B).

Considering protein identifications of at least 2/3 unique peptides there were 929 unique proteins in all three repetitions of P20 compared to 1865 unique proteins in two of the replicates. There were 1103 unique proteins in all three P100 replicates. In the combined P20 and P100 there were 1676 unique proteins in all three repetitions (Fig. 2). The second column from the right, (the middle P20 sample) is an outlier. Including the P20 outlier reduced the similarity between samples to 34%. whereas excluding it increased identity between the remaining two samples to 90.8%. This specimen was attributed to some technical issues experienced during the sample treatment for MS since gel electrophoresis (Fig. S10) performed utilizing 10% of the starting material showed a very similar amount and protein pattern complexity and size distribution after silver staining. Moreover, the sample had a different peptide signature according to protein identifications compared to the other two replica points (Fig. 3 Venn Diagram). Therefore, it was decided to remove it from further analysis and calculations.

Considering the proteins identified in all three replicates, 398 proteins were uniquely identified in P100 and 224 were uniquely identified in P20 (Fig. 3). Of note, in the combined P20 + P100 there were 473 uniquely expressed proteins that were not identified in either fraction alone.

We compared our data from the P-100 fraction with a recently published list of putative contaminants of urinary vesicles [23]. Even though purification method was different there is a very good representation with similar ranking of uEV hallmark proteins (Fig. S11). Next, we compared both our fractions to another data set that used a different purification method [24]. A venn diagram included in the supplementary section (Fig. S12) shows that 94% and 93% of our P20 and P100 respectively are identified in this previously published dataset. The Correll paper included more cases, had different MS sample preps and different MS instruments/ methods, and hence there are slight differences compared to our data.

We explored protein content of specific biomarkers of the different nephron parts in each fraction (supplementary Fig. 4). The P100 fraction contained similar amounts of Aquaporin 2, CD9, DPP4, Podocalyxin and SLC12A3, despite loading higher total protein amounts as can be seen in the Coomassie blue stain (supplementary Fig. 3).

Functional and cellular compartment differences between fraction

We analyzed functional networks using gene ontology biological processes (GOBP) (Fig. 4). Functional pathways increased in P20 compared to P100 included pathways related to cellular response to stress (cell cycle arrest, localization to telomeres and transcription regulation). Functional pathways increased in P100 compared to P20 included immune activation pathways (neutrophil processing, receptor signaling, phagocytosis) and extracellular vesicles processing. We analyzed cellular Component using Gene Ontology Cellular Component (GOCC) (Fig. 5). P20 was enriched compared to P100 for the proteosome, and ion channels. P100 was enriched compared to P20 for the lysosome, and distinct polarization characteristics of the extracellular membrane (basal vs. apical).

Discussion

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) play a crucial role in intercellular communication, and their proteomic characterization provides valuable insights into tissue-specific biomarkers. Our study comprehensively analyzed urinary EV (uEV) subfractions obtained via differential centrifugation, revealing a 90% proteomic overlap between the low-speed (P20) and high-speed (P100) pellets. Notably, we observed distinct enrichment patterns, with P20 exhibited an enrichment of proteasome components while analysis of P100 proteome had no advantage in terms of expression profile in any of the proteins examined. By implementing a low ionic strength buffer wash for P20 and size-exclusion chromatography for P100, we effectively reduced THP contamination, thereby improving proteomic detection with a simplified approach. Comparing our data to two recently published data sets we show a high yield of accuracy and reproducibility, positioning our method as one of the applicable and reliable options for uEV extraction.

Our analysis was conducted on healthy urine, allowing us to establish baseline proteomic signatures with a novel approach to remove THP and compare results across differential centrifugation steps. To our knowledge, the majority of studies focus on uEV proteomic analysis of the high centrifugation pellet. One other urine study focused on comparison across differential centrifugation steps comparing the fractions of soluble protein vs. the low centrifugation pellet (P20) and high centrifugation pellet (P150) (24)). Most studies used DTT for THP removal [21]. The more recent paper by Khoo et al. showed that adding an alkaline wash to the THP reduction with DTT improved EV isolation and depleted both THP and other contaminants [23]). This paper included also a sample of “soluble proteins” from cell free urine for proteomic analysis and compared those to uEV enriched samples by differential ultracentrifugation through low (p20) and high centrifugation (p150) from healthy urine and urine samples of prostate cancer patients [24]. The investigators showed that protein cargo is selectively packed into EVs in processes that are dysregulated during malignant transformation. Future studies should try and compare the various methods directly which is beyond the scope of this project.

Yet, the data is not comparable to our data as Khoo et al. utilized DTT for THP removal. One other study in glioblastoma patients identified distinct uEV proteomic alterations in different uEV fractions. Interestingly, in that study THP removal was not emphasized, highlighting the need for optimized extraction methods in disease cohorts [29]. Additionally, studies on other biofluids and cell culture systems have reported variability in the proteomic content of low- and high-speed centrifugation pellets, supporting the idea that fraction-specific profiling can reveal overlooked biological components [30, 31]. EVs isolated from a colon cancer cell line showed that small EVs promote cellular adhesion vs. large EVs that were enriched in proteins associated with ribosome and RNA biogenesis, processing and metabolism [32].

High-speed centrifugation (> 100,000 × g) remains the prevailing method for isolating urinary extracellular vesicles (uEVs) due to their low-density nature; however, this approach is associated with several limitations, including vesicle deformation, co-sedimentation with high-abundance urinary proteins such as THP, and the potential loss of structural integrity and specific EV subtypes [33]. To overcome these limitations, alternative isolation techniques have been proposed, such as precipitation-based methods and density gradient ultracentrifugation. Nevertheless, these approaches introduce their own challenges: precipitation typically requires high salt concentrations (> 40%), which promote nonspecific protein aggregation, while gel filtration and density gradient methods are influenced by variables such as column packing, flow rate, and pore size, and are generally more labor-intensive [25, 34]. Recent efforts have sought to improve EV purification using density gradient separation, but these methods remain labor-intensive [23, 25]. The importance of proteomic analyses in uEV research is growing, as mass spectrometry-based approaches have facilitated the identification of disease biomarkers across various body fluids [26]. The challenge of THP contamination remains, necessitating novel strategies for its removal.

Our study expands upon previous work by demonstrating that the low-speed P20 uEV fraction, often discarded in standard protocols, contains unique proteomic signatures, including a notable enrichment of proteasome components, particularly subunits of the 19 S regulatory particle. While previous studies have reported proteasome secretion in apoptotic vesicles from cultured vascular smooth muscle and epithelial cells [35], its enrichment in urinary EVs points to a broader physiological role in kidney cell communication. Functional analysis indicated immune activation in P100 and protein degradation in P20, suggesting complementary biological roles.

Our study presents a technically simplified and efficient method for uEV isolation that can also incorporate both the low- and high-speed pellets under optimized buffer conditions, minimizing THP interference and reducing processing complexity. This inclusive approach enhances proteomic sensitivity and enables detection of proteins that might otherwise be missed when analyzing either pellet alone, thus supporting the routine inclusion of the P20 fraction for comprehensive uEV profiling, in particular for biomarker discovery studies. This enrichment and THP-depletion strategy was applied previously [36], where it demonstrated reliable recovery of uEV-associated proteins and vesicle markers.

It is of note, that in the combined P20 + P100 there were 473 uniquely expressed proteins that were not identified in either fraction alone. While it is generally expected that simpler, individual fractions yield more identifiable proteins due to reduced sample complexity, in our case, the combined P20 + P100 fraction may have resulted in higher effective concentrations of certain proteins that were near the limit of detection in the individual fractions. This concentration effect could have enhanced ionization efficiency and peptide detectability during mass spectrometry, enabling the identification of additional proteins not observed in P20 or P100 alone. Furthermore, the combined sample may have facilitated detection of proteins present at low abundance in both fractions, which only became detectable when their cumulative signal surpassed analytical thresholds.

Despite its strengths, our study has some limitations. While our combined use of ionic strength modulation and size-exclusion chromatography effectively minimized contamination, subtle co-sedimentation effects may still persist, particularly when using short chromatography columns. Additionally, 6 M guanidine treatment may have introduced structural alterations in some EVs, leading to increased polydispersity and potential fragmentation. Future studies should validate our findings across a broader cohort to determine interindividual variability in uEV proteomics.

In conclusion, our study presents a novel and practical approach to uEV isolation by utilizing low-speed centrifugation with optimized buffer conditions—an accessible method for laboratories without ultracentrifugation capabilities. We demonstrate for the first time that the low-speed P20 fraction contains proteasome components, most notably subunits of the 19 S regulatory particle, providing new insights into renal EV biology. Moreover, the P20 fraction can be isolated using standard tabletop centrifuges, offering greater flexibility for a wider range of research settings. While users may opt to include the high-speed P100 pellet—enriched in classical tetraspanin-positive EVs—this step requires more labor-intensive procedures, including ultracentrifugation and SEC for effective removal of THP. Our combined fractionation strategy not only reduces labor and minimizes sample loss, but also improves proteomic yield and depth by enabling detection of underrepresented cellular components. Future studies should expand on this approach in disease-specific cohorts to validate its diagnostic and mechanistic utility in disorders of the genitourinary system and beyond, as the present work is limited to healthy individuals.
