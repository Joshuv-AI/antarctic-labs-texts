Hepatic and splenic tuberculosis following infliximab therapy for refractory ulcerative colitis: a case report and literature review
Authors: Shuo Chen, Yanfen Shi, Yang Pang, Xiaodi Wang, Fang Liu, Shiyu Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) inhibitors are first-line agents for steroid-refractory/resistant ulcerative colitis (UC), but their immunosuppressive effect elevates the risk of opportunistic infections, particularly tuberculosis (TB). Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) characterized by mucosal inflammation of the colon and rectum. For patients with moderate-to-severe UC refractory to conventional therapy (aminosalicylates, glucocorticoids), biologic agents are recommended such as anti-tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) agents, anti-integrin agents, interleukin (IL)-12/23 inhibitors and Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors [1]. Infliximab, a chimeric anti-TNF-α monoclonal antibody, is effective in both inducing and maintaining UC remission. However, its immunosuppressive effect elevates the risk of opportunistic infections, particularly tuberculosis (TB) [2].

TB associated with TNF-α inhibitor therapy often presents atypically, with a significantly higher rate of extrapulmonary involvement compared to the general population [3]. Hepatic and splenic TB are rare extrapulmonary forms. Due to nonspecific clinical manifestations and lack of typical imaging features, hepatic and splenic TB are frequently misdiagnosed as drug-induced liver injury, tumors, or IBD-related liver involvement. Here, we report a case of hepatic and splenic TB in a UC patient receiving infliximab therapy, and review relevant literature to discuss the risk factors, diagnosis, and treatment.

Case presentation

Clinical history

A 55-year-old male was first diagnosed with UC in 2003 due to abdominal pain and hematochezia. He achieved symptom remission after 3 months of sulfasalazine treatment and remained stable for nearly 18 years. In March 2021, he developed recurrent abdominal pain and diarrhea. Colonoscopy showed mucosal hyperemia from the splenic flexure to the rectum, along with scattered patchy erosions and scars. Remission was re‑induced with mesalazine. In March 2024, he underwent surgery for perianal abscess, followed by recurrence of abdominal pain and hematochezia postoperatively. In April 2024, colonoscopy revealed diffuse mucosal hyperemia, edema and extensive erosions involving the entire colon, with the transverse colon and rectosigmoid colon being severely affected. Widespread deep longitudinal ulcers were observed, which represented a typical endoscopic manifestation of concurrent cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection (Fig. 1, A-D). Subsequently, detection of CMV nucleic acid in the intestinal mucosa was positive. CMV DNA was detected by quantitative real-time PCR using the DaAn Gene HCMV Fluorescent-PCR Kit (DaAn Gene Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China), with a limit of detection (LOD) of 1.0 × 10⁴ copies/mL. The result was expressed as copies/mL of DNA eluate, and the measured value was 3.79 × 10⁴ copies/mL, which was distinctly positive according to the kit’s official Ct value and S-curve interpretation criteria. Mucosal samples were normalized by identical tissue weight and fixed-volume DNA elution prior to qPCR loading. Plain chest-abdomen-pelvis computed tomography (CT) showed no thoracic abnormality, but revealed colorectal wall thickening and mesenteric fat stranding in the pelvis, suggestive of active IBD. He received standard treatment with glucocorticoids and ganciclovir, and symptoms were relieved. Of note, for the concurrent CMV colitis in our patient, confirmatory CMV histopathology and immunohistochemistry were not performed. The diagnosis was clinically supported by the combination of typical endoscopic features, elevated tissue CMV nucleic acid load by standardized qPCR detection, and clinical remission after ganciclovir treatment.

In July 2024, when prednisone was tapered to 20 mg/d, abdominal pain and hematochezia recurred. Colonoscopy revealed mucosal congestion and edema in the rectum and sigmoid colon, with scattered superficial ulcers, indicating reactivation of ulcerative colitis. Laboratory tests for tuberculosis (purified protein derivative (PPD) and T-SPOT.TB), blood and mucosal polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for CMV and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), and toxin gene testing for Clostridium difficile were all negative. Chest CT was unremarkable. Computed Tomography Enterography (CTE) showed diffuse thickening of the colorectal wall without abnormalities in the small intestine, which was suggestive of UC. Previous perianal abscess resolved completely after surgical intervention without recurrence, and pelvic MRI and perianal ultrasound revealed no significant perianal abnormalities.

Due to glucocorticoid dependence, he was treated with vedolizumab 300 mg. However, the patient still had more than 10 episodes of bloody purulent stools per day, which persisted and even worsened after one week of vedolizumab treatment. Given the lack of early clinical improvement and the urgent need for rapid disease control in a patient with acute severe and glucocorticoid‑dependent UC, the decision was made to switch to infliximab rather than waiting the vedolizumab induction series. Infliximab 300 mg was then administered, leading to rapid symptom remission.

The patient received regular infliximab therapy, with sustained remission of abdominal pain and hematochezia. However, he developed intermittent fatigue, low-grade fever, and upper respiratory tract infection symptoms. These symptoms were alleviated several times after receiving treatments with levofloxacin and Chinese patent medicine at a local clinic. Chest radiography and sputum examination performed during these episodes showed no signs of pulmonary tuberculosis. After 3 doses of infliximab in October 2024, re-evaluation showed significant improvement in inflammatory indicators and colonoscopy findings, but elevated hepatic enzymes: alkaline phosphatase (ALP) 185 U/L (normal range: 45–125 U/L) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) 207 U/L (normal range: 10–60 U/L). In November 2024, abdominal enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at another hospital showed slightly enlarged liver, edema of Glisson’s capsule, slightly enlarged lymph nodes in the hepatic hilum, multiple hypointense lesions in the liver during portal phase (considering intrahepatic bile duct hamartoma), and multiple round hypointense lesions in the spleen (considering small hemangiomas or iron deposition nodules). He was advised to observe and follow up regularly by hepatobiliary surgery expert.

Further evaluation and diagnosis

In April 2025, following 6 doses of infliximab, the patient developed fever and headache without abdominal pain or hematochezia. Admission laboratory tests revealed the following: hemoglobin 113 g/L (↓), ALP 165 U/L (↑), GGT 119 U/L (↑), sodium 134.4 mmol/L (↓), fibrinogen 6.55 g/L (↑), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) 52.4 s (↑), D-dimer 1.09 mg/L (↑), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 77 mm/h (↑), C-reactive protein (CRP) 72.75 mg/L (↑); tumor markers and serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) level were normal; T-SPOT.TB showed A 32 (positive, normal < 6), B 38 (positive, normal < 6) and the PPD test was strongly positive. Gastroscopy was unremarkable. Colonoscopy showed a deformed and persistently open ileocecal valve, normal terminal ileum (10 cm examined), multiple polypoid changes from the ileocecal junction to the ascending colon, and diffuse cicatricial changes with multiple polypoid changes from transverse colon to rectum, suggestive of UC in remission (Fig. 1, E-H). Enhanced abdominopelvic CT demonstrated diffuse nodules in the liver and spleen, newly developed compared with the CT scan from 2024.

To further clarify the nature of liver nodules, an ultrasound-guided liver biopsy was performed. Ultrasound revealed multiple hypoechoic lesions in the liver, the largest measuring 1.2 × 0.8 cm in the left medial lobe with well-defined borders. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound showed that the nodule exhibited hypoenhancement later than the surrounding liver parenchyma in the arterial phase, and rapid fade in the portal and delayed phases. Pathological examination of the biopsy specimen showed epithelioid granulomatous inflammation with necrosis and multinucleated giant cell reaction (Fig. 2). Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), acid-fast, and periodic acid-silver methenamine (PASM) stains were all negative. However, Xpert mycobacterium TB/RIF test on the liver biopsy tissue was positive.

Treatment and outcome

Based on the patient’s symptoms of fatigue and fever, positive mycobacterium TB test results, and imaging and pathological findings, the patient was considered to have developed hepatic and splenic tuberculosis during infliximab therapy. Since May 2025, the patient has received quadruple anti-tuberculosis therapy with isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. Drug susceptibility testing was performed, including rifampicin resistance loci (all five loci in the ABCDE regions tested negative). Liver function tests were monitored every 4–8 weeks throughout treatment, and no elevation of transaminases exceeding three times the upper limit of normal was observed. Mesalazine treatment for ulcerative colitis continues, while infliximab therapy has been suspended. During anti-tuberculosis therapy, the patient’s fatigue and fever improved compared to previous status, and hemoglobin, ESR, CRP, and ALP, GGT (Fig. 3) gradually returned to normal. In December 2025, after a 9-month discontinuation of infliximab, the patient experienced recurrent abdominal pain and hematochezia. Colonoscopy showed significant mucosal hyperemia and edema, as well as widespread erosions and shallow ulcers, in the rectosigmoid area located within 15 cm from the anal verge (Fig. 1, I-L). Enhanced abdominal and pelvic CT showed multiple hypointense nodules in the liver and spleen, showing improvement compared to the May 2025 scan (Fig. 4). This indicates a recurrence of ulcerative colitis, with improved tuberculosis of the liver and spleen compared to previous findings, suggesting effective anti-tuberculosis therapy.

For ulcerative colitis, considering the patient’s history of biologic use and the current infectious status of hepatic and splenic tuberculosis, guselkumab was initiated. By December 2025, after 7 months of anti-TB therapy, the patient’s fever and fatigue had resolved, ALP/GGT normalized, and imaging showed improvement. TB was considered controlled but not completely cured. An infectious disease specialist and a clinical pharmacy specialist approved guselkumab initiation. Liver function and clinical symptoms were monitored every 4 weeks. Repeat CT at 3 months showed no TB progression. The patient has received three doses of guselkumab 200 mg intravenously at weeks 0, 4, and 8, while continuing oral mesalazine. In March 2026, following three courses of guselkumab treatment, the patient’s clinical symptoms, inflammatory markers, and colonoscopy findings have significantly improved (Fig. 1, M-P). Guselkumab was given 200 mg subcutaneously at week 12, and the subsequent dose is planned for 8 weeks later. Regarding hepatic and splenic tuberculosis, the patient remains on the four-drug quadruple anti-tuberculosis regimen and is still in the intensive treatment phase. The intensive phase was prolonged because follow-up imaging at 9 months still demonstrated residual hepatic and splenic nodules, prompting the infectious disease specialist to continue the four-drug regimen until complete radiographic resolution. A comprehensive reassessment will be performed after completing 12 months of anti-tuberculosis therapy to decide whether to de-escalate to a two-drug consolidation regimen thereafter.

Discussion and literature review

Biologic agents and the risk of tuberculosis

TNF-α inhibitors, such as infliximab, have dramatically improved outcomes for patients with IBD. Despite their efficacy, the heightened risk of opportunistic infections, especially TB, remains a critical concern in clinical practice [4, 5]. A recent meta-analysis of 130,114 IBD patients treated with TNF-α inhibitors reported 373 cases of TB, yielding a pooled prevalence of 0.08% [6]. Pulmonary TB is the most common form of the disease, while extrapulmonary involvement is rare. Hepatic and splenic TB, an exceptionally rare manifestation, has not been previously documented in IBD patients receiving biologic therapy [5, 7, 8].

The risk of active TB is not uniform and is influenced by several key factors. Firstly, the TB burden in the patient’s country of residence is a major determinant. The pooled prevalence of TB among biologic-treated patients is 0.02% in low-burden, 0.21% in medium-burden, and 1.59% in high-burden countries [6]. This geographic disparity underscores the critical importance of rigorous screening and monitoring in high-prevalence regions like China. Secondly, the risk is time-dependent, with the highest incidence of active TB occurring within the first six months of biologic initiation [9]. In the present case, the patient developed symptoms and liver enzyme abnormalities after approximately two months of infliximab therapy (after three treatment courses), consistent with this high-risk period. Thirdly, the cumulative immunosuppressive effect of sequential biologic use may further elevate susceptibility. The patient in this case was switched from vedolizumab, an integrin inhibitor with a relatively low TB risk [10], to infliximab with a greater TB risk [11–13]. The sequential use of biologics can result in a compounded immunosuppressive state, increasing vulnerability to infections [14].

Clinical challenges in diagnosing hepatic and splenic TB

The diagnosis of hepatic and splenic TB remains challenging due to its low incidence and non-specific clinical manifestations, which commonly include fever, fatigue, respiratory symptoms, and elevated liver enzymes [15, 16]. These constitutional symptoms are often initially attributed to more prevalent conditions, contributing to diagnostic delays. In this case, the patient’s early symptoms of fatigue and low-grade fever were initially ascribed to infliximab-related adverse effects, while concurrent elevations in ALP and GGT were interpreted as drug-induced liver injury (DILI) [16].

Diagnostic complexity is further compounded by the heterogeneity of imaging features. Hepatic and splenic TB can manifest as multiple nodules, diffuse infiltration, or abscesses on imaging [17]. Contrast-enhanced imaging may present “ring enhancement” or “fast-in and fast-out” pattern, which are non-specific and can overlap with findings seen in primary or metastatic malignancies and pyogenic abscesses. In this patient, the atypical imaging characteristics initially prompted suspicion of neoplastic disease by radiologists and hepatobiliary surgeons.

Another factor contributed to the delayed diagnosis in this case. The patient’s prior treatment with levofloxacin for a presumed respiratory tract infection likely exerted a transient anti-mycobacterial effect, temporarily masking disease progression while reducing the mycobacterial load and potentially interfered pathogen detection tests of TB [18]. This case highlights that while baseline TB screening (e.g. PPD, T-SPOT.TB) may be negative, regular monitoring during therapy is crucial for early detection of primary infection or reactivation of latent TB. However, false-negative baseline test results can occur, particularly in patients with severe inflammation, corticosteroid use, or immunosuppression. Therefore, the patient’s PPD and T-SPOT.TB converting from negative to positive during treatment suggested possible new TB infection or reactivation of prior latent TB infection, although baseline false-negative results cannot be entirely excluded. Mycobacterial culture is well-known to be time-consuming (requiring approximately one month at our center) with a relatively low positivity rate. In contrast, the Xpert MTB/RIF assay offers high sensitivity and specificity for the detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, with a much shorter turnaround time. Given the limited amount of liver biopsy tissue obtained, priority was given to histopathological examination and Xpert MTB/RIF testing. A positive Xpert MTB/RIF result was received promptly, so mycobacterial culture was not pursued subsequently. Current guidelines recommend mandatory baseline TB screening tests and chest CT for all patients initiating biologic therapy [19, 20]. For those with latent TB infection, three weeks of preventive anti-tuberculosis treatment is advised before starting biologics, with ongoing 6–9 months anti-tuberculosis treatment during biologic therapy [19, 21].

Beyond these diagnostic challenges specific to hepatosplenic TB, clarifying the differential diagnosis of granulomatous hepatitis is also essential to confirm the final diagnosis. Fungal infection was primarily excluded based on negative fungal staining results of liver biopsy specimens, absence of typical supportive imaging manifestations, and confirmed positive Xpert MTB/RIF findings consistent with tuberculous infection. Additionally, the patient presented with relatively mild clinical symptoms and achieved obvious improvement after levofloxacin treatment, which was inconsistent with the severe systemic toxic reactions and typical abdominal manifestations of invasive hepatic fungal infection. Sarcoidosis was excluded due to the absence of hilar or mediastinal lymphadenopathy on chest CT, normal ACE level, and the lack of cutaneous or ocular manifestations. Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) was unlikely because anti‑mitochondrial antibody (AMA) testing was negative, and histological examination revealed granuloma formation without accompanying bile duct destruction. Other causes of non‑caseating granulomas, such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and IgG4‑related disease, were also ruled out. Vasculitis antibody panels and serum IgG4 levels were negative, and liver pathology showed no evidence of vasculitis, lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, or storiform fibrosis.

Therapeutic management and future considerations

The cornerstone of managing active TB in the context of biologic therapy is the immediate discontinuation of the immunosuppressive agent and the prompt initiation of anti-tuberculosis treatment. Due to the poor penetration of anti-TB drugs and the slow resolution of lesions, extrapulmonary TB, including hepatic and splenic involvement, typically requires a longer treatment course of at least 12 months [22]. The standard regimen consists of an intensive 2–3 month four-drug induction phase (isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide), followed by a 9–10 month consolidation phase de-escalated to a two-drug regimen (isoniazid and rifampicin) [23, 24]. Given the rarity and severity of combined hepatic and splenic tuberculosis in the setting of immunosuppression, standard anti-tuberculosis regimens are insufficient to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes, thereby necessitating intensified and prolonged anti-tuberculosis treatment [25–26]. This patient was initiated on a standard four-drug intensive regimen, with a total planned treatment duration of 12 months. Regimen de-escalation to two-drug maintenance will be determined at the 12-month follow-up assessment based on clinical, laboratory and imaging responses. We acknowledge that a 12-month four-drug intensive regimen is not standard. Most guidelines recommend de-escalation after 2 months. Our approach was individualized based on persistent imaging abnormalities and specialist consultation. This should not be generalized without infectious disease input.

Consistent with the typical clinical course of UC, the patient maintained long-term remission for 18 years with single-agent sulfasalazine therapy, responded well to corticosteroid treatment, and had no evidence of small bowel involvement, persistent perianal disease or non-caseating granulomas. These clinical, endoscopic, and radiological findings are highly suggestive of ulcerative colitis, although a diagnosis of Crohn’s disease or IBD-unclassified cannot be entirely excluded. An interesting observation in this case was the sustained remission of ulcerative colitis for approximately 9 months following infliximab cessation, managed with mesalazine monotherapy. This may be partially attributable to the anti-inflammatory properties of the anti-TB medications, particularly rifampicin and isoniazid, which have been shown to modulate inflammatory pathways [27, 28]. Mechanistically, isoniazid exerts anti-inflammatory effects through multiple pathways, including suppression of IL-1 production, inhibition of reactive oxygen species (ROS) release, downregulation of the transcriptional regulatory activity of NF-κB and activator protein 1 (AP-1), and activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) expression [29]. Additionally, recent evidence suggests that isoniazid can attenuate intestinal inflammation by limiting the mucosal colonization of pathobionts such as segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB), thereby reducing microbial-driven inflammatory responses in the gut [30]. These mycobacteria-independent immunomodulatory effects may have contributed to the prolonged disease remission observed in this patient during anti-TB therapy.

The selection of biologic agents for managing the patient’s ulcerative colitis during reactivation and subsequent therapeutic adjustments presents a critical clinical decision. Initially, vedolizumab was administered, but we acknowledge that a one-week interval is insufficient to evaluate its full therapeutic response, as its clinical efficacy typically manifests after induction (weeks 6–14) in both clinical trials and real-world practice [31, 32]. However, the patient presented with glucocorticoid-dependent, acute severe active UC (≥ 10 bloody stools per day, Mayo endoscopic score 3 points) and failed to achieve symptom relief after one week of vedolizumab treatment, which necessitated an immediate adjustment of the therapeutic strategy. Given that the patient had previously received a course of glucocorticoid therapy, repeated administration of high-dose glucocorticoids was clinically inappropriate. Compared with vedolizumab, infliximab exerts more potent therapeutic effects with a more rapid onset of action, making it the preferred agent for this acute therapeutic transition [33]. Following this initial switch, the subsequent selection of a long-term maintenance biologic required careful consideration of safety, particularly in the context of the patient’s prior TB history. While vedolizumab has a favorable safety profile with a low risk of TB reactivation due to its gut selectivity, its earlier inadequate response to short-term therapy precluded its reuse. Upadacitinib, an oral JAK1 inhibitor, demonstrates superior efficacy but its mechanism of action inherently suppresses immune function and elevates TB risk [34]. These safety considerations prompted a search for an alternative with a more favorable risk profile in the context of prior TB. Ustekinumab, which targets the IL-12/23 pathway, has shown a reassuring safety record. A 1-year multicenter retrospective cohort study demonstrated that its use in patients with Crohn’s disease and latent tuberculosis or inactive hepatitis B did not increase the risk of viral reactivation [35]. Guselkumab offers even greater precision by selectively targeting IL-23 alone, thereby preserving the IL-12 pathway that is crucial for host defense against intracellular pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Therefore, Guselkumab theoretically presents a lower risk of TB reactivation compared to dual pathway inhibitors like Ustekinumab [36]. However, its safety during active extrapulmonary TB has not been established. Our decision was made with multidisciplinary input and close monitoring. Consequently, it was chosen as the subsequent biologic for this patient, a decision supported by its current favorable efficacy and safety profile. Our experience highlights the urgent need for individualized therapeutic decision-making in acute severe and refractory UC, which requires careful balancing of acute disease severity, treatment response kinetics, and the potential risks of premature treatment switching and immunosuppression-related complications.

Conclusion

Hepatic and splenic TB is a rare but serious opportunistic infection in UC patients receiving biologic therapy, with particular clinical attention warranted in individuals exposed to sequential biologic treatment. Clinicians should remain vigilant for nonspecific symptoms (such as fatigue, fever) and abnormal liver enzymes, avoid misdiagnosis as drug-related adverse reactions, and perform timely imaging and pathological examinations. Routine TB screening (PPD, T-SPOT.TB) before and during biologic therapy is essential for early detection. Discontinuation of previous biologics and initiation of prolonged anti-TB therapy (at least 12 months) can improve prognosis, and anti-TB drugs may have synergistic effects on UC control. Novel biological agents offer additional therapeutic options for IBD, particularly for patients who develop opportunistic infections such as TB during anti‑TNF‑α therapy.
