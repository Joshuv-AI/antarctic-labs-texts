Internal gelation-based alginate hydrogel films incorporating Quercus infectoria gall extract for multifunctional wound dressing applications
Authors: Suttiwan Wunnoo, Punnita Wae-Asae, Sutathip Kaewrak, Kitiya Ekchaweng, Supayang Piyawan Voravuthikunchai, Chanawee Jakkawanpitak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic wounds pose a major clinical challenge, as persistent infection, excessive inflammation, and oxidative stress significantly impede tissue repair. Developing multifunctional biomaterials capable of addressing these factors is essential for effective wound management. In this study, alginate-Quercus infectoria gall (QIG) hydrogel films were fabricated via internal gelation using calcium carbonate and glucono-δ-lactone to achieve uniform crosslinking. The optimized formulation exhibited adequate wound-fluid uptake (716.18 ± 25.05%), a balanced water vapor transmission rate (1,304.08 ± 80.67 g/m2/day), and high optical transparency (79.94 ± 3.48% transmittance), allowing visual monitoring of the wound bed without dressing removal. The hydrogel film demonstrated excellent encapsulation of QIG extract, achieving a high encapsulation efficiency (> 99%), and displayed a biphasic release profile, with approximately 62.57 ± 6.06% of the extract released within 24 h. Biological evaluations revealed that the films exhibited strong antioxidant activity and significant anti-inflammatory effects, evidenced by a 50.08 ± 3.19% reduction in nitric oxide production in LPS-stimulated macrophages. Moreover, the alginate–QIG hydrogel film demonstrated potent antibacterial efficacy, achieving up to 99.99% inhibition of bacterial growth after 24 h of treatment. Collectively, these findings indicate that alginate–QIG hydrogel films combine desirable physicochemical and biological characteristics, highlighting their potential as naturally derived multifunctional dressings for effective wound management.

Introduction

Wound healing is a complex biological process that progresses through overlapping phases, including hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. Disruption of these phases by factors such as infection, excessive inflammation, or oxidative stress can impair the normal repair process, often leading to chronic wounds that are difficult to manage1. Bacterial infection is one of the most frequent complications in open wounds, where persistent microbial colonization and toxin release impair tissue regeneration and delay the healing process2. The resulting sustained inflammatory response, driven by bacterial toxins and prolonged immune activation, accelerates tissue degradation and disrupts the normal repair cascade by inhibiting both the migration and proliferation of fibroblasts and keratinocytes3,4. Moreover, oxidative stress, caused by an imbalance between the generation of reactive oxygen species and antioxidant defenses, further contributes to cellular injury and delays tissue regeneration5. These combined effects ultimately delay wound closure and contribute to the development of chronic wounds. Therefore, the development of multifunctional wound dressings capable of preventing infection, reducing inflammation, scavenging reactive oxygen species, and supporting tissue regeneration has become an important focus in the design of advanced biomaterials for effective wound management.

Alginate is a naturally occurring polysaccharide that has gained widespread use in biomedical research and applications because of its excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and non-toxic characteristics6. It is composed of repeating units of β-D-mannuronic acid (M) and α-L-guluronic acid (G) arranged in alternating or homopolymeric blocks along the polymer chain7. When exposed to divalent cations such as calcium, the carboxyl groups of alginate form ionic crosslinks, resulting in hydrogel formation. Gelation can occur through two main approaches: external and internal methods. In external gelation, calcium ions diffuse from the surrounding solution into the alginate matrix, often producing non-uniform crosslinking and heterogeneous gel structures8. In contrast, internal gelation relies on the gradual release of calcium ions from slightly soluble calcium salts that are evenly dispersed within the alginate solution in the presence of a weak acid. As the pH slowly decreases, calcium ions are released inside the gel and initiate crosslinking throughout the entire matrix rather than only at the surface. This controlled mechanism promotes homogeneous gel formation and allows accurate regulation of gelation kinetics9. The resulting hydrogels display a well-organized three-dimensional network with tunable porosity and enhanced mechanical stability, making them highly suitable for various biomedical uses, particularly in wound-healing applications.

Alginate hydrogels have been widely used as drug delivery carriers because their unique physicochemical properties enable efficient encapsulation, protection, and controlled release of therapeutic agents. The mild gelation conditions of alginate make it suitable for incorporating sensitive molecules such as antibiotics, proteins, and plant-derived bioactive compounds without compromising their stability or biological activity10. In addition, the hydrated and porous three-dimensional network of alginate hydrogels facilitates the diffusion of encapsulated substances while maintaining a moist microenvironment that supports cell proliferation and tissue regeneration11. These properties make alginate hydrogels ideal for biomedical applications that require controlled and sustained delivery of therapeutic agents to support wound healing and tissue repair.

Although alginate hydrogels possess excellent physicochemical and mechanical properties, their lack of inherent biological activity limits their capacity to actively facilitate wound management. Pure alginate primarily functions as a structural matrix that maintains moisture and provides physical protection, but does not contribute to infection control or oxidative balance at the wound site12. To overcome this limitation, we incorporated Q. infectoria gall extract into the alginate hydrogel system to enhance its therapeutic functionality. QIG has long been used in traditional Thai and other Asian medicines, primarily in crude extracts or aqueous preparations, for the treatment of wounds13,14. The galls are rich in hydrolysable tannins and phenolic acids, including gallotannin, gallic acid, and ellagic acid, which play critical roles in neutralizing reactive oxygen species, suppressing inflammation, and inhibiting microbial growth15,16. Therefore, incorporating QIG extract into alginate hydrogels is expected to enhance their biological functionality, transforming them from passive dressings into bioactive materials capable of actively promoting wound healing.

Several previous studies have demonstrated that QIG extract can be incorporated into various material systems, including patches, gels, and topical formulations, for different functional applications13,16,17. In terms of film-based materials, alginate has also been used to fabricate films containing nutgall extract, which have been reported to exhibit antioxidant and antimicrobial properties18. However, those films were prepared via simple mixing followed by solvent evaporation and were not specifically designed for biomedical applications. As a result, key requirements for advanced wound dressing applications, including controlled physical properties, mechanical performance under hydrated conditions, fluid management capability, and integrated biological functionality, were not systematically addressed.

This study reports the development of alginate–QIG hydrogel films as advanced wound dressing materials. The hydrogel was intentionally converted into a film format to improve handling, mechanical stability, and storage convenience, while allowing rapid rehydration upon contact with wound exudate to recreate a moist wound environment. The films were fabricated using an internal gelation technique, in which calcium carbonate and glucono-δ-lactone were employed as ionic crosslinking agents to achieve uniform gelation throughout the polymer matrix. Glycerol was incorporated as a plasticizer to enhance flexibility and mechanical strength. The resulting films were comprehensively characterized for their morphology, chemical structure, mechanical performance, swelling behavior, water vapor transmission rate, transparency, and release characteristics. Furthermore, their biological properties, including cytocompatibility, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory effects, were systematically examined (Fig. 1). Collectively, the results demonstrate that these naturally derived hydrogel films exhibit favorable physicochemical and biological properties, making them promising candidates for advanced wound management.Fig. 1Schematic overview of the study scope and workflow. Results and discussion

Structural and mechanical characterization of alginate hydrogel films prepared by internal gelation

Internal gelation was performed using 25 mM GDL to enable consistent Ca2+ ion release from CaCO3 for homogeneous hydrogel formation. As shown in Fig. 2a, the combination of 25 mM GDL with varying CaCO3 concentrations (6.25 to 50 mM) successfully produced solid alginate hydrogels. After dehydration at 60 °C, the hydrogels transformed into thin film structures that were flexible and could be tweezed without breaking. The pH evolution during the internal gelation process is shown in Fig. 2b. Upon dissolution, glucono-δ-lactone gradually generates H+ ions, which promote the dissolution of CaCO3 and the subsequent release of Ca2+ ions for ionic crosslinking of alginate chains. Notably, higher CaCO3 concentrations resulted in higher final pH values, reflecting the increased buffering capacity of CaCO3 and the greater availability of Ca2+ ions during gelation. For effective clinical performance, wound dressing films should possess adequate mechanical strength to endure handling and application, while maintaining sufficient flexibility to conform closely to the wound surface. Reported mechanical properties of many commercial wound dressings show Young’s modulus values typically ranging from 0.06 to 35.97 MPa, tensile strength between 0.04 and 4.60 MPa, and elongation at break from 27.13 to 814%, depending on the material type (e.g., hydrogel, hydrocolloid, or multilayer composite)19. In this context, the alginate hydrogel films developed in this study exhibited Young’s modulus values of 1.31 to 1.86 MPa, tensile strengths of 0.82 to 1.13 MPa, and elongations of 97 to 120%, indicating mechanical performance comparable to that of established commercial wound dressing standards. Collectively, these properties confirm that the developed alginate hydrogel films possess an optimal balance of strength and flexibility suitable for effective wound care applications.

Microstructural characteristics of alginate hydrogels and water-related properties of the corresponding hydrogel films

The internal microstructure of the alginate hydrogels was examined using SEM. The alginate hydrogels exhibited the interconnected porous morphology characteristic of ionically crosslinked networks, with pore size and compactness dependent on CaCO3 concentration. Hydrogels prepared with 6.25 mM CaCO3 exhibited larger pores and a relatively loose network, whereas those containing 50 mM CaCO3 showed smaller, denser, and more uniformly distributed pores (Fig. 3a). The quantitative pore size distribution analysis further supports these observations, showing that hydrogels prepared with 6.25 mM CaCO3 exhibited a median pore diameter of 44.52 μm (Fig. 3b), while those prepared with 50 mM CaCO3 had a median pore diameter of 27.01 μm (Fig. 3c). The swelling behavior of alginate hydrogel films prepared with varying CaCO3 concentrations was examined to evaluate their fluid uptake capacity under simulated wound fluid conditions. Representative photographs in Fig. 3d show that all alginate hydrogel films maintained their macroscopic integrity during incubation in simulated wound fluid for up to 72 h, without visible disintegration, indicating that the films can accommodate fluid uptake while preserving their physical form. The quantitative swelling behavior of the alginate hydrogel films is shown in Fig. 3e. All formulations exhibited a biphasic swelling profile, characterized by rapid water uptake during the initial stage followed by a gradual approach to equilibrium. Notably, the swelling ratio decreased with increasing CaCO3 concentration, suggesting that a higher crosslinking density restricts polymer chain mobility and reduces water diffusion within the gel matrix. The film prepared with 6.25 mM CaCO3 displayed the highest swelling ratio of 716.18 ± 25.05%, while films prepared with 12.5, 25, and 50 mM CaCO3 exhibited progressively lower values of 561.17 ± 12.12%, 470.44 ± 3.58%, and 449.42 ± 3.53%, respectively. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the controlled swelling behavior of the hydrogel films enables effective absorption and retention of wound exudate, which is desirable for superficial and moderately exuding wounds.

The water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) of the alginate hydrogel films was measured to assess their capacity to maintain an optimal moisture balance for wound healing (Fig. 3f). The open surface exhibited the highest WVTR (2,375.30 ± 209.59 g/m2/day), representing unrestricted evaporation, whereas the closed surface showed the lowest value (46.57 ± 10.33 g/m2/day), indicating complete occlusion. A balanced WVTR is essential for maintaining the optimal hydration level required for effective wound healing. Dressings with excessively high WVTR promote rapid moisture loss, which may result in wound bed dehydration, desiccation of newly formed tissue, and delayed epithelialization. Conversely, dressings with very low WVTR tend to trap exudates and moisture, resulting in tissue maceration and a higher risk of bacterial growth. The intermediate WVTR values observed for the alginate hydrogel films (1,187.65 ± 184.84 to 1,304.08 ± 80.67 g/m2/day) demonstrate their ability to prevent excessive water loss while maintaining sufficient hydration at the wound interface. These values fall within the desirable range reported (954.8–2028.3 g/m2/day), known to support optimal moisture balance and gas exchange during wound healing20. Moreover, this moderate permeability aligns with the moist wound-healing concept, which has been shown to effectively promote keratinocyte migration, angiogenesis, and granulation tissue formation21. Accordingly, the alginate hydrogel films developed in this study exhibit well-balanced WVTR characteristics that may provide a moist and physiologically favorable environment potentially beneficial for wound healing.

Structural and optical characterization of alginate–QIG hydrogel films.

To prepare a multifunctional hydrogel, Q. infectoria gall extract was incorporated into alginate hydrogel films at concentrations up to 4 mg/mL. The results in Table 2 confirm that QIG extract can be efficiently incorporated into alginate hydrogel films (> 99%) without altering their dimensional uniformity or swelling properties. The weight variation among different extract formulations was within a narrow range (± 2%), indicating consistent film fabrication. The chemical composition of the alginate–QIG hydrogel films was analyzed by FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 4a). The blank alginate film displayed characteristic absorption bands at 3360 cm−1, 2976 cm−1, 1603 cm−1, 1412 cm−1, and 1026 cm−1, corresponding to the typical functional groups of alginate22. Upon incorporation of QIG, additional absorption features were observed above the alginate baseline at approximately 1209, 1339, and 1694 cm−1, which were absent or less pronounced in the alginate control. The presence of overlapping bands confirms the successful incorporation of QIG into the alginate network. The visual appearance and optical transparency of the alginate–QIG hydrogel films are presented in Fig. 4b, c. All films exhibited a uniform color distribution, with color intensity increasing as the concentration of QIG extract increased. Despite this darker coloration, the underlying text remained clearly visible when the films were placed over a printed letter “A” (Fig. 4b), showing that the films retained sufficient transparency for visual inspection of the wound. The UV–Vis transmittance spectra further supported these observations. The blank alginate film exhibited the highest transmittance across the visible range (400–800 nm), with an average transmittance of 85.18 ± 0.88%. Incorporation of the QIG extract resulted in a gradual decrease in transmittance, yielding average transmittance values of 84.06 ± 2.14%, 79.94 ± 3.48%, and 73.00 ± 7.97% for the films containing 0.25, 1, and 4 mg/mL of extract, respectively (Fig. 4c). Transparency is a key functional attribute of modern wound dressings, allowing direct visualization of the wound bed without removal. This allows real-time monitoring of exudate levels, tissue color, and signs of infection. Consequently, dressing changes can be performed less frequently, minimizing pain and secondary trauma. In addition to transparency, the alginate–QIG hydrogel films demonstrated a decrease in light transmittance in the ultraviolet (UV) region (below 400 nm), implying a potential UV-filtering effect. Open wounds are particularly susceptible to UV radiation because the absence of the epidermal barrier leaves the underlying dermal tissue directly exposed to environmental factors. UV irradiation can generate excessive reactive oxygen species, resulting in lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, and DNA damage in skin cells23. These oxidative effects disrupt fibroblast function, reduce collagen synthesis, and degrade extracellular matrix components, thereby delaying wound healing24. Hence, the alginate–QIG hydrogel films not only provide high transparency for wound monitoring but also offer protective capabilities that shield the wound from harmful UV exposure.

Release kinetics and antioxidant activity of alginate–QIG hydrogel films

The cumulative release profile of total tannins from alginate–QIG hydrogel films is shown in Fig. 5a. All formulations exhibited a biphasic release pattern with a rapid initial burst within the first 1 h, followed by a slower and sustained release phase. This behavior closely paralleled the swelling profile observed in Fig. 3e, suggesting that the initial burst was driven by rapid hydrogel swelling, which opened diffusion pathways for the physically entrapped QIG molecules. Although the total QIG content differed among formulations (0.25, 1, and 4 mg/mL extract), the cumulative percentage of tannins released after 24 h remained relatively similar (61.54 ± 5.26% to 62.57 ± 6.06%). This indicates that the release rate was primarily governed by the polymer matrix rather than by the extract concentration gradient. Kinetic modeling further supported this interpretation. Among the models tested, the Korsmeyer–Peppas model provided the best fit with R2 ≈ 0.80, and a release exponent of n = 0.15, confirming that the mechanism followed Fickian diffusion. This indicates that the extract was released predominantly through diffusion within the hydrated alginate matrix. In clinical practice, there is no universally established guideline for determining the optimal frequency of wound dressing replacement. However, in exudative wounds, such as burns, chronic ulcers, or infected wounds, dressings are typically replaced daily based on the volume of wound exudate19. The antioxidant activities of the released extracts were further evaluated using DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays. The results showed a direct correlation with the cumulative release profile (Fig. 5a), suggesting that the increase in antioxidant activity was driven by the continuous diffusion of the extract from the hydrogel. Among all formulations, the alginate–QIG hydrogel film containing 4 mg/mL extract exhibited the highest antioxidant activity throughout the 24 h period in all assays, followed by the 1 mg/mL and 0.25 mg/mL formulations. In contrast, the alginate film without extract showed no detectable antioxidant activity (Fig. 5b–d). These findings indicate that the released extract remained chemically stable and biologically active throughout the diffusion process. The sustained release of antioxidants is particularly beneficial for wound healing, especially in chronic and burn wounds, where excessive reactive oxygen species cause oxidative damage, prolong inflammation, and delay the healing process24,25. Our previous study demonstrated that Q. infectoria gall extract exhibits potent antioxidant and cytoprotective activities, effectively protecting L929 fibroblasts from H2O2-induced oxidative injury and supporting normal cell migration15. By maintaining redox balance and limiting oxidative injury, the QIG-loaded alginate hydrogel film may help protect cells from oxidative stress and create a microenvironment conducive to tissue repair and regeneration. Together, these findings suggest that the alginate–QIG hydrogel film could serve as a promising multifunctional wound dressing for future applications in managing oxidative stress-related wounds.

Biocompatibility of alginate–QIG hydrogel films

The cytocompatibility of alginate–QIG hydrogel films was evaluated using L929 fibroblast cells through the MTT assay, as shown in Fig. 6. Microscopic observations revealed that the untreated control and cells exposed to the conditioned medium from the alginate hydrogel film exhibited uniform distribution and a confluent monolayer, indicating healthy cell proliferation. After MTT treatment, these cells showed intense purple formazan coloration, confirming high metabolic activity. In contrast, cells treated with 10% DMSO (positive control) appeared sparse and weakly colored, reflecting loss of viability. Cells cultured with conditioned media from alginate–QIG hydrogel films containing 0.25 and 1 mg/mL extract maintained dense, uniformly stained layers comparable to the control, indicating a non-cytotoxic effect. However, a decrease in cell density and formazan intensity was observed in the 4 mg/mL QIG extract-loaded alginate hydrogel film group (Fig. 6a). Quantitative MTT results confirmed these findings. The untreated control group and cells treated with conditioned medium from the alginate hydrogel film showed cell viabilities of 100.00 ± 7.07% and 101.02 ± 5.53%, respectively. Cells treated with the released medium from the alginate–QIG hydrogel films containing 0.25 and 1 mg/mL extract exhibited cell viabilities of 100.88 ± 5.32% and 94.76 ± 7.33%, respectively, which were not significantly different from the control (p > 0.05). According to the criteria of ISO 10,993–5:2009, a material is regarded as non-cytotoxic when cell viability exceeds 70% compared with the untreated control26. In this study, conditioned media obtained from the alginate hydrogel film resulted in fibroblast viability above this threshold, confirming the biocompatibility of the alginate matrix and the CaCO3-GDL internal gelation system. When QIG extract was incorporated into the alginate matrix, fibroblast viability was found to be dependent on extract concentration. Films containing 0.25 and 1 mg/mL QIG extract showed cell viability above 70%, indicating acceptable cytocompatibility. In contrast, a significant decline in viability was observed for the film containing 4 mg/mL extract, which is classified as moderately cytotoxic. The concentration-dependent effect observed in this study is consistent with previous reports indicating that the biological activity of Q. infectoria gall extract is influenced by dosage. At low concentrations (< 10 µg/mL), the extract has been shown to promote proliferation in human fetal osteoblast cell lines27, whereas at high concentrations (> 300 µg/mL), it has been reported to induce autophagic cell death in colorectal cancer cells28. This biphasic response may be attributed to the presence of polyphenolic compounds, which act as potent antioxidants at low levels but can exhibit pro-oxidant or protein-precipitating properties at higher concentrations29. Therefore, maintaining an optimal concentration of QIG extract within the alginate matrix is crucial to ensure cytocompatibility while effectively harnessing its bioactive potential.

Anti-inflammatory activity of alginate–QIG hydrogel films

The anti-inflammatory potential of alginate–QIG hydrogel films was assessed using LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages. As shown in Fig. 7a, conditioned media obtained from the alginate hydrogel film and from QIG-loaded films containing 0.25 and 1 mg/mL of extract did not significantly affect macrophage viability compared with the control (p > 0.05), confirming the non-cytotoxic nature of these formulations. For inflammation activation, RAW264.7 macrophages were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL), which markedly increased nitric oxide (NO) production compared with the untreated control, indicating successful induction of the inflammatory response. Co-treatment with indomethacin (50 µg/mL), a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, significantly suppressed NO release from 64.45 ± 4.65 µM in the LPS-only group to 20.25 ± 4.65 µM (p < 0.05), confirming its anti-inflammatory effect. Conditioned media obtained from the alginate hydrogel film did not significantly alter NO production levels in LPS-treated cells (64.30 ± 0.75 µM, p > 0.05), suggesting that the alginate matrix itself possessed no inherent anti-inflammatory activity. In contrast, co-treatment of LPS with conditioned medium from alginate–QIG hydrogel films containing 0.25 or 1 mg/mL QIG extract significantly reduced NO production to 51.12 ± 1.81 and 32.28 ± 2.05 µM, respectively (Fig. 7b). The potent anti-inflammatory activity of the alginate–QIG hydrogel film highlights its potential as an advanced wound dressing for chronic and excessive inflammatory conditions such as diabetic ulcers and burn wounds. In these wounds, prolonged macrophage activation results in the excessive release of nitric oxide and pro-inflammatory cytokines1,5,25. The sustained inflammation leads to the overproduction of reactive oxygen species, causing lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and degradation of the extracellular matrix24. Consequently, these processes hinder fibroblast proliferation, delay re-epithelialization, and compromise overall tissue repair5. A previous study has demonstrated that Q. infectoria gall extract exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects by downregulating the Set7/NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to reduced expression of inflammatory mediators30. Furthermore, ointment formulations containing Q. infectoria gall extract have been shown to decrease IL-6 and TNF-α levels at the wound site and accelerate wound closure in streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice by shortening the inflammatory phase14. Hence, the incorporation of QIG extract into an alginate-based hydrogel system offers a promising strategy for maintaining a controlled release of its bioactive compounds, thereby allowing continuous modulation of inflammation and oxidative stress at the wound site. Collectively, these findings support the potential of alginate–QIG hydrogel films as an effective multifunctional wound dressing with strong translational potential for clinical application in advanced wound care.

The plain alginate hydrogel film exhibited no inhibitory activity, confirming the absence of inherent antibacterial effects. To further assess the stability of the alginate–QIG hydrogel film, the samples were subjected to stress conditions, including autoclaving and repeated freeze–thaw cycles. Following exposure to these conditions, the samples retained their original appearance, showing no noticeable changes in color or transparency. Conclusion

This study successfully developed alginate–QIG hydrogel films using an internal gelation technique, resulting in wound dressing materials with favorable physicochemical and biological properties. The optimized formulation exhibited appropriate mechanical strength, controlled swelling behavior, and a balanced water vapor transmission rate, which are beneficial for maintaining a moist environment conducive to wound healing. The incorporation of Q. infectoria gall extract further enhanced the functionality of the films by imparting antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities, thereby integrating physical wound coverage with active therapeutic effects. Owing to the use of naturally derived, biocompatible materials and a scalable fabrication process, this system shows strong potential for further development and commercialization as a next-generation bioactive wound dressing. Future in vivo evaluations and long-term stability studies will be essential to confirm its therapeutic efficacy and support clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Dried Q. infectoria galls were obtained from a commercial herbal supplier, authenticated for botanical identity by Dr. Sukanya Dej-adisai, and deposited as voucher specimen No. SKP225170901 at the Herbarium, Faculty of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Prince of Songkla University, Hat Yai, Songkhla, Thailand. Sodium alginate (medium viscosity, approximate Mw = 100,000–200,000 g/mol), bovine serum albumin (BSA), 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), ferric chloride hexahydrate, and 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). TRIzol™ Reagent and RevertAid RT Reverse Transcription Kit were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). EvaGreen® qPCR Mix Plus was purchased from Solis BioDyne (Tartu, Estonia). For cell culture, Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, high glucose), fetal bovine serum (FBS), Antibiotic–Antimycotic solution (100 ×), and trypsin–EDTA (0.25%) were purchased from Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). For bacterial culture, Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA) and Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB) were obtained from HiMedia Laboratories (Mumbai, India). All other chemicals and solvents were of analytical grade and obtained from Krungthepchemi Co., Ltd. (Bangkok, Thailand).

Preparation of Q. infectoria gall extract

The ethanolic extract of Q. infectoria galls was obtained using previously reported procedures31. Briefly, powdered Q. infectoria galls were macerated in 95% ethanol at a weight-to-volume ratio of 1:5 (w/v) for extraction. The mixture was left at room temperature for seven days and then filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper to remove insoluble materials. The solvent was evaporated under reduced pressure at 60 °C using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph Hei-VAP Core, Germany) to obtain the crude ethanolic extract. For experimental applications, the crude extract was dissolved in distilled water to prepare a stock solution of 100 mg/mL and sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 µm syringe filter before use.

Fabrication of Q. infectoria gall extract-loaded alginate hydrogel films

Alginate hydrogels were fabricated using a modified internal gelation method based on the previously described protocol9, with adjustments for film preparation. Briefly, sodium alginate powder (2% w/v) was dissolved in distilled water under continuous stirring at room temperature until a clear, homogeneous solution was obtained. Glycerol (5% v/v) was added as a plasticizer to improve film flexibility. The alginate solution was then cast into silicone molds, and internal ionic crosslinking was initiated by adding glucono-δ-lactone (GDL) and calcium carbonate (CaCO3) at varying concentrations to determine the optimal gelation ratio. After 16–24 h of gelation at room temperature, the hydrogels were dried at 60 °C for an additional 16–24 h, or until reaching a constant weight, to produce hydrogel films.

For extract-loaded films, Q. infectoria gall extract was incorporated into the alginate solution at final concentrations of 0.25, 1.0, and 4.0 mg/mL, which were selected based on previously reported antimicrobial activity of the extract32. The extract-containing solution was then cast into silicone molds, and internal ionic crosslinking was initiated by adding CaCO3 and GDL. The mixtures were left to complete gelation for 16–24 h, followed by drying at 60 °C for 16–24 h. The dried films were sterilized under UV light for 15 min on each side and stored at room temperature in moisture-protected containers until use.

Physicochemical properties and characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

The internal structure of the alginate hydrogels was examined using SEM. After gelation, the samples were frozen and subsequently freeze-dried to preserve their porous architecture. The dried hydrogels were sectioned to expose the cross-section and subsequently coated with gold before imaging. SEM observations were performed using a Quanta 400 FEG instrument (FEI, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. The pore size of the hydrogels was quantified from SEM micrographs using ImageJ software (version 1.54f, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and presented as a histogram to illustrate the overall pore size distribution.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode was used to characterize the samples. Dried hydrogel films were placed directly on the ATR crystal, and spectra were acquired in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 using an INVENIO S FTIR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). The obtained spectra were analyzed to identify characteristic absorption bands corresponding to functional groups.

Mechanical tests

A universal testing machine (Shimadzu AGX-100kNV2, Japan) was used to evaluate the tensile properties of the hydrogel films, following the ASTM D882 standard with slight modifications. Hydrogel films of varying formulations were cut into strip specimens with dimensions of 30 × 90 mm2. The thickness of each specimen was measured using a vernier caliper (Mitutoyo, CD-8″BS, 0.01 mm accuracy) prior to testing. Specimens with a gauge length of 50 mm were stretched at a stretching rate of 500 mm/min at room temperature until failure. Thickness, tensile strength, elongation at break, and Young’s modulus were determined and expressed as mean ± standard deviation from three replicates per formulation.

Water vapor transmission rate (WVTR)

The WVTR of the films was measured based on ASTM E96 with minor modifications. Hydrogel film samples were cut into circular discs and mounted over the openings of glass bottles (13.5 mm diameter). Each bottle contained 10 mL of distilled water and was sealed around the edges with parafilm to prevent leakage. The open and closed systems were also included as controls, in which bottles were left uncovered (open system) and fully sealed with parafilm to prevent vapor transmission (closed system), respectively. The initial weight of each bottle was recorded before placing them in an incubator maintained at 37 °C. Following 24 h of incubation, the bottles were reweighed. Determination of tannin content

The tannin content of Q. infectoria gall extract was determined according to the AOAC Official Method 952.0334. Briefly, the sample was reacted with Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and sodium carbonate solution, incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. A calibration curve was prepared using tannic acid standards, and the tannin content of the samples was expressed as a percentage of tannic acid equivalents (% TAE, w/w).

Evaluation of antioxidant activity

The aliquots obtained during the in vitro release study were subjected to antioxidant activity assessment. Antioxidant capacity was evaluated using DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays, following established protocols15. Briefly, for the DPPH assay, 20 µL of sample was mixed with 180 µL of 0.2 mM DPPH solution, incubated in the dark for 40 min, and the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. For the ABTS assay, 20 µL of sample was mixed with 180 µL of the ABTS·+ working solution, incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature, and the absorbance was measured at 734 nm. For the FRAP assay, 20 µL of sample was added to 180 µL of FRAP reagent and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. The absorbance was then measured at 593 nm. For all assays, antioxidant capacity was determined using a Trolox calibration curve and expressed as µM Trolox equivalents.

Biocompatibility evaluation

The cytocompatibility of the hydrogels was assessed using an indirect method in accordance with the ISO 10993–5:2009 guidelines for the biological evaluation of medical devices. The extraction medium of each sample was prepared following the protocol previously described35. Anti-inflammatory test

The anti-inflammatory activity of the Q. infectoria extract-loaded alginate hydrogel film was evaluated using RAW 264.7 murine macrophages (ATCC® TIB-71™, Manassas, VA, USA; CVCL_0493) following the previously described protocol36. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 7.5 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To evaluate nitric oxide (NO) production, cells were treated with LPS (100 ng/mL) in the presence or absence of conditioned medium from the hydrogel films for 24 h. Indomethacin (50 µg/mL) was included as a reference anti-inflammatory drug control. After incubation, the culture supernatant was mixed with Griess reagent containing 1% sulfanilamide and 0.1% N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine dihydrochloride. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene (Actb), and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method38.

Antibacterial assay

Agar well diffusion assay (zone of inhibition)

Antibacterial activity was evaluated using an agar diffusion method adapted from CLSI guidelines for antimicrobial disk susceptibility testing (CLSI M02). Briefly, overnight cultures of Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard (~ 1 × 108 CFU/mL) and uniformly spread onto MHA plates. Sterile hydrogel film discs (6 mm in diameter) were prepared by UV irradiation for 15 min on each side. The discs were then placed on the surface of the inoculated agar. A commercial silver alginate wound dressing was used as a reference control. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and antibacterial activity was assessed by measuring the diameter of the clear inhibition zones (mm) surrounding each sample.

Time-kill kinetics assay

The bactericidal kinetics of alginate–QIG hydrogel films were determined using a time-kill assay adapted from ASTM E2149-13a39. Briefly, overnight bacterial cultures, including Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 and Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212) and Gram-negative bacteria (Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 19606, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700603, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853), were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard and subsequently diluted in MHB to obtain a final bacterial concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Sterile hydrogel film samples (0.05 g each) were placed in 24-well plates and incubated with 1 mL of the bacterial suspension at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals (0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h), aliquots were collected, serially diluted, and plated on tryptic soy agar using the drop plate method. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the resulting colonies were counted to determine the number of viable bacteria. The control group consisted of bacterial suspensions incubated under the same conditions without the film. All assays were conducted in triplicate, and mean bacterial counts (log CFU/mL) were plotted against incubation time.

Accelerated stability testing

In accelerated stability testing, QIG extract-loaded hydrogel films were subjected to high-stress conditions to predict their performance and stability. The stress conditions included (i) autoclaving (121 °C, 15 psi, 20 min) and (ii) freeze–thaw cycling (− 20 °C for 12 h followed by 60 °C for 12 h, repeated for six cycles). These conditions were selected to mimic thermal and mechanical stresses that may occur during processing, handling, or storage. After these treatments, the films were visually inspected for changes in physical appearance, such as color and transparency, to assess the stability of the incorporated bioactive compounds. In addition, their biological stability was evaluated through antibacterial activity testing, as described in the “Agar well diffusion assay” section. The performance of the stress-treated films was subsequently compared with that of freshly prepared films.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate and repeated independently at least three times. Results are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical evaluation was carried out using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple-range test was applied to determine significant differences among groups. Distinct letters assigned to datasets denote statistically significant differences at p < 0.05.
