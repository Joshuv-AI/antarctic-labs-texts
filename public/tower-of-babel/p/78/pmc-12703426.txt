Bio‐Based Fluorescent Epoxies: Emission Control through Molecular Packing and Phase Separation
Authors: Siddhant Bhardwaj, Andrew Becker, Dhananjay Dileep, Demetrius Finley, Jared Kaczor, Samuel Chambers, Gautham Pullela, Violet Krider, Michael Forrester, George Kraus, Eric W. Cochran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study demonstrates the sustainable conversion of agricultural waste into high‐performance fluorescent epoxy resins through direct blending of coumaric acid epoxy (CE) and ferulic acid epoxy (FE) derived from corn stover. These bio‐based resins exhibit tunable photoluminescence, with emission maxima shifting from 299 nm to 841 nm as FE content increases from 0 to 33 wt%. In contrast, blends based on commercial bisphenol A diglycidyl ether (BADGE) reach emission saturation around 550 nm and exhibit phase separation at higher FE concentrations, as confirmed by light microscopy. The synthesized CE–FE blends achieve superior or comparable thermomechanical properties, including flexural moduli (3.2–3.4 GPa), glass transition temperatures, and storage moduli, relative to commercial BADGE‐based resins. Impact toughness is consistent across all blends except for BADGE containing 33 wt% FE, which decreases significantly from 63 J m−1 (neat BADGE) to 12 J m−1 due to phase separation. Enhanced fluorescence intensity in CE‐rich blends is attributed to their compact aromatic structures, limiting free volume and nonradiative decay. Unlike existing fluorescent epoxy systems requiring complex synthesis or additives, this straightforward blending approach provides a sustainable, high‐performance alternative ideal for practical integration into biorefineries, with promising applications in optical sensors, bioimaging materials, and smart coatings.

Introduction

Corn stover constitutes around 27.2% of global agricultural waste, making it a highly abundant biomass resource.[ 1 ] The United States leads in its production, with annual outputs projected to reach nearly 364 million tons by 2040.[ 1 , 2 ] Currently, bioethanol production is a primary use for corn stover, potentially yielding between 23 and 53 million tons of ethanol annually. Despite its abundance, bioethanol production from corn stover faces challenges, particularly the high‐energy demands and costs associated with pretreatment processes.[ 3 , 4 ] Pretreatment, necessary for breaking down lignin and releasing cellulose, accounts for about 16–19% of biorefinery capital expenses.[ 5 ] Furthermore, lignin significantly restricts enzymatic hydrolysis, while enzymes constitute nearly 70% of total production costs.[ 6 ] The concentrations of hydroxycinnamic acids (HCAs), particularly coumaric and ferulic acids, vary significantly depending on the biomass source.[ 7 , 8 ] For instance, corn stover exhibits substantially higher concentrations of coumaric and ferulic acids compared to other lignocellulosic feedstocks such as switchgrass, wheat straw, and bagasse.[ 7 ] Consequently, understanding how the ratios of these acids influence resin properties is necessary to optimize their application in bio‐based epoxy formulations derived from diverse biomass sources.

An attractive solution to enhance the economics of biorefineries is the extraction of valuable HCAs, primarily coumaric and ferulic acid, during pretreatment. Recent studies, such as that conducted by Brown et al., have shown that mild‐alkaline pretreatment can effectively reduce lignin content from ≈17 wt% to 5 wt%, thereby facilitating the extraction of these valuable compounds. Technoeconomic assessments suggest that integrating HCA extraction into bioethanol production could significantly lower ethanol's minimum selling price by ≈45% compared to conventional methods.[ 9 ] Corn stover is particularly promising for HCA extraction, with coumaric acid (CA) concentrations reaching 38.7 mg g−1 and ferulic acid (FA) concentrations between 4.5 and 7.5 mg g−1.[ 7 ] These concentrations of coumaric and ferulic acids are reported to be highest for corn stover biomass when compared with other grass‐based biomass sources such as sorghum, switchgrass, big bluestem, etc.[ 7 ]

HCAs are versatile chemicals utilized in industries ranging from food additives and pharmaceuticals to sunscreens and bio‐based polymer precursors.[ 10 , 11 , 12 ] Specifically, coumaric‐ and ferulic‐acids‐derived epoxy resins are emerging as sustainable alternatives to petroleum‐based resins, addressing environmental and health concerns associated with traditional bisphenol A (BPA)‐based materials. Bio‐based epoxy precursors have previously been derived from sources like vanillin,[ 13 ] guaiacol, furan,[ 14 ] eugenol,[ 15 ] rosin,[ 16 ] and isosorbides.[ 17 ] However, these precursors typically lack sufficient reactive sites for adequate curing. Recent research has thus focused on developing multifunctional bio‐based epoxy precursors with enhanced properties such as flame retardancy, biodegradability, antimicrobial activity, and recyclability.[ 18 , 19 , 20 ] Examples include flame‐retardant epoxy resins synthesized from silicon‐bridged diepoxies derived from eugenol and self‐healing triepoxy vitrimer precursors developed by Zhang et al. from vanillin and guaiacol.[ 21 , 22 ] Nevertheless, current methods for extracting and functionalizing these precursors remain complex, costly, and energy‐intensive, creating obstacles for practical implementation within biorefineries.

The present study addresses these challenges by focusing on coumaric and ferulic acids from corn stover, which naturally contain multiple reactive sites. These reactive sites make coumaric and ferulic acids ideal for directly synthesizing epoxy‐functional resins without additional complicated modification steps. Recent studies by Song et al. and Ye et al. have confirmed the feasibility of producing high‐performance thermosets directly from pure coumaric and ferulic acids.[ 23 , 24 ] However, the economic integration of these processes into existing bioethanol plants remains challenging due to the substantial cost and energy required to isolate and purify coumaric and ferulic acids from biomass mixtures.

While most existing research emphasizes properties such as flame retardancy and recyclability, fluorescent bio‐based epoxies represent advanced materials with unique benefits and a wide range of potential applications. Fluorescent epoxy resins combine mechanical durability with distinctive optical properties, making them particularly valuable for real‐time structural health monitoring, chemical sensing, anticounterfeiting, and smart coatings capable of visually indicating corrosion or mechanical damage.[ 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 ] Typically, fluorophores such as coumarin 6,[ 27 ] lumogen red F350,[ 27 ] rhodamine derivatives,[ 28 ] or additives like 9‐anthracenemethoxyl glycidyl ether[ 29 ] have been physically incorporated into epoxy resins to achieve fluorescence. However, incorporating these additives can weaken thermomechanical properties, pose environmental risks, and complicate manufacturing processes. Thus, developing inherently fluorescent bio‐based epoxy systems—particularly from readily available and renewable biomass sources like coumaric and ferulic acids—offers significant advantages in sustainability, process simplification, and material multifunctionality.

Therefore, bio‐derived precursors such as vanillin, coumarin, and cardanol have been chemically modified to produce fluorescent epoxy monomers. For instance, vanillin was modified into an aldehyde‐epoxy monomer and crosslinked with long‐chain thiols to create recyclable, fluorescent epoxy resins.[ 30 ] Another approach involved creating fluorescent bio‐based epoxy resins from multiarm epoxy monomers derived from cardanol and cured with thiols.[ 31 ] Despite these developments, synthesizing such fluorescent monomers involves complex, multistep processes, limiting the scalability and practical applicability of these epoxy resins (Figure 1 ).

A) Previously reported methods involve multiple modifications of bio‐based precursors to add more reactive sites for more epoxy groups, and further modification steps are involved to develop fluorescent bio‐based epoxy. B) This work reports one‐pot synthesis of fluorescent bio‐based epoxy resins without any pigment or additive.

This study addresses these limitations by directly utilizing coumaric and ferulic acids mixtures obtained during corn stover pretreatment to produce fluorescent epoxy resins. This method avoids costly purification and functionalization steps entirely (Figure 1). Here, we explore how different ratios of coumaric and ferulic acids affect the fluorescence characteristics, thermomechanical properties, and curing kinetics of the resulting bio‐based epoxy resins. Additionally, we demonstrate how ferulic acid interacts with bisphenol A diglycidyl ether (BADGE) and CA, influencing the glass transition temperature and photoluminescence properties. To the best of our knowledge, investigating blends of (CA) and (FA) for high‐performance fluorescent bio‐epoxies without any additives/dyes/pigments hasn't been reported before. This work highlights the potential of these materials as sustainable, easily scalable alternatives to traditional BPA‐based resins, capable of integrating directly into existing bioethanol biorefinery processes.

Curing Kinetics and Thermal Analysis

Differential scanning calroimetry (DSC)‐based curing kinetics of CE blends were similar to BADGE, coumaric epoxy and coumaric‐BADGE blend with similar peak temperatures (T p) (Figure S3 and Table S1, Supporting Information). FE blends were not considered for DSC curing kinetics due to their miscibility issues. The endothermic peaks of the three blend mixtures fall in a similar temperature range from 60 to 1000 °C. The Kissinger model was used to fit the curing kinetics to calculate the activation energy. (1) ln(βTp2)=ln(AREa)−EaRTp where β is the heating rate, T p is the peak temperature, A is the pre‐exponential factor, E a is the activation energy, and R is the gas constant.

Activation energies are mentioned in Figure 2B. The curing kinetics for all of the blends and BADGE were similar, and the activation energies for CE blends and commercial BADGE were comparable to each other in the range of 66 kJ mol−1 to 62 kJ mol−1, which aligns with the previously reported values.[ 24 ] This suggests that the CE are similar to BADGE, which is good from a commercialization perspective.

A) TGA of the bio‐epoxy blends. B) Kissinger plot for calculating activation energies of the curing of coumaric, BADGE blends. C) Flexural modulus plot of the bio‐epoxy blends. D) Impact toughness plot of the bio‐epoxy blends.

The activation energies were also determined using the Flynn–Wall–Ozawa method, as described in Appendix X2 of ASTM E698‐18.[ 32 ] The values obtained were in close agreement with those from the Kissinger method. The fitted curve for the Flynn–Wall–Ozawa analysis, along with the calculated activation energies, is provided in Figure S4, Supporting Information.

Several blends of BADGE, coumaric, and ferulic were prepared as summarized in fgr:table that were characterized for their thermal properties through thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Figure 2A). The decomposition temperature at 5 wt% and 30 wt% mass loss was lower for coumaric (287 °C) than BADGE (347 °C) that can be attributed to the labile double bond present outside of the coumaric aromatic ring that can be cleaved into other small molecules, as also confirmed in previous studies.[ 23 , 33 ] However, the decomposition temperature (T d,5) decreased when FE loading was increased from 0 to 33 wt% for both BADGE and coumaric blends; this can be attributed to the labile double bond present in FE.[ 23 , 33 ] However, the char residue was highest for all of the coumaric blends was higher than BADGE blends, highest noted for 2C1F (29.5%) and increased with FE loading. The robust char residue is attributed to the higher crosslinking density introduced with FE addition. Nonetheless, the coumaric blends showed comparable thermal stability with BADGE with much higher char residue, which might reflect the greater flame retardancy of coumaric blends.

Mechanical Properties

The flexural moduli for all epoxy–FE blends fall within a narrow range of ≈3.2–3.4 GPa, with minimal variation across the different compositions. On a side note, 50 wt% ferulic acid blend with BADGE (1E1F) was tested for this testing, and this 1E1F was not appropriate for other testing. Blends containing higher proportions of CE (3C1F, C100, and 1C1E) tend to exhibit slightly higher moduli, while BADGE‐rich blends (3E1F, 2E1F, and 1E1F) trend toward the lower end. However, considerable overlap in the interquartile ranges and whiskers suggests these subtle differences lie within experimental variability. This observation indicates that changes in crosslink density or FE dispersion have limited influence on stiffness.

These blended epoxies were then tested for their impact strength. The impact testing showed the highest toughness for C100, which is higher than the BADGE and other blends with varying compositions of FE. It is interesting to note that the 3E1F was the most brittle with the lowest impact strength. Moreover, samples 2E1F and 2C1F (33 wt% FE loading) were extremely brittle for the impact testing. It is also noteworthy that on the addition of FE, the impact strength reduced to some extent that suggests the addition of FE makes the blends more brittle. This hints at the possible phase separation of FE in BADGE and CE blends, which aligns with our previous observation of the immiscibility issues of FE with BADGE during blend mixing.

The phase separation created microstructures with varied stress‐concentration effects in the polymer matrix, thereby increasing the brittleness. Similar results have also been reported in previous studies showing that the formation of microstructure domains increases the brittleness, which aligns with our observation.[ 34 ]

Dynamic Mechanical Analysis

Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) was performed on the BADGE and CE blends to evaluate stiffness across different FE loadings. The mechanical properties of these blends were also compared against neat BADGE. The DMA results (Figure 3A) indicated that all blends of CE and FE exhibited higher storage moduli than BADGE. Notably, blend 2E1F showed the highest storage modulus of 1400 MPa, compared to 550 MPa for BADGE at 20 °C. This increase can be explained by the rigid packing of BADGE's phenyl groups with FE at lower temperatures. As the temperature increased, the polymer chains gained more thermal energy, which enhanced their mobility and promoted microphase separation, leading to a sharp drop in storage modulus. A similar pattern was seen for 2C1F, which had a higher modulus than C100 at lower temperatures but then declined quickly with heating, also suggesting phase separation. These findings are consistent with the impact tests, where both 2E1F and 2C1F were very brittle, likely because of microphase separation.

A) storage modulus of the bio‐epoxy blends. B) tanδ curve of the bio‐epoxy blends showing glass transition temperature.

The other blends (3C1F, 3E1F, and 1C1E) exhibited storage moduli within a narrow range of 900–1100 MPa. These results suggest that blending FE and CE with BADGE and each other leads to increased rigidity of the polymer chains. Specifically, the addition of 33 wt% FE more than doubled the storage modulus compared to pure BADGE. Compared to BADGE, the compact aromatic structures of FE and CE allow polymer chains to pack more closely, enhancing overall rigidity.

Typically, more rigid polymers show higher glass transition temperatures (T g); however, an inverse trend was observed in these blends. As shown in Figure 3B, the tanδ peaks represent mechanical glass transition temperatures. Blend 2E1F, despite having the highest storage modulus, exhibited a lower T g than C100 and other blends. Moreover, the T g values for BADGE, 2E1F, and 3E1F were similar, indicating that adding FE had little influence on T g. The observed lower T g in 2E1F despite high storage modulus could be explained by the presence of bulky phenyl rings in BADGE, which increase free volume, while aromaticity simultaneously enhances rigidity. This balance maintains the T g similar to BADGE but significantly increases the storage modulus.

For CE blends, T g increased with FE loading up to 25 wt% (3C1F) reaching 97 °C, but decreased to 88 °C when FE loading increased to 33 wt% (2C1F). This behavior is hypothesized to result from phase separation at higher FE concentrations, forming aggregated FE clusters. These clusters introduce network heterogeneity, disrupting nearby polymer chains and lowering the T g. This phase separation phenomenon and formation of aggregated nanostructures align with previous findings by Raghuwanshi et al. who reported that ferulic acid at concentrations above 20 wt% in PLA formed aggregated nanostructures.[ 34 ] At 20 wt%, interactions among ferulic acid molecules were limited, but at 40 wt%, these interactions intensified, leading to nanoscale aggregated domains.[ 34 ] Similarly, increasing FE concentration from 25% to 33% likely leads to phase separation, with the resulting aggregates acting as plasticizers, thus reducing T g.

CE and FE blends exhibited improved storage moduli compared to pure BADGE, highlighting their potential for commercial bio‐based epoxy applications. Additionally, various blends could be formulated with BADGE to increase biocontent within existing BADGE manufacturing processes without compromising performance.

Fluorescence Emission

As shown earlier, CE–FE blends exhibit thermomechanical properties comparable to or exceeding those of BADGE, offering a promising alternative for industrial applications where performance and sustainability are both critical. In addition to these mechanical advantages, these materials display inherent photoluminescence, broadening their potential utility in functional materials design.

This behavior is illustrated by the emission spectra in Figure 4 . Neat BADGE showed no detectable fluorescence. This lies within our expectations due to the two phenolics being connected with a bulky isopropylidene group, aggrandizing free volume, disrupting planarity, and it not being configured in the donor–acceptor relation suited for fluorescence. However, the introduction of FE led to a marked increase in fluorescence emission. Notably, the 2E1F and 3E1F blends exhibited nearly identical fluorescence intensities, suggesting a saturation in optical enhancement most likely arising due to aggregation‐caused quenching despite strong intrinsic fluorescence that is further discussed in Section 2.5.

Fluorescence emission spectra demonstrating the increase in emission with FE loading.

Fluorescence intensity in CE‐based blends followed a more progressive trend. CE matrix creates a tightly packed network upon curing, minimizing nonradiative decay pathways, often dissipating as heat. This aligns with the study that demonstrated 2‐phenylidene (rigid) had higher quantum yield values 0.78 than stilbene (less rigid) with quantum yield of 0.04.[ 35 ] Increasing the proportion of FE led to stronger emissions, with the 2C1F blend (33% FE) showing the highest intensity. The 3C1F formulation exhibited an intermediate response, falling between 2C1F and the CE baseline. These results indicate that FE concentration and dispersion play an integral role in tuning photoluminescent behavior.

It can also be inferred that emission is not driven by FE's native fluorescence, which is weak on its own, but instead emerges when FE is incorporated into the CE network that forms an intermolecular charge transfer (ICT). This reasoning is supported by the literature that showed the neat cinnamic acid to be nonflorescent but fluorescent in its ester form.[ 36 ] Another study showed coumarin carboxylic acid derivatives with lower quantum yield when compared to its conjugated form with peptides.[ 37 ]

Therefore, the emission in CE–FE blend system can be attributed to the donor–acceptor arrangement that is conducive to promoting ICT, fortuitous to the ferulic epoxy.[ 38 ] This is also supported by clusteroluminescence,[ 30 ] a phenomenon observed to be natural to the aggregation (at both molecular and macromolecular length scales) of chromatophoric substructures (electron‐rich atoms such as oxygen on aromatic rings), extending conjugation and inducing fluorescence through spatial interactions. Curing of FE/coumaric epoxies leads to ring opening, generating additional hydroxyl groups contributing to fluorescence intensity. Further theoretical and experimental studies by Ghigo et al. on ferulic acid illustrate multiple excitation pathways with S0→S1 excitation between HOMO (hydroxy/methoxy donor) orbitals and LUMO (pi orbitals) of the carboxy vinyl moiety being primarily responsible for fluorescence.[ 39 ] The paper also elaborates on excitation to higher states S4 and S5, which decay nonradiatively to S1 before fluorescing.

Importantly, this functionality is achieved without adding extrinsic fluorophores or postsynthetic modification. The ability to generate tunable emission directly from bio‐derived monomers simplifies processing while retaining a fully renewable material profile. This combination of performance and functionality positions CE–FE systems for applications that extend beyond traditional structural uses, such as innovative coatings,[ 25 ] optical sensors,[ 40 ] or solar collectors[ 27 ] requiring both material sustainability and added responsiveness.

Light Microscope Analysis of Epoxy Blends

Light microscopy imaging was performed to assess FE distribution within the polymer matrices and its correlation with fluorescence intensity to further understand differences in fluorescence emission observed between coumaric and BADGE epoxy blends. Images in Figure 5A,B demonstrate that fluorescence visibly increases with higher FE loading, consistent with the fluorescence emission spectra.

A) BADGE blends showing phase separation 2E1F composition and fluorescent in CFP wavlength light. B) Images of CE blends fluorescent in DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole) wavelength light with no observed phase separation.

Interestingly, BADGE and coumaric blends exhibited fluorescence under cyan fluorescent protein (CFP) and 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) filters of 436 nm and 365 nm wavelength respectively after FE was incorporated into the polymer matrices. Differences in fluorescence behavior under these excitation wavelengths are attributed to distinct structural arrangements of FE molecules within each epoxy type. BADGE epoxy contains two bulky phenyl groups, acting as spacers, thereby increasing the distance between FE molecules. Conversely, C100 possesses a compact aromatic structure, resulting in tighter molecular packing and enhanced fluorescence emission within the DAPI range.

An especially notable feature from the microscopy images is the presence of luminescent violet clusters in the 3E1F blend (25 wt% FE). These clusters exhibit fluorescence predominantly under DAPI excitation, indicating FE phase separation within the less densely packed BADGE epoxy matrix. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images further supported the phase separation in 2E1F as evidenced by bright clusters (see Figure S5, Supporting Information) that were not present in BADGE and 3E1F. The relatively looser arrangement in BADGE permits greater polymer chain mobility, allowing FE molecules to aggregate and emit light at distinct wavelengths. Notably, such clusters were absent in the 2E1F blend (33 wt% FE), suggesting that phase separation is concentration‐dependent, a finding consistent with previous literature reports.[ 34 ] This also explains why phase separation does not occur in CE blends due to their inherently tighter molecular arrangement.

Additionally, the pronounced brittleness observed during impact testing of the 3E1F blend (Figure 2D) aligns with the presence of these aggregated FE clusters. Restricted molecular motion within the clusters contrasts sharply with more mobile polymer regions outside the aggregates, creating nonuniform stiffness zones and contributing significantly to increased brittleness.

Intercorrelation of Fluorescence and Glass Transition

When comparing the tanδ plot (Figure 3B) with fluorescence emission spectra (Figure 4), there is a relation of glass transition temperature with the emission intensity of CE and BADGE blends. This observation presents a rationale for the significantly high fluorescence emission intensity observed for CE blends versus BADGE blends, even when both contain the same loading of FE. In Figure 3B, the glass transition temperature of CE blends is higher than BADGE blends. The elevated T g value suggests that CE blends have comparatively less free volume than BADGE blends, mainly because of the compactness of aromatic rings of CE and FE when compared with BADGE blends. This leads to the more segmental, vibrational, and rotational chain movements in BADGE blends. Therefore, when the BADGE blends are excited by a light source, the energy gained is spent through nonradiative channel decays through rotational and vibrational energy losses, reducing the emission intensity. On the contrary, the CE blends have restricted chain movements and more packed structures that minimize nonradiative decay and maximize emission intensity.

The effect of temperature and free volume on fluorescence intensity has been studied previously by Priestley et al. and Kim et al.[ 41 , 42 ] With increasing temperature, the fluorescence intensity decreases, and this variation can be used to estimate the glass transition temperature. Figure 6B provides a schematic illustration of the fluorescence intensity shifts during the transition and is included to explain the concept rather than to present experimental data. Such dependency of fluorescence and temperature can be due to the molecules or chains gain high energy and increase in the vibrational/rotational relaxations that lead to the decrease in the fluorescence emission through nonradioactive decay channels. Also, exceeding the glass transition temperature, the chains’ movements are less restricted, leading to more free volume and thereby more collisions and energy dissipation through relaxation movements and reduction in fluorescence intensity. This dependency of fluorescence with temperature and glassy‐rubber polymer matrix reported in these studies aligns with our explanation of BADGE and CE blends mentioned in the previous paragraph.

A) Correlation of fluorescence emission, glass transition temperature, and blend composition. B) Illustrative figure of the effect of fluorescence emission with glass transition temperature.

Among the CE blends, the 2C1F composition showed the highest fluorescence intensity, despite having a lower glass transition temperature (T g) than the 3C1F blend (Figure 6A). This inverse relationship suggests that fluorescence emission in this system is influenced by more than just bulk rigidity. In 2C1F, a 33 wt% loading of FE introduces a high concentration of chromophores that remain uniformly dispersed throughout the coumaric network. This dispersion minimizes aggregation and enables efficient donor–acceptor charge transfer between ferulic and coumaric units. The FE molecules form locally dense, viscous regions that reduce nonradiative decay while promoting planar conformations favorable for radiative emission. Although these identical substituents introduce additional free volume—loosening the overall network and contributing to the reduced T g —they also increase the number of reactive sites and facilitate chromophore mobility at the microscopic level. The result is a formulation where enhanced emission arises from a well‐balanced combination of molecular packing, local viscosity, and chromophore density, rather than increased macroscopic stiffness.

Conclusions

Fluorescent epoxy resins were developed by blending (CE and FE), derived from corn stover, without using additives, dyes, or chemical modifications. Tunable fluorescence emission was achieved by varying the CE/FE ratio, with emission maxima shifting from 299 nm to 841 nm as FE loading increased from 0 to 33 wt%. Phase separation of FE was observed only in BADGE blends at higher FE loadings, correlating with a notable drop in impact toughness to 12 J m−1. In contrast, CE‐based systems maintained uniform dispersion. Thermomechanical properties—including flexural modulus, glass transition temperature, storage modulus, and impact toughness—were found to be broadly comparable to those of conventional BADGE resins across most compositions, with flexural moduli observed within a narrow range of 3.2–3.4 GPa. The influence of molecular packing on emission intensity was also identified; CE blends, due to their compact aromatic structures, exhibited reduced free volume and nonradiative decay, leading to stronger fluorescence than BADGE‐based systems at similar FE content.

Additionally, it was demonstrated that CE and FE can be incorporated into BADGE in small proportions to enhance fluorescence and maintain mechanical performance. This compatibility with BADGE enables seamless integration of bio‐based content into existing epoxy manufacturing infrastructure, improving material functionality while maximizing cost efficiency. CE–FE blends’ curing kinetics and activation energies closely matched those of BADGE, further supporting their industrial viability. These fluorescent bioepoxies present opportunities for application in innovative coatings, chemical sensors, anticounterfeit packaging, and optical devices, where mechanical durability and intrinsic fluorescence are desired. Overall, a simple blending approach delivered structural performance and optical functionality, offering a scalable path transforming agricultural waste into sustainable epoxy systems with advanced capabilities.

Experimental Section

Materials

BADGE, Miller‐Stephenson EPON Resin 828 with an epoxy equivalent weight of 190, was purchased from Ellsworth adhesives. 99% diethanolamine (DEA) was purchased from Acros Organics. Coumaric and ferulic acid with epoxy equivalent weight of 138 gm mol−1 and 153 gm mol−1, respectively, were purchased from AmBeed, and epichlorohydrin and tetrabutylammonium bromide were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich.

General Method of Coumaric & Ferulic Epoxy Synthesis

Fifty grams of coumaric acid was added to a dry round‐bottom flask with a magnetic stir bar, followed by 475.5 mL of epichlorohydrin. Next, 9.82 g of tetrabutylammonium bromide was added. The solution was refluxed at 8 °C for 3 h. The solution was cooled to 0 °C. After cooling, 240 mL of a 20 wt% solution of sodium hydroxide was added to the solution along with another 9.82 g of tetrabutylammonium bromide. The solution was then stirred at 0 °C for 1 h. The solution was placed in a separatory funnel and the aqueous phase was separated. To the organic phase was added 200 g of chloroform. The solution was washed three times with deionized water (250 mL) and brine (250 mL). The dichloromethane was removed by rotary evaporation. Toluene was added to the solution (150 mL) and removed by rotary evaporation. The solution was then dissolved in dichloromethane and run through a pad of dried silica. The dichloromethane was then removed by rotary evaporation, and the solution was placed under high vacuum at 6 °C overnight. The yield over different runs of these reactions ranged from 41% to 50%.

For ferulic acid, the process was similar, but after washing with brine, the solution was dried with sodium sulfate. The excess epichlorohydrin was removed by rotary evaporation, and then the product was recrystallized with methanol and hexane 1:1 and obtained a 60% yield.

Curing of Coumaric and Ferulic Epoxy Blends

100 parts by weight (pbw) BADGE was mixed with 12 pbw DEA, which corresponds to a 1:0.31 molar epoxy:DEA ratio and was used for all blends. The epoxies were heated to 60 °C under vacuum for one hour and then mixed with the proper molar amount of DEA. The mixed samples were held at 60 °C for an additional hour to degas, and then poured into 128 × 12.7 × 5 mm molds. After an additional hour at 60 °C under vacuum to degas, the vacuum was released and the samples were cured at 71 °C for 24 h. Next, the samples were wet sanded to remove air voids to a final 1000 grit surface finish. The samples varied in thickness between 2.5 and 5.75 mm. After the curing, DEA had <1% insoluble components not contributing to the crosslinking in the final cured material.

Curing Kinetics of Coumaric and BADGE Blends

The curing kinetics were analyzed with a Thermal Analysis Discovery 2500 DSC in a nitrogen environment. CE and BADGE were dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 50 °C. Three samples of BADGE and coumaric acid (C100) were cured with DEA for kinetics in the ratios 100:12 pbw (BADGE:DEA), 100:15 pbw (C100:DEA), and a 50:50 ratio by weight of BADGE/DEA and coumaric/DEA (1C1E). The blends were cured from 5 °C to 200 °C at four different heating ramp rates (5 °C min−1, 10 °C min−1, 15 °C min−1, and 20 °C min−1).

Thermomechanical Properties Characterization: Flexural Testing

Universal Testing Machine Liyi LY‐1066 A was used to measure flexural properties in a three‐point bend test configuration with a 100 kg load cell. The samples were flexurally tested to a maximum of 5% flexural strain. The testing span was set to 16 times the thickness of each sample. The testing rate was 0.01 mm mm min−1.

Thermomechanical Properties Characterization: Impact Testing

After flexural testing, nonshattered samples were cut in half to make two IZOD samples, notched, and tested with a LiYi‐LY‐KJLD5.5 pendulum impact tester set at 1.0 J pendulum energy and 3.5 m s−1 impact speed.

Thermomechanical Properties Characterization: DMA Testing

TA Instruments Ares G2 rheometer was used for the DMA testing for all the bioepoxy blends. The measurements were done on a torsion fixture in oscillation temperature ramp testing mode. Temperature was heated at a rate 5 °C min−1 from 25 °C to 120 °C with a strain% of 0.1% at an angular frequency of 1 rad s−1.

Thermo‐Mechanical Properties Characterization: Thermogravimetric Analysis

TGA was run on a Netzsch STA 449 F1 Jupiter system with alumina pans. The system ramped from 40 °C to 650 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 under a nitrogen flow.

Thermo‐Mechanical Properties Characterization: Light Microscope Imaging

A Zeiss Axio Imager Z.2 microscope was used to image all the bioepoxy blends. The cured sample was placed on a slide under the Zeiss microscope and was analyzed for fluorescent in DAPI, CFP, and bright‐field filters with 10 × zoom. The DAPI filter excites at ≈365 nm and detects emission around 460 nm (blue region). CFP excites at ≈436 nm and detects emission near 480–490 nm (cyan region). Zen software was used for the image analysis. All samples were irradiated with same exposure time was kept for all the samples for comparative analysis.

Thermomechanical Properties Characterization: Fluorescence Spectroscopy

A Perkin Elmer LS 55 fluorescence spectrometer was used to measure the steady‐state emission for the blended bioepoxy samples. The samples were excited at 390 nm wavelength and irradiated from 405 nm to 700 nm wavelength range at 7 nm slit width with 300 nm mm−1 scan speed.

Fluorescence spectroscopy was carried out on the solid Izod bars of the epoxy blends with the dimensions 128 mm length, 12.8 mm width, and thickness varied from 2.5 to 5.75 mm.
