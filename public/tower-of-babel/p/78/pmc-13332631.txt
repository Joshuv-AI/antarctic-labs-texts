Intraprocedural Leaflet Dysfunction and the Importance of Device Planning in Contemporary Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Craig Basman, Vandan Upadhyaya, Ryan Kaple
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Pompei et al1 describe a patient with symptomatic severe aortic stenosis who underwent transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with a balloon-expandable transcatheter heart valve, followed by emergent valve-in-valve (ViV) redo TAVR due to intraprocedural frozen leaflet. Although uncommon, this case underscores the importance of preoperative planning, early recognition, and prompt therapy for acute valvular failure.

Frozen leaflet after TAVR is estimated to occur in approximately 0.1% to 0.3% of procedures, often resulting in acute hemodynamic deterioration and mortality.2 The etiology can be from extrinsic structures “pinning” the leaflet (eg, protruding native leaflet calcification), iatrogenic damage to the leaflet during preparation, or from prosthetic deformation/leaflet injury during deployment or post-dilation. Recognition may be challenging because frozen leaflet is not reliably diagnosed by fluoroscopy alone and typically requires transesophageal echocardiography. Several treatment strategies exist:1.Percutaneous manipulation (using catheters to “release” the leaflet)2.Balloon valvuloplasty—to reduce transcatheter deformation and restore leaflet mobility3.ViV (redo TAVR)4.Surgical replacement.

Recognition and Hemodynamic Stabilization

When hypotension follows TAVR deployment and severe aortic regurgitation is identified, frozen leaflet should be included in the differential. Repositioning the guidewire is the initial maneuver to see whether there is improvement, but if this does not improve hemodynamics, then immediate stabilization is the next step. Given that rapid pacing (approximately 80-100 beats/min) and pharmacologic support are often insufficient to stabilize acute aortic regurgitation, mechanical circulatory support may be necessary. Peripheral cannulation for venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) can rapidly stabilize the patient, though it increases left ventricular afterload burden in patients already struggling from left-sided overload due to aortic regurgitation. However, a brief VA-ECMO run to enable definitive therapy can be life-saving. Alternatively, the use of left atrial VA-ECMO is more time consuming but offers the advantage of mechanical decompression of the left heart for longer procedures.3 After stabilization, then accurately diagnosing the mechanism of aortic regurgitation is the next step. It can be difficult to differentiate paravalvular leak from frozen leaflet on fluoroscopy or transthoracic echocardiography, so transesophageal echocardiography is key for accurate diagnosis.

Treatment for Frozen Leaflet

The authors' initial use of catheter-based manipulation with a pigtail catheter is a reasonable conservative approach. In our experience, a stiffer, shaped guide catheter can be more effective for “unpinning” a transcatheter leaflet because the pigtail catheter may not generate enough force most of the time. Balloon post-dilation has been reported, but it carries the risk of further leaflet damage or prosthetic distortion; it is often the next option for refractory cases before redo TAVR. If these maneuvers fail, redo TAVR provides a definitive bailout. Importantly, not all patients are candidates for emergent redo TAVR because of the risk of coronary occlusion. Advanced computed tomography is therefore critical to evaluate coronary anatomy and anticipate ViV strategies in both the acute and long-term settings. For example, if the top of the transcatheter implant sits above the sinotubular junction, then sinus sequestration may be a risk. Hence, the same lifetime management planning performed at the index TAVR (eg, whether a patient would be a candidate for future redo TAVR) is equally essential for managing acute transcatheter heart valve failure.

Conclusions

Pompei et al1 demonstrate a thoughtful, stepwise approach to acute TAVR failure due to frozen leaflet. Their algorithm provides a practical framework for similar complications and reinforces the value of preprocedural computed tomography planning—not only for long-term management but also to enable prompt, definitive therapy when complications arise. This case exemplifies the proper response: when acute valvular failure occurs, do not remain “frozen”—stabilize the patient, identify the cause, and use preoperative imaging to guide timely intervention.
