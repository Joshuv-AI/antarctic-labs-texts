Considerations for emergency airway management and resuscitation in cancer patients: a review
Authors: Wei-Lin Tallie Chua, Arif Azam, Monica Wattana, Tareg Bey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer patients frequently present to the emergency department (ED) with malignancy or treatment-related critical illness, requiring airway intervention or resuscitation. Multiple factors such as anatomical distortion, treatment sequelae, frailty, and complex goals-of-care considerations create unique challenges. This narrative review aims to provide practical, evidence-informed guidance in airway management and resuscitation of oncologic patients.MethodsWe conducted a review of the literature addressing airway management, physiologic alterations associated with malignancy and cachexia, pharmacologic considerations for rapid sequence intubation, and resuscitation outcomes in cancer populations. We synthesized relevant clinical guidelines and studies to develop an overview of recommendations applicable to ED practice.ResultsCancer patients present numerous airway management challenges due to tumor or surgical airway alternations, post-radiation fibrosis, edema, and bleeding. Physiologic factors—including reduced functional residual capacity, anemia, muscle wasting, and systemic inflammation—shorten safe apnea time, increase risk of peri-intubation hypoxia, and hemodynamic instability. Malnutrition, organ dysfunction, and chemotherapy toxicity alter pharmacokinetics, necessitating individualized selection and dosing of induction and paralytic agents. Early anticipation of difficulties in airway management, optimized preoxygenation, advanced airway techniques, and multidisciplinary involvement are essential. Resuscitation planning must incorporate realistic prognostication and clear discussions regarding code status. Late or absent documentation of goals-of-care remains common.ConclusionsAirway management and resuscitation in cancer patients require a specialized, patient-centered approach. Future research should address comparative outcomes in the cancer population and support development of ED-specific oncologic protocols. Integration of structured goals-of-care frameworks and early palliative care help ensure ethically aligned, high-quality emergency care.Keywords: Resuscitation, Airway, Oncology, Intubation, Cancer

Background

Patients with cancer frequently present to emergency departments (EDs) with urgent conditions that are directly related to their malignancy or treatment. As the prevalence of cancer continues to rise globally, the volume of cancer-related ED visits has increased and is projected to grow further [1, 2]. This thus places demands on emergency physicians, who must rapidly manage a heterogeneous population with complex medical needs.

A significant proportion of cancer-related ED visits involve critically ill patients requiring immediate intervention. In a cross-sectional study of ED visits by cancer patients in the United States, 21.3% were classified as high-acuity, highlighting the need for timely, skilled resuscitation and airway management [3]. In these scenarios, delays or errors can have profound consequences for patient outcomes. Caring for critically ill cancer patients presents unique challenges that extend beyond the immediate technical demands of resuscitation. In addition to the technical skills, emergency physicians must also tailor interventions to the patient’s oncologic and physiologic context. The type, location, and stage of malignancy, and tumor-related complications can dramatically alter anatomy and physiology. Treatment-related factors—surgical modifications, radiation-induced tissue changes, or the systemic effects of chemotherapy or immunotherapy—may further complicate airway management and resuscitative efforts. A nuanced understanding of these variables is essential to optimize outcomes and minimize complications in this vulnerable population.

In addition, emergency physicians frequently confront complex prognostic and ethical decisions. Aggressive interventions may not always align with the patient’s goals of care, particularly in those with advanced disease. Therefore, developing a structured approaches to end-of-life discussions, do-not-resuscitate (DNR) orders, and patient-centered treatment preferences are essential. Effective communication and shared decision-making can ensure that interventions are appropriate, ethically justified, and aligned with the patient’s values.

We aim to provide practical guidance in managing the critically ill oncological patient in the ED. We will explore both clinical and technical considerations that are unique to this population and the ethical dimensions surrounding end-of-life discussions. By synthesizing current evidence and practical strategies, this review seeks to enhance emergency care in these patients while supporting informed, patient-centered decision-making.

Methods

A structured literature search was performed of English-language publications indexed in PubMed. Search strategies included combinations of relevant Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keyword terms, such as “oncology” and “emergency airway management,” as well as condition-specific terms including “mediastinal mass” and “airway management.” Additional targeted searches were conducted using related keywords to capture relevant literature not identified in the initial search. Articles were screened for relevance to emergency airway management in patients with cancer and selected based on clinical relevance, recency (limited to the past 23 years), and study type, with priority given to review articles and key primary studies when applicable. The reference lists of selected articles were also manually reviewed to identify additional pertinent publications.

Discussion

Indications for tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation

Many cancer patients in the ED are critically ill. Early recognition of those who will require advanced airway management, mechanical ventilation, and subsequent admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) is essential to optimize outcomes. Timely identification and intervention is paramount, as delayed intubation is associated with morbidity and mortality [4].

Airway compromise may result from tumor, mass effect, trauma, infections, or expanding hematomas involving the upper airway. Changes in mental status may impair airway protection and cause hypoventilation, thus necessitating prompt intervention. Such changes may arise from toxic-metabolic causes, including hepatic encephalopathy or opioid overdose, or from intracranial pathology such as hemorrhagic brain metastases and vasogenic edema.

Progressive pulmonary disease represents another indication for early airway management. Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, especially in the setting of lung cancer or metastases may require intubation with worsening respiratory status and hypercapnia. Hypoxemic respiratory failure and hypercapnic respiratory failure, may also result from pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome, pulmonary edema, significant malignant pleural effusions, or intrapulmonary hemorrhage.

Respiratory muscle weakness from neuromuscular conditions such as Guillain-Barré syndrome, myositis, or myasthenia gravis—or from exhaustion following prolonged hyperventilation—can rapidly compromise ventilation. Cancer patients receiving noninvasive ventilation (NIV) may have diminished physiological reserves and fatigue more quickly, increasing their risk of intubation.

Finally, significant hemodynamic instability, severe metabolic derangements–including acidosis, sepsis, electrolyte disturbances—and rapid fluid shifts, can further compromise respiratory function and should be factored into airway assessment [5].

Airway management considerations

Anatomical challenges

Airway management in cancer patients is often complicated by anatomical distortion caused by tumor growth, treatment effects, or prior surgery. Head and neck tumors can often significantly alter anatomy. Mass effect from either extrinsic compression or intraluminal obstruction may compromise patency and visualization.

Anterior mediastinal masses, in particular, present a unique airway challenge because sedation, neuromuscular blockade, and the supine position can exacerbate mass effect, resulting in complete and potentially fatal central airway obstruction. Compression of the trachea or main bronchi may worsen with loss of spontaneous ventilation or recumbency. General management principles include maintaining the patient in an upright position when tolerated, avoiding deep sedation and neuromuscular blockade whenever possible, preserving spontaneous ventilation, and optimizing hemodynamic stability [6]. In selected patients with severe airway compromise, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) implantation and ventilation prior to airway intervention has been described [7]. However, tracheal intubation alone may not relieve airway obstruction as the compression is often distal to the glottis. Definitive management may require bronchoscopic airway stenting before ECMO can be safely discontinued. Because these advanced resources are not universally available, early multidisciplinary consultation and expedited transfer to a tertiary center with ECMO and interventional bronchoscopy capabilities should be considered when clinically feasible.

Radiotherapy to the head and neck can result in trismus secondary to temporomandibular joint fibrosis, as well as upper airway edema, mucositis, and soft tissue fibrosis. These changes may lead to restricted cervical mobility, reduced mouth opening, diminished tissue compliance, and distorted airway anatomy, thereby complicating direct laryngoscopy and other airway maneuvers [8,9]. Fibrosis and anatomical distortion may also make emergency surgical airway access technically challenging. Consequently, patients with a history of head and neck radiotherapy have a higher likelihood of difficult airway management. Careful pre-intubation airway assessment and planning is therefore essential, and an awake flexible fiberoptic intubation strategy, should be strongly considered [10].

Surgical interventions such as laryngectomy, tracheostomy, or complex reconstructive flap procedures alter anatomic relationships of the upper airway and neck, frequently obscuring anatomical landmarks. These structural changes pose challenges across multiple stages of airway management. Bag-valve-mask (BVM) ventilation can be hindered by altered facial contours resulting from tumor bulk, scarring, or surgical resection, leading to poor mask seal [11, 12]. Placement of supraglottic airway devices may be difficult or impossible with oral or oropharyngeal distortion, postoperative scarring, edema, or active mucositis. These conditions narrow available space, obscure insertion landmarks, and increase trauma or bleeding risk during airway manipulation.

Direct laryngoscopy is often limited by restricted mouth opening, distorted pharyngeal anatomy, or immobile soft tissues. In such cases, video laryngoscopy or flexible fiberoptic intubation may provide superior visualization and improve the likelihood of successful intubation. Subglottic tumors or tracheal involvement can further compromise ventilation, even if airway access is achieved.

Finally, emergency cricothyroidotomy may be extremely difficult in patients with prior head and neck surgery or radiotherapy [13]. Fibrosis, scarring, and obliteration of tissue planes can obscure the cricothyroid membrane and render standard techniques ineffective. In these scenarios, early identification of at-risk patients and the consideration of alternative strategies—such as awake flexible fiberoptic or planned surgical tracheostomy—are critical to ensure patient safety.

Common airway surgical alterations

A significant proportion of patients with head and neck malignancies present with surgically altered airways, most commonly following tracheostomy or laryngectomy. Familiarity with the anatomic changes associated with these procedures, the potential complications, its maturity, and any associated pathology is essential to ensure safe and effective management in the emergency setting.

Early and late complications of tracheostomies

Accidental decannulation or tracheostomy change within the first postoperative week carries significant risk. Evidence suggests that early tube manipulation, particularly within the first six days after surgical creation of the tract, is associated with increased mortality, primarily due to false passage formation and loss of airway access. In contrast, a retrospective study found that first tube changes performed between days 7 and 9 were associated with significantly lower all-cause mortality [19].

In a large retrospective series of surgical tracheostomy patients the overall postoperative complication rate within 14 days was 9%. The most common complications were bleeding (34%), tracheitis (17%), and tube dislodgement (10%) [20]. These findings underscore the importance of vigilant airway assessment and familiarity with tracheostomy care principles.

Emergency airway management in patients with total laryngectomy requires particular attention as these patients have complete separation between the upper and lower airways. Oral or nasal intubation in such patients is futile; oxygenation and ventilation must occur exclusively through the neck stoma. Recognition and rapid adaptation of airway management strategies are critical to avoid delays.

Finally, although rare, airway fires remain a serious potential complication, particularly during procedures involving high-flow oxygen and the use of heat-generating devices such as defibrillators or fiberoptic light sources. While most reported in operative settings, these events may occur in the ED during oxygen therapy and resuscitation [21, 22]. Examples include use of high flow nasal oxygenation (HFNO) ventilation or BVM in conjunction with defibrillators. Awareness and prevention are essential to mitigate this risk.

Cancer or treatment-related edema

Edema is common in cancer patients and can significantly complicate airway management. Corticosteroids are frequently used in oncology for both therapeutic and symptomatic purposes, such as in hypopituitarism, adrenal insufficiency, brain metastases, spinal cord compression, or superior vena cava syndrome [23, 24]. Edema may also arise directly from malignancy, cancer-directed therapies including chemotherapy or immunotherapy, disruption of lymphatic drainage, or the presence of large tumors or effusions [25]. Airway edema can rapidly compromise patency and complicate intubation or ventilation, particularly in patients presenting with progressive dyspnea, stridor, or facial swelling.

Multiple aspects of airway management can be affected. Facial swelling, particularly involving the cheeks, lips, and perioral tissues can prevent effective mask seal for BVM ventilation. Neck and pharyngeal soft tissue edema increases upper airway collapsibility, further complicating positive-pressure ventilation.

Supraglottic airway placement may also be challenging with oropharyngeal edema. Reduced space, distorted landmarks, and mucosal swelling can hinder insertion or increase malposition risk. Swelling of the tongue and oral tissues—sometimes exacerbated by fluid retention or rapid weight gain—may obscure the operator’s view and complicate placement.

Increased neck circumference or pharyngeal soft tissue can restrict the space available for direct laryngoscopy and may impede optimal alignment of the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axes. Edema of the tongue, uvula, or epiglottis may affect views, necessitating greater force during laryngoscopy and increasing the risk of trauma.

Patient positioning is critical. Ramped positioning has been shown to improve airway alignment and ventilation in obese or edematous patients [26]. Standard supine positioning may exacerbate airway collapse in patients with generalized edema or weight gain, highlighting the importance of individualized positioning strategies.

In summary, edema in cancer patients—whether treatment-related, disease-related, or steroid-modulated—can affect every stage of airway management. Awareness of the challenges, along with tailored positioning and airway adjunct strategies, is essential to optimize effective airway management in this high-risk population.

Hemoptysis and hematemesis in cancer

Hemoptysis and hematemesis are important considerations in airway management for oncology patients. Hemoptysis may present in lung cancer, while hematemesis may occur with esophageal or gastric malignancies, or hepatocellular carcinoma with varices secondary to cirrhosis [27–29]. Furthermore, many patients with cancer develop cytopenias, including thrombocytopenia, which may be profound and associated with an increased risk of clinically significant bleeding. In addition, cancer patients are frequently prescribed anticoagulation due to a higher incidence of venous thromboembolism. The combination of these factors places cancer patients at increased risk of significant hemorrhage involving the lungs, airway, or aerodigestive tract. Prompt platelet transfusion and reversal of anticoagulation should be considered as additional strategies to minimize bleeding risk in such cases.

In massive hemoptysis or hematemesis, securing a definitive airway for airway protection is often necessary. These conditions are particularly challenging because blood in the oropharynx or hypopharynx can obscure the laryngeal view, making direct or video laryngoscopy difficult. Suction alone may be insufficient during rapid sequence induction (RSI) and intubation. In such cases, the Suction-Assisted Laryngoscopy and Airway Decontamination technique can be employed to facilitate visualization and intubation by continuously suctioning blood and secretions while introducing the endotracheal tube [30].

In massive hemoptysis, positioning the patient in the lateral decubitus position with the bleeding side down can also improve laryngeal view [31]. For hemoptysis resulting from pulmonary hemorrhage, nebulized tranexamic acid may improve hemostasis with relatively minimal adverse reactions [32].

Impact of cachexia and weight loss

Cachexia is common in cancer patients and can complicate airway management. Loss of buccal fat and muscle tone can hollow out the cheeks, creating large leaks during BVM ventilation.

Several strategies have been described to overcome these difficulties. Buccal packing with folded, compressed gauze can help improve mask seal [33]. In addition, a modified two-hand E-C technique, in which the thumbs apply pressure to the sides of the nose while maintaining the standard finger positions along the mandible, can further optimize mask seal [34]. Another technique described is to pull the sunken cheeks upwards to improve mask seal [35]. Awareness of these adaptations is important as standard BVM techniques may be inadequate in this population. Physiological challenges

Cancer represents a profoundly catabolic state that leads to widespread physiological alterations and its attendant implications. Cancer cachexia, a multifactorial syndrome characterized by progressive muscle wasting and metabolic dysfunction, results from both increased protein degradation and reduced protein synthesis. In addition to loss of skeletal muscle mass, there is often lipolysis, depletion of adipose tissue, and selective fat loss, culminating in profound weakness, fatigue, and diminished functional reserve [36].

Cachexia extends beyond skeletal muscle, frequently affecting cardiac and respiratory muscles, the liver, and the immune system. Elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines—such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15)—drive catabolism and suppress appetite and anabolic pathways [37]. Concurrently, increased hepatic synthesis of acute-phase proteins diverts amino acids and metabolic resources away from tissue maintenance and repair [38]. These processes contribute to malnutrition, respiratory muscle weakness, hypoalbuminemia, and immunosuppression, cumulatively reducing the ability to respond to physiologic stress, including the demands of critical illness.

Many cancer patients also have pre-existing organ dysfunction related either to the etiology of their malignancy or to comorbid conditions. For example, cirrhosis is common in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, while COPD and emphysema are frequently seen in those with lung cancer. These baseline impairments further limit cardiopulmonary reserve and can be exacerbated by tumor burden, anemia, infection, or treatment-related toxicity.

Additionally, paraneoplastic syndromes, particularly those associated with lung and hematologic malignancies, may result in electrolyte disturbances, metabolic abnormalities, or neuromuscular dysfunction.

Beyond the systemic effects of cancer itself, oncologic therapies—including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted biologic agents—can impair tissue healing, induce pulmonary or cardiac toxicity, and reduce physiologic reserve. Pharmacologic considerations in rapid sequence intubation

Unique physiological and pharmacologic alterations in cancer and cancer treatment require careful consideration in the choice and dosing of RSI agents. Both cancer and its therapies produce systemic effects that influence drug metabolism, distribution, and elimination, while the patient’s nutritional and functional status further modulates these responses.

Treatment-related toxicities

Many chemotherapeutic agents induce cardiotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and hematologic abnormalities. Agents such as doxorubicin may lead to cardiomyopathy, electrocardiographic changes, and arrhythmias, which increase the risk of hemodynamic compromise during induction and paralysis [41]. In such cases, sedative or paralytic agents with minimal cardiac depressive effects should be favored. Similarly, chemotherapy-induced nephrotoxicity can impair potassium excretion, augmenting the risk of hyperkalemia when succinylcholine is used.

Furthermore, immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)–related myasthenia can result in destruction of acetylcholine receptors, producing resistance to depolarizing agents (e.g., succinylcholine) and heightened sensitivity to non-depolarizing muscle relaxants, necessitating careful titration. Consequently, non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockers such as rocuronium are preferred for RSI in these populations.

Metabolic and nutritional effects on pharmacokinetics

Cancer and its treatments frequently lead to malnutrition, cachexia, and dehydration through anorexia, poor oral intake, and metabolic alterations. One study found that over half of cancer patients exhibit moderate to severe malnutrition [42], which in another study was associated with increased treatment toxicity, reduced tolerance and compliance to therapy, and a reduced response to treatments [43]. In addition, advanced age and the cumulative effects of chemotherapy and immunotherapy may impair cardiac, hepatic, and renal function and alter protein binding and drug distribution parameters.

Together, malnutrition, dehydration, and organ dysfunction, substantially alter pharmacokinetic parameters—including absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion [44].

It is also important to consider the immediate pharmacokinetic effects and toxicity in cancer patients with malnutrition and dehydration. These include potentiating acute cardiovascular adverse effects of RSI medications, including hypotension, arrhythmias, and prolonged drug action. These alterations can increase the risk of drug toxicity and delay post-intubation recovery of consciousness. In contrast, Hijiya et al. reported no significant long-term effects on morbidity or mortality among pediatric patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia receiving chemotherapy who had normal or elevated body mass index values [46].

Overall, RSI drug selection and dosing must be individualized according to the patient’s physiological reserve, cancer stage, age, and hydration status to minimize adverse hemodynamic effects. In cachectic, older, or dehydrated patients with advanced cancer, initial dose reduction of RSI medications is advisable to prevent hypotension, arrhythmias, and delayed recovery following induction. These effects are further amplified in critically ill or hemodynamically unstable patients, where underlying shock, metabolic acidosis, or respiratory failure increase susceptibility to post-intubation cardiovascular collapse. A large prospective study of critically ill patients undergoing intubation reported cardiovascular instability in 42.6%, hypoxia in 9.3%, and cardiac arrest in 3.1% of cases—underscoring the importance of conservative dosing and pre-intubation optimization in such populations [47].

Additional physiologic and immunologic considerations

Immobility from spinal cord compression, paralysis, or severe deconditioning—common complications in advanced malignancy—can cause upregulation of extra-junctional acetylcholine receptors, leading to potassium efflux and life-threatening hyperkalemia following succinylcholine administration. Consequently, non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockers such as rocuronium are preferred for RSI in this population.

Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) is associated with significant metabolic disturbances, including hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, and hyperuricemia, which may progress to acute kidney injury, cardiac dysrhythmias, tetany, and seizures. These abnormalities have important implications for peri-intubation management, particularly with respect to the risk of peri-induction cardiovascular instability. In this context, succinylcholine is generally avoided due to the potential for exacerbating hyperkalemia and is typically not recommended in patients with TLS.

Cancer patients frequently exhibit immunosuppression due to cancer-related or treatment-induced pancytopenia or neutropenia, rendering them highly vulnerable to rapid deterioration from infection and sepsis. In sepsis, these patients demonstrate enhanced sensitivity to the hemodynamic effects of sedatives and opioids, resulting in more rapid and sustained hypotension. Adjusting dosages to minimize these adverse effects while maintaining adequate sedation and paralysis is essential to prevent peri-intubation complications. Approach considerations

Early recognition of a potentially difficult airway is critical when managing ED cancer patients. A thorough medical history review can provide valuable clues, including prior diagnoses of.

cancer in the upper aerodigestive tract, previous radiation, or surgical intervention [48]. These are frequently associated with altered anatomy, fibrosis, and reduced tissue compliance, which pose risks with airway management.

Imaging studies play an important role in anticipating airway difficulty. The need for additional imaging must be balanced with hemodynamic stability, as airway and ventilatory compromise may preclude new studies. When prior imaging is available, its recency and relevance should be assessed—tumor progression or post-treatment changes may render older studies less reliable. Predictive imaging markers on computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging include reduced mandibular and pharyngeal dimensions, increased pre-epiglottic and anterior neck soft tissue thickness, and tumor-related mass effect such as luminal narrowing, tracheal deviation, or distortion of supraglottic and glottic anatomy [49, 50].

At the bedside, traditional predictors of difficult intubation—such as the Mallampati score—have demonstrated limited predictive value in the general population [51]. Their utility in cancer patients is likely further reduced given the anatomical distortions and soft-tissue changes associated with malignancy and prior therapies. Flexible fiberoptic airway evaluation can provide direct visualization of the airway and may assist in stratifying risk, if the patient’s condition and available resources allow [52].

Previous anesthetic or intubation records also provide crucial information; documented prior difficulty with airway management is a strong predictor of future challenges [53].

For patients with suspected or known difficult airways, a proactive approach is essential. If available, video laryngoscopy is preferred over direct laryngoscopy, and where feasible, video laryngoscopy may be considered as the first-line approach for all RSI intubations [54]. For anticipated severe difficulty, awake intubation or flexible fiberoptic-assisted techniques may be indicated, and preparation for emergency cricothyroidotomy must be made in the event of a “can’t-intubate, can’t-ventilate” scenario.

Non-invasive modalities such as HFNO and NIV should be considered as much as possible, if appropriate. However, given the reduced physiological reserve common in cancer patients, the risk for deterioration is high, especially when the cause of respiratory failure is not rapidly reversible. In more easily reversible causes of hypoxia such as pulmonary edema from heart failure, NIV in cancer patients seem to point to better outcomes [55]. Therefore, cancer patients on non-invasive modalities require close continuous monitoring and a readiness to proceed to definitive airway management in the event of decompensation.

Prior to intubation, preoxygenation and hemodynamic optimization are essential to mitigate the risk of peri-intubation hypoxia, hypotension, and cardiovascular collapse. Preparation should include ensuring availability of vasopressors, airway adjuncts, and resuscitation equipment. Finally, early multidisciplinary involvement, including anesthesiology and otolaryngology, is strongly recommended for any patient identified as high-risk for difficult airway management.

Where available and feasible, bedside ultrasound can be used in conjunction with traditional techniques in confirming tracheal tube placement and position [56].

Etomidate

Etomidate is an ultra–short-acting, non-barbiturate hypnotic agent. Etomidate is distinguished from other induction agents by its stable cardiorespiratory profile, producing little cardiovascular or respiratory depression. It provides a rapid onset of anesthesia, with loss of the eyelash reflex typically occurring within 116 s following a single bolus dose [57].

The principal adverse effect of etomidate is transient adrenal suppression due to inhibition of 11-β-hydroxylase in the adrenal cortex [58]. In a meta-analysis of 111 randomized trials involving 2,704 patients, Kotani et al. reported a significant increase in mortality associated with etomidate use in critically ill patients [59]. Etomidate has also been shown to increase the risk of adrenal insufficiency in these patients, underscoring the need for caution in critically ill populations.

Interestingly, etomidate has recently been classified as a class C controlled substance in Singapore, making it illegal to manufacture, import, export, possess, or consume the drug without authorization. Under the Misuse of Drugs Act, controlled substances are categorized from class A (most strictly regulated) to class C (least regulated) [60].

Ketamine

Ketamine, a phencyclidine derivative, is a rapid-acting induction agent [58].

Ketamine is notable for its hemodynamic stability, producing minimal reductions in blood pressure or cardiac output, even in critically ill patients. Its pharmacologic effects are mediated primarily agonistic activity at γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors contributing to its sedative and anticonvulsant properties. Furthermore, ketamine exhibits opioid receptor binding, conferring additional analgesic effects.

Ketamine may be used as a sole induction agent to achieve favorable intubating conditions. Its bronchodilatory properties make it the induction agent of choice in patients with reactive airway disease or asthma, particularly when airway hyperreactivity or bronchospasm is anticipated [61].

Advantages of ketamine over etomidate are yet unclear. In a randomized trial of critically ill patients, ketamine use was not associated with higher in-hospital mortality compared to etomidate but was associated with higher cardiovascular collapse during intubation [62]. In a systematic review, ketamine had no effect on first attempt intubation success or mortality but was associated with decreased need for vasopressors and decreased adrenal suppression compared with etomidate [63]. More recently, in a large cohort study of critically ill adults, etomidate use in RSI was associated with higher in-hospital mortality compared with ketamine [64]. More definitive research is needed, particularly in the context of oncologic patients.

Propofol

Propofol is a short-acting intravenous anesthetic agent that exerts its hypnotic effects primarily through modulation of GABA receptor activity, resulting in sedative, hypnotic, and anticonvulsant properties.

While the recommended induction dose is 2.0 to 2.5 mg/kg, the optimal dose should be adjusted based on age, comorbidities, and American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status [65]. Elderly patients or those with reduced physiological reserves should receive lower initial doses, with titration to effect to achieve adequate conditions for tracheal intubation while minimizing hemodynamic compromise.

The adverse effects of propofol are well documented. The most frequently reported is pain on injection, while more clinically significant reactions include cardiovascular depression, manifested as bradycardia and hypotension [65, 66]. These effects are dose-dependent and may be more pronounced in hypovolemic or critically ill patients, necessitating careful hemodynamic monitoring during induction.

Midazolam

Midazolam is a light-sensitive short-acting benzodiazepine. The induction dose for RSI should be individualized according to the patient’s age, comorbidities, and hemodynamic status [67].

Underdosing of midazolam during ED RSI is a recognized issue, often resulting from incorrect dose estimation rather than intentional dose reduction [68]. Clinicians should be aware that adequate dosing is essential to achieve appropriate sedation depth and facilitate safe airway management.

Adverse effects include hypotension, tachycardia, and respiratory depression, particularly with rapid intravenous administration. Clinically significant respiratory depression can occur even at doses as low as 0.15 mg/kg, and the risk increases when co-administered with opioids such as fentanyl [69]. Careful titration and continuous hemodynamic and respiratory monitoring are therefore recommended during use.

Succinylcholine

The selection of the optimal neuromuscular blocking agent and dosage are critical to achieving effective paralysis while minimizing complications during RSI. Succinylcholine is a rapid acting depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent with an onset of action of about 60 s [70].

Its rapid onset and short duration of action make it suitable for RSI. However, its use requires careful consideration of patient comorbidities and contraindications, particularly in oncology.

Absolute contraindications include hypersensitivity to succinylcholine, history of malignant hyperthermia, myopathies, major trauma or acute burns, extensive muscular denervation, upper motor neuron injury, and ocular conditions such as angle-closure glaucoma or penetrating eye injury [71].

Following administration, serum potassium levels may increase by 0.5 to 1.0 mmol/L [71]. Consequently, succinylcholine should be avoided in patients with end-stage renal disease or documented hyperkalemia, where this transient elevation may precipitate dysrhythmias.

Cancer patients may be at heightened risk for hyperkalemia due to immobility, muscle wasting, or spinal cord compression syndromes, all of which contribute to muscular denervation. These pathophysiologic states increase the expression of extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors, amplifying potassium efflux with depolarizing agents.

Caution is also needed in patients with myasthenia gravis, myositis, or ICI–associated neuromuscular syndromes. In myasthenia gravis, the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor density at the neuromuscular junction is markedly reduced, leading to resistance to depolarizing agents such as succinylcholine and exquisite sensitivity to non-depolarizing agents such as rocuronium [72].

Therefore, succinylcholine should be avoided in patients with myasthenia gravis, myositis, or ICI treatment. ICI treatment can rarely produce myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenia gravis overlap syndrome [73]. In these populations, rocuronium is preferred, with dosing adjusted to the lower end of the therapeutic range to mitigate prolonged paralysis.

Rocuronium

Rocuronium is a non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent of the aminosteroid class, with a short onset time of approximately 60 s [70].

Compared with succinylcholine, rocuronium offers a superior safety profile, particularly regarding cardiovascular and metabolic stability. Rocuronium is associated with fewer adverse effects, including lower risk of bradyarrhythmias and hyperkalemia, making it an attractive alternative in patients with renal dysfunction, neuromuscular disease, or other contraindications to depolarizing agents.

A systematic review in the Cochrane Database comparing succinylcholine and rocuronium found that succinylcholine generally produced superior intubating conditions, although the difference was dose-dependent. In studies where rocuronium was administered at doses ≥ 0.6 mg/kg and succinylcholine at doses ≥ 1.0 mg/kg, succinylcholine maintained a modest advantage in achieving excellent intubation conditions [74]. Nevertheless, the hemodynamic stability and lower incidence of severe adverse events with rocuronium often make it a suitable agent for RSI, especially in hyperkalemia or muscle denervation.

Opioid adjuncts

Opioids are frequently used RSI adjuncts to enhance intubating conditions and attenuate the sympathetic response triggered by airway manipulation. These agents can reduce the associated tachycardia and hypertension, thereby minimizing myocardial oxygen demand—an important consideration in hemodynamically fragile oncologic patients with reduced cardiopulmonary reserve.

Fentanyl is particularly favored due to its rapid onset (30 to 60 s) and short duration of action. It is sometimes administered prior to induction, particularly in combination with ketamine, to blunt the hypertensive response that may occur with ketamine-induced catecholamine release [75].

However, both fentanyl and morphine carry the risk of respiratory depression, apnea, and hypotension, especially when co-administered with sedative-hypnotic agents such as midazolam or propofol. Caution and appropriate dosing are warranted in elderly, cachectic, or hemodynamically unstable patients, as well as patients with hepatic or renal dysfunction due to alterations in opioid metabolism and clearance.

Technical considerations

In summary, choice and technique of airway management must be carefully individualized in cancer patients in the ED, considering tumor-related anatomical changes, prior surgery, radiation, treatment, and physiological reserve.

HFNO or NIV should be considered as first-line modalities in patients with respiratory distress when there is no evidence of airway obstruction. These approaches can provide effective oxygenation and reduce the need for intubation in appropriately selected patients, particularly patients with rapidly reversible causes of respiratory failure.

When tracheal intubation becomes necessary, clinicians must recognize that cancer patients—especially those with head and neck malignancies—are at higher risk of difficult or failed intubation due to airway distortion, tissue friability, or obstruction. Early preparation, use of advanced airway techniques (e.g., video laryngoscopy, awake flexible fiberoptic intubation), and prompt involvement of expert airway support are critical to optimizing success and safety.

In the event of a failed definitive airway, BVM or supraglottic airway ventilation should be employed as temporizing measures to aintain airway patency and adequate oxygenation as the immediate priority until the arrival of further support from an airway team.

In cannot-intubate, cannot-ventilate scenarios, emergency cricothyroidotomy is the recommended next step for establishing a surgical airway. However, in cancer patients, particularly those with friable tumor tissue or post-radiation fibrosis and anatomical distortion in the head and neck region, cricothyroidotomy may be challenging or unfeasible. In these cases, alternative initial strategies—such as awake tracheostomy or flexible fiberoptic-guided intubation—should be considered and may offer a safer approach than RSI [12].

A team-based approach is essential. Early coordination with anesthesiology, otolaryngology, and, when appropriate, oncology teams facilitate safe and effective airway management, ensuring that both procedural and disease-specific complexities are adequately addressed.

Resuscitation considerations

Hemodynamic instability

Cancer patients are at a markedly increased risk of post-induction hemodynamic instability or cardiovascular collapse [76]. This vulnerability often arises from hematology-oncology specific etiologies, including neutropenic sepsis, hemorrhage, tumor lysis syndrome, chemotherapy cardiotoxicity, adrenal insufficiency, and immune-related toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome. These factors collectively compromise cardiovascular reserve and blunt compensatory responses to induction agents and positive-pressure ventilation.

Whenever feasible, underlying precipitating factors should be identified and corrected early, and disease-specific therapies (e.g., stress-dose corticosteroids for adrenal crisis, tumor lysis management protocols) should be initiated prior to induction. Careful optimization of fluid balance and hemodynamic status should likewise precede RSI. This requires a tailored approach to fluid resuscitation and vasopressor use, balancing the need for adequate perfusion with the heightened risk of pulmonary edema in patients with capillary leak or cardiotoxicity. Judicious pre-induction management and close hemodynamic monitoring are therefore critical to minimizing peri-intubation cardiovascular collapse in this high-risk population.

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation and code status considerations

Return of spontaneous circulation can be achieved in a substantial proportion of cancer patients following cardiopulmonary resuscitation; however, survival to hospital discharge and long-term survival remain poor and often brief [77]. Yeung et al. reported even lower survival rates among patients with advanced or untreatable malignancies, emphasizing the importance of prognosis in guiding resuscitative decision-making [78].

Critically ill cancer patients presenting to the ED pose unique challenges to healthcare providers. When patients present to their primary treatment facility, clinicians may have ready access to oncologic records, treatment regimens, disease stage, and prognosis. Conversely, patients who present to outside EDs often arrive without accessible medical documentation, leaving emergency physicians to make time-sensitive decisions with limited context.

A DNR order applies specifically to situations of cardiopulmonary arrest, indicating that resuscitative efforts should not be initiated once breathing and cardiac activity have ceased. However, intubation is often also performed as part of the resuscitative efforts during cardiac arrest. As such, clinicians should discuss DNR status in conjunction with do-not-intubate directives, as the latter pertains to airway management prior to and during cardiac arrest. Clarifying these distinctions helps ensure that patients’ wishes are respected and that consent is informed and specific.

In Texas, for example, patients and prehospital providers may present an out-of-hospital do-not-resuscitate (OOH-DNR) order, a legally recognized document under the Texas Health and Safety Code [79]. The OOH-DNR directs healthcare professionals to forgo resuscitative attempts while continuing to provide other forms of emergency and comfort care to ensure a natural death with dignity. Similar frameworks exist in other jurisdictions, though regulations and terminology may vary by state or country.

Within the hospital, DNR status may be documented in the medical record. It is advisable to confirm and, when necessary, renew an in-hospital DNR order for cancer patients upon each admission—particularly in cases of critical illness or ICU transfer. Importantly, patients’ wishes regarding resuscitation may evolve over time. For instance, some may temporarily elect full code status for procedures requiring intubation or general anesthesia [80].

In the primary care setting, the prevalence of advance care directives remains low, with fewer than 30% of patients having completed such documents [81]. While advance directives are legally binding, they are not medical orders, and translation into actionable in-hospital DNR orders is often required. The American College of Emergency Physicians has recommended a standardized framework addressing 17 key components to guide the implementation of OOH-DNR policies [82].

Despite increasing awareness, discussions surrounding code status are frequently delayed. In a study involving 1,047 hospitalizations of patients with advanced cancer, physicians failed to document code status in 52.1% of cases, resulting in an implicit assumption of full code. Among patients who transitioned from full code to DNR, the most common triggers were acute clinical deterioration (15.4%), discontinuation of cancer-directed therapy (17.2%), and conversations regarding futility of care (15.4%) [83].

In a five-year retrospective study from a major cancer center in China, where no formal advance directive legislation exists, 80% of patients had DNR orders signed, often very late in the disease course. The median time to DNR signing was one day before death, with most completed within the final three days of life [84]. Timing correlated strongly with disease severity and rapid clinical decline, underscoring the need for earlier goals-of-care discussions.

For emergency physicians, determining a patient’s code status is a critical step in managing any critically ill individual, particularly when aggressive resuscitation may be futile. When no DNR documentation exists, the physician should promptly clarify and document the patient’s wishes, provided the patient retains decision-making capacity. When such capacity is absent, surrogates such as spouses, parents, or adult children—and, when possible, the treating oncologist—should be engaged in shared decision-making.

Ethical dilemmas may arise when a critically ill cancer patient presents without family, documentation, or advance directives. In such scenarios, emergency physicians must balance the ethical imperative to preserve life with the principle of non-maleficence, often initiating resuscitation until sufficient information becomes available to guide patient-centered decision-making.

Conclusions

Airway management and resuscitation in patients with cancer represent complex and underexplored areas within emergency medicine. Airway challenges in the oncologic population remain relatively underrepresented in the literature; however, with the rapid evolution of cancer therapies, understanding their impact on patients who present with airway emergencies is increasingly important for emergency physicians. The interplay of cancer-related physiological alterations, treatment-induced toxicities, and ethical challenges creates a distinct clinical context that demands specialized approaches. Although the broader literature on airway management is extensive, outcomes specific to oncology populations remain less well defined. Further research is needed to better characterize these differences, including comparative outcomes of intubation in patients with prior cancer therapies—such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy—versus those without such exposures, as well as outcomes following different airway and resuscitative techniques in this population.

Equally critical is the integration of patient-centered care principles into ED practice. Timely access to documentation such as DNR orders and accurate information regarding disease stage and prognosis can help emergency physicians make appropriate, goal-concordant decisions. In addition to understanding the legal framework surrounding DNR and advance care planning, emergency physicians should cultivate communication skills to conduct these sensitive discussions with confidence and compassion. Structured frameworks may be particularly useful in facilitating such conversations [85].

Finally, embedding palliative care pathways within ED workflows represents an important step toward improving the quality and timeliness of supportive care for patients with advanced malignancy. Early palliative involvement not only ensures alignment of care with patient values but also optimizes resource utilization and overall quality of life [86].
