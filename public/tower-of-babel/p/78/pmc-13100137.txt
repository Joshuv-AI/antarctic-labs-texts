p38 MAPK orchestrates cross-tissue potassium homeostasis for survival
Authors: Rong Huang, Fangchao Hu, Yiyao Li, Qifei Gao, Jintao Huang, Jiarui Wang, Wei Lu, Liping Wang, Yapeng Fang, T. Keith Blackwell, Ziyun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potassium is vital for life, yet how potassium homeostasis is maintained at the tissue or organismal level under dietary scarcity remains poorly understood. Stress-activated signaling pathway p38 MAPK is implicated in immune response and aging, but its specific role in low potassium response is unclear. Here we show that a specific p38 MAPK–ATF-7 pathway orchestrates cross-tissue potassium homeostasis in Caenorhabditis elegans. It drives transcriptional upregulation of a crucial P-type ATPase pump CATP-3 specifically in the hypodermis, a process that integrates cell-autonomous mechanisms with non-autonomous ASI neuronal signals, thereby enhancing organismal survival during potassium deficiency. Notably, this regulation is distinct from canonical osmotic stress responses, revealing a specialized and conserved survival strategy. Analogous p38-mediated control of P-type ATPases occurs in yeast and mammalian cells, suggesting broad relevance. Our findings redefine potassium regulation as a cross-tissue process linked to lifespan, stress signaling, and innate immunity with potential implications for aging and age-related diseases.

Introduction

Potassium is vital for cellular and tissue function, primarily obtained through dietary intake in humans1. Inadequate intake, a persistent global issue2–4, can lead to potassium deficiency (PD), causing hypokalemia ( < 3.5 mmol/L) and life-threatening consequences5. Although the critical role of dietary potassium in regulating lifespan has been recognized in model organisms, studies investigating this relationship and the underlying mechanisms remain scarce6. Potassium ion (K+) homeostasis is regulated at the organismal level via dietary intake and renal excretion, and within cells by Na+/K+-ATPase and K+ channels through active and passive transport, respectively7. Yet, whether these processes are coordinated across tissues to mitigate PD stress remains elusive. Although low potassium upregulates Na+/K+-ATPase expression in specific cells, such as renal tubular cells8,9, the extent of synchronized regulation across tissues or the whole organism is unclear. Such coordination could enhance survival under PD, suggesting an evolutionary drive to adapt to potassium scarcity. Elucidating these mechanisms holds promise for advancing our understanding of cross-tissue responses to PD.

The p38 family of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), conserved across species, governs cellular responses to diverse stresses, including environmental insults, infections, tumorigenesis, and growth-related challenges10,11. In Caenorhabditis elegans, p38 MAPK signaling underpins longevity during dietary restriction, modulated by nutrient availability12. This suggests p38 may orchestrate broader metabolic defenses, potentially including potassium regulation. Here, we harnessed C. elegans’s genetic and phenotypic strengths to uncover a p38 MAPK pathway that boosts PD survival. This pathway coordinates K+ homeostasis across tissues, acting both autonomously and nonautonomously to regulate innate immune response genes and a P-type ATPase potassium pump. These findings reveal that animals can detect and respond to potassium scarcity through a cross-tissue signaling network, rather than isolated cellular responses. The integration of sensory neuron input with ion transport in distal tissues reshapes our understanding of potassium homeostasis, linking it to lifespan regulation and immune-like defense programs. This work identifies a conserved, systemic strategy for coping with low potassium stress that may inform approaches to aging and age-related diseases in mammals.

Results

Potassium plays a key role in the survival of p38 mutants

To investigate responses to insufficient availability of external potassium in C. elegans, we developed a method for rapidly assessing their lifespan in liquid culture without E. coli OP50-1 (a streptomycin-resistant strain that is widely used as a standard food source for C. elegans in laboratories), thereby minimizing the confounding effects of food-derived potassium. This approach utilizes day 3 adult worms to avoid interference with growth, development, or reproduction (Fig. 1a), with all survival data recorded from adult day 3 onward. Initially, wild type (N2) worms in distilled water without salt supplementation exhibited a mean lifespan of ~19 days, comparable to that on standard nematode growth medium (NGM) plates with live OP50-1 (Fig. 1b, and Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Data 1)13. This observed lifespan likely reflects the net effect of two counteracting factors: prolongation arising from bacterial food deprivation14–16 and a countervailing shortening induced by the absence of essential salts. Although p38 MAPK signaling is well known for its roles in longevity and innate immunity in C. elegans, prior studies have also implicated the NSY-1(ASK1 MAPKKK)–SEK-1(MKK3/MKK6 MAPKK)–PMK-1(p38 MAPK) cascade in ionic and osmotic stress adaptation, including its function within the OSR-1–CaMKII–NSY-1 pathway for hyperosmotic resistance and its regulation of sodium leak channel activity that might influence Na⁺/K⁺ homeostasis17–19. Together with our preliminary observations showing hypersensitivity of sek-1(km4) null mutants to electrolyte depletion, these findings prompted us to investigate whether the p38 MAPK pathway plays a critical role in sensing and responding to potassium-deficient stress. In contrast, loss of sek-1 only minimally affects survival in NGM media20, but results in death within 2 days in water (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1a). We also observed that NaCl supplementation in liquid culture had a less beneficial effect on the survival of wild type animals compared to KCl (Fig. 1b, d). High NaCl concentration (250 mM) shortened lifespan due to hyperosmotic stress, while KCl supplementation caused no such effect. Notably, KCl significantly extended the lifespan of wild type worms across tested concentrations (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, KCl, rather than NaCl, markedly rescued the survival of sek-1 and pmk-1 (p38 MAPK) null mutants in distilled water (Fig. 1b–e and Supplementary Fig. 1a). This effect is consistent with a model in which the apparent rescue reflects a combination of a general lifespan-extending effect of KCl and the alleviation of the increased susceptibility of sek-1 and pmk-1 mutants to PD. In addition, supplementation with Ca2+ failed to improve the survival of sek-1 mutants under water conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1b), further supporting the idea that p38 signaling is particularly required for survival under conditions of limited exogenous K+, rather than upon exposure to Na+, Ca2+ or Cl–. Together, these results suggest that p38 signaling promotes survival by mitigating PD (100 mM NaCl without exogenous K+) stress and argue against a primary role for nonspecific osmotic imbalance.

We investigated the effects of ion concentrations on worm survival and found that elevated K+ levels were crucial for extending longevity in wild type worms (Supplementary Fig. 1a). However, direct pairwise comparisons showed that replacing K⁺ with Na⁺ (50 mM K-phosphate buffer vs. 50 mM Na-phosphate buffer, or 50 mM KCl vs. 50 mM NaCl) dramatically reduced lifespan of sek-1 and pmk-1 mutants, whereas switching anions while keeping the cation constant (50 mM K-phosphate vs. 50 mM KCl, or 50 mM Na-phosphate vs. 100 mM NaCl) had minimal effect (Fig. 1f–h). We also examined whether culture conditions (solid vs. liquid) influenced survival under PD, found that sek-1 or pmk-1 mutants were specifically sensitive to PD under both conditions, whether worms were grown on solid agar plates or in liquid culture in the presence of E. coli OP50-1 (Supplementary Figs. 1c, d).

Extracellular pH can influence membrane potential and ion flux by modulating proton-sensitive K⁺ channels and, consequently, voltage-gated ion channel activity; therefore, we tested whether altered H⁺ levels contribute to the survival defects observed under PD. Interestingly, wild type worms survived longer in low-pH (4.3) medium than in high-pH (9.3) medium, regardless of K+ or Na+. Nevertheless, sek-1 mutants exhibited a greater dependence on K+ than on pH (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f). In addition, because the p38 MAPK pathway plays a central role in innate immunity, we also assessed pathogen sensitivity to determine whether the PD phenotype might be secondary to immune defects. Neither Na+ nor K+ influenced pathogenic survival in either slow-killing or fast-killing assays in wild type and sek-1 animals (Supplementary Fig. 1g, h), indicating a distinct process between p38-mediated pathogenic resistance and PD survival.

Furthermore, KCl supplementation rescued sek-1(km4) survival in a strictly time-dependent manner: adding 50 mM KCl within the first 2 h of transfer to PD medium (100 mM NaCl) fully restored lifespan to normal K+ levels, whereas additions at later time points ( ≥ 2 h) yielded only partial or no rescue (Fig. 1i). This indicates that p38 signaling mediates a rapid, early protective response that must be engaged within a narrow temporal window to prevent irreversible physiological damage during acute PD. Notably, under PD conditions, sek-1 mutants exhibited severe stress phenotypes, including reduced body length, head contraction, and bursting of the intestine and eggs, resulting in rapid death (Fig. 1j, and Supplementary Fig. 1i and Supplementary Video 1–4). These phenotypes were likely due to muscle spasms, as evidenced by contracted and disorganized muscle fibers observed under microscopy. Finally, to exclude altered feeding behavior as a confounding factor, we measured pharyngeal pumping under the same liquid survival conditions. PD exposure did not significantly alter pharyngeal pumping rates in wild-type worms, indicating that potassium deficiency per se does not impair pumping behavior in a normal genetic background (Supplementary Fig. 1j). In contrast, pmk-1 mutants exhibited a significant reduction in pumping rates under PD conditions, consistent with decreased overall physiological activity or heightened stress susceptibility specific to this genotype.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that SEK-1 is essential for survival under PD conditions in the absence of exogenous K+, underscoring its role in mitigating potassium stress. These systematic analyses demonstrate that survival of p38 pathway mutants under PD depends strictly and specifically on extracellular K⁺. This requirement is independent of accompanying anions (Cl⁻ or phosphate), phosphate itself, buffering capacity, pH, the presence of E. coli OP50-1, other cations present in standard growth media (including Ca2+ and Mg2+), as well as culture format (liquid or solid).

PD survival requires the p38–ATF-7 pathway

To further elucidate the role of p38 MAPK signaling in worm survival under PD conditions, we analyzed the entire pathway, comprising TIR-1 (TIR domain protein), NSY-1 (MAPKKK), SEK-1 (MAPKK), PMK-1 (p38 MAPK), and the transcription factor ATF-7 (ATF2/ATF7/CREB5) (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 1). These components are critical for innate immunity and longevity in C. elegans12,21,22. The loss-of-function mutants—including sek-1 (km4 and ag1, null) (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data 1)20, sek-1 and pmk-1 double mutants (Fig. 2c), and nsy-1 (ok593 and ag3, null/strong loss-of-function) (Fig. 2d)—exhibited similarly shortened survival times compared to wild type animals under PD conditions, suggesting that these core p38 MAPK components are essential. TIR-1, an upstream regulator of the p38 cascade known for pathogen response23, showed differential effects: tir-1 mutants impairing immunity (qd4, tm3036, TIR-domain loss or deletion)24, had shorter lifespans, whereas the immunity-sparing tir-1 (ok1052)25 displayed survival comparable to wild type worms (Fig. 2e). Next, we investigated the role of ATF-7, a transcription factor downstream of the p38 MAPK cascade that regulates immunity genes (Fig. 2a)22. ATF-7 acts as both transcriptional activator (when phosphorylated by PMK-1) and repressor (when unphosphorylated) of target genes. Under PD conditions, a loss-of-function atf-7(lf) mutant significantly reduced survival, while a gain-of-function atf-7(gf) mutant, acting solely as a repressor, exhibited even shorter survival than atf-7(lf) (Fig. 2f), mirroring their effects on pathogen resistance22. The markedly stronger phenotype of atf-7(gf) therefore reveals that active repression of these targets by unphosphorylated ATF-7 is considerably more deleterious than simple absence of activation during PD stress, highlighting the critical need for precise p38-dependent phospho-regulation of ATF-7. Notably, atf-7(lf) partially mitigated the survival defect of sek-1 or pmk-1 mutants (Fig. 2g), consistent with the loss of ATF-7’s repressor function. Collectively, our data demonstrate that the TIR-1–p38 MAPK–ATF-7 pathway is essential for survival under PD, sharing mechanistic similarities with its established roles in immunity and dietary restriction induced longevity12,22.

The ocr-2(yz5) and ocr-2(ak47) alleles are loss-of-function mutations in the TRPV channel OCR-2, whose activity in ADF neurons regulates tph-1 expression and serotonin biosynthesis, and is also required for innate immunity24,26. The tph-1(n4622) allele is a presumed null deletion27. Notably, ocr-2(yz5), ocr-2(ak47), and tph-1(n4622) mutants displayed only modest reductions in survival under PD (Fig. 2h), indicating that serotonin biosynthesis contributes only minimally to the PD response.

In the context of lifespan determination in C. elegans, genes associated with chemical sensing, osmotic response, and cytoprotection might contribute to survival under PD28,29. Surprisingly, deletion of some well-studied genes, including those involved in insulin/IGF-1 and mTOR signaling13, reduced survival less severely than sek-1 deletion (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting that PD survival depends more critically on the p38/ATF-7 pathway. To identify suppressors of sek-1(km4) lethality under PD conditions, we performed an unbiased forward-genetic suppressor screen. From ~6 million F2 L1 sek-1(km4) mutants exposed to PD conditions, 120 survived, 58 reached adulthood, and 18 produced progenies. Mapping the six longest-surviving mutants revealed all carried loss-of-function mutations in atf-7, including one known allele (Fig. 2i). Their survival resembled that of atf-7(lf) and sek-1;atf-7 double mutants (Fig. 2j). Together, these findings indicate that the p38–ATF-7 pathway is specifically critical for responding to PD conditions.

PD induces expression of p38–ATF-7-regulated genes

The essential role of the p38–ATF-7 pathway in survival under PD suggests that its downstream target genes are critical for this process. To identify PD-responsive genes, we performed mRNA expression profiling in wild type worms exposed to PD (100 mM NaCl), high potassium (100 mM KCl), or NGM (51 mM NaCl, 25 mM potassium phosphate buffer, KPB) conditions under solid agar plates with OP50-1 for 4 h. This represents the standard growth condition and was used to assess transcriptional responses under physiologically relevant, food-present conditions. Relative to NGM, PD induced 998 downregulated and 1241 upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and compared to high potassium, 1028 downregulated and 1340 upregulated DEGs, with substantial overlap between these sets. In contrast, only 137 genes were downregulated and 133 upregulated between high potassium and NGM (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Data 2). The markedly fewer transcriptional changes under KCl-replete conditions do not indicate lack of specificity; rather, they reflect successful physiological rescue. We analyzed PD-specific DEGs (892 upregulated, 636 downregulated) between PD and either high potassium or NGM conditions (Fig. 3d, e). PD-upregulated genes were enriched in the hypodermis and excretory system (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and associated with cuticle development, innate immunity, apoptosis, and potassium ion import (Fig. 3f). These included known immunity genes encoding Metridin-like ShK toxins and CUB-like domain proteins (Supplementary Fig. 3b), and 115 SEK-1-induced genes (Fig. 3g, h)12. In contrast, PD-downregulated genes showed no such enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 3d–f). Notably, ATF-7 regulated more PD-upregulated genes than downregulated ones, unlike other transcription factors with balanced regulation (Supplementary Fig. 3g). In addition, we performed mRNA expression profiling in wild type and pmk-1(km25) worms exposed to PD (100 mM NaCl) and high potassium (100 mM KCl) in liquid culture without OP50-1 for 4 h (Fig. 3i, j), conditions that recapitulate the liquid survival assays. This experiment was specifically designed to isolate the effects of PD and ionic environment without confounding influences from bacterial metabolism or liquid-associated feeding behavior. PD induced the upregulation of 105 genes (Supplementary Data 2), many of which were enriched in the hypodermis, excretory system, and pathways associated with immune response (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Among these, 24 genes were dependent on PMK-1 for their upregulation, and a substantial fraction overlapped with the 115 genes previously identified as induced by SEK-1 (Fig. 3k,l). Together, our data suggest that many p38–ATF-7-dependent genes, known to protect against pathogens12, are also induced under PD conditions.

The p38–ATF-7 pathway target CATP-3 mediates PD survival

Many downstream genes of the p38–ATF-7 pathway are essential for immunity and dietary restriction induced longevity12,22. To identify PD-responsive targets, we tested both the top PD-upregulated and SEK-1-upregulated genes for their roles in survival under PD conditions (Fig. 3h,l). RNA interference (RNAi) and mutation analyses showed that most had negligible effects individually. However, deletion of catp-3, a conserved P-type ATPase potassium pump (Supplementary Fig. 4a), led to rapid death under PD conditions, with phenotypes resembling those of p38–ATF-7 pathway mutants, including muscle spasms (Supplementary Fig. 3h–j; Supplementary Video 5,6). The catp-3 mutant was highly sensitive to PD, showing significantly reduced survival at potassium levels below 5 mM (Fig. 3m and Supplementary Data 1), yet exhibited wild type lifespan and pathogen resistance under standard conditions (Fig. 3n and Supplementary Fig. 3k). These data suggest that CATP-3 is specifically required for survival under PD conditions.

To confirm the Na+/K+-ATPase activity of CATP-3, we conducted whole-cell patch-clamp experiments in HEK293T cells overexpressing catp-3. Notably, these cells showed significantly reduced pump activity upon treatment with the Na+/K+-ATPase inhibitor ouabain compared to mock-transfected cells, indicating CATP-3 contributes to pump function (Fig. 3o,p). In C. elegans, catp-3 has several paralogs with conserved ATPase domains but distinct expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. 4a–d). However, only catp-3 was essential for survival under PD conditions (Supplementary Fig. 4e), suggesting that p38–ATF-7-mediated upregulation of catp-3 evolved to specifically protect against PD. A decrease in extracellular K⁺ typically hyperpolarizes the membrane potential, which would reduce excitability. However, prolonged reduction in extracellular K⁺ can lead to secondary depletion of intracellular K⁺ due to passive leaks and incomplete compensation by other transporters. Thus, the upregulation of catp-3 under PD conditions may enhance pump capacity to restore intracellular K⁺ levels, thereby stabilizing membrane potential, osmotic balance, and cellular viability under PD conditions. In contrast, 100 mM KCl does not impose PD and thus does not activate this compensatory transcriptional response, explaining why catp-3 is not upregulated in the high-K⁺ condition.

Hypodermal expression of CATP-3 mediates PD survival

To examine the expression pattern of catp-3, we generated a knock-in strain with an 858 bp mCherry sequence inserted before the catp-3 stop codon. Expression was predominantly observed in the hypodermis, a single epithelial layer encasing the animal (Fig. 4a, b), which regulates body size, shape, and mechanical coupling between cuticle, muscles, and neurons30. We also assessed a transgenic line overexpressing a catp-3::GFP translational fusion driven by the catp-3 promoter, confirming localization to hypodermal cells, including seam cells (Fig. 4c). To explore CATP-3’s role in the hypodermis, we generated three extrachromosomal transgenic arrays. Ex1 and Ex2 express catp-3::GFP under its native promoter with (Ex1) or without (Ex2) co-injecting marker rol-6(su1006) plasmid, while Ex3 uses the hypodermis-specific semo-1 promoter32. The gain-of-function rol-6(su1006) allele produces a dominant Roller phenotype that allows easy identification of transgenic animals and does not affect lifespan under our conditions (Fig. 4e). All three transgenes rescued the reduced lifespan of catp-3 mutants under PD conditions (Fig. 4e–g and Supplementary Data 1). In contrast, catp-3 expression in muscle or ASI neurons had no significant effect on PD survival (Fig. 4h, i). These data demonstrate that catp-3 is required in the hypodermis for PD survival.

Developmental gene expression timing critically influences metabolism, behavior, and aging in adulthood33–35. Given that catp-3 expression rises across the four larval stages (Fig. 5b, c), we tested whether p38 MAPK regulates this increase and its relevance to PD survival. Notably, RNAi targeting sek-1 and pmk-1 altered PD survival and catp-3 expression in a developmentally timed manner (Fig. 5d–h and Supplementary Data 1). Two-generation (F2) RNAi treatment produced the most severe phenotypes, consistent with sek-1 and pmk-1 mutants. The markedly stronger phenotypes with F2 sek-1 RNAi compared to single-generation (F1) RNAi reflects the expected difference in knockdown depth and developmental timing: F2 treatment depletes both maternal and zygotic SEK-1 from embryogenesis onward, whereas F1 treatment leaves substantial maternal protein intact, particularly in neurons (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f). Since catp-3 is expressed robustly beginning at L2, RNAi initiated at L2/L3 (24 h post-L1 synchronization) had less effect than at earlier stages, while L4-stage RNAi impacted survival or catp-3 expression only minimally (Fig. 5d–h). These results suggest that p38–ATF-7-induced catp-3 expression must initiate during larval development to ensure adult PD survival.

As fasting/feeding states are known to modulate p38/MAPK signaling12, we tested whether KCl supplementation induces a fed-like signaling state or suppresses the fasting response in C. elegans. Neither qPCR nor RNA-seq analyses revealed significant changes in the expression of canonical fasting-responsive genes (lgg-1, atgl-1, sod-3, and pha-4) between PD and KCl conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d), indicating that potassium supplementation does not reverse the fasting transcriptional program.

To determine whether p38/MAPK signaling is dynamically regulated by potassium availability or constitutively required under PD, we examined the expression of two established PMK-1 downstream targets, sysm-1 and catp-3. While sysm-1 expression was primarily dependent on feeding status and showed minimal or no induction by PD, catp-3 was robustly induced under PD and rapidly suppressed upon KCl supplementation, both in the presence and absence of food (Supplementary Fig. 5e, f). These results indicate that p38/MAPK signaling is not globally activated during PD, but instead regulates a potassium-responsive subset of target genes, supporting a model in which p38 activity is required for survival without uniform stress-induced activation.

Given that p38 MAPK-mediated innate immunosenescence occurs across species36, we observed an age-dependent decline in catp-3 expression (Fig. 5i–k) and the levels of PD-induced catp-3 mRNA expression (Fig. 5l, m). Collectively, these data suggest that catp-3 is transcriptionally regulated by p38–ATF-7 signaling in response to PD.

p38 pathway signaling functions tissue-specifically to mediate PD survival

p38–ATF-7 pathway components are expressed across multiple tissues, including hypodermis, intestine, neurons, and muscles, as shown by fluorescence microscopy and single-cell sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 6)31. Prior studies indicate that SEK-1 acts in the intestine to mediate pathogen defense and oxidative stress response27,37, and in multiple tissues for dietary restriction induced longevity12. Expecting hypodermal sek-1 expression to be essential for PD survival, we tested tissue-specific requirements. Expression of sek-1 under its native promoter fully rescued PD survival, but we could not establish transgenic lines with sufficient hypodermal sek-1 expression, possibly due to toxicity. Intestinal sek-1 expression partially rescued PD survival (Fig. 6a, c), though less effectively than its role in pathogen resistance (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Data 1)27. ASI sensory neurons in the head detect environmental cues like chemoattractants, temperature, and food availability, regulating metabolism38. They are critical for dauer formation, satiety quiescence, and dietary restriction induced longevity39, including p38-dependent innate immunity to pathogens40. These roles suggest that ASI neurons might sense PD conditions. Remarkably, ASI-specific sek-1 expression effectively rescued the reduced lifespan of sek-1(km4) mutants under PD conditions, outperforming intestinal expression (Fig. 6a, b). This was confirmed in integrated transgenic lines (Fig. 6c). Thus, sek-1 expression in ASI neurons provides significant protection against PD stress, suggesting that these neurons may perceive PD and trigger a protective gene expression response.

Interestingly, sek-1 expression in the intestine and ASI neurons differentially regulated downstream genes. Intestinal SEK-1 markedly increased the expression of the three well-characterized PMK-1/ATF-7 immune target genes (sysm-1, C17H12.8, K08D8.5) while suppressing catp-3, whereas ASI-specific SEK-1 fully restored catp-3 expression but only partially increased immune gene levels (Fig. 6d). This aligns with ASI SEK-1 partially rescuing pathogen resistance, unlike catp-3 expression (Fig. 6e). Thus, p38 MAPK signaling for innate immunity and PD responses varies across tissues in its downstream gene regulation.

To clarify the tissue-specific roles of p38 MAPK signaling in PD survival, we performed sek-1 RNAi in neurons, hypodermis, intestine, and muscle. RNAi-deficient rde-1(ne219) mutants exposed to F2 sek-1 RNAi maintained normal PD survival (Fig. 6f). Notably, sek-1 RNAi in neurons and hypodermis significantly reduced PD survival (Fig. 6g, h), similar to wild type worms treated with F2 sek-1 RNAi (Fig. 5e). Although hypodermal SEK-1 rescue experiments were infeasible due to potential toxicity, hypodermal-specific RNAi confirms that p38–ATF-7 signaling in this tissue is critical for PD survival, consistent with CATP-3’s role. Interestingly, intestinal sek-1 RNAi also reduced PD survival (Fig. 6i), whereas muscle RNAi had no substantial effect (Fig. 6j).

Consistent with the modest PD survival reduction seen with F1 sek-1 RNAi (L4 to day 3 adults) in wild type worms, sek-1 RNAi in neurons, hypodermis, intestine, and muscle showed minimal effects (Supplementary Fig. 7a–d). Notably, F1 sek-1 RNAi significantly reduced intestinal SEK-1 but not in ASI neurons (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f), where earlier RNAi initiation (e.g., L1) was required. Furthermore, sek-1 deletion blocked PD-induced catp-3 mRNA expression, an effect also seen with sysm-1 (a p38 target) but not catp-2 (a paralog) (Fig. 6k–m). In sek-1 mutants, PD-induced catp-3 expression remained absent despite endogenous or hypodermal catp-3 overexpression, whereas catp-3 mutants overexpressing catp-3 showed no such defect (Fig. 6k–m), confirming that sek-1 is required specifically for p38-induced gene expression in response to PD. Together, these findings indicate that p38–ATF-7 signaling regulates PD-induced catp-3 expression via cell-autonomous mechanisms (primarily in the hypodermis) and non-cell-autonomous pathways (mainly via ASI and other neurons) (Supplementary Fig. 7g). We propose that CATP-3, an ATPase potassium pump, plays a critical role in excitable tissues (muscles and neurons) by maintaining intracellular K+ levels under PD conditions. However, CATP-3 expression in muscle or ASI neurons had no significant effect on PD survival (Fig. 4h, i), suggesting that CATP-3 functions primarily in the hypodermis to indirectly support muscle and neuronal function, possibly via hypodermal-mediated mechanisms that presumably include enhancement of K+ uptake. We speculate that the strong induction of catp-3 specifically under PD represents a physiologically appropriate compensatory response: severe extracellular K⁺ depletion collapses the K⁺ gradient, depolarizes excitable tissues, and triggers lethal muscle spasms and neuronal dysfunction (Fig. 1j, Supplementary Video 1–6). Upregulation of the hypodermal Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase CATP-3 increases pump density, thereby enhancing K⁺ reuptake capacity into the animal. Because the hypodermis is the major non-excitable epithelial barrier in C. elegans and directly contacts pseudocoelomic fluid and muscles, elevated hypodermal CATP-3 likely restores organismal K⁺ homeostasis by: re-establishing trans-hypodermal K⁺ transport; repolarizing adjacent muscle and neurons indirectly; preventing catastrophic ion leakage and volume dysregulation. In contrast, KCl-replete conditions (100 mM KCl) maintain normal extracellular K⁺, eliminating the need for compensatory pump upregulation—hence catp-3 expression remains basal (Supplementary Fig. 7g). This is directly analogous to the well-documented upregulation of renal Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 and β1 subunits during dietary or pathological potassium deficiency in mammals.

Conservation of p38-mediated potassium pump regulation in mammalian and yeast

Our findings suggest that p38-mediated potassium pump expression in PD defense may be evolutionarily conserved. In mammals, however, p38α deficiency results in embryonic lethality41 and the more complex tissue environment could lead to differences in tissue-specific K+ homeostasis mechanisms compared to C. elegans, despite conserved regulatory pathways. To explore this, we used the C2C12 mouse myoblast cell line, a model widely employed in muscle research for its rapid differentiation into contractile myotubes42, to investigate p38’s role in K+ regulation.

C2C12 myoblast differentiation into muscle requires p38 MAPK signaling42. Under PD conditions (0 mM KCl), proliferation in growth medium (GM) was unaffected (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 8a), but K+ was essential for myotube formation in differentiation medium (DM) (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 8b). K+ withdrawal from differentiation medium disrupted myotube morphology within 24 h, causing atrophy and cell death (Supplementary Fig. 8c). To identify p38 target genes during C2C12 myoblast differentiation, we performed mRNA expression profiling in wild type and Mapk14−/− cells exposed to GM and DM (Fig. 7c). Compared to GM, DM upregulated 2345 genes in wild type cells (Fig. 7d and Supplementary Data 2). Under DM conditions, 2157 genes were upregulated in a p38-dependent manner when comparing Mapk14−/− to wild type cells, whereas only 1337 genes were p38-dependent under GM conditions (Fig. 7d). These results indicate a more prominent role for p38 during cell differentiation. Among the 2345 DM-upregulated genes in wild type cells, 371 were upregulated under either GM or DM conditions (Fig. 7e, h) and were associated with sarcomere and actin filament organization, skeletal muscle development, differentiation, and contraction, underscoring p38’s critical role in C2C12 myoblast differentiation (Fig. 7g and Supplementary Fig. 8h, i). These genes included several well-characterized transcription factors, such as Peg3 (C2H2-type zinc finger protein, involved in cell proliferation), Zbtb18 (C2H2-type zinc finger protein, involved in neuronal development), Mef2d (myocyte-specific enhancer factor 2, regulates muscle and neuronal differentiation), Mef2c (Myocyte Enhancer Factor 2 C, involved in myogenesis), Myod1 (Myogenic Differentiation 1, promotes muscle cell differentiation and regeneration), and Myf5 (Myogenic Factor 5, drives muscle-specific gene transcription and differentiation), all of which act downstream of Atf7 or Atf2—two key transcription factors regulated by p38 MAPK signaling (Fig. 7f,j and Supplementary Data 2).

Additionally, only three DM-upregulated Na+/K+-ATPase genes (Atp1a1, Atp1a2, Atp1a3) were p38-dependent (Fig. 7i and Supplementary Fig. 8g). Notably, Atp1a2, a subunit associated with familial hemiplegic migraine type 2 (FHM2) and other neurological disorders, was predominantly expressed in neurons and skeletal muscle in mouse and human43–45, distinct from Atp1a1 and Atp1a3 (Supplementary Fig. 8j, k). Furthermore, siRNA-mediated knockdown of Mapk14 or Atp1a2 inhibited differentiation, confirming their essential roles (Fig. 7k–m). Thus, these findings suggest that p38 MAPK regulates transcriptional Na+/K+-ATPase expression in response to PD and differentiation, possibly involving other transcription factors via an Atf7-dependent manner, a mechanism likely conserved in mammals.

The budding yeast strain Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4742, widely used in aging studies, harbors three P-type ATPase genes: ENA1, ENA2, and ENA5. These genes encode proteins, each comprising 1,091 amino acids with over 97% sequence identity (Supplementary Fig. 8n). ENA1 encodes a sodium P-type ATPase critical for sodium detoxification and serves as a direct downstream target of the bZip transcription factor SKO1 (the yeast homolog of ATF-7) in response to osmotic stress46,47. Similar to observations in C. elegans and mammals, ENA1 expression is significantly higher than that of ENA2 and ENA5, and ENA2 expression is minimal under standard potassium conditions but is induced by either Hog1 (the yeast homolog of p38) or low potassium (Fig. 7n). Additionally, 18 strongly interacting transcription factors, which are Sko1-mediated downstream targets, are upregulated by Hog1 and likely contribute to regulating P-type ATPase gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 8l, m). Notably, wild type yeast cells exhibit larger cell sizes in water and slightly reduced sizes in hyperosmotic conditions (50 mM NaCl + 300 mM KCl). However, deletion of HOG1 or ENA1,2,5 abolishes these cell size changes, suggesting that Hog1 and the P-type ATPases play a role in modulating yeast cell size (Fig. 7m and Supplementary Fig. 8o). These alterations could also activate p38 signaling through osmotic, mechanical, or membrane tension sensors48,49. Collectively, our results indicate that p38-mediated P-type ATPases regulation as a highly conserved mechanism across evolution orchestrates cellular response to low potassium conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8p).

Discussion

The p38 MAPK pathway, conserved across species, orchestrates cellular responses to diverse stress and differentiation10,11. Here, we unveil its pivotal role in PD defense, a function previously unrecognized. In C. elegans, a p38–ATF-7–CATP-3 axis boosts survival under PD by coordinating potassium homeostasis across tissues. Unlike localized Na+/K+-ATPase regulation, this systemic mechanism integrates ASI neuron sensing with hypodermal CATP-3 upregulation, distinct from osmotic control (Supplementary Fig. 7g). Under normal conditions, catp-3 and p38–ATF-7 expression in the hypodermis remain low (Supplementary Figs. 4, 6), possibly to avoid metabolic costs given Na+/K+-ATPase’s high ATP consumption, but PD triggers a rapid, nonautonomous response that also activates innate immunity genes (Fig. 3). Organisms can thus survive extended potassium deprivation, a critical adaptation in potassium-scarce environments. This discovery redefines potassium homeostasis as an organism-wide, stress-responsive network, contrasting with prior cell-centric models50.

This cross-tissue coordination echoes in mammals. In mouse C2C12 myoblasts, p38 drives Atp1a2 expression to sustain muscle differentiation under PD (Fig. 7i), mirroring C. elegans’s p38–CATP-3 reliance51–53. While C. elegans lacks a complex ion exchange organ comparable to the kidneys, its neuronal-epithelial axis suggests a primitive blueprint for potassium defense, potentially conserved across evolution. However, mammalian systems likely integrate additional layers–such as renal feedback–absent in worms, warranting further exploration. In yeast, neither Hog1 nor the three P-type ATPases appear to be required for growth or PD survival, likely reflecting the limited cell size dynamics of yeast compared with excitable cells in higher organisms, and/or compensation by redundant regulatory mechanisms. However, the role of p38-mediated P-type ATPase expression in regulating cell size appears to be conserved, a finding that was previously unrevealed.

Potassium’s role extends beyond PD to dietary restriction or dietary deprivation, where C. elegans survives over 40 days via p38 signaling12,16. We show potassium is a limiting factor in dietary deprivation survival, with p38 and K+ interdependence evident in sek-1 mutants’ rapid death under deprivation (Fig. 1). Potassium outperforms sodium in extending lifespan, independent of pH or osmotic stress (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1), highlighting its specificity. Aging compounds this dynamic: p38 signaling declines with age in excitable tissues36,54,55 (Fig. 5), potentially impairing Na+/K+-ATPase function and disrupting muscle and neuronal integrity–processes tied to neurodegenerative and cardiovascular diseases10,45. Our findings position potassium homeostasis as a nexus of immunity, longevity, and aging, with p38 as a linchpin. In humans, where hypokalemia remains a clinical challenge, this pathway offers a framework to probe ion dysregulation in aging and disease. Future studies could target p38 to restore potassium balance, potentially mitigating neurological decline or metabolic disorders.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. (1) The hypodermal p38–ATF-7–CATP-3 axis exerts a critical role in PD survival in C. elegans. However, direct evidence regarding how p38 and PD influence hypodermal cell dynamics remains absent. (2) The nonautonomous regulation of catp-3 expression by p38–ATF-7 signaling appears to be dependent on developmental timing. The mechanisms underlying this temporal specificity are not yet characterized. (3) While p38 and potassium are essential for the differentiation of C2C12 muscle cells, these factors appear dispensable for muscle function in C. elegans. This discrepancy suggests a potential evolutionary or context-specific divergence in regulatory mechanisms, which remains unexplored and merits further investigation. (4) The p38–ATF-7 signaling pathway likely transcriptionally regulates Na+/K+-ATPase expression across species, from yeast to mammals. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this regulation are not fully elucidated. Future studies using C2C12 or human skeletal myoblast cells could provide deeper insights into the transcriptional and molecular interactions involved.

Methods

Worms

Worms were maintained at 20 °C on standard nematode growth medium (NGM) agar plates seeded with Escherichia coli OP50-1 (a streptomycin-resistant strain for worm food). Strains obtained from other sources were outcrossed to the laboratory N2 strain as indicated in Supplementary Table 1. The sek-1 transgenic strains were a gift of Nick Bishop and Leonard Guarente (MIT).

Microbe Strains

E. coli OP50-1 was cultured overnight in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with 10 mg/L streptomycin at 37 °C. Following incubation, 1 mL of the liquid culture was seeded onto standard nematode growth medium (NGM) plates (100 mm × 15 mm petri dishes) and allowed to grow for 2 days at room temperature. For RNAi feeding experiments, HT115 bacteria harboring the pL4440 plasmid were used as an empty vector (EV) control. These RNAi cultures were grown overnight at 37 °C in 50 mL conical tubes containing 5 mL of LB medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL carbenicillin. After overnight incubation, the cultures were diluted 1:5 in LB medium containing carbenicillin to allow for re-entry into the logarithmic growth phase ( ~ 6 h). Subsequently, the cultures were centrifuged at 3,900 g for 10 minutes and concentrated to a final volume of 3 mL in LB medium supplemented with 0.2 g/L (1 mM) IPTG before being seeded onto NGM plates containing 50 μg/mL carbenicillin and 0.2 g/L IPTG. Growth-arrested bacteria for liquid survival assays experiments were prepared according to the protocol described as described previously12. Specifically, OP50-1 bacteria were cultured in 500 mL of LB medium supplemented with 10 mg/L streptomycin in a 2 L flask overnight at 37 °C. The cultures were then centrifuged at 3900 g for 10 minutes, and the bacterial pellets were resuspended in S basal (1 × SB solution: 5.85 g/L NaCl, 1 g/L K2HPO4, 6 g/L KH2PO4, autoclaved) supplemented with 5 mg/L cholesterol, 50 mg/L ampicillin, 10 mg/L kanamycin, 1 mg/L tetracycline, and 50 mg/L nystatin to an OD600 of 3. The bacterial suspension was maintained at room temperature for ~7 h before being stored at 4 °C for up to one week. For pathogenesis experiments a single colony of P. aeruginosa PA14 was cultured in LB without antibiotics at 37 °C.

Worm transgenic strain construction

Extrachromosomal arrays were generated by injecting transgene DNA (10 ng/mL) into the gonads of young adult animals, either with or without the rol-6 marker (pRF4) at 50 ng/mL. Unless otherwise noted, transgene DNA was created using a fusion PCR method. The catp-3p::GFP transcriptional fusion was produced by fusion PCR, amplifying a 3797 bp catp-3 promoter and a 1,964 bp GFP + 3’UTR from vector pPD95.67. The catp-3::GFP translational fusion was constructed by PCR amplification of a 10,010 bp fragment from catp-3 fosmid transgenes (C09H5.2, TransgeneOme), which included a 3847 bp catp-3 promoter, a 3,700 bp catp-3 genomic region, a 1044 bp 2xTY1::EGFP::3xFlag sequence, and a 1419 bp 3’UTR. The semo-1p::catp-3, myo-3p::catp-3 and gpa-4p::catp-3 translational fusions were generated by PCR fusion. This involved amplifying a 2983 bp semo-1 promoter, 2991 bp myo-3 promoter, and 2,903 bp gpa-4 promoter, respectively, each fused with a 4116 bp catp-3 gDNA including the 3’UTR from worm genomic DNA. The catp-3(syb843) mutation was introduced by CRISPR-Cas9 knock-in of an 858 bp mCherry sequence before the termination codon of catp-3, performed by SunyBiotech Co., Ltd (Fuzhou, China).

Liquid survival assays

All worms were maintained at 20 °C for at least two generations prior to the initiation of the assays. Synchronized populations of L1-stage animals were obtained through hypochlorite treatment and subsequently allowed to develop at 20 °C on standard NGM plates seeded with OP50-1. After two days, the animals were transferred to standard NGM agar plates supplemented with 5 mg/L cholesterol, 100 mg/L FUdR, and an antibiotic cocktail (50 mg/L ampicillin, 10 mg/L kanamycin, 1 mg/L tetracycline, 50 mg/L nystatin), and seeded with cold- and antibiotic-treated OP50-112. Three days later, the day 3 adult animals were washed twice in 100 mM NaCl (PD) or the indicated solution and then transferred to individual wells of a 96-well microplate containing 150 μL of 100 mM NaCl or the indicated solution, supplemented with 5 mg/L cholesterol, 20 mg/L FUdR, and the antibiotic cocktail but without OP50-1. Each well contained 10–20 worms, and the plates were maintained at 20 °C in the dark without shaking. Worms exhibiting pallor, a transparent appearance, a straight or rigid body, or complete immobility were classified as dead. Unless otherwise indicated, all worm PD survival assays reported in this study were performed using this method.

To exclude the effects of other NGM components, a potassium-free NGM solution was prepared using 100 mM NaCl, 25 mM sodium phosphate buffer (SPB: 2.38 g/L NaH2PO4 and 735 mg/L Na2HPO4) and supplemented with 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, 2.5 g/L peptone, 5 mg/L cholesterol, 100 mg/L FUdR, and the same antibiotic cocktail. Synchronized worms were washed off standard NGM plates with this solution, rinsed twice, and transferred to the potassium-free NGM media for subsequent assays.

Solid PD assays with OP50-1

To establish solid PD conditions with OP50-1, potassium was removed from both the agar plates and the bacterial food source. First, agar plates were prepared in a manner similar to standard NGM plates, except that NaCl and potassium phosphate buffer were replaced with either 100 mM NaCl, 100 mM KCl, or NGM solution (51 mM NaCl, 25 mM potassium phosphate buffer, KPB). All plates were supplemented with 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, 5 mg/L cholesterol, 100 mg/L FUdR, and an antibiotic cocktail consisting of 50 mg/L ampicillin, 10 mg/L kanamycin, 1 mg/L tetracycline, and 50 mg/L nystatin. Second, overnight cultures of OP50-1 were washed twice with distilled water and resuspended in water containing 5 mg/L cholesterol and the same antibiotic cocktail at an optical density of OD600 = 3 in 50-mL conical tubes. The bacterial suspensions were maintained at room temperature for 7 h and subsequently stored at 4 °C for one week. Third, immediately prior to plate seeding, the supernatant was removed and bacteria were resuspended in 3 ml of fresh 100 mM NaCl, 100 mM KCl, or NGM solution (51 mM NaCl, 25 mM KPB) containing the same antibiotic regimen and 100 mg/L FUdR. This step generated a concentrated OP50-1 suspension (OD600 ≈ 50), which was then seeded onto the corresponding salt agar plates. Finally, synchronized worms at different developmental stages were washed off standard NGM plates using the corresponding 100 mM NaCl, 100 mM KCl, or NGM solution. After two rapid washes with the same solution, animals were transferred onto the matched salt agar plates for subsequent assays.

Worm microscopy and image analysis

For fluorescence analysis, animals were immobilized for 5 minutes in M9 buffer with 0.06% tetramisole, mounted on 2% agarose pads on glass slides, and imaged using ZEN 2012 software on a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 fluorescence microscope equipped with a Zeiss AxioCam HRc high resolution 13 MP color CCD microscope camera. Fluorescence intensity was quantified with NIH ImageJ software (Version 1.50). For assessments of movement behavior, body shape, muscle morphology, and other phenotypes, animals were observed without immobilization under the specified conditions. Videos of the animals’ status were recorded using an Olympus IX51 inverted microscope equipped with fluorescence and phase contrast capabilities, utilizing cellSens standard 1.12 software.

Pathogenesis assays

The standard slow-killing assays (small lawn) using P. aeruginosa PA14 were performed as described previously12. Briefly, single PA14 colonies were inoculated into 3 ml LB broth and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Subsequently, 5 µL of the overnight culture was spotted at the center of 35-mm NGM agar plates supplemented with 20 mg/L FUdR. For the fast-killing assay (big lawn), the agar plate composition mirrored that of the slow-killing assay, but 5 µL of PA14 culture was spotted onto each plate and spread to cover the entire agar surface, as previously reported27. The seeded plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C, then equilibrated at room temperature overnight. ~40 animals were transferred to each prepared plate, with five plates evaluated per strain. The assays were conducted at 20 °C, and animals unresponsive to gentle prodding with a platinum wire were recorded as deceased.

Pharyngeal pumping assay

Pharyngeal pumping was quantified by counting the number of pharyngeal contractions under a Zeiss Stereo Discovery V12 microscope at 100× magnification. To minimize variability caused by irregular pumping rhythms, contractions were recorded over a continuous 3-min interval for each worm. For each strain and each treatment, 15 healthy worms were analyzed in a blinded manner.

EMS mutagenesis screening assay

Worm genetic screen procedure based on the classical method described previously56. Synchronized L4-stage sek-1(km4) worms are collected and suspended in a 0.05 M ethyl methane sulphonate (EMS) solution, followed by gentle shaking at 20 °C for 4 h to induce random mutations. After the incubation, the mutagenesis is terminated by treating the worms with 1% sodium thiosulfate, and the EMS is thoroughly removed through multiple washes. The worms are then transferred to NGM plates for a 3-days recovery and egg-laying, and then bleached to obtain a synchronized population of F2 eggs. The F2 generation was allowed to hatch and develop on agar plates containing 100 mM NaCl, seeded with E. coli OP50-1 as a food source. This selective step aimed to identify worms with altered potassium sensitivity. A subset of 120 L4-stage worms was isolated for further analysis. These worms were transferred to individual wells of a 24-well plate and allowed to lay eggs. From these, 58 adult worms that successfully laid eggs were selected for subsequent analyses. These 58 F2 adult worms were subjected to a standard liquid survival assay in 100 mM NaCl. Worms exhibiting a rescue phenotype, indicated by enhanced survival under PD conditions, were identified. These rescued worms underwent a second round of the liquid assay to measure survival duration. The six longest-surviving candidate mutants were then selected for further genetic mapping using deep sequencing, following previously established methods57. The ld09 allele introduces a premature stop codon (GAA → TAA), producing a truncated protein that lacks the C-terminal bZIP DNA-binding and leucine-zipper dimerization domains, predicting molecular null. The ld18 allele is a well-established loss-of-function mutation reported in previous studies22. The ld37 allele contains a 158-bp deletion within the coding region, which removes part of the functional domain and is also considered a loss-of-function allele. The ld42 and ld44 alleles encode conservative hydrophobic substitutions within the leucine zipper (Leu→Phe and Ala→Val, respectively), which likely perturb hydrophobic-core packing and affect DNA binding. Finally, the ld53 allele introduces a premature stop codon (CAA → TAA) upstream of the C-terminal bZIP domain, eliminating both DNA-binding and dimerization domain, thereby predicting null.

Worm RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) samples preparation

For solid agar plate assay, ~2000 synchronized L4-stage wild type (N2) animals were washed twice in either 100 mM NaCl, 100 mM KCl, or NGM solution (51 mM NaCl, 25 mM KPB) and then transferred to agar plates supplemented with 100 mM NaCl, 100 mM KCl, or NGM solution (51 mM NaCl, 25 mM KPB). These plates were seeded with OP50-1 bacteria that had been treated with the corresponding solutions. After 4 h, the animals were collected and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Genes are upregulated (PD up) or downregulated (PD down) under PD (100 mM NaCl) conditions were identified by comparison with both the 100 mM KCl and NGM conditions in N2 animals (100 mM NaCl vs 100 mM KCl & 100 mM NaCl vs NGM).

For liquid assay, ~1000 synchronized day 1 adult N2 or pmk-1(km25) animals were quickly washed twice in either 100 mM NaCl or 100 mM KCl solution and then transferred to 100 mM NaCl or 100 mM KCl solution without OP50-1. After 4 h, the animals were collected and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

RNA-seq

In this study, total RNA was extracted and purified from all samples using M5 HiPer Universal Plus RNA Mini Kit (MF167, Mei5 Biotech, Beijing, China). All RNA samples were sent to Novogene Co., Ltd (Beijing, China) for quality control (Sample QC). After RNA library preparation, cDNA libraries are sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq platforms using a paired-end 150 bp sequencing strategy. ~6 Gb of clean data per sample was obtained, with a uniquely mapped read rate exceeding 75%. Three biological replicates were included.

Processing and analysis of RNA-Seq data

FASTQ files were aligned to C. elegans WBcel269, Mus musculus GRCm39 or Saccharomyces cerevisiae R64-1-1.dna.toplevel reference genome using STAR (version 2.7.1a) with a 2-pass alignment procedure58. Gene and transcript quantification was carried out using RSEM (version 1.3.3)59. Differential expression analysis was conducted in R (version 4.3.1) using the DESeq2 package (version 1.40.2)60. Differentially expressed genes were defined with an adjusted P (false discovery rate, FDR) < 0.05.

GO term and pathway annotation

The WormBase enrichment suite was used for tissue analysis, while DAVID (version 2021) functional annotations were employed for GO biological process (GOTERM_BP_DIRECT) and InterPro protein domain enrichment analysis. Heatmaps were generated from the counts using Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus). Genes network graphs were created using STRING.

PD-mediated genes analysis

Heatmaps and gene expression cluster plots were generated using Morpheus (Broad Institute). Functional annotations, including biological processes and human ortholog matching, were retrieved from WormBase (build WS269). The WormBase enrichment suite (build WS289) was used for tissue enrichment analysis, while DAVID functional annotations (version v2022q1) were employed for Gene Ontology and InterPro protein domain enrichment analysis61. InterPro categorizes sequences at the superfamily, family, and subfamily levels and predicts the presence of functional domains and important sites62. By searching for specific transcription factor binding sites within the promoter region of genes [-1000, +100] using the Gene Transcription Regulation Database (GTRD) (v21.12)63, we identified a total of 271 transcription factors and their target genes. We then conducted an over-representation analysis on the PD-mediated gene sets. This analysis mapped the PD-mediated genes to the target genes of the 271 transcription factors and identified statistically significant enrichment of transcription factors using a custom model on the g:Profiler platform64.

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)

For each treatment, ~200 synchronized animals were collected, and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (T9424, Sigma-Aldrich), and purified with Direct-zol™ RNA Kits (R2050, Zymo Research). First-strand cDNA was synthesized in duplicate from each sample (11752050, Invitogen First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix). SYBR green (11760500, Invitogen) was used to perform qRT-PCR (ABI 7900). At least three biological replicates were examined for each sample. Gene expression fold change was calculated using the ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are as follows: catp-3 Forward: 5′-TCA TAT AAC AGC GCA AGA CC-3′ and catp-3 Reverse: 5′-CCA TCC AAT TGT GTT GAT TGC-3′; catp-2 Forward: 5′-ACA ACA GTT ATA ATA GTG GCC G-3′ and catp-2 Reverse: 5′-GAG CTG GAT TTG AAG TGA TCG-3′; C17H12.8 Forward: 5′-CAT GTT GAA CGA TTT GCG A-3′ and C17H12.8 Reverse: 5′-GCA ATC GAC AGT GAA ATT CTC-3′; K08D8.5 Forward: 5′-TTT ATG CGA CTG CTG TTG C-3′ and K08D8.5 Reverse: 5′-CTC ACG TTT GCA TTG TAT GGA-3′; sysm-1 Forward: 5′-TAC ACT GCT TCA GAG TCG TG-3′ and sysm-1 Reverse: 5′-CGA CAA CCA CTT CTA ACA TCT G-3′; tba-1 Forward: 5′-CCT TCG TTC ACT GGT ACG TC-3′ and tba-1 Reverse: CAG CCA AGT CTT CAC GAG CC-3′; lgg-1 Forward: 5′-ACC CAG ACC GTA TTC CAG TG-3′ and lgg-1 Reverse: 5′-ACG AAG TTG GAT GCG TTT TC-3′; atgl-1 Forward: 5′-GAT CGA CCG ATG ATT TAT CGA G-3′ and atgl-1 Reverse: 5′-GAGCCAATCCACATTTGGTC-3′; sod-3 Forward: 5′-ATG GAC ACT ATT AAG CGC GA-3′ and sod-3 Reverse: 5′-GCC TTG AAC CGC AAT AGT G-3′; pha-4 Forward: 5′-TCA AAG AGG AGC CAG AGT CG-3’ and pha-4 Reverse: 5′-ACA CTT GAA GCA GCC GAC GA-3’.

Gene tissue expression analysis

The heatmap shows relative gene expression (dot color) and proportion (dot size) of catp-3 and its paralogs across all neurons and other tissues in L4 stage wild type worms. This heatmap was generated from C. elegans Neuronal Gene Expression Network (CeNGEN)31. The proportion is percentage of cells expressing the gene within each neuron cluster. This result uses thresholded data from threshold 2, and therefore only contains data from neuronal cell types. The scaled TPM (transcripts per million) expression value is the z-score for the gene in that neuron. The z-score represents how many standard deviations the TPM expression of a given gene in a given neuron is from the mean TPM for that gene. It is calculated by subtracting the mean TPM expression for the gene across all neurons from the TPM expression of the gene in the given neuron, then dividing by the standard deviation of TPM values for that gene. For the purpose of identifying alignment lengths, protein sequences were obtained from the NCBI RefSeq database release 207. In scenarios where multiple isoforms were available, the longest isoform was utilized. Alignments of the sequences were carried out via EMBOSS water software utilizing the BLOSUM62 matrix and retaining all default configurations.

Mammalian cells

HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C with controlled moisture. C2C12 mouse myoblast cell line was purchased from the American type culture collection (ATCC, CRL-1772). Cells were cultured and maintained in growth medium (GM) containing high glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2. GM was replaced every two-days and cells were subcultured when they reached 70%-90% of confluence. Cells were seeded at 6-well plates and proliferated for 2 days to reach 50%-60% confluency in GM. The culture mediums were replaced with differentiation medium (DM) containing the DMEM medium supplemented with 2% horse serum (16050130, Gibco). The DM medium was replaced every 24 h to stimulate myotube formation.

Mammalian cell culture, vector construction, and transfection

The CDS of capt-3 was codon-optimized and synthesized by GenScript Biotech Corp. (Jiangsu, China), and then cloned into pcDNA3.1(+) vector. Mammalian cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C with controlled moisture. HEK293T cells were transfected using Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent Kit (Invitrogen). Plasmids for transfection were extracted from E. coli (DH5α) using QIAGEN Plasmid Mini Kit (Qiagen), and 2 μg plasmid DNA was added into each well of 6-well plates (Nunc) containing the cells (70%–80% confluent). The ratio of plasmids pcDNA3.1(+)-capt-3 and pEGFP-C1 for co-expression of capt-3 and EGFP was 10:1. To confirm that the cells were successfully transfected, green fluorescent signals were examined using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Lecia DMi8 Inverted Microscope) before patch clamp.

Whole-cell patch-clamp recording

The whole-cell patch-clamp experiments were performed using an Axopatch-200B patch-clamp setup (Axon Instruments, CA, USA) with a Digitata1550 digitizer (Axon Instruments, CA, USA). Clampex10.7 software (Axon Instruments, CA, USA) was used for data acquisition and Clampfit 10.7 was used for data analysis. Na+/K+ pump currents (Ip) were recorded in HEK293T cells transfected with capt-3 as previously described65. The bath solution contained 144 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM KCl, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 2 mM BaCl2, 5 mM NiCl2, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4 (adjusted with NaOH); the pipette solution contained 110 mM CsCl, 40 mM NaCl, 10 mM NaOH, 3 mM MgCl2, 6 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES, 10 mM Mg-ATP, pH 7.4 (adjusted with CsOH). The osmolarity was adjusted to 315 mOsm with glucose. The voltage was held at 0 mV while recording, and 1 mM ouabain (4995, Sigma-Aldrich) was added into the bath solution as indicated.

Microscopy analysis of C2C12 cell morphology

Cell morphology was recorded using an Olympus IX51 inverted microscope equipped with fluorescence and phase contrast capabilities, utilizing cellSens standard 1.12 software.

Generation of Mapk14−/− C2C12 cells

C2C12 Mapk14 knockout cells were constructed using the CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing system. The GenCrispr NLS-Cas9-EGFP Nuclease, which harbored the green fluorescent protein (GFP) sequence for visual verification of transfection (Z03393; Genscript, China), was transfected into C2C12 cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 6-well plates. Clones were then isolated in accordance with the recommended protocol for the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Briefly, at 72 h after transfection, cells that successfully transfection of the Mapk14 sgRNA/Cas9 were screened by Fluorescence Activating Cell Sorter (FACSmelody, BD biosciences, America) and plated at ∼1 cell/well in 96-well plates. Eight clones were further subcultured until reaching high confluence in 6-well plates. Stable C2C12 cell lines showing successful knockout of Mapk14 were confirmed by DNA sequencing using forward and reverse primers (forward 5′-TGG GGC CTT TAC TTT GAC CA-3′ and reverse 5′-CCT CGC GAT GAT CTG ACA GC-3′).

siRNA transfection in C2C12 cells

siRNA was synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China), and the siRNA sequences of each gene are as follows: Mapk14 sense 5′-GGU CAC UGG AGG AAU UCA ATT-3′ and Mapk14 antisense 5′-UUG AAU UCC UCC AGU GAC CTT-3′; Atp1a2 sense 5′-GAG ACC CGC AAU AUC UGU UUC TT-3′ and Atp1a2 antisense 5′-GAA ACA GAU AUU GCG GGU CUC TT-3′; NC (negative control) sense 5′-UUC UCC GAA CGU GUC ACG UTT-3′ and NC antisense 5′-ACG UGA CAC GUU CGG AGA ATT-3′. At this time, the C2C12 cells were transfected with siRNA for the first time. 2 μL Lipofectamine 2000 and 40 pmol siRNA were diluted and mixed using Opti-MEM in each well of 24 plates. After gently mixing the mixture, let it stand for 20 min, replace the new culture medium for C2C12, and add the siRNA-lipo2000 mixture so that the volume of each well is 500 μL. The cells were cultured for 18 h and siRNA was transfected again using the same method. After 12 h, the cell density reached about 90%. The growth medium containing siRNA was removed, and the cells were cultured using DM. DM was replaced every 2 days, and cell morphology changes were continuously recorded.

The wild type yeast strain Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4742 (MATα his3Δ1 leu2Δ0 lys2Δ0 ura3Δ0) and its derivative single-gene deletion mutant strains, all in the BY4742 genetic background, were procured from Horizon Discovery (Cambridge, UK). Twelve to twenty-four colonies were screened by PCR using two primer pairs: Pair 1 (forward: 5′-AGA GCA GTA TTC CCG AAT TAT-3′, reverse: 5′-TGA CGA AGC TCA ATT AGT CG-3′) and Pair 2 (forward: 5′-AGG TCT TAG AGC AGT ATT CCC-3′, reverse: 5′-GCA TGG CAA ACG AGA TTA TC-3′). Successful gene disruptions were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The pML104 vector was subsequently removed via counter-selection on 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA) medium, and plasmid loss was verified by PCR.

Yeast RNA-Seq analysis

Yeast strains (BY4747 and hog1Δ), preserved at -80 °C, were streaked onto YPD agar plates and incubated at 30 °C for 2 days or until visible colonies appeared. Single colonies were picked and inoculated into 1.0 mL of YPD liquid medium, and cultured for 2 days at 30 °C in a shaking incubator at 220 rpm. The cultures were diluted 10-fold with water and stored at 4 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, 5 µL of the diluted culture was inoculated into 1.0 mL of SD medium supplemented with either 1 mM or 7 mM KCl and incubated at 30 °C for 2 days in a shaking incubator at 220 rpm67. For each sample, ~1 mL of yeast culture was collected and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage.

Yeast cell imaging

A 5 μL aliquot of water-diluted yeast culture was inoculated into 1.0 mL of YPD liquid medium and incubated for 2 days at 30 °C in a shaking incubator at 220 rpm. The resulting culture was washed three times with water and resuspended in one of the following solutions: water, 50 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaCl + 5 mM KCl, or 50 mM NaCl + 300 mM KCl. The suspensions were stored at 4 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, a small volume of each cell suspension was placed on a clean glass slide, spread evenly, and sealed with a coverslip. Imaging was performed using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope in brightfield mode with a 100× oil immersion objective lens (NA 1.4). Images were acquired using the LAS X software and saved in uncompressed TIFF format. To avoid imaging bias, all images were captured under identical light intensity and exposure settings. Image analysis was conducted using ImageJ (1.54e) software. The following processing steps were applied sequentially: Process → Binary → Make Binary → Dilate → Fill Holes → Watershed, to segment individual cells. Particles with an area between 10–50 μm² and a circularity of 0.7–1.0 were selected for quantitative analysis. The ggridges plots were generated using OmicStudio (https://www.omicstudio.cn, version V1.0) to visualize the distribution of cell size measurements across the indicated experimental groups. Single-cell size data were imported into OmicStudio and displayed as ridge density plots based on kernel density estimation. Each ridge represents the probability density distribution of cell size for one group, with ridge transparency (alpha) set to 0.6, allowing comparison of distribution patterns, dispersion, and shifts in central tendency between groups.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The significance of comparisons between two groups was determined by unpaired two-tailed t tests as indicated. Differences among means were analyzed using one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0). When ANOVA analysis revealed significant differences, pair-wise comparisons were performed using Dunnett’s test or Tukey post-hoc test as indicated. The P values (log-rank test) for lifespan comparison, mean and median lifespan were determined using JMP software (Pro 17.0.0). All experiments were repeated independently at least three times. All lifespan results shown in figure panels are representative of three independent experiments, with detailed in Supplementary Data 1. Information about statistical tests and n numbers is provided in the respective figures and figure legends. Sample sizes were chosen based on the reproducibility of the experiments and are consistent with standard practices in the field.
