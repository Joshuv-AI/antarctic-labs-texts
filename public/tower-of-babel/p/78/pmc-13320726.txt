Ischial Pressure Ulcer Reconstruction: A Critical Review of the Paradigm Shift From Wound Coverage to Functional Restoration
Authors: Chengying Huang, Shaohua Wang, Jielong Lin, Shunbin Wang, Zhaorong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIschial pressure ulcers (IPUs), severe complications in patients with prolonged immobility, are challenging due to high recurrence. Moving beyond anatomical closure, the field now prioritises functional‐integrated reconstruction. This paradigm emphasises radical debridement and the transfer of well‐vascularized perforator flaps to obliterate dead space, cover wounds, and restore protective sensation, aiming to break the recurrence cycle and restore patient autonomy. This review critically synthesises contemporary advances driving this shift. It details the development of precision and minimally invasive perforator flap techniques, including sensory nerve coaptation and chimeric designs. The integration of multimodal perioperative strategies, such as antibiotic‐loaded bone cement and fluorescence angiography, is discussed. The adjunctive role of traditional Chinese medicine and the exploratory potential of regenerative approaches (stem cells, 3D‐bioprinting) are considered. Finally, practical recommendations for implementing this holistic approach are provided, along with a clinical flowchart to guide staged management. By consolidating these advances, this review provides a framework to transition IPU management from simple wound healing towards comprehensive functional and social rehabilitation.Keywords: ischial pressure ulcer, multidisciplinary care, paradigm shift, perforator flap, reconstruction, sensory recovery

Introduction

Ischial pressure ulcers (IPUs) represent a severe and recalcitrant complication in patients who are bedridden or dependent on wheelchairs. Among individuals with long‐term immobilisation due to spinal cord injuries or severe neuromuscular disorders, the ischial tuberosity is the second most common site for pressure injury development, following the sacrum, with a global reported prevalence ranging from 3.4% to 32.4% [1, 2]. The underlying pathophysiology stems from the combined effects of sustained mechanical pressure, shear forces, and frictional damage, culminating in ischemic tissue necrosis [3, 4]. As the primary weight‐bearing point in the seated position, the unique anatomical and biomechanical characteristics of this region predispose any developing ulcer to progress rapidly into a deep cavity, often extending to the bone and resulting in a complex, hard‐to‐heal wound. Systemic comorbidities frequently accompany these lesions, including neurotrophic disorders post‐spinal cord injury, malnutrition, anaemia, and hypoalbuminemia [5, 6, 7]. More alarmingly, IPUs are plagued by high postoperative recurrence rates, reported between 15% and 30%, underscoring the limitations of surgical interventions focused solely on wound closure [8, 9, 10]. These factors collectively inflict significant patient suffering and risk of severe infection, lead to protracted treatment courses, and cause exponential growth in healthcare costs, imposing a recurring physical and psychological burden on patients and their families [11, 12]. Consequently, IPUs remain a pressing and unresolved challenge in both clinical practice and public health.

The therapeutic approach to IPUs has evolved significantly from passive expectancy of healing to proactive surgical reconstruction. Early strategies, exemplified by random‐pattern flaps and the gluteus maximus myocutaneous flap, achieved wound coverage often at the expense of critical hip extension function [13, 14]. Advances in the understanding of perforator vasculature anatomy have since elevated the treatment goal from mere “wound closure” to “holistic reconstruction emphasizing both function and form” [15, 16, 17]. Modern management paradigms emphasise radical debridement of necrotic tissue followed by single‐stage reconstruction using well‐vascularized tissue flaps. As discussed throughout this review, this approach aims to simultaneously obliterate dead space, provide durable surface coverage, and, where possible, restore protective sensation, thereby disrupting the vicious cycle of recurrence [1, 18].

This review aims to critically delineate the latest advancements in IPU reconstruction. It will critically examine the precision application of perforator flap techniques, explore integrated multi‐modal treatment strategies, and analyse comprehensive perioperative management optimization. By synthesising these contemporary concepts and technologies, we intend to provide clinicians, particularly those in primary trauma and reconstructive surgery settings, with a reference that bridges theoretical depth and practical guidance. Ultimately, we hope to contribute to the ongoing paradigm shift in this field—from “anatomical coverage” to “functional restoration.”

The Evolution of Treatment Philosophy: From Anatomical Closure to Integrated Functional Reconstruction

Over the past decade, the treatment philosophy for IPUs has undergone a profound paradigm shift. The core objective has evolved from the singular pursuit of anatomical wound closure to a comprehensive systems‐engineering approach dedicated to integrated structural and functional reconstruction. Importantly, long‐term success is defined not merely by wound closure, but by the systematic restoration of physiological function and social participation—key to definitively breaking the “compression‐ischemia‐necrosis‐recurrence” cycle [8, 19, 20]. Contemporary management is built upon three interdependent and synergistic dimensions: the synergistic restoration of structure and function, evidence‐based multi‐layered recurrence prevention, and continuous rehabilitative integration aimed at maximising quality of life.

Synergistic Restoration of Structure and Function

The cornerstone of modern reconstructive strategy lies in addressing the anatomical defect while concurrently restoring sensory, motor, and biomechanical function. The application of sensory nerve coaptation techniques aims to re‐establish protective sensation, enabling patients to perceive pressure and pain—a “biological early‐warning system” that fundamentally alters recurrence risk. Preliminary evidence suggests this may reduce the incidence of asymptomatic pressure injuries, but data from large‐scale studies are lacking [21]. Simultaneously, the use of perforator flaps appears to provide reliable coverage while preserving the innervation and continuity of donor muscles, potentially offering a significant functional advantage over traditional myocutaneous flaps, although direct comparative studies are limited [15, 16, 22]. Furthermore, flap designs that mimic normal soft‐tissue stratification may improve pressure distribution over the ischial region. Biomechanical simulations suggest this could establish a dual preventive barrier against recurrence, but clinical validation is needed [23, 24].

Multi‐Layered Recurrence Prevention

Modern protocols emphasise proactive intervention from the local to the systemic level to forestall recurrence. The implantation of perforator flaps with axial blood supply may provide physiological reserve to resist pressure‐induced ischemia. Retrospective data suggest a potential reduction in long‐term recurrence rates, but these findings await confirmation in prospective studies [10, 25]. For the characteristic deep sinus tracts of IPUs, “anatomical packing” with well‐vascularized muscle flaps has been reported to eliminate dead space and potentially enhance local infection control, though evidence is primarily derived from small case series [26, 27]. Concurrently, systematic perioperative optimization and management of nutritional and metabolic parameters, such as serum albumin, prealbumin, and blood glucose, have been established as foundational for reducing flap complication risks and ensuring reconstructive success [7, 9].

Quality of Life and Social Reintegration as the Ultimate Endpoint

All therapeutic endeavours ultimately converge on enhancing patient quality of life and facilitating the return to societal roles. This is operationalised through the integration of enhanced recovery after surgery principles into clinical pathways to mitigate surgical stress and shorten recovery periods [28]. It is further realised via individualised, gradual sitting retraining programs based on pressure mapping systems, scientifically guiding patients back to functional sitting. Most critically, it is sustained through long‐term, consistent support provided by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) encompassing surgery, rehabilitation, nutrition, and psychology. This patient‐centered continuum of care model not only significantly improves physical and mental health scores but also enhances treatment adherence and addresses psychosocial barriers, potentially reducing postoperative recurrence risk [8, 29].

The holistic evolution of this treatment philosophy signifies that IPU reconstruction has transitioned from an isolated surgical technique to a comprehensive, multidisciplinary practice integrating principles from anatomy, biomechanics, nutrition, rehabilitation science, and psychology. It imposes new demands on surgeons, requiring a shift beyond traditional technical skill to embrace systemic clinical thinking and lifelong management capabilities focused on the patient's long‐term functional outcome. This transition is essential to truly achieve the fundamental leap from “healing a wound” to “rebuilding a life”.

Advances in Precision and Minimally Invasive Flap Techniques

The reconstruction of IPUs has entered a new era characterised by precision, minimal invasiveness, and functional optimization. This paradigm shift is propelled by revolutionary breakthroughs in vascular anatomy knowledge and a patient‐centric surgical philosophy focused on functional outcomes. Traditional techniques involving large‐scale sacrifice of muscle and skin are being progressively superseded by refined perforator‐based flap procedures. This transition is driven not only by the pursuit of maximally reliable flap perfusion but also by the goals of maximising donor‐site function preservation, minimising surgical trauma, and actively restoring protective sensation in the recipient area. Together, these principles lay a solid anatomical and physiological foundation for achieving definitive, single‐stage, and permanent reconstruction [16, 30, 31].

Visualisation‐Guided Precision Design and Planning

The precision of modern flap surgery begins with preoperative “visualisation” planning. Computed tomographic angiography (CTA) or magnetic resonance angiography enables precise mapping of perforator vessel course, calibre, and their three‐dimensional spatial relationship to bone, serving as the primary basis for individualised design [30, 32]. Utilising this imaging data, surgeons can perform “surgical simulation” preoperatively, even employing three‐dimensional (3D)‐printed anatomical models to accurately measure flap rotation arcs and pedicle length, thereby optimising the surgical plan and significantly reducing intraoperative exploration time [33, 34]. Guided by this navigation and an understanding of the “points, lines, and surfaces” of perforator vessels, highly flexible flap designs have been described in the literature. However, their advantages over conventional approaches remain to be established in comparative studies. The propeller flaps based on a single perforator can undergo wide‐angle rotation to cover laterally located defects [35, 36]. The V‐Y advancement flap, based on multiple perforators, can close midline wounds with minimal tension during advancement [37]. Furthermore, under the concept of “supermicrosurgery”, intraoperative selective thinning of flaps, removing excess adipose tissue, ensures robust blood supply while achieving an ideal thickness and texture match with the recipient site, resulting in a reconstruction that is both substantial and refined [38, 39].

Function‐Oriented Composite and Sensory Reconstruction Techniques

For the characteristic deep bone exposure or sinus tracts in the ischial region, chimeric perforator flaps offer an “all‐in‐one” solution. This technique utilises multiple branches originating from the same main vessel, which simultaneously carry skin, muscle, and fascia, forming a composite tissue flap connected at the vascular pedicle [40]. These flaps have been suggested to offer enhanced resistance to infection and improved healing potential, based on retrospective observations [40]. They enable simultaneous dead space obliteration using the muscular component and surface coverage with the skin component, achieving “obliteration and coverage” in a single stage while minimising donor‐site morbidity [16, 41]. Some comparative clinical studies have reported reductions in deep infection rates and long‐term recurrence with composite flaps [10], though these findings are derived from retrospective analyses and require prospective validation.

A further leap in technical advancement is sensory reconstruction. Represented by the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve vascularized flap, microsurgical coaptation of the flap's sensory nerve to a recipient nerve aims to restore protective sensation for pressure, pain, and temperature in the reconstructed area [31, 42]. Preliminary long‐term follow‐up data suggest that patients who successfully regain sensation may experience a reduction in recurrence risk attributable to insensate pressure, although these findings are derived from small, single‐center studies. Such patients potentially regain a “biological early‐warning system”, transforming from passive weight‐bearing subjects into active participants capable of adjusting posture and engaging in self‐protection [43, 44]. This has been described as a qualitative leap forward in both psychological and physiological rehabilitation, but large‐scale prospective validation is needed.

Innovative Strategies for Complex and Recurrent Defects

Faced with extensive wounds or unfavourable local conditions, “relay flaps” have been proposed as an innovative strategy by linking two adjacent perforator flaps in series, akin to a relay race, with the potential to extend the coverage area based on anatomical studies [16, 35, 45]. For patients with tight periwound skin, preoperative subcutaneous placement of a tissue expander in the donor area has been reported to “induce” the generation of additional healthy skin over several weeks through saline infusion. This technique may create a reservoir of ample, high‐quality tissue for subsequent flap transfer, potentially allowing for tension‐free direct closure of the donor site and purportedly preventing secondary deformity [46, 47]. Collectively, these techniques have been suggested to constitute a versatile “surgical toolbox” for managing a wide spectrum of complex defects, although clinical experience remains limited and comparative data are sparse.

Integrated Multimodal Therapy and Perioperative Optimization

Successful reconstruction of IPUs depends on a modern treatment system that integrates advanced debridement techniques, precise preoperative planning, and patient‐centered perioperative rehabilitation. This synergistic approach maximises surgical success rates and ensures durable long‐term outcomes.

Radical Debridement and Staged Infection Control Strategies

Complete eradication of infected and necrotic tissue is an absolute prerequisite for surgical success. The “En Bloc” radical debridement technique mandates the complete en masse removal of the ulcer, underlying bursa, sclerotic fascia, necrotic soft tissue, and infected bone cortex as a single three‐dimensional unit, thereby avoiding the dissemination of infectious particles that may result from piecemeal resection [26, 48]. Pathological studies indicate that margins obtained with this technique exhibit a bacterial burden significantly reduced compared to traditional sharp curettage [27].

Following radical debridement, for cases complicated by confirmed osteomyelitis or extensive bone cavities, the one‐stage application of vancomycin/tobramycin‐loaded polymethylmethacrylate bone cement has been used to serve a dual purpose. It reportedly acts as a spacer to maintain the soft‐tissue cavity while locally eluting high concentrations of antibiotics, often several hundred times the minimum inhibitory concentration, for several weeks. Available studies suggest this technique may reduce the positive rate of deep tissue bacterial cultures prior to secondary flap reconstruction, though these findings are based on retrospective data [27, 49, 50]. For wounds not amenable to immediate closure or with incompletely controlled infection, negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) has been shown to promote granulation tissue growth and reduce edema through macrostrain, while minimising the risk of cross‐infection by creating a sealed environment in clinical series [51, 52, 53]. This approach may help establish favourable local conditions for definitive flap repair, although randomised controlled trials comparing NPWT with conventional wound care in this specific context are lacking.

Intraoperative Real‐Time Navigation and Perfusion Assessment

Meticulous flap design requires equally precise surgical execution. At this critical stage, modern surgery has moved beyond reliance on visual inspection and experience alone, adopting real‐time navigation and assessment technologies centered on indocyanine green fluorescence angiography (ICGFA) [54, 55, 56]. This shift enables a “visualisation” of surgery from macroscopic anatomy to microscopic perfusion, significantly enhancing procedural accuracy and decision‐making science.

Following intravenous ICG injection, near‐infrared fluorescence imaging systems capture and display real‐time hemodynamics within tissues. In experienced hands, this allows for non‐invasive, dynamic, and quantitative assessment of arterial inflow and venous outflow at multiple critical junctures: before complete flap elevation, after transfer, and following vascular anastomosis. Consequently, it may enable precise delineation of the flap's safe harvesting boundaries, guide accurate thinning and shaping, and provide immediate verification of anastomotic patency. Proponents suggest this capability facilitates the intraoperative detection and management of thrombosis, vasospasm, or technical flaws in anastomosis, potentially minimising the risk of the catastrophic complication of postoperative vascular crisis, though evidence of improved clinical outcomes remains limited to observational studies [54, 57].

Multidisciplinary Perioperative Management and Rehabilitation

Contemporary perioperative management has evolved from a traditional model of “passive recovery” to a proactive, function‐oriented, data‐driven, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation system. Its core objective is the restoration of patient function and societal reintegration [19, 58]. The successful implementation of this system relies on a supportive ecosystem of multidisciplinary collaboration that extends beyond surgery, with a core component being a graded pressure management regimen designed to provide planned “pressure conditioning” to the transplanted flap [59, 60].

The synergy within the MDT is considered paramount. The reconstructive surgeon oversees the surgical procedure and overall healing trajectory. Rehabilitation therapists are responsible for individualised pressure management, sitting posture training, and muscle strengthening. For instance, to address core muscle atrophy in long‐term bedridden IPU patients, they may implement progressive resistance training, such as bridge exercises and side‐lying leg lifts, combined with neuromuscular electrical stimulation. Available studies suggest such interventions can improve seated balance scores, as measured by the Berg Balance Scale, though evidence specific to the IPU population is limited [61, 62]. Nutritionists ensure perioperative optimization of key nutritional parameters, providing essential metabolic support for healing [7, 63]. Psychologists and social workers address anxiety and depression, enhance treatment adherence, connect patients with community resources, and facilitate a successful return to society [64, 65]. For the prevalent depressive and anxious states among these patients, integrating cognitive‐behavioural therapy with virtual reality (VR) social scenario simulations has been proposed to aim to improve psychological status, enhance treatment adherence, and accelerate social reintegration in preliminary studies [66]. Emerging research suggests that this integrated care model may improve patients' quality‐of‐life scores and potentially enhance treatment adherence, with some studies reporting effective disruption of the “surgery‐recurrence” cycle [19, 67]. However, these findings require replication in larger, well‐designed trials. This integrated paradigm marks an elevation of the treatment goal from simple “wound closure” to a trinity of “biological healing, functional restoration, and social reintegration”. By providing continuous, longitudinal care, it fundamentally aims to break the cycle of recurrence and reshape the patient's long‐term destiny and quality of life.

Exploratory Adjunctive Role of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in Comprehensive Care

It should be noted at the outset that the evidence for TCM in this context is preliminary, derived primarily from small clinical studies and animal experiments, with high‐quality randomised controlled trials currently lacking. Beyond the modern surgical reconstructive framework, TCM, with its unique theoretical system and extensive empirical history in China, has been explored as a potential adjunct in perioperative conditioning and chronic wound management. Its application should ideally adhere to the principle of ‘integrating Chinese and Western medicine for complementary advantages’ under the guidance of experienced practitioners. In TCM theory, pressure ulcers are categorised as “bedsores”, with a core pathogenesis attributed to “deficiency in origin and excess in manifestation” resulting from prolonged immobilisation. The “deficiency in origin” has been proposed to correspond to modern concepts of malnutrition and circulatory impairment, while the “excess in manifestation” has been suggested to align with local tissue necrosis and related pathological processes [68, 69]. Accordingly, TCM management of pressure ulcers in the perioperative period involves internal and external strategies. Internal treatment seeks to supplement qi, invigorate blood circulation, expel toxins, and foster tissue regeneration. External treatment focuses on removing necrosis, promoting granulation, and creating a moist healing environment (conceptualised as “nourishing pus to grow flesh”). These approaches are intended to complement modern surgical techniques.

In exploratory clinical integration, TCM regimens have been reported to exhibit multi‐target synergistic effects. For systemic conditioning, formulas such as Buyang Huanwu Decoction that replenish qi and activate blood circulation, or injections like tanshinone, have been employed to improve microcirculation and correct anaemia, thereby potentially creating favourable conditions for surgery [70, 71]. For wound preconditioning, medicinal decoctions containing herbs such as Astragalus for fumigation and washing, or topical application of traditional ointments, have been observed to exert anti‐inflammatory and other effects, possibly providing a more suitable recipient bed for flap transplantation [72, 73]. During postoperative rehabilitation, acupuncture at specific acupoints or moxibustion has been used to regulate the spleen and stomach and alleviate pain, while combined with limb massage to prevent disuse atrophy [74, 75].

Given the current limited evidence base, incorporating TCM modalities into a multidisciplinary treatment pathway may offer adjunctive benefits, including potentially reduced complication risks, accelerated recovery, and possible lower recurrence rates. This represents a preliminary, exploratory characteristic Chinese approach to the comprehensive management of pressure ulcers that clearly warrants further investigation through rigorously designed trials [68, 74].

Innovative Biomaterials and Regenerative Approaches

The management of IPUs has entered a new phase, shifting from passive reliance on natural tissue healing to the active application of regenerative medicine and biomaterial technologies to precisely modulate the repair process. These cutting‐edge approaches serve as powerful adjuncts, employing engineered bioactive materials and cellular therapies to regulate the healing microenvironment at molecular and cellular levels. They offer therapeutic potential that extends beyond the capabilities of traditional surgical interventions for complex, refractory wounds.

Active Wound Bed Preparation and Optimization

Prior to definitive flap reconstruction, transforming an unstable wound bed plagued by infection, necrosis, and ischemia into a well‐vascularized, granulating “ideal recipient site” is paramount for surgical success. Modern strategies aim to shift from “awaiting healing” to “engineering healing”. Intelligent negative pressure wound therapy (iNPWT) represents an evolution of traditional NPWT. New‐generation NPWT systems integrate real‐time pressure sensing with feedback regulation, exudate composition monitoring, and built‐in channels for topical drug delivery. These systems not only promote granulation via precise mechanical forces but can also detect signs of infection and deliver localised antimicrobials. Some clinical studies suggest iNPWT may reduce the time from bridging therapy to flap surgery compared to standard NPWT, though the evidence base is still evolving [76, 77, 78]. Platelet‐rich plasma (PRP) delivers a high concentration of autologous growth factors to accelerate granulation. A more advanced derivative, platelet lysate (PL), is produced through freeze–thaw lysis of PRP. PL offers the advantages of sustained growth factor release, stable bioactivity, and ease of use. The application of PL has been shown to improve outcomes in flap transplantation in small animal studies and preliminary clinical reports [79, 80]. Acellular matrix scaffolds, such as acellular dermal matrix derived from porcine or human cadaveric skin, or extracellular matrix scaffolds, provide a three‐dimensional guiding framework for cell migration and angiogenesis. Beyond merely filling dead space, their biomimetic architecture can direct host cell migration, differentiation, and neovascularization, effectively inducing “in situ tissue regeneration”. These scaffolds are applicable to cases with deep tissue loss but an intact overlying skin envelope [81, 82]. Clinical experience, however, remains limited.

Bio‐Enhancement of Flap

At the core stage of flap transplantation, biotechnology is being leveraged to “functionally enhance” the flap itself, improving its survival rate and integration quality under adverse conditions like ischemia or infection. One frontier direction is the development of bioactive functionalized flaps. This involves pre‐treating a harvested conventional or perforator flap ex vivo with a “bioactive coating”. For instance, spraying the flap's fascial surface with a nanofiber membrane loaded with antibiotics or vascular endothelial growth factor can confer active resistance to infection and promote anastomosis with recipient vessels upon transfer. This technique has been validated in animal models, demonstrating improved flap survival in infected environments [83, 84]. Another avenue involves exploring the local injection or scaffold‐based delivery of adipose‐derived mesenchymal stem cells to augment anti‐inflammatory and pro‐angiogenic effects [85, 86].

Future Perspectives: Bioprinting and Engineered Tissue Constructs

More prospective explorations involve utilising “organ‐on‐a‐chip” technology to construct in vitro models that simulate the complex microenvironment of an IPU, incorporating hypoxia, mechanical stress, and specific microbial flora. These models could enable high‐throughput screening for personalised optimal treatment regimens [87]. Furthermore, 3D bioprinting technology holds the promise of using a patient's own cells as “bioink” to precisely fabricate personalised skin and subcutaneous tissue composites with pre‐formed vascular networks [88, 89]. This approach aims to achieve true “on‐demand regeneration” of both function and form. However, the translation of these technologies into widespread clinical application faces significant challenges, including standardisation, scalability, cost‐effectiveness, and the need for rigorous clinical validation.

The application of these innovative materials and biotechnologies signifies a strategic shift in reconstruction from the macroscopic “anatomical rebuilding” towards microscopic “cellular and molecular‐level regulation”. As potent synergistic tools, they empower clinicians to devise more individualised and forward‐looking comprehensive treatment plans for complex cases, ultimately improving the patient's overall recovery trajectory.

Current Evidence Landscape and Future Directions

The management of IPUs, while having achieved significant progress, now stands at a pivotal crossroads. It faces the dual imperative of requiring higher‐level evidence‐based medical support while simultaneously embracing technological innovations. The foundation of current clinical decision‐making and the trajectory of future breakthroughs collectively sketch the developmental blueprint for this field.

Clinical Consensus, Evidence Gaps, and Methodological Challenges

A clear expert consensus and foundational principles exist within the global reconstructive surgery community [10, 19, 90]. There is strong agreement that successful reconstruction relies on four critical pillars: (1) radical, tumour‐like debridement; (2) filling and coverage with well‐vascularized autologous tissue, particularly perforator flaps; (3) achieving truly tension‐free closure; and (4) implementing comprehensive and systematic perioperative management. The efficacy of these principles has been repeatedly validated through cohort studies and systematic reviews [10, 15, 91].

However, the field confronts significant evidentiary challenges. Firstly, there is a scarcity of high‐level direct comparative evidence. The current evidence base predominantly originates from retrospective case series (Level III‐IV evidence). There is a severe lack of prospective randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that directly compare different flap techniques—such as the inferior gluteal artery perforator flap and the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve flap—in terms of long‐term outcomes, including 5‐year recurrence rates, functional recovery, and quality of life. Consequently, “optimal flap selection” remains largely dependent on surgical team experience and preference rather than the highest level of evidence [10]. Secondly, heterogeneous outcome reporting impedes evidence synthesis. Significant variations exist across studies in defining “success” and in the measurement of outcome indicators, such as the choice between focusing solely on flap survival or including additional measures like sensory recovery and sitting tolerance, making meaningful meta‐analysis difficult and hindering the formulation of robust, unified recommendations [10, 15].

To address the limitations of traditional RCTs, the field is shifting towards more pragmatic evidence‐generation pathways. Real‐world studies utilising large, multicenter clinical registries and prospective cohort studies employing standardised Core Outcome Sets are becoming vital bridges, connecting high‐quality clinical observation with rigorous experimental design. These approaches provide increasingly robust and clinically relevant data to inform clinical decision‐making [15, 19].

Key Frontier Directions for Future Development

The treatment paradigm for IPUs is rapidly evolving from a “surgical art” reliant on individual skill to a “reconstructive science” integrating precision anatomy, regenerative engineering, and data intelligence. Future breakthroughs will depend on deep cross‐disciplinary integration among surgical technology, regenerative medicine, data science, and healthcare systems, manifesting in four key directions.

Technical Integration: Robotic Assistance and Intelligent Navigation

The fusion of surgical robotic systems with high‐precision preoperative imaging, such as CTA, has been proposed to offer sub‐millimetre accuracy in perforator vessel localization and dissection. Robotic arms may offer stability and precision surpassing human capability, particularly when combined with augmented reality navigation, which superimposes preoperatively planned flap outlines and vascular courses onto the surgical field in real‐time. This approach not only holds the potential to elevate surgical accuracy and safety to a new level but also significantly shortens the learning curve for surgeons, making complex perforator flap techniques more accessible [32, 33, 92]. However, clinical experience with these technologies in IPU reconstruction remains limited.

Bioengineering: Bioactive and Intelligent Flaps

Future flaps may transcend simple tissue transplants, evolving into engineered “bioactive composites”. Research directions include developing absorbable biomaterials loaded with antimicrobial peptides, growth factors, or exosomes, and integrating them with flaps to confer active infection resistance and pro‐angiogenic properties [93, 94, 95]. Another avenue is employing 3D bioprinting technology to use a patient's own cells as “bioink” to fabricate personalised soft tissue flaps with pre‐formed vascular networks, aiming for genuine “on‐demand regeneration” [33, 34]. These approaches remain largely investigational, and significant technical hurdles must be overcome before clinical translation.

Personalised Prediction: Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Big‐Data Prognostic Modelling

Leveraging AI, particularly deep learning algorithms, to analyse vast clinical datasets, including medical imaging, genomic information, metabolic markers, and electronic health records, could facilitate the construction of individualised prognostic prediction models [96, 97]. Such models might predict potential complication risks, expected healing timelines, and even long‐term recurrence probabilities for different reconstructive options preoperatively, thereby assisting surgeons and patients in making optimal, data‐driven decisions. Prospective validation of these models is awaited.

Dissemination and Access: Technical Standardisation and Tele‐Collaboration Networks

To ensure broader patient access to advanced techniques, promoting standardisation and accessibility is essential. This involves creating standardised teaching protocols for reliable and relatively straightforward procedures like the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve flap, using high‐fidelity surgical simulators and VR training systems [98, 99, 100]. Simultaneously, leveraging 5G communication and remote surgical guidance platforms to establish “hub‐and‐spoke” collaboration models could enable experts at top‐tier institutions to guide surgeons in primary care settings through critical procedural steps in real‐time, potentially elevating the standard of care across the healthcare network [100, 101, 102].

In summary, the future of IPU treatment is being shaped by the convergence of precision surgery, regenerative medicine, data science, and systems engineering. The present mission is to adhere to consensus‐validated principles while prudently evaluating and integrating emerging technologies. Future success will be defined by achieving long‐term, high‐quality functional life for patients through precise, interdisciplinary intervention.

Practical Guidelines for Clinical Implementation

For surgeons practicing in primary care hospitals or emergency surgical settings, translating advanced research findings into safe and feasible clinical practice is particularly critical, given the constraints of limited medical resources and the high‐risk patient population. The following core recommendations aim to establish a robust and actionable treatment framework:

Mastery of Core Reconstructive Techniques in Resource‐Limited Settings

Among the wide array of flap techniques, primary care physicians should prioritise mastering one or two types of flaps that are reliable in design, consistent in blood supply, and straightforward to harvest. These should serve as the “primary tools” for managing the majority of cases. In this regard, the posterior femoral cutaneous neurovascular flap is a highly valuable option [35, 103]. Its advantages are multifold. First, its anatomy is consistent. Its blood supply relies on the rich vascular network surrounding the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, with a clear surface projection along the line connecting the ischial tuberosity and the midpoint of the popliteal fossa, making it easy to locate. Second, it offers functional benefits. It can be designed as a sensory flap by including the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, providing the potential for sensory recovery in the reconstructed area, which holds long‐term value in preventing recurrence. Third, it is highly versatile. It can effectively cover most wounds in the ipsilateral ischial tuberosity and sacrococcygeal region through rotation or advancement techniques. Fourth, it minimises donor site morbidity. After harvest, the donor site can usually be closed directly, and its location on the posterior thigh offers good concealment. As noted in Section 6.1, however, the evidence supporting these advantages remains primarily at the level of retrospective case series.

To master such a core technique, a structured pathway is recommended. This begins with simulation training using surgical atlases, anatomical videos, and animal models for repeated practice in flap design and harvest [104, 105]. Subsequently, staged clinical application should be undertaken, starting with small to medium‐sized, non‐severely infected wounds to gradually build hands‐on experience in assessing flap rotation arcs, judging tension, and managing the donor site. Finally, the process requires systematic reflection and refinement, which involves meticulously documenting key operative details for each case, such as perfusion at the flap's distal margin and suture tension, to establish a personal technical database for continuous optimization [106, 107].

Establishing Effective Multidisciplinary Collaborative Systems

As a local manifestation of systemic disease, the long‐term successful management of IPUs cannot rely on surgery alone. Therefore, primary care institutions should proactively establish or integrate into a MDT care pathway [19, 108]. The core collaborative elements of this system encompass several key phases. First, the preoperative assessment phase requires the involvement of nutritionists and clinical pharmacists to develop and implement a scientifically sound nutritional enhancement plan, with the goal of elevating serum albumin to ≥ 35 g/L and correcting haemoglobin to ≥ 100 g/L [7, 63]. This intervention is among the most cost‐effective measures for reducing postoperative complications such as flap edge necrosis and infection. Second, perioperative management necessitates close communication with the anesthesiology department to ensure intraoperative hemodynamic stability and to design an effective postoperative analgesia regimen, thereby avoiding hypotension or pain‐induced stress responses that could compromise flap perfusion [109]. Finally, the postoperative rehabilitation stage must be led collaboratively by rehabilitation specialists and nursing staff. This phase involves enforcing a strict “graded pressure management protocol” to prevent accidental pressure on the flap during activities like toileting or transfers, which includes the judicious use of pressure‐relieving mattresses and the implementation of individualised turning schedules and gradual sitting retraining programs [59].

Adhering to the Staged Treatment Principle

Strategic treatment planning should adhere to a pragmatic “staged treatment” principle. In patients exhibiting significant indicators of infection including copious purulent discharge or systemic inflammatory response, as well as those with suspected deep osteomyelitis or critically unstable systemic conditions, single‐stage flap reconstruction is contraindicated [26, 53, 110]. Adopting a staged approach in such cases is essential to minimise the risk of catastrophic surgical failure. Figure 1 presents a clinical flowchart that visually summarises this staged management algorithm, guiding clinicians from initial patient assessment through Phase I (infection control), Phase II (definitive flap reconstruction), and Phase III (multidisciplinary rehabilitation). The implementation of this staged strategy unfolds across two distinct phases. The initial phase focuses on infection control and improving systemic conditions [26, 27]. This stage involves radical debridement (“En Bloc” resection) to remove all necrotic tissue [26], followed by wound management using NPWT or antibiotic‐loaded bone cement/sponges to control infection and promote granulation [53]. Concurrently, systemic management includes targeted antibiotic therapy and aggressive nutritional support [7, 110]. Subsequently, the definitive reconstruction phase is typically initiated 2–4 weeks later, when wound exudate has diminished, granulation tissue is healthy and beefy‐red, systemic infection markers have normalised, and nutritional parameters have improved [27, 53]. Proceeding with a meticulously designed flap coverage surgery at this juncture has been reported to significantly increase the likelihood of success [8, 10].

Clinical flowchart for staged management of IPU patients. This flowchart integrates the key principles discussed in this review. Patients are first assessed for active infection. Those with signs of infection (purulence, osteomyelitis, or systemic inflammatory response) enter Phase I (infection control) before proceeding to Phase II (definitive flap reconstruction). Flap selection is guided by defect size and sensory requirements. Intraoperative ICGFA is recommended to confirm flap perfusion before transfer. All patients should complete Phase III (multidisciplinary rehabilitation).

For primary care surgeons, the core competency in performing such procedures lies not in the quantity of techniques mastered, but in the deep integration of limited technical skills with rigorous perioperative management protocols, coupled with the wisdom and resolve to choose the conservative yet safe strategy of “control first, repair later” in complex situations. By focusing on core flap techniques, building collaborative networks, and adhering to the principle of staged treatment, it is possible to provide patients with safe, effective, and high‐quality care that aligns with modern reconstructive principles, even in relatively resource‐limited settings.

Conclusion

IPU management has undergone a fundamental paradigm shift, evolving from a narrow focus on anatomical wound closure to a comprehensive, patient‐centered approach prioritising functional and sensory restoration. However, as detailed in Section 6.1, it must be acknowledged that most supporting evidence remains at Level III‐IV, and high‐quality randomised controlled trials are urgently needed to validate many of the techniques described herein.

Several key conclusions emerge from this review. First, perforator flaps with sensory reconstruction represent the current optimal approach, though direct comparative studies against traditional flaps remain lacking. Second, multidisciplinary perioperative management—particularly nutritional optimization, graded pressure protocols, and team‐based rehabilitation—demonstrates the strongest evidence for reducing recurrence. Third, both Traditional Chinese Medicine and regenerative biomaterial approaches remain exploratory, requiring rigorous validation before clinical recommendation. Fourth, for resource‐limited settings, a staged treatment algorithm prioritising infection control before flap coverage is recommended to improve success rates.

Looking ahead, the field continues to advance through the convergence of surgical innovation, regenerative medicine, and digital technology. Emerging tools such as robotic‐assisted surgery, bioactive flap engineering, 3D bioprinting, and AI‐driven prognostic modelling hold promise for further personalising and optimising care. However, the translation of these advances into widespread practice requires addressing persistent evidence gaps, standardising outcome measures, and enhancing accessibility through training and tele‐collaboration.

Ultimately, the contemporary management of IPUs demands a holistic, staged, and collaborative framework. Surgeons must not only master core reconstructive techniques but also embrace the roles of systemic clinical thinker and team leader within a multidisciplinary continuum. By adhering to evidence‐based principles while thoughtfully integrating technological innovations, clinicians can effectively transition from healing wounds to rebuilding functional lives, offering patients with IPUs a sustainable path towards autonomy, dignity, and social reintegration.
