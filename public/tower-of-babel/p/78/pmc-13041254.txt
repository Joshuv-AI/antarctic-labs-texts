Temporomandibular joint disorders in patients with systemic sclerosis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Kiana Azadpeyma, Seyed Habib Zayeni, Pooneh Ghavidel Parsa, Pedram Hajibagheri, Maryam Basirat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsSystemic sclerosis (SSc) is a connective tissue disease that can affect multiple organs, including the musculoskeletal system and the temporomandibular joint (TMJ). This study aimed to determine the frequency and clinical characteristics of temporomandibular disorders (TMD) among patients with SSc.MethodsIn this cross-sectional analytical study, 69 patients diagnosed with SSc were evaluated through structured clinical examination and some Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders which was symptom-based and performed using the DC/TMD questionnaire as a screening tool. Patients’ disease duration, severity (assessed by Metsger severity score) and skin involvement (assessed by the modified Rodnan skin score) were also recorded.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 52.26 ± 10.76 years; 82.6% were female. The median disease duration and severity were 9 years (IQR: 3–13), years and 3(IQR: 2.0–6.0), respectively. Based on DC/TMD criteria, 63.8% of the patients met diagnostic criteria for TMD. No statistically significant associations were found between TMD presence and patients’ age, disease duration, or skin score (p > 0.05).ConclusionTMD is highly prevalent among patients with systemic sclerosis, affecting more than 60% of the studied cohort. These findings underscore the need for routine TMJ screening in SSc patients, as early recognition and intervention may mitigate the impact of orofacial dysfunction on quality of life.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a chronic autoimmune connective tissue disease characterized by skin fibrosis and widespread vascular dysfunction, and is clinically categorized into limited cutaneous (lcSSc) and diffuse cutaneous (dcSSc) subtypes [1]. In addition to cutaneous manifestations, the disease frequently involves internal organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, lungs, heart, and kidneys [2]. Common extracutaneous complications include esophageal dysmotility, restrictive lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, myopathy, cardiomyopathy, and progressive renal impairment [3]. Musculoskeletal involvement—including arthralgia, arthritis, and tendinopathy—is also prevalent, affecting 40–90% of SSc patients [4].

Among the musculoskeletal and orofacial complications, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) involvement and associated muscular abnormalities have gained increasing attention [5]. SSc can cause osseous resorption at muscle attachment sites, particularly at the mandibular angle, condylar head, coronoid process, and digastric region [6]. This is attributed to increased fibrosis and reduced vascular supply in the masticatory muscles, resulting in atrophy and chronic pressure on bony structures [1].

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) are characterized by pain and dysfunction of the masticatory muscles and the TMJ itself, often presenting as limited mandibular movements, stiffness, headaches, and jaw pain [7]. Joint abnormalities such as condylar resorption, erosive synovitis, synovial fibrosis, muscle atrophy, and arthritis may all contribute to TMD in SSc patients [8]. Although often overlooked, these symptoms can significantly impair oral function and quality of life [9]. Proper clinical assessment based on diagnostic criteria is essential to distinguish TMD-related symptoms from other causes of orofacial pain [10].

Despite the clinical relevance, few studies have comprehensively assessed TMD in patients with SSc using standardized protocols such as the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD). Previous investigations have yielded conflicting results: Matares reported that TMD is a common feature in SSc patients [11], while Pellicano found no statistically significant difference in TMD prevalence compared to healthy controls [8], and Crincoli identified a significantly higher frequency of oral and TMD symptoms in SSc individuals [5]. Given these discrepancies, the present study aims to assess the prevalence of TMD in patients with systemic sclerosis, using the DC/TMD protocol.

Methods

Patients and settings

This cross-sectional study included 69 patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc), selected by convenience sampling from a total of 110 patients referred to the Razi Rheumatology Clinic in Rasht, Iran, between September and December 2023. A total of 110 patients with systemic sclerosis were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 41 patients were excluded due to refusal to participate (n = 14), use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs within the last four weeks or corticosteroid/immunosuppressive therapy within the previous three months (n = 15), history of migraine (n = 5), and presence of extensive dental caries or active dental infection (n = 7). Finally, 69 patients were included in the final analysis.

All participants received a full explanation of the study, and written informed consent was obtained. The study protocol was approved by the ethical committee of Guilan University of Medial Sciences [IR.GUMS.REC.1399.055, Date: 2020-05-06]. The sample size was determined through an a priori power analysis based on the findings of Crincoli et al. The inclusion criteria were patients with SSc according to the 2013 ACR/EULAR Classification Criteria for Systemic Sclerosis which incorporate clinical features, serological markers, and skin involvement scoring (confirmed by a rheumatologist), and who had signed the inform consent. The exclusion criteria included refusal to provide informed consent or presence of overlapping autoimmune diseases, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) degenerative diseases, migraine, musculoskeletal or neurological diseases which were unrelated to SSc, recent maxillofacial trauma, history of TMJ surgery, or ongoing orthodontic treatment, use of prostanoids or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDS) within the preceding four weeks, presence of active dental infections, extensive dental caries, or untreated odontogenic pain and dental treatments within the last three months, and changes in systemic corticosteroids or immunosuppressive therapy during the previous three months.

Data collection

Patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc) were classified into limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis (lcSSc) and diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis (dcSSc) subsets based on the extent and anatomical distribution of skin involvement [12]. Disease severity and skin involvement, were assessed using medsger severity scale and the modified Rodnan Skin Score (mRSS) respectively [13], with data extracted from medical records. The DC/TMD questionnaire was completed through clinical examination and structured interviews conducted by two final-year dental students under the supervision of an oral and maxillofacial medicine specialist. All assessments were performed according to the standardized Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular disorder(DC/TMD) [14], a validated tool consisting of 14 questions evaluating TMJ-related symptoms including: joint pain; headache characteristics; referred pain; jaw locking in open or closed position; pain during chewing hard foods; discomfort when holding teeth together; and pain during various jaw movements. A positive response to at least one question indicated TMJ dysfunction.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize qualitative data (frequency and percentage) and quantitative data (mean and standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and skewness coefficient. Comparative analyses were performed using the independent t-test for normally distributed continuous variables and chi square and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Non-normally distributed variables (skin involvement, disease duration and severity) were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 69 patients diagnosed with SSc participated in the study. The age of participants ranged from 24 to 70 years, with a mean of 52.26 years. The majority were female (82.6%), while 17.4% were male. The mean disease duration and skin involvement (mRSS) were 9.65 ± 6.95 years and 13.23 ± 8.17 respectively. As for disease subtype, 89.85% had diffuse cutaneous SSc, while 10.14% had the limited form (Table 1).

TMD was identified in 63.8% of the participants. Among females, 64.9% were diagnosed with TMD compared to 58.3% of males. No statistically significant associations were found between TMD status and sex (p = 0.746), age (p = 0.140), disease duration (p = 0.255), disease severity (p = 0.406) and skin involvement(P = 0.119). Similarly, no significant differences were observed when analyzing TMD status across education levels (p = 0.626) (Table 2).

male patients with TMD had a longer median disease duration compared to those without TMD. Among females, median skin involvement and disease duration were consistently higher in those with TMD compared to those without TMD (Table 3).

The most prevalent clinical signs among SSc patients with TMD included opening derangement (57.9%), tooth wear (53.6%), and contact in the balancing side (52.2%). Joint sounds were reported in 34.8% of patients, with unilateral sounds in 20.3% and bilateral in 14.5%. Restriction in mandibular movements was evident in the reduced mean values of jaw opening (36.94 ± 9.68 mm), lateral excursions (7.74 ± 4.18 mm), and protrusion (5.23 ± 3.05 mm). Masticatory muscle tenderness was found in 13% for the pterygoid muscles, 18.8% for the masseter, and 5.8% for the temporalis muscles (Table 4).

Arthralgia was the most commonly reported symptom, affecting 46.4% of patients. Among these individuals, half experienced joint pain during activities such as kissing, yawning, or speaking. Pain during chewing hard food was reported by 46.9% of those with arthralgia, while 31.3% experienced pain during jaw movement, and 28.1% while holding their teeth together. Headache was present in 26.1% of the participants, with various aggravating factors including chewing hard food (27.8%), moving the jaw (16.7%), and jaw clenching (16.7%). Joint sounds were reported by 24.6% of patients. Episodes of closed jaw locking occurred in 8.7%, and open locking in 5.8%. The mean duration of TMJ pain among those affected was 4.18 years (SD = 3.34). Patients who experienced headaches reported a mean headache duration of 6.67 years (SD = 3.63) (Table 5).

Discussion

The findings of our study examining TMD in patients with systemic sclerosis revealed an insight into the complex interplay between autoimmune pathophysiology and orofacial manifestations. With 63.8% of our 69-patients meeting DC/TMD diagnostic criteria for TMD, these results not only confirm the substantial orofacial burden of SSc but also raise important questions about disease monitoring and management paradigms in rheumatologic practice. The prevalence observed in our study occupies the middle range of the 40–90% spectrum reported in existing literature [4], in contrast Crincoli et al. [5] reported TMJ disorder in about 90% of patients, and Matarese et al. [11] documented a high prevalence of TMJ pain, among affected individuals. conversely Chiara Pellicano et al. [8] found that the incidence of TMJ disorder in subjects with scleroderma was similar to that in healthy controls. This variability likely reflects several key factors: differences in disease duration Among studied populations, variations in SSc subtype distributions, referral-based nature of treatment center, methodological differences in TMD assessment, and evolving diagnostic criteria over time. In the present study, the prevalence of temporomandibular joint disorder (TMD) among patients with systemic sclerosis did not differ by age, sex, or education level, aligning with Crincoli et al. [5] who reported no significant associations with these factors. Notably, many patients without a formal TMD diagnosis still had objective jaw dysfunction.

The mean lateral mandibular movement was approximately 7.8 mm, the mean anterior movement about 5.3 mm, and the mean vertical movement around 37 mm. These values diverge from Crincoli et al. [5, 11] and Matarese et al., [11] with relatively less movement restriction observed in this cohort. Differences may reflect variations in sample size and disease severity. These findings suggest that simple continuous measures of jaw mobility (e.g. maximal interincisal opening) may provide greater sensitivity in assessing temporomandibular involvement in SSc than rigid diagnostic thresholds.

our findings indicate a high frequency of mandibular deviation among patients with systemic sclerosis, particularly with rightward deviation. The observed occlusal alterations, including dental wear and non-working side interferences, suggest compensatory adaptation to asymmetric mandibular mechanics.

Muscle palpation revealed relatively low rates of masticatory muscle pain, implying that muscular involvement is not the primary driver of TMD manifestations in this cohort. Instead, occlusal and soft-tissue changes related to skin fibrosis appear to play a more substantial role. This aligns with known pathophysiological mechanisms of systemic sclerosis, in which collagen deposition and tissue stiffening can affect mandibular movement and chewing function [1, 2, 15, 16]. Imaging studies in systemic sclerosis provide structural support for our clinical observations. Although advanced imaging was not included in our protocol, previous CBCT and MRI investigations have consistently reported features such as condylar flattening, erosions, synovial fibrosis, and widened periodontal ligament spaces. These radiologic patterns align with the functional limitations and occlusal changes observed in our cohort, suggesting that TMJ involvement in SSc follows a characteristic structural profile [15, 17]. The development of TMD in patients with SSc appears multifactorial. Periarticular fibrosis – driven by excessive collagen-producing myofibroblasts – is a hallmark of SSc and likely affects TMJ capsule and ligaments just as it does skin and visceral organs [17]. Vascular damage in patients with SSc (microangiopathy) could also contribute [16]. Chronic hypoperfusion of the mandibular condyle may predispose to degeneration, analogous to digital ischemic losses seen in systemic disease [15]. Finally, a low-grade inflammatory component may provoke synovitis [18]; indeed, autoimmunity in patients with SSc can induce joint inflammation and secondary osteoarthritis [19, 20]. These overlapping mechanisms may explain why some patients develop TMJ dysfunction early in their disease.

TMD in SSc seldom occurs in isolation. Xerostomia, microstomia, and salivary dysfunction are very common in SSc, as are dental and periodontal problems [6, 21]. In one review 80% of SSc patients reported orofacial symptoms including dry mouth, caries, and periodontitis [22]. Severe caries, pulpitis, periapical infections, and advanced periodontal disease may cause referred pain to the jaw, alter occlusion, or lead to changes in mandibular function due to discomfort, tooth mobility, or asymmetric chewing. These factors can mimic or exacerbate symptoms of TMD, including jaw pain, deviation, and masticatory dysfunction. Therefore, patients with active caries or significant periodontal pathology should be evaluated carefully, and such conditions should be considered in the differential diagnosis of TMJ-related symptom [1, 23–25]. It is noteworthy that SSc patients with significant oral involvement score markedly worse on the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP) [26]. For example, Baron et al. found mean OHIP scores of 41.6 in SSc versus 26.7 in controls, indicating far greater oral-health disability [27]. Within our study, 46.4% reported jaw pain and ~ 50% had difficulty chewing or talking. Poor mastication and trismus can worsen nutrition in SSc – a serious concern given frequent GI dysmotility – creating a vicious cycle of undernutrition and systemic decline.

The findings emphasized routine TMJ screening in all SSc patients. Relying on spontaneous reporting of jaw symptoms likely under-detects TMD. Simple office measures (maximal mouth opening, lateral movements, palpation of joint sounds) are quick and sensitive; even modest reductions from normal should prompt further evaluation. If subclinical dysfunction or pain is found, a multidisciplinary approach is warranted. Recent study suggested that targeted physiotherapy can improve TMJ mobility in SSc. In a 2024 study, patients completing a home exercise program (including jaw-opening and passive stretching exercises) significantly increased their mouth opening [28]. The addition of an ultrasound-guided manual therapy component yielded even greater gains. Although data are limited, these results imply that early, tailored rehab can partially reverse functional loss. Similarly, custom intraoral appliances (to correct occlusion or stabilize the joint) and myofascial release techniques may relieve symptoms.

This study is limited by four main factors. First, while we used the DC/TMD questionnaire and interview for TMD diagnosis, the subsequent physical examination focused on jaw movement due to the extensive questionnaire scope, precluding a detailed quantitative assessment of dental caries and periodontal health. Second, the lack of advanced imaging (MRI/CBCT) prevented correlation of clinical symptoms with structural joint pathology. Third, the modest single-center sample may limit generalizability, particularly for rarer findings. Finally, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes causal or temporal inferences regarding progression of temporomandibular disorders. In addition, clinical variables beyond skin involvement, as well as overall systemic sclerosis disease activity, were not evaluated. Fibromyalgia was not assessed and may represent a potential confounding factor. Therefore, future longitudinal studies are recommended to incorporate comprehensive orofacial assessments, including standardized oral radiography for caries and periodontal bone loss, as well as functional measurements (MHISS or OHIP) and patient-reported outcome measures.

Conclusions

Therefore, while TMD management in the general population is typically initiated upon the onset of symptoms, for patients with SSc, routine screening, detailed assessment, and early diagnosis of temporomandibular joint involvement are clinical imperatives. This proactive, preventive approach is essential to preserve jaw function, facilitate essential oral care, maintain nutritional intake, and ultimately enhance quality of life. Comprehensive evaluation of the temporomandibular joint should be considered a vital, non-negotiable component of the multidisciplinary management protocol for systemic sclerosis.
