Concomitant Pulsed Field Ablation and Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion for Atrial Fibrillation With Dextrocardia
Authors: Gangbin Chen, Guizhou Ma, Chumin Ni, Xinjie He, Zhixiong Cai

ABSTRACT

History of Presentation

A 61-year-old male patient presented to our institution with palpitations for 2 years. Admission physical examination: blood pressure was 140/80 mm Hg, heart rate was 100 to 160 beats/min with irregular rhythm, apical impulse was located at the fifth intercostal space along the right midclavicular line, and no cardiac murmurs auscultated.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of cerebral infarction, coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, and coronary artery stent implantation. He had previously experienced urinary tract bleeding during antithrombotic therapy. He had a 40-year smoking history and no history of alcoholism.

Differential Diagnosis

The potential mechanisms of arrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation (AF), atrial tachycardia, and atrial flutter, should be identified. Abnormalities on cardiac physical examination suggest the need to differentiate between dextrocardia, right-shifted heart, or right-rotated heart, and computed tomography (CT) is helpful for differentiation.

Investigations

Electrocardiography revealed dextrocardia and AF with rapid ventricular response (Figure 1A). Chest and abdominal CT showed dextrocardia and situs inversus abdominis (Figure 1B). Left atrial CT demonstrated dextrocardia with normal morphology of all pulmonary veins (PVs) (Figures 1C and 1D). Echocardiography showed a left atrial diameter of 40 mm and an ejection fraction of 68%.

Management

The CHA2DS2-VASc score was 4, and the HAS-BLED score was 3, indicating the suitability for concomitant catheter ablation and left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) for rhythm control, as well as for reduction of thromboembolic and bleeding risk. After being fully informed of the risks and benefits of the surgery, the patient consented to undergo the procedures.

Pulsed field ablation procedure

Given the abnormal structure of the heart, pulsed field ablation (PFA)-based pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) combined with LAAO was guided by intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) under general anesthesia to ensure the safety and precision of the procedure. ICE (Biosense Webster) was used to exclude left atrial appendage (LAA) thrombus, guide the transseptal puncture, visualize proper contact with the tissue, and monitor the pericardium (Figures 2A to 2F). DecaNav (Biosense Webster) was advanced to the coronary sinus, and a quadripolar electrode was advanced to the right ventricle. Transseptal puncture was a challenging task, and we completed it under ICE and x-ray guidance (Figure 2C). Heparin (12,000 IU) was administered according to the patient's body weight, and the activated coagulation time was maintained for more than 350 seconds. Left atrial angiography was performed to clarify the anatomy of the PVs and LAA. The CARTOSOUND FAM Module (Biosense Webster) failed to reconstruct the left atria and Pentaray (Biosense Webster) was used to create an electroanatomical voltage map of left atria and PVs under the assistance of visualizable steerable sheath (Vizigo, Biosense Webster) (Figures 2G and 2H). Bilateral PVI was performed using a strict tissue-contact confirmation with ICE and tissue proximity indication (Figures 2D to 2F). PFA was performed with 30 mL/min irrigation during energy delivery to avoid heat stacking. Complete PVI was confirmed by remapping with the Pentaray catheter (Figures 2I and 2J).

LAAO procedure

After completing PFA, pulmonary ridge thickness was increased whereas LAA landing zone diameter did not change as evaluated by ICE (Figures 3A and 3B). The Vizigo sheath was exchanged for a 14-F Watchman access sheath (Boston Scientific), and selective LAA angiography was performed using a 6-F pigtail. The diameter and morphology of the LAA were measured and evaluated by selective LAA angiography in the left anterior oblique 30° with caudal 20° view (Figure 3D). A Watchman FLX 31-mm device (Boston Scientific) was selected and delivered into the LAA. The device was releasing after the following criteria were met: 1) optimal position of the device was confirmed by imaging, 2) no displacement during pull testing, 3) a compression ratio of 22%, and 4) no peri-device leaks were detected by ICE and contrast-enhanced imaging (Figures 3C to 3E). The patient experienced no peri-procedural complications and was discharged 2 days after the procedure.

Follow-up

Antithrombotic medication with rivaroxaban (15 mg/d) and aspirin (100 mg/d) was administered after the procedure. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence of AF and cardiac CT confirmed a complete LAA seal without leak or device-related thrombus (Figure 4). Rivaroxaban was discontinued, and the patient was switched to aspirin (100 mg/d) and clopidogrel (75 mg/d) for antiplatelet therapy.

Discussion

Mirror-image dextrocardia is a rare anomaly, with an incidence of about 1:10,000 in the general population, and can be associated with significant additional cardiac malformations.1,2 Although the actual AF prevalence in dextrocardia remains unknown, it is predicted to increase with population aging. The reversal of the whole heart undoubtedly poses challenge to interventional procedures of AF.3 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the feasibility of concomitant PFA and LAAO for atrial fibrillation in the presence of mirror-image dextrocardia.

In patients with high stroke risk and who are ineligible for long-term oral anticoagulation, concomitant catheter ablation and LAAO offer a single-procedure approach for rhythm control and thromboembolic risk reduction. Although many cases reported concomitant radiofrequency ablation and LAAO in patients with dextrocardia,4,5 there are no documented instances of this procedure leveraging PFA in this patient population. PFA is a novel, nonthermal energy, and effective modality that showed favorable safety and efficacy in AF.6 Recently, combined PFA and LAAO was shown to be feasible and safe with favorable outcomes, including high procedural success and low rates of periprocedural complications.7 Moreover, in the combined procedure, PFA was associated with lower peri-device leak incidence and shorter procedural times compared with radiofrequency ablation.8,9 In this case, concomitant PFA and LAAO was successfully performed guided by multimodal imaging, no arrhythmia was recorded, and complete LAA seal was demonstrated by cardiac CT during follow-up. We also found that PFA did not significantly alter the LAA landing zone diameter, which lies more distally at the level of the circumflex artery, despite that proximal ridge edema was observed. This finding is consistent with previous study and suggesting that no adverse impact on device sizing immediately after PFA procedure.10

Although previous literature showed that inverted x-ray imaging to improve fluoroscopic guidance may be beneficial to the operation,11 ICE offers more advantages during the procedures. ICE provides real-time visualization to guide the transseptal puncture avoiding mechanical damage to the surrounding structures. ICE can guide the catheter position and confirm the location of the PFA catheter and visualize proper contact with the tissue to be ablated.12 Recently, a deep learning–based imaging algorithm integrated with ICE (CARTOSOUND FAM Module) has been developed and demonstrated to be both feasible and accurate in AF ablation; unfortunately, we found that this module could not be used in dextrocardia because the algorithm was limited only to patients without anatomical variations.13,14

Although the procedure was performed safely and effectively, the follow-up duration remains insufficient; longer follow-up is necessary to assess the durability of lesions and clinical efficacy.

Conclusions

PFA-based PVI combined with LAAO guided by ICE is a feasible, safe, and high-efficiency choice yielding favorable outcomes for AF in patients with dextrocardia. PFA procedures could induce pulmonary ridge edema but did not affect LAA landing zone dimension.
