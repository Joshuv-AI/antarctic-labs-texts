Phenotypic heterogeneity of type 2 diabetes and risks of all-cause and cause-specific mortality
Authors: Zixin Qiu, Frank Qian, Jun Liu, Rui Li, Hancheng Yu, Yue Wang, Xiao Zhang, Tingting Geng, Xuefeng Yu, Oscar H. Franco, An Pan, Maigeng Zhou, Kai Huang, Gang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryType 2 diabetes (T2D) is a heterogeneous condition, but its phenotypic variation and links with mortality are unclear. We apply the discriminative dimensionality reduction with trees (DDRTree) algorithm to seven clinical variables in 10,091 adults with newly diagnosed T2D from a nationally representative Chinese cohort. Distinct mortality patterns are observed across phenotypes. Cardiovascular mortality is highest in the most hypertensive and obese individuals, while diabetic ketoacidosis/coma mortality is largely driven by the combination of hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia. Additionally, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease mortality is higher in those with elevated high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and total cholesterol levels. These patterns are similar in UK Biobank, though cardiovascular mortality is highest in those with dyslipidemia and obesity. Predictive models incorporating these variables show good performance and an online tool is provided for individual risk prediction. Overall, this study visualizes phenotypic variation in T2D and its impact on mortality, underscoring the need for personalized treatment strategies.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) continues to be a global public health issue.1 In 2021, the estimated number of individuals living with T2D was approximately 537 million worldwide, and China has the world’s largest epidemic of diabetes.1,2 The mortality rate in people with T2D, particularly from cardiovascular disease (CVD), remains significantly high, highlighting the urgent need for effective management strategies.3 T2D is a highly heterogeneous disease, with varied clinical presentations and disease trajectories.4,5 Multiple pathogenic pathways contribute to its onset and progression.4,5 However, variations in T2D phenotypes are often overlooked in patient care.6 Recognizing this heterogeneity, recent studies have classified diabetes into distinct phenotypic clusters based on clinical characteristics, which help differentiate the risk of complications and inform therapeutic decisions.7,8,9,10,11,12

However, advances in biomarkers have led to a growing recognition that T2D should not only be viewed with discrete and defined clinical stages, but as a multifaceted process moving along a seamless continuum, where adjacent severities blend gradually but extremes remain distinct.4 Furthermore, some evidence suggested that models treating T2D as a phenotypic continuum may be more effective than discrete stratification in predicting complications.13 The discriminative dimensionality reduction with trees (DDRTree) algorithm offers a promising approach that simplifies complex, multi-dimensional data into a more interpretable two-dimensional tree structure.14,15 By applying DDRTree, diabetes phenotypes could be visualized as a continuous spectrum, capturing the gradual transitions between phenotypes.

Recent research primarily from Europe has demonstrated the utility of DDRTree in visualizing diabetes phenotypes and linking them with metabolic and clinical outcomes, such as insulin sensitivity, inflammation, and macrovascular and microvascular complications.16,17,18,19 However, existing studies have primarily focused on metabolic traits, with limited exploration of all-cause and cause-specific mortality risk across diverse populations. Different populations with diabetes exhibit varying characteristics due to genetic, environmental, and lifestyle differences. For instance, in Chinese population, T2D often develops at a lower body mass index (BMI),20 and shows distinct patterns in terms of subsequent sequelae21 compared to Western populations. Understanding the heterogeneity of T2D and how it translates into mortality risk variation across different populations could provide valuable insights for advancing personalized medicine and more effectively reducing diabetes-related mortality.

To fill these knowledge gaps, we applied data dimensionality reduction on routinely collected clinical data in people with newly diagnosed T2D, leveraging nationwide data from China and the UK. We aimed to reduce the complex phenotypic characteristics of T2D into a non-linear, two-dimensional tree structure, and to visualize how all-cause and cause-specific mortality risks (death from CVD, cancer, diabetic ketoacidosis [DKA]/coma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD]) vary across this phenotypic spectrum. Additionally, we examined the predictive ability of these routinely collected clinical indicators for mortality outcomes.

Results

Phenotypic characteristics and model performance

The phenotypic characteristics of the study population at the T2D diagnosis are described in Table 1. The participants in the China Chronic Disease and Risk Factors Surveillance (CCDRFS) (n = 10,091) had a mean age of 58.43 (SD 11.11) years, 46.70% of participants were male. The participants in the UK Biobank (UKB) (n = 5991) had a mean age of 59.50 (SD 7.10) years, 61.79% of participants were male. The seven variables used to determine the phenotype tree, along with their corresponding values in the CCDRFS and UKB cohorts, were as follows (mean [SD]): high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C): CCDRFS 1.29 (0.39) mmol/L, UKB 1.20 (0.30) mmol/L; total cholesterol (TC): CCDRFS 5.23 (1.04) mmol/L, UKB 5.18 (1.27) mmol/L; triglycerides (TG): CCDRFS 2.03 (1.57) mmol/L, UKB 2.36 (1.29) mmol/L; hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c): CCDRFS 6.44 (1.40) %, UKB 7.00 (1.29); BMI: CCDRFS 25.61 (3.66) kg/m2, UKB 31.52 (5.64) kg/m2; systolic blood pressure (SBP): CCDRFS 141.94 (20.73) mm Hg, UKB 143.10 (17.41) mm Hg; diastolic blood pressure (DBP): CCDRFS 81.27 (11.32) mm Hg, UKB 84.19 (10.10) mm Hg. The generalized additive model (GAM) models predicting DDRTree dimension 1 and dimension 2 are described in Tables S1 and S2. The adjusted R2 of the model was used as a measure of performance.16 The model predicting dimension 1 (x coordinate) had an adjusted R2 of 0.87, while the model predicting dimension 2 (y coordinate) had an adjusted R2 of 0.85–0.86. We found very strong correlations between the dimensions calculated using the validation dataset and those predicted using the derivation dataset (ρdim1 = 0.94–0.96; ρdim2 = 0.89–0.92, all p < 0.001). Additionally, internal validation across 50 iterations resulted in an adjusted Rand index (ARI) value of 0.81 in CCDRFS and 0.89 in UKB. Phenotypic heterogeneity at T2D diagnosis in CCDRFS

To illustrate and describe the phenotypic variation present across the tree, data for seven distinct clinical characteristics were superimposed, as depicted in Figure 1A. The tree structure provides a continuous representation of diabetes heterogeneity, where the number of branches may vary depending on input data; however, the dimensions remain stable. Our focus is on how phenotypes and mortality risks shift along these dimensions, rather than the specific number of branches. Two metrics were employed to evaluate the distribution patterns of these phenotypes across the tree: first, each phenotype was regressed against the two tree dimensions (Figure 1B), and second, the global Moran’s I values were calculated to gauge the strength of spatial correlation (all p < 0.001) (Figure 1C). Based on all these measures, HDL-C, triglycerides, SBP, and DBP were the most prominently distributed phenotypes across the tree, followed by BMI, and then HbA1c and TC. The individuals located in the right part of the tree had higher levels of blood pressure (βdim1 = 1.00–1.01, p < 0.001; βdim2 = 0.07–0.20, p < 0.001); those in the upper part of the tree had higher BMI (βdim1 = 0.11, p < 0.001; βdim2 = 1.03, p < 0.001) and triglycerides levels (βdim1 = −0.30, p < 0.001; βdim2 = 1.29, p < 0.001) and lower HDL-C levels (βdim1 = −0.01, p > 0.05; βdim2 = −1.57, p < 0.001); those in the left and upper part of the tree had higher HbA1c levels (βdim1 = −0.43, p < 0.001; βdim2 = 0.60, p < 0.001); and those in the left part of the tree had higher TC levels (βdim1 = −0.63, p < 0.001; βdim2 = −0.23, p < 0.001). We found no significant differences in distribution between individuals on antihypertensive or cholesterol-lowering medications and those not taking these medications, as well as between smokers and non-smokers (Figure S1). When fasting plasma glucose and 2-h glucose were superimposed on the tree (Figure S2), fasting plasma glucose and 2-h glucose showed a distribution similar to HbA1c. Heterogeneity of mortality in CCDRFS

We then investigated how the phenotypic variation at diagnosis translates to variation in risk of all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality. Over a 6.4-year follow-up period, there were 575 deaths (1,221.79 per 100,000 person years), including 275 from CVD (584.34), 153 from cancer (325.10), 45 from COPD (95.62), and 15 from DKA/coma (25.49) in the derivation dataset. These mortality rates were similar to a previous national study in China.22 Hazard ratios (HRs) for each outcome were predicted and superimposed on the tree (Figures 2A–2D). Additionally, Figure 2E showed the HRs of DDRTree dimensions from the competing risk model. Global Moran’s I, depicted in Figure 2F, quantifies the global spatial correlation, indicating a strong and significant spatial correlation (p < 0.001) for each outcome across the tree. The greatest risk of all-cause mortality was observed in the most hypertensive individuals in the right region of the tree, although this association was of borderline statistical significance (HRdim1 = 1.14 [95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.00, 1.31]; HRdim2 = 0.94 [95% CI = 0.80, 1.11]). The risk of CVD mortality was positively associated with the first and second dimension, although the latter did not reach statistical significance (right and upper-right; HRdim1 = 1.34 [95% CI = 1.07, 1.67]; HRdim2 = 1.13 [95% CI = 0.90, 1.43]) (Figures 2A and 2B). This area predominantly included hypertensive and obese individuals. By contrast, the risk of mortality from DKA/coma showed a distinct pattern, with the highest risk observed in individuals located in the upper-left region of the DDRTree (HRdim1 = 0.24 [95% CI = 0.11, 0.50]; HRdim2 = 4.27 [95% CI = 1.26, 14.47]) (Figure 2C). This area was characterized by a joint metabolic profile including elevated HbA1c, dyslipidemia (i.e., elevated TC and triglycerides as well as reduced HDL-C levels) and moderately elevated BMI. When probability for use of insulin was superimposed on the tree (Figure S4), the distribution was similar to the risk of mortality from DKA/coma. Additionally, the risk of COPD mortality was higher in those with high HDL-C and TC levels toward the bottom left of the tree (HRdim1 = 0.53 [95% CI = 0.32, 0.89]; HRdim2 = 0.31 [95% CI = 0.13, 0.73]) (Figure 2D). The risk distribution for cancer mortality across the tree closely mirrored that of all-cause mortality, although the HR did not reach statistically significance (HRdim1 = 1.15 [95% CI = 0.87, 1.52]; HRdim2 = 0.98 [95% CI = 0.70, 1.38]) (Figure S5). Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with pre-existing conditions (CVD and cancer) did not materially change the results, although some associations were no longer statistically significant due to reduced sample size (Figure S6).

We additionally performed phenotype classification using the k-means clustering method for comparative purposes. Following the approach of previous studies,7 we applied TwoStep clustering to seven phenotypic variables, residualized for age and gender. In this method, the first step estimates the optimal number of clusters on the basis of silhouette width and the second step does k-means clustering. This analysis indicated that two clusters were optimal (Figure S7). Notably, one of the clusters displayed substantially worse cardiometabolic profiles across all phenotypic variables (BMI, blood pressure, lipid levels, and HbA1c) (Table S3). This high-risk cluster was associated with elevated risk of CVD and DKA/coma mortality, but not with cancer, COPD, or all-cause mortality (Table S4).

DDRTree analysis in UKB

Using the same methodology, we applied the DDRTree algorithm to construct a new phenotype tree leveraging data from UKB. Each phenotypic variable showed a distinct distribution across the UKB phenotype tree (Figure 3). This distribution was similar among individuals taking antihypertensive or cholesterol-lowering medications and those not on these medications, as well as between smokers and non-smokers (Figure S8). Over a 14.6-year follow-up period, there were 1,194 deaths (2,314.02 per 100,000 person years), including 309 from CVD (598.85), 546 from cancer (1058.17), 25 from COPD (48.45), and 22 from DKA/coma (42.64). When mortality risks were superimposed on the tree, substantial variation was also observed across different patient phenotypes (Figures 4A–4D). The global and local Moran’s I, shown in Figures 4F and S9, indicates a strong and significant spatial correlation (p < 0.001) for each outcome across the tree. The risk of all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality had a similar pattern across the tree, with the greatest risk observed in individuals who were most obese and dyslipidemic (with low HDL-C and high TG levels) and had moderate hyperglycemia (HRdim1 = 0.60–0.83; HRdim2 = 1.05–1.15) (Figures 4; S10). These patterns differed somewhat from those observed in the CCDRFS. Visualizing individual risk in relation to their phenotype

To support clinicians and patients in visualizing individual profiles at the time of T2D diagnosis and understanding associated risks of all-cause and cause-specific mortality, we developed web applications (https://zixin59.shinyapps.io/shinyapp2/ for CCDRFS, https://zixin59.shinyapps.io/shinyapp3/ for UKB). These tools map newly diagnosed patients onto a diabetes continuum (tree structure) and predicts their cumulative risk of cause-specific mortality over six or fourteen years, based on their age, gender, and seven other continuous phenotypic measures that define the tree. Since no DKA/coma deaths occurred within the first year of follow-up in CCDRFS, the survival curve begins after the first year. Using these continuous phenotypic measures, along with age and gender, the AUC for all-cause and cause-specific mortality (i.e., CVD, DKA/coma, and COPD) ranged from 0.789 to 0.958 in CCDRFS over a 6-year follow-up and ranged from 0.677 to 0.769 in UKB over a 14-year follow-up (Table 2). The internal validation yielded AUC from 0.784 to 0.937 in CCDRFS, and from 0.735 to 0.973 in UKB (Table 2), indicating strong calibration. Across both CCDRFS and UKB datasets, the models using DDRTree-derived dimensions showed comparable predictive performance to models using the original clinical variables. Discussion

Based on two large nationwide cohorts from China and the UK, we employed a novel approach to perform dimensionality reduction on metabolic indicators related to obesity, HbA1c, blood lipids, and blood pressure in individuals with T2D. The resulting tree structure visualizes the complex phenotypic variation in people with T2D and allows an overlay of mortality outcomes. Distinct patterns of risk distribution for different mortality outcomes were observed across the phenotype tree. Additionally, including these seven phenotypic variables in predictive models for mortality demonstrated good predictive performance.

Compared with traditional methods for diabetes classification, such as clustering methods, the DDRtree approach more accurately captures the complexity and continuum of diabetes progression.13,23 Rather than assuming homogeneity within subgroups, integrating continuous phenotypic measures to predict specific outcomes at the individual level could offer greater clinical value.13,23 Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of DDRTree in characterizing diabetes phenotypes and their associations with metabolic and clinical outcomes. For example, a study from the Scotland showed how insulin requirements and risks of microvascular and macrovascular complications differ by diabetes continuum (tree structure).16 Another analysis of German Diabetes Study further explored the pathophysiological underpinnings of tree structure, examining factors such as insulin sensitivity, insulin secretion, adipose tissue distribution, and pro-inflammatory profiles.17 Our study extends previous studies by applying DDRTree to a nationally representative Chinese cohort and investigating its association with all-cause and cause-specific mortality risk.

In our analysis of CCDRFS, individuals characterized by increased blood pressure and obesity had the greatest risk of CVD mortality. This indicates that high blood pressure and obesity are important triggers of CVD in Chinese population with T2D. This finding contrasts with the Scottish study, where the risk of CVD was primarily driven by a combination of obesity and dyslipidemia in individuals with T2D.16 In our analysis of UKB, we observed a similar pattern, consistent with the Scottish study’s findings. This discrepancy could be partly explained by the higher stroke burden observed among Chinese individuals with T2D,21 where hypertension is a more prominent risk factor for stroke mortality.24 Differences in genetic background, lifestyle behaviors, and medical practices between China and Western countries may further account for these divergent findings. For example, a novel variant at PDE1A has been identified in Chinese patients with T2D, which is associated with both CVD and blood pressure.25 However, this association was less pronounced in European populations.25 Additionally, the traditional Chinese diet, typically high in sodium, could exacerbate hypertension, thus elevating the risk of stroke and other blood pressure-related conditions.26,27 Conversely, Western diets, often rich in saturated fats and cholesterol, could lead to a higher prevalence of dyslipidemia and coronary heart disease in these populations.28 Our study reveals distinct T2D phenotypes associated with the CVD mortality risk in China and the UK, underscoring the importance of understanding each country’s unique challenges and contexts in formulating effective diabetes management and CVD prevention strategies.

The rates of CVD mortality remain high in Chinese population with diabetes,22 in contrast to high-income countries, which have seen a decline in CVD mortality since the 1990s.29 Our findings suggest that more efficient strategies for managing hypertension and obesity in individuals with diabetes may be critical in reversing the rising trend of CVD mortality in China. Currently, management of cardiovascular risk factors among individuals with diabetes in China remains suboptimal. For instance, only 45.0% of individuals achieved blood pressure control below 140/90 mm Hg in 2015, compared to 70.4% in the US.30,31 Additionally, obesity has emerged as a major public health issue in China, with obesity rates rising more rapidly than in many high-income countries.32 To effectively address these challenges, healthcare policymakers and practitioners must develop targeted interventions. These should include improving access to preventive services, prioritizing screening for hypertension and obesity, and promoting education on healthy lifestyle choices for individuals with diabetes.

DKA/coma, the leading contributors to diabetes-specific mortality, is one of the most severe acute metabolic complications of both type 1 and T2D.33 We found that the risk of DKA/coma mortality is greatest in individuals who have higher HbA1c and dyslipidemia. This can be explained by the mechanism of insulin deficiency caused by beta cell dysfunction.34 The lack of insulin prevents the body from utilizing glucose for energy, leading to hyperglycemia, fat breakdown, and ketone production, which in turn cause acidosis.34 Additionally, we observed that individuals with these characteristics are also more likely to receive insulin treatment in CCDRFS. There is ongoing debate about whether insulin deficiency or insulin resistance is more closely related to the initiation of insulin therapy in T2D. A Swedish study found that patients with beta-cell deficiency progress rapidly to requiring insulin,35 while research from Scotland suggested that insulin resistance is the strongest determinant for insulin requirement.16 The inherent vulnerability of poorer beta-cell function in individuals of Asian descent36 may partly explain the critical role of beta-cell dysfunction in driving the subsequent need for insulin therapy observed in our study.

Our study also demonstrated that the risk of COPD mortality was highest in individuals who have higher HDL and TC levels. While previous research indicated that elevated HDL levels were associated with reduced lung function through various pathways affecting endothelial function,37 the role of TC in COPD mortality remains unclear in general populations.38,39 Some studies demonstrated that diabetes contributes to lung function decline through mechanisms including chronic systemic inflammation, obesity, and leptin and insulin resistance,40 However, few studies explored the relationship between cholesterol levels and respiratory outcomes in individuals with diabetes. Our findings imply that cholesterol metabolism may play a crucial role in COPD mortality among individuals with diabetes, highlighting the need for further research to better understand these associations.

In the present study, we constructed separate DDRTree models for the Chinese and UK cohorts to account for well-established differences in genetic backgrounds, environmental factors, phenotype distributions, and disease patterns between populations. Future studies could consider constructing distinct trees for heterogeneous populations to ensure model accuracy, while mapping new cohorts onto existing trees could be explored when cohorts are similar or comparative analyses are prioritized, with careful validation.

The strengths of our study include the use of a large national sample of individuals with newly diagnosed T2D in China and the UK. The seven variables used to construct the phenotype tree are based on standard, easily obtainable measurements, suggesting that this method has the potential to be a valuable tool for providers caring for patients with T2D. Furthermore, the web-based tool we developed holds potential for individualized risk assessment, though its applicability requires further validation.

Conclusions

Leveraging nationwide data from China and the UK, we applied data dimensionality reduction on routinely collected clinical data to visualize diabetes phenotypic heterogeneity on a tree-like structure. Our findings reveal significant variability in mortality risks across the complex phenotypic spectrum of T2D. Additionally, our data highlight the need for tailored interventions to address the specific health risks and needs of diverse populations. These findings may help enhance the precision of risk stratification, facilitate early intervention, and optimize resource allocation for individuals with T2D.

Limitations of the study

First, the small number of deaths from DKA/coma reduced the precision of our estimates, although we observed consistent results across both cohorts. Second, this analysis was based on a limited set of clinically available phenotypes without considering potential interactions among them. While this enhances interpretability and clinical applicability, future studies incorporating more comprehensive biomarker data and advanced modeling approaches are warranted to provide further insights into diabetes heterogeneity. Third, the UKB is limited to adults aged 40–69 at baseline, and some selection bias may exist in the CCDRFS despite its nationally representative design and high response rate. Caution is therefore warranted when generalizing our findings, and external validation in independent populations is needed. Fourth, although we focused on newly diagnosed T2D cases to minimize the influence of diabetes medications on phenotypic variables, we acknowledge that medication use during follow-up could still influence disease trajectories. Future longitudinal studies with detailed treatment data are needed to further evaluate the robustness of these phenotypes over time and in treated populations.

Experimental model and subject details

Study populations

The CCDRFS is a nationally representative study organized by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). It was designed to monitor the epidemiology of noncommunicable diseases and their risk factors in China. In the 2013 survey, 179,347 participants were enrolled from 298 sites across all 31 provinces in mainland China using a multistage stratified sampling design, achieving a response rate of 93.4%.41 Trained interviewers from local CDCs carried out the face-to-face interviews, physical measurements, biochemical sample collection and sample pretreatment. The CCDRFS were approved by the ethical review committee of the Chinese CDC and other participating institutes. All participants signed the written informed consent. The details of study design are described elsewhere.41

The UKB is a large, population-based prospective study. Between March 2006 and July 2010, it recruited 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years from 22 assessment centers across England, Wales, and Scotland. Each assessment center visit lasted approximately 90 min, during which participants underwent questionnaire administration, physical measurements, and the collection of biological samples. UKB were approved by the ethical review committee of the UK National Health Service (NHS) National Research Ethics Service. All participants signed the written informed consent.

The following data were used to define newly diagnosed T2D at baseline: hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels, oral glucose tolerance test results of fasting plasma glucose and 2-h plasma glucose levels or hospital inpatient data, if available.42 Cohort-specific definitions of T2D are detailed below. Participants diagnosed with diabetes before age 35 (likely type 1 diabetes), those with incomplete data on clinical risk factors and those with the outlier values (the top and bottom 0.5% of the distribution of clinical measures) were excluded. Baseline characteristics were similar between individuals with and without available clinical phenotype data (Table S5). In total, 10,091 and 5991 patients were included in the analysis with complete data in CCDRFS and UKB, respectively (Figure S11).

Method details

Definition of newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetes

In CCDRFS, newly diagnosed cases of T2D were identified based on criteria from the American Diabetes Association, ensuring no prior history of diabetes at baseline. The diagnosis required meeting at least one of the following conditions: 1) plasma glucose level ≥7 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) with fasting time ≥8 h, 2) postprandial 2- hour plasma glucose level ≥11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) from an oral glucose tolerance test), 3) Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level ≥6.5% (48 mmol/mol).42

In UKB, newly diagnosed cases of T2D were defined as either HbA1c ≥ 6.5% with no prior history of diabetes at baseline or a recorded diagnosis of diabetes in the hospital inpatient dataset within one year before or after the baseline.

Measurements of T2D-related phenotypes

We used nine phenotypes that are commonly measured in routine clinical practice and reflect various factors known to be altered in T2D and linked to variation in mortality risk, which include age of diagnosis, gender, HbA1c, BMI, HDL-C, TG, TC, SBP and DBP at diagnosis. These phenotypes were assessed using standardized protocols in each cohort. In CCDRFS, Anthropometric variables such as height, weight, were measured by trained staff in accordance with World Health Organization standards. BMI was calculated from weight/height2 (kg/m2). After 5 min rest, three consecutive measures of blood pressure) were assessed using the Omron digital blood pressure monitor (Omron, Dalian, China) with 1-min between each measure. The mean of these 3 measurements was considered as participants’ blood pressure. Levels of TC, triglyceride, and HDL-C were tested by using a ROCHE COBAS C702 Automated clinical chemistry analyzer. TC levels were measured by cholesterol oxidase method, HDL-C levels by direct method, and triglyceride levels by enzymatic method. HbA1c was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography analysis. Details of the laboratory procedures and quality control methods have been described previously.43

In UKB, BMI was similarly calculated. After 5 min of seated rest, blood pressure was measured on 2 consecutive occasions with a 1-min interval; the mean of the first and the second automated readings was adopted for the data analysis whenever available. HDL-C, triglycerides, and TC were assayed using the AU5800 analytical platform (Beckman Coulter). HDL-C was analyzed using enzyme immune-inhibition method. Triglyceride and TC were assayed using enzymatic analysis. HbA1c was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography analysis. The laboratory procedures and quality control methods can be found in the UKB’s online protocol (www.ukbiobank.ac.uk).

Ascertainment of death

Mortality was ascertained by linking participants’ name and ID number to the China’s Disease Surveillance Points system through December 31, 2019. All death records were reviewed annually, following quality control procedures.44 In UKB, deaths were obtained through death certificates held within the NHS Information Center (England and Wales) and the NHS Central Register (Scotland) to December 20, 2022. Underlying causes of death were classified according to the 10th revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Injuries, and Causes of Death. Our primary outcomes are the four major causes of death among people with diabetes in both China and the UK, namely death from CVD, cancer, DKA/coma and COPD.22,29 The Table S6 shows specific codes for causes of death.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Discriminative Dimensionality Reduction with Trees

To better organize the phenotype data for individuals at the time of T2D diagnosis, we utilized the DDRTree algorithm.14,15 DDRTree is a dimensionality reduction approach that combines a reverse graph structure embedding algorithm with a mapping function. It projects multi-dimensional into a 2-dimensional space structured as a tree. DDRTree has been shown to outperform other dimensionality reduction methods, such as local linear embedding and principal component analysis, in terms of accuracy and normalized mutual information across various datasets.15 For our analysis, we first applied a rank-based inverse normal transformation to standardize the seven continuous phenotypic variables. We then adjusted each phenotype for age and gender using linear regression analysis, creating an age- and gender-residualized phenotype matrix. This adjustment helps to prevent age and gender—major drivers of adverse outcomes—from dominating the phenotypic tree structure, allowing clearer identification of metabolic heterogeneity independent of these demographic factors. The residualized matrix was subsequently used to construct a two-dimensional minimum spanning tree. In this structure, the distal ends of the branches represent extreme phenotypes, while the center of the tree represents mixed phenotypes.

Internal validation

The cohort was randomly split into two independent subsets: 80% of participants were assigned to the derivation set for model training, and the remaining 20% formed the validation set for internal testing. The baseline characteristics between the derivation and validation sets were comparable (Table S7). In the derivation set, we applied the DDRTree algorithm to perform dimensionality reduction on the phenotypic variables, deriving coordinates for Dimension 1 and Dimension 2. Next, a GAM was trained within the derivation set to predict these two dimensions. The GAM incorporated age, gender, and the seven phenotypic variables as predictors, using smooth functions and penalized regression splines to capture potential nonlinear relationships. Using the fitted GAMs, we predicted Dimension 1 and 2 values for individuals in the validation set. Consistent with a previous study,18 these predicted values were then compared with the actual DDRTree-derived coordinates calculated directly from the validation set using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (ρs), assessing the stability of the DDRTree algorithm.

We also estimated the ARI to validate the stability of the DDRTree algorithm. ARI quantifies the agreement for positioning the individual in the same tree branch at different executions of the algorithm. The ARI ranges from −1 to 1, with values close to 1 indicating perfect agreement. We considered an ARI >0.65 as evidence of good stability. We conducted 50 iterations of subsampling. In each iteration, 10% of the total sample population was randomly excluded, and the DDRTree model was reconstructed using the remaining data. Branch labels were assigned to each individual based on their position in the tree structure. While estimating the ARI, we excluded the contribution from individuals located in the principal tree trunk or the center of the tree. Pairwise ARIs were calculated based on the remaining branch labels to evaluate the consistency of individual assignments across iterations.

Risk estimates of diabetes-related outcome

Person-time was calculated as the interval between baseline and the date of death, or censoring, whichever occurred first. Competing risk model (Fine–Gray model) was used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs, constructed with DDRTree dimensions as covariates. In competing risk models, cause of death other than the one of interest was considered as a ‘competing event’. To avoid reverse causation, we excluded individuals who died within the first year of follow-up. We also conducted sensitivity analyses excluding participants with pre-existing conditions (CVD and cancer). Using these model and each individual’s position in reduced space, we predicted HRs for that individual. These HRs were superimposed on the tree diagram to visualize the heterogeneity in mortality risk. Additionally, we assessed the probability of insulin use as the outcome among individuals with previously diagnosed T2D in CCDRFS. These individuals were mapped to tree using a ‘mapping function’16; which uses generalized additive models to predict the low-dimensional coordinates based on age, gender, and seven phenotypic variables and then assigns each individual to the nearest point on the reference tree based on Euclidean distance. Insulin use was defined as self-reported use of insulin, and we constructed a logistic model with DDRTree dimensions as covariates. The predicted probability for insulin use was superimposed on the tree.

Quantitative the distribution pattern across the tree

We quantified the variation in phenotypes and outcomes across the tree using two approaches. First, we examined phenotype distribution by regressing each phenotype against the tree dimensions. To assess mortality risk distribution, we used HR from a competing risk model, illustrating how mortality risk varied across the two-dimensional space. Second, we performed spatial autocorrelation analysis with global and local Moran’s I statistics, considering each individual’s spatial coordinates and the associated phenotypic values or HRs. A positive Moran’s I indicates clustering of similar values, while a negative value suggests clustering of dissimilar values. To estimate local Moran’s I, we considered 100 neighbors and calculated the Moran’s I for each individual.

We developed the R Shiny app and prediction models for all-cause and cause-specific mortality. Using the ‘mapping function’, we determined the position of newly diagnosed T2D patients on the tree. To predict the risk of all-cause and cause-specific mortality, we employed the cox models constructed with age, gender and the seven other continuous phenotypic measures for each outcome. The predictive performance of the model was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Then, we used Kaplan-Meier methodology to generate estimates of cumulative risk of mortality.
