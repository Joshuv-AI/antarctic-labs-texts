Safety and effectiveness of marjoram and cinnamon herbal tea supplementation in managing polycystic ovary syndrome: a pilot randomized, double-blind, controlled trial
Authors: Nasreen Ghafor Majeed, Mohammed Ibrahim Mohialdeen Gubari, Paxshan Ashraf Mohammed, Kosar Shirwan Tahir, Jeza Muhamad Abdul Aziz, Jalal Karim Omar, Abdelrahman M. Makram, Omar Mohamed Makram, Chro Najmaddin Fattah, Rebwar Hassan Khdhir, Lava Abdullah, Nguyen Tien Huy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine disorder among women of reproductive age and the most common cause of infertility due to a lack of ovulation. This pilot study investigated the efficacy and safety of a three-month herbal tea intervention combining marjoram and cinnamon for managing PCOS symptoms.Methods:A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial was conducted with women diagnosed with PCOS (n = 60). A limited sample size was used for this pilot study. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a twice-daily herbal tea (n = 30) or a placebo tea (n = 30) for 3 months. Monthly monitoring assessed compliance and adverse effects.Results:Only 36 participants completed the treatment in the herbal intervention and placebo groups. No major side effects were reported in either group. In the placebo group, a significant decrease in anti-Müllerian hormone levels (P= 0.023, 95%-CI: 0.27 to 2.99), and significant increases in prolactin (P= 0.032, 95%-CI: −11.20 to −0.59) and high-density lipoprotein (P= 0.042, 95%-CI: −7.10 to −0.15) levels were observed. In the herbal intervention group, a significant increase in follicle-stimulating hormone levels (P= 0.032, 95%-CI: −0.95 to −0.05) and a significant decrease in AMH levels (P= 0.003, 95%-CI: 0.58 to 2.56) were found.Conclusion:This pilot trial suggests that a combination of marjoram and cinnamon herbal tea may positively influence hormonal profiles in women with PCOS, warranting further investigation with larger sample sizes and longer durations to confirm these findings and explore the clinical significance of the observed changes.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) stands as the most prevalent endocrine disorder among women of reproductive age, affecting 6% to 20% globally, depending on diagnostic criteria[1–3]. Characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology, PCOS is a leading cause of infertility and hirsutism. Moreover, PCOS is associated with significant metabolic disturbances, increasing the risk of insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease[4,5]. The exact etiology of PCOS remains elusive, with both genetic and environmental factors implicated[7]. Where insulin resistance, obesity, inflammation, and oxidative stress may be involved in the pathogenesis[8]. Despite its high prevalence and substantial health implications, a definitive cure for PCOS is lacking. Current management strategies primarily focus on symptom alleviation through lifestyle modifications and pharmacological interventions like oral contraceptives, anti-androgens, ovulation induction drugs, and insulin sensitizers[9–12]. However, these treatments often address individual symptoms[13], can have limitations due to side effects, contraindications, and compliance issues, particularly with long-term use[14].

Botanical medicine has been used for centuries as a therapy[15]. Within the last 10 years, herbal medicines have received significant attention in global health[16]. Studies have shown that two out of five PCOS women used medicinal plants to alleviate their symptoms[9,10]. Since some of these substances exhibit steroidogenic effects, express estrogen receptor proteins, decrease androgen levels, and enhance glucose uptake[2]. One advantage of herbal medicine over conventional therapy is that it is presumably safer, given that they are available in nature[17].

Several specific herbs have been investigated for their potential benefits in PCOS. Marjoram (Origanum majorana), a member of the mint family traditionally used for hormonal imbalances, possesses anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties and has shown promise in improving insulin sensitivity and reducing androgen levels[18,19]. Similarly, spearmint tea (Mentha spicata) has demonstrated anti-androgenic effects by lowering testosterone levels and impacting ovarian follicle development in PCOS patients, alongside exhibiting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities[16,17,20]. Cinnamon (Cinnamomum zeylanicum), from the Lauraceae family, also displays antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, may improve insulin sensitivity, and has been reported to decrease AMH levels in women with PCOS[2,7]. Lastly, Vitex castus is well known traditionally for its ability to regulate the menstrual cycle[7]. Through its anti-androgenic and phytoestrogenic properties[21].

While individual herbs have shown promise, evidence supporting the use of herbal combinations for PCOS management remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the role of a readily accessible herbal mixture of marjoram and cinnamon in managing PCOS in affected women.

Materials and methods

Study design and protocol development

The pilot study was set as randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial. The gynecologists, and the patients were blinded to the study group category. Of eighty women, only Sixty with untreated PCOS were enrolled in the study and randomly allocated to either the intervention (n = 30) or placebo group (n = 30) using Random Allocation Software, version 1.0.0. The study followed the CONSORT criteria (Fig. 1)[22].Figure 1.Flow chart of enrolment and analysis (CONSORT diagram).

Flow chart of enrolment and analysis (CONSORT diagram).

Participants

We included women aged 16–36 years who were diagnosed with PCOS. The diagnosis of PCOS was made according to the Rotterdam criteria[23]. Which were selected due to their widespread acceptance in clinical and research settings[23–25].

Exclusion criteria were menarche less than 2 years; any chronic illness, such as renal disease, diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disease, or bleeding tendency; and long-term pharmacological treatments. Participants were also excluded if they were receiving conventional therapy for PCOS, were pregnant, or were already using any herbal treatment.

Diagnostic criteria

To diagnose PCOS using the Rotterdam criteria, the patient must exhibit at least two of three elements: clinical and biochemical hyperandrogenism, oligo-ovulation or anovulation, and polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) on ultrasound[24].

Clinical hyperandrogenism was defined by the presence of hirsutism (modified Ferriman-Gallwey score ≥ 8 severe and persistent acne, or alopecia[26]. In cases where clinical hyperandrogenism was unclear, biochemical hyperandrogenism was assessed by measuring free testosterone and the free androgen index (FAI = [total testosterone/SHBG] × 100) in the early follicular phase. If these values were within the laboratory’s normal range but suspicion remained, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S) levels were measured. Laboratory-specific normal ranges were used[26]. For reference, the upper limits for total testosterone were 0.383 ng/mL for women under 18 years and 0.481 ng/mL for older women, with a lower limit for SHBG of 32.4 mIU/mL and a normal FAI value below 5qq443. Age-specific normal ranges for DHEA-S were also utilized: 16–19 years (65.1–368 µg/dL), 20–25 years (148–407 µg/dL), 25–34 years (98.8–340 µg/dL), and 35–45 years (60.9–337 µg/dL). Markedly elevated androgen levels prompted exclusion of other androgen-secreting conditions (e.g., androgen-secreting tumors, non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia). Oligo-ovulation was defined as an oligomenorrhea cycle longer than 35 days. Amenorrhoea meant the absence of menstruation for 6 months, while a frequent cycle was diagnosed when the cycle was shorter than 21 days[1,19]. PCOM was defined as the presence of ≥20 follicles per ovary (measuring 2–9 mm in diameter) or an ovarian volume ≥10 Ml[25].

Investigations

On the second or third day of the spontaneous or withdrawal cycle, blood was collected from all participants and sent for investigation in the morning after overnight fasting for 10 hours. We analyzed LH, FSH, CBC, C-reactive protein, lipid profile (total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein [HDL], low-density lipoprotein [LDL], triglyceride [TG][22], glucose, and insulin, from which insulin resistance was obtained using the HOMA-IR calculator ([fasting blood glucose, mg/dL] * [fasting insulin IU/mL] divided by 22.5 value of 3.8), indicating insulin resistance. All participants then had an abdominal ultrasound because most of the participants were virgo intacta. Patients were also sent for body composition tests to assess body mass index and waist-hip ratio.

Material preparation

Factory provides the powder from dried leaves of two herbal plants (marjoram and cinnamon), and one biologist who has long experience in herbal medicine prepared it, and a placebo material from a vegetable which has not found to have any effects traditionally or by any evidence on our target, after boiling it for sufficient period then dried them. Herbal materials were put on the container at a therapeutic dose; again, placebo material, similar outer package of both, was used. Participants were advised to prepare powdered tea by steeping the container in a glass of hot water, leaving it for 10 minutes, and then drinking it without eating or drinking anything 1 hour before and after, two times daily for three months.

Follow-up

Participants were monitored monthly for general well-being, adverse effects, and adherence to the assigned treatment protocol. Any participant demonstrating consistent reluctance or inability to use the materials correctly was excluded. At the end of the 3-month intervention, all baseline investigations, including ultrasound and body composition tests, were repeated on the 2nd or 3rd day of the menstrual cycle. Menstrual cycle length and Ferriman scores were also reassessed at this final visit. During the study, 24 participants withdrew due to lost contact or inability to follow up and changed decision, only 36 participants completed the trial (intervention group: n = 21, placebo group: n = 15).

Data analysis

The analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version[22]. Descriptive statistics were used to determine the frequency, number, mean, and standard deviation of the basic characteristics of the participants. The paired t-test was used to compare the mean difference between the pre- and post-placebo and herbal treatments for parametric variables. The P-value was tested at a significance level of P < 0.05. No sample size calculation was done for the pilot study. It was intended that a sample of 35–40 participants may be enough to yield preliminary results.

Results

None of the participants reported any major side effects. In the intervention group, minor side effects included mild colicky abdominal pain and bitter and bad taste. The patients felt more relaxed and slept well, had better bowel motion and gaseous distension before tea consumption, and reported that constipation and gaseous distension were now better. The final number of participants was 36, of whom 21 and 15 participated in the intervention and placebo groups, respectively. The ages of the participants ranged from 16 to 36 years. More than half of the participants were privately employed, while the rest were either unemployed or students. Most resided in urban areas. In both groups, half of the women were married, and half were not. Discussion

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder that affects women of reproductive age. It is characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovaries[27]. The management of PCOS typically involves lifestyle modifications, pharmacological interventions, and surgery. However, there is a growing interest in the use of herbal remedies for the management of PCOS[28,29].

Herbal remedies have been used for centuries to treat various ailments, including hormonal imbalances. Some herbal remedies, such as cinnamon, green tea, ginger, Cimicifuga racemosa, Nigella sativa, and Vitex agnus-castus, have shown promising effects in managing PCOS. These herbal remedies have been shown to improve menstrual regularity, hormonal profiles, and metabolic parameters in women with PCOS[7,9,30].

This pilot randomized, double-blind, controlled trial provides preliminary evidence on the safety and potential effectiveness of a marjoram-cinnamon herbal tea supplement in the management of PCOS. The findings suggest that this herbal intervention is well-tolerated and may influence key hormonal and metabolic parameters associated with the condition. The results showed that herbal intervention led to a significant decrease in AMH (P= 0.003). The reduction in AMH observed in our intervention group aligns with the findings of a study on marjoram tea, which demonstrated its ability to significantly reduce adrenal androgens in women with PCOS[19]. High serum AMH levels represent a key feature of PCOS, indicating altered folliculogenesis and increased numbers of small antral follicles typical of the syndrome[31]. Our findings are consistent with the preliminary studies of herbal treatments. For instance, a study on spearmint tea, another herbal therapy with anti-androgenic properties, also demonstrated a trend toward reducing androgen levels, which are closely linked to AMH secretion[32]. A similar study by Haj-Husein et al showed that marjoram tea markedly decreased adrenal androgens in PCOS women, implying a modulatory effect on the steroidogenic pathway[19]. This suggests that certain herbal compounds may modulate ovarian folliculogenesis, potentially improving ovulatory function.

On the other hand, significant increase in FSH levels (P = 0.032) was reported in the herbal group, a change not seen in the placebo group. An increase in FSH could be beneficial, as it may help stimulate follicular development and maturation, counteracting the arrested folliculogenesis common in PCOS[33]. This effect on FSH, coupled with the reduction in AMH, points toward a potential normalizing effect of the marjoram-cinnamon on the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis activity, a key therapeutic target in PCOS management[34]. This is consistent with the proven phytochemical activities of marjoram, such as flavonoids and phenolics affecting neuroendocrine function[35].

Compared with a few previous reports of cinnamon monotherapy, our combination herbal intervention tea provided no significant beneficial effect on metabolic variables such as fasting insulin and insulin resistance, and lipid profile during the study period. This is in contrast with other studies, which reported that cinnamon significantly reduced fasting insulin and insulin resistance in PCOS subjects[10,36,37]. A review by Farzana et al investigated the effects of medicinal plants on PCOS. They observed positive effects in PCOS patients, including decreased LH and insulin levels, which aligns with the findings of our study. Furthermore, other research suggests that medicinal plants such as cinnamon species, Vitex agnus-castus, Foeniculum vulgare Mill., and Linum usitatissimum L. appear to regulate menstrual cycles and improve hormonal and metabolic indicators[36,38].

The discrepancy between our findings and prior research could be attributed to several factors, including cinnamon delivered in a low dose in the form of a tea compared to a concentrated extract in other studies or to their particular synergistic combination with marjoram, or to the relatively short length of our pilot study, which may result inability to induce detectable metabolic changes in a small group of subjects. The placebo group results were notable for a significant increase in prolactin and HDL levels. The increase in prolactin was unexpected and its clinical significance in this context is unclear. The rise in HDL, however, is a positive finding, though it is difficult to attribute to the placebo itself and may be related to other lifestyle factors.

The results of this study showed that the herbal plant mixture was well tolerated, with no major side effects. Minor side effects in the intervention group included mild colicky abdominal pain, bitter taste, and bad taste. The patients reported feeling more relaxed and sleeping better with improved bowel movement and gaseous distension. Marjoram has been traditionally used to aid digestion and reduce stress[39]. Similarly, cinnamon has been previously shown to be safe and well-tolerated in dietary doses[40].

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several notable strengths. The randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled design provides a robust framework for evaluating the efficacy of the herbal intervention while minimizing bias. The prospective monthly monitoring allowed for careful assessment of well-being, adverse effects, and compliance. Furthermore, the study addressed a clinically relevant issue, i.e., the management of PCOS, using standardized diagnostic criteria and assessing a range of pertinent hormonal and metabolic parameters. Finally, the preregistration of the trial enhances transparency and rigor.

Despite the identified strengths, this study was subject to several limitations. The relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle but potentially important effects. Furthermore, the single-center design and recruitment methods could introduce selection bias, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings. The inherent taste and smell of the herbal mixture raised the possibility of unintentional unblinding of participants. The short intervention duration of three months may not have been sufficient to observe long-term effects or capture the full spectrum of potential benefits. Additionally, dietary intake and lifestyle factors were not strictly controlled or monitored during the study, which could have influenced the results and acted as confounding variables. Finally, the COVID-19 pandemic presented challenges during the study period, contributing to loss to follow-up for some participants.

These limitations underscore the need for future research with larger, multi-center trials, longer follow-up periods, and strategies to minimize the risk of unblinding, as well as methods to account for dietary and lifestyle influences and employ more objective outcome measures where possible.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this double-blind randomized controlled trial investigating a herbal plant mixture (marjoram and cinnamon) suggest a potential influence on hormonal profiles in women with PCOS, as evidenced by the significant increase in FSH and decrease in AMH levels. While no major side effects were identified within the three-month intervention, further research is necessary to confirm these hormonal changes, elucidate their clinical significance in managing PCOS symptoms, and determine the long-term effects of this herbal approach. These preliminary results warrant larger, longer-duration studies to fully evaluate the potential of this herbal combination as a management strategy for PCOS.
