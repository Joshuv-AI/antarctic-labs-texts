Innate knowledge of herbal medicine among the Sonowal Kachari tribe of Lakhimpur district, Assam, India
Authors: Pramod Chandra Dihingia, Deepika Borah, Wungsim Zimik, Prabhakar Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe medicinal value of herbal plants is preserved in selected tribal communities of India.ObjectiveThe main objective of this study is to identify the traditional knowledge of herbal medicines in the Sonowal Kachari tribe of Lakhimpur District, Assam, India.Materials and methodsThe extensive data was collected in this study between April 2022 and November 2023 in the selected village of Assam, India through a face-to-face interview using a standardized questionnaire. In total, 180 elderly people, including males and females, were chosen in the age group of 50 and older.Results and discussionThe results indicated that 39 plants have medicinal value. The informants reported that they used leaves (54.34 %), followed by roots (10.87 %), whole plants (8.7 %), fruits (8.7 %), flowers (6.52 %), bulbs, bark, rhizomes, latex, and stems (2.17 % each) for medicines. Out of 39 plant species reported, the study identified the use of four types of plants for the treatment of fever, followed by three plants each for the treatment of cold cough, dysentery, and skin diseases; 3 plants for the treatment of hair growth, stomach pain, bleeding, arthritis, rheumatic pain, tonics, and diarrhea; and one plant for the treatment of pneumonia, stimulants, indigestion, anti-inflammatory, burns, liver and urinary trouble, kidney stone, eczema, bone fracture, jaundice, anti-diabetic, carbuncle, constipation, dying nail, anemia, cuts and wounds, high blood pressure diseases. The study also revealed that herbs (46.15 %), trees (25.64 %), climbers (15.38 %), and shrubs (12.82 %) were used for treatment by Sonowal Kachari tribe.ConclusionsThis study highlights the Sonowal Kachari tribe of Assam has significant contribution to herbal medicine and offers a promising repository for discovering new therapeutics.Keywords: Drug discovery, Tribe, Herbal medicine, Sonowal Kachari, Traditional knowledge

Introduction

Since the inception of human civilization, plant resources have been a source of medicine and food for the survival of ancient people. Herbal medicines have little to no side effects compared to chemically prepared drugs. The ethnic groups have innate knowledge from their forefathers about medicinal plants and their usefulness in treating different diseases. In the literature, sufficient evidence is available about using medicinal plants in India through the Ayurveda, Siddha, and Unani systems of medicine fabkant [1]. Though various traditional systems of medicine are practiced around the world with self-belief and social causes, Indian traditional medicine is found to be the most ancient practice [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) revealed that herbal medicines cater to the health needs of about 80 % of the world's population, particularly millions of people in rural areas of developing countries [3]. However, the innate knowledge of herbal medicine varies community- and geographical location-wise. The North-East region of India is located at the foothills of the eastern Himalayas, and it differs from mainland India in respect of the agro-climatic and geographical diversity that belongs to the foothills of the eastern Himalayas and is naturally rich in herbal medicine. The ethnic and cultural diversity of North-East India is attractive, and there is more information about medicinal plants for the treatment of various diseases because of the abundance of herbs [4]. Traditionally, the ethnic group of people acquired knowledge and the benefits of medicinal plants from their forefathers [5]. In rural areas, some elderly people, called Baidya (an Ayurvedic Physician in Sanskrit language), treat diseases by applying herbs and thus earn a livelihood. They usually do not want to disclose their innate knowledge to the people. Arunachal Pradesh and north Assam belong to the foothills of the eastern Himalayas in India and are said to be the natural gardens of herbal medicine [6]. The indigenous plant species used in food, herbal medicine, handicrafts, hunting, and culture revealed that the resource of bio-culturally rich plant biodiversity is the backbone of the livelihoods of the Apatani tribe in Arunachal Pradesh [7]. A similar study was conducted on herbal medicinal plants among the tribes of Assam in the last couple of years. Literature reveals the prevalence of traditional treatment methods among the Mishing, Bodo, Santhal, Karbi, Sonowal Kachari tribes of Assam, India [8].

Due to deforestation and social changes, folk culture and tradition have faced serious threats that may lead to the loss of traditional practices soon [9]. Despite the threats, among the economically weaker section of rural tribal families, herbal medicines are being used for treatment, and the rural medicinal sector has traditionally occupied a vital position in the socio-cultural and spiritual area traditionally [10]. Moreover, the new generation prefers traditional wisdom less than readily available allopathic medicine. Hence, it is necessary to conduct a scientific study on the traditional knowledge of herbal medicine among ethnic groups with a view to preservation and documentation at present. Recognizing herbal medicine's importance, usefulness, and reliance on healthcare, it is felt that a broad study of herbal medicinal plants is necessary. Therefore, the present study has been undertaken to (i) identify the traditional knowledge of herbal medicines of the Sonowal Kachari tribe of Lakhimpur district, Assam, and (ii) make a strategy to conserve them from extinction that would be helpful for future reference in drug discovery.

Materials and methods

Geographical identity of the study area

The study was conducted in Chalajan Kachari village, Lakhimpur district, Assam, India (Fig. 1). The village is predominated by the Sonowal Kachari tribe, which is one of the indigenous tribes of Assam. It is located 18 km from the sub-district headquarters Ghilamara and 90 km from the district headquarters Lakhimpur of Assam state in India.

Map of Lakhimpur district, Assam, India.

Lakhimpur district is bound by Arunachal Pradesh on the north, Biswanath district on the west, Dhemaji district on the east, and the Brahmaputra River on the south. The district lies between 26o48' and 27o53' of northern latitude and 93o42' and 94o20' of east longitude (approx) [11]. The agro-climatic condition of Chalajan Kachari village is similar to the foothills of Arunachal Pradesh, an environment with heavy rainfall and high humidity. The climatic conditions of this area are highly humid in the summer and pleasant in the winter; therefore, the soil is favorable for significant herb species.

The Sonowal Kachari tribe

The Sonowal Kacharis are one of the aboriginal plain scheduled tribes of Assam, India. They evolved from a section of the Kachari's family. The Sonowal Kachari tribe belongs to the mongoloid race of people. Their skin colour is yellowish brown, and they have black, thick, straight hair. Their face is broad and flat with prominent cheekbones. Their eyes are obliquely set with narrow slit openings and are brown [12]. The Sonowal Kachari tribes are health conscious and have their indigenous methods of treating different kinds of diseases. Different types of easily accessible herbs and leaves of wild creepers are used for treatment as medicine, following the knowledge inherited from their ancestors [13]. The exact origin of the tribe is not found in literature from where they came and how the word Sonowal was derived. However, according to some historians, they were mostly gold washers in the erstwhile Lakhimpur district of Assam, India. According to 2011 census of India, the total population of the Sonowal Kachari tribe in Assam is 2,53,344 in number, which comprises 1,27,692 males and 1,25,652 females [14]. The majority of people are living in rural areas (2,31,061 nos.), and very few are settled in urban areas (22,283 nos.). The census revealed that the literacy rate is 85.4 percent, where the female literacy rate is found to be lower (80.5 %) than the male counterpart (90.4 %). The Sonowal Kachari tribe is resourceful in their folklore and traditional life, rich in art and culture. The major folk dances of the Sonowal Kachari tribes are related to the cropping season and include Bohua dance, Hugra dance, Haidang dance, Leseri dance, Log Bihu, Kulabudi dance, and Huori [15]. Unlike other tribal societies in Assam, the Sonowal Kachari tribe maintains a unique social system [16]. The system is called "bari," which is based on matrilineal origin. In this system, women play a pivotal role in the community's social and economic activities. According to the “bari” system, properties are received from the mother's side of the family. In this system, the head of each “bari” is a woman, where inheritance passes from mother to daughter [12]. Hence, women occupy an important place in their society. They are directly involved in agricultural activities about the production of crops, harvesting, and selling in the market. They have to do very hard labour to improve their economic condition. Agriculture is the prime source of income for the Sonowal Kachari tribe. The economic condition of the tribe is not good; they have to earn their livelihood by hard work.

Selection of subjects

Prior to the selection of subjects, a list of elderly people was prepared in consultation of the leader of the village. For the study, 180 elderly people, including males and females, in the age group of 50 and older were selected. The enlisted people who volunteered to make themselves available were accepted as subjects.

Method of data collection

The study collected data through a face-to-face interview with the villagers. Before the interview, a standard questionnaire was prepared in the local language based on the Nordic questionnaire, including demographic information and knowledge of herbal medicine in the community used for healthcare. The Nordic questionnaire consists of structured multiple-choice questions and can be used as a self-administered questionnaire or as an interview [17]. It was initially developed in the framework of a project supported by the Nordic Council of Ministers.

Nordic questionnaire was not directly used in the present study; however, a new set of questionnaires was prepared based on the Nordic questionnaire. The Nordic questionnaire has many advantages such as the questions being standardized, which allows for consistent results, the questionnaire being free to use, the questionnaire being used on large populations, etc. Therefore, the questionnaire is fit for all regional communities.

To evaluate the questionnaire in advance to assess whether the questions will cause any problems for respondents and interviewers, and meet its intended objectives a pre-test of the questionnaire was conducted [17]. In the pre-test, ten respondents were included based on age and gender [18]. The common people of the community found some difficulties in understanding some words/names of herbal medicinal plants mentioned in the questionnaire. Therefore, the questionnaire was modified according to the respondents’ convenience words/names. The survey was conducted from April 2022 to November 2023. The leader of the village, Gram Pradhan (Gaonburha), and the secretary of the Village Defence Party (VDP) were contacted personally, and the aim and usefulness of the survey were explained in advance. The survey procedure was also described in detail to them, and after getting their consent to cooperate with the study, meetings with the elderly villagers were conducted.

Results and discussion

The study reveals that the elderly people in the community are well aware of the side effects of allopathic medicine. They rely on herbal medicine rather than allopathic medicine. The present study retrieved 39 nos. of medicinal plants, including shrubs, trees, climbers, etc., that are used for the treatment of skin diseases, fever, digestive problems, diabetes, diarrhea, bone fractures, kidney stones, menstrual problems, jaundice, cough, chronic dysentery, arthritis, rheumatic pain, cuts and wounds, anemia, liver and urinary troubles, hair growth, etc. The herbal medicinal plants with their scientific name, local names, habits, used parts, and applications are illustrated in Table 1.

In the present study, the informants reported the maximum use of leaves (54.34 %), followed by roots (10.87 %), whole plants (8.7 %), fruits (8.7 %), flowers (6.52 %), bulbs, bark, rhizomes, latex, and stems (2.17 % each). Previous researchers have also reported the maximum use of leaves for the preparation of herbal medicine [[19], [20], [21]]. The reason behind the maximum use of leaves might be the easy collection and processing [22].

Out of 39 plant species reported, the study identified the use of four plants for the treatment of fever, followed by three plants each for the treatment of cold cough, dysentery, and skin diseases; two plants each for the treatment of hair growth, stomach pain, bleeding, arthritis, rheumatic pain, tonics, and diarrhea; and one plant each for the treatment of pneumonia, stimulant, indigestion, anti-inflammatory, burns, liver and urinary trouble, kidney stones, eczema, bone fracture, jaundice, anti-diabetic, carbuncle, constipation, dying nail, anemia, cuts and wounds, and high blood pressure. The present study also revealed that out of 39 species, the most frequently used is herbaceous life form (46.15 %), followed by trees (25.64 %), climbers (15.38 %), and shrubs (12.82 %). The outcome of the research is aligned with previous studies that also showed the use of herbaceous life forms for the treatment of diseases [23].

Herbaceous species are abundant in nature comparatively. Hence, these species are often used as medicine. In a similar study on the herbal medicine of the Irula tribes of the Southern Western Ghats, India also enlightened the higher use of herbaceous life forms for treating disease [24].

The findings of this study reveal a profound repository of herbal medicinal knowledge among the Sonowal Kachari tribe of Lakhimpur district, Assam, highlighting their reliance on local flora for treating a variety of ailments. Identifying 39 medicinal plants and their diverse applications demonstrates the tribe's comprehensive understanding of plant-based therapeutics. The predominance of herbaceous plants and the extensive use of leaves reflect a sophisticated approach to natural medicine, underscoring the potential of these plants in modern drug discovery. This study documents valuable traditional practices and emphasizes the need for further scientific exploration and validation of these remedies. This study unlocks new avenues for developing effective and sustainable healthcare solutions by bridging traditional knowledge with contemporary research.

Recommendations for future work

The traditional knowledge of herbal medicines is acquired through unwritten hereditary processes, exchanged through generations orally, and not scientifically structured. Among the young generation, interest in traditional medicinal plants is declining. They need to be encouraged to study and preserve this traditional knowledge for the betterment of mankind. A model for the conservation of herbal medicinal plants and promotion of herbal medicinal knowledge among the new generation is advocated, as shown in Fig. 2. Moreover, to protect and promote herbal medicinal knowledge, equal importance must be given by all ethnic groups of people to the government.

Model for conservation and promotion of herbal medicinal knowledge.

In this study, the model implies that for conserving medicinal plants from extinct species, a broad initiative is to be taken at the departmental level by collecting medicinal plants from different sources and distributing them to individuals. The modern health infrastructure, services, and professionals play a significant role in shaping the treatment-seeking behavior of the community. In rural and semi-urban areas, including those inhabited by the Sonowal Kachari tribe, healthcare services often exist in parallel with traditional medicine practices. Government initiatives such as the establishment of primary health centers and the availability of community health workers have improved access to basic medical care. However, the preference for traditional herbal medicine remains prevalent, largely due to the community's historical reliance on these practices and the scarcity of specialized medical professionals in remote regions. The presence of health professionals offering allopathic treatments, while accessible, has not entirely supplanted the community's traditional practices. The inherent trust in indigenous healers and the ease of access to locally available herbal remedies further influence the tribe's health-seeking behavior, particularly in treating common ailments like fevers, coughs, and skin diseases. The duality of health systems, modern and traditional, reveals the community's cautious approach to embracing new healthcare models, highlighting the need for more inclusive health policies that integrate both systems. This coexistence of systems emphasizes the significance of understanding local treatment preferences to improve healthcare outcomes and ensure effective health service delivery. For the promotion of that herbal medicine, a market chain is to be created by the department at the local level. As shown in the model, the Ministry of Ayush and the Government of India should also take up conservation and further utilization schemes by establishing the Regional Institute of Folklore Medicine and Research Centre (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

The herbs used in day-to-day life by the Sonowal Kachari have high amounts of medicinal properties like phytochemicals. Therefore, the present study emphasizes the rich herbal medicinal knowledge embedded within the tribe of Lakhimpur district, Assam, India. This comprehensive survey, encompassing 180 elderly individuals, has illuminated the extensive use of 39 medicinal plants, showcasing the tribe's reliance on nature for healthcare solutions. Such ethnobotanical wisdom is invaluable, providing insights into the therapeutic potential of various plants, particularly in an era where the search for novel drugs is paramount.

The herbal medicinal practices of the Sonowal Kachari tribe offer a promising repository for discovering new therapeutics. By bridging traditional knowledge with modern scientific research, there lies an opportunity to validate and possibly integrate these remedies into mainstream medicine. Furthermore, the preservation and documentation of this indigenous knowledge are crucial for cultural heritage and biodiversity conservation. Previous researchers also give importance for the conservation of medicinal plants and further study for the discovery of crude drugs [25].

Overall, this study reinforces the Sonowal Kachari tribe's significant contribution to herbal medicine and offers a promising repository for discovering new therapeutics. Such endeavors could lead to breakthroughs in drug discovery, offering novel treatments and promoting sustainable healthcare solutions.
