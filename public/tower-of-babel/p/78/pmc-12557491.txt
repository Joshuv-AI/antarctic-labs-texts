Spatializing Ames: Complementing the Ames, IA housing dataset for spatial social-ecological analysis
Authors: David Barbour, Michail Fragkias
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper introduces a spatially-explicit dataset as a complement to a well-known economics and data science dataset - the Ames, Iowa housing dataset. We combine geospatial land cover data with the locations of houses sold between 2006–2010, allowing researchers to explore questions such as the impact of surrounding land cover on property values or the valuation of ecosystem services that result from different types of land cover. We guide researchers through creating spatially-explicit variables and integrating the housing data with land cover information from the National Land Cover Database (NLCD). The new dataset allows users to explore spatially-explicit information relationships in housing markets and replicate the analysis for any US region. The dataset is also a valuable tool for teaching econometrics and data science while exploring linkages between social, ecological and spatial phenomena.

Background

Geospatial data and analysis involve datasets and methods that explore spatial features of socioeconomic and environmental phenomena. They have become more prominent in the last few decades given technological advances that allow for the increase in the availability of geolocated data (data that can be assigned coordinates) and the development of new methods for their analysis.

Spatially-explicit data and analysis are valuable for the social sciences because they allow researchers to study “social-spatial linkages” - interconnections of characteristics and behaviors of individuals, households, or communities in geographical space. The many benefits of using spatially-explicit data in the social sciences include, but are not limited to, understanding human-environment interactions [6] and studying spatial dynamics, such as actor mobility, spatial interactions, and the influence of proximity and distance. Researchers have thus been able to explore questions related to spatial inequality, resource allocation, and more broadly, the spatial organization of human activities.

Spatially-explicit data and analysis can be also focused centrally on human behavior: social sensing focuses on human / social environments and records various human behavioral and social activities, such as human mobility, social interactions, social–economic activities, and urban dynamics. Spatially-explicit analysis also allows leveraging spatial heterogeneity and relational dependencies inherent in human-environment systems for the purposes of empirical modelling.

Data Description

This dataset introduces new variables to the pre-existing geocoded Ames, Iowa housing dataset. We create 80 new variables for 2930 individual home observations that describe the land around each home, based on the Federal National Land Cover Database. The NLCD was chosen for its broad data availability across the US for many years, as well as open data access to enable reliable reapplication. We create 3 buffer sizes for each home: 300 m, 600 m and 1000 m. Years were determined by the availability of the legacy NLCD data via the FedData Package in R. There has been a recent release of the annualized version of the NLCD database in October of 2024 [7]. Should the package be updated to include annual data and not just the legacy data years, our process will provide more richness for dynamic social-ecological analysis. The first 44 of our new variables describe the proportional land cover of land around each home at each available buffer size. For the 1000-meter buffer we provide an additional 36 variables to better describe land cover through a set of landscape metrics. The application of these metrics is constrained by the necessity of diversity in land coverage for the creation of a metric with variability. A variety of distances can and have been used to represent the neighbourhood scale; we choose a kilometer as a representative distance across studies exploring social-ecological interactions through walkability, transit and health across several themes. The 30-meter resolution of the NLCD data is also a limiting factor for the creation of metrics for smaller buffers, particularly when it comes to connectivity of patches. These additional metrics include an aggregate index, land cover patch counts within each buffer, and diversity metrics such as Shannon’s and Simpson’s diversity and evenness indices. Several research hypothesis can be formulated on the contribution of landscape metrics on the value of housing from a social-ecological perspective (for example, higher diversity of land cover types around a house can be increasing the value of a home differentially across different neighborhood sizes due to the opportunities that distinct land cover types can offer for recreation).

Summary Statistics of New Data.

Fig. 1 covers the broad strokes of our data creation process. First, we import Tigris shape files to concentrate on our area of interest. Then we import both sources of data and reproject into the NLCD's native projection type for accurate pairings, specifically NAD83 Conus Albers EPSG 5070. Finally, we generate buffers, also in EPSG 5070, and use the intersection of the buffers and NLCD rasters as our landscape for processing metrics. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

We create a spatially-explicit dataset on open-space using land cover information to accompany a well-known housing dataset: the Ames, IA housing data [5]. We also perform exploratory spatial data analysis on the new dataset. The inclusion of spatially explicit variables in analysis allows for the study of housing market related features that require an explicit consideration of the spatial arrangement of economic activity (e.g. land use, zoning, etc.) and spatial distribution of ecosystem services. For example, the mix of different types of land cover existing around a specific house requires knowledge regarding the distribution of land cover across space.

While the original dataset was lacking spatial information when it was first created and published, we are able to elevate the Ames housing data to a spatially-explicit dataset given more recent work that added the coordinates of each house in the dataset [1]. The dataset contains 2930 observations and a large number of descriptors (23 nominal, 23 ordinal, 14 discrete, and 20 continuous) that can be used for analysing the housing market [5]. It also includes the geographic coordinates (lat/long) of each house [1]. Enhancing this dataset with data from the National Land Cover Database (NLCD), a major land cover database for the United States, provided by the US Geological Survey (USGS) positions researchers well for addressing important questions on the functioning of urban social-ecological systems. We also aim to operationalize the enhanced Ames, IA housing dataset as both a research and pedagogical tool.

Our data processing in R 4.4.1 uses the tigris package, to fetch city boundaries for Ames Iowa, and create an expanded bounding box for the area around it. The FedData package allows us to download NLCD (National Land Cover Database) data for our region of interest. In our case raster data for 2006, 2008, and 2011, clipped to Ames and the nearby area. The initial housing data is split and paired with its respective raster based on the available NLCD data years; NLCD 2006 is applied to 2006 & 2007, NLCD 2008 is applied to 2008 & 2009, and NLCD 2011 is applied to 2010 (Table 1).

For our presentation sample we use 100-meter buffers on NLCD data restricted to the Ames city boundary to show our process and improve visibility. 2011 NLCD data is applied to all homes in the following graphs. Possible mismatch in boundaries could exist depending on time period and location due to shifting boundaries (due to an expansion, annexation, or separation of administrative units). We use the 2011 boundary data even when splitting time periods, as it is the closest available year for our timeframe, but be sure to cross reference available years with the census Bureau's TIGER/Line Shapefiles for method reapplication. So long as the required data is contained within the raster file area the analysis is unaffected. Due to this potential issue, data is pulled from the NLCD after creating an expanded bounding box around the area of interest (In our case Ames) to increase data coverage. To make sure analysis is unaffected in a reapplication use case, ensure buffers don’t overreach the edge of the data pulled. This will result in incorrect values from missing data cells. A visual overlay should suffice to weed out potential issues, while a simple adjustment of the size of this bounding box should provide the additional coverage needed as buffer sizes expand.

Fig. 2 shows the boundary of the city of Ames, IA (blue solid outline) overlaid with the NLCD database in 2011. Notice how the NLCD raster is larger than the city boundary, this is due to the expanded bounding box increasing the size of coverage. Without it, any home on the edge of the boundary would immediately have its buffer stretch past the data available. The code used to generate this data, including the adjustable bounding box selection, is also openly available via GitHub and can be found through the Zenodo open data repository [4]. Using longitude and latitude information for each house [1] we create a shapefile point layer for our observations. We reproject the shapefiles so that they exist in a common coordinate system and overlay the house points on the NLCD maps (Fig. 3). Specifically, we reproject into NAD83 Conus Albers EPSG 5070 which is a metered projection designed to preserve area across large swathes of the contiguous united states. These methods may be less reliable and a new projection to be used for NLCD coverage areas outside of the lower 48 (e.g. Alaska).This reprojection is important to align the housing data with our rasters, and to make sure the buffers generate in the native format of the NLCD prior to measurement. We then conduct buffer analysis on the locations of each house in our dataset. We start by creating 100-meter radius buffer zones, also in EPSG 5070, around houses using the st_buffer function. Note that this radius is research-context specific and multiple buffers are typically created for sensitivity analysis. We extract NLCD land cover data within each buffer using the ‘exactextractr’ package [13]. This operation is identified as ‘clipping’ in geoprocessing analysis. We then visualize buffers overlaid on NLCD data (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Visual representation of 100 m buffer application over each home in the dataset.Fig. 4:

Next, we convert the NLCD information we have extracted into land cover percentages for each buffer by dividing the number of pixels in each land cover type by the total number of pixels in the buffer zone (Fig. 4). We then associate NLCD numeric codes to descriptive land cover types. Finally, we filter and summarize the land cover data then join the processed land cover data back to the housing dataset for further analysis and visualization.

The large shift in proportions emphasizes the need for justification in boundary selection given the research context. We provide multiple buffer options for the dataset we create and provide the full automated code producing the dataset so that other buffer sizes can be created. Urban, suburban, and rural homeowners may place different values on proximity to various land covers. For example, owners in central areas of a city may place greater value on access to businesses within walking distance.

In the assembly of the dataset this process is repeated in the case of the 300-meter, 600-meter, and 1000-meter buffers. With the addition that the original house points are divided into 3 groups based on the year each house was sold. Following the process outlined above, values are extracted using their respective NLCD coverage years, and the separate time periods are row-bound before being joined back into the original dataset. The NLCD data is only one of several spatial datasets available via the Feddata package. Other datasets, canopy coverage for example, could use the same process created here to improve this and other datasets further.

To expand the capabilities and fit of this dataset towards social-ecological research we calculated landscape metrics. Landscape metrics quantify the spatial patterns of land use/cover, in terms of composition and configuration. For these calculations, we utilize the landscapemetrics package in R [9]. These include diversity metrics such as Shannon’s diversity index and evenness index as well as Simpson’s diversity index and evenness index. We also included patch counts broken down by total of each land cover type and in total overall. Along with an aggregate index to describe just how aggregated that diversity may be. These additional metrics were only measured at the 1000-meter level due to a certain level of diversity necessary to make them meaningful, and to capture the overall neighbourhood. Since one of the limitations of the NLCD is it’s 30-meter resolution, a larger buffer is preferable for this aspect.

The process borrowed from our prior one, passing each of our 1000-meter buffers through with their appropriate NLCD raster based on the year the home was sold in the exact breakdown as before (Table 1). All metrics used the "queens' case” determinant for connectivity of patches, that is both orthogonally adjacent and diagonally adjacent squares. This allows more ‘stringy’ patches to be measured as one unit. Since we’re analysing our data at a 30-meter resolution it’s important allow for this diagonal case, due to the possibility of 10 or 20 m of land cover bridging an edge while the respective tiles are assigned to a more dominant land cover for each tile. By adding further data of how the buffers are made up, beyond basic proportions, we hope to provide additional options for analysing the dataset. With metrics for the amount of coverage, diversity of coverage, concentration of coverage, and overall balance between land cover types; Researchers now have many options for exploring the new data.
