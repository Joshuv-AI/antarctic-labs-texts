Growth-Friendly Surgery Improves Diaphragm Positioning, Thoracic Dimensions, and Patient-Reported Outcomes in Neuromuscular Scoliosis
Authors: Zaid Elsabbagh, William ElNemer, Peter G. Gabos, Gregory J. Redding, Paul D. Sponseller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Neuromuscular scoliosis (NMS) can displace the diaphragm into the thorax, reducing lung volume and impairing respiration. As an alternative to posterior spinal fusion, growth-friendly surgery (GFS) allows thoracic growth while correcting deformity. We evaluated associations of GFS with thoracic dimensions and diaphragm positioning, hypothesizing that GFS would alleviate respiratory constraints in pediatric NMS.Methods:We reviewed data from 45 patients who underwent GFS for NMS at a mean age of 8 years, from 2003 to 2022. Radiographic measures were lung volume, space available for lung (SAL) ratio, diaphragm intrusion index (DII), diaphragm vertebral level (DVL), T1-T12 height, and T1-S1 height, assessed preoperatively, postoperatively, and at 2-year follow-up. We also analyzed Early-Onset Scoliosis 24-item Questionnaire (EOSQ-24) outcomes related to lung function. A “latest follow-up” time point was included for patients with extended data (mean: 7.2 years; range: 2.0-18.5). Paired t tests compared preoperative and follow-up values. Alpha = 0.05.Results:Mean lung volume increased from 1,992 cm3 preoperatively to 2,917 cm3 postoperatively and 3,210 cm3 at 2 years (p < 0.001), remaining stable (3,590 cm3) at latest follow-up. SAL ratio improved from 0.79 preoperatively to 0.91 postoperatively and 0.89 at 2 years, with no change at final follow-up. DII improved from 0.59 (right) and 0.56 (left) preoperatively to 0.69 (right) and 0.70 (left) at 2 years, with no decline thereafter (p < 0.001). DVL showed bilateral descent by approximately 1.3 vertebral levels through 2 years and remained stable. T1-T12 and T1-S1 heights increased to 21 and 34 cm, respectively, at 2 years (p < 0.001). EOSQ-24 scores improved postoperatively, with further gains by 2 years in energy (from 55 to 83), pulmonary function (from 69 to 94), and fatigue (from 48 to 19).Conclusions:GFS was associated with improvements in thoracic dimensions, diaphragm position, lung volume, and respiratory-related quality of life in pediatric NMS, underscoring the potential of GFS to address deformity and long-term respiratory health in this population.Level of Evidence:Level III. Introduction

Neuromuscular scoliosis (NMS) is a severe spinal deformity in patients with underlying neuromuscular conditions1-3. Progressive curvature can distort thoracic geometry, displace the diaphragm, and reduce lung volume, causing respiratory impairment4-6. Respiratory compromise in NMS is multifactorial, reflecting respiratory muscle weakness, reduced chest wall compliance from deformity progression, and impaired diaphragmatic mechanics6.

Assessment of pulmonary function is often limited by the need for patient cooperation during spirometry, which may be impractical in children with cognitive or motor impairments7-9. Alternative evaluations, such as radiographic lung volume estimates and patient-reported outcomes, are valuable. Radiographic lung area–based estimates have been validated against anatomic lung volumes and correlate strongly with volumetric imaging, supporting their use when advanced modalities are unfeasible10. Dynamic magnetic resonance imaging has also been explored in early-onset scoliosis to assess thoracic motion and diaphragm excursion, although its clinical use remains limited by cost and availability11.

Growth-friendly surgery (GFS) techniques were developed to manage spinal deformity while preserving spinal and thoracic growth in skeletally immature patients12–14. By enabling continued axial growth, GFS may promote improvements in thoracic volume and diaphragm position, enhancing respiratory mechanics15,16. In NMS, deformity often follows thoracolumbar “collapsing” patterns rather than the primary thoracic curves typical of idiopathic scoliosis17. Progressive pelvic obliquity can elevate the pelvis toward the lower ribs, reducing intrathoracic space and altering diaphragm contour and excursion18. Compared with idiopathic scoliosis, rib deformity is less prominent and curve apices are more caudal, making diaphragm mechanics particularly relevant in NMS19. Although posterior spinal fusion has been evaluated for its effects on thoracic dimensions20, the impact of GFS on diaphragm positioning and lung volume remains incompletely defined.

Here, we evaluated associations of GFS with diaphragm intrusion, thoracic dimensions, and respiratory outcomes in pediatric patients with NMS. Diaphragm “intrusion” refers to cephalad displacement of the hemidiaphragm into the thoracic cavity, which can reduce available lung space and alter inspiratory mechanics. Using radiographic measurements and patient-reported outcomes, we assessed the respiratory effects and durability of GFS over time.

Methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained before study initiation (IRB00043838).

Study Design

We retrospectively analyzed skeletally immature patients with NMS who underwent GFS from 2003 to 2022 at a US academic center (The Johns Hopkins Hospital, Baltimore, MD). Inclusion criteria were skeletal immaturity at the time of GFS, diagnosis of NMS (caused by cerebral palsy, spinal muscular atrophy, or spina bifida), and availability of preoperative, immediate postoperative, and 2-year radiographs. No patients were excluded for incomplete follow-up; all 45 included patients had complete radiographic data at these time points. The “latest follow-up” time point was a composite reflecting each patient’s most recent available radiographic assessment: 2 years (n = 7), 5 years (n = 23), 10 years (n = 9), and 15 years (n = 6). Reduced sample sizes at later time points reflect the enrollment period rather than loss to follow-up, as patients with longer-term data also had complete data at all preceding time points. Early-Onset Scoliosis 24-item Questionnaire (EOSQ-24) data were variably available for 25 patients at the preoperative, postoperative, and 2-year time points; missing data primarily reflected patients with severe neuromuscular impairment for whom caregivers did not complete the instrument. The missingness of data by characteristic is presented in Supplemental Tables 1 to 3.

Patients

Of 254 patients treated with GFS, 106 were skeletally immature with NMS; 45 had complete radiographic data and were included (Table I). Curve apex location was categorized as thoracic (T1-T10), thoracolumbar (T11-L2), or lumbar (L3-S1). Thoracic kyphosis was measured on standing lateral radiographs using T5–T12 and T2–T12 Cobb angles.

Radiographic Outcome Measures

Radiographic measurements were obtained using Carestream Vue Picture Archiving and Communication System software, version 12.2 (Carestream Health, Rochester, NY). Outcomes were lung volume, space available for lung (SAL) ratio, diaphragm intrusion index (DII), diaphragm vertebral level (DVL), T1-T12 height, and T1-S1 height, measured on standing biplanar radiographs as previously described20. Measurement details are provided in the Appendix. Postoperative respiratory events were recorded descriptively.

Patient-Reported Outcomes

Patient-reported energy, fatigue, and pulmonary function were assessed using the EOSQ-24, a validated instrument for early-onset scoliosis21. EOSQ scores range from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating better function or less fatigue. For children unable to communicate independently (most patients with Gross Motor Function Classification System [GMFCS] levels IV–V), the EOSQ-24 was completed by a parent or caregiver.

Statistical Methods

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and clinical variables, with continuous data presented as means and SDs and categorical data as counts and percentages. Paired Student’s t tests compared preoperative values with 2-year and latest follow-up outcomes using matched data. Categorical comparisons used χ2 tests. Two-tailed p-values <0.05 were considered significant. Linear mixed-effects models were used for each outcome to account for within-patient correlations and unbalanced follow-up, with time point as a categorical fixed effect (preoperative referent) and a random intercept per patient. Degrees of freedom were approximated using the Satterthwaite method. Analyses were performed using R, version 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2024).

Results

Forty-five patients were included, with a mean age of 8.0 ± 2.4 years; 26 (58%) were female. The most common neuromuscular diagnoses were cerebral palsy (62%), spina bifida (16%), congenital myopathy (13%), and spinal muscular atrophy (9%; Table I). GMFCS levels were recorded to reflect neurologic severity. Among patients with cerebral palsy, GMFCS levels were as follows: Level I (n = 1), Level II (n = 2), Level III (n = 0), Level IV (n = 2), and Level V (n = 30). Most curves had a thoracolumbar apex (T11-L2; n = 29), followed by thoracic apices (T1-T10) in 14 patients; lumbar apices (L3-S1) were uncommon (n = 2).

Respiratory outcomes were reviewed to contextualize postoperative pulmonary health. In the year before surgery, 11 patients experienced at least 1 pneumonia episode, compared with 9 patients during the 2 years after surgery. Respiratory-related hospitalizations occurred in 12 patients preoperatively and 10 postoperatively. Noninvasive ventilation requirements remained stable, with 9 patients using bi-level or continuous positive airway pressure before surgery and 8 patients continuing use postoperatively. Oxygen dependence was uncommon and unchanged, identified in 4 patients both preoperatively and at latest follow-up. Postoperative respiratory morbidity did not increase over time.

Lung Parameters

Calculated lung volume increased from 1,992 ± 576 cm3 preoperatively to 3,210 ± 566 cm3 at 2 years (p < 0.001), remaining stable at latest follow-up (3,590 ± 569 cm3; Fig. 1 and Table II). The SAL ratio improved from 0.79 ± 0.10 preoperatively to 0.89 ± 0.07 at 2 years (p < 0.001) and remained stable at 0.89 ± 0.06 at latest follow-up.

Diaphragm Position and Intrusion

Right-sided DII improved from 0.59 ± 0.09 preoperatively to 0.69 ± 0.06 at 2 years (p < 0.001), with no decline at latest follow-up (0.69 ± 0.06). Left-sided DII increased from 0.56 ± 0.06 preoperatively to 0.70 ± 0.05 at 2 years (p < 0.001), remaining stable at latest follow-up (0.70 ± 0.05).

Right-sided DVL improved from 7.3 ± 0.6 vertebral levels preoperatively to 8.6 ± 0.5 at 2 years (p < 0.001), further increasing to 8.7 ± 0.5 at latest follow-up. Left DVL increased from 7.7 ± 0.9 preoperatively to 9.0 ± 0.5 at 2 years (p < 0.001), reaching 9.1 ± 0.6 at latest follow-up. Thoracic kyphosis increased from 20° ± 13° preoperatively to 28° ± 12° at 2 years (p = 0.003), with values stable at 29° ± 12° at latest follow-up.

Thoracic Height

T1-T12 height increased from 18 ± 4 cm preoperatively to 23 ± 5 cm at 2 years, for a cumulative gain of 5 ± 4 cm (p < 0.001; Fig. 2). At latest follow-up, this height remained stable (23 ± 6 cm). T1-S1 height increased from 26 ± 5 cm preoperatively to 34 ± 6 cm at 2 years, representing a total increase of 8 ± 4 cm (p < 0.001). Final follow-up height was 35 ± 5 cm.

EOSQ-24

Complete EOSQ-24 data were available for 25 patients (Fig. 3). Energy level scores improved from 55 ± 17 preoperatively to 83 ± 20 at 2-year follow-up (p < 0.001); at latest follow-up, energy scores remained high at 76 ± 23. Pulmonary function scores improved from 69 ± 22 preoperatively to 94 ± 14 at 2 years (p < 0.001), remaining stable at 93 ± 14 at latest follow-up. Fatigue scores improved from 48 ± 26 preoperatively to 22 ± 23 at 2 years (p < 0.001); at latest follow-up, fatigue remained low at 27 ± 28.

Other Analyses

All radiographic outcomes improved from preoperative values at every postoperative time point, with gains maintained through latest available follow-up. For EOSQ-24 domains, improvements in energy, pulmonary function, and fatigue were observed at 2 years (all, p < 0.001); the postoperative fatigue comparison was not significant (p = 0.225). Estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts are presented in Supplemental Tables 4 and 5.

Discussion

Respiratory compromise in NMS is multifactorial and may arise from respiratory muscle weakness, reduced chest wall compliance, abnormal diaphragmatic mechanics, and structural factors such as pelvic obliquity or chest wall deformity3. In patients with thoracolumbar curves, pelvic obliquity can elevate the pelvis toward the lower ribs on the concave side and reduce excursion space for the ipsilateral hemidiaphragm22,23.

In this cohort of pediatric patients with NMS, GFS was associated with improved diaphragm position, reduced diaphragmatic intrusion, and increased lung volume across short-term and long-term follow-up. Improvements in DII and DVL were maintained through a mean follow-up of more than 7 years, indicating durable anatomic changes.

These anatomic improvements were accompanied by favorable changes in energy, fatigue, and pulmonary function. Although many patients were unable to self-report, and outcomes therefore reflect caregiver responses, mean improvements exceeded suggested thresholds for clinical importance, supporting functional relevance24.

Previous studies in idiopathic scoliosis link surgical correction to improved pulmonary function, largely attributed to restoration of thoracic alignment and volume20,25-27. Improved diaphragmatic positioning and increased thoracic volume likely contribute to postoperative lung expansion in NMS. Increased lung volume may reduce elastic loading on the diaphragm and improve mechanical efficiency, consistent with diaphragm length-tension relationships and thoracic compliance28,29. GFS also supports vertical spinal growth and increases intrathoracic space30,31, which is important for long-term respiratory health32. Despite the predominance of thoracolumbar curves, T1-T12 height increased, suggesting preservation of thoracic growth potential even with caudal apices16,33.

Enhanced SAL ratios, together with improvements in DII and DVL, indicate more favorable thoracic geometry after GFS. Even modest improvements may reduce diaphragmatic or abdominal encroachment on lung space in NMS, which is important because restrictive lung disease is a major contributor to morbidity6,14,20. Postoperative respiratory morbidity did not worsen over time and followed similar directional trends to the radiographic improvements.

Several limitations warrant discussion. First, the cohort reflects the heterogeneity intrinsic to NMS, including variation in diagnosis, ambulatory status, associated medical devices, and curve patterns, which may limit generalizability. Static radiographic measures such as DII and SAL ratio provide valuable anatomic insight but are indirect surrogates for pulmonary function20,34. Diaphragm function encompasses excursion, force generation, and fatigability, none of which can be assessed on static radiographs. Radiographs were obtained without standardized breath-hold instructions, and because diaphragm position varies across the respiratory cycle, some measurement variability is expected. Although improvements in diaphragm alignment were observed, these anatomic changes cannot be assumed to translate directly into improved lung mechanics, as spirometry and dynamic respiratory assessments were not performed. Accordingly, these findings represent associations rather than proof of causality. Future studies using dynamic modalities, such as diaphragmatic ultrasonography or other motion-based imaging techniques, may better characterize diaphragmatic function in this population.

Second, lung volume prediction equations derived from healthy adult populations may introduce inaccuracies when applied to pediatric patients with NMS. Age-related variation in spinal and thoracic growth potential may further influence postoperative changes, as younger patients have substantially greater growth potential than older children, contributing to variability in radiographic and volumetric measures. The predominance of thoracolumbar apex curves may also limit generalizability to other scoliosis subtypes23,35.

Third, the cohort included both high-tone disorders (e.g., cerebral palsy) and low-tone disorders (e.g., spinal muscular atrophy), which may follow distinct respiratory trajectories. Pulmonary decline in hypotonic disorders often reflects progressive respiratory muscle weakness, whereas hypertonic disorders may restrict chest wall compliance through rigidity. Differences in thoracic cage morphology across neuromuscular conditions may also influence diaphragm shape and radiographic lung measurements. The absence of a nonoperative or alternative treatment control group prevents causal interpretation, and the sample size precluded subgroup analyses or detection of smaller effect sizes23,35.

Fourth, multiple comparisons were performed across time points and outcomes without formal p-value adjustment. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the consistent directionality of effects across all outcomes and time points, we prioritized characterizing the magnitude and trajectory of change over type I error control. Accordingly, p-values should be interpreted in the context of the overall pattern of results rather than in isolation.

Finally, increases in thoracic height and intrathoracic volume likely reflect both rod-driven distraction and normal somatic growth, which cannot be distinguished in this study. However, evidence demonstrates correlations between radiographic lung areas and pulmonary parameters, supporting their use in this population, for whom conventional pulmonary function testing is often challenging10,29.

This study contributes important data to the limited literature on pulmonary outcomes after GFS in patients with NMS22,33,36. The use of validated radiographic measures enabled evaluation of respiratory changes in a population for whom conventional pulmonary function tests are often impractical20,26,37. The longitudinal study design provides insight into both the immediate and sustained effects of GFS on diaphragm positioning and respiratory function15,38,39.

Conclusion

GFS supports thoracic development and enhances diaphragm positioning, leading to sustained improvements in lung volume and diaphragm intrusion, as well as improvements in energy levels and fatigue. These gains were observed at 2 years and maintained through longer-term follow-up, with an average duration of 7 years. These findings highlight the need to consider diaphragmatic outcomes when evaluating surgical options in pediatric NMS. Given the potential respiratory advantages of early intervention, timing of surgery warrants careful consideration.
