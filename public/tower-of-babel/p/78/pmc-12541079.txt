Molecules interacting with CasL-Like 2 enhances tumor angiogenesis and progression by activating mTOR/HIF1α/VEGF pathway in kidney renal clear cell carcinoma
Authors: Yuxin Song, Jiajun Zhang, Guocheng Liu, Xiaoxiao Jiang, Ningchuan Sun, Han He, Shu Song, Wei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), as one of the most angiogenically active urological tumors, has not yet been fully elucidated its molecular mechanisms. Molecules interacting with CasL-Like 2 (MICAL-L2), a unique oxidoreductase, is known to be involved in cytoskeletal regulation. However, its role and mechanism in tumor angiogenesis remain unclear. This study aims to reveal the specific mechanism by which MICAL-L2 regulates KIRC angiogenesis through cytoskeletal dynamics and to explore its clinical translational value and significance. Bioinformatics database analysis showed that MICAL-L2 was significantly overexpressed in various solid tumors and was closely associated with shorter overall survival in KIRC patients. Gene set enrichment analysis demonstrated that MICAL-L2 expression was significantly correlated with members of the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) family and was closely related to angiogenesis pathways. Analysis of human KIRC cell lines revealed that the upregulation of MICAL-L2 stimulated the release of various pro-angiogenic cytokines, primarily through the activation of a novel mTOR/HIF1α/VEGF pathway, promoting tumor angiogenesis. Further studies using cultured KIRC cell lines unveiled that MICAL-L2 co-localized with F-actin in the cytoplasm and promoted tumor angiogenesis remodeling in vitro. Additionally, analysis of clinical datasets indicated that high expression of MICAL-L2 was associated with drug resistance to various anti-angiogenic agents and actin inhibitors in cancer. This study reveals a new mechanism in which MICAL-L2, under the tumor hypoxic environment, promotes KIRC angiogenesis and progression by activating the “cytoskeletal remodeling-mTOR/HIF1α/VEGF signaling pathway” using integrated analysis of clinical samples and cell models, providing new theoretical evidence and potential intervention targets for the development of combination therapies targeting the tumor microenvironment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20400-3.Keywords: MICAL-L2, Kidney renal clear cell carcinoma, Cytoskeletal remodeling, Angiogenesis, Treatment resistanceSubject terms: Cancer, Molecular biology

Introduction

The aggressive phenotype of tumors is often associated with a hypoxic and immunosuppressive microenvironment. In this setting, tumor cells and immune cells, such as TH2 and M2 macrophages, secrete numerous pro-angiogenic and pro-tumorigenic cytokines1,2. Angiogenic sprouting is a process in which vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and its receptor VEGFR2 play crucial roles in promoting tumor development, invasion, and metastasis. Additionally, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and angiopoietin mediate excessive pro-angiogenic signals2–4. In eukaryotic cells, the process of angiogenesis is highly dependent on the actin cytoskeleton. Actin monomers form endothelium-specific structures such as lamellae, pseudopodia, and filopodia to support endothelial cell morphogenesis and migration, and trigger the formation of junction-associated intermittent lamellipodia (JAIL) to regulate cell-to-cell adhesion5,6. Disruptions in the redox regulation of actin through upstream signaling pathways have been implicated in the development of various types of tumors7. Therefore, combining anti-angiogenic agents with cytoskeleton regulator agents might elicit synergistic anti-tumor effects that warrant further investigation.

Human MICAL (microtubule-associated monooxygenase, calponin, and LIM domain-containing) proteins constitute a family of evolutionarily conserved flavoprotein oxidoreductases. This family includes three MICAL proteins (MICAL1, MICAL2, and MICAL3) and two MICAL-Like homologs (MICAL-L1 and MICAL-L2)8. Among these, MICAL-L2 has been identified as an intriguing gene. It has been previously reported to be overexpressed and to play a crucial pathogenic role in non-small cell lung cancer, gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, and ovarian cancer9–12. Functionally, MICAL-L2 directly oxidizes F-actin using its intrinsic redox potential or indirectly interacts with actinins to regulate actin filament disassembly and cytoskeletal rearrangement13. Furthermore, as a Rab13 effector protein, MICAL-L2 mediates the recycling and assembly of tight junction proteins at cell-cell contact sites, which accelerates the process of epithelial cell scattering as well as tumor invasion and metastasis14,15. Current research has predominantly focused on the role of MICAL-L2 in the autonomous behaviors of tumor cells. However, the role of MICAL-L2 in remodeling the tumor microenvironment, particularly in angiogenesis, has not been sufficiently and thoroughly explored.

In this study, we investigated the expression of MICAL-L2 across various human malignancies and its association with clinical features using bioinformatics analysis of public datasets and immunohistochemistry (IHC) on human KIRC tissue samples. Bioinformatic analysis revealed a close relationship between MICAL-L2 and the HIF family, shedding light on its novel role in tumor angiogenesis. Utilizing kidney cancer as a model, we combined functional studies in cultured KIRC cell lines (786-Oand Caki-1) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) to investigate the role of MICAL-L2 in tumor angiogenesis. Our findings indicated that MICAL-L2 co-localized with F-actin in KIRC cells upregulated HIF1α expression and activated the HIF1α/mTOR/VEGF pathway, thereby promoting tumor vascularization in co-culture models. Pharmacological inhibition of actin polymerization in cells and genetic inhibition of MICAL-L2 in vitro both reversed these effects. Further bioinformatic analysis predicted that MICAL-L2 expression levels in tumors could serve as a predictor of sensitivity to anti-angiogenic drugs.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The mRNA and protein levels of the MICAL family across various tumors were analyzed using samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://www.cancer.gov/tcga) and the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC, https://proteomics.cancer.gov/programs/cptac) through UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) webtool16. Cox’s regression analysis was conducted employing the R package ‘survival’ based on the TCGA Pan-Cancer (PANCAN, N = 10535, G = 60499) database. Statistical significance was defined as |Log2FC| > 1 and p < 0.05.

The Stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were calculated using the R package ‘ESTIMATE’, and their correlation with MICAL-L2 expression was analyzed using the ‘psych’ algorithm17. Tumor-immune cell interactions were analyzed using the ‘IPS’ and ‘TIMER’ algorithms18,19. The relationship between MICAL-L2 and immune checkpoint inhibitory and stimulatory molecules was examined using the Immune checkpoint gene analysis module of the Sangerbox (http://sangerbox.com/) webtool20.

The protein sequence and 3D model of MICAL-L2 were downloaded from EMBL-EBI (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/)21,22. STRING (https://string-db.org/) was utilized to predict the most related functional partners of MICAL-L2 and construct a protein-protein interaction network23. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of MICAL-L2 on Gene Ontology-Biological Process (GO-BP) was performed using the Co-Expedia (https://www.coexpedia.org/) webtool24. The enrichment analysis of the MICAL-L2 and VEGF signaling pathway was based on KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html)25–27.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) images of MICAL-L2 and HIF family members were downloaded from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/) database28.

Clinical samples collection and ethics

Human kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) tissue samples and paired adjacent normal kidney tissues were obtained from Yancheng NO.1 People’s Hospital (n = 53). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yancheng NO.1 People’s Hospital. All patients provided informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell culture

Human KIRC cell lines 786-O, Caki-1, and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were acquired from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). 786-O and Caki-1 cells were cultured in RPMI1640 medium (Gibco, USA), while HUVECs were cultured in DEME medium (KeyGEN BioTECH, China). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA), 50 U/ml penicillin, and 50 µg/ml streptomycin. The cells were cultured at 37 °C with a humidity of 5% CO2.

Cytochalasin B Treatment: experiments involving cultured cells (786-O, Caki-1, HUVECs) were treated with 1 µM of Cytochalasin B (53373ES03, Yesheng, China) for 24 h to inhibit actin polymerization. No cytotoxicity was detected at this drug concentration.

Ridaforolimus Treatment: experiments involving cultured cells (786-O, Caki-1, HUVECs) were treated with 10 nM of Ridaforolimus (53386ES08, Yesheng, China) for 24 h to inhibit the mTOR signaling pathway. No cytotoxicity was detected at this drug concentration.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed on paraffin-embedded human KIRC and adjacent normal tissue sections to detect the expression of MICAL-L2 (1:50, Proteintech, China), HIF1α (1:50, Proteintech, China), VEGFA (1:50, Proteintech, China), CD31 (1:200, Proteintech, China), and CD34 (1:50, Proteintech, China).

Both the evaluation of KIRC tissue specimens and the experimental results were scanned and judged using the double-blind method. Immunohistochemical staining was semi-quantitatively assessed based on staining intensity and the percentage of positive tumor cells, according to the following criteria: score 0: no staining or < 10% positive cells; score 1: faint staining in ≥ 10% of cells; score 2: moderate staining in ≥ 10% of cells; score 3: strong staining in ≥ 10% of cells. Samples were subsequently categorized as either low expression (scores 0–1) or high expression (scores 2–3) for statistical analysis. Microvessel density (MVD) was assessed by two experienced pathologists according to a previously described method29. Briefly, three areas of highest vascular density (hotspots) within tumor sections stained with CD34 antibody were selected at low magnification (×40). Microvessels were counted in ten separate ×400 fields (0.25 mm2 per field) within each hotspot. A microvessel was defined as any CD34-positive endothelial cell cluster with a visible lumen. The mean vessel count per field was calculated and recorded as the MVD value (vessels/HPF).

Plasmids and siRNA transfections

KIRC cell lines (786-O and Caki-1) were plated at 60% confluence in 6-well plates. For gain-of-function: cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1-MICAL-L2 plasmid or empty vector control (pcDNA3.1; RIBO Biotechnology, China) using Hieff Trans® Liposomal Reagent (Yeasen, China) and Opti-MEM (Invitrogen, USA). For loss-of-function: cells were transfected with MICAL-L2-targeting siRNA (5′-CGACGTGAATATCTGCAACAT-3′) or negative control siRNA (5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′; RIBO Biotechnology, China) using identical reagents. All transfections followed manufacturer protocols. Functional assays were initiated 48 h post-transfection.

Western blot

RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with protease inhibitor complete™ III ULTRA (Roche) was utilized for the extraction of total proteins from KIRC cell lines (786-O and Caki-1). Protein quantification was performed using the enhanced BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China) prior to separation by 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transfer onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The membrane was blocked with Tris buffer containing 0.1% Tween-20 and 5% nonfat milk overnight at 4 °C. Rabbit antibodies specific for MICAL-L2 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), GAPDH (1:1000, Proteintech, China), Tubulin (Proteintech, China), HIF1α (D1S7W, 1:1000, Proteintech, China), p-AKT (Ser473, CST, USA), AKT (1:1000, Proteintech, China), ERK (1:1000, Proteintech, China), p-ERK (Thr202/Tyr204, 1:1000, CST, USA), p-mTOR (Ser2448, 1:1000, CST, USA), mTOR (1:1000, CST, USA), VEGFA (Proteintech, China), p-VEGF Receptor 2 (Tyr1175/D5B11, 1:1000, CST, USA), and VEGFR2 (1:1000, Abcam, China) were used to incubate with the membrane overnight, followed by treatment with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10000; CST, USA). The signal was detected using the enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Yesheng, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The original, unprocessed blot images were provided in the supplementary materials. The membranes were cut into strips at specific molecular weight positions before antibody incubation to ensure that each section was probed with the intended antibody reactions.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RNA was extracted from KIRC cell lines (786-O and Caki-1) using TRIzol (15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and homogenized. cDNA was synthesized with a HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase kit (Vazyme, China). RT-qPCR (Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCRMaster Mix, China) was performed on HIF1α and VEGF, using GAPDH as a reference housekeeping gene. Primers for HIF1α (Forward: 5′-GAACGTCGAAAAGAAAAGTCTCG-3′, Reverse: 5′-CCTTATCAAGATGCGAACTCACA-3′), VEGFA (Forward: 5′- GGGCAGAATCATCACGA AGT-3′, Reverse: 5′-AAATGCTTTCTCCGCTCTGA-3′), and GAPDH (Forward: 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′, Reverse: 5′- GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′) were purchased from Genescript.

Cell proliferation and viability assays

After 48 h of transfection with MICAL-L2 siRNA or control siRNA, 786-O cells were seeded into 96-well plates. Then, 50 µL of EdU (Abbkine, USA) culture medium was added to each well and the plates were incubated for 2 h. Following this, 50 µL of DAPI was added for staining, and the plates were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 5 min. The 786-O cells were then observed under a fluorescence microscope, followed by photographing and statistical analysis.

After 48 h of transfection with MICAL-L2 siRNA or pcDNA3.1-MICALL2, 786-O cells were seeded into 96-well plates. Then, 20 µL of CCK-8 solution (Biosharp, China) was added to each well and the plated were incubated for 4 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The average absorbance of three replicates was calculated to determine cell viability. Absorbance values were normalized to the respective control group (set as 100%) using the formula: [(OD_treatment - OD_blank)/(OD_control - OD_blank)] × 100%. The formula for calculating cell viability was as follows: Cell viability (%) = [( Experimental group average absorbance ) - ( Blank group average absorbance )] / [( Control group average absorbance )-( Blank group average absorbance) ]×100%.

Cells co-culture and HUVECs tubule formation assay

An indirect non-contact co-culture model using KIRC cell lines (786-O) and HUVECs was employed in this assay. In this model, HUVECs were co-cultured with MICAL-L2-overexpressing 786-O cells or control 786-O cells using a transwell plate with a 0.4 μm pore filter ( Corning). The 786-O cells were plated in the upper chamber at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2, and HUVECs were plated in the bottom chamber at the same density. They were co-cultured for 72 h. Subsequently, the HUVECs were incubated with 5-chloromethylfluorescein diacetate (CMFDA, Yeasen, China) for 15 min under normal cell growth conditions. The storage solution of CMFDA was previously diluted to the working solution concentration of 1 µM with serum-free medium and preheated to 37 °C. Next, the culture solution was gently replaced with fresh medium. In advance, a 24-well plate was coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Bedford, MA) and polymerized for 30 min at 37 °C. HUVECs (2 × 104 cells/cm2) were incubated in 200 µl conditioned medium for 8 h before imaging. The capillary tubes were quantified under a 100× bright-field microscope by measuring the total length of the completed tubule structures. Three independent experiments were conducted for each treatment.

Immunofluorescence

786-O and Caki-1 cells grown on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% formaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline at room temperature for 15 min. Following permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline for 15 min and blocking with 5% goat serum in phosphate-buffered saline for 45 min, the cells were incubated at 4 °C with MICAL-L2 antibodies (1:50, Proteintech, China) overnight and subsequently incubated with Alexa 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, USA) for 30 min. F-actin was labeled using Actin-Tracker Green (Beyotime, China). Fluorescent images were acquired using ZEISS Axio Vert.A1 microscope and ZEISS Axiocam 305 color camera.

Drug sensitivity analysis

The drug sensitivity analysis was conducted utilizing the ‘Candidate Agents’ module of the BEST webtool (https://rookieutopia.hiplot.com.cn/app_direct/BEST/)30. Data from bulk samples and cancer cell lines were sourced from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC), Cancer Therapy Response Portal (CTRP), and Precision Cancer Medicine Database (PRISM) databases. Drug response prediction was executed using a ridge regression algorithm implemented in the oncoPredict R package31. The model was trained using the transcriptional expression profiles and drug response data from cancer cell lines, achieving satisfactory predictive accuracy as evaluated by default 10-fold cross-validation. The importance of drugs was ranked based on their correlations, and the top 30 sensitive and resistant compounds were listed.

Statistical analysis

All data analysis was conducted using R vision3.6.3 and SPSS vision 25.0. For normally distributed data, independent samples were analyzed with the unpaired t-test, and paired samples with the paired t-test. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test (for independent samples) or the Wilcoxon signed - rank test (for paired samples) was used. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson correlation analysis (for continuous variables) or the chi-square test (for categorical variables). A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs and charts were created using GraphPad Prism vision 8.2.1 and Adobe Illustrator 2020.

Results

MICAL-L2 is involved in mediating the tumor angiogenesis signaling network

To investigate the relationship between the expression of the MICAL family and survival outcomes in various cancers, we conducted a bioinformatic analysis. The analysis of TCGA (Figure S1A) and CPTAC (Figure S1B) databases revealed that both mRNA and protein expression levels of MICAL-L2 were generally upregulated in pan-cancers and closely associated with a poor clinical prognosis. Furthermore, univariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that elevated MICAL-L2 expression was linked to shorter overall survival (Figure S1C) and progression-free interval (Figure S1D) in glioma, kidney cancer, uveal melanoma, and adrenocortical carcinoma.

Immune estimate analysis indicated a significant positive correlation between MICAL-L2 expression and immune and stromal components in several cancers, including glioma, kidney cancer, acute myeloid leukemia, neuroblastoma, and pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (Figure S2A-C). Additionally, MICAL-L2 expression was positively correlated with endothelial cell infiltration (Figure S2D), CD4 + T cell infiltration (Figure S2E), and specific immunomodulatory genes (Figure S2F) in multiple cancers. These findings collectively suggested that MICAL-L2 was closely associated with stromal remodeling and cytokine recruitment within the tumor microenvironment.

We began examining the structure of MICAL-L2, which contained 904 amino acids and three primary functional domains (Fig. 1A, B). The top 10 predicted functional partners of MICAL-L2 included F-actin cross-linking proteins and small GTPases of the Rab family (Fig. 1C). Next, we performed GSEA analysis to further elucidate the potential biological pathways associated with MICAL-L2. This analysis revealed that MICAL-L2 was significantly enriched in hypoxia-related pathways, including Rho protein signal transduction, vascular endothelial growth factor production, and vesicle transport along actin filaments (Fig. 1D).

MICAL-L2 drives abnormal proliferation in KIRC

The prognosis and clinical treatment of KIRC patients are closely related to angiogenesis. Therefore, we validated our bioinformatic analysis specifically in a KIRC model. Data extracted from the GEO and TCGA databases indicated that MICAL-L2 mRNA (Fig. 2A) was significantly elevated in KIRC tissues than in adjacent normal kidney tissues. Similarly, IHC assay revealed that MICAL-L2 protein level was upregulated in KIRC specimens (n = 7) (Fig. 2B, C) and was correlated with patients’ clinical tumor stage (n = 53) (Table 1). Specifically, MICAL-L2 expression was closely associated with TNM stage (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, using the median MICAL-L2 mRNA level as a cutoff, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that KIRC patients with high MICAL-L2 expression had significantly shorter OS (TCGA: p = 0.00015; E-MTAB: p = 0.017) (Fig. 2E, F).

To investigate the potential role of MICAL-L2 in tumor behavior, we assessed the effect of altering MICAL-L2 expression on KIRC cells (Fig. 2G, H). We found that knockdown of MICAL-L2 significantly inhibited cell proliferation (Fig. 2I, J) and viability (Fig. 2K) in 786-O cell lines, while overexpression of MICAL-L2 promoted these processes (Fig. 2L). Collectively, our results demonstrated that MICAL-L2 functions as a tumor promoter in various human solid tumors and could represent a novel target for intervening tumor behavior.

MICAL-L2 overexpression promotes angiogenesis in KIRC cells

Upon conducting IHC on KIRC clinical samples, we observed a heightened level of microvessel density (MVD) in samples with elevated MICAL-L2 expression (Fig. 3A, B). Furthermore, HUVECs co-cultured with the MICAL-L2-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3C) formed more stable loops and tubes, whereas HUVECs in the control group organized fewer tubular structures (Fig. 3D, E). The TCGA data from the GSEA analysis indicated that the VEGF signaling pathway was significantly enriched in MICAL-L2-high-expressing KIRC samples (Fig. 3F).

Additionally, the expression of various angiogenic factors, including ANG and VEGFA, was increased in MICAL-L2-overexpressing KIRC cells (Fig. 3H). Meanwhile, in the co-cultured HUVECs, the total expression levels of VEGFR2, AKT, and ERK remained relatively unchanged, while the abundance of their phosphorylated forms (p-VEGFR2, p-AKT, and p-ERK) was significantly increased (Fig. 3G). The above results suggested that MICAL-L2 critically promotes angiogenesis in KIRC .

MICAL-L2 overexpression promotes KIRC cells angiogenesis by activating the mTOR/HIF1α/VEGF pathway

Subsequently, we investigated how MICAL-L2 promoted tumor angiogenesis. We had already found that MICAL-L2 was closely associated with stromal remodeling, cytokine recruitment, and the formation of a tumor hypoxia microenvironment (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, the HPA database confirmed a positive correlation between MICAL-L2 and HIF family members in various tumors (Figure S3).

Next, we conducted IHC staining on consecutive pathological sections of KIRC tissues (Fig. 4A) and scored the expression levels of MICAL-L2, HIF1α, and VEGFA individually (Fig. 4B). The results indicated that these three proteins were co-expressed at high levels in KIRC tissues compared to normal kidney tissues. Moreover, overexpression of MICAL-L2 significantly increased the expression of HIF1α and VEGFA in 786-O cells, as confirmed by RT-qPCR (Fig. 4C). Data from the CPTAC database revealed a significant elevation in MICAL-L2 expression in KIRC patients with altered mTOR pathways (Figure S4A). Ridaforolimus, an mTOR inhibitor, downregulated the expression of HIF1α and VEGFA in MICAL-L2-overexpressing 786-O cells (Fig. 4D). In vitro angiogenesis assay further confirmed that the novel mTOR/HIF1α/VEGFA signaling pathway was activated in MICAL-L2-overexpressing 786-O cells (Figure S4B).

MICAL-L2 co-localizes with F-actin and promotes angiogenesis remodeling in KIRC

Previous studies have shown that in the resting state, the DUF3585 domain of MICAL-L2 binds to the CC terminal to achieve self-inhibition. However, when a small molecule Rab protein binds to DUF3585, the CC terminal is released, allowing the middle LIM domain to bind actinin and initiate actin polymerization and crosslinking8. MICAL-L2 is recruited to cell-cell junctions through its interaction with actinin-4 and actinin-132,33.This conformational change in MICAL-L2 is crucial for the outward radiative migration of epithelial cells, which might also be important in the invasion and migration of tumor cells.

The immunofluorescence assay demonstrated that MICAL-L2 co-localized with F-actin in KIRC pathological paraffin sections (Fig. 5A), 786-O cells (Fig. 5B), and Caki-1 cells (Figure S4C). The expression of F-actin was increased in the MICAL-L2-overexpressing group under a laser scanning confocal microscope, compared to the MICAL-L2-siRNA group (Figure S4D-E), suggesting an enhanced polymerization and assembly of the cytoskeleton. Furthermore, treatment with the actin polymerization inhibitor Cytochalasin B reduced the expression of HIF1α and VEGFA in MICAL-L2-overexpressing cells (Fig. 5C-D), indicating that MICAL-L2-induced actin dynamics play a crucial role in tumor hypoxia-related pathways.

The expression of MICAL-L2 correlates with the sensitivity of various cancers to antiangiogenic agents and microtubule inhibitors

To investigate the effect of MICAL-L2 expression on drug sensitivity, we obtained the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for cancer cell lines treated with target drugs and integrated the gene expression profiles of tissue samples into a drug sensitivity matrix using the OncoPredict algorithm. The data for each group were depicted in Fig. 6, with each data point representing the sensitivity of a tissue group to a specific drug. We observed that tumors with high MICAL-L2 expression tended to develop resistance to multiple anti-VEGF agents, including Amuvatinib, Axitinib, Brivanib, Imatinib, Ki8751, Lenvatinib, Masitinib, Motesanib, Nintedanib, Pazopanib, Sunitinib, Sorafenib, Tandutinib, Tivozanib, and Vandetanib, as well as the microtubule depolymerization inhibitor Docetaxel, across different cancer types. In contrast, high MICAL-L2 expression was positively associated with sensitivity to the actin polymerization inhibitor Cytochalasin B in liver hepatocellular carcinoma and mesothelioma. These findings underscored the influence of MICAL-L2 on tumor cytoskeletal dynamics and subsequent angiogenesis progression.

Discussion

The intercellular communication of cytoskeleton dynamics at the molecular level has been extensively studied in both homeostasis and pathogenic processes. Actin filaments, as integral components of the cytoskeleton, govern various physiological processes such as cellular endocytosis, motility, cytokinesis, and organelle transport34.Under pathological conditions, the modulation of the actin cytoskeleton has emerging interrelations with epithelial-mesenchymal transition, mitochondrial movement, and cancer metastasis35–38. Particularly when the cell environment becomes anaerobic, activation of the Rho family of small guanosine triphosphatases (Rho GTPases) signaling influences the actin cytoskeleton, inducing filament rearrangement and stress fiber assembly in specific cells39,40.Despite the potential of actin dynamics as a target for disrupting tumor blood supply and inhibiting tumor invasion, research has not extensively explored the effect of cytoskeleton rearrangement on tumor angiogenesis.

Previous studies have recognized that the MICAL family contributes to a wide array of biological events. During cytokinesis, MICAL3 acts as a midbody-associated scaffold for vesicle targeting, and MICAL-L1 facilitates the transport of Rab11a-positive endosomes along the cytokinetic bridges41,42. Additionally, MICAL1 increases breast tumor invasiveness by linking to Rab35 and activating ROS/Akt signaling43,44. Barravecchia et al reported that MICAL2 is primarily expressed in tumor-associated neo-angiogenic endothelial cells but not in the normal capillary bed, which is crucial for the local inflammation-induced VEGF response45. Most members of the MICAL family utilize their Flavo-Mono-Oxygenase Domain (FAD) to facilitate actin-binding, oxidation, and depolymerization through reversibly post-translational redox modifications of the methionine 44 residues in the D-loop of actin46–48. However, MICAL-L2 is a unique member that lacks the FAD and instead possesses distinct binding sites for small GTPases of the Rab family. Rab13 mediates its protein-protein interaction with actin filaments through the zinc finger motifs of its LIM domain, which functions as an actin filament depolymerization inhibitor13. Rab8 competes with Rab13 to bind MICAL-L2 and coordinates the assembly of tight junctions and adherens junctions49.

Our findings revealed that MICAL-L2 was the most frequently upregulated member of the MICAL family across various cancers and was significantly correlated with advanced clinical stages and poor patient outcomes (Figure S1).The results aligned with previous studies on non-small cell lung cancer, gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, and ovarian cancer9–12. Bioinformatic analysis revealed that MICAL-L2 was abundant in angiogenesis and hypoxia pathways and also played a role in Rho protein signal transduction and endothelial cell chemotaxis (Fig. 1). In addition, this study also found that high expression of MICAL-L2 positively correlated with resistance to anti-angiogenic agents and microtubule inhibitors, further revealing its potential role in tumor drug resistance (Fig. 6). These findings prompted this study to further explore the relationship between MICAL-L2 and the well-known hypoxia-related HIF family, hypothesizing whether MICAL-L2 could influence tumor response to hypoxic environments by regulating the expression or activity of the HIF family.

Furthermore, we selected KIRC to validate our bioinformatics analysis results for several reasons. First, KIRC is known to have a rich blood supply and is highly responsive to anti-VEGF agents, causing mutations in VHL that disrupt the degradation of HIF and activate downstream hypoxia-driven genes50,51. Second, the expression of MICAL-L2 in KIRC tissues was significantly higher than in the paired normal kidney tissues and correlates with fatal clinical outcomes (Fig. 2). Finally, pathway enrichment analysis indicated that MICAL-L2 was enriched in nephron development and renal filtration. Notably, single-cell cluster analysis revealed a high expression of MICAL-L2 in the CD34-labeled endothelial cells (Fig. 3), representing specific tumor-induced neovascularization rather than inflammation or injury-associated neovascularization52. In summary, the selection of KIRC as our research model provided a suitable system for investigating the role of MICAL-L2 in hypoxia-induced angiogenesis and uncovering the underlying molecular mechanisms.

We observed the co-localization of MICAL-L2 with F-actin in the cytoplasm of KIRC cells (Fig. 5), supporting its role as an F-actin assembly regulator, consistent with previous reports32,47,53. Given that hypoxia is a hallmark of most solid tumors, a better understanding of the hypoxic tumor environment in relation to cytoskeletal dynamics could pave the way for novel therapeutic strategies. Most current work attributes the formation of hypoxia to increased spatial cellular density and irregular erythrocyte flow33, and focuses on the series of tumor-associated cellular changes in the microenvironment, including the elongation of endothelial cells, infiltration of suppressive immune cells, activation of M2-like macrophages, evolution of mesenchymal stem cells from epithelium to mesenchyme, depolarization of fibroblasts, and loosening of cell junctions5,54–58. Differently, we attempted to explain the dynamics of oxygen deprivation at the subcellular scale, proposing that MICAL-L2-induced oxygenation cycles enhanced the hypoxic level in the tumor microenvironment, and tumor cells acquired more aggressive behaviors from this process.

Our findings further revealed that high expression of MICAL-L2 promoted KIRC cell invasion and tumor neovascularization through the involvement of the HIF family. Inhibiting actin polymerization in MICAL-L2-overexpressing KIRC cells reversed the elevation of HIF1α. Moreover, phosphorylated mTOR levels were elevated during this process, playing a crucial role in mitochondrial respiration coupled to ATP production and further inducing high intracellular oxygen consumption59. Targeting the mTOR pathway was also reported as a method to extend the time window of blood vessel normalization in human fibrosarcoma, prostate cancer, breast cancer, and granulosa cell tumor60–63. Therefore, our results provided evidence that the increased accumulation of actin filaments and endothelial cell tubulogenesis was regulated in an mTOR/HIF1α/VEGF-dependent manner.

Our study demonstrated the potential use of MICAL-L2 expression levels as a predictive biomarker for individual drug sensitivity to cytoskeleton regulators and mTOR/HIF1α/VEGF pathway-specific blockers in various tumors. While some of these agents have been reported to cause blood vessel regression, the mechanisms underlying this effect remain unclear, likely due to their direct targeting of immature endothelial cells and increased susceptibility to collapse64,65. Our research sheds new light on this problem, revealing that tumor cells overexpressing MICAL-L2 activate hypoxia-responsive elements (HREs) and trigger the translation of pro-angiogenic agents such as VEGFs, PDGFs, and FGFs. These small signal transducers were then transported to the membrane receptors of endothelial cells and indirectly promoted tumor angiogenesis.This finding also provides new clues for our subsequent in-depth study of the molecular signaling networks involved in the angiogenesis process.

The findings of this study pave the way for further exploration in related research directions. Further studies on the spatial and temporal fluctuations of MICAL-L2 expression in a tumor model with periodic hypoxia could provide a more physiologically relevant understanding of its role in the biological system. Whether MICAL-L2 can serve as a clinical prognostic marker or drug target requires further experimental evidence.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that MICAL-L2 co-localized with F-actin in the cytoplasm of KIRC cells, supporting its role as a regulator of F-actin assembly. Furthermore, under hypoxic tumor conditions, MICAL-L2 activated the mTOR/HIF1α/VEGF pathway, promoting the release of pro-angiogenic factors. These factors bind to the VEGFR2 receptor on vascular endothelial cells, subsequently activating the AKT/ERK signaling pathways to drive angiogenesis (Fig. 7). Our findings not only advanced the theoretical understanding of cytoskeletal dynamics in tumor angiogenesis but also identified potential therapeutic targets and translational research directions for addressing resistance to anti-angiogenic therapies.
