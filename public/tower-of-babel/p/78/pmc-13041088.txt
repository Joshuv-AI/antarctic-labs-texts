Subgingival bacterial profiles and antimicrobial resistance patterns in periodontitis: a multi-center observational study
Authors: Meng Xu, Teng Xu, Jingwen Li, An Wang, Shuwei Wang, Shi Wu, Yida Cai, Yi Zhou, Xiaohua Qin, Haihui Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSubgingival bacterial profiles and antibiotic resistance patterns in individuals with periodontitis exhibit significant geographical variations. However, data from the Chinese population remain scarce. This study aimed to characterize the subgingival bacterial profiles and antibiotic resistance patterns among individuals with periodontitis in Shanghai, China.Materials and methodsA three-center observational study was conducted from 2021 to 2022, enrolling individuals with periodontitis. Subgingival plaque samples were collected for culture, species identification by matrix assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, VITEK MS), and antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the agar dilution method for anaerobes and the broth dilution method for aerobes. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and periodontal characteristics of participants, bacterial prevalence, and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles. Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC50 and MIC90) values and resistance rates were calculated for each antimicrobial agent.ResultsA total of 240 individuals with periodontitis were enrolled. Streptococcus spp. (97.5%) and Neisseria spp. (80.8%) were the most prevalent aerobes. Among anaerobes, Prevotella spp. (86.3%), Fusobacterium spp. (53.3%), and Capnocytophaga spp. (42.1%) were predominant. Most bacterial species showed low resistance rates to amoxicillin (< 10%), except for Prevotella spp. (32.1%). Anaerobic isolates exhibited low resistance rates to metronidazole and all isolates remained highly susceptible to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid. However, high resistance rates to clindamycin were observed in Prevotella spp. (45.7%), Porphyromonas gingivalis (37.5%), and Streptococcus spp. (36.5%).ConclusionsThe subgingival bacterial profiles in this cohort exhibited low resistance to commonly used antimicrobials, except for clindamycin. All these highly susceptible antimicrobials could be considered in specific cases, such as Stage III or IV, Grade C periodontitis in young individuals.Trial registrationThis study is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Identifier: ChiCTR2100048025; 28 June 2021).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-07894-7.

Introduction

Periodontitis, a chronic multifactorial inflammatory condition associated with dysbiotic plaque biofilms, causes tissue destruction around the teeth, primarily involving anaerobes and aerobes [1]. Socransky et al. classified subgingival bacteria into microbial complexes based on their co-aggregation patterns, commonly referred to by color designations [2]. Although antimicrobials are not routinely recommended for the treatment of periodontitis, the 2020 European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) S3-level clinical practice guideline suggests that antimicrobials may be indicated as an adjunct to mechanical debridement in specific cases, such as young adults with generalized Stage III periodontitis [3].

Regional variations in the distribution of the subgingival bacterial communities are well-documented [4], with factors such as geographic and climatic conditions, population density, and public health measures contributing to these differences [5]. For instance, the prevalence of Prevotella intermedia in the subgingival plaque of individuals with periodontitis in Germany was 33.1–53.5% [6], but 90% in Colombia [7]. Similarly, the prevalence of Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) was 67.1–69.3% in Germany [6], but 46.3% in Japan [8]. Regional variations in antimicrobial susceptibility profiles are similarly pronounced [9, 10]. These disparities are likely driven by factors including antibiotic prescriptions and environmental antibiotic exposure [11, 12]. For example, Prevotella isolates demonstrated high resistance rates to amoxicillin (58.8%) and clindamycin (50.0%) in the United States [13], but lower resistance rates (< 10%) in India [14].

However, subgingival bacterial profiles and antimicrobial resistance patterns in Chinese individuals with periodontitis remain poorly characterized. Region-specific data from China are needed to provide descriptive evidence on antimicrobial susceptibility patterns in periodontal pathogens. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate subgingival bacterial profiles and antimicrobial resistance patterns among individuals with periodontitis in Shanghai, China.

Materials and methods

Study population

This three-center observational study was conducted to investigate the composition and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of the subgingival microbiota among patients with periodontitis in Shanghai, China—a population for which comprehensive microbiological and resistance data remain scarce. This multi-center, cross-sectional study consecutively enrolled individuals with periodontitis aged 18–75 years with ≥ 20 natural teeth, who visited Huashan Hospital, Shanghai Jing’an Dental Clinic, or the Dental Diseases Prevention and Treatment Center of Jiading District between June 2021 and June 2022.

Inclusion criteria were (1) age 18–75 years; (2) at least 20 natural teeth; and (3) overall good health. Exclusion criteria included (1) comorbidities such as diabetes, HIV, or inflammatory rheumatic diseases; (2) current or former smokers (those who quit within the past 12 months); (3) long-term use of immunosuppressants or anti-inflammatory drugs; (4) pregnancy or lactation; and (5) receipt of periodontal or antibiotic treatment within 3 months before clinical examination [15, 16]. Eligible participants were diagnosed according to the 2018 classification of periodontal diseases [1]. Detailed diagnostic inclusion criteria are provided in Additional file 1.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Huashan Hospital (Huashan IRB #2021-038). All participants provided written informed consent. The study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100048025) and reported in accordance with STROBE guidelines.

Sample collection and transportation

Subgingival plaque specimens were collected following a standardized procedure. After isolating the sampling area with cotton rolls and carefully removing saliva and supragingival plaque with a sterile curette, four sterile absorbent paper points (Gapadent Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) were inserted into the gingival sulcus or periodontal pocket for more than 20 s. Samples from participants with periodontitis were collected from the teeth with the deepest periodontal pocket in each quadrant. All paper points from the same participant were placed in an Eppendorf tube containing sterile, pre-reduced transport fluid [12] and stored at 4 °C in a refrigerator or in a 0 °C insulated container during transport. The time from sampling to inoculation did not exceed 8 h [17]. All subgingival plaque samples were sent to the Institute of Antibiotics at Huashan Hospital for culture, identification, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Bacterial culture and identification

Plaque specimens were vortexed for 30 s to ensure homogenization. Graded dilutions (10–1, 10–2, 10–3, and 10–4) were prepared using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and 100 µl of each dilution was inoculated onto three types of agar plates and spread evenly. These agar plates included Columbia blood agar (Bio-Kont Laboratory Supplies, Guangzhou, China), anaerobic basal blood agar (Oxoid Limited, Basingstoke, UK), and selective Brucella blood agar (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) containing amikacin, vancomycin, hemin (5 mg/l), vitamin K (1 mg/l) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 5% sterile sheep blood. Columbia blood agar plates were incubated at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator for 1 day, while the other two types of plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2–5 days under anaerobic conditions (80% N2, 10% CO2, and 10% H2). Bacterial strains were identified using the matrix assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, VITEK MS with Knowledge Base v3.2; bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed on isolates of Prevotella spp., Actinomyces spp., Fusobacterium spp., Capnocytophaga spp., Veillonella spp., Parvimonas micra (P. micra), P. gingivalis, Streptococcus spp., Granulicatella spp., Gemella spp., and Neisseria spp. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined using the agar dilution method for anaerobes and the broth dilution method for aerobes, according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) document M100 [18]. Fastidious bacteria were tested according to CLSI document M45-A2 [19]. Bacteroides fragilis ATCC 25,285, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213, Neisseria gonorrhoeae ATCC 49,226, and Streptococcus pneumoniae ATCC 49,619 were used as quality controls. Antimicrobial agents, including amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, cefuroxime, moxifloxacin, levofloxacin, clindamycin, metronidazole, doxycycline, and minocycline (National Institutes for Food and Drug Control, China), were tested according to the respective species guidelines and references [18, 19].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic, clinical, microbiological, and antimicrobial data. Continuous variables (e.g., age, probing depth [PD], clinical attachment level [CAL], percentage of sites with bleeding on probing [BOP]) were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups (Stage I–II vs. Stage III–IV) were performed using Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables (e.g., gender) were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test. The prevalence of bacterial species and genus was compared across subgroups stratified by periodontitis stage, gender, and age using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. For each antimicrobial agent, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values (MIC₅₀ and MIC₉₀) and resistance rates were calculated.

Binary logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate factors associated with (1) clindamycin resistance and (2) the presence of major periodontal pathogens (Prevotella spp. and P. gingivalis). Univariable analyses were first conducted, followed by multivariable models including variables of clinical relevance: age, sex, periodontitis stage, mean PD, CAL, percentage of sites with BOP, and study center. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated per unit increase for continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R (RStudio version 2023.09.1 + 494).

Antimicrobial susceptibility

The antimicrobial susceptibility of various aerobic and anaerobic bacteria isolated from individuals with periodontitis is summarized in Table 3. Prevotella spp. exhibited the highest rate of amoxicillin resistance (32.1%), followed by Capnocytophaga spp. (8.9%) and P. gingivalis (3.1%). Among the anaerobes, Prevotella spp., Capnocytophaga spp. and P. gingivalis demonstrated the highest rates of clindamycin resistance (45.7%, 40.2% and 37.5%, respectively). Among the aerobes, 36.5% of Streptococcus spp. and 34.5% of Granulicatella spp. isolates were resistant to clindamycin.

Anaerobes generally showed low resistance rates (< 2%) to doxycycline and minocycline, except for Veillonella spp. (3.1% and 15.6% resistance, respectively). Similarly, most anaerobes exhibited low resistance to moxifloxacin, whereas Prevotella spp. and Veillonella spp. showed relatively higher resistance rates of approximately 15%.

Among the anaerobes, only Actinomyces spp. (82.6%) and Capnocytophaga spp. (62.5%) exhibited high resistance to metronidazole. All metronidazole-resistant strains were still sensitive to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid.

No statistically significant differences were observed in the resistance rates of anaerobes and aerobes to any of the antimicrobials tested among various periodontitis groups, as summarized in Table 3. In univariable logistic regression analyses, mean CAL was significantly associated with clindamycin resistance (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.61–0.97; P < 0.05). After adjustment for potential confounders in the multivariable model, BOP remained independently associated with clindamycin resistance (OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.00–1.04; P < 0.05) (Table 4). In analyses of bacterial presence, multivariable logistic regression showed that BOP was independently associated with the presence of Prevotella spp. (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.02–1.06; P < 0.05), while mean PD was independently associated with the presence of P. gingivalis (OR 2.10, 95% CI 1.22–3.78; P < 0.05) (Tables S2 and S3).

Discussion

This multi-center cross-sectional study investigated subgingival bacterial profiles and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns in individuals with periodontitis in Shanghai, China. The findings provide region-specific descriptive data from Shanghai and offer a scientific reference to inform awareness of antimicrobial susceptibility patterns in subgingival bacteria. These results may support antimicrobial stewardship efforts in periodontal practice, aligning with the global call for responsible antimicrobial use in dentistry. The antimicrobial susceptibility results indicate that commonly used antimicrobials remain effective in vitro against clinical isolates, except clindamycin. However, in alignment with current guidelines, these findings call for a restrictive use of antimicrobials in periodontitis treatment. These antimicrobials should be reserved for specific high-risk cases as adjuncts to mechanical debridement, rather than used routinely [3]. Given the observed resistance profile, clindamycin should be interpreted cautiously when considered as a potential therapeutic option, particularly in the absence of susceptibility testing.

The microbial profiles in our cohort demonstrated a high prevalence of Streptococcus spp. (97.5%) and Neisseria spp. (80.8%) among aerobes, while Prevotella spp. (86.3%) was the dominant anaerobe. These findings are consistent with classical complexes [2] and prior reports identifying Prevotella and Streptococcus species as key colonizers in periodontitis, but they also highlight regional variations [4, 6, 7, 20]. For instance, the prevalence of P. gingivalis in different stage groups in our study (20.5%, 42.9% and 43.7%) was markedly lower than in German cohorts (67.1–69.3%) [6], yet closer to Japanese data (46.3%) [8]. Notably, the detection of Streptococcus sinensis in our cohort, not previously reported elsewhere, underscores unique regional microbial signatures [4, 5].

According to antimicrobial prescription guidelines for dentists, amoxicillin combined with metronidazole remains the first-choice antimicrobial treatment for Stage III–IV, Grade C periodontitis [21]. In this study, all bacterial isolates were susceptible to both amoxicillin and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, while Prevotella spp. showed a 32.1% resistance rate to amoxicillin alone but retained full susceptibility to the combination agent. This pattern mirrors reports from Japan, where Prevotella strains from oral infections showed similarly high amoxicillin resistance (30.5%) but 100% susceptibility to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid [22]. Furthermore, periodontal isolates from other regions have demonstrated high sensitivity to both amoxicillin and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid [6, 23]. In our study, anaerobic isolates exhibited lower resistance rates (0–12.5%) to metronidazole. This finding is consistent with reports from Germany, Japan, and the United States, where P. gingivalis, Fusobacterium spp., and Prevotella spp. typically show < 1% resistance [6, 13, 22]. Crucially, all metronidazole-resistant isolates in our study remained susceptible to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, suggesting that this combination may remain a reliable adjunctive regimen in Shanghai, China, particularly for high-risk groups requiring antimicrobials.

For penicillin-allergic patients, cephalosporins, quinolones, and tetracyclines may serve as alternative antimicrobials [24]. Our isolates demonstrated high susceptibility to cefuroxime, doxycycline, minocycline, and moxifloxacin, mirroring the 100% doxycycline sensitivity reported in Germany [6]. Indian data similarly indicate high susceptibility to moxifloxacin, amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, and doxycycline (> 90%), though with lower susceptibility to cefuroxime (77.5%) and clindamycin (37.5%) [14]. However, anaerobic bacteria—particularly Prevotella spp. (49.6%) and P. gingivalis (37.5%)—exhibited markedly higher clindamycin resistance rates than those reported elsewhere [6, 13]. This finding is consistent with previous studies from China involving periodontal abscesses, gingivitis, and healthy populations [25–27], and may be linked to regional variations in antibiotic prescription practices and environmental exposure.

The most concerning observation was the high resistance to clindamycin among both anaerobes (Prevotella spp. 45.7%; P. gingivalis 37.5%) and aerobes (Streptococcus spp. 36.5%; Granulicatella spp. 34.5%), while resistance rates to other antimicrobials remained lower. These rates exceed those reported in India (< 10%) [15] and even surpass recent German and U.S. reports for P. gingivalis [6, 23]. This resistance pattern likely reflects cumulative antibiotic selection pressure within the local population [11], potentially related to historical prescribing practices in China, where macrolides (11.5%) and lincosamides (1.2%) are commonly used in stomatology, whereas extended-spectrum penicillin (7.9%) and penicillin/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations (4.1%) are prescribed less frequently [28]. Contrarily, amoxicillin (> 50%) and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (24%) are more commonly used in other countries [24, 29]. Although clindamycin is not widely prescribed in routine dental practice in China, macrolides—particularly erythromycin—have historically been used more extensively [11]. Because macrolides and lincosamides share overlapping ribosomal target sites, resistance mechanisms such as the MLSB phenotype may confer cross-resistance to both classes. Thus, the elevated clindamycin resistance observed in our isolates is more plausibly related to macrolide-associated selection pressure rather than direct clindamycin use. These findings highlight the substantial antibiotic selection pressure in this population and suggest that clindamycin should be used with extreme caution in Shanghai and likely other regions of China without prior susceptibility testing. However, treatment decisions must rely on standardized periodontal management guidelines, individual patient assessment, and local stewardship policies rather than culture-based data alone.

The multivariable analyses allowed us to identify factors independently associated with bacterial presence and antimicrobial resistance after adjustment for potential confounders. After adjustment, only a limited number of clinical parameters remained associated with these outcomes, whereas most variables showed no independent associations. This strengthened the validity of the observed associations and reduces the risk of confounding inherent to descriptive analyses in observational studies.

This study has some limitations. First, potential population biases include geographical restriction (samples from only three hospitals) and the limited sample size. Second, specialized culture methods were not used for certain fastidious periodontal pathogens, such as Aggregatibacter, Tannerella, and Treponema species. Therefore, the present findings may not fully capture the entire subgingival microbiome. Future studies should involve larger sample sizes and more robust methodologies to better elucidate subgingival bacterial profiles and antimicrobial resistance patterns.

Conclusion

Culture-based methods identified Streptococcus spp., Neisseria spp., Prevotella spp., Fusobacterium spp., Capnocytophaga spp., Actinomyces spp., P. gingivalis, and P. micra as the predominant cultivable microorganisms in subgingival biofilm from patients with periodontitis in Shanghai, China. The subgingival antimicrobial resistance profiles in this cohort exhibited low resistance to commonly used antimicrobials for oral infections but notably high resistance to clindamycin. Current clinical guidelines do not recommend the routine use of antimicrobial agents as an adjunct to subgingival debridement in periodontitis is not recommended. In selected cases, such as young patients with Stage III or IV, Grade C periodontitis, certain antimicrobials with favorable susceptibility profiles may be considered, with careful clinical judgment and stewardship oversight, whereas clindamycin should be avoided or used only with confirmed susceptibility in Shanghai.
