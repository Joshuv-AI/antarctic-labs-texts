Genetic manipulation of the genes for clonal seeds results in sterility in cotton
Authors: Hongjia Qian, Jianfei Guo, Huazhong Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeterosis is a common phenomenon in plants and has been extensively applied in crop breeding. However, the superior traits in the hybrids can only be maintained in the first generation but segregate in the following generations. Maintaining heterosis in generations has been challenging but highly desirable in crop breeding. Recent study showed that maternally produced diploid seeds could be achieved in rice by knocking out three meiosis related genes, namely REC8, PAIR1, OSD1 to create MiMe in combination with egg cell specific expression of BBM transcription factor, a technology called clonal seeds. Interestingly, there has been very limited reports indicating the feasibility of this approach in other crops.ResultsIn this study, we aimed to test whether clonal seeds could be created in cotton. We identified the homologs of the three meiosis related genes in cotton and used the multiplex CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing system to simultaneously knock out these three genes in both A and D sub-genomes. More than 50 transgenic cotton plants were generated, and fragment analysis indicated that multiple gene knockouts occurred in the transgenic plants. However, all the transgenic plants were sterile apparently due to the lack of pollen. Pollination of the flowers of the transgenic plants using the wild type pollens could not generate seeds, an indication of defects in the formation of female sexual cells in the transgenic plants. In addition, we generated transgenic cotton plants expressing the cotton BBM gene driven by the Arabidopsis egg cell specific promoter pDD45. Two transgenic plants were obtained, and both showed severely reduced fertility.ConclusionsOverall, our results indicate that knockout of the clonal seeds related genes in cotton causes sterility and how to manipulate genes to create clonal seeds in cotton requires further research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05674-5.

Introduction

Cotton as a crop plays a crucial role in supplying essential raw materials for both human necessities and industrial products [1]. Cotton has been cultivated in more than 80 countries and provides 35% of the world's fiber [2]. Due to the increasing global population and climate change, it is important to develop cotton varieties with desirable traits such as high yield, good fiber quality, and stress resistance through genetic breeding [3]. Hybrid breeding has been a major breeding technique to create hybrids with superior traits in many crops, especially in cereals such as rice and maize. The advent of the commercial hybrid maize variety in 1930 marked a significant milestone in hybrid breeding and agricultural production, which exemplified the remarkable successes in heterosis [4]. The first-generation hybrids (F1) generated by genetic crossing possess the superior traits than their inbred parents due to heterosis, also referred as hybrid vigor, however, these traits are lost in subsequent generations owing to genetic segregation through sexual reproduction [5].

Asexual reproduction via seeds, known as apomixis, has the potential to preserve the heterosis observed in F1 hybrids for multiple generations by bypassing meiosis and fertilization [6]. This process results in self-propagating offspring that are genetically identical to the maternal plant, enabling successive generations to inherit the maternal genome without undergoing genetic recombination [7, 8]. Engineering synthetic apomixis involves three developmental steps: producing unreduced (diploid) female gametes without meiosis (apomeiosis), embryo development from the egg cell without fertilization (parthenogenesis), and functional endosperm formation [9]. According to the published paper by Khanday et al., knocking out three meiosis related genes, namely SPO11, REC8, OSD1 could create MiMe (Mitosis instead of Meiosis) and in combination with egg cell specific expression of BBM transcription factor, diploid rice seeds could be produced maternally without pollination and fertilization [10].

Meiosis is a fundamental process for sexual reproduction, generating haploid male and female gametes. Following fertilization, the resulting diploid chromosomes become established in the succeeding generation [11]. Meiosis consists of one round of DNA replication and two rounds of chromosome segregation, which includes homologous chromosome separation in the first division and sister chromatids separation in the second division. Therefore, meiosis produces four haploid gametes. Different from meiosis, mitosis involves a single round of DNA replication followed by sister chromatids segregation, producing two daughter diploid cells [12]. MiMe is an artificial process switching meiosis into mitosis during sexual cell formation. MiMe can be triggered by the concurrent mutations in three key genes—SPO11 (Sporulation), REC8 (REC8 Meiotic Recombination Protein) and OSD1 (Omission of Second Division), which orchestrate essential meiotic processes [6]. Mutations in these genes lead to the substitution of meiosis with a division resembling mitosis, ultimately generating clonal diploid male and female gametes [5].

SPO11 belongs to the type II topoisomerase-like enzyme, and is one of the nine important genes for the introduction of DNA double strand breaks (DSBs) during meiosis [13]. The repair of DSBs through homologous recombination (HR) proceeds with the process of synapsis and the homologous genetic information exchanges in the first meiotic division [14]. In addition, chromosomal recombination is positively correlated with the levels of DSB, which implies that SPO11 plays a crucial role in genetic recombination and pairing during meiosis [15]. The spo11 mutants in Arabidopsis exhibit much shorter siliques compared to the wild type, accompanied by a complete absence of seeds. This phenotype clearly indicates a fertility defect in these mutants [16]. REC8 is a cohesin belonging to the SMC (Structural Maintenance of Chromosomes) protein superfamily that is essential for maintaining sister chromatids cohesion [17]. It ensures the association of sister chromatids through centromeres, facilitating their movement together during meiosis I. However, REC8 dissipates in meiosis II, allowing sister chromatids to segregate to different poles [18]. In rice, the sterile phenotype observed in the REC8 ortholog OsRad21-4 mutant is attributed to abnormal pollen development. While the morphology of mature anthers appears similar to that of the wild type, there is a notable reduction in the number of pollen grains, and the size of the pollen grains in the mutant exhibits abnormalities [19]. In Arabidopsis, DIF1 (DETERMINATE, INFERTILE1) is a homolog of the Schizosaccharomyces pombe REC8/RAD21 cohesin gene. The mutant dif1 displays sterility in both male and female parts in Arabidopsis. Notably, the mutant plants show an increased number of flowers compared to the wild type, a characteristic phenotype shared with other male-sterile mutants in Arabidopsis. The female sterility observed in the mutant is also attributed to the disruption of DIF1, which consequently leads to abnormal embryo sac development. This highlights the essential role of DIF1 in the sexual reproductive processes of Arabidopsis, influencing both male and female fertility [20]. OSD1 is a plant-specific APC/C (anaphase promoting complex/cyclosome) regulator controlling meiotic cell cycle transitions, especially from meiosis I to meiosis II [21]. Mutation of OSD1 results in the premature exit from meiosis after meiosis I [22]. The osd1 mutant exhibits a normal phenotype, no lethal gametes, and normal fertility in Arabidopsis [9]. Overall, spo11 mutation disrupts chromosome recombination and pairing, whereas mutation in REC8 promotes the separation of sister chromatids instead of homologous chromosomes during meiosis I, leading to equational rather than reductional chromosome segregation. The osd1 mutation prevents the occurrence of the second meiotic division. Therefore, simultaneously knocking out these three genes results in MiMe and the production of unreduced gametes with genetic information identical to the parental plant.

For a successful clonal seed formation, initiation of embryogenesis from the diploid egg cell formed via MiMe is an essential process, and the transcription factor BABY BOOM (BBM) was found to be required for this process in rice [10]. BBM is a transcription factor belonging to the large family of AP2/ERF (APETALA 2/ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR) [23]. The role of BBM in plant cell pluripotency was first identified from Brassica napus as a marker for microspore embryogenesis and promoting spontaneous somatic embryogenesis in B. napus and Arabidopsis when overexpressing the BnBBM gene [24]. Following this discovery, several studies also confirmed that overexpression of the BBM gene could enhance plant regeneration via in vitro somatic embryogenesis in other plant species such as Populus tomentosa [25]. Horstman et al. [26] found that the Arabidopsis BBM gene is a key regulator of cell totipotency because ectopic overexpression of BBM induces spontaneous somatic embryogenesis from cotyledons. As a master regulator, the BBM gene modulates the expression of the so-called LAFL network genes including LEC1, ABI3, FUS3, and LEC2, and thus controls the developmental processes during embryogenesis. The BBM-like gene in Pennisetum squamulatum is the casual gene for parthenogenesis and haploid induction in this apomictic plant [27]. Genetic analysis identified a single dominant locus called apospory-specific genomic region (ASGR) responsible for the maternal haploid formation in this species [28]. In 2015, Conner et al. found that the ASGR region contains three highly similar BBM-like genes and one of the BBM-like gene named PsASGR-BBML is expressed in egg cell before fertilization. Knockdown of the PsASGR-BBML by RNAi reduced the parthenogenesis in pearl millet carrying this locus, while introducing the genomic region of the PsASGR-BBML including the native promoter into pearl millet induced maternal haploid formation [27]. These results revealed that the egg cell expression of PsASGR-BBML gene is necessary and sufficient for parthenogenesis in pearl millet and thus modulation of the BBM gene could be a promising way for maternal haploid induction in other crops. In fact, egg cell specific expression of the PsASGR-BBML gene in maize and rice has confirmed that this approach is feasible in haploid induction in cereals [29].

The sperm cell-specific BBM has been successfully used to induce parthenogenesis in rice. The specific expression of BBM in male gametes prior to fertilization was proven by Khanday et al. showing that BBM expression was only detected in male gametes rather than female gametes. Upon fertilization, the BBM transcription factor proteins are brought to the zygote where the male-derived BBM triggers the expression of the maternal allele of the BBM gene, resulting in biallelic expression of the BBM gene and promotion of embryogenesis [10]. Together with the fact that ectopic expression of BBM induces cell division, dedifferentiation, and somatic embryogenesis in plants, these studies further supported the essential function of BBM in the initiation of plant embryogenesis [30]. Based on the previous findings, fertilization-independent embryo development from egg cells could be triggered by the egg cell specific expression of the BBM gene.

In this study, we employed multiplex CRISPR/Cas9 gene knockout system attempting to simultaneously knock out the orthologous genes of SPO11, REC8 and OSD1 to create MiMe in cotton. We also generated cotton transgenic lines with egg cell specific expression of the cotton BBM gene by using the egg cell specific promoter pDD45 from Arabidopsis. Highly efficient gene editing and multiple gene knockouts were observed. However, all the gene knockout transgenic plants exhibited sterile phenotype and the fertility of the BBM transgenic plants was also severely reduced.

Materials and methods

Plant Materials

Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) variety Coker 312 was used in this study for cotton transformation and gene editing. The seeds of cotton Coker 312 was provided by Prof. Hong Zhang at the Department of Biological Sciences, Texas Tech University.

Construction of the vectors with triple knockout CRISPR system

The cotton SPO11 gene (Gohir.A01G090600, Gohir.D01G075900), OSD1 gene (Gohir.A11G313900, Gohir.D11G331100), and REC8 gene (Gohir.A13G115300, Gohir.D13G118300, Gohir.A08G227800, Gohir.D08G248100) were identified based on the sequence similarity search against the Arabidopsis SPO11-1 (At3g13170), OSD1 (At3g57860), and REC8 (At5g05490) protein sequences with the BLAST tool and cotton database (G. hirsutum V3.1) in Phytozome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/blast-search). The GhSPO11 proteins showed about 69% identity and 80% similarity with AtSPO11-1, the GhOSD1 proteins exhibited about 42% identity and 56% similarity with AtOSD1, and the N-terminal and C-terminal of GhREC8 proteins displayed about 43% identity and 56% similarity with AtREC8 (Supplementary Fig. S1). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the evolutionary relationship of the cotton SPO11, OSD1, REC8 and BBM with the homologous proteins from other plant species (Supplementary Fig. S2).

To knock out the three genes for MiMe in cotton, two pairs of oligos serving as guide RNAs for gene editing of SPO11, OSD1 and REC8 respectively, were designed and commercially synthesized (Supplementary Table S1). The oligos were designed to simultaneously target both A and D sub-genomes, and thus a single sgRNA can guide CRISPR system to knock out the homologous genes in both sub-genomes. Oligos were annealed in a thermocycler by heating at 95 ⁰C for 5 min and then ramping down to 25 ⁰C at 0.1 ⁰C per second. The multiplex gene knockout CRISPR/Cas9 system obtained from Dr. Jian-Kang Zhu’s lab [31] includes three vectors psgR-AtU6-26, psgR-AtU3b and psgR-At7SL for gRNA expression cassettes and the final binary vector pCAMBIA1300-Cas9. Three gRNA vectors were digested by BbsI and ligated with the annealed oligo duplexes, creating three plasmids psgR-AtU6-26-sgRNASPO11, psgR-AtU3b-sgRNAOSD1 and psgR-At7SL-sgRNAREC8. The gRNA expression modules containing Promoter-gRNA-Ter, were excised from these plasmids by using HindIII-XhoI, XhoI-XbaI and XbaI-XmaI digestion, respectively. The released fragments with the gRNA expression modules were ligated to the destination vector pCAMBIA1300-Cas9 to create the plasmid pCAMBIA1300-Cas9-sgRNASPO11-sgRNAOSD1-sgRNAREC8 (Fig. 1A).Fig. 1The constructs and cotton transformation. Construction of the plant expression vector with pDD45:BBM

The promoter pDD45 was amplified from Arabidopsis genomic DNA with specific primers (F 5’-CCGGAATTCAAATGTTCCTCGCTGACGTAAGAAGA-3’ and R 5’- ACGCGTCGACACTTGTGTTAGAAGCCATTATTCTTTCT-3’), which was used for egg cell specific expression of BBM for apomixis induction in cotton. The BBM genes (Gohir.A08G227000, Gohir.D08G247400) in the A and D sub-genomes of cotton were identified based on the Arabidopsis allele. The cotton BBM showed about 44% identity and 56% similarity with the Arabidopsis BBM (At5g17430) (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2). The pDD45 promoter was first inserted into the EcoRI and SalI cloning site of pCAMBIA1380. The BBM genes were amplified from the cotton genomic DNA by PCR with the primers (F 5’-ACGCGTCGACATGGCTTCCATGAACAACTGGT-3’ and R 5’-AAAACTGCAGTTATGTATCATTCCATACTGTGAATGTTGG-3’), which were designed to amplify the BBM genes from both A and D sub-genomes. The PCR-amplified fragments with the A and D genome BBM genes were then cloned into the pCAMBIA1380-pDD45 vector digested with SalI and PstI to create pCAMBIA1380-pDD45:BBM (Fig. 1A). The constructs were verified by sequencing.

Cotton transformation

The plasmid pCAMBIA1300-Cas9-sgRNASPO11-sgRNAOSD1-sgRNAREC8 and pCAMBIA1380-pDD45:BBM were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 by electroporation, respectively. Cotton transformation was conducted by agrobacterium-mediated transformation using wild type cotton Coker 312. Briefly, cotton seeds were treated with concentrated sulfuric acid to remove the fibers. The delintted seeds were then sterilized by 70% ethanol for 30 s followed by sterilizing with 12% Clorox solution for 20 min. The sterilized seeds were washed several times with sterilized water and then dried in a sterile hood. The dried seeds were sowed on germination medium in glass test tubes and cultured in the dark at 30 ℃ for 10–14 days. To prepare the Agrobacteria for transformation, 5 mL Agrobacterium culture harboring the plasmids was grown with appropriate antibiotics at 30℃ overnight with vigorous agitation. Cotton hypocotyls were cut into pieces and soaked into diluted Agrobacterium culture solution. After blotted off the excess Agrobacterium solution on a sterile filter paper, hypocotyl pieces were placed on the coculture medium in the dark at room temperature for three days. After coculture, hypocotyl pieces were transferred onto callus induction medium in a petri dish and cultured under dim lights at 30℃. The hypocotyl pieces were transferred to new callus induction medium once a month until the callus became big enough, and the callus tissues were removed from hypocotyls and placed on embryo induction medium under dim light at 30℃. The callus tissues were transferred to a new embryo induction medium once a month until the embryonic calli appeared. The embryonic calli were transferred to new embryo induction medium every two weeks for proliferation. To induce somatic embryo formation, embryonic calli were put in liquid medium and spread on embryo development medium under light at 30℃. After about 4–6 weeks, the visible embryos were placed on embryo elongation medium to promote embryo elongation and desiccation under light at 30℃. After about 5–6 weeks, the elongated embryos were transferred to the embryo germination medium and cultured until roots and a true leaf appeared. The young seedlings with several true leaves and a vigorous root system were transplanted into soil under 14 h light 30℃ and 10 h dark 25℃ cycles in a growth chamber (Fig. 1B).

Verification of the transformed plants by PCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from the leaves of transformed plants using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method, and PCR amplification of the transgenes was conducted. The primers for PCR amplification of the Hygromycin gene were F 5’-GATGTAGGAGGGCGTGGATA-3’ and R 5’-GTACTTCTACACAGCCATCG-3’. A pair of primers for PCR amplification of the gRNA region (F 5’-AAATAAGGCTAGTCCGTAGCGC-3’ and R 5’-AAACTTCGACAATCAGTCACGCGTC-3’) were also used for verification of the transgenes. The primer pair flanking part of the pDD45 promoter and the 5’ end of the BBM gene (F 5’- CACATCGACAACACTATAGC-3’ and R 5’-ATCACCTCCTCCATTGGTTG-3’) was used to verify the plants with the pDD45:BBM transgene.

Verification of gene editing by fragment analysis and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 20 transgenic plants harboring the gene knockout modules using the CTAB method. The PCR for fragment analysis was carried out in two steps. The first round of PCR was conducted by using a forward primer appended with the M13F sequence at its 5’ end and the reverse primers specifically designed to amplify either A or D genome sequence flaking the gRNA targeted regions. The primers used are shown on Supplementary Table S2. The second round of PCR was conducted by using a FAM fluorescent labeled M13F primer (5′6-FAM/M13F) and the same reverse primer, which generated fluorescent labeled PCR products. The second round PCR products were commercially analyzed for fragment analysis. The first round PCR products were also used for sanger sequencing to determine the exact gene edits in the targeted genes.

Among the 20 transgenic plants tested, most of the transgenic plants harbored mutations in the eight targeted genes. Most of the knockout genes contained two types of editing in the homologous chromosomes, while SPO11D, REC8 8A, REC8 13A, and REC8 8D had the same editing in both chromosomes in some of the lines. These results indicate that the multiplex gene knockout system we employed in our research is highly efficient and can be used to knockout multiple genes simultaneously.

Verification of the mutations by sequencing

To verify the fragment analysis results, we selected five transgenic plants and PCR amplified the regions covering the gRNA targeted sequences. The PCR products were sequenced to detect the specific knockout events. This sequencing approach offers a detailed and high-resolution examination of the genetic modifications, enabling us to precisely identify the locations and nature of insertions, deletions, or other alterations introduced into the transgenic plants by gene editing. Results showed that some of the targeted genes had the same modifications and other targeted genes harbored different modifications in the five tested transgenic plants (Fig. 2B).

Sterility of transgenic plants with multiple gene knockouts

Despite prolific flowering, all the gene knockout transgenic plants were sterile, as none of their flowers produced pollen (Fig. 3). The absence of pollen grains indicates a significant impact of knocking out the meiosis related genes on male sexual cell formation and male fertility in these plants. Pollination of the flowers in transgenic plants using the WT pollens could not generate seeds, an indication of defects in the formation of female sexual cells in the transgenic plants. Overall, our results indicate that knockouts of the genes related to MiMe in cotton cause sterility, and how to optimize the manipulation of the genes for clonal seeds in cotton requires further research.Fig. 3Flower phenotypes. Morphological changes of the gene knockout plants

All the transgenic cotton plants showed abnormal morphological changes compared to the WT cotton (Fig. 4). WT cotton typically exhibits three similar sized bracts per flower. However, some transgenic cotton plants deviated from this morphology, featuring only two bracts per flower. Notably, bracts of some flowers were closed, preventing the flowers from blooming. In some transgenic cotton plants, three bracts were present, but one showed significantly smaller size than the other two (Fig. 4B). Another morphological change was the petals of flowers. A regular open flower from WT cotton has five evenly sized petals, while some of the transgenic plants showed six petals in some flowers, and some of the transgenic plants showed small circle (not fully opened) or staggered opening of the flowers. Almost all the transgenic plants showed smaller sized flowers compared with WT, and the stigma part was far away from the stamen part (Figs. 3 and 4B). Furthermore, side branches of the WT cotton plants usually have one node at one position. However, some of the transgenic plants showed multiple nodes for every side branch. Some plants had shortened internodes and thus nodes were concentrated on main branches. In addition, some of the transgenic plants were shorter than the WT cotton (Fig. 4A). Usually, leaves from WT cotton exhibit a smooth and flat fan-shaped appearance consisting of three segments with about seven tips, while some leaves of transgenic plants had less tips, and some of them exhibited upward curled leaves (Fig. 4B). Some transgenic plants had similar sized leaves while some of them had smaller sized leaves, when compared with the WT. An interesting developmental change was that almost all transgenic plants contained more gossypol glands than WT, especially the gossypol glands appeared in the stigma of transgenic plants while not in that of the WT (Fig. 3C). The wide range of morphological changes suggest that the genes for MiMe are important for cotton growth and development. For the transgenic cotton plants expressing the BBM gene driven by the Arabidopsis egg cell specific promoter pDD45, two T1 transgenic plants were generated and were verified by the PCR amplification of the transgenes (Fig. 5). We observed that embryonic calli formation was more difficult in the transformation with pDD45-BBM than with the knockout constructs, which may be attributed to the specific expression of BBM driven by the egg-specific promoter. Both transgenic plants showed severely reduced fertility. Interestingly, one of the transgenic plants produced five seeds from three bolls and three seeds were able to germinate (Fig. 5A). Morphological changes of the pDD45:BBM transgenic plants

Two T1 pDD45-BBM transgenic plants exhibited abnormal morphological changes in comparison to WT cotton (Fig. 5A). These included dense branches due to shortened internodes and clustered flowers usually with three flowers blooming together. Most of the leaves of the T1 transgenic plants consisted only three tips. All flowers were devoid of pollens, featuring numerous gossypol glands on the stigma, which was notably distant from the stamens (Fig. 5Ae).

All three T2 pDD45-BBM seedlings grew normally although only one of the three T2 plants contained the pDD45-BBM transgene (Fig. 5B). The transgenic T2 plant showed sterility even though the flowers contain some pollen grains with apparently reduced amount, whereas the T2 WT plants produced bolls. This further suggests that expressing pDD45:BBM results in reduced fertility in cotton. Pollination of the stigma of the T1 and T2 pDD45-BBM transgenic plants with WT pollens did not generate seeds, suggesting that BBM expression may also affect female sexual cell formation.

Discussion

Conventional breeding techniques aiming to enhance crop quality include yield selection, pedigree creation, genetic backcrossing, mutational breeding, heterosis breeding, and marker-assisted breeding [32]. Among these, heterosis breeding is an important way to achieve the breeding goal by improving lint yield and fiber quality in cotton. The F1 hybrids demonstrate accelerated maturation, increased yield, superior fiber quality, as well as enhanced resistance to both biotic and abiotic stresses when compared to their parental varieties. Typically, the production of commercial F1 hybrids involves manual emasculation and pollination (HEP), which results in high costs, and thus F2 seeds were often used for cotton cultivation [33]. However, F2 seeds are not considered hybrid seeds because of the traits segregation and usually have lower yield than the F1 true hybrids. Therefore, maintenance of the heterosis of F1 hybrids in following generations is of high significance in heterosis breeding in cotton.

In this study, we attempted to combine MiMe with egg cell specific expression of BBM transcription factor to induce clonal seeds in cotton, which has been shown to be a feasible approach in monocot rice [10]. Although a very recent report indicated the feasibility of creating MiMe and thus polyploid tomato [34], whether clonal seeds can be achieved in other dicot crops such as cotton is largely unexplored. We created cotton transgenic plants for MiMe by simultaneous knockout of the three genes for clonal seeds, i.e. SPO11, REC8, and OSD1, but unexpectedly, all transgenic plants exhibited sterility due to defects in pollen formation and very likely defects in the formation of female gametophytes. Similarly, the transgenic plants specifically expressing the cotton BBM in egg cells also displayed significantly reduced fertility. In addition, genetic modifications of these genes also resulted in other morphological phenotypes, although we could not rule out that random mutations may also contribute to some of those changes. However, the sterility observed in the knockout transgenic plants is likely attributed to the mutational impacts of the three genes that are required for meiosis, which is an essential process for sexual reproduction. In Arabidopsis, knockout of SPO11 and REC8 genes results in sterility, while knockout OSD1 leads to the formation of diploid gametes. When combining the double knockout mutant spo11/rec8 with the knockout mutant osd1, fertile diploid gametes without genetic recombination can be formed in Arabidopsis [9]. Therefore, complete knockout of OSD1 in the background of spo11/rec8 is essential for fertile gametes formation. In the five sequenced lines of the knockout mutants, we found that the OSD1 in one of homologous chromosomes in A genome (A2 chromosome in Fig. 2B) harbors a three base pair deletion which results in a change of two amino acids in OSD1. One with a three-nucleotide deletion may be a partial knockout and the mutated OSD1 may still possess functions. This could account for the sterile phenotype of the triple knockout mutants. Another possibility is that the cotton genome may contain other genes complementing the OSD1 functions. However, searching for homologous genes of the Arabidopsis OSD1 or the OSD1 homolog UVI4 did not identify other OSD1-like proteins in the cotton genome. Continuation of this study could involve the design of two gRNAs for the simultaneous knockout of OSD1, addressing the potential sterility issue.

For the pDD45:BBM expressing transgenic plants, five seeds from three bolls were obtained and three seeds were able to germinate. Upon inspection by PCR, it was observed that among the three T2 plants, only one of them possesses the transgene. Hence, the production of these three T2 plants could be attributed to self-pollination, despite the limited pollen grains observed in the two T1 plants. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that the seeds were produced due to crossing with WT (growing in the same growth chamber), although artificial crossing using WT pollens with the stigma of the transgenic plants was not successful. It is also possible that the T2 seeds were generated through asexual reproduction without fertilization. In this case, the T2 plants should be haploid, which requires further investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study achieved remarkable success by leveraging CRISPR-Cas9 technology to simultaneously generate multiple knockout events, demonstrating a highly efficient gene editing system in cotton. In particular, our approach enabled simultaneous knockout of genes in both A and D sub-genomes, even in the first generation. Future research is needed to test the strategies of production of clonal seeds in cotton by modern technologies.
