Supramolecular liquid–liquid extraction enabled by Cy2Q[6] for highly selective separation of benzene/cyclohexane mixtures
Authors: Yuanxin Yang, Lixia Chen, Zhu Tao, Tao Hou, Xi-Sheng Wang, Qing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The efficient separation of benzene (C6H6) from cyclohexane (C6H6) remains a longstanding challenge due to their similar molecular structures and nearly identical boiling points. Herein, we report a supramolecular liquid–liquid extraction strategy based on a water-soluble dicyclohexyl-substituted cucurbit[6]uril (Cy2Q[6]) for this demanding separation. Spectroscopic studies reveal that Cy2Q[6] can encapsulate both guests individually, while competitive binding experiments demonstrate a pronounced preference for C6H6 in binary systems. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further confirm the higher thermodynamic stability of the Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H6 complex compared to Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H12. Exploiting this selective molecular recognition, complete separation of an equimolar C6H6/C6H12 mixture is achieved in a single extraction step under ambient conditions, affording quantitative selectivity toward C6H6. The aqueous Cy2Q[6] phase exhibits excellent recyclability with minimal performance loss over multiple cycles. Furthermore, simulated industrial experiments demonstrate efficient removal of trace C6H6 impurities, highlighting the practical applicability of this system. This work establishes a sustainable supramolecular extraction paradigm and provides an energy-efficient alternative to conventional distillation for the separation of structurally similar hydrocarbon mixtures.

Introduction

With the continuous advancement of industrialization, climate change driven by global warming has emerged as a pressing global challenge.1,2 In this context, reducing energy consumption and promoting sustainable development have become critical strategies for mitigating environmental impact. In the chemical industry, separation and purification processes are indispensable for obtaining high-purity products, improving resource efficiency, and minimizing energy waste. However, conventional separation technologies are still dominated by distillation.3,4 It is estimated that distillation accounts for approximately 10–15% of the global energy consumption,5 and its energy-intensive and environmentally burdensome nature is increasingly incompatible with the principles of green chemistry. Consequently, the development of energy-efficient and sustainable separation technologies has become a central research focus.

Among various separation challenges, the discrimination of structurally similar molecules—such as olefin/paraffin and aromatic/cycloalkane systems—remains particularly difficult.6 Benzene (C6H6) is a fundamental feedstock7 widely used in the production of styrene, phenol, cyclohexane, and adipic acid, as well as in pharmaceutical synthesis. Cyclohexane (C6H12) is also an important industrial chemical, serving as a solvent,8 fuel additive,9 and synthetic intermediate.10 In industrial processes, C6H6 hydrogenation often generates cyclohexane and related intermediates, leading to complex mixtures. Therefore, the efficient separation of C6H6 and C6H12 is essential for ensuring product purity and downstream process performance. However, this separation is exceptionally challenging due to their similar molecular sizes and nearly identical boiling points (C6H6: 80.1 °C; C6H12: 80.7 °C),11,12 as well as their tendency to form azeotropic mixtures.13 Although extractive and azeotropic distillation have been employed industrially,14,15 these methods typically involve complex operation and high energy input, limiting their sustainability.

To address the inherent limitations of distillation, researchers have developed a variety of novel separation techniques for aromatic/alicyclic hydrocarbon mixtures. Adsorption separation based on porous materials such as metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), molecular sieves and porous organic polymers (POPs)16–18 can reduce overall energy consumption, yet suffers from slow adsorption equilibrium, poor regeneration performance and inferior thermal stability. Membrane separation19 features high equipment integration, continuous operation and mild operating conditions. However, its practical large-scale application is hindered by the trade-off between permeability and selectivity, as well as membrane fouling and swelling issues. Liquid–liquid extraction is another promising alternative with the advantages of flexible operation and fast mass transfer.20,21 Nevertheless, conventional extraction systems rely heavily on large amounts of volatile and toxic organic solvents, which pose severe environmental and safety risks, along with poor recyclability.22,23 In general, the above conventional separation technologies exhibit evident deficiencies in energy efficiency, separation performance, environmental friendliness and reusability.

Against this background, supramolecular extraction technology based on molecular recognition24 has emerged as a highly competitive research direction, owing to its prominent advantages including high selectivity, low energy consumption, excellent reusability and good adaptability to complex systems. Cucurbit[n]urils (Q[n]s),25–27 as a class of fourth-generation supramolecular macrocycles, have attracted significant attention due to their rigid pumpkin-shaped structures, consisting of a hydrophobic cavity and carbonyl-fringed portals. This unique architecture enables strong and selective binding toward a wide range of guest species, including neutral molecules, cations, protonated amines, organic dyes, and metal ions.28–30 Accordingly, Q[n]-based materials have been successfully applied in solid-state separations, such as benzene/thiophene, tetrahydrofuran/pyrrolidine, and pyridine/3-methylpyridine systems.31–33 However, these systems are predominantly based on solid-phase adsorption, which may suffer from diffusion limitations and limited process adaptability. In contrast, homogeneous aqueous systems for liquid–liquid extraction offer superior mass transfer and operational flexibility, yet their application remains limited due to the poor solubility of native Q[n]s in water. To address this limitation, structural modification has been employed to enhance solubility. The introduction of cyclohexyl substituents can disrupt intermolecular packing, reduce lattice energy, and improve interactions with water molecules, thereby significantly increasing aqueous solubility. As a result, dicyclohexyl-substituted cucurbit[6]uril (Cy2Q[6])34,35 exhibits excellent water solubility and provides a viable platform for supramolecular liquid–liquid extraction.

Despite these advances, the application of Cy2Q[6] in the separation of azeotropic or structurally similar mixtures has not yet been explored. The C6H6/C6H12 system represents a prototypical and challenging case (Fig. 1a and b). Herein, we report the first use of an aqueous Cy2Q[6] solution as a supramolecular extractant for the efficient separation of a C6H6/C6H12 mixture (v/v = 1 : 1) (Fig. 1c). Remarkably, complete separation is achieved in a single extraction step, affording quantitative selectivity toward C6H6. This outstanding performance arises from the preferential recognition of C6H6 by Cy2Q[6] and the formation of a thermodynamically stable host–guest complex. Moreover, the Cy2Q[6] aqueous phase is environmentally benign, readily recyclable, and maintains consistent separation efficiency over multiple cycles. This work establishes a supramolecular liquid–liquid extraction strategy and provides a sustainable and highly efficient approach for the separation of challenging C6H6/C6H12 mixtures.

Results and discussion

To evaluate the feasibility of using an aqueous Cy2Q[6] solution for the separation of C6H6 and C6H12, the host–guest interactions between Cy2Q[6] and each guest molecule were first systematically investigated in pure aqueous media. Cy2Q[6] was synthesized in our laboratory according to a previously reported procedure (Fig. 2a and S1, S2). At 298 K, C6H6 were added to the solution of Cy2Q[6] in D2O, and the interaction was characterized by 1H NMR spectroscopy. Notably, the proton signals of C6H6 exhibited significant changes compared to those of the free guest. In particular, all aromatic protons (Ha) displayed an upfield shift (Δδ = −0.68 ppm, Fig. 2b, c and S3), indicating that the C6H6 molecule is encapsulated within the hydrophobic cavity of Cy2Q[6], forming a host–guest complex.36,37 A similar phenomenon was observed for C6H12. In an equimolar Cy2Q[6]/C6H12 system (D2O 298 K), the guest proton signals also shifted upfield (Δδ = −0.73 ppm, Fig. 2d, e and S4), suggesting that C6H12 can likewise be included within the Cy2Q[6] cavity.

Partial 1H NMR spectra (400 MHz, D2O, 298 K) of and its host–guest systems: (a) Cy2Q[6] alone; (b) Cy2Q[6] with C6H6; (c) C6H6 alone; (d) Cy2Q[6] with C6H12; (e) C6H12 alone; (f) Cy2Q[6] in the presence of a C6H6/C6H12 mixture.

Although both guests are capable of forming inclusion complexes with Cy2Q[6] in single-component systems, their relative binding preferences require further clarification. Therefore, competitive binding experiments were performed by simultaneously introducing equimolar amounts of C6H6 and C6H12 into an equimolar aqueous solution of Cy2Q[6]. The resulting 1H NMR spectrum (Fig. 2f) shows only the characteristic signals corresponding to the Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H6 complex (, δ = 6.46 ppm), while no observable chemical shift changes were detected for C6H12. These results clearly demonstrate that Cy2Q[6] exhibits a pronounced preferential recognition and selective encapsulation of C6H6 over C6H12 in the binary mixture, providing a molecular-level basis for the subsequent separation performance.

To gain deeper insight into the origin of the competitive selectivity of Cy2Q[6] toward C6H6 and C6H12, DFT calculations were further performed. The optimized geometries of the host–guest complexes formed between Cy2Q[6] and each guest molecule are shown in Fig. 3a, b and S5. The calculated binding energies reveal a significant difference between the two systems: the Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H6 complex exhibits a binding energy of −91.53 kJ mol−1, whereas the Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H12 complex shows a much weaker interaction with a binding energy of −30.84 kJ mol−1. The substantially more negative binding energy indicates a markedly stronger interaction and higher thermodynamic stability for the Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H6 complex. Multiple weak C–H⋯O hydrogen bonds are formed between the C–H groups of the C6H6 and the carbonyl oxygen atoms at the portals of Cy2Q[6] in the Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H6 complex. All bond lengths are shorter than the sum of the van der Waals radii of C–H and O atoms (approximately 2.72 Å), confirming the existence of distinct directional attractive interactions. By contrast, no contacts shorter than the sum of van der Waals radii are observed between the C–H bonds of C6H12 and the portal carbonyl oxygen atoms in the Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H12 complex, with all interatomic distances exceeding 2.90 Å. This indicates that only non-specific van der Waals interactions exist between C6H12 and Cy2Q[6], without directional weak bonding. Differential charge density calculations were also performed. Obvious charge accumulation (green isosurface) was observed between the C6H6 plane and the portals of Cy2Q[6], accompanied by directional charge depletion (yellow isosurface) at the edges of the C6H6 (Fig. 3c) and the inner wall of the cucurbituril cavity. This phenomenon reveals strong polarization, electron rearrangement and charge transfer of the π-electron cloud of C6H6 during the interaction with the cavity, with a total charge transfer of 0.65e. In comparison, the yellow and blue isosurfaces are extremely sparse in the C6H12 system (Fig. 3d), and the corresponding charge transfer is only 0.37e, demonstrating that no detectable electron redistribution occurs between C6H12 and Cy2Q[6]. These results clearly elucidate the origin of the selective recognition behavior. Firstly, demonstrating that Cy2Q[6] preferentially binds C6H6 over C6H12 from an energetic perspective. Secondly, combined with geometric structure comparison and differential charge density analysis, the selectivity of Cy2Q[6] toward C6H6 originates from the unique synergistic mechanism driven by the π-electrons of C6H6 molecules. C6H6 can form a directional weak-bond network with the cucurbituril portals via C–H⋯O hydrogen bonds, and its π-electron cloud also undergoes polarization rearrangement and charge transfer to generate electronic responses. These two effects synergistically strengthen the host–guest attraction. In contrast, C6H12 lacks conjugated π-electrons, so it fails to form short-range C–H⋯O weak hydrogen bonds or induce electron polarization and rearrangement, and only weak non-directional van der Waals interactions exist between the two species. Importantly, this theoretical finding is in excellent agreement with the competitive 1H NMR results, collectively confirming the pronounced binding preference of Cy2Q[6] toward C6H6 in the binary system and providing a solid molecular-level rationale for the observed separation performance.

Following the elucidation of the selective encapsulation behavior of Cy2Q[6] toward C6H6 and C6H12, its application in liquid–liquid extraction was further investigated. To simulate a practical separation process, a two-stage extraction system consisting of two glass extraction towars was employed. Initially, 20 mg of Cy2Q[6] was dissolved in 10 mL of distilled water to afford a homogeneous aqueous phase. In the first extraction column, a 1 : 1 (v/v) mixture of C6H6 and C6H12 was introduced into the Cy2Q[6] solution. The biphasic system was stirred at room temperature for 6 h to ensure sufficient mass transfer, followed by standing for 30 min to allow complete phase separation, yielding distinct aqueous and organic layers. The aqueous phase after extraction was analyzed by 1H NMR spectroscopy. Compared to the pristine Cy2Q[6] solution, significant spectral changes were observed. In particular, a set of signals corresponding to the aromatic protons (Ha) of C6H6 appeared with pronounced upfield shifts (Fig. 4a), indicating the formation of the Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H6 inclusion complex in the aqueous phase. Notably, no proton signals attributable to C6H12 were detected, suggesting that C6H12 does not participate in effective complexation. These results demonstrate that Cy2Q[6] selectively captures C6H6 from the binary mixture during the extraction process.

Liquid–liquid extraction of C6H6 and C6H12 mediated by Cy2Q[6]. (a) Partial 1H NMR spectrum (400 MHz, D2O, 298 K) of the aqueous phase after the first extraction step. (b) Partial 1H NMR spectrum (400 MHz, CDCl3, 298 K) of the organic phase obtained after the second (back-extraction) step.

Subsequently, the aqueous phase containing the Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H6 complex was transferred to a second extraction tower and subjected to back-extraction using chloroform to release the guest molecule. Chloroform was chosen due to its strong affinity for C6H6 and its suitability for 1H NMR analysis. The 1H NMR spectrum of the resulting organic phase (Fig. 4b) shows exclusively the characteristic signals of C6H6, with no detectable contribution from C6H12. To quantitatively evaluate the separation efficiency, gas chromatography (GC) analysis was further performed. The results indicate that the purity of the isolated C6H6 reaches 100% after a single extraction cycle (Fig. S6). These findings unequivocally demonstrate that the Cy2Q[6]-based aqueous extraction system enables highly efficient and selective separation of C6H6 from C6H12.

The recyclability of separation materials is a crucial criterion for evaluating their economic feasibility and practical applicability. Therefore, assessing the cycling stability of the system is essential for demonstrating its potential in real-world applications. In this work, the recyclability of the Cy2Q[6] aqueous system was systematically investigated through consecutive extraction cycles. The results show that, after five cycles, the Cy2Q[6] solution retains its high efficiency and selectivity for the separation of the C6H6/C6H12 mixture, with no noticeable decline in separation performance (Fig. 5 and S7–S11). After five consecutive extraction cycles, the recovered aqueous solution of Cy2Q[6] was characterized by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S12). The resulting spectrum showed no significant changes in chemical shifts or peak patterns compared with that of the as-prepared Cy2Q[6], indicating no obvious structural degradation during the recycling process. These findings demonstrate the excellent reusability and operational stability of the system, highlighting its strong potential for practical and industrial separation applications.

Recycling performance of Cy2Q[6] for the separation of C6H6 and C6H12 over five consecutive cycles.

Given the excellent separation performance of Cy2Q[6] in liquid–liquid extraction, its applicability under practical conditions was further evaluated using a simulated industrial sample. To better mimic real-world scenarios involving trace impurity removal, a C6H6/C6H12 mixture with a volume ratio of 1 : 99 was employed, and an excess of aqueous Cy2Q[6] solution was used to selectively remove trace amounts of C6H6. GC analysis shows that, after extraction, the mass fraction of C6H12 in the organic phase increases from 98.76% to 99.34%, which falls within the accuracy range of the GC measurement (Fig. 6 and S13, S14), indicating effective removal of trace C6H6 impurities. These results further demonstrate the high selectivity of Cy2Q[6] toward C6H6, even at low concentrations, highlighting its strong potential for practical applications in the purification of cyclohexane and the removal of trace aromatic contaminants in industrial processes.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a supramolecular liquid–liquid extraction strategy based on an aqueous solution of cyclohexyl-substituted cucurbit[6]uril (Cy2Q[6]) for the efficient separation of C6H6 and C6H12. Spectroscopic studies reveal that Cy2Q[6] is capable of encapsulating both guests individually; however, competitive experiments demonstrate a pronounced preferential recognition of C6H6 in binary systems. This selectivity is further supported by DFT calculations, which show a significantly stronger binding interaction for the Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H6 complex compared to Cy2Q[6]⊃C6H12. Leveraging this molecular recognition behavior, a two-stage liquid–liquid extraction process was established, achieving complete separation of C6H6 from an equimolar mixture in a single extraction step with quantitative selectivity. The system exhibits excellent recyclability, maintaining its separation performance over multiple cycles without noticeable degradation. Moreover, simulated industrial experiments demonstrate effective removal of trace C6H6 impurities, highlighting its practical applicability. This work not only provides a green, energy-efficient alternative to traditional separation technologies but also establishes a general supramolecular extraction paradigm based on host–guest recognition. Such a strategy holds great promise for addressing challenging separations of structurally similar and azeotropic mixtures in the petrochemical industry.
