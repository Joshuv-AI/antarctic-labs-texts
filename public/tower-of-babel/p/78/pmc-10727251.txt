Connecting the Dots: Inflammatory Burden and Outcomes in Heart Failure
Authors: Michele Golino, Francesco Moroni, Antonio Abbate
Published: 2023

Heart failure (HF) is widespread and exacts a high toll in terms of morbidity and mortality. 1 Systemic inflammation has been shown to play a fundamental role in HF development and progression. Notably, up to two‐thirds of patients with HF have evidence of systemic inflammation by means of elevated biomarkers, and a high burden of inflammation was shown to associate with a significantly higher incidence of HF‐related hospitalizations or death. 2 , 3 The inflammatory response is particularly striking in patients with acute HF, where comorbidities and acute stressors can trigger the activation of the nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain–leucine‐rich repeat and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 inflammasome, causing the release of the proinflammatory cytokines IL (interleukin)‐1β and IL‐18. 4 CRP (C‐reactive protein) is an inflammatory protein produced by the liver in response to IL‐6, a secondary cytokine downstream of IL‐1. Therefore, CRP is widely used as a surrogate for IL‐1 activity, which in turn showed direct cardio‐depressant effects in cardiac cells and animal models, 5 , 6 and increased activity in patients with HF. 5 Even a minimally elevated CRP, measured with high‐sensitivity assays (hsCRP [high‐sensitivity CRP]), have been linked to worse prognosis among patients hospitalized for HF. 7 In addition, recent evidence suggests that the long‐term persistence of inflammation may contribute to cardiovascular disease. Cumulative hsCRP (ie, the area under the curve of multiple hsCRP measurements) may be a clinically useful surrogate for persistent inflammation. Preliminary data have suggested that cumulative hsCRP may outperform baseline hsCRP in predicting the risk of developing new‐onset HF. 8 However, to date, no data are available on the prognostic value of long‐term cumulative hsCRP levels in established HF.

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Dr Zhang and colleagues present a study that for the first time explores the association between long‐term cumulative hsCRP levels within 1 year after discharge and mortality during the next 3 years, in patients hospitalized for acute HF and included in the China PEACE 5p‐HF Study (China Patient‐Centered Evaluative Assessment of Cardiac Events Prospective Heart Failure Study). 9 The study enrolled patients hospitalized with a primary diagnosis of decompensated chronic HF or new‐onset HF, regardless of the left ventricular ejection fraction, from 52 hospitals across 20 provinces in China, from August 2016 to May 2018. The population included 1281 patients (median age, 64 years [54–73 years], 35.4% women, 35.4% with HF and preserved ejection fraction, 27.7% with HF and mildly reduced ejection fraction, and 36.8% with HF and reduced ejection fraction). hsCRP was measured at admission and at 1 and 12 months after discharge. The authors evaluated long‐term cumulative hsCRP levels using 2 approaches: cumulative hsCRP level quartiles and cumulative times of high hsCRP levels (ie, the number of times that hsCRP was above the time‐specific cutoff for high risk as identified in a training cohort). Patients were categorized into 4 groups based on cumulative hsCRP level quartiles as well as the number of times hsCRP levels exceeded the cutoff values. The researchers considered all‐cause death as the primary clinical outcome and cardiovascular death as the secondary outcome, both 12 months after discharge.

The levels of hsCRP were 3.5 mg/L (1.5–9.7 mg/L) at admission, 1.5 mg/L (0.7–3.2 mg/L) at 1 month, and 1.7 mg/L (0.7–3.8 mg/L) at 12 months after discharge. The median long‐term cumulative hsCRP level from admission to 12 months after discharge was 26 mg/L (13–56 mg/L) mg/L×month. Over the median follow‐up of 4.8 years, 374 (29.2%) patients died, and 293 (22.9%) experienced cardiovascular death. Patients in the highest quartile of cumulative hsCRP had a 2.4‐fold higher risk of all‐cause death and a 2.6‐fold higher risk of cardiovascular death compared with the lowest quartile. Similarly, patients with elevated hsCRP at all 3 time points had a 2.8‐fold higher risk of all‐cause death and a 3.1‐fold higher risk of cardiovascular death than those with hsCRP levels lower than the cutoff values at all 3 time points (reference group). These findings are notable for several reasons. First, by evaluating the duration and intensity of inflammation, the cumulative approach offers a comprehensive assessment of the impact of hsCRP on outcomes. Second, the study demonstrates that repeated measurements of hsCRP could serve as a more valuable tool for identifying patients at high risk of death beyond the traditional risk scores (ie, Get With The Guidelines‐Heart Failure score) or N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide level. Such measurements are simple and cost‐effective, making them feasible for routine evaluation in patients with HF.

The results also shed light on the potential mechanisms linking hsCRP to HF progression. Systemic inflammation appears to take a pivotal role in promoting adverse cardiac remodeling and left ventricular dysfunction, ultimately contributing to the worsening of HF. 5 A better understanding of these mechanisms could pave the way for targeted interventions and personalized treatments. However, 1 of the limitations of the study is that it included only Chinese patients who survived >1 year from discharge (890 died before 1 year) and with complete hsCRP data (2736 were excluded for lacking these data). Notably, patients included in the final population tended to be younger, have fewer renal and pulmonary comorbidities, and have better left ventricular ejection fraction than patients who were excluded, which raises the interesting hypothesis that assessment of inflammation can be of particular interest for granular stratification of lower‐risk subjects.

In conclusion, elevated cumulative hsCRP levels are associated with worse outcomes in patients with HF, and repeated measurements could identify patients at higher risk. Notably, CRP is a surrogate for IL‐1 activity, and IL‐1–mediated inflammation is not only a marker of higher risk in HF but has been shown to have a pathogenic role. IL‐1 blockade with anakinra (recombinant IL‐1 receptor antagonist) has been shown to significantly reduce CRP levels, 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 improve cardiorespiratory fitness in patients with HF, 11 , 12 , 13 as well as reduce HF‐related hospitalizations and HF‐related mortality. 14 Colchicine is another anti‐inflammatory agent that decreases IL‐1β production, consequently reducing CRP levels, and is effective in preventing recurrent atherothrombotic events, 15 , 16 but its efficacy in HF is unknown. An attractive alternative therapeutic target is IL‐6, given its central role as downstream effector of IL‐1–mediated inflammation. The ongoing HERMES (Effects of Ziltivekimab Versus Placebo on Morbidity and Mortality in Patients With Heart Failure With Mildly Reduced or Preserved Ejection Fraction and Systemic Inflammation) trial (NCT05636176) is currently randomizing >5000 patients to receive either IL‐6–targeted antibody ziltivekimab or placebo. Its highly anticipated results will be available within the next 5 years and are likely to provide a paradigm shift in the treatment of HF.
