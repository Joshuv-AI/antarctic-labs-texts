Strengthening hospital infrastructure in a war context: the case of the Mopti regional hospital
Authors: Fanny Chabrol, Pierre Coulibaly, Abdourahmane Coulibaly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In sub-Saharan Africa, public hospitals should play a key role in providing quality, affordable health care and contribute to robust health systems, particularly in war-torn contexts. In Mali, a multidimensional crisis has had a severe impact on the health of the people and on the overall health infrastructure and its capacity to respond to the crisis. Public hospitals suffer from multiple long-standing constraints that are rendered even more acute in the context of war. This paper presents governmental, development, and humanitarian efforts and interventions aimed at strengthening hospital infrastructure in Mopti, central Mali. Three key dimensions of hospital infrastructure are fundamental to ongoing operations: human resources, governance, and equipment/maintenance. The results revealed two approaches to strengthening hospital infrastructure: a developmental approach, aimed at constructing new buildings and implementing hospital autonomy-oriented reforms, and a pragmatic approach that focuses more on concrete solutions to the most urgent needs. The insights presented here argue for stronger linkages between these two approaches to reinforce the everyday functionality of health care infrastructures in destabilized contexts.

Introduction

Since 2012, Mali is facing a multidimensional crisis at all security, institutional, and humanitarian levels that is aggravated by climate change and has caused a massive displacement of populations, particularly the most vulnerable, i.e. women and children [1]. Increasing violence by armed groups, including kidnappings, targeted attacks, and intercommunal conflicts, severely impacts humanitarian access and operations. All levels of the health system in Mali are impacted, and many health centres are not working. Displacement and increased insecurity have disrupted access to water, sanitation, and hygiene services [2].

Successive governments in Mali have committed to improving access to health care. Mopti was chosen by the government as a pilot region for its health and social development program strategy because the region was facing serious socioeconomic, security, and health challenges. The first program (PADSS-I) and second phases (PADSS-II, that started in 2016) of this program were intended both to support the demand for care (through mutual health insurance) and to enhance the supply of care (through health infrastructure improvements, from primary to regional hospital levels). These program phases received financial and technical support from a French cooperation agency. Additionally, with the rapidly worsening security context, humanitarian and military interventions have been undertaken to strengthen the infrastructure of the main regional hospital to cope with the consequences of the conflict. Despite the acute stakes to ensure access to health care in this war-torn region, very few studies are available at the level of hospital care. For hospitals, these major challenges are rendered even more acute in wartime contexts, because they provide emergency care for war victims. In this study, we aimed to fill this literature gap by documenting efforts by the Malian government and its cooperation partners to improve the functioning, equipment, and maintenance of a regional hospital in northern Mali.

Public hospitals in Mali are characterized by recurrent financial, human resources and material deficits [3]. While hospital reform advocating hospital autonomy was adopted in 2002, it has been only partially implemented. Access to care continues to worsen in hospitals, and citizens and health professionals regularly decry these deplorable situations. Hospital infrastructure encompasses multiple essential aspects of hospital organization and functioning, such as financing, governance, human resources, and the organization of care, as well as the equipment, buildings, and maintenance that enable daily operation (including water and electricity management).

This conceptualization of hospital infrastructure emerges from two strands of literature. First, studies of health system resilience have been conducted [4, 5]. The notion of “hospital resilience” has focused primarily on disaster recovery [6]. Additionally, the concept of “everyday resilience” [7] emphasizes disrupted routines, daily challenges, and “the capacity to continue to deliver services of good quality and respond effectively to wider health challenges.” [8]Recent studies of hospitals coping with the COVID-19 pandemic [9] have helped identify crucial dimensions of hospital organization in the face of sudden disruptions: human resources, finance, governance, equipment, communication, and others [10]. Second, an anthropological framing of imbalances caused by external interventions and debilitations inherited from colonization and extending to the impact of structural adjustment policies in the 1990s up to disease-specific vertical programs [11, 12]. Hospital infrastructures are not only concrete and tangible (human resources, finance, leadership, buildings, networks, logistics), but also affective (people desire to work there, bring improvements, or leave) [13]. This study aims to analyze the multifaceted efforts taken by different governmental and cooperation actors to improve the functioning, equipment, and maintenance of a regional hospital in northern Mali.

Health development in a war context

Northern Mali is today one of the poorest and most neglected areas of the world, where humanitarian assistance is urgently needed. Civil conflict has impacted child mortality through excess deaths, mainly due to lack of access to safe sanitation and a reduction in vaccination rates [14]. Deliveries attended by skilled health professionals reduced between 2012 and 2015 in conflict regions from 40 to 36% respectively [15]. Because of worrying health indicators throughout from 2010s, particularly concerning maternal and child health [16], the Mopti region was considered most in need of health infrastructure renovations. Organizations’ ability to deliver services in northern Mali was impacted by the unpredictable and rapidly changing nature of the conflict [15].

Mopti region severely hit by the crisis and violence

As the conflict that broke out in the north of Mali in 2012 extended southwards and worsened, intercommunity violence and attacks by armed groups against civilians were reported in the Mopti region, with numerous atrocities against the civilian population [17]. Conflict-related violence has led to a demand for the care of victims, particularly in surgery, traumatology, and medical imaging. Gradually, violence is becoming an integral part of public health, increasingly involving physicians and all health personnel, taking care of victims of war trauma and victims of sexual violence [1]. Additionally, priority was given to strengthening the blood transfusion system due to the elevated mortality of women during childbirth from hemorrhaging, mainly due to the lack of blood and the failure of the referral system [16].

Sominé Dolo hospital in Mopti–Sévaré

Sominé Dolo Hospital is the only referral hospital (secondary level) for the region. In 2012, under the PADSS I program, the hospital was moved to a new building in nearby Sevaré, but is still widely referred to as Mopti hospital. The hospital has 144 beds and 17 medical and surgical departments. There are 181 permanent staff, 41 of whom are contractual staff paid from the hospital’s own resources. Given the security context, the PADSS II (2013–2018) was intended to strengthen and stabilize the health system of the Mopti region, seen as a buffer zone to prevent extension of the conflict southwards. The Mopti hospital was slated to become a referral hospital [18]. PADSS II aimed to build two new services (blood bank and sickle cell center), support hospital governance, and address medical and technical training needs. In addition to PADSS II, the hospital’s cooperation with a humanitarian aid organization (H-Org, for purposes of this article) was strengthened as of 2019. H-Org is present in the northern part of Mali and was already supporting the hospital in Gao. In both Gao and Mopti, the organization supports the care of the wounded (hiring and training staff) and provides material equipment for emergency services (surgery, traumatology, transfusion).

Methodology

Study design and data collection

This is a case study that uses an inductive method to allow participants to express their views and researchers to reorient their questions over the course of the inquiry [19]. Overall, 28 qualitative interviews were conducted by two experienced anthropologists. The respondents included directors and department heads in Malian hospitals, government officials and consultants, heads of development programs and humanitarian missions, healthcare professionals, and academics (Table 1). [Interview guidelines are available in an online repository]. In a total of 28 interviews, only two respondents were female, an unfortunate bias caused by the purposive sampling strategy. Most stakeholders involved in the PADSS II and Mopti Hospital were male. Data collection took place between 2018 and 2022, in France and Mali, in two distinct periods. From March 2018 to April 2018, consultations and exploratory interviews were conducted with French stakeholders involved in the funding of the PADSS II in Mali, and in March 2020, interviews were conducted with actors from Malian institutions and the hospital’s cooperation partners, focused on the development and objectives of PADSS II. Between September 2021 and January 2022, interviews were conducted about the 2002 hospital reform, as this theme emerged inductively and called for deeper exploration. The interviews were all conducted either in Paris or in Bamako or by telephone from Bamako due to the security context, which prohibited travel to Mopti.

Interviews (2018–2022)

Data analysis

The different periods of data collection allowed for an iterative process of data analysis and triangulation that was ensured in the course of the last interviews. Three recurring themes—human resources, governance, and equipment/maintenance—corresponding to key dimensions were identified and are congruent with the most recurrent resilience dimensions emerging from other studies [9, 20]. These dimensions were then analysed in terms of what was tangible or already achieved, on the one hand, and the expectations, hopes, and solutions envisioned or imagined by health professionals and public health actors, on the other hand (See Table 2).

Ethics approval

The research was approved by the ethics committee of the Mali National Institute of Public Health Research (34/2018/INRSP). All individuals were informed of the purpose of the survey and ethical considerations. They were given the opportunity to withdraw from the study at any time. All interviewees provided verbal informed consent. The study did not include patients.

Results

Human resources

Severe deficiencies

A lack of human resources was considered the main obstacle to any tangible improvement in the hospital’s functioning and the improvement of care. In 2022, for example, there were only two gynecologists and four midwives, whereas at least four and eight were needed, respectively, according to the Mopti Hospital Five-Year Plan (2020–2025). Medical and nursing staffing levels were sparse, a condition often expressed in the singular, or as absent: “There is no radiologist…. There is only one pediatrician.” (ADMIN_HOSP2). These shortages were experienced very acutely in the areas of trauma and surgery in the war zone:The only trauma specialist we have here died [in October 2021]; there’s no replacement, so there’s a military doctor who serves as a trauma specialist. In our zone, these things [such as having a trauma specialist] should be automatic…. So these are small staffing problems that are undermining our functioning. (HCP_Mopti2)

In our zone, these things [such as having a trauma specialist] should be automatic…. So these are small staffing problems that are undermining our functioning. (HCP_Mopti2)

Hiring and assignments

Positions were filled by appointment by the Ministry of Public Health, but health professionals were not attracted to such assignments, leading to repeated vacancies: “We’re missing high-level health technicians, people are transferred [here], they come and then leave, they never return” (HCP_Mopti4). Calls had been issued for assignments to Mopti, but were unsuccessful in attracting applicants. The hospital did not have sufficient financial means to recruit specialized personnel, as this level of autonomy and resources was not in effect. The hospital depended on the State for recruitment.We were promised a radiologist last year by the civil service… has that been forgotten?… The diabetologist is supposed to arrive, [but] he’s already almost gone…. We’re forgotten, the staff distribution means we’re ineffective in certain areas. (HCP_Mopti2)

Professional exhaustion

This sense of being forgotten by the State was experienced as loneliness and “work overload” at the hospital, resulting in constant physical and emotional fatigue:Because of the precarious staffing, we don’t get a rest after a night on call, so the law isn’t respected… so it may be that, given all these situations, some people might feel frustrated and then ask to leave. (HCP_Mopti4)

Salaries and benefits

To cope with these difficulties, Mopti Hospital receives subsidies from H-Org that enable it to hire human resources to address the most urgent needs related to the care of the wounded:The [H-Org] has been our partner since 2019, [which] has allowed us to recruit some doctors paid by them, and also nurses, to strengthen the services they support, because the [H-Org], they’re very interested in care of the wounded. (HCP_Mopti4).

The [H-Org] has been our partner since 2019, [which] has allowed us to recruit some doctors paid by them, and also nurses, to strengthen the services they support, because the [H-Org], they’re very interested in care of the wounded. (HCP_Mopti4).

The support was presented as cooperative and helped the administration pay salaries.We discussed with the hospital how we could increase the number of human resources. That’s why we recruited more than 40 staff members of all grades: doctors, nurses, physiotherapists, nurse anaesthetists, one surgeon, one trauma specialist, and other support staff. We try to pay them every month. It’s not a salary; these are premiums; we pay the hospital, and the hospital uses that to arrange a contract with each person working in this activity. (H_ORG1).

Training

The PADSS II provided individual training in France. A gynecologist received several training periods of a few weeks in a referral hospital. The maintenance engineer underwent two years of specialized training in France. This resulted in long periods of vacancy when they were not replaced. To counter this, H-Org provided onsite war surgery training that combined theory and practice: a “war surgery seminar”, and a 14-days “war surgery course” that included participants from outside the hospital, notably from Bamako and even Niger, all in 2021. With the hospital administration, H-Org provided training on crisis management in case of a sudden influx of patients. All hospital staff were trained, and a stock of materials was reserved to address such an event. This training, developed over the past three years, provided knowledge that benefited other professionals beyond Mopti Hospital.

Governance

The objective of the PADSS II was to strengthen hospital governance by helping the hospital achieve greater autonomy, in line with the hospital reform law adopted by Mali in 2002. In particular, support for governance was intended to lead to the approval and adoption of a hospital plan. This component did not achieve the desired results. While a new building (part of the PADSS II) had been completed, it was not functional at the time of the study, and other PADSS II components (hospital plan, computerization) had not materialized at all, partly because the 2002 hospital reform had not been implemented. Tensions between donor agencies and implementers (the consulting firm providing project management assistance) also led to delays.

The respondents reported that the hospital’s self-financing capacity was limited to 25–30%. They felt that the hospital should be less dependent on state subsidies and be able to raise its fees. Health professionals believe that this would give the hospital more means to buy equipment and thereby upgrade the rank of referral hospital. With respect to fees, only care provided to the war wounded was entirely free, and covered by the H-Org.Any [care for] wounds due to war, in any case, the initial treatment, is done free of charge. Often, even certain complementary examinations, they [H-Org] cover those. They pay for hospitalization for war victims. (HCP_Mopti2)

Any [care for] wounds due to war, in any case, the initial treatment, is done free of charge. Often, even certain complementary examinations, they [H-Org] cover those. They pay for hospitalization for war victims. (HCP_Mopti2)

While many health professionals favoured raising fees, they noted that the hospital’s board of directors was opposed to such an increase, to ensure access to care for the greatest number of people.

Organization of medical specialties

Many professionals interviewed wanted to raise the hospital’s level to tertiary care (referral hospital) and criticized the current organization into specialty poles (medicine, surgery). They argued for organizing services separately and more autonomously: “They made a ‘surgery pole’ and a ‘medicine pole’. All the medical specialties are in one place, and all the surgical specialties are in another” (HCP_Mopti2). They thought, for instance, that “Mopti deserves to have a cardiology service and an infectious disease service” (HCP_Mopti3). Professionals also complained about a lack of space, as they were compelled to share premises:I’m an anaesthetist, I don’t have an office to consult, I use the paediatric office for my consultations…. We have about ten surgeons who all have problems getting space. (HCP_Mopti2).

I’m an anaesthetist, I don’t have an office to consult, I use the paediatric office for my consultations…. We have about ten surgeons who all have problems getting space. (HCP_Mopti2).

Failed attempt to computerize patient management

To strengthen hospital governance, PADSS II intended to computerize patients’ medical records and admissions. This component was eagerly awaited by hospital professionals in Mopti, who saw it as way to ensure transparency and control of the hospital’s financial revenues: “Computerization is needed to be able to track patients and pathologies” (HCP_Mopti2); “Hospital computerization is [intended] to reduce human involvement in the financial process” (ADMIN_Mopti3). This project did not materialize because of an incomplete call for tenders. Even though it was not carried out, professionals referred to this project in the present tense, showing their strong support and intensity of expectations: “It’s a very good project; at least it enables good cost recovery and resource tracking” (HCP_Mopti4).

PADSS II targeted key aspects of hospital governance, in line with the key principles of Mali’s 2002 hospital reform, i.e., financial autonomy based on a hospital project and the upgrading of infrastructure and computerized information systems. However, these interventions were doomed to failure, as the 2002 hospital law was not implemented in the country: “What’s been missing all these years are the decrees to apply the new hospital law. There hasn’t been the political will to implement these texts” [ADMIN_HOSP2].

Equipment and maintenance

No maintenance policy

Even though the head of the maintenance department had been trained and a maintenance department was included in the organizational chart, maintenance activities encountered many serious problems. Delays in ordering equipment or spare parts and repairs were extremely long, which impeded service:The administration gets by. For example, you request something this year, maybe next year or the year after that, they’ll bring it to you. And maybe by then that thing won’t even be needed anymore. (HCP_Mopti2)

The administration gets by. For example, you request something this year, maybe next year or the year after that, they’ll bring it to you. And maybe by then that thing won’t even be needed anymore. (HCP_Mopti2)

Maintenance specialists were not involved in assessing needs, and there were needs, especially for spare parts, that were not covered in the budget. The lack of a maintenance policy often results in gaps between equipment-related planning and execution.

Decisive support in the context of the conflict

H-Org focused on various specific aspects of the hospital’s infrastructure. First, it provided solutions for essential infrastructure to mitigate water and power outages. It built a cistern connected to the water distribution network. A generator was installed to support the oxygen extractors and provide the fuel needed to operate the generator. Six latrine blocks were built to improve hygiene and sanitation. In addition to supplementing the salaries of health care workers, H-Org supported patients (war wounded) and their accompanying families and blood donors (often family members), for example, by providing meals and snacks. According to several doctors and hospital officials, the H-org addressed a wide range of urgent problems and offered effective and appropriate responses.

Military support during the COVID-19 pandemic

The military force active in the region with a UN mandate had been supporting the Mopti hospital since 2020, with the conversion of a hangar into hospital and office space and the construction of a new building to conduct Covid PCR testing.

Discussion

Two approaches to strengthening hospital infrastructure

The ability of Mopti Hospital to continue functioning daily lies at the junction of two noncontradictory and complementary approaches to infrastructure strengthening (Table 2). The first is a developmental approach that values the creation of concrete infrastructures (new hospital, new blood bank) and pursues objectives linked to structural reforms, e.g., implementation of the 2002 Malian hospital autonomy reform. This approach has been promoted since the 1990s by donors, particularly French bilateral donors, who believe that public hospitals will gain efficiency through more autonomy. French medical cooperation actors are committed to a policy of hospital-based development that values hospital construction and various forms of hospital twinning [21]. Construction continues to be a preferred cooperation tool, particularly for French public aid, but the burden of operation and maintenance is left to the States, which, like Mali, do not have the necessary means to ensure the functioning of existing facilities [22]. Hence, these developmental policies suffer from a gap between ambition and reality. While there are visible accomplishments today, such as new buildings, the operation and maintenance of the new services are not guaranteed and depend on the hospital’s ability to increase its autonomous resources (health care fees).

The second is a pragmatic approach directly associated with the war context and the field of action of the humanitarian organization. These are correlated mainly with the security context (care for the wounded, trauma care) and the hospital’s most urgent needs (staff recruitment and remuneration, functioning of basic infrastructure and equipment). Humanitarian actors’ responsiveness to needs is facilitated by their presence in the daily life of the hospital and repeated discussions with professionals and administrators. By virtue of its life-affirming ethos, humanitarian action is more concerned with urgent human needs for assistance than with broader structural reforms.

Everyday hospital operations in a war-torn region

Everyday operations or resilience, i.e., the ability to overcome recurrent shocks and disruptions, was facilitated by the second approach. Ensuring the essential aspects of water and electricity supply enabled continuity of care, as did ensuring salary payments for staff recruited to meet emergency needs. H-Org provided concrete responses to a public structure on a daily basis through a concerted approach. This was in contrast to the “humanitarian toolkit” paradigm [23], which has often described humanitarian action as the deployment of infrastructures separate from the public health system, such as field hospitals and treatment centres during the Ebola epidemic [24]. In Mopti, the potential pitfall of creating an intervention parallel to the health system was avoided, but only temporarily, as support is expected to be discontinued once the war has ended. In addition to direct and indirect war victims, healthcare infrastructures are increasingly being targeted in attacks globally; should this happen in Mopti, it could have a serious impact [25].

The developmental approach, on the other hand, tends to fail to materialize and foster expectations that have potentially negative effects on the accessibility of care. For example, the autonomy of medical or surgical services was seen as an appropriate solution to increase hospital revenues. Our respondents wished to achieve autonomy and therefore favoured solutions that would lead to an increase in fees charged. Even though they were aware of the poverty of the population, professionals saw this as the only solution to ensure that the hospital would have sufficient resources to recruit staff without waiting for public service assignments. In their view, the solution was to reinforce health insurance rather than to provide free care to patients. Fee exemption policies are unpopular with health care professionals [26].

Donors and the Malian government are still driven by the conviction that hospital autonomy would be the most effective structural reform for increasing the efficiency of hospital care. Despite the incomplete nature of hospital reform in Mali (and in other African countries), this idea remains the only prospect among national and international actors to improve hospital efficiency [27]. Hospital autonomy was again advocated after COVID-19 [28], showing the persistence of ideas guiding public health policies despite their lack of success [29, 30]. This underscores the influence of ideology in health policy development, as seen also with results-based performance, which continued to be promoted even though it was not backed by positive results [31].

Exceptional mobilization in extreme situations

In this war-torn context, the imperative of providing adequate hospital care is acute. As reflected in other studies, there is a need for a more effective coordination between humanitarian health actors, development actors and the military. The constraints of the prevailing humanitarian health funding mechanisms in Mali have been a major barrier to the timely delivery of essential health services to populations in need in Mopti region [15]. Donors should allow for more flexibility in their funding and allow for long-term financing [3]. The coordination of humanitarian support is crucial as well as the mobilization of the international community. The support to hospital care and infrastructure, particularly first referral hospitals, should be put at the international agenda to foster coordinated actions [32]. In recent conflicts globally, hospitals have too frequently been considered as war targets. According to international humanitarian law, health establishments and units, including hospitals, should not be attacked. Our study shows that this protection should be extended to the wounded and sick as well as to medical staff and means of transport. The protection and strengthening of health infrastructure should be a priority for an UN international initiative or special mechanism in the coming years.

Limitations

We acknowledge various limitations to this study. Due to the security context, we were not able to do on-site field research observations; hence, we only captured the perspectives of health professionals, public health officials, and international partners without comparing these against observations on the ground. We could assess neither the quality of care nor the direct impact on health care of the war context.

Conclusion

Mopti hospital is struggling daily to provide services in a war context. However, health professionals, government officials, and partners tried to strengthen the hospital’s capacities and translate their aspirations for improvement. There are three essential dimensions that are key to hospital infrastructure strengthening in a war context: human resources, governance, and equipment/maintenance and two interconnected approaches—developmental and pragmatic—to enhance hospital infrastructure. Considering the hospital as an infrastructure demonstrates the importance of providing basic human and material resources to ensure health care provision on a daily basis i.e., to support its continuing operations. Greater coordination among development sectors and humanitarian actors and exceptional and efficient mobilization of financial and human resources have the potential to improve access to specialized care in a conflict-ridden region. Only at the level of international actors such as the United Nations and international law and humanitarian bodies could this level of protection and multisectoral support for health infrastructure during conflict be achieved.
