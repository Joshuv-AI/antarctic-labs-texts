Respiratory microbiome dysbiosis in STAT3 hyper-IgE syndrome (Job syndrome)
Authors: Cristian Roca, Matthew A Greenwald, Bryan T Zorn, Ling Sun, Gang Chen, Alexandra F Freeman, Richard C Boucher, Kenneth N Olivier, Matthew C Wolfgang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSTAT3 hyper-IgE syndrome (STAT3-HIES), historically known as Job syndrome, is a rare primary immunodeficiency caused by dominant-negative mutations in STAT3. STAT3-HIES is characterized by impaired mucosal immunity, connective tissue abnormalities, and recurrent cutaneous and pulmonary infections. Although STAT3-dependent immune and epithelial pathways are central to airway host defense, the respiratory microbiome in STAT3-HIES remains poorly defined. To address this question, we profiled upper and lower respiratory tract microbiomes using 16S rDNA gene sequencing of oral rinse samples from eight STAT3-HIES participants and eight matched healthy controls, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples from eight STAT3-HIES participants and six healthy controls that passed sequencing quality thresholds. Oral rinse samples from STAT3-HIES participants showed lower phylogenetic diversity and significantly altered microbial community structure compared with controls. In BALF, α-diversity was comparable between groups, but β-diversity differed significantly, indicating altered lower airway microbial community composition in STAT3-HIES. Differential abundance analysis identified enrichment of the genus Rothia in STAT3-HIES BALF, and Rothia relative abundance positively correlated with BALF IL-1β, IL-1RA, and IL-8. Although limited by cohort size, these findings provide the first characterization of respiratory microbiome alterations in STAT3-HIES and suggest that impaired STAT3-dependent immune pathways may influence airway microbial ecology and inflammatory responses.Keywords: STAT3, Hyper-IgE syndrome, Job syndrome, Microbiome, Rothia, Airway

Introduction

Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) is a multifunctional transcription factor that integrates cytokine and growth factor signaling through the canonical Janus kinase (JAK)-STAT pathway [1–3]. STAT3 regulates diverse biological processes including cell proliferation, differentiation, immune signaling, and tissue development [4]. Proper STAT3 signaling is critical for host defense against bacterial and fungal pathogens at mucosal surfaces [5]. Dysfunctional STAT3 signaling has been implicated in numerous inflammatory diseases and immunodeficiencies [6].

Dominant-negative mutations in STAT3 cause STAT3 hyper-IgE syndrome (STAT3-HIES), a rare primary immunodeficiency characterized by markedly elevated serum IgE levels (> 2000 IU/mL), eczema, recurrent infections, and connective tissue abnormalities [7–11]. A defining immunologic feature of STAT3-HIES is impaired differentiation of Th17 lymphocytes, which is essential for epithelial barrier immunity [12–15]. Consequently, individuals with STAT3-HIES are particularly susceptible to infections caused by pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and Candida species.

Pulmonary disease is common in STAT3-HIES, with approximately 90% of affected individuals experiencing recurrent pneumonia [10, 16]. Repeated infections often lead to structural lung damage including bronchiectasis and pneumatoceles. Recent work has also demonstrated that STAT3 mutations compromise airway epithelial innate defenses beyond the well-described immune-cell defects [17]. Sun et al. show that STAT3 dysfunction reduces CFTR mRNA and protein expression, impairs CFTR-dependent fluid and mucin secretion, acidifies airway surface liquid, and diminishes antimicrobial peptide and cytokine/chemokine expression, collectively leading to defective mucociliary clearance and impaired bactericidal activity [17]. Critically, their clinical sputum analyses reveal that STAT3-HIES airways resemble pathophysiologic features observed in other muco-obstructive airway diseases such as cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis, with STAT3-HIES sputum showing increased mucus concentration and heightened viscoelasticity, consistent with thick, tenacious secretions that are difficult to clear [17]. In muco-obstructive airway diseases, airway microbial communities play a central role in disease progression [18–20].

STAT3-HIES structural abnormalities, combined with impaired immune signaling, may alter the airway microbial environment and promote persistent colonization by opportunistic microbes such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, nontuberculous mycobacteria, and Aspergillus species [21, 22]. Despite these clinical observations, the respiratory microbiome of STAT3-HIES has not been systematically characterized, and to our knowledge no prior studies have directly examined microbial community structure in the lower airways of STAT3-HIES individuals.

Since prior microbiome studies in STAT3-HIES have focused largely on the skin and oral mucosa, little is known about microbial community dynamics in the respiratory tract [23–26]. Here we performed microbiome analysis using samples from a previously published cohort [17] to characterize oral and lower airway microbiomes in STAT3-HIES. Given the broad role of STAT3 in epithelial and immune regulation, we hypothesized that STAT3-HIES would be associated with distinct microbial community structures in both the upper and lower respiratory tract.

Methods

Sample population and collection

This report utilizes specimens collected and archived from a previously published study cohort [17]. Respiratory tract specimens were collected with informed consent under IRB protocol #07-H-0142 at the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (Bethesda, MD). Healthy volunteers needed to be between 18 and 75 years old in good general health without clinically significant medical history, physical exam findings or screening lab and chest x-ray abnormalities with ability to comply with requirements for conscious sedation and willingness to donate specimens for storage and future research. Individuals with a smoking history of 10 pack-years or more, current smokers, or tobacco free for less than a year were excluded. Upper respiratory oral rinse (OR) and lower respiratory bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples were collected from eight STAT3-HIES participants and eight age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Subjects were not to eat or drink anything for six hours prior and two hours after the procedure. The bronchopulmonary segment for BALF collection was selected according to predefined criteria, with preference given to the middle lobe or lingula. BALF was collected by instilling 50 mL of sterile saline into the bronchopulmonary segment via bronchoscope, followed by gentle suction aspiration. Oral rinse samples were collected by instructing participants to gargle sterile saline for 30 s before expelling the rinse into sterile collection tubes. Samples were immediately frozen at − 80°C until analysis.

Microbiome sequencing

DNA was extracted from frozen oral rinse and BALF samples using the DNeasy PowerSoil kit (Qiagen). Two samples (negative controls) with molecular grade water underwent DNA extraction and sequencing at the same time as the study samples using the same techniques. Negative controls were used to decontaminate the study samples using the prevalence method of decontamination [27]. Bacterial DNA was amplified using the Quick-16S library preparation kit (Zymo Research), targeting the V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene. Sequencing the V4 region alone has been shown to more closely resemble full-length 16S taxonomic assignment compared to V3-V4, in a phenotypically similar rare lung disease [28]. Intermediate region between V3-V4 regions may be highly variable and drive Phred scores (quality scores) lower than V4 alone. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq2000 platform with paired-end reads. Sequencing quality was assessed using FastQC prior to downstream analysis. Two samples (two healthy control BALF samples) were excluded due to low-quality scores.

Bioinformatic processing and statistical analysis

Sequence reads were mapped to the Human Oral Microbiome Database (HOMD) [29] and processed using the DADA2-QIIME2 pipeline to generate amplicon sequence variant (ASV) tables. Taxonomic assignments were integrated with sample metadata using the Phyloseq package in R (v4.4.1). Rarefaction was performed at 5,000 reads. α-diversity was assessed using Shannon and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity metrics. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. β-diversity was evaluated using UniFrac distance and PERMANOVA. Microbiome differential abundance analysis was performed using Analysis of Composition of Microbiome with Bias Correction (ANCOM-BC2) [30], to compare taxa across groups while correcting for both sample-specific and taxon-specific bias.

Cytokine and chemokine measurements

Cytokine and chemokine measurements were obtained from the original Sun et al. dataset [17]. BALF cytokines were quantified using a U-PLEX multiplex immunoassay (Meso Scale Diagnostics). In brief, 96-well plates were coated with linker-coupled capture antibodies for one hour, followed by three washes with PBS with 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST). 25µL of standards and samples were added and incubated for one hour at room temperature with shaking. Plates were then washed three times, incubated for one hour with detection antibodies then followed by another three washes. After adding 150µL of reading buffer per well, plates were immediately read on a MESO QUICKPLEX SQ 120 instrument using an electrochemiluminescent detection method. DISCOVERY WORKBENCH software (Meso Scale Diagnostics) was used for sample analysis. Standard curves were generated using a weighted four-parameter logistic model fit. Correlations between microbial taxa and cytokine levels were evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis in GraphPad Prism.

Results

Study cohort

Upper respiratory oral rinse and lower respiratory BALF samples were obtained from eight STAT3-HIES participants and eight matched healthy controls. All STAT3-HIES participants were clinically stable at the time of sampling, although several exhibited chronic pulmonary complications including bronchiectasis and pneumatoceles (Table 1). All STAT3-HIES participants were maintained on their chronic antimicrobial regimens. Because discontinuing therapy solely for research purposes was not ethically permissible given the risk of pulmonary deterioration, antimicrobial exposure represents an important study limitation and potential confounding variable.

Upper airway microbiome

Analysis of oral rinse samples revealed a trend toward reduced bacterial α-diversity in STAT3-HIES participants compared with healthy controls, as measured by both Shannon diversity (richness and evenness) and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (total branch length represented). (Fig. 1A-B). This suggests that STAT3-HIES oral communities are not only less taxonomically diverse but also occupy a narrower span of the bacterial phylogenetic tree. β-diversity analysis demonstrated significant separation between groups (Fig. 1C; PERMANOVA P = 0.01), indicating shifts in overall community composition between groups based on between-sample dissimilarity metrics. Despite these community-level differences, substantial inter-individual variability limited detection of statistically significant genus-level differences (q > 0.1) (Fig. 1D-E). The most notable shift was a non-significant increase in the relative abundance of the genus Gemella in STAT3-HIES samples (Fig. 1F).

Lower airway microbiome

α-diversity of BALF samples was comparable between STAT3-HIES participants and controls (Fig. 2A-B). However, β-diversity analysis revealed significant differences in microbial community composition between groups (Fig. 2C; PERMANOVA P = 0.02). This result implies that STAT3-HIES and controls harbor a similar number of taxa and comparable within-sample richness/evenness (Fig. 2A-B), but composition of those communities differs between groups (i.e., relative abundance of taxa present) (Fig. 2C). BALF samples contained low levels of opportunistic respiratory pathogens including Haemophilus, Pseudomonas, Stenotrophomonas, and Staphylococcus, which were not detected in corresponding oral rinse samples (Figs. 1D-E and 2D-E). Compared to healthy controls, STAT3-HIES participants showed lower relative abundance of several putative respiratory commensals including Prevotella, Fusobacterium, and Saccharibacteria TM7 G1 and a significant enrichment of the genus Rothia (Fig. 2F).

Association between Rothia and airway inflammation

Correlation analysis demonstrated that relative abundance of Rothia positively correlated with BALF levels of IL-1β, IL-8, and IL-1RA across the combined cohort (Fig. 2G–I). However, Rothia abundance did not correlate with clinical disease severity, including ppFEV1 and the presence of bronchiectasis or pneumatoceles (Table 1).

Discussion

This study provides an initial characterization of respiratory microbiome alterations in individuals with STAT3-HIES. Despite the small cohort size, we identified differences in both microbial diversity and community composition associated with STAT3-HIES. The most notable finding was enrichment of Rothia in the lower airways of STAT3-HIES participants, which correlated with inflammatory cytokine levels.

The oral microbiome of STAT3-HIES individuals exhibited reduced phylogenetic diversity and significantly divergent community structure (β-diversity) compared to healthy controls. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating immune dysregulation in the oral mucosa and oral microbial dysbiosis in STAT3-HIES [31]. Oral dysbiosis is a hallmark for the development of dental caries [31, 32] and may explain the increased incidence of caries in STAT3-HIES compared to the general population [33].

In contrast, the lower airway microbiome showed preserved overall diversity but distinct taxonomic composition. A major contributor to this divergence was the enrichment of Rothia in BALF samples. Interestingly, this enrichment was not observed in matched oral rinse samples, suggesting that expansion of Rothia may reflect local immune and epithelial conditions associated with STAT3-HIES rather than simple upper airway contamination.

One potential explanation for these microbial shifts is the central role of STAT3 in Th17-mediated mucosal immunity. STAT3 signaling is required for differentiation of Th17 cells affecting the production and signaling of IL-17 and IL-22, cytokines that regulate epithelial antimicrobial peptide expression, neutrophil recruitment, and mucosal barrier integrity [13–15, 34]. Impaired Th17 responses in STAT3-HIES may therefore alter the selective pressures that normally shape airway microbial communities, allowing expansion of organisms that are otherwise minor members of the respiratory microbiome.

Rothia species are Gram-positive facultative anaerobic, encapsulated bacteria, commonly found in the oral cavity and respiratory tract [35, 36]. In our cohort, the relatively abundance of Rothia in the lower airways of STAT3-HIES correlated positively with IL-1β, IL-8, and IL-1RA in BALF. Moreover, when these cytokine and chemokines were analyzed by disease, only IL-1β reached statistical significance between groups, with IL-1β elevated in STAT3-HIES [17]. These cytokines are key mediators of innate immune responses and neutrophil recruitment, suggesting that Rothia may contribute to or be shaped by local inflammatory signaling in the lungs.

Rothia species are emerging as influential members of the lung microbiome in individuals with chronic muco-obstructive diseases and have been reported to exert both anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory effects, depending on host context and immune status [37, 38]. In addition to immune signaling defects, dominant-negative STAT3 mutations also disrupt epithelial host defense pathways that maintain airway microbial homeostasis. Impaired STAT3-dependent signaling in STAT3-HIES has been shown to alter mucociliary clearance and airway surface liquid regulation through defects in ciliogenesis, reduced CFTR expression, and altered mucin production [17]. These epithelial abnormalities may further influence microbial community structure by altering airway surface liquid composition and mucociliary transport, creating ecological niches that favor colonization by specific bacterial taxa [17]. However, mechanisms by which Rothia contributes to or responds to disease pathology remain enigmatic. Rothia mucilaginosa and Rothia aeria have been linked to pneumonia in both immunocompromised individuals and immunocompetent individuals with chronic lung diseases or structural airway abnormalities like bronchiectasis [39, 40]. However, other studies suggest Rothia can attenuate inflammatory signaling, in part by dampening activation of the NF-κB pathway [37, 41, 42]. Ultimately, these reports highlight the need to delineate species- and strain-specific functions of Rothia within the lung microbiome, particularly in the context of disease specific immune dysregulation.

This study has several limitations, including potential for oral contamination during bronchoscopy, sequencing only the 16S V4 region, confounding antibiotic exposure in the STAT3-HIES group, and most notably a small cohort size (n = 6–8). The pronounced interindividual variability further limited the ability to draw robust conclusions. Given the small sample size and subject variability, the overall outcomes drawn may be sensitive to minor changes in the dataset. However, because Rothia enrichment was observed in BALF, but not in matched oral rinse samples, this finding may reflect microbial community differences within the lower airways rather than simple carryover from the upper airway. Future studies using metagenomic sequencing and larger multicenter cohorts, with additional disease control groups, such as individuals with cystic fibrosis or primary immunodeficiencies, who also receive chronic antibiotic therapy and could help disentangle antibiotic-specific effects, will be required to define species-level microbial signatures and determine whether airway microbiome alterations contribute to pulmonary disease progression in STAT3-HIES.
