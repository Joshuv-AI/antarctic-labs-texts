Structural and functional role of methylene linkers in self-coacervation-mediated membrane interaction and antiviral defense
Authors: Nikesh Dewangan, Indrani Das Jana, Priyaraj Pal, Arindam Mondal, Pradip Kumar Tarafdar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesigning small-molecule-based (MW ≤ 1500) self-coacervates and tuning their dynamic properties holds great significance in the regulation of biological events. Herein, we designed a series of pKa tunable polyionic lipidated flavonoids (LFs), where the aromatic core of flavonoid and the acyl chains serve as stickers, the hydrophilic polyanionic moieties interact via multivalent COO‾…HOOC hydrogen bond formation, leading to coacervation at low concentrations (10 µM). Inspired by nature’s selection of amino acids (glutamic acid vs 2-amino hexane dioic acid) with the profound role of methylene units, we screened a library of LFs to explore the influence of methylene linker length (n = 0-3). FRAP studies suggest that shorter linkers (n = 0, 1) produce gel-like coacervates, whereas longer linkers (n = 2, 3) yield more fluidic droplets. The self-coacervates exhibited strong membrane association, resulting in the formation of 2D domains and/or nano-coacervate-like domains within the lipid bilayer. The domains MFEA (n = 2) are membrane-active, inhibit membrane fusion, and protect the cells from SARS-CoV-2 and influenza infections, highlighting the potential as a broad-spectrum antiviral. Our design of amphiphilic self-coacervates offers a versatile new platform for engineering functional materials and highlights the role of methylene linker length for tuning coacervate mechanics. Subject terms: Self-assembly, Biophysical chemistry

Introduction

Compartmentalisation and spatial organisation of biomolecules become a central focus in systems biology and systems chemistry1. Researchers have developed a variety of synthetic compartments, including liposomes, proteinosomes, polymersomes, and coacervates, to understand how compartmentalisation influences biological function. These model systems provide valuable insights into how physical boundaries influence molecular interactions and reaction dynamics, thereby guiding the design of functional, cell-like structures2–4. The artificial cell-like compartments, often known as protocells, provide a platform for investigating programmable long-range communication5, regulated enzymatic activity, morphological differentiation6 etc. In recent years, coacervates have emerged as model protocells due to their selective encapsulation and dynamic behaviour4,7. Scientists have reported coacervates from synthetic molecules, polyelectrolytes, peptides, and nucleic acid-based polymers, to engineer such artificial compartments that mimic certain features of biological cells and protocells8–11. The coacervate droplets exhibit features such as a crowded molecular interior and the spontaneous sequestration of diverse molecules, making them effective microenvironments for enhancing biochemical processes12, facilitating biomolecular synthesis13–17, and the construction of functional living materials18. Broadly, coacervation can be classified into two categories: simple and complex, each driven by distinct molecular interactions4,19. Simple coacervation or self-coacervation typically involves a single type of small molecules or macromolecules, undergoing a liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS)19. This process is governed by the principles that drive protein aggregation, including electrostatic attractions, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals forces. Interestingly, the formation of amyloid aggregates, primarily driven by hydrophobic interactions, can be viewed as an irreversible endpoint of simple coacervation20. In contrast, the formation of complex coacervates is primarily driven by electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged macromolecules, particularly polyelectrolytes21. Recent research has highlighted a growing body of work focused on complex coacervation; however, significantly fewer studies have explored self-coacervation, especially in the context of small molecules19,22–25. Most of the existing literature on self-coacervation is centred around polymers or peptides. For example, Chowdhury et al. reported self-coacervation using a pyroglutamic acid-based homopolymer22, Chen et al. demonstrated similar behaviour in soy glycinin, a plant-derived protein23. Additionally, the Spruijt group has also shown that short peptides can undergo liquid-liquid phase separation26, although the concentrations required are relatively high, typically in the millimolar range. Despite these advances, achieving self-assembly with low-molecular-weight compounds (MW < 2500) that form well-defined coacervate compartments remains a significant challenge19,27. Various strategies, such as the delicate balancing of the electrostatic repulsion27, pi-pi stacking17,26, Hofmeister effect28, stochastic kissing of membranes29 have been employed to design self-coacervates. Despite this, LLPS formation with small molecules at low micromolar concentrations, which exhibits tunable dynamic properties, requires a major breakthrough in the field. In this study, we report that a pKa tunable polyionic lipidated-flavonoids (LFs), a small molecule, undergoes self-coacervation at remarkably low concentrations (~10 μM). Inspired by nature’s selection of amino acids (ornithine vs lysine; glutamic acid vs 2-amino hexane dioic acid) with the profound role of methylene linker lengths30–32, we explored the influence of methylene linker length (n = 0–3) of anionic natural and unnatural amino acids on self-coacervation (Fig. 1A). Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP) experiments revealed that LFs with longer linkers (n = 2 and 3; MFEA and MFHA) form more fluid, liquid-like coacervates. In contrast, shorter linkers (n = 0 and 1; MFMA and MFDA) yielded gel-like coacervates. Notably, the coacervates exhibited strong membrane association, and resulting in the formation of 2D domains and nano-coacervate-like structures in the lipid bilayer (Fig. 1B). The membrane domains of LFs (particularly MFEA with n = 2) inhibited the membrane fusion by modulating the membrane physical properties. Since membrane fusion is an integral process of viral infection and virus-membrane interaction33, the inhibition of fusion by LFs was utilised to find a potent broad-spectrum fusion inhibitor34. We showed that MFEA coacervates effectively, blocking the infection from two different viruses, SARS-CoV-2 and Influenza, citing its broad-spectrum antiviral potential.

Results and discussion

Design of self-coacervation of lipid-based flavonoid derivatives

The design of small molecule-based self-coacervate formation is tricky and, therefore, is seldom reported19,35. The sticker-spacer-sticker model was proposed to design the self-coacervates of peptides and other small molecules. This model, along with other designs, relies on the delicate manipulation of electrostatic forces and π − π stacking between monomeric building blocks to induce LLPS. However, a strong electrostatic interaction and strong π−π stacking may often lead to thermodynamically stable aggregate formation. We took the polyphenolic moiety (flavonoid) as the building block due to its self-assembly properties36. In a previous report, we demonstrated that the myristoylated flavonoid (chrysoeriol), when anchored into the membrane bilayer, forms a domain via self-assembly37. Since the molecule is a ‘Lipidated-Y-shaped’, capable of π−π stacking, and has four carboxylic acid moieties, we believe that pKa perturbation and multivalent COO‾…HOOC hydrogen bonding might lead to interesting self-assembly properties. While the π−π stacking, hydrophobic interactions, and COO‾…HOOC hydrogen bonding is associative and aggregation-prone; the fine balance of electrostatic repulsion between the free carboxylates might lead to metastable LLPS formation.

We conjugated the lipidated flavonoid core with natural and unnatural anionic acids having different methylene units (Fig. 1A). The synthesised compounds, myristic-flavonoid di-malonic acid (MFMA; no methylene unit between the carboxylic acids, n = 0), myristic-flavonoid aspartic acid (MFDA, n = 1), myristic-flavonoid glutamic acid (MFEA, n = 2), and myristic-flavonoid di-hexane dioic acid (MFHA, n = 3), were characterised by NMR and HRMS (Figs. S1–15). Using fluorescence properties of the flavonoid core, we found that the lipidated flavonoids conjugated with terminal carboxylic acids (LFs) exhibit a broad fluorescence peak with a tail at higher wavelengths (Fig. 2A–D). Deconvolution of the peak suggests the presence of additional peaks (450, 500 nm). The tail at higher wavelengths and the red-shifted peak (~500 nm) might suggest aggregation of LFs in pH 7.4 buffer (Fig. 2A–D). The Nile Red (NR) fluorescence experiments were subsequently performed to understand the microenvironment of the aggregates. NR is a solvatochromic dye, and we found that the NR emission maximum decreased (blue-shifted) with increasing concentrations of LFs. We plotted the emission maxima of NR (in the presence of increasing concentrations of LFs) to determine the critical aggregation concentration (CAC) (Fig. 2E–H)38. The estimated CAC of LFs was in the range of 5–10 µM. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) studies (performed at 25 µM) with LFs revealed the presence of polydisperse large nanostructures ranging from 0.5 to 1.5 µm (Fig. 2I–L).

Next, we investigated the encapsulation properties of LF aggregates using confocal microscopy with two model dyes: Rhodamine B (hydrophilic) and Coumarin7 (hydrophobic). Both fluorophores were localised in the spherical compartments of MFMA, MFDA, MFEA, and MFHA (Fig. 3A–L), demonstrating that the LF-based assemblies can encapsulate guests across a wide polarity range. The encapsulation of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic fluorophores suggests that the LFs might form coacervate-like compartments (Fig. 3C, F, I, L)26. The DIC images also support the formation of coacervate-like structures (Fig. S16). The concentration-dependent studies on self-coacervation revealed that MFMA with no methylene linker does not form coacervate at 10 μM concentration, whereas the LFs with different methylene linker lengths, MFDA (n = 1), MFEA (n = 2), and MFHA (n = 3), formed coacervates at concentrations as low as 10 μM (Fig. S17). This highlights the role of the methylene linker length in the formation of coacervates. To further investigate the dynamics of self-coacervates, we conducted FRAP experiments using sulforhodamine B. The swift fluorescence recovery for MFEA (~100% in 80 s) and for MFHA (~80% within 80 s) confirmed that the structures are coacervate droplets formed through liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) (Fig. 3M–P)27. In the case of MFMA and MFDA, the recovery was (~60% and ~50% in 80 s), suggesting a more gel-like behaviour. The FRAP recovery halftime (t1/2) MFEA and MFHA were lower than the t1/2 of MFMA and MFDA (Fig. 3M–P). These results reveal that the methylene linker lengths can influence the behaviour and dynamic properties of the self-coacervates.

Encapsulation of MFEA and Cryo-TEM Structure

Our FRAP data suggest that MFEA coacervates (n = 2) exhibit more dynamic and fluid-like compartments compared to others. MFEA droplets also exhibited coalescence, further emphasising the fluidic nature of the compartment (Fig. S18). The MFEA coacervates efficiently sequestered a range of hydrophobic dyes with different structures, such as coumarin 343, thiazole orange, and Nile Red (Fig. 4A–C). In parallel, the coacervates also encapsulated several hydrophilic dyes, including sulforhodamine B and calcein, indicating a balanced internal microenvironment that sequesters molecules with a wide polarity range (Fig. 4D, E). Notably, the system also demonstrated the ability to encapsulate bioactive compounds, such as the anticancer drug doxorubicin, further underscoring its potential as a multifunctional delivery platform (Fig. 4F). These results highlight the robust and efficient encapsulation capacity of MFEA coacervates, suggesting promising applicability in drug delivery and compartmentalisation. Cryo-TEM studies revealed that the MFEA coacervates were spherical. The magnified image suggests a possible porous network (Fig. 4G, H).

Interaction of coacervate with membrane

The interaction between coacervates and biological membranes is fascinating and leads to wetting39, the formation of nanotubular structures40, membrane ruffling41, scission of endosome membranes42, membrane penetration43, endocytosis44,45. However, the membrane interaction of self-coacervates from small molecules is yet an underexplored area of research. To explore the potential interaction between LF-based coacervates and the membrane, LFs were added to giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) at 1:25 ratio, and confocal microscopic experiments (405 nm, flavonoid moiety) were performed. In the case of MFMA, we found that the molecules were partitioned uniformly, suggesting the absence of any macroscopic domains (Fig. 5A). On the other hand, MFDA, MFEA, and MFHA were not uniformly distributed in the membrane and formed necklace-like domain structures (Fig. 5B–D). In our earlier report, we found a similar domain-like structure with MFDA37. These results suggest that the methylene linker length might play an important role in domain structure in the membrane.

Next, we incubated LFs in the membrane at a 1:25 ratio and conducted atomic force microscopic (AFM) studies to assess domain formation. The results revealed clear differences among the compounds: while MFMA did not induce any distinct domain formation in the membrane (height of the bilayer ~4 nm), MFDA, MFEA, and MFHA formed well-defined domains in the membrane (Fig. 5E–H). We examined the height profiles, assuming a bilayer thickness of ~ 4 nm, and found that the domain heights of MFDA and MFHA are between 6 and 8 nm. Interestingly, the MFEA domain height was significantly larger, ranging from 12 to 14 nm (Fig. 5I–L). This observation prompted us to question the nature of these larger nanodomains with MFEA, specifically, whether they represent a distinct type of domain at the membrane interface (Fig. 5K). We anticipated that the large domains of MFEA (with two methylene units, n = 2) might consist of 2D domains and perhaps membrane-bound nano-coacervates, suggesting a unique interaction with the membrane. To investigate this, confocal imaging was performed with MFEA in GUVs (1: 25 ratio) using 405 nm, 488 nm, and 552 nm laser channels. The GUVs were labelled with Rhodamine-PE (red channel) to visualise the lipid bilayer, and calcein (green channel) was used as a hydrophilic dye for the coacervate marker. Interestingly, the green fluorescence signal from hydrophilic calcein co-localized with the rhodamine-PE of the membrane, suggesting the possible presence of nano-coacervate-like domains in the membrane (Fig. 5M–P). Control vesicles with no MFEA didn’t show any calcein incorporation (Fig. S21). These findings indicate the formation of membrane-bound coacervate-like domains in the lipid bilayer, which we termed membrane-bound nano-coacervate-like domains (MCD). Since the LF-based self-coacervates contain a hydrophobic lipid tail, their interaction with the membrane is likely to be unique and differs from the conventional coacervate-membrane interaction. Coacervates generally wet the membrane and exhibit endocytosis44,45. Given the lipidic nature of the LFs, they may exhibit additional partitioning properties to the bilayer, which complicates the membrane interaction equilibrium. Therefore, we propose that the LFs may wet the membrane and partition into the bilayer, resulting in the formation of a 2D domain. Additionally, the lipidic tail may also inhibit the endocytosis of coacervates, and thus a membrane-bound nano-coacervate state may be formed under appropriate conditions. It is possible that the membrane-bound nanocoacervate state (MCD) is a metastable state, and further studies are required to understand its phase behaviour. The interaction of self-coacervates of amphiphilic molecules and membrane opens a new field in membrane biology.

Membrane physical properties and implications on fusion

The unique ability of MFEA to interact at the membrane prompts us to investigate how it alters the membrane microenvironment. Small unilamellar vesicles (DOPC/DOPE/DOPS/Chol (44:30:6:20)) were added gradually into 2 µM MFEA, and the fluorescence spectra were recorded by exciting the flavonoid core at 340 nm. The emission was blue-shifted, and the intensity was increased by the addition of membrane (vesicles) (Figs. 6A and S22). This suggests that MFEA interacts with the membrane and partitions into the hydrophobic region (blue shift). To assess the membrane microenvironment, we performed Nile Red (NR) experiments. NR was incorporated into the membrane, and the fluorescence emission was observed at 635 nm. Upon addition of MFEA (1: 100 MFEA: lipid), the NR emission intensity decreased and red-shifted (Fig. 6B). This suggests that NR partitions into more hydrophilic domains in the membrane. Next, we measured the change in the zeta potential of the membrane in the presence of MFEA (n = 2) and MFMA (n = 0). Both LFs (MFMA and MFEA) carry four carboxylic acids, yet they showed different impacts on zeta potential. MFEA reduced the zeta potential significantly, whereas MFMA reduced the zeta potential marginally (Fig. 6C). To gain deeper insight into the surface potential modulation of MFEA in the presence of membranes, we performed a bromothymol blue (BTB) assay at pH 7.4 using 10 mM TES buffer to determine the interfacial pH46. The absorbance at 414 nm and 619 nm revealed that the I414/I619 of MFEA (in membrane) was much higher than that of MFMA (in membrane) (Fig. 6D). We propose that the interfacial pH is more acidic in MFEA than in MFMA. The results indicate that the apparent pKa of MFEA in the membrane is lower than that of MFMA in the membrane; therefore, MFEA exists in a more dissociated (anionic) form, leading to a greater negative surface potential (Fig. 6C). We propose that the extra methylene groups in MFEA (n = 2) decrease the charge-charge repulsion between the carboxylate groups to reduce the apparent pKa (Fig. 6E)30–32,47. However, the I414/I619 value of MFEA was greater than one, suggesting that interfacial pH was below 7.0. We propose that at this pH, not all the carboxylic acid groups (a total of four) will be in dissociated form and might exist in partially deprotonated form. Next, the bulk pH of the membrane-bound MFEA was measured at different NaOH concentrations. We could not determine the pKas accurately, but the pKa4 is likely to be in the range of 9.5 (Fig. S23). This also supports that at a pH of 7.4, the MFEA in the membrane exists in a partially deprotonated species. The partially deprotonated -COO― can form an H-bond with the -CO2H (undissociated) group of adjacent molecules to form a network of H-bonding48. These interactions might lead to supramolecular interactions to form a domain. To check the role of H-bonding, the MFEA coacervates were incubated in 500 mM urea and guanidinium hydrochloride. The presence of urea and guanidinium hydrochloride destroyed the coacervate’s structure, supporting the role of H-bonding interactions in the coacervates (Figs. S19 and 20).

Next, using steady-state anisotropy measurements with the TMA-DPH probe, we evaluated the changes in membrane interfacial order upon incorporating MFEA and MFMA. We found that MFEA increased the TMADPH anisotropy in a concentration-dependent manner, suggesting that the MFEA domain increased the membrane order48,49. Earlier, it was reported that the macromolecular coacervates can enhance the lipid packing and membrane stability45,50,51. MFMA also increased the membrane order; however, the impact is much lower compared to MFEA (Fig. 6F). We synthesised MEEA with no flavonoid moiety (Figs. S8 and S24) and investigated how it affects the TMA-DPH anisotropy. We found that MEEA with no flavonoid did not change the membrane order. This suggests an important role of the flavonoid moiety in increasing the membrane order (Fig. 6F). Since MFEA and MFMA increased the membrane order and reduced the zeta potential, it is expected that the lipidated flavonoids (LFs) might inhibit the membrane fusion, a key biological process during enveloped virus infection33,52,53. We prepared two types of vesicles: one “unlabelled” vesicle composed of DOPC/DOPE/DOPS/Chol (44:30:6:20) and one “labelled” vesicle consisting of DOPC/DOPE/Chol/DOPS/NBD-PE/Rh-PE (44:27:20: 6:1.5:1.5). The labelled and unlabelled vesicles were mixed in a 1:4 ratio at 37 °C in a pH 7.4 buffer. Membrane fusion was initiated by adding 5% (w/v) polyethylene glycol. As fusion proceeds, fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) efficiency decreases due to the dilution of the labelled dyes between the fused and unfused vesicles. The control vesicles with no LFs, demonstrating a fusion efficiency of ~21% (Fig. 6G). We incorporated MFMA (with a methylene linker of n = 0) into the vesicles at a 1:100 LF-to-lipid ratio and found that the compound reduced membrane fusion to ~12% (Fig. 6G), highlighting the potential of LFs in fusion inhibition. Our earlier report with MFDA (methylene linker length n = 1) reduced the fusion to 7%37. Next, the role of fusion inhibition of MFEA with two methylene units and MFHA with three methylene units was tested. MFEA reduced the membrane fusion to ~ 3%, and MFHA reduced the fusion to ~4%. We plotted the percentage of fusion inhibition against the number of methylene linkers and found that the percentage of inhibition nearly saturated at n = 2 (MFEA) (Fig. S25). These results indicate that the methylene linker length at the terminal end might be important in membrane fusion inhibition, and MFEA with two methylene units is a potent inhibitor. To check the role of flavonoid moiety towards fusion inhibition, we incorporated MEEA into the membrane (Fig. 6H). MEEA (with no flavonoid moiety) showed almost no inhibition, clearly indicating that the flavonoid structure is essential for membrane fusion inhibition. (Fig. 6H). Next, we investigated the role of the free carboxylic acid group at the terminal end. We modified the lead molecule (MFEA) by blocking all free acid groups into methyl ester, resulting in the derivative MFEM (Figs. S4 and S24). In contrast, this modification slightly increased the membrane fusion process, emphasising the critical role of the terminal free acid group in fusion inhibition (Fig. 6H). The fusion inhibition experiments also suggest the notion that the -CO2H and -OOC H-bonding might have a significant role in fusion inhibition. We note that MFEA, which forms membrane-bound nano-coacervate-like domains (MCD), exhibits excellent fusion-inhibitory properties. Since the domain formation might be more favourable at a higher MFEA: lipid ratio, we investigated the role of MFEA in concentration-dependent fusion experiments (Fig. 6I). The rate and extent of membrane fusion decreased with increasing concentrations of MFEA in the membrane. Remarkably, at an LF-to-lipid ratio of 1:50, the membrane fusion was completely inhibited (Fig. S26).

Screening of antiviral activity of MFEA against enveloped viruses

Membrane biophysical studies indicate that MFEA inhibits membrane fusion by altering the membrane’s physical properties, suggesting its potential antiviral activity against enveloped viruses. Prior studies have demonstrated that reporter pseudovirus-based entry assays for SARS-CoV-2, and influenza are well-suited for examining viral entry into susceptible host cells37,54. To explore this, we generated HIV-1-based reporter lentiviruses containing firefly luciferase or ZsGreen reporter genes in their genomic backbone, along with the SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein as the sole surface-expressed protein. This well-standardised system allowed us to study the extent of viral entry into HEK293T cells overexpressing ACE2 surface receptors (HEK293T-ACE2). These reporter pseudoviruses express the luciferase gene as part of their viral gene expression, and the luciferase activity could be monitored as a quantitative readout of virus infection in HEK293T-ACE2 cells. The antiviral activity against pseudoviruses clearly demonstrated that MFEA significantly reduced the entry of SARS-CoV-2 in a dose-dependent manner, achieving an ~80% reduction in viral entry at a concentration of 100 µM, with an IC50 of 39.3 μM (Fig. 7A). However, MEEA, the molecule that does not have a flavonoid skeleton but has four -CO2H groups, did not show any inhibition upon SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus entry into susceptible cells at any given concentration of the molecule (Fig. 7A). We further investigated the antiviral properties of MFEA against another enveloped RNA virus that utilises the membrane fusion process to enter susceptible cells. In this regard, we utilised an infectious reporter influenza A/H1N1/WSN virus as described by Tran et al.55. This is a reporter influenza virus that expresses a nano-luciferase gene as part of its viral gene expression. This luciferase activity could be monitored as a quantitative readout of virus infection in MDCK cells. As evidenced in Fig. 7B, MFEA demonstrated inhibitory effects on influenza A virus infection as well, with an IC50 value of 155.8 µM. Collectively, these findings confirm the broad-spectrum efficacy of MFEA in inhibiting SARS-CoV-2 and influenza A virus infections. Together, these results strongly support the antiviral potential of MFEA coacervates as a broad-spectrum inhibitor against a range of enveloped viruses.

Binding of MFEA to the RBD of the spike protein

The antiviral studies with MFEA demonstrate significantly higher viral inhibition and show some specificity toward SARS-CoV-2 than influenza (IC50-SARS-CoV-2 ~ 39.3 µM; IC50-Influenza ~155.8 µM), suggesting potential interactions between MFEA and the SARS-CoV-2 virus. To explore this further, we conducted an autodocking study between the receptor-binding domain (RBD, PDB ID: 6M0J) of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and the model molecule hexyl-flavonoid diglutamic acid (HFEA), which resembles MFEA. This docking study revealed an interaction between the RBD and HFEA, with a binding energy of approximately −7.0 kcal/mol (Fig. 7D). Next, we measured the fluorescence emission intensity of MFEA at 450 nm in the presence of purified RBD. Upon addition of RBD, we observed an increase in MFEA fluorescence intensity and a blue shift, suggesting that hydrophobic regions of RBD might interact with MFEA (Fig. S27). Next, we determined MFEA anisotropy with increasing RBD concentrations and plotted it in Fig. 7E. The MFEA anisotropy increased with RBD concentrations, suggesting a potential secondary interaction between MFEA and RBD, which is consistent with the autodocking study. Regarding safety, cytotoxicity assays indicated that MFEA and MEEA remain non-toxic on HEK293T cells at concentrations up to 200 µM (Fig. 7C). We tested the haemolytic activity of MFEA to evaluate potential effects on haemolysis. At a concentration of 40 µM, none of these compounds showed significant haemolysis (Fig. S28), supporting their suitability for therapeutic use. We propose that the primary function of MFEA, the broad-spectrum mechanism, arises from its ability to inhibit membrane fusion by altering the membrane’s physical properties. It exhibits potency against various viruses, including SARS-CoV-2 (IC50 ~ 39.3 µM) and influenza (IC50 ~ 155.8 µM). In addition, the secondary interaction of MFEA with SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD provides an additional layer of specificity, and the antiviral potency is higher towards SARS-CoV-2 than that of influenza.

Small molecule-based self-coacervate formation at low micromolar concentrations is challenging; however, it holds a promising platform to address key supramolecular interactions related to biological perspectives19,35,45. Many designs draw inspiration from sticker and spacer models investigated in amyloid-related proteins/peptides, while the amphiphile-based designs involve a 3D network of tubular micellar or other aggregates35. Amphiphiles, like MEL-A56, hyamine 162257, A10B8 (gemini)58, were reported to form coacervates at 2–10 mM. The schematic model of self-coacervation of amphiphilic (lipidic) molecules was proposed. These models include micellar networks or the formation of a sponge phase, having a dense network of bilayers and microscopic aqueous channels59–61. In our design, the aromatic cores of flavonoids and the hydrophobic interaction of acyl chains serve as stickers, bringing monomers together, while the flexible O-CH2C=O groups and hydrophilic polyanionic moieties act as spacers, facilitating the LLPS formation. It appears that the molecules were self-assembled and interconnected via multivalent COO‾…HOOC hydrogen bonding leads to the coacervation (Fig. 1B). The low CCC (~10 µM) as compared to other self-coacervates (2–10 mM)26,27,56–58 might be achieved due to multivalent intra- and intermolecular interactions and repulsive ionic interactions. We measured the zeta potential of the MFEA coacervate at various pHs. The decrease in zeta potential of MFEA from pH 7.4 to pH 9.5 (Fig. S29) suggests that at pH 7.4, not all the carboxylates were deprotonated. Therefore, COO-…HOOC hydrogen bonding interactions are possible at a pH of 7.4. However, strong associative interactions (H-bonds) often lead to aggregation, and a balance of electrostatic repulsion between the carboxylates is crucial for forming the coacervates. Therefore, electrostatic repulsion of anionic headgroups (free carboxylates) also helps to stabilise the coacervates from aggregation. To investigate the role of electrostatic interactions, we examined the stability of coacervates at high concentrations of NaCl. We found that MFMA, MFDA, MFEA, and MFHA did not form coacervates at high salt concentrations, citing the crucial role of electrostatic interactions (Fig. S19). Coacervate stability was also examined in the presence of urea and guanidinium hydrochloride to investigate the role of H-bonding22. Both compounds (urea, guanidinium hydrochloride) disrupt the self-coacervation at 0.5 M concentrations, supporting our proposed model (Figs. 19 and 20). While screening the LFs, we found that the methylene linker length of natural and unnatural anionic amino acids played a crucial role. It is interesting to note that the natural glutamic acid-containing LF (MFEA, n = 2) formed more dynamic and fluidic coacervates. The MFEA coacervates interact with the membrane and induce the formation of 2D domains, membrane-bound nano-coacervate-like structures. These domains are membrane-active and modulate the surface potential, interfacial pH, and the membrane ordering (Fig. 6C–F). At first glance, it appears that the highly dynamic and fluid-like MFEA coacervate (FRAP data) and membrane ordering are contradictory. However, the lipidic nature of the LFs (hydrophobic acyl chain) results in membrane partitioning through their lateral interactions at the membrane surface and induces the formation of 2D domains. The lipidic tail appears to inhibit endocytosis of coacervates44,45, a phenomenon reported in coacervate-membrane interaction. We therefore suggest that the interaction between lipidic coacervate and membrane is unique and differs from that of conventional protein-based, DNA-based, and polymeric coacervates. Once MFEA coacervate interacts with the membrane, the partitioning and subsequent remodelling processes lead to a dynamic equilibrium among the membrane, coacervate wetting, 2D domains, and might result in the formation of a metastable membrane-bound nanocoacervate state. The coacervate-membrane interaction has been reported to enhance lipid packing and membrane stability45,50,51.

The membrane-active nature of MFEA was further explored to investigate its role in membrane fusion and viral infection. An effective fusion inhibitor combats the viral infection, as membrane fusion is a key process for the viral entry into the cells33,62,63. Antiviral compounds that block fusion by passively interfering with membrane physical properties rather than targeting a specific viral protein hold promise in the development of broad-spectrum antivirals that exhibit efficacy against a broad range of viruses48,53,64. Due to antigenic shift and antigenic drift of pathogenic viruses, there is a clear need to develop broad-spectrum antivirals beyond the ‘one-bug-one-drug’ paradigm34. Among the LFs, the superior membrane-active MFEA was found to be the most potent fusion inhibitor (Fig. 6G, I). It appears that MFEA 2D domains and membrane-bound coacervate-like structures ordered the membrane interface to impede the hemifusion, an intermediate step essential for fusion (Fig. 7F). We further propose that membrane-bound nano-coacervates may interfere with the transition from hemifusion to pore formation by creating additional barriers in the fusion kinetics (Fig. 7F). MFEA exhibited prominent antiviral efficacy and protected the cells from SARS-CoV-2 and influenza infections, highlighting its potential as a broad-spectrum antiviral (Fig. 7E, G). Together, the design of self-coacervates at low micromolar concentrations, their membrane-active nature, broad-spectrum antiviral potential, and interaction with viral proteins might serve as a versatile new platform for exploring the therapeutic potential of coacervates.

Conclusion

We report a comprehensive design of flavonoid-based amphiphilic self-coacervates (LFs), tunability of the critical coacervate concentration, dynamic properties, and membrane interaction to form domains. Inspired by the sticker-spacer model investigated in IDPs, we suggest that the aromatic core of flavonoid and the hydrophobic effect of acyl chains serve as stickers, while the methylene-linked hydrophilic polyanionic groups act as spacers, leading to coacervation at low concentrations (10 µM). While exploring the role of the number of methylene units of natural and unnatural anionic amino acids linked to LFs, FRAP analysis suggests that the natural glutamic acid-containing LF (MFEA, n = 2) formed more dynamic and fluidic coacervates than the LFs conjugated with non-natural ones. While macromolecular coacervates were shown to interact with membranes and exhibit endocytosis, the lipid tails of amphiphilic MFEA coacervates and their affinity for membranes result in a distinct dynamic equilibrium among 2D domains, membrane-bound nano-coacervate-like structures. These domains ordered the membrane interface, modulated the surface potential, and interfered with fusion by creating barriers for hemifusion and pore formation. Since an effective membrane-active fusion inhibitor can combat viral infections from enveloped viruses, the broad-spectrum antiviral potential of MFEA coacervates was established against SARS-CoV-2 and influenza infections. The enhanced antiviral efficacy of MFEA coacervates against SARS-CoV-2 was attributed to secondary interactions between spike RBD and MFEA. Our generalised designs on the physicochemical properties of self-coacervates and their membrane interaction might hold great significance in cellular organisation, the regulation of biological events, and the therapeutic development of broad-spectrum antivirals against enveloped viruses.

Methods

Lipid-based flavonoid (LF) synthesis

The following Myristoyl flavonoid compounds have been synthesised: Myr-flavonoid di-malonic acid (MFMA), Myr-flavonoid di-aspartic acid (MFDA), Myr-flavonoid di-glutamic acid (MFEA), Myr-flavonoid di-hexane dioic acid (MFHA), Myr-flavonoid di-glutamate methyl ester (MFEM), and Myr-glutamic di-glutamic acid (MEEA). All the lipid-based flavonoids were synthesised by following the synthetic scheme in Fig. S1. The compounds were purified by column chromatography and characterised by NMR and HRMS (Figs. S2–S15). Detailed synthetic procedures were embedded in the supporting information.

Fluorescence emission spectra of LFs

We prepared 30 µM solutions of LFs (MFMA, MFDA, MFEA, MFHA) in 10 mM TES buffer, pH 7.4, and excited the molecules at 340 nm. The emission was recorded from 360 to 600 nm. The measurements were carried out using a Hitachi F-7000 spectrofluorometer (Japan) equipped with a 1 cm path length quartz cuvette. Both excitation and emission slits were set to a nominal bandpass of 5 nm for consistent detection conditions.

Nile red fluorescence study for compartment formation

Nile Red (NR), a solvatochromic dye, was employed to investigate the critical aggregation concentration and compartment formation. Two micromolar NR was added in 1 mL of 10 mM TES buffer (pH 7.4), and the emission spectrum was recorded from 550 to 700 nm with an excitation wavelength set at 534 nm. Next, different concentrations of LFs (MFMA, MFDA, MFEA, MFHA) were added, equilibrated, and the NR emission spectra were recorded. The fluorescence measurements were conducted using a Hitachi F-7000 spectrofluorometer (Japan) with a 1 cm path length quartz cuvette. Both excitation and emission slits were maintained at a 5 nm bandpass to ensure consistent detection parameters throughout the experiment.

Dynamic light scattering

The size of the aggregates was measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (UK) equipped with a He–Ne laser at 633 nm. Twentyfive micromolar of MFMA, MFDA, MFEA, and MFHA samples were prepared in pH 7.4 (10 mM TES buffer), and the hydrodynamic diameter was measured at 25 °C. Three different experiments were performed to get the average data.

Cryo-TEM imaging

Twentyfive micromolar of MFEA in a pH 7.4 10 mM TES buffer was prepared and equilibrated for 10 min. Two microliters of solution was deposited on a carbon-covered Cu (copper) grid (300 mesh), followed by immediate vitrification of the grids by placing it inside the GATAN CP3 cryo-plunger. After that, TEM images were recorded in JEOL JEM-2100F microscope. The TEM images were further analysed in ImageJ software.

Confocal microscopy to analyse coacervation

Twentyfive micromolar solution of LFs was prepared in 10 mM TES buffer at pH 7.4 and equilibrated for 10 min. Fluorescence dyes were added, and the mixture was incubated for 20–30 min. The sample was drop-cast onto a clean glass slide and carefully covered with a cover slip to create a uniform imaging surface. Confocal laser scanning microscopy was performed using a Leica SP8 system (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with an HC PL APO 63×/1.40 oil-immersion objective lens. For imaging, excitation was performed using a 488 nm and 552 nm laser line, suitable for exciting the fluorophore molecules. LFs solutions at different concentrations (5 µM, 10 µM, 25 µM, 50 µM, and 100 µM) were prepared, incubated with rhodamine B (concentration 0.2 µM or other fluorophore at 0.2 µM), and confocal imaging (552 nm laser) was performed to investigate concentration-dependent coacervation. Time-dependent images were captured with 25 μM MFEA to investigate coalescence. The effect of NaCl, urea, and guanidinium hydrochloride was determined with 25 µM LFs, and rhodamine B was used as the fluorophore.

FRAP experiment

The FRAP experiment was performed using 25 µM LFs in a pH 7.4 buffer with an Olympus IX83 microscope. The dispersion of LFs was treated with sulforhodamine B for 30 min. Pre-bleaching images were acquired with an imaging power set at 4%. Subsequently, we selected the entire droplet at a specific focus plane as an ROI and bleached it at 25% power using the 552 nm laser. The fluorescence intensity of the ROI over the duration of the experiments and fluorescence recovery was determined by measuring the fluorescence intensity of the ROI during the post-bleach sequence. Three or more measurements were conducted for each sample. The fluorescence intensities of the ROIs were extracted using ImageJ. The recovery time (t1/2) was determined as per the reported procedure65.

Preparation of giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs)

GUVs were prepared using the reported literature66. DOPC/DOPE/Chol/DOPS (44 30 20 6) was dissolved in 1 mL of CHCl3-methanol (3:1) and placed in an RB flask. The concentration of lipids was 2 mM. Next, 4 ml of buffer (pH 7.4, 10 mM TES buffer) was added. The solution was then rotated at 40 °C under reduced pressure to completely evaporate the organic component. The formation of GUVs was characterised under an optical microscope.

2D domain formation on confocal microscopy

MFMA, MFDA, MFEA, and MFHA were added to GUVs at 1:25 LFs: lipid ratio and equilibrated for an hour. The sample was drop-cast onto a clean glass slide and imaged with a confocal laser-scanning microscope as mentioned above (Leica SP8, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Flavonoids were excited at 405 nm, and the images of GUVs (incorporated with LFs) were recorded.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

MFMA, MFDA, MFEA, and MFHA were incubated with lipid membranes (DOPC/DOPE/Chol/DOPS (44 30 20 6)) at 1 to 25 LFs: lipid ratio (250 µM lipid and 10 µM LFs). The solutions were drop-cast on silicon wafers. The samples were dried under a vacuum. AFM images were recorded in a Cypher Asylum Research AFM (OXFORD Instruments). The height of the bilayer and domain was analysed.

Membrane-bound nano-coacervate and confocal microscopy

Rhodamine-PE-labelled GUVs (DOPC/DOPE/Chol/DOPS/Rh-PE) were prepared using the reported literature66. Calcein was added to MFEA coacervates, incubated for 30 min, and the calcein-loaded MFEA coacervates were added to the GUVs at a MFEA-to-lipid ratio of 1:25. The sample was incubated for 30 min, drop-cast onto clean glass slides, and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica SP8, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) in 405 nm, 488 nm, and 552 nm laser channels. Rhodamine-PE (red channel) was useful to visualise the lipid bilayer, and calcein (green channel) was used as a hydrophilic dye for the coacervate marker. The MFEA domains were observed via the 405 nm channel (blue, flavonoid). Control experiments with no flavonoid but in the presence of calcein were also performed.

Preparation of small unilamellar vesicles

Lipid mixtures of DOPC/DOPE/Chol/DOPS/NBD-PE/Rh-PE (44: 27: 20: 6: 1.5: 1.5) and DOPC/DOPE/Chol/DOPS (44: 30: 20: 6) were prepared in 3:1 (v/v) mixture of CHCl3 and methanol. The lipid films were obtained by purging a dry nitrogen stream to evaporate the solvent. The lipid film was vacuum-dried for 14 h to completely remove any remaining organic solvent. Then, the lipid film was hydrated in 10 mM TES buffer (pH 7.4) containing 100 mM NaCl and 1.0 mM CaCl2 for 3 h at 35 °C. The solution was sonicated for 20 min in a probe sonicator. After sonication, the solution was passed through a 50 nm polycarbonate filter (11 times) to obtain small unilamellar vesicles.

Fusion assay

The fusion kinetics were monitored by fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) between NBD (donor) and Rhodamine B (acceptor), which were covalently bound to the DOPE lipid at the head group. In our experiment, we mixed the probed vesicles (DOPC/DOPE/Chol/DOPS/NBD-PE/Rh-PE at 44: 27: 20: 6: 1.5: 1.5 ratio) and the unprobed vesicles (DOPC/DOPE/Chol/DOPS at 44: 30: 20: 6 ratio) at a 1:4 molar ratio. The fusion was triggered by 5% (wt/wt) polyethylene glycol (PEG) at a total lipid concentration of 0.2 mM. PEG brings the membrane closer and induces fusion. Upon fusion with unprobed vesicles, the FRET efficiency decreases. The emission intensity of the donor (NBD) at 535 nm with time was monitored in a Hitachi F-7000 spectrofluorometer. Slits of 5 nm were used in both the excitation and emission throughout the experiment. We prepared the stock lipidated flavonoid (LF) solution (1 mM) in DMSO, and small aliquots were added to the vesicle mixture to get the desired LF/lipid ratio. Cell lines, plasmids and Viruses

Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells (#CRL-3216) and Madin−Darby canine kidney (MDCK) (CCL-34) cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) along with anti-mycotic agent (Gibco) containing Penicillin, Streptomycin and Amphotericin B at 37 °C and in 5% CO2.

PA-2A-Swap-Nluc (PASTN) reporter virus, which is based on the Influenza viral strain A/ WSN/33 (H1N1) was used for infecting the MDCK cells (PMID: 24089552)55.

The lentiviral backbone plasmid (pHAGE2-EF1aInt-ACE2-WT) expressing the human ACE2 gene (BEI # NR-52512), lentiviral plasmid (pHAGE-CMV-Luc2-IRES-ZsGreen-W) bearing the luciferase and ZsGreen ORFs (BEI # NR-52516) along with the plasmids encoding HIV helper proteins including Gag-Pol (pHDM-Hgpm2) (BEI # NR-52517), Tat (pHDM-tat1b) (BEI # NR-52518) and Rev (pRC-CMV-Rev1b) (BEI # NR-52519), were obtained from BEI Resources. Plasmid (pHDM-IDTSpike-fixK) encoding codon optimised SARS-CoV-2 WT spike protein (S) glycoprotein (Genbank NC_045512) was obtained from BEI Resources (BEI # NR-52514).

Production of SARS-CoV-2-based pseudoviruses

Generation of SARS-CoV-2 pseudoviruses expressing WT spike glycoproteins by pseudotyping HIV-1-based lentiviral system was originally described by Crawford et al., 202067. Briefly, for generating respective pseudoviruses, HEK 293T cells were cotransfected with the lentiviral plasmid expressing the firefly luciferase protein along with plasmids encoding HIV-1 proteins- Gag-Pol, Rev, and Tat and also with plasmid encoding either SARS-CoV-2 S or VSV-G by using calcium phosphate transfection kit (Takara-Bio). The supernatant containing the viral particles was harvested at 60 h post-transfection (hpt) and was used freshly.

Pseudoviral entry assay

HEK 293T cells were transduced with lentiviral plasmids overexpressing hACE2 protein. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, cells were infected with lentiviruses pseudotyped with either SARS-CoV-2 S or VSV G at their surface. The efficiency of these pseudoviruses to enter HEK 293T cells overexpressing the ACE2 receptor protein was estimated by measuring the firefly luciferase signal expression within the infected cells at 18 hpi by using the Luciferase assay system kit, Promega. The amount of virus thus entering into the cells is a direct measure of the amount of luciferase signal generated by using a luminometer -Glomax 20/20 (Promega).

Role of MFEA on pseudovirus entry

HEK 293T cells transduced to express hACE2 receptor protein were treated with increasing concentrations of LFs (MFEA and MEEA) for 1 h at 37 °C. At the same time, SARS-CoV-2 was also treated with the same concentrations of the LFs (MFEA and MEEA) for 1 h at room temperature. Thereafter, hACE2-HEK 293 T cells were infected with the viral inoculum for 1 h at 37 °C. The cells were replenished with the same concentration of the MFEA and MEEA as before. At 18 hpi, the cells were lysed, and the luciferase signal thus generated was measured using a Luminometer. Every experiment was repeated thrice, and each data point is representative of three independent experiments.

Influenza Virus entry assay

The Nano-Luciferase reporter assay was used to determine the luciferase activity of a reporter influenza viral strain A/ WSN/33 (H1N1), as previously mentioned by Tran et al. (PMID: 24089552)55. MDCK cells seeded in a 96-well plate were treated with increasing concentrations of MFEA for 1 h at 37 °C. At the same time, the influenza viruses were also treated with different concentrations of MFEA 1 h at room temperature. Thereafter, the cells were infected in triplicate with treated inoculum of the nanoluciferase influenza A reporter virus (PASTN) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 for 1 h. Thereafter, the cells were replenished with the same concentration of the MFEA and MEEA. The infected cells were harvested at 8 hpi, and the luciferase activity of the virus was measured using the Nano-Glo luciferase assay system (Promega). Luminescence was detected using a Luminometer (Glomax 20/20, Promega), a direct measure of viral entry into cells. Every experiment was performed in triplicate, and each data point was independently repeated at least three times. Graphs were plotted using Graphpad Prism/Origin and represented as mean standard deviations (n = 3).
