The relationship between creative learning environment and adolescents’ mathematical problem-solving ability: a moderated mediation model
Authors: Yongzhao Wang, Lijun Zhou, Bingqing Xie, Qiantong Su, Lisha Wang, Hua Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesMathematical problem-solving ability (MPSA), as a core indicator for measuring students’ higher-order thinking development, is crucial for fostering the formation and transfer of their innovative capabilities. However, the complex mechanisms underlying MPSA require further exploration. The current study aims to deepen the understanding of the psychological mechanisms linking creative school and classroom environments (CSCE) to MPSA by examining the mediating effect of creative self-efficacy (CSE) and the moderating effect of ICT usage (ICTU).MethodologyBased on PISA 2022 and drawing on a sample of adolescents in Korea (N = 6,364), the current study employed descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and regression analysis, as well as constructed a moderated mediation model to systematically examine the complex mechanisms through which CSCE, CSE, and ICTU are associated with adolescents’ MPSA.ResultsCSCE, CSE, and ICTU were all positively correlated with and significantly predicted MPSA. Further analysis revealed that CSCE was not only directly associated with MPSA, but also indirectly associated via CSE, which provides additional support for social cognitive theory. The moderating analysis further revealed that ICTU operated a dual role within the model. On the one hand, ICTU positively moderated the CSCE → CSE pathway, strengthening the positive association between the environment and self-efficacy. On the other hand, it negatively moderated both the CSCE → MPSA and CSE → MPSA pathways, suggesting that excessive ICTU may weaken the conversion of environmental and self-belief inputs into problem-solving capacity through increased cognitive load. Furthermore, subgroup analysis revealed that the pattern of associations varies across different gender and mathematical literacy groups, specifically in terms of the direction and intensity of the mediation patterns and moderating effects, which differ according to group characteristics.ConclusionThe present study further preliminarily elucidates the complex psychological mechanisms through which creative learning environments are associated with mathematical problem-solving ability, provides empirical insights into the application of social cognitive and constructivist theories in high-pressure educational contexts, and offers guidance on implementing differentiated teaching interventions in the reducing academic burden and enhancing efficiency reforms in South Korea and similar educational systems.Keywords: Creative learning environment, Creative self-efficacy, Mathematical problem-solving ability, Moderating mediation, Subgroup analysis

Introduction

In the context of digital technologies that are fundamentally reconfiguring social structures, cultivating talent with exceptional problem-solving capacity has become the cornerstone mission of education systems worldwide. Problem-solving ability not only underpins adaptation to rapidly changing environments, but also functions as a key driver of innovation and sustainable development, prompting its inclusion as a central domain in national competency frameworks and large-scale international assessments [1–3]. Mathematics, a discipline that fosters systematic thinking and logical reasoning, has shifted its educational focus from computational skill mastery to the development of mathematical problem-solving abilities (MPSA) [4]. MPSA is defined as an individual’s capacity to engage in mathematical reasoning and solve problems in different contexts by using processes such as representation, application, interpretation, evaluation and reasoning, which is a core component of mathematical literacy [5]. However, despite the deepening integration of information and communication technology (ICT) into education, its potential to enhance students’ higher-order thinking skills remains largely untapped [6, 7]. Therefore, it is imperative both theoretically and practically to systematically map the factors associated with students’ MPSA, which addresses the talent-development needs of the new era while improving the quality and effectiveness of mathematics education.

The development of MPSA is a complex process involving multidimensional interactions between environment, cognition and behavior. The creative school and classroom environment (CSCE), creative self-efficacy (CSE) and information technology use (ICTU) are considered key variables representing environmental support, individual cognition and behavioral tools, respectively. These variables are theoretically and empirically linked to the development of MPSA [8–10]. Based on social cognitive theory, individuals, behavior and environment form an interactive triad [11]. As a crucial environmental input, creative learning environments may be associated with individuals’ cognitive beliefs, which in turn may be related to their learning behaviors and performance. Concurrently, constructivist learning perspectives emphasize that learning is an active process of knowledge construction within supportive contexts, where ICT tools serve as critical technological mediators [12]. However, the existing research predominantly examines isolated variables or fails to fully account for the unique cognitive demands and operational mechanisms of mathematics within specific educational contexts. In the context of the Korean education system, which demonstrates outstanding PISA performance yet faces significant academic pressure, the underlying psychological mechanisms and the role of ICT in relation to MPSA remain unclear. Therefore, the present study integrates social cognitive and constructivist perspectives to systematically examine the complex patterns of association among CSCE, CSE and ICTU in relation to MPSA among Korean adolescents.

Creative learning environments: the cornerstone of mathematical problem-solving development

Schools and classrooms are core micro-systems that facilitate adolescents’ social interaction and cognitive development [13]. A creative school environment specifically refers to a supportive structural framework that fosters psychological safety and space for trial and error, cultivated through an open and inclusive teaching atmosphere that encourages exploration and diverse expression [14]. According to constructivist theory, effective mathematics learning occurs when students actively retrieve and reorganize prior knowledge while attempting various strategies in response to authentic, complex and open-ended problem situations [12]. Creative classroom environments facilitate such constructive learning by designing unconventional problems, encouraging multiple solution paths and advocating process-oriented assessment. Furthermore, social cognitive theory suggests that environments can directly or indirectly influence individual cognition and behaviour through alternative experiences, social persuasion and other mechanisms [11]. In the mathematics classrooms, the affirmation by teachers of innovative solutions and peer-to-peer intellectual exchanges are critical environmental factors.

Concurrently, empirical evidence further demonstrates that CSCE is positively associated with students’ inquiry enthusiasm and cognitive flexibility, thereby showing a consistent link to the continued refinement of MPSA. For example, a cross-cultural meta-analysis conducted revealed that creativity-supportive class environments are not only positively related to pupils’ creative-thinking performance, but also show positive associations with their academic engagement and task persistence within highly structured domains such as mathematics [15]. The finding has been corroborated in the Korean educational context. The study, drawing on a nationally representative sample of elementary and secondary students, reported that a creative classroom climate emerged as a significant predictor of students’ mathematical-modeling performance, with an effect size surpassing that of traditional instructional variables, thereby underscoring the unique contribution of creative environments to advanced mathematical competencies [16]. Collectively, the results intimate that CSCE may function as a pivotal external factor associated with the development of adolescents’ MPSA.

Creative self-efficacy: the cognitive bridge linking creative environments to mathematical ability

CSE, as a pivotal cognitive factor connecting external environments with individual behavior, is considered to play a central role in the process of mathematical problem-solving. CSE is defined as an individual’s belief in their ability to generate novel ideas and employ innovative methods to solve problems [9, 17, 18]. Within the social cognitive theory framework, self-efficacy acts as a crucial cognitive mediator through which environments are theorized to be associated with behavior [11, 19]. When students are placed in environments that support innovation, they gradually build confidence in their creative abilities through direct experiences of success, observing peer role models, receiving encouragement from teachers, and experiencing positive emotions.

The empirical research also strongly supports the central role of self-efficacy in mediating the relationship between environment and individual performance [9, 20–25]. For instance, a meta-analysis synthesizing 189 studies revealed that self-efficacy significantly mediates the relationship between environmental factors and academic performance, accounting for approximately 40% of environmental influence [26]. The prior study further indicated through longitudinal research that the junior secondary stage constitutes a critical period for developing CSE, with environmental support during that phase showing enduring associations with its long-term development [27]. Moreover, the research emphasized that students with high self-efficacy are more adept at employing metacognitive strategies like planning, monitoring, and reflection, demonstrating greater perseverance and emotional stability when encountering difficulties, and these characteristics are in turn related to higher mathematical learning performance [28]. It was similarly observed that when tackling open-ended mathematical tasks, students with high CSE were more inclined to employ multiple representational modes, such as graphical, symbolic, and verbal representations [29]. The problem-solving pathways exhibited greater flexibility, and their solutions demonstrated significantly higher accuracy and completeness compared to students with low self-efficacy. Consequently, CSE may act as the pivotal psychological bridge that links creative-learning-context support to cognitive-motivational resources, thereby being associated with higher-quality engagement and superior MPSA [30–32].

ICT use: a strategic tool empowering mathematical problem-solving processes

The use of ICT in the process of mathematics learning refers to how frequently and in what manner students use digital tools for exploration, verification and expression, reflecting their behavioral tendency to regulate the learning process through technology [33–35]. From a theoretical mechanism perspective, ICTU may moderate the relationship between creative environments and MPSA through multiple pathways. Firstly, as part of the learning environment, ICT tools can provide instant feedback and visualization support, thereby enhancing students’ exploratory experiences and their perception of success. The phenomenon may amplify the positive association of creative environments on self-efficacy [18, 36]. The constructivist perspective also posits that technological tools facilitate deep exploration, broadening students’ thought processes, lowering the threshold for trial and error and reinforcing their inclination towards exploration in open-ended tasks [37].

However, cognitive load theory also highlights potential risks, inappropriate or excessive ICT use may introduce external cognitive load, distracting students from core mathematical concepts and disrupting deep learning [38, 39]. Additionally, empirical research similarly reveals the dual nature of ICT effectiveness. For instance, the cross-national studies have revealed an inverted U-shaped relationship between the frequency of ICT use at school and maths achievement, with moderate use yielding the best results [40]. Further research indicates that the effectiveness of ICT is closely tied to implementation methods, with tools that support inquiry-based learning being more strongly associated with problem-solving skills than tools that facilitate rote practice [10]. Furthermore, the prior research suggest that ICTU can amplify the impact of environmental factors. For instance, within technology-rich learning environments, the positive impact of creative settings is particularly pronounced on digital platforms that foster collaboration and exploration [41, 42]. Consequently, the theoretical and empirical evidence converge in proposing that ICTU significantly moderates the creative-environment’ MPSA linkage, a hypothesis that merits direct scrutiny to illuminate how digital contexts reshape the development of MPSA.

Despite progress in the existing research, there are still gaps that need to be addressed. Firstly, the insufficient research has been conducted into the patterns of association of creative environments in mathematics, a discipline that relies heavily on logical and abstract thinking. Secondly, there is a lack of systematic integration of the synergistic impact of CSCE, CSE and ICTU on MPSA. Thirdly, high-pressure, high-performance educational systems such as that in Korea offer a unique context in which to examine these mechanisms, yet large-scale empirical research based in the setting is scarce. The current study addresses these gaps by focusing on Korean adolescents to construct a moderated mediation model, systematically examining the complex patterns through which creative learning environments, CSE and ICTU are interrelated during the process of mathematics learning. Such explorations could hopefully contribute to developing a more discipline-specific theory of MPSA development, providing empirical foundations for advancing mathematics education reform.

The present study

Drawing on social cognitive theory and constructivism to address the aforementioned research gap, the current study aims to systematically reveal the pattern of associations between CSCE and MPSA by constructing a moderated mediation model. The focus is on exploring the mediating and moderating roles of CSE and ICTU within the mechanism, revolving around four core research questions: (1) investigate whether CSCE is significantly associated with adolescents’ MPSA. (2) examine whether CSE acts as a bridging factor between CSCE and MPSA. (3) explore whether ICTU exhibits a moderating effect in the mediating mechanism of MPSA. (4) ascertain whether the pattern of associations for MPSA exhibits group heterogeneity based on students’ gender and mathematical literacy levels.

The first research hypothesis (H1) posits that CSCE significantly and positively predicts adolescents’ MPSA. The second hypothesis (H2) indicates that CSE mediates the relationship between CSCE and MPSA. The third hypothesis (H3) emphasizes that ICTU may exhibit a significant moderating effect across all three pathways in the mediational model of CSCE on MPSA. To be specific, ICTU might demonstrate significant moderating effects in the CSCE → CSE, CSCE → MPSA and CSE → MPSA pathways. The fourth hypothesis (H4) proposes that the pattern of associations underlying MPSA exhibits significant differences across characteristics such as gender and mathematical literacy. Methods

Participants

The present study utilizes data from the 2022 PISA survey of South Korean adolescents as its analytical sample. PISA, as a global educational assessment programme, focuses on evaluating the literacy levels of 15-year-old students in mathematics, reading and science. Conducted every three years, the 2022 assessment focused on mathematical literacy and involved 81 countries or regions. To ensure the high quality and representative of the analytical sample, the current research processed the data through the following steps. Firstly, the listwise deletion method was employed to exclude samples with missing values across all core variables (including CSCE, CSE, ICTU, and MPSA). Subsequently, Little’s MCAR test was applied to the missing value patterns of the remaining sample, with results indicating a completely random missing (MCAR) pattern. Next, for cases within the remaining sample with missing values in some core variables, multiple imputation methods were employed to impute the missing data [43, 44]. After screening, the final analysis sample comprised 6,364 students. Among them, a total of 3,099 (48.70%) participants were female and the remaining 3,265 (51.30%) were male, reflecting balanced gender representation. The average age was 15.772 years, with a mean Economic, Social and Cultural Status (ESCS) index of approximately 0.239, slightly above the OECD average.

Instruments

Creative school and class environment

Creative school and class environment was measured using the CREATSCH scale from PISA 2022, which assesses students’ recognition of the extent to which creative thinking is nurtured and supported within their school and classroom settings. The scale was comprised of six items, including “My teachers give me enough time to come up with creative solutions on assignments”, “My teacher values students’ creativity”, “At school, I am given a chance to express my ideas”, and so on. It employed a four-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (4). The OECD used weighted maximum likelihood estimation (WLE) to analyze students’ responses to six relevant questions. Setting the average level of OECD students to 0 and the standard deviation to 1 standardized the data to derive a composite index named CREATSCH, which measures the creative school and classroom environment as perceived by students. For the scale used in the current study, the Cronbach’s coefficient was 0.897, indicating good internal consistency. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) revealed that all items loaded on a single factor with loadings ranging from 0.650 to 0.852, demonstrating robust factor structure stability. Further analysis indicated a composite reliability (CR) of 0.899 and an average variance extracted (AVE) of 0.599, both exceeding the critical thresholds, thus confirming the measurement model possesses sound convergent validity. Moreover, in accordance with the Fornell-Larcker criteria, the square roots of the AVEs exceeded the correlations among all variables, fully validating the scale’s superiority in terms of discriminant validity. In summary, the scale demonstrated sound reliability and validity in the present research, possessing dependable measurement performance [45, 46].

Creative self-efficacy

The creative self-efficacy scale assesses students’ confidence in accomplishing a series of tasks reflecting creative thinking skills. The scale comprises ten items, such as coming up with creative ideas for school projects, being creative, inventing new things, coming up with many good ideas for helping people in need, and so on. A four-point scale is employed, ranging from “not at all confident” (1) to “extremely confident” (4). The OECD also derived a composite index of creative self-efficacy based on responses to 10 items, denoted as CREATEFF. The scale demonstrated high reliability with a Cronbach’s coefficient of 0.955.

The EFA results revealed factor loadings for each item ranging from 0.659 to 0.929, indicating robust construct validity. Furthermore, the CR and AVE values were 0.955 and 0.680 respectively, both within critical thresholds. The square roots of AVE exceeded the correlation coefficients between variables, demonstrating sound discriminant validity and convergent validity.

ICT use

The current study employs three core scales from the PISA 2022 ICT questionnaire as measurement tools for the “ICT usage” variable: “Subject-related ICT use during lessons (ICTSUBJ)”, “Use of ICT in enquiry-based learning activities (ICTENQ)”, and “Support or feedback via ICT (ICTFEED)”. Specifically, the ICTSUBJ scale comprises four items, measuring the frequency with which students utilize digital resources in mathematics, science, and other subject-specific lessons. It employs a five-point scale ranging from “never or almost never” (1) to “every lesson or almost every lesson” (5), with “I do not have this subject” (6) designated as the missing value. The ICTENQ scale was comprised of ten items assessing students’ frequency of using digital resources in inquiry-based learning activities, such as “creating multimedia presentations containing images, sound, or video”, “tracking progress on assignments or projects”. A five-point scale was employed ranging from “never or almost never” (1) to “daily or almost daily” (5). The ICTFEED scale comprises four items measuring the frequency with which students receive support and feedback via digital resources, including “reading or listening to teacher feedback on assignments and academic performance”, “reading or listening to peer feedback on my assignments”. The OECD also derived composite indices for ICT-related variables based on students’ responses to items across three dimensions, denoted as ICTSUBJ, ICTENQ, and ICTFEED. The scale also employs a five-point rating scale. The reliability analysis revealed Cronbach’s coefficients of 0.813, 0.947, and 0.925 for the three scales respectively, all indicating high levels of internal consistency. The result confirms the sound reliability and internal consistency of the ICTSUBJ, ICTENQ, and ICTFEED scales employed in the present study for measuring ICT usage. The EFA results revealed that the factor loadings for each item across the three scales ranged from 0.425 to 0.956, indicating robust construct validity for all scales. Furthermore, the CR values were 0.845, 0.947, and 0.926 respectively, while the AVE values were 0.592, 0.645, and 0.759 respectively. All fell within the critical range, and testing confirmed that the square roots of AVE were higher than the correlation coefficients between variables. The findings demonstrate that each scale possesses a credible foundation of reliability and validity.

Mathematical problem-solving ability

The current study measures students’ MPSA with their PISA 2022 scores on four interrelated dimensions: formulation, application, interpretation and evaluation, and reasoning. As detailed in the PISA 2022 technical report [47], each dimension yields ten pseudo-true values via item response theory modeling, based on students’ actual responses. These are denoted as PVMPFS (representation), PVMPEM (application), PVMPIN (interpretation and evaluation) and PVMPRE (reasoning). While analyzing multiple sets of PVs simultaneously and combining results via Rubin’s rules is the best practice for statistical completeness, estimates based on a single set of PVs have been shown to be sufficiently stable in large-scale studies [48–50]. The official PISA data analysis manual states that using a single PV set is acceptable for large-scale analyses focusing on group-level relationships rather than precise individual ability values, since sampling error far exceeds the variation between PVs [51]. Following established field practice, and to ensure that the results of the model remain concise and interpretable, the present research employs the first plausible value (PV1) for each dimension in the primary analyses. To derive a comprehensive indicator of mathematical problem-solving ability for subsequent holistic model testing (e.g. correlation analysis, regression and moderated mediation models), the study calculates the arithmetic mean of students’ PV1 scores across each dimension to form a single, continuous MPSA composite score. The composite score represents students’ overall performance across the core cognitive dimensions of mathematical problem solving and has a weighted mean of 500 and a standard deviation of 100. This construction method ensures the indicator encompasses the multidimensional structure of MPSA, while remaining suitable for subsequent statistical analyses based on single dependent variables and focusing on overall ability.

Control variables

To eliminate potential confounding effects from demographic variables, the present study incorporated gender, age, grade level, and ESCS as control variables into the analytical model, enabling a more accurate estimation of the relationships among CSCE, CSE, ICTU, and MPSA.

Data analysis

PISA employs a two-stage, non-probability random sampling design, comprising two stages: stratified sampling of schools and random sampling of students within schools. To correctly handle the complex sampling design and ensure that the research findings are representative of the entire population of 15-year-old students in South Korea, the present study strictly adheres to the OECD guidelines in all analyses and systematically applies the final student composite weights (W_FSTUWT). Specifically, the current research first used Python statistical software to perform weighted pre-processing on the complete dataset following the aforementioned interpolation, assigning each student a weight value consistent with their representativeness within the population, thereby generating a weighted dataset that reflects the structural characteristics of the population.

Subsequently, all core statistical analyses were conducted using this weighted dataset, including descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation analysis and multiple linear regression analysis performed using SPSS, structural equation modeling using AMOS to test for mediating effects, and tests of moderated mediation models using the PROCESS macro (Model 59). Within the framework, all statistical software utilized the variance-covariance matrix of the weighted sample when calculating parameter estimates, thereby correcting for estimation biases that might arise from the unequal probability sampling design. Consequently, all parameter estimates obtained from the correlation analysis, regression models, structural equation models, and moderation models reflect the complex characteristics of the sampling design, ensuring the unbiasedness of the parameter estimates and the validity of inferences regarding the target population. The standard errors, confidence intervals and significance test results reported in the current study were all calculated using standard algorithms in the relevant statistical software based on the aforementioned weighted datasets, and thus accurately reflect the uncertainty of the population parameters.

To test the significance of the mediating effect, the present study employed a bias-corrected Bootstrap method, with 5,000 repetitions. The Bootstrap process was also conducted using the weighted dataset, with each sample retaining the weighting structure of the original data, thereby ensuring the reliability of the test results under the complex sampling design. It should be noted that, although the current study used the final student composite weight (W_(FSTUWT)) for parameter estimation to correct for bias in point estimation resulting from unequal probability sampling, the balanced repeated weights or Jackknife method recommended by PISA for estimating standard errors were not employed. All the standard errors, confidence intervals and significance test results reported in the research were calculated using the relevant statistical software’s standard algorithms, based on the variance–covariance matrix of the weighted sample. While the method reflects the fundamental characteristics of the sampling design, it may underestimate the standard error to some extent compared to the gold standard of using repeated weights. If the bias-corrected 95% confidence interval obtained does not include zero, the effect is considered statistically significant at the 0.05 level. In addition, the present study examined measurement invariance systematically across different gender and performance groups using multi-group confirmatory factor analysis. The results indicated that the unconstrained model fit well, confirming configural invariance. Judgments regarding metric invariance, scalar invariance and residual invariance were made based on whether RMSEA and CFI values fell below the 0.01 threshold, suggesting that measurement invariance is acceptable [52–54].

Results

Common method bias

To facilitate further in-depth analysis, the section conducted common method bias tests on all items of variables such as CSCE, CSE and ICTU, with a view to eliminating errors arising during the survey process and enhancing the accuracy of the results. The current research first employed Harman’s one-factor test, which revealed five common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The first common factor accounted for 31.592% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%; therefore, it is concluded that the impact of common method bias on this study falls within an acceptable range [55, 56]. Concurrently, the study also applied the Unmeasured Latent Method Construct (ULMC) approach to further validate the finding [57]. The test results indicate that the difference between the U-model and the R-model is not significant ( 0.05), suggesting that although common method bias exists, it is not severe and is therefore acceptable. Overall, cross-validation using both methods confirms that the data used in the present study is not affected by common method bias, ensuring the validity and reliability of the observed relationships among variables.

Descriptive statistics andcorrelation analysis

To further explore the potential relationships between CSCE, CSE, ICTU and MPSA, descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analyses were conducted on each variable, with results presented in Table 1. It is evident that the mean CSCE for adolescents (0.153) exceeds the OECD average, indicating widespread recognition that school and classroom environments effectively foster and support creative thinking. However, the mean CSE score was 0.130 with SD of 1.123, indicating that Korean adolescents’ CSE levels were slightly below average overall, with considerable individual variation. The mean ICTU score was 0.354 with SD of 0.874, showing that Korean adolescents’ ICTU frequency was also below the OECD average, though with relatively smaller inter-individual differences. The mean for MPSA was 529.209 with SD of 107.019, indicating that Korean adolescents’ MPSA were generally at a high level, though individual’s capability varied considerably. Furthermore, the means for all four dimensions were at relatively high levels with small differences between them, suggesting that Korean adolescents developed more evenly across all dimensions of mathematical problem-solving. Pearson correlation analysis further revealed significant positive relationships among the variables. Specifically, adolescents’ CSCE, CSE, ICTU, and MPSA scores, along with their respective sub-dimensions, exhibited significant positive correlations, suggesting that the factors may be collectively related to higher MPSA to some extent, thereby showing an association with their comprehensive development.

The mediating effect of creative self-efficacy

A structural equation model was constructed using AMOS, which incorporated CSE as a mediating variable within the relationship pathway between CSCE and MPSA. The analysis demonstrated excellent model fit: (167) 370,128.517, CFI 0.953, TLI 0.947, NFI 0.953, IFI 0.947, RMSEA 0.072. The model fit indices approached ideal reference standards, indicating strong alignment with the sample data [58]. The analysis based on standardized direct effects (Table 3) reveals that CSCE is significantly associated with both students’ CSE and MPSA ( 0.303 and 0.025). The result highlights that when students are situated within school and class environments fostering a strong creative cultivation atmosphere, their CSE tends to be higher. Consequently, they exhibit greater self-confidence when confronting creative learning tasks, thereby enhancing their performance in solving problems. Moreover, CSE itself showed a positive association with MPSA ( 0.137), reflecting that the greater students’ confidence in their mathematical learning abilities, the better their performance in solving problems during the learning process. The current study further employed the bias-corrected percentile Bootstrap method to test the significance of CSE’s mediating effect, with results presented in Table 4 and Fig. 2. The indirect effect value for CSE was 0.042, accounting for 62.50% of the total effect. The direct effect was 0.025, with the 95% confidence interval not including zero, indicating that CSE partially mediates the relationship between CSCE and MPSA.

The results highlight that ICTU amplifies CSCE’s predictive role on CSE (llow ICTU 0.256, lmedium ICTU 0.303, lhigh ICTU 0.351), demonstrating that ICTU positively moderates CSCE’s forward prediction of CSE. However, ICTU inhibits the relationship of CSCE on MPSA (llow ICTU 4.129, lmedium ICTU 2.393, lhigh ICTU 0.657), and the predictive effect of CSE on MPSA (llow ICTU 6.149, lmedium ICTU 4.892, lhigh ICTU 3.636), indicating that ICTU exerts a negative moderating effect on both the positive predictive effects of CSCE on MPSA and CSE on MPSA. However, the direction of the moderation varies depending on the pathway: ICTU has a positive moderating effect on the CSCE → CSE pathway and a negative moderating effect on the CSCE → MPSA and CSE → MPSA pathways.

The Moderating Effect Analysis of ICTU on Different Pathways

Furthermore, to examine whether different ICT dimensions play distinct roles within the model, the section repeated the mediation analysis with moderators, using ICTSUBJ, ICTENQ and ICTFEED respectively (see Table S1 in the Supplementary Materials for detailed results). The results indicate that all three dimensions significantly and positively moderate the CSCE→CSE path, and significantly and negatively moderate the CSE→MPSA path, which is entirely consistent with the direction observed for the composite indicator. On the CSCE→MPSA path, ICTSUBJ and ICTENQ exhibited negative moderation, whilst ICTFEED exhibited positive moderation. Given the high consistency of the three dimensions across the two core paths, and as the composite indicator enhances statistical power and simplifies subsequent group analyses, the current study adopted ICTU as the composite indicator for ICT usage in the main model and multi-group tests.

Subgroup analysis of mechanisms influencing mathematical problem-solving ability

To systematically clarify the robustness of the complex “CSCE → MPSA” pattern across different demographic characteristics, this subsection conducted subgroup analyses based on adolescents’ gender and mathematical literacy (ML), aiming to develop differentiated intervention pathways and enhancement strategies tailored to the environment–individual fit.

Gender-based subgroup analysis

To further investigate whether individual differences in gender characteristics are related to the mechanism through which CSCE is associated with MPSA, the first part categorizes participants by gender to examine the mediating effect of CSE and the moderating effect of ICTU, with the results presented in Tables 6 and 7. Table 6 reveals significant differences in the mediating role of CSE between CSCE and MPSA across male and female groups. Specifically, the indirect effect value of CSE for males was 9.849, accounting for 45.40% of the total effect, indicating that CSE partially mediated the relationship between CSCE and MPSA in the male group. However, an intriguing finding is that among female participants, CSE does not mediate between CSCE and MPSA but instead exhibits a suppression effect. Although CSE has a positive indirect association on female students’ abilities with an indirect effect value of 5.930, it exerts a negative direct association on MPSA ( 4.848). As the indirect and direct effects point in opposite directions, and the absolute value of the indirect effect exceeds that of the direct association, the overall effect is weakened. The result indicates that, without considering the role of CSE, the negative impact of the creative environment on mathematical ability would be masked. In other words, CSE mitigates the environment’s detrimental effect on ability, rendering the overall effect appear weaker or even insignificant. The suppression effect may stem from the creative environment’s high uncertainty and low structure clashing with female students’ preference for structured learning, together with their marginalizing experiences of gendered creative stereotypes that deprive them of genuine participation and support and ultimately are associated with lower problem-solving capabilities [59–61].

The pattern aligns with the characteristic of high-proficiency learners, whose mechanisms for internal regulation through external tools are relatively mature. In summary, the moderating effect of ICTU exhibits a dynamic evolution with increasing academic attainment, precisely delineating systematic differences in ICT resource activation and conversion efficiency across performance levels, providing clear empirical support for digital interventions.

Discussion

The current study systematically examined the pattern of associations between CSCE and MPSA among Korean adolescents, with particular emphasis on the mediating effect of CSE and the moderating effect of ICTU. The research yielded the following four valuable findings, which were centered on four core questions.

The first finding of the present study indicates that CSCE is significantly and positively associated with students’ MPSA. Specifically, students whose school and class environments place a greater emphasis on fostering and supporting creative thinking demonstrate a stronger ability to solve complex mathematical problems. The result closely aligns with social cognitive theory, reflecting the interactive relationship between the individual, behavior, and environment [18]. A creative classroom environment, as a supportive contextual factor, is linked to pupils’ confidence and repertoire of strategies for solving mathematical problems by providing a safe space for trial and error and demonstrating diverse problem-solving models, including the teacher’s inventive demonstrations and peers’ alternative approaches, thereby being associated with the manifestation of their problem-solving abilities. Constructivist learning theory posits that learning constitutes a process whereby learners actively construct internal mental representations rather than passively receiving information, which was strongly supported by the present study [62]. In the context of mathematics education, the finding implies that genuine mathematical understanding stems from students’ active exploration of mathematical relationships and the internalization of knowledge. A creative classroom environment provides precisely the ideal soil for such active construction by setting open-ended mathematical tasks and encouraging students to formulate and verify conjectures. The pattern of associations linking MPSA has received empirical support within the field of mathematics education [63–65]. For instance, a large-scale cross-national study based on PISA data found that class-room teaching practices emphasizing inquiry and innovation were significantly and positively correlated with students’ performance in mathematics [66, 67] indicated that encouraging students to explain their reasoning processes and establish knowledge connections in mathematical classroom environments not only is associated with higher mathematical achievement, but also shows a marked association with their ability to solve unconventional problems. Furthermore, [68] longitudinal research shows that students in schools using open- ended teaching methods excel in standardized tests and demonstrate superior conceptual understanding and problem-solving skills. Similarly, [69] demonstrated the intrinsic symbiotic relationship between mathematical creativity and problem-solving ability, arguing that teaching practices that foster creativity directly encourage students to develop more flexible and effective solutions. In summary, cultivating a creative learning environment that encourages risk-taking and values diverse approaches is an effective and essential way to improve students’ MPSA and mathematical literacy.

The second finding of the current research is that CSE exerts a significant partial mediating effect in the relationship between CSCE and MPSA. To be specific, CSCE is not only positively associated with students’ MPSA, but also exerts an indirect association by enhancing their CSE, which is an intrinsic motivational belief. The finding is consistent with a central tenet of social cognitive theory, namely that the environment (CSCE) does not merely determine behavior directly (MPSA), but also exerts its influence through a key mediating variable: an individual’s cognition and self-beliefs (CSE). When students inhabit environments that encourage creativity and tolerate risk-taking, their experiences of successfully navigating creative tasks continually reinforce their ‘I can do it’ conviction. The heightened sense of self-efficacy is in turn associated with greater effort, employ superior strategies, and demonstrate stronger resilience when confronting complex mathematical problems. Moreover, the mediating mechanism of CSE has also received extensive empirical support. For instance, a meta-analysis study confirmed that self-efficacy serves as a key mediating pathway linking diverse learning environments to students’ academic achievement [26]. More directly, research within the discipline of mathematics has found that opportunities and encouragement gained in the classroom setting are associated with significantly higher mathematical self-efficacy, which in turn is related to higher mathematical achievement and problem-solving performance [70, 71]. Although these studies predominantly focus on mathematical self-efficacy, their conclusions align closely with the present research, collectively affirming that self-belief serves as a pivotal role for transforming knowledge into capability. The current study focuses self-efficacy within the domain of creative learning, identifying CSE as the key psychological element linking creative learning environments to MPSA, driving the internal transformation from perceiving environmental support to demonstrating exceptional capability.

Furthermore, while the direct effect of CSCE on MPSA in the current study was significant, its standardized coefficient was relatively small ( 0.022). In contrast, the mediating path accounted for a larger proportion (62.50%) of the total effect. The pattern aligns with multi-step mediation theory, which posits that direct effects often diminish along extended pathways where external environments are related to final outcomes via individuals’ internal cognitive mechanisms—a common phenomenon in complex psychological models [72]. Nevertheless, even modest direct effects may have theoretical and practical significance. According to trickle-down theory, sustained and stable environmental support can have modest short-term associations, which accumulate into meaningful long-term impacts on student development by fostering synergistic effects (e.g. self-efficacy) over extended periods [73]. Thus, the present research confirms not only that self-belief is a pivotal bridge that transforms environmental support into practical competence, but also that educational practice should prioritize systematic instructional design. The approach transforms creative environments into enduring intrinsic motivation and cognitive resources for students, thereby fostering the development of higher-order mathematical abilities more effectively.

The third finding of the present study, through the construction of a moderated mediation model, confirmed that ICTU plays a key and systematic moderating role in the complex mechanism linking ‘creative environment’ and ‘mathematical problem-solving ability’. The in-depth analysis indicates that the moderating effect of ICTU exhibits clear path-specificity: On the CSCE → CSE pathway, ICTU exerts a positive moderating effect, significantly enhancing the association of the creative environment on self-efficacy, revealing that digital tools provide a broader platform for the generation and expression of creative ideas; whereas on the CSCE → MPSA and CSE → MPSA pathways, ICTU exhibits a negative moderating effect, simultaneously weakening the direct promotional roles of both environmental support and self-belief on problem-solving ability. The seemingly contradictory result can be explained by the dual compensatory and enhancing properties of cognitive load theory and technological application [38, 40]. The findings of the current study should be interpreted with caution in light of the inherent nature of PISA assessment tasks. PISA mathematics questions incorporate conventional tasks that require algorithmic reasoning, open modeling tasks that demand creative reasoning, and projects that fall between these two extremes [74]. The phenomenon implies that the measurement of PISA itself relates to students’ ability to manage cognitive load and engage in creative exploration simultaneously. While cognitive load theory focuses on task demands on working memory resources, creativity demands emphasize the extent to which tasks invite novel solutions and diverse representations [75]. Within the framework, ICT’s dual moderating effects may reflect distinct mechanisms. The positive moderation along the CSCE → CSE pathway is more closely aligned with creative facilitation, whereby technological tools are associated with students’ self-efficacy in tasks that demand creativity by supporting the visualisation of ideas and rapid iteration. Conversely, negative moderation on the CSCE → MPSA and CSE → MPSA pathways is more likely to map to a cognitive load mechanism. In other words, high-intensity or inappropriate ICTU introduces external cognitive load, occupying limited mental resources and disrupting the conversion process from environmental support and self-belief to actual problem-solving performance. This finding suggests that the moderating role of ICTU is not simply one of promotion or inhibition, but rather exhibits clear boundary effects and path-specificity. The differing signs of the three dimensions in the CSCE→MPSA pathway in the supplementary analysis warrant further explanation. The negative moderation of ICTSUBJ and ICTENQ may stem from cognitive load mechanisms: the use of subject-specific or inquiry-based ICT in classroom settings requires students to process digital information and subject content simultaneously, which is likely to introduce external cognitive load, thereby inhibiting the direct translation of a creative environment into problem-solving ability [38, 40]. In contrast, the positive moderation of ICTFEED may reflect the unique buffering effect of feedback-based ICT, its delayed and social feedback can reduce students’ perceived uncertainty regarding creative tasks, enhance psychological safety, and thus directly promote performance [41, 42]. Despite the difference, the three dimensions exhibit a completely consistent direction of moderation on the more critical CSCE→CSE and CSE→MPSA pathways, indicating that ICT use (regardless of specific type) positively reinforces the environment’s influence on self-efficacy whilst negatively attenuating the transformation of self-efficacy into higher-order cognition. The effect of “double-edged sword” is universal across dimensions. Therefore, the use of a composite indicator can effectively capture the core moderating patterns of ICT use.

The current study employs subgroup analysis among Korean adolescents to reveal significant differences based on gender and mathematical literacy in the pattern of MPSA associations, thereby substantiating the fourth finding of the research. Specifically, with regard to gender, CSE exhibited partial mediation among male students. Among female students, however, it displayed a masking effect where the sign of the indirect effect was opposite to that of the direct effect and the absolute value of the indirect effect was greater than that of the direct effect, thereby weakening the total effect or even rendering it non‑significant [76]. At the same time, the moderating effect of ICTU was significant among female students across the CSCE → CSE, CSCE → MPSA and CSE → MPSA pathways, with positive moderation exhibited by the first two and negative moderation by the latter. Amongst male students, however, ICTU only exerted a significant moderating effect on the CSCE → CSE and CSCE → MPSA pathways, whilst the moderating effect was not significant on the CSE → MPSA pathway. These results further illustrate the gender differences in moderating effects. Regarding mathematical proficiency, the mediating effect of CSE exhibited systematic variations across academic literacy levels, while the moderating effect of ICTU displayed gradient differences. To be specific, it was discontinuous and strongly inhibitory in the low performers, formed a synergistic closed loop in the mid performers, and manifested as selective moderation in the high performers. The systematic differences likely stem from complex interactions among multi-level factors. With regard to the masking effect in gender differences, the seemingly contradictory pattern observed among female students, specifically that CSCE has a negative direct effect on MPSA but a positive indirect effect via CSE, can be explained more robustly by integrating stereotype threat theory with social cognitive theory. Firstly, according to stereotype threat theory [77], women have long faced the negative stereotype that women are not good at maths in the fields of mathematics and science, which is particularly pronounced in task contexts requiring creative and unconventional problem-solving. Subsequent meta-analyses have further confirmed that the negative impact of stereotype threat on women’s mathematical performance is more pronounced in tasks of higher difficulty [59]. Although CSCE advocate open exploration and diverse expression, the high uncertainty, low structure and encouragement of trial and error that accompany them may, conversely, exacerbate female students’ anxiety and insecurity regarding their own mathematical abilities. Consequently, in the absence of sufficient self-efficacy to protect them, they directly impair their performance in mathematical problem-solving. Furthermore, the relevant research indicates that creativity itself is subject to gender bias at the cultural-cognitive level; creative thinking is often perceived as associated with traditional masculine traits such as independence and autonomy. The phenomenon may lead to women facing additional social evaluation pressures in creative task contexts [60]. The pattern explains why the direct path from CSCE to MPSA exhibits a negative association among female students. Secondly, according to social cognitive theory [78, 79], self-efficacy is not only a predictor of behaviour but also a cognitive coping mechanism through which individuals resist negative environmental influences in stressful situations. When female students perceive high levels of CSE in a creative learning environment, they are more inclined to reinterpret environmental challenges as surmountable difficulties rather than threats to their own abilities. The positive belief system effectively buffers the emotional and cognitive load caused by stereotype threat, thereby exerting a positive protective role via an indirect pathway. The related research also indicates that the protective effect of self-efficacy on female students’ mathematics performance is often stronger than that for males, as females are more susceptible to the influence of evaluative threats from the environment [76]. In other words, high CSE ‘masks’ the negative direct effect of CSCE on MPSA, making the overall effect appear weak or even positive. Meta-analytic evidence from existing research also supports this view: creative self-efficacy plays a crucial bridge between environmental support and creative performance, and this mediating effect is more pronounced in groups facing higher social evaluation pressure [80]. Statistically, the masking effect manifests as an indirect effect that is opposite in sign and greater in magnitude than the direct effect; its theoretical essence lies precisely in self-efficacy acting as a coping mechanism to compensate for and offset the negative impacts of a stereotype-threatening environment. Therefore, the masking effect of CSE among female students is not a statistical illusion, but rather reflects the process by which individual cognitive beliefs actively resist adverse environmental factors under the pressure of gender stereotypes. Furthermore, the comprehensive sensitivity of female students to ICT moderation may be related to their greater focus on social interaction, feedback support and process details when using technology, which results in ICT tools being more deeply embedded in all aspects of their learning process. Regarding differences in mathematical literacy, the underlying factors may include gradient distributions of metacognitive abilities and internalized motivation levels. For students with low performance, weak foundational knowledge and strategies can limit their ability to convert environmental resources into higher-order skills, resulting in limited mediating effects. For mid performers, the suppression effect they observe may stem from a structural cognitive dilemma. At the stage, they possess the foundational knowledge for exploration yet lack the advanced metacognitive skills required to integrate multiple cognitive demands. When the openness of creative exploration cannot be effectively reconciled with the constraints of mathematical rigor, heightened self-efficacy may paradoxically deepen their entanglement in the resulting cognitive conflict, thereby amplifying confusion and frustration during the learning process [81]. In contrast, high-performing students demonstrate a clear, complete mediating model, progressing from environmental beliefs to competence. This stems from their robust knowledge base and efficient metacognitive abilities, which enable them to transform environmental challenges into growth momentum. The finding strongly corroborates Bandura’s theory that individuals of exceptional ability are better at using environmental resources to maintain and improve their motivation. The patterns underlying MPSA may be both universal and population-specific, according to subgroup analyses in the current study. The specific pathways from the supply of external resources to their internalization by individuals could be understood with the help of the preliminary empirical evidence. It suggests that future educational interventions must transcend traditional uniform models and construct differentiated support systems that can identify and respond to learners’ gender characteristics and cognitive readiness.

Although the current study provides some valuable empirical findings, it is important to bear in mind several limitations when interpreting the results. Firstly, the inferences drawn are based on cross-sectional data. While the theoretical model posits a directional relationship of creative environment → self-efficacy → problem-solving ability, the cross-sectional nature of the data precludes the establishment of causality. For example, students with stronger MPSA may be more likely to perceive an environment as creative or have greater self-efficacy, which suggests the possibility of reverse causality or mutual influence. The current study identifies a theoretically plausible and statistically significant association pathway, providing a crucial foundation for subsequent longitudinal or experimental research. However, the direction of causality requires verification in future studies. Secondly, the PISA data is characterised by a hierarchical structure in which students are nested within schools. To assess the strength of clustering effects at the school level, we calculated the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) for MPSA. The results showed an ICC value of 0.291, indicating that approximately 29.1% of the total variance in MPSA can be attributed to differences between schools. The value is classified as moderately high, confirming the significant influence of school-level environmental factors on student ability. Although the current study focuses on psychological mechanisms at the individual level examining the mediating and moderating relationships of CSCE, CSE and ICTU, and although complex sampling weights (W_FSTUWT) were applied in the data analysis to correct for design effects, the standard errors and significance test results obtained in the study may still be influenced by school-level clustering effects, as a multilevel linear model was not employed to account for random effects at the school level, nor were the balanced repeated weights (BRR) or Jackknife method recommended by PISA used to estimate standard errors. Future research could build upon this by employing multilevel structural equation modelling or multilevel mediation and moderation models, whilst simultaneously examining the interaction between school-level environmental factors and individual-level variables, to provide a more comprehensive explanation. Thirdly, there are certain limitations in the measurement and analysis of ICT use. The PISA 2022 assessment of ICT use focused primarily on frequency of use, failing to adequately distinguish between quality, type and specific teaching contexts; this limits a deeper understanding of the moderating effects of ICT. Then, although the current study combined the three dimensions into a composite indicator based on the consistency of their directions along the core pathways to maintain model parsimony, supplementary analyses (see the Results section and Table S1) indicate a difference in sign along the CSCE→MPSA pathway. The discrepancy suggests that reflective ICT may possess unique social support functions, whilst subject-based and inquiry-based ICT are more likely to induce cognitive load. Due to the high multicollinearity among the three dimensions and the study’s focus on the overall moderating pattern of ICT use, it was not possible to disentangle the independent contributions of each dimension in depth. Future research should develop more refined measurement tools to distinguish the purpose, context and level of cognitive engagement in ICT use, and employ latent variable interaction models or Bayesian multi-group analyses to examine the differential moderating pathways of different ICT dimensions between creative learning environments and mathematical problem-solving ability. Fourthly, the present study’s measurement of MPSA relies on PISA’s composite assessment. As previously noted, PISA items combine task types with varying cognitive demands and creativity requirements. While the finding reflects the complexity of real-world problem solving, it makes it difficult to distinguish rigorously whether research findings stem from students’ ability to manage general cognitive load or from abilities specific to creative problem solving. Future research could employ carefully designed experimental tasks to examine the specific impact of creative environments and beliefs on pure creative mathematical reasoning (e.g. proposing unconventional solutions or constructing novel models), while controlling cognitive load levels. The result would provide a more targeted test of the theoretical mechanisms underlying the current model. Fifthly, in terms of measurement and estimation methods, the present study clearly defines the approach to MPSA. Although the first set of PV1 was selected for the main analysis based on official guidelines and previous studies, the approach means that the statistically most robust estimation procedure, which is based on all plausible values and Rubin’s rule, was not adopted. Consequently, the parameter estimates in the research (particularly the standard errors) may not be as precise as theoretically possible. When conditions permit, future research should employ models incorporating all plausible values for estimation. The approach would provide a more robust statistical foundation for further validating the robustness of the study’s findings. Sixthly, in terms of statistical inference methods, it is worth noting that the present study employed W_(FSTUWT) to correct for bias in point estimates, but did not use the BRR weights or the jackknife method recommended by PISA to estimate standard errors. The current standard error calculation is based on the variance-covariance matrix of the weighted sample. While the method reflects the effects of complex sampling designs to some extent, it may underestimate the standard error compared with repeated-weight methods. Where possible, future research should use the repeated weights provided by PISA alongside Rubin’s rule to estimate standard errors, thereby improving the accuracy and reliability of statistical inference. Finally, while the sample of Korean adolescents in the current research offers a unique perspective due to their distinctive exam oriented education culture and highly developed ICT infrastructure, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to other educational contexts. The cross-cultural comparative research is therefore needed in the future to examine the boundary conditions and applicability of this theoretical model across different educational systems. Such exploration would help to build a more universal theoretical framework and provide empirical evidence for differentiated educational interventions.

Conclusion

The present study systematically examined the patterns through which CSCE is associated with adolescents’ MPSA by constructing a moderated mediation model. The main findings are summarized as follows: (1) There are significant positive correlations between the variables. (2) CSCE, CSE and ICTU all significantly predict adolescents’ MPSA. (3) CSCE is not only directly associated with MPSA but also shows an indirect association via the mediating role of CSE, suggesting that the pattern underlying MPSA may be a complex, multi-level and multi-factorial process. (4) ICTU plays a moderating role in the pathway through which a creative environment is linked to MPSA, and the direction of the moderation exhibits pathway-specificity. (5) The pattern through which CSCE is related to MPSA differs across groups of different genders and levels of mathematical literacy.

Based on the findings of the present study, the following practical recommendations may serve as a guide for educators when developing intervention strategies: (1) Upgrade physical teaching spaces to create intelligent, creative classroom environments. The current research indicates that CSCE is a key factor associated with the sustained development of students’ problem-solving abilities. Consequently, it is recommended that educational administrators implement a ‘Creative Teaching Certification Scheme’, incorporating students’ creative expression and interdisciplinary project-based learning into teacher evaluations. Specifically, classrooms should be refurbished intelligently, drawing inspiration from smart study concepts, including deploying IoT interactive whiteboards and smart desks to record students’ thought processes and collaborative efforts when tackling open-ended problems in real time. Concurrently, guided by the OECD’s Creative Thinking Framework, weekly dedicated sessions for open-ended problem-solving should be established in subjects such as mathematics, systematically encouraging pupils to propose and articulate multiple solution approaches. (2) Design tiered challenge tasks to establish a supportive efficacy cultivation system. Teachers should devise progressively layered learning tasks, breaking complex assignments into incremental sub-goals to ensure students experience success. Guided by self-efficacy theory, they ought to institute a “growth-oriented” assessment regime that privileges the innovative heuristics displayed during problem-solving over the mere correctness of the final answer, using process-focused praise to reinforce students’ emergent capability beliefs. (3) Standardize ICT usage and implement tiered technology integration strategies that empower users. Educational authorities must clarify the pedagogical role of technological tools at different cognitive levels and guide the application of ICT systematically from foundational operational practice towards higher-order enquiry activities. In order to create an inclusive and effective learning environment, differentiated instruction must be implemented systematically. Students with weaker mathematical foundations should be encouraged to use interactive tools for basic tasks to build confidence in technological application. Conversely, students with stronger mathematical abilities should be given access to advanced functionalities such as data analysis and algorithmic modeling to support innovative enquiry. (4) Construct a multidimensional support system to enhance mathematical problem-solving capabilities systematically. Enhancing pupils’ MPSA is a complex, systemic endeavors requiring a tripartite support network involving schools, families and society. At school, problem solving is integrated into daily teaching and reinforced through project-based learning. At the family level, an environment that fosters mathematical dialogue is encouraged, which encourages children to articulate their problem-solving approaches. At the societal level, mathematical practice activities are conducted, leveraging resources such as science museums and universities. Concurrently, teacher professional development is strengthened to equip educators with core strategies such as heuristic teaching, thereby establishing a comprehensive educational framework that bridges classroom learning to real-world application.

The current study focuses on the patterns that relate to adolescents’ ability to solve mathematical problems. It has sought to make exploratory innovations across three dimensions: the theoretical framework, the methodology and the practical application. Firstly, the present research proposes a moderated mediation model incorporating CSCE, CSE and MPSA, with the aim of integrating social cognitive theory and a constructivist perspective. By incorporating CSCE, CSE and ICTU into a unified analytical framework, the study preliminarily explores the causal pathway from environment to beliefs to competence, as well as the moderating mechanisms involved. Such exploration could provide a new analytical approach that addresses the limitations of traditional research, which tends to examine cognitive and non-cognitive factors in isolation. Secondly, drawing upon large-scale PISA assessment data from Korea, the study employs a refined subgroup analysis of gender and mathematical literacy, which not only validates the model’s applicability across diverse cultural contexts, but further reveals the heterogeneous patterns of associations across different groups. Notably, the suppression effect observed among female students and the gradient evolution of ICT moderation effects across varying performing levels provide robust empirical evidence for implementing differentiated educational policies. Thirdly, the present study proposes a targeted intervention approach that integrates motivational empowerment with technological adaptation, based on the dual roles of ICTU: motivational empowerment and cognitive interference. The concept transcends the traditional mindset that more technological application is always better, offering an operationalized, personalized pathway for the effective implementation of mathematics teaching in a digital context.
