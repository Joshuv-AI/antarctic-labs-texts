Performance of ultrasonography in diagnosis of calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease amongst Bangladeshi patients: an observational study
Authors: Farzana Shumy, Jannatul Ferdous, Ahmad Mursel Anam, George A. W. Bruyn, Zahid Amin, Minhaj Rahim Choudhury, Md Ismail Hosen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDue to protean presentations, poor sensitivity of radiographs and variable reliability of synovial fluid analysis, diagnosis of Calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (CPPD) is a challenge. This study addressed the diagnostic performance of musculoskeletal ultrasound (MSK-USG) in identifying CPPD features in hyaline cartilage, fibrocartilage, tendon, and synovial fluid. We compared ultrasound findings in CPPD to those in osteoarthritis (OA) and gout and healthy control.Materials & methodsIn this retrospective exploratory study, MSK-USG performed between February 2022 and July 2023 were reviewed. Patients retrospectively fulfilled the ACR/ EULAR classification criteria 2023 (score ≥ 56) were included (n = 16). Clinical phenotypes included acute CPP arthritis (44%), CPPD with OA (44%), and chronic CPP arthritis (12%). Ultrasound images were evaluated at common CPPD target sites: hyaline cartilage and fibrocartilage of the knee, wrist triangular fibrocartilage, metacarpophalangeal joints, joint capsules, tendons, and synovial membranes. Ultrasound images of CPPD cases were compared to those of OA, gout, and healthy controls. Sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values were calculated for Ultrasound features.ResultsCompared to healthy controls, Ultrasound features showed a sensitivity of 94.12% (CI: 71.31–99.85), specificity of 100% (CI: 86.77–100), positive predictive value of 100%, and negative predictive value of 99.59% in diagnosing CPPD. Ultrasound findings in hyaline cartilage showed a sensitivity & a specificity of 100% each. Fibrocartilage involvement showed a sensitivity of 93.75% and specificity of 86.67%. Whereas these figures were lowest for tendons with sensitivity of 12.5% (CI: 1.55–38.35) and specificity of 86.67% (CI: 69.28–96.24), respectively. Compared to OA, sensitivity and specificity were 100% and 93.75%, respectively. Specificity was lower when compared to gout (40%).ConclusionsMSK-USG demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy in identifying CPPD, especially in hyaline cartilage and fibrocartilage. While these findings support the use of Ultrasound as an adjunctive diagnostic tool, particularly in distinguishing CPPD from OA, the small sample size and retrospective design limit generalizability. Prospective studies with larger cohorts and crystal-confirmed cases are needed for validation.

Introduction

Calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (CPPD) is the third most common inflammatory arthritis amongst certain populations characterized by the deposition of calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystals mainly in the articular tissues, frequently in the fibrocartilage and hyaline cartilage [1–3].

Research in CPPD is always hindmost and no data exist on prevalence of CPPD from South Asia including Bangladesh in published literature. The heterogeneity of presentations, lack of sensitivity of radiographic imaging in diagnosis, variable credence of synovial fluid analysis makes the diagnosis of CPPD laborious [2, 4].

However, with the successful use of musculoskeletal ultrasound (MSK-USG) in the recent years, the new ACR-EULAR classification criteria 2023 for CPPD incorporated ultrasound in it [2, 4–6].

This exploratory study aimed to assess the diagnostic accuracy of MSK-USG in detecting CPPD features in hyaline cartilage, fibrocartilage, tendons, and synovial membrane in a Bangladeshi cohort. Findings were compared with those in age and sex-matched patients with osteoarthritis (OA), gout, and healthy controls.

Materials & methods

Patients & study design

In this retrospective exploratory study, all the MSK-USG scans performed from February 2022 to July 2023 in the rheumatology unit were retrieved and reviewed.

All patients retrospectively fulfilled the ACR/EULAR classification criteria 2023 for CPPD i.e., scoring more than 56 points were included [2]. Exclusion criteria were age < 18 years, a history of intraarticular corticosteroid injection within 3 months, or alternative diagnoses like rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

Clinical and laboratory data of the patients meeting the entry criteria as defined above (n = 16), were recorded from the electronic medical record of the hospital. The ultrasonographic features were largely retrieved from the ultrasound database of the department, except a few documented prospectively, more so of the controls. To distinguish sonographic features of CPPD, ultrasound images were compared with that of 30 healthy controls, 16 patients with OA and 5 patients with gout. Clinical diagnosis of OA was based on ACR criteria [7] and exclusion criteria were history of acute inflammatory arthritis, Prior identification of CPP crystals or urate crystals in synovial fluid, coexisting autoimmune diseases (e.g., RA, SLE). For gout group, patients were selected who fulfill 2015 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for gout [8]. Exclusion criteria were concomitant diagnosis or clinical suspicion of CPPD and absence of typical clinical features.

Clinical assessment

Acute CPP arthritis: The rapid development of severe joint pain, swelling and tenderness that reaches its maximum within 6–24 h, especially with overlying erythema, is highly suggestive of acute crystal inflammation though not specific for acute CPP crystal arthritis [3].

Chronic CPP crystal inflammatory arthritis presents as chronic oligoarthritis or polyarthritis with inflammatory symptoms and signs and occasional systemic symptoms (with elevation of C-reactive protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR); this may also include superimposed flares typical of crystal inflammation [3].

Ultrasound assessment

The scans of all the patients who fulfilled the ACR-EULAR classification criteria for CPPD were retrospectively evaluated independently by one rheumatologist and a radiologist. Both sonographers—the rheumatologist and radiologist—had formal training in MSK-USG, including certified workshops. Each had more than five years of experience performing MSK-USG, ensuring standardized acquisition and interpretation of ultrasound images.

To overcome the challenges of disagreement between the 2 sonographers, the Interobserver agreement was assessed by the ‘κ’appa coefficient.

Ultrasonographic images were obtained by a Samsung RS85 prestige machine equipped with a 14 − 7 MHz linear transducer.

The cartilage of the metacarpal heads from the second to the fifth finger was scanned longitudinally on the dorsal side of the hand with the fingers in maximum flexion. The cartilage of the femoral condyles was observed by transverse anterior scans with the knee in maximum flexion and posterior scans with the leg extended. The triangular ligament of the carpus was studied with multiplanar scans, rotating the probe around the ulnar compartment of the wrist [9]. The medial and lateral menisci of the knee were examined with multiplanar scans with the knee flexed at 30–45 and completely extended [9].

Definitions of ultrasound imaging features of CPPD in the various structures imaged are described as per validated published literature and mentioned under the following paragraphs [10].

Statistical analysis

The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the accuracy of the MSK-USG test for diagnosing patients with CPPD was calculated using the MedCalc web package [11] According to the MedCalc tool, the confidence intervals for sensitivity, specificity and accuracy are calculated using the exact Clopper-Pearson method. Additionally, the confidence intervals for the likelihood ratios are calculated using the & q Log method of Altman et al. 2000 [12].

The confidence intervals for the predictive values are calculated using the standard logit confidence intervals except when the predictive value is 0 or 100%, in which case a Clopper-Pearson confidence interval is reported [13].

For analysis, the disease prevalence used in this study is 7% for the CPPD vs. healthy control groups, 20% for the CPPD vs. OA group and 5% for the CPPD vs. gout group based on existing data.

Square Hospital ethical clearance committee approval was taken in prior for this study outlined as above. As this is retrospective study, the data were collected from electronic record. Human Ethics and Consent to Participate declarations: not applicable.

Discussion

Literature reports CPPD as a relatively common disease amongst elderly persons in certain populations and it is known to be characterized by deposition of CPP crystals in joint and periarticular tissues [9]. [14], Aging is a major risk factor for CPPD, which was reconfirmed in our study given the mean age of 65 years in our cohort [3]. However, due to lack of appropriate diagnostic test, it is difficult to diagnose CPPD and the exact prevalence and incidence is not known in most countries including Bangladesh [9]. Due to difficulties in diagnosis, chronic CPPD are usually diagnosed as seronegative RA by most medical practitioners and, therefore, the 2011 EULAR recommendation proposed CPPD as a differential diagnosis of seronegative RA and chronic inflammatory arthritis in the elderly population [3, 15]. Moreover, radiography does not exclude the diagnosis of CPPD and low sensitivity of crystals in synovial fluid due to interobserver variability and due to several other obstacles like small crystal size, weak or absent positive birefringence, difficulties in aspiration of joint fluid especially from small joints emphasize the importance of doing MSK-USG for the diagnosis of CPPD [2, 16]. In this study, synovial fluid analysis (SFA) was done only in eight patients and none of them were positive for CPP crystal and failure of detection of crystals might be due to lack of expertise for detection of crystal as well. In addition, the ‘‘shedding theory’’ of CPP also explains the low sensitivity of detection of crystals, as they primarily form in articular tissues rather than shedding into synovial space following diverse stimuli. That means, CPPD could be present in joint tissue much before crystals could be detected in SFA; therefore, imaging technique like ultrasound could identify these crystals better in tissues as was found in our study [6].

In this study, high sensitivity and specificity strengthen the case for MSK-USG as a diagnostic modality of CPPD disease, which also echo 2023 ACR/ EULAR classification criteria that give a major weightage to ultrasound features for diagnosing CPPD disease. Imaging and clinical features or identification of CPPD crystals from synovial fluid was sufficient for classification of CPPD when these are present [2]. Compared to healthy controls, sensitivity of MSK-USG for diagnosing CPPD in our study was 94.12%, while it was 100% when compared to OA and gout. The specificity, however, were 100%, 93.75% and 40% respectively, compared to healthy control, OA, and gout; and this is consistent with other published literature [17]. Other studies, however, found varied metrics such as specificity of 77.1% and sensitivity of 74.4% by Lee KA et al. We found hyaline cartilage calcification with 100% sensitivity and 93.75% specificity, which is consistent with some studies. Better visualization by parapatellar view and presence of effusion can further increase the visualization of hyaline cartilage. This specific ultrasound finding has high specificity because of its ability to differentiate various forms of crystal arthropathies [4]. Out of our 16 cases, synovial effusion was present in 15 cases. In this study, fibrocartilage involvement (Fig. 1A) showed a little bit lower sensitivity of 93.75% than hyaline cartilage (Fig. 1B). Whereas, in some studies fibrocartilage involvement is seen more than any other structure and underlying pathogenic aspects do support this observation as CPP crystals first deposit on fibrocartilage and only at a later stage, they deposit in hyaline cartilage [6, 9, 18]. Varus knee alignment that generally determines a medial meniscus extrusion from the joint line, help ultrasound to access a larger portion of the meniscus allowing a better identification of CPP crystals [14]. But there are complexities in identifying crystals in fibrocartilage, as the ligaments crossing these structures are hyperechogenic resembling crystals and thereby, making it sometimes difficult to identify [6].

Tendon involvement showed 12.5% sensitivity with 93.3% specificity indicating late involvement of tendon structure in the natural course of CPPD [1].

This study showed high positive predictive value (PPV) both in comparison to healthy controls and diseased controls namely OA at 100% and 80%, respectively; however, in comparison to gout, the PPV was only 8.06%. This high PPV indicate, MSK-USG can be used as a routine test to diagnose CPPD; on the other hand, very high negative predictive value (NPPV), found in this study while comparing to healthy control (99.59%), as well as in comparison with OA (100%) indicate that MSK-USG can be used as screening test for CPPD.

Regarding clinical phenotypes, acute CPP arthritis and OA with CPPD constituted 44% each of the CPPD cases in our study, whereas chronic CPP arthritis constituted only 12% of our cases. CPPD with OA is a frequent manifestation and it is a difficult subtype to be diagnosed, as some grade of OA changes do co-exist in the same joint. However, CPPD with OA is associated with more inflammatory features and the main difference is presence of effusion and higher white cell count in synovial fluid [3, 6, 16]. Our study also demonstrated similar findings.

One of the major strengths of our study includes demonstration of diagnostic performance of MSK-USG alone as a diagnostic tool. All the images are evaluated by both radiologist and rheumatologist. Despite the small sample size, especially for the gout, and the absence of crystal confirmation through synovial fluid analysis or histopathology, our findings support the diagnostic utility of MSK-USG, in line with the 2023 ACR/EULAR classification criteria. These results highlight the potential of MSK-USG as a frontline imaging modality for CPPD diagnosis, especially in resource-constrained settings where crystal analysis is not routinely available.

Our study does have several limitations. Our sample size is small. Despite being diagnosed as CPPD disease according to ACR-EULAR criteria 2023, none of the cases was proven by positive crystal analysis and histopathological data. Without histopathological analysis, it may not be possible to detect the exact nature of calcifications visualized by MSK-USG, especially for differentiation between basic calcium phosphate (BCP) and CPP crystals [4, 19]. Also we applied the new ACR-EULAR criteria largely on a retrospective cohort, necessitating a prospective validation study to prove our point that ultrasonography alone is a highly effective method to diagnose CPPD.

In conclusion, this exploratory study demonstrates that MSK-USG is a valuable bedside tool with excellent sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value in the CPPD, particularly in distinguishing it from OA. Among the anatomical sites evaluated, hyaline cartilage and fibrocartilage calcification showed the highest diagnostic accuracy, reinforcing its role as a key sonographic feature of CPPD. In contrast, tendon involvement was infrequently detected and demonstrated the lowest sensitivity, suggesting its limited utility in early diagnosis. Larger, prospective studies incorporating crystal-proven cases are needed to validate these findings and to further define the diagnostic role of MSK-USG in CPPD diagnosis.
