Assessing cardiovascular benefits of intensive blood pressure lowering in high‐risk undiagnosed HFpEF patients
Authors: Xinru Liu, Zhiyan Wang, Chang Hua, Yanfang Wu, Yangyang Tang, Yuling Xiong, Jingwei Liu, Jiaqi Zhang, Qiang Lv, Chao Jiang, Jianzeng Dong, Xin Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsHeart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is often underdiagnosed. This study evaluates the HFpEF‐ABA score's ability to identify high‐risk, undiagnosed HFpEF subgroups with elevated cardiovascular event rates and assesses the impact of intensive blood pressure control in these populations.MethodsA post‐hoc analysis of the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT) was performed. The HFpEF‐ABA score identified high‐risk individuals with undiagnosed HFpEF. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to examine interactions between HFpEF‐ABA score groups and intensive blood pressure control on major cardiovascular outcomes. The primary outcome was a composite of myocardial infarction (MI), acute coronary syndrome not resulting in MI, stroke, acute decompensated heart failure and cardiovascular disease death.ResultsAmong 9265 patients (mean age, 67.9 ± 9.4 years; 35.5% females), 559 primary outcomes occurred during a median follow‐up of 3.2 years. An HFpEF‐ABA score ≥ 90% was associated with a higher risk of the primary outcome [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR), 1.96 (1.57–2.44); P < 0.001]. When treated as a continuous variable, higher HFpEF‐ABA scores were independently associated with an increased risk of the primary composite outcome (P = 0.001), with a modest non‐linear relationship observed (P for non‐linearity = 0.040). In the intensive treatment group, the absolute reduction in primary outcomes was 5.0 per 1000 patient‐years for scores < 90% and 11.2 per 1000 patient‐years for ≥ 90%. Intensive blood pressure control reduced primary outcomes in both groups [<90%: aHR, 0.75 (0.62–0.90); ≥90%: aHR, 0.76 (0.51–1.13)] with no significant heterogeneity (P for interaction = 0.944). Serious adverse events did not increase in either group [<90%: aHR, 1.04 (0.96–1.11); ≥90%: aHR, 1.06 (0.88–1.28); P for interaction = 0.801].ConclusionsThe HFpEF‐ABA score identifies high‐risk patients with undiagnosed HFpEF who have elevated cardiovascular event rates and benefit from intensive blood pressure control without an increased risk of serious adverse events.

Introduction

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is a growing public health issue, with its prevalence rising due to an aging population and the increasing incidence of obesity, diabetes and hypertension. 1 Diagnosing HFpEF remains challenging, especially in ambulatory patients presenting with exertional dyspnoea, as symptoms and haemodynamic abnormalities often appear only during physical exertion. 2 , 3 Haemodynamic exercise testing has thus become the gold standard for confirming or ruling out HFpEF. 4 , 5 , 6 However, its widespread clinical application is limited by constraints in time, equipment and trained personnel. Community‐based studies suggest that a significant proportion of HFpEF cases remain undiagnosed. 7 The H2FPEF and HFA‐PEFF scores, developed and validated against clinical assessments, demonstrate high discriminatory power for identifying patients with unexplained dyspnoea and have been adopted in clinical trials. 5 , 8 However, these scores require echocardiographic measurements, which are not well‐standardized and are often unavailable in primary care settings or clinical trial cohorts. This limitation prompted the development of the HFpEF‐ABA score, which estimates the likelihood of HFpEF based on three universally accessible criteria: age, body mass index (BMI) and a history of atrial fibrillation (AF). 9

For years, trials in HFpEF yielded limited success in improving survival until recent breakthroughs with sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and finerenone. 10 SGLT2 inhibitors, such as dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, have shown significant benefits by reducing cardiovascular events and HF hospitalizations in HFpEF populations, establishing their role as a foundational treatment. 1 , 11 , 12 Similarly, the recent FINEARTS‐HF trial, which included patients with HFpEF and mildly reduced ejection fractions, showed that finerenone significantly reduced HF events and demonstrated a trend toward reducing cardiovascular mortality. 13 These improvements were achieved through mechanisms independent of blood pressure lowering. It remains unclear whether intensive blood pressure lowering could reduce cardiovascular events in patients with high HFpEF‐ABA scores, who are likely undiagnosed cases of HFpEF.

In our study, we aim to leverage the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT) database to apply the HFpEF‐ABA score for identifying patients at high risk for undiagnosed HFpEF. 9 Subsequently, we evaluated whether this high‐risk subgroup experiences cardiovascular benefits from intensive blood pressure lowering comparable to those observed in the remaining SPRINT population.

Methods

Data source

The research data for our study were obtained from the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute's BioLINCC data repository, which houses the SPRINT study dataset. Access to this dataset can be requested by researchers through the website (https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/home). The SPRINT was a randomized, open‐label clinical trial conducted at 102 sites across the United States, with approval from the respective institutional review boards. Detailed descriptions of the study design, methodology and primary findings have been previously published. 14 , 15 Participants aged ≥50 years with high cardiovascular risk and a screening SBP of 130 to 180 mmHg were included. High cardiovascular risk was defined as one of the following: clinical or subclinical cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, a Framingham 10 year cardiovascular disease risk score ≥15%, or age ≥75 years. The exclusion criteria included diabetes mellitus, previous stroke, heart failure and a diagnosis of dementia. A total of 9361 participants were randomized between November 2010 and March 2013 to achieve an SBP target of either <120 mmHg (intensive group, n = 4678) or <140 mmHg (standard group, n = 4683). The protocol recommended, but did not mandate, the use of drug classes with strong evidence for reducing cardiovascular outcomes, including thiazide‐type diuretics, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) and beta‐adrenergic blockers. The trial was terminated early in August 2015 due to significant benefits observed in major cardiovascular events. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment.

Ascertainment of AF

A digital 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG) was obtained at baseline using a GE MAC 1200 electrocardiograph (GE, Milwaukee, WI, USA). ECG interpretation was performed centrally at the Epidemiological Cardiology Research Center, Wake Forest School of Medicine (Winston‐Salem, NC, USA). AF was defined as the presence of AF on the baseline ECG or a self‐reported history of AF.

Calculation of the HFpEF‐ABA score

The HFpEF‐ABA score is a clinical model that estimates the probability of HFpEF based on age, BMI and history of AF (current or prior). The score is derived using a logistic regression equation to calculate the log odds: Log odds = −7.78875077116607 + 0.0625642747159337 (age) + 0.135149246234458 (BMI) + 2.04080564803471 (AF if present). 9

The HFpEF probability score is then calculated from log odds using the formula: P = 1/(1 + e^(−log odds)), resulting in a score ranging from 0% to 100%, with higher scores indicating a higher probability of HFpEF. For this analysis, participants were categorized into two groups based on their HFpEF‐ABA score: <90% and ≥90%.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint of our study was a composite of myocardial infarction (MI), acute coronary syndrome not resulting in MI (NONMIACS), stroke, acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) and cardiovascular disease death (CVD death). Secondary endpoints included the individual components of the primary composite, all‐cause mortality and a composite of the primary outcome or all‐cause mortality. The safety outcomes included serious adverse events and specific adverse events of hypotension, syncope, bradycardia, electrolyte abnormality, injurious fall and acute kidney injury. The definitions for primary, secondary and safety outcomes followed those outlined in the SPRINT study protocol. 14 For these analyses, only the first occurrence of each outcome was considered.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were described by treatment arm and baseline HFpEF‐ABA score. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Baseline characteristics were compared between treatment groups using t‐tests or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous variables and χ 2 tests for categorical variables. Primary and secondary outcomes in patients stratified by the HFpEF‐ABA score (<90% vs. ≥90%) were assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals. The primary and secondary outcomes, as well as safety outcomes, were adjusted for sex, race, baseline SBP, smoking status, history of cardiovascular disease and history of kidney disease. Restricted cubic splines were used to evaluate the linear association between the HFpEF‐ABA score and outcomes. The fully adjusted model included three knots at the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of the HFpEF‐ABA score. The homogeneity of treatment effects and safety outcomes between subgroups (HFpEF‐ABA score: <90% vs. ≥90%) was assessed by adding an interaction term to the statistical models, with significance determined by a likelihood ratio test. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2 and IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 9265 participants were included in this analysis after excluding 96 individuals with missing data on the HFpEF‐ABA score. Of the 9265 enrolled participants, 35.5% were female, with a mean age of 67.9 ± 9.4 years. Participants were categorized into two groups based on the HFpEF‐ABA score: <90% and ≥90%.

Baseline characteristics

At baseline, 834 participants (9.0%) had an HFpEF‐ABA score ≥90%. Among these, 442 (53.0%) were in the intensive treatment group, and 392 (47.0%) were in the standard treatment group. Baseline characteristics of participants with an HFpEF‐ABA score ≥90% and those with scores <90%, stratified by treatment group, are presented in Table 1. Overall, there were no significant baseline differences between the standard and intensive treatment groups in patients with an HFpEF‐ABA score ≥90%. Similarly, for patients with a score <90%, no notable baseline differences were observed between the two treatment groups.

Relationship between HFpEF‐ABA score and outcomes

During a median follow‐up of 3.2 years, 559 primary outcomes were recorded. Participants with an HFpEF‐ABA score ≥90% had a significantly higher incidence of the primary outcome after adjusting for sex, race, baseline SBP, smoking status, history of cardiovascular disease and kidney disease [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR), 1.96 (1.57–2.44)]. For secondary outcomes, all endpoints, except for NONMIACS, demonstrated a significantly higher risk in participants with an HFpEF‐ABA score ≥90%, compared with those with a score <90% (Figure 1).

When treated as a continuous variable, higher HFpEF‐ABA scores were independently associated with an increased risk of the primary composite outcome (P = 0.001), with a modest non‐linear relationship observed (P for non‐linearity = 0.040). Significant associations were also identified for stroke, ADHF, cardiovascular death, all‐cause mortality and the composite of the primary outcome or death (all P ≤ 0.001). A borderline association was noted for myocardial infarction (P = 0.039) while no significant association was found for NONMIACS events (P = 0.540) (Figure S1).

The efficacy of intensive blood pressure treatment for groups with HFpEF‐ABA score ≥90% and <90%

The absolute event reduction was 11.2 per 1000 patient‐years in patients with an HFpEF‐ABA score ≥90% and 5.0 per 1000 patient‐years in those with a score <90%, when comparing intensive versus standard treatment for the primary outcome (Figure 2). In participants with an HFpEF‐ABA score <90%, the HR for the intensive treatment group was 0.75 (95% CI, 0.62–0.90), adjusted for sex, race, baseline SBP, smoking status, prior cardiovascular disease and prior kidney disease. A similar trend was observed in the subgroup with an HFpEF‐ABA score ≥90% [HR 0.76 (95% CI, 0.51–1.13)]. No significant interaction was found between HFpEF‐ABA score groups and the risk of the primary outcome with intensive blood pressure‐lowering treatment (P for interaction = 0.944) (Figure 3). This result was consistent for secondary outcomes, including ADHF, cardiovascular death, all‐cause death and the composite outcome of primary events or death.

The safety of intensive blood pressure treatment for groups with HFpEF‐ABA score ≥90% and <90%

Serious adverse events occurred in 1536 participants (36.6%) in the intensive‐treatment group with an HFpEF probability <90%, and in 246 participants (55.7%) with an HFpEF probability ≥90%. Intensive blood pressure control did not significantly increase the risk of serious adverse events in either group [HR, 1.04 (95% CI, 0.96–1.11) for HFpEF <90% and 1.06 (95% CI, 0.88–1.28) for HFpEF ≥90%; P for interaction = 0.801], adjusted for sex, race, baseline SBP, smoking status, previous cardiovascular disease and previous kidney disease. However, intensive blood pressure control significantly increased the risk of hypotension, syncope, electrolyte abnormalities, and acute kidney injury (Figure 4).

Discussion

This post hoc analysis of the SPRINT study identified that participants with an HFpEF probability score of ≥90% were associated with an increased cardiovascular risk. Notably, this high‐risk subgroup experienced cardiovascular benefits from intensive blood pressure lowering comparable to those observed in other participants, suggesting that in individuals with HFpEF, aggressive blood pressure management offers therapeutic advantages in addition to the benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors and finerenone.

Overcoming HFpEF underdiagnosis: New insights from clinical tools

Diagnosing HFpEF remains a significant challenge. Selvaraj et al. reported that up to one‐third of patients with dyspnoea in the community may have unrecognized HFpEF contributing to their symptoms. 7 In a study by Wu et al., among 3727 consecutive patients with HF and a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥ 50%, only 8.3% received a clinician‐assigned diagnosis of HFpEF, while 75.4% met the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) criteria for HFpEF but did not have a formal diagnosis. 16 This underdiagnosis is attributed to several factors. First, in ambulatory patients presenting with exertional dyspnoea, symptoms and haemodynamic abnormalities often become apparent only during physical exertion. 2 , 3 Second, dyspnoea is a non‐specific symptom, frequently attributed to conditions such as lung disease, ageing, frailty, AF or obesity, particularly when patients are evaluated in non‐cardiology clinic settings without access to echocardiography or cardiology referral. 9

A recent article published in Nature Medicine noted that echocardiography measures accounted for only two out of nine possible points in estimating HFpEF probability using the H2FPEF score. 9 This led to the hypothesis that HFpEF likelihood might be reasonably estimated at the initial point of care using only clinical data, which could guide echocardiography and further testing. The authors derived and validated a new screening tool, the HFpEF‐ABA score, for diagnosing HFpEF in patients with dyspnoea, based on three universally available clinical metrics: age, BMI and a history of AF. 9 In contrast, the HFA‐PEFF score requires 11 variables, and the H2FPEF score requires six variables. 5 , 8 The HFpEF‐ABA score demonstrated good discriminatory power, with an area under the curve of 0.8399. 9 In our study, we also validated this score, finding that individuals with an HFpEF‐ABA score ≥90% are more likely to have undiagnosed HFpEF with elevated cardiovascular event rates.

Optimizing blood pressure control in HFpEF: Evidence and controversies

Hypertension is present in 90% or more of patients with HFpEF in current clinical trials. 17 , 18 However, current guidelines offer limited recommendations for blood pressure targets in patients with hypertension and HFpEF, and there is insufficient evidence to establish the efficacy and safety of blood pressure lowering in this group. 19 , 20 , 21 Although the efficacy of sacubitril–valsartan has been studied in patients with HFpEF, the blood pressure‐lowering effect observed in those studies was insufficient to achieve a target of <120/80 mmHg. 22 , 23 A meta‐analysis included a total of 10 RCTs involving 13 091 patients with HFpEF, demonstrating that the intervention group (SBP reduction from 134.7 to 130.2 mmHg) exhibited a significantly lower hospitalization rate for HF compared with the control group (SBP range of 134.4 to 133.3 mmHg). 24 These findings suggest that a modest reduction in SBP by 3 mmHg, targeting an SBP of approximately 130 mmHg, may be associated with a reduced hospitalization rate in HFpEF patients. 24 However, it remains unclear whether further reduction in SBP would yield additional benefits in this patient population. A recent observational study from the OPTIMIZE‐HF registry found that an SBP < 120 mmHg at discharge was associated with higher risks of HF readmission and all‐cause mortality, although it is limited by potential confounding factors and may reflect more advanced disease and lower cardiac output. 25 Another post‐hoc analysis of the SPRINT trial evaluated a small patient subgroup with incident ADHF and stratified them by LVEF. The analysis found no significant impact on the subsequent risk of all‐cause events or HF events across different LVEF groups. However, this study is limited by the small sample size of 69 HFpEF patients, which provides insufficient statistical power to draw definitive conclusions. 26 Our research supports the idea that intensive blood pressure reduction in high‐risk HFpEF patients can offer cardiovascular benefits. However, it is essential to closely monitor for potential serious adverse events, including hypotension, syncope, electrolyte imbalances and acute kidney injury, to ensure patient safety.

Pathophysiological links between hypertension and HFpEF

Hypertension remains the most common comorbidity in HFpEF patients, 27 , 28 with complex mechanisms linking it to HFpEF, including microvascular dysfunction (oxidative stress, increased inflammation, endothelial dysfunction and fibrosis), macrovascular dysfunction and structural heart disease. 29 , 30 , 31 Inadequate hypertension control may lead to left ventricular hypertrophy, left atrial remodelling, diastolic dysfunction, abnormal ventricular arterial coupling and end‐organ damage; it has been conjectured that blood pressure control could be related to improvement in outcomes. 22 , 30 , 32 , 33 β‐blockers, diuretics, calcium channel blockers and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors have shown significant left ventricular hypertrophy regression. 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 The SPRINT‐HEART study demonstrated modest reversal of left ventricular concentric remodelling with intensive blood pressure control, 38 and the VALIDD trial highlighted improvements in diastolic function markers with effective hypertension management, regardless of the medication used. 39 Although the exact mechanism is unclear, Solomon et al. found that improvements in annular relaxation velocity, a marker of left ventricle diastolic dysfunction, were correlated with the degree of SBP reduction. Notably, patients achieving the lowest SBP levels showed the highest final diastolic relaxation velocities. These findings may provide support for our study. 32

Limitations

We hypothesize that individuals with an HFpEF‐ABA Score ≥90% are at high risk for undiagnosed HFpEF. Our findings show that this score correlates with increased cardiovascular risk in the SPRINT cohort. However, it has not yet been validated against a gold‐standard diagnostic method to confirm HFpEF diagnosis in these patients. Due to limitations in the database, data on NT‐proBNP and relevant echocardiographic parameters, such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and longitudinal left ventricular function, were not available to assess their association with increased cardiovascular risk. 40 Furthermore, the SPRINT study excluded certain high‐risk populations, such as individuals with diabetes, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the effects of medications beyond blood pressure lowering may have influenced the study outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this post hoc analysis of the SPRINT trial demonstrates that the HFpEF‐ABA Score serves as a valuable tool for identifying participants at high risk for undiagnosed HFpEF. Intensive blood pressure lowering provided similar cardiovascular benefits in this subgroup of patients as observed in the broader cohort. However, further studies are needed to determine the optimal SBP treatment targets in patients with HFpEF.
