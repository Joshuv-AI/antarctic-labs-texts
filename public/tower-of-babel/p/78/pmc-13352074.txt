Burden and risk of elderly atopic dermatitis in Europe: A 2021 global burden of disease analysis☆
Authors: Zhengyang Zhou, Hongxing Wang, Bin Yang, Xiaoyu Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe burden of atopic dermatitis (AD) in elderly is increasing recently, so it is necessary to deepen the recognition.MethodsWe extracted prevalence, incidence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for elderly-AD in Europe used Global Burden of Disease (GBD) and analyzed its related factors.ResultsThe burden of elderly-AD in Europe increased significantly from 1990 to 2021 (changed prevalence: 2.09%; changed DALYs: 11.58%) and females are higher than males. From the perspective of regional heterogeneity, the burden of elderly-AD in Western Europe is much heavier than other areas. Socio-demographic index (r = 0.497, P < 0.001), high alcohol use (r = 0.50, P < 0.001) and high red meat diet (r = 0.46, P < 0.001) are positively related to the incidence of elderly-AD, while low nuts and seeds intake (r = −0.50, P < 0.001), low seafood omega-3 fatty acids diet (r = −0.74, P < 0.001), low omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids diet (r = −0.43, P < 0.001) and high sodium diet (r = −0.62, P < 0.001) are negatively related to the incidence rate of elderly-AD.ConclusionThe burden of elderly-AD is relatively high level in Europe with regional and gender disparities, and dietary habits are probably contributor.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is the most common chronic inflammatory skin disease with relapsing course across the globe.1, 2, 3 Current research shows that the global prevalence of AD is as high as 2.6%, and the number of people affected by AD exceeds 200 million.4 The considerable prevalence and extensive patient burden of AD, coupled with its hallmark severe pruritus and complex symptomatology, contribute not only to marked deterioration in quality of life and psychological well-being but also impose multifaceted challenges on public health infrastructure and societal resource allocation.5, 6, 7

In recent years, elderly-AD has garnered increasing public attention due to its distinct and multifaceted nature.8,9 Typically, patients with AD who are over the age of 60 or 65 are clinically classified as elderly-AD.10,11 Rather than manifesting with lesions confined to the creases of the folds, elderly-AD patients commonly present with lichenified eczema in the antecubital and popliteal fossae.12,13 Furthermore, compared with other age groups of AD patients, elderly-AD patients have a more severe trend of rash and itching, and are more likely to develop into serious erythroderma.14 The treatment process of elderly-AD patients is also prone to adverse reactions.10 With the increasing severity of population aging, the burden of elderly-AD on individuals, families, and socio-economic will gradually increase. Therefore, it is imperative to foster a more comprehensive understanding of AD in the elderly population.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database is a global resource on disease and health, developed jointly by the World Health Organization (WHO), the World Bank, and other institutions. Its goal is to bring together worldwide data on disease burden, risk factors, and health trends, thereby supplying a scientific foundation for research, policy-making, and public health interventions. At the core of its approach is the assessment of how diseases affect population health and the use of metrics such as prevalence, incidence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) to quantify the overall burden of disease.

This research provides a detailed analysis of elderly-AD across Europe by extracting prevalence, incidence and DALYs for elderly-AD in Europe from 1990 to 2021 in GBD database, offering systematic estimates of elderly-AD burden in this region. The influence of social economic development and dietary-related factors on the burden of elderly-AD are also thoroughly elaborated in this study.

Materials and methods

Data source

The GBD 2021 study provides comprehensive information on the burden of diseases, injuries, and related factors across different age groups and genders from 1990 to 2021, covering 371 diseases and injuries in 204 countries and territories.15,16 To ensure such breadth and reliability, it integrates data from multiple sources, including scholarly publications, disease registries, household surveys, and health service utilization records. Detailed descriptions of the GBD methods, such as the search and selection process, as well as the burden estimation process, can also be found in previous publications.15,16 Consequently, by leveraging this invaluable resource, we can track historical trends and anticipate future patterns, assess disease burden temporally from retrospective perspectives, and generate evidence that informs health policy decisions.

According to the GBD 2021 study, AD is classified under the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10: L20). In this study, we obtained the data for European regions (western Europe, central Europe, and eastern Europe) and 44 countries at the website (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). The disease burden estimation conducted in this study utilized the following measures: incidence, prevalence, DALYs, socio-demographic index (SDI), and summary exposure value (SEV). All estimates were accompanied by 95% uncertainty intervals (UI), which were calculated by conducting 1000 draws from the posterior distribution of each estimate, thereby producing the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile points. These standard measures of AD enable the identification of patterns in occurrence, progression, and decline across populations, thereby enhancing our understanding of collective disease dynamics, distribution trends, and underlying determinants.

Joinpoint regression model

Traditional regression approaches estimate an overall time-trend across the study period but cannot reveal localized changes. In contrast, the Joinpoint regression model detects statistically significant inflection points (“joinpoints”), partitions the series into linear segments, and estimates the annual percent change (APC) for each segment.17 This method was extensively utilized in analyzing temporal trend in disease burden.18, 19, 20

Model form was selected according to the outcome distribution: for approximately normally distributed outcomes with sufficiently large samples we used a linear specification y = α+βx+ε; for outcomes following exponential or Poisson behavior (such as rates), we used a log-linear specification ln(y) = α+βx+ε. Here, α is the intercept, β the slope, and ε the error term. In this study, we employed the grid search method for analysis. The optimal number of joinpoints was chosen via the permutation test, comparing models between a prespecified minimum and maximum number of joinpoints; when 2 models were not statistically distinguishable, the model with fewer joinpoints was preferred Segment-specific trends were summarized as APC, calculated as APCi=(exp(βi)-1) × 100%. An overall summary measure, the average annual percent change (AAPC), was computed as a duration-weighted mean of segment slopes: AAPC= (exp (∑iβiWi/∑iWi) −1) × 100%, where Wi denotes the length (years) of segment i.

In simpler terms, the Joinpoint regression model acts as a "trend-change detector.", particularly useful for identifying shifts or changes in trends within data. The Joinpoint approach identifies specific years (inflection points) where a trend significantly shifts. Through dividing the data into segments and calculating trends for each segment, the model helps to highlight important turning points that might otherwise be overlooked. This allows us to more precisely identify phase changes within the data, revealing potential effects of policy, shifts in social behavior, or changes in disease patterns, thereby providing a more reliable foundation for understanding the severity of disease.

Correlation and ecological analysis

The SDI is a composite indicator of income, educational attainment, and fertility, with higher values indicating more advanced socio-demographic development. We linked country-year SDI to AD metrics (prevalence, incidence, and DALYs) for 44 European countries from 1990 to 2021. Bivariate associations were estimated using Pearson correlations with r and P-values, as well as visualized with scatter plot and non-parametric smoother. In the ecological analyses, dietary related factors were expressed as SEV (%). Their associations with AD incidence were then evaluated using unweighted Pearson correlations, case-weighted correlations, and inverse-variance-weighted correlations. To accommodate potential non-linearity and confounding, we fit generalized additive models with spline terms for SEV. Moreover, multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for the false discovery rate (FDR).

Statistics and visualization

The statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.4.1) and Joinpoint Regression Program (version 4.9.1.0). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered as the statistical significance.

Results

Elevation burden in AD among elderly population across European regions

We firstly utilize the latest GBD dataset to analyze the age-standardized DALYs rates across different continents including Africa, America, Asia and Europe. Our findings show that Europe has the highest age-standardized DALYs for AD in 2021, with a significant upward trend from 1990 to 2021, but other continents do not have this characteristic (Fig. S1).

Across Europe and its 3 regions (Western, Central, and Eastern Europe), AD prevalence and incidence are low during middle age but rise sharply in the elderly (Fig. 1). Specifically, the prevalence and incidence of AD in the elderly begin to rise after the age of 55, particularly the prevalence peaks at age 80–84 and the incidence at 70–74, both male and female have similar upward trends. In addition, the elderly-AD prevalence and incidence are above overall level of Europe in Western Europe but below it in Central Europe and Eastern Europe in both sexes. Trends in elderly-AD incidence and prevalence in Europe with gender differences

In the whole Europe, the incidence of elderly-AD (aged 55+ years) shows a fluctuating decline from 1990, with a marked reduction from 2001 to 2011(APC = −0.40, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). After 2011, elderly-AD incidence rose until 2019 (APC = 0.25, P < 0.001), then drop sharply from 2019 to 2021 (APC = −4.04, P < 0.001). Interestingly, gender differences in incidence were evident in the early 1990s, with higher rates in female, but the difference gradually diminished over time. However, prevalence increased markedly from 1995 to 2000 (APC = 0.80, P < 0.001) and remained high with minor fluctuations thereafter (Fig. 2E). In Central Europe, the incidence remained relatively stable from 1990 to 2019 (APC = 0.01, P < 0.001), followed by a sharp decline from 2019 to 2021 (APC = −6.67, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Prevalence stayed high and stable throughout the study period (Fig. 2F). Female consistently exhibited higher level in both incidence and prevalence than male, but both sexes have a decrease in incidence after 2019 until 2021 (Female APC = −6.51, P < 0.001; Male APC = −6.78, P < 0.001) without obvious change in prevalence.

In Eastern Europe, the incidence initially increased during the 1990s but began to decline after 2000, with a significant drop after 2019 (APC = −8.11, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2C). Similarly, the prevalence rose during the 1990s to nearly 1.25%, declined from 2000 to 2006 (APC = −0.87, P < 0.001), stabilized in the following years with a high level, and showed a really slight decline after 2019 (APC = −1.22, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2G). As in other regions, female has higher incidence and prevalence than male in Eastern Europe throughout the whole study period.

Similar with the overall European trend, the incidence of AD among elderly in Western Europe showed a decline started in 1990, with a pronounced reduction between 1998 and 2010 (APC = −0.60, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2D). After that, there is a slight increase from 2010 to 2014 (APC = 0.19, P = 0.044) and much more significant increase from 2014 to 2019 (APC = 0.72, P < 0.001), finally dropped from 2019 to 2021 (APC = −1.75, P < 0.001). In particular, the incidence rate of elderly AD in male in Western Europe has been higher than that in female since 1990. This pattern contrasts with the overall European trend, where females generally exhibit higher incidence rates, and differs from observations in Central Europe and Eastern Europe as well, highlighting notable regional heterogeneity in elderly AD incidence. It is worth pointing that the prevalence rate of elderly-AD patients in Western Europe rose slightly alongside tiny fluctuations between 1990 and 2021 (Fig. 2H), staying in a high level all the time. Interestingly, the prevalence is higher in female population than male in this region, which is contrast with the observed sex difference in incidence. This discrepancy likely reflects demographic characteristics, for example, the longer life expectancy of females, which leads to a higher cumulative prevalence of AD among female in older age.

In conclusion, both the prevalence and incidence rate of elderly-AD in the whole Europe, including Western Europe, Central Europe and Eastern Europe, are still at a high level. Although the incidence has declined over time, especially from 2019 to 2021, which may reflect missed diagnosis, delayed reporting, increased mortality caused by the pandemic among elder population during the COVID-19 pandemic.21 However, the prevalence shows no decrease and instead a slight increase from the perspective of whole study period, indicating that the burden of elderly-AD remains serious and has not been effectively alleviated.

Heterogeneity in incidence and prevalence of elderly-AD across European regions

Across distinct European regions, the Western Europe (1.763% [95%UI 1.638 to 1.899]) has the highest prevalence of elderly-AD, followed by Eastern Europe (0.996% [95%UI 0.949 to 1.048]) and the lowest in Central Europe (0.545% [95%UI 0.507 to 0.584]) (Table S1). Specifically, countries like France (2.308% [95%UI 2.079 to 2.563]), Denmark (2.1% [95%UI 1.854 to 2.366]), Italy (2.019% [95%UI 1.911 to 2.132]) have significantly higher prevalence than other Western European countries. In Eastern Europe, represented by Estonia (1.459% [95%UI 1.379 to 1.546]) and the Russian Federation (1.136% [95%UI 1.082 to 1.194]), has a higher prevalence rate than the rest of this region. Conversely, Central Europe generally has a generally low prevalence. Across the whole Europe, female prevalence is generally higher than male, particularly in Western Europe (such as France, Italy, United Kingdom, Spain, Finland and Sweden) (Fig. 3A), where female prevalence ranges from 1.8% to 3.0%, compared to 1.2%–1.8% in male.Fig. 3Geographic distribution of AD prevalence and incidence among elderly aged 55+ years in Europe by sex in 2021. Incidence rates (Fig. 3B) exhibited a similar regional pattern to prevalence rates, with the highest rates in Western Europe (0.05% [95%UI 0.045 to 0.056]) (such as France, Spain, Denmark, Italy and the United Kingdom), intermediate rates in Eastern Europe (0.026% [95%UI 0.023 to 0.028]), and the lowest rates in Central Europe (0.021% [95%UI 0.019 to 0.024]) (such as Romania, Montenegro) (Table S2). But unlike prevalence rates, there are no significant gender differences in incidence rates across all 3 regions, indicating a more even distribution of new cases between males and females.

We analyzed the relationship between AD prevalence, incidence, DALYs and the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) in elderly in different European countries (Fig. 4). The results revealed a positive correlation between SDI and all 3 rates (prevalence, incidence, and DALYs) in Europe, indicating that higher socio-demographic development is associated with increased burden of elderly-AD. The incidence of elderly-AD demonstrates a moderately strong positive correlation with SDI (r = 0.497, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). In Western European countries, both the initial SDI and elderly-AD incidence levels are relatively high. The incidence generally declined as SDI increased, followed by a slight rebound, and ultimately decreased again once the SDI reached a relatively high level (after 2019, SDI>0.8 to 0.9). In contrast, Central and Eastern European countries show little change in incidence despite rising SDI, with levels remaining consistently low and stable.

The trends for prevalence and DALYs are similar to those of incidence, showing a moderately strong positive correlation with SDI (Prevalence: R = 0.551, P < 0.001; DALYs: R = 0.55, P < 0.001). However, a key distinction is that whether the country is in Western Europe, Central Europe or Eastern Europe, both the prevalence and DALYs fail to decline alongside the reduction in incidence. Instead, they show a slight increase with higher SDI level. Anyway, the results indicate that the burden of elderly-AD remains high throughout Europe, with a slight upward trend.

Our analysis highlights the complex linkage between SDI and the burden of elderly-AD across the whole Europe. Although much more advanced levels of social economic development may contribute to mitigating elderly-AD incidence due to factors like better healthcare systems, early interventions, improved public awareness or something else, the prevalence and DALYs do not decrease at the same time, conversely instead increase. In short, the disease burden of AD in the elderly constitutes a continuous and incremental challenge to public health across the Europe.

Dietary and behavioral related factors for elderly-AD incidence in Europe

A cross-sectional ecological analysis is conducted to examine the relationship between dietary habits and the incidence of elderly-AD in Europe in 2021 (Fig. 5). The analysis result reveals that SEV of high alcohol use (r = 0.50, P = 0.000568) (Fig. 5A) and diet high in red meat (r = 0.46, P = 0.00161) (Fig. 5C) are significantly positively associated with European elderly-AD incidence with strong biological plausibility, suggesting that these may be intervention factors worthy of priority attention. In contrast, SEV of diet low in seafood omega-3 fatty acids (r = −0.74, P = 9.72e-09) (Fig. 5D), omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (r = −0.43, P = 0.00359) (Fig. 5E), nuts and seeds (r = −0.50, P = 0.000485) (Fig. 5B) are negatively associated with elderly-AD incidence. Furthermore, high sodium intake exhibited a similar to inverted U-shaped relationship but not a linear relationship (r = −0.62, P = 8.83e-06) (Fig. 5F), the incidence rate peak is around the medium SEV (about 40%) and is lower at both ends, suggesting complex interactions within the context of dietary structure. In short, different countries have varying incidence levels of elderly-AD due to differences in dietary habits and food intake. Perhaps this also reflects the difference in economy and geographical location or other factors, which leads to the difference in the burden of elderly-AD. Disscussion

This study analyzes the burden of AD among adults aged 55+ years in Europe, and further examines socioeconomic development level (measured by SDI), as well as dietary status, both of which may significantly influence this burden. We defined elderly-AD cases as those occurring in adults aged 55+ years, rather than using the clinical threshold of 60 years, because our findings indicate that the burden of AD begins to rise from the age of 55 in Europe compared to middle age.

We clearly demonstrate region-specific patterns in the burden of AD among European elderly. Prevalence and incidence rise sharply after mid-life, with female showing persistently higher burden than male but gender differences have narrowed over time. It is also worth pointing that the heterogeneity between different regions of Europe. In detail, the Western Europe carries the heaviest burden, followed by Eastern Europe, while Central Europe maintains relatively low and stable levels. Most importantly, although the incidence of AD among the elderly in Europe declined especially during the latest years, the prevalence remains at a persistently high level even has an upward trend, indicating that the burden of AD in older population continues to be substantial and serious.

The relationship between social development level and the burden of elderly-AD is also elaborated in this research. The positive association analysis between SDI and elderly-AD burden suggests that socioeconomic development initially increases the incidence of elderly-AD, but at higher levels of development, the incidence of elderly-AD in Europe seems that tend to decline slightly, likely reflecting improved healthcare systems, preventive measures and public awareness. Interestingly, especially in Western European countries, the most developed part of the whole Europe, the prevalence rate of elderly-AD is still at a high level and has a slight upward trend, which seems to contradict the decline of incidence.

Moreover, our study suggests that the dietary patterns and habits, particularly high alcohol consumption, high red meat intake, and deficiencies in seafood-derived fatty acids, nuts and seeds, playing as potential contributors to regional differences in elderly-AD incidence. These dietary factors may influence the progression of AD through various mechanisms. Excessive alcohol consumption may exacerbate AD symptoms by promoting skin inflammation, immune activation, and damaging the skin barrier. Additionally, high alcohol use has been shown to elevate serum IgE level,22 which may contribute to the increased risk of elderly-AD as well. Additionally, excessive intake of red meat leads to the accumulation of N-glycolylneuraminic acid, a substance that humans cannot synthesize but is abundantly present in red meat.23 This may induce alterations in the gut microbiome, compromise the integrity of the gut epithelial barrier, and impair both systemic and local immune tolerance. Through the gut-skin axis, such changes may disrupt the skin barrier and homeostasis, thus promoting the progression of AD via enhancing skin inflammation and immune responses24, 25, 26. These may partly explain the observed association between diet high in red meat and elderly-AD. Furthermore, omega-3 and omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids are essential for maintaining skin barrier integrity. A deficiency in these fatty acids can impair the skin barrier, leading to dryness, inflammation and itching, which are common symptoms of AD.27,28 A diet rich in these fatty acids may help improve skin barrier function and reduce the likelihood of developing AD. Moreover, sodium, one of the most frequently consumed elements in human daily diet, has been implicated in the increased risk of AD if people have a high sodium diet.29 Sodium content in lesional skin of AD patients is notably elevated compared to healthy skin, and NaCl has been identified as an ionic checkpoint that influences type 2 immunity with potential clinical relevance for AD.30 Therefore, reducing dietary sodium intake may serve as a cost-effective, low-risk strategy for managing AD, potentially improving clinical outcomes with minimal side effects.

It is worth pointing that a certain degree of heterogeneity may exist due to differences in data aggregation, diagnostic approaches and reporting standards across different areas and countries. Even so, the present analysis provides a valuable and comprehensive perspective on the burden of AD among the elderly in Europe, and the insights remain meaningful greatly.

Taken together, our findings not only elaborate on the burden and regional heterogeneity of elderly-AD, but also emphasize the dual role of social development and lifestyle factors in shaping the burden of elderly-AD in Europe. While the decline in incidence may partly reflect advances in social economic conditions, preventive strategies, healthier diets, and more equitable healthcare access, our analysis reveals that prevalence has remained persistently high level even alongside an upward trend. Perhaps the sustained burden underscores the pressing need for long-term, multidimensional approaches to effectively mitigate the impact of AD among aging European populations.
