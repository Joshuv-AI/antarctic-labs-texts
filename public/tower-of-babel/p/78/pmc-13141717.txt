Limited wing extension is associated with woody breast in broilers and their inability to self-right
Authors: Cirenio Hisasaga, Maja M Makagon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe fact that woody breast (WB) is currently detected only post-mortem limits our understanding of this economically relevant broiler myopathy. We aimed to determine whether restricted wing movement could serve as a non-invasive indicator of WB in live birds, and whether it would affect a broiler’s ability to self-right. We tested 58 fast growth broilers across three commercial farms within two days of market age (45-52 days of age). Broilers were categorized based on the distance between the glenohumeral joints measured to the nearest half centimeter across the back while the wings were extended backwards: complete extension (0 cm), moderate wing partition (1.5-5 cm) and pronounced wing partition (≥ 5.5 cm). Each broiler was then placed on its back, and time to self-right was recorded (up to 60 s maximum). Post-mortem palpation of the Pectoralis major muscle was used to assess WB severity. Moderate or pronounced wing partition detected WB with an accuracy of 93.1%, sensitivity of 95.5%, specificity of 85.7%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 95.5%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 85.7%. Pronounced wing partition detected severe WB with an accuracy of 82.8%, sensitivity of 84.6%, specificity of 81.3%, PPV of 78.6%, and NPV of 86.7%. Self-righting was affected by both wing partition and WB presence and severity. Broilers with a pronounced wing partition were less likely to self-right than those with no or moderate wing partition (one-tailed Fischer’s Exact Test, p < 0.05). All 14 broilers without WB successfully righted themselves whereas only 6 of 26 broilers with severe WB were able to do so. The median times to self-right differed by WB severity (Kruskal-Wallis χ² = 24.36, p < 0.05; 1.36 s, 1.86 s, and 60 s for broilers without WB, with moderate, and with severe WB, respectively). Our findings show that wing partition is an accurate, non-invasive indicator of WB in live birds. Furthermore, pronounced wing partition is associated with impaired self-righting ability reflecting a bird welfare concern.Keywords: Broiler, Self-right, Wing extension, Wing partition, Woody breast

Introduction

Woody or wooden breast (WB) is a myopathy that poses an economic challenge to the broiler industry from a decrease in breast yield and quality (Kuttappan et al., 2016). The exact cause of WB is unknown but seems to be associated with fast growth rate and high breast yield (Sihvo et al., 2017; Caldas-Cueva and Owens, 2020). Post-mortem palpation of the Pectoralis major muscle for hardness is the common method for assessing WB (Caldas-Cueva and Owens, 2020). The development of alternatives to palpation, such as instrumental (Mudalal et al., 2015; Soglia et al., 2017), imaging (Chao et al., 2010; Hanning et al., 2018), and histological (Kuttappan et al., 2013; Soglia et al., 2016) measures have focused primarily on post-mortem assessments. Meanwhile, the ability to detect WB in live broilers is critical for furthering research on the development and mitigation of this myopathy.

Limited wing extension has been identified as a possible indicator of WB in live birds (Kawasaki et al., 2016). A relationship between WB and compromised wing movement may exist as the Pectoralis major, along with the Supracoracoideus muscle, is involved in the extension and flexion of the wings. Specifically, the contraction of the Pectoralis major muscle, and relaxation of the Supracoracoideus muscle, results in a downward wing movement. While an upward wing movement is a result of the relaxation of the Pectoralis major muscle and contraction of the Supracoracoideus muscle (Butler, 1991; Beaufrère, 2009; Biewener, 2011). WB compromises the function of the Pectoralis major muscle and has been suggested to contribute to a constant contraction of the muscle (Gall et al., 2019). Therefore, limited wing movement could be evidence of WB presence. This relationship is supported by preliminary findings from Kawasaki et al. (2016) who reported that broilers affected with WB have limited wing abduction (extension) whereas those without myopathy of the Pectoralis major muscle were able to extend their wings to achieve back-to-back contact. However, these findings were based on a sample size of three, limiting the conclusions that can be made.

If WB limits wing extension, it could also limit a bird’s ability to execute behaviors that rely on wing use, such as self-righting. Although this relationship has not been tested directly, Che et al. (2023) compared presence of WB in live broilers that were found on their backs (in a dorsal recumbent position) and randomly selected upright birds. WB was moderately associated with having been found in a dorsal recumbent position. The authors noted that most broilers found on their backs had limited wing extension whereas randomly selected broilers (control) were able to fully abduct their wings backwards; however, wing extension was not quantified. Thus, the relationship between WB, wing extension and ability to self-right remains unclear.

Our study aimed to determine whether limited wing extension could serve as an indicator of WB in live market age broilers, and to clarify the relationship between limited wing extension, WB and ability to self-right. Based on the preliminary findings from Kawasaki et al. (2016) and Che et al. (2023), we hypothesized that limited wing extension would be indicative of the presence of WB, and that pronounced limitation in wing movement would be indicative of severe WB and inability to self-right.

Materials and methods

Housing and animals

Study methods were approved by the UC Davis IACUC. A total of 58 broilers from four barns located across three different commercial farms were evaluated. All farms were located in the same geographical region (Central Valley, California) and were owned by the same company and managed under the same general management protocols. The housing structures were curtain-sided with several waterlines and feedlines running across the houses and the floor lined with wood shavings as litter.

Eleven broilers were collected from Farm A at 48 d of age, 26 from Farm B at 52 d, and 21 from Farm C at 45 d. Broilers from Farms A and B were at market age, whereas those from Farm C were within two days of reaching market age. Broilers represented three different fast-growth strains which could not be visually distinguished. Therefore, the sampled birds were treated as a single mixed-strain population.

Birds were selected for inclusion based on a qualitative assessment of wing extension meaning the degree to which they could extend their wing backwards while standing upright. We aimed to collect a balanced number of broilers from each of three wing extension categories at each barn: broilers able to touch wings behind their backs, able to rotate their wings behind their back but unable to touch the wings together, or unable to rotate wings behind their backs. Initially, birds were selected using catch frames. However, few broilers with a pronounced wing partition were identified in this manner. Subsequently, we walked the length of the barn and randomly selected birds from various transects which were delineated by the drinker and feeder lines (see: Marchewka et al., 2013), and from different distances from the entry door. Broilers with signs of lethargy or visible health issues such as swelling of the head, eyes, comb, or wattles were excluded based on exclusion criteria that were established before the start of the study. Data collection commenced once 8-12 broilers representing birds from all three wing extension categories were identified and placed in a catch pen at the front of the barn. The wingspan test and flip over test were conducted inside the barn adjacent to the holding pen; WB assessment was carried out outdoors.

Wingspan test

In a standing position, each broiler’s wings were extended backwards to obtain a wing partition distance (Fig. 1). One researcher placed their hands along the bird’s axillary region and raised the wings dorsally until the wings reached the point of resistance. The point of resistance was defined as the point past which the wings would not move even when additional pressure was applied. A second researcher measured the partition between the glenohumeral joints in the wings to the nearest 0.5 cm using a ruler. Wingspan was categorized as: wing partition 0 cm (wings completely touched when pulled backwards), moderate wing partition (wings that did not touch and with a partition of 1.5-5 cm), or pronounced wing partition (wings did not touch; ≥ 5.5 cm partition). None of the birds had a wing partition measuring 1 cm (Fig. 2); therefore, 1 cm wing partition was not included in any of the categories. These cut-off values were based on preliminary data collection, which confirmed that multiple measurements of wing partition on the same bird yielded similar values (within 0.5 cm), and equated a partition of approximately 6 cm as representing birds that were unable to move their wings above their backs.

Self-righting test

Following the wingspan test, each bird was placed gently flat on its back onto the litter and immediately released. The time (sec) it took the bird to self-right was recorded. Broilers who were not able to self-right within a minute received a maximum time of 60 sec. The test was conducted by a single individual (C.H.) inside the barn and timed by an assistant. Following the test, each broiler was tagged with a numbered zip tie for identification purposes and placed back into the holding pen with the other 8-12 individuals.

Breast assessment

Birds were euthanized by cervical dislocation after all birds from the holding pen completed the wingspan and flip over tests. The carcasses were moved to an outdoor dissection station and examined in random order. The left breast muscle was removed, weighed and assessed for WB based on tactile assessment using a three-point modified version of Tijare et al. (2016). Breasts were classified as being normal (no WB) or having moderate or severe WB. Normal breasts displayed no hardening. Breasts that displayed a hardening in the cranial region were classified as having moderate WB, and those displaying hardening in the medial and caudal region were classified as having severe WB. WB assessment was performed by a single trained individual (C.H.). For the purpose of obtaining inter-rater reliability, a second assessor (M.M.) independently evaluated breast samples from 12 broilers, with 100% agreement between evaluators.

Data analysis

Following Tracy et al. (2019), we calculated the accuracy (percent of true positives and true negatives), specificity (percent of true negative), sensitivity (percent of true positive), positive predictive value (PPV; ratio of true positives over the true and false positives), and negative predictive value (NPV; ratio of true negatives over the true and false negatives) of using moderate (partition of 1.5-5 cm) to pronounced wing partition (partition of ≥ 5.5 cm) as an indicator of WB and using pronounced wing partition as an indicator of severe WB. A one tailed Fisher’s Exact Test was conducted to evaluate the hypothesis that birds with pronounced wing partition had a higher likelihood of being unable to self-right. A Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare median self-righting times among birds with no WB, and those with moderate and severe WB. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with a Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons

Breast weight was analyzed using a linear mixed model with WB severity as the main effect and farm as a random effect (lme4, Bates et al., 2015). The assumption of normality was met based on a Quantile-Quantile plot that included a Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test, a dispersion test, an outlier test, a within-group deviation from uniformity, and a Levene Test for homogeneity of variance (DHARMa package, Hartig, 2022). Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1 (R Core Team, 2022) on macOS Sequoia 15.0.

Results

Fig. 2 shows the distribution of broilers with no, moderate and severe WB in relation to the wingspan measure (cm). In total, 14 broilers had no wing partition, 16 had moderate wing partition, and 28 had pronounced wing partition. As a method for determining WB presence (moderate or severe WB), wing partition (moderate or pronounced) had an accuracy of 93.1%, with a sensitivity of 95.5%, specificity of 85.7%, PPV of 95.5%, and NPV of 85.7%. Of the 44 birds with moderate to pronounced wing partition, 42 individuals had WB (true positive) while 2 individuals had no WB (false positive). Of the 14 individuals with no wing partition, 2 had WB (false negative) and 12 had no WB (true negative).

As a method to determine severe WB, pronounced wing partition had an accuracy of 82.8% with a sensitivity of 84.6%, specificity of 81.3%, PPV of 78.6%, and NPV of 86.7%. From 28 birds with a pronounced wing partition, 22 individuals had severe WB (true positive), and 6 individuals had no or moderate WB (false positive); all 28 had the myopathy. Of the 30 individuals with no or moderate wing partition, 4 had severe WB (false negative) while 26 had no or moderate WB (true negative).

Broilers with a pronounced wing partition were less likely to self-right than those with no or moderate wing partition (one-tailed Fischer’s Exact Test, p < 0.05), with an odds ratio of 27.30 (95% confidence interval: 6.10-162.70). Of the 28 broilers with a pronounced wing partition, only 5 were able to self-right within the 60 s test period. Of the 30 broilers classified as having no or moderate wing partition, 26 were able to right themselves and did so in under 2.5 s. The four that did not self-right within the 60 s trial had moderate WB.

When accounting for WB severity, 20 out of 26 (76.9%) broilers with severe WB and 7 out of the 18 (38.9%) birds with moderate WB were unable to self-right within the 60 s limit. All 14 birds without WB were able to self-right themselves. The median time to self-right was different among WB severities (Kruskal-Wallis χ² = 24.36, p < 0.05; Fig. 3). Broilers without WB had the shortest median time at 1.36 s (IQR: 0.78-1.65), followed by those with moderate WB at 1.86 s (IQR: 1.24-60), and severe WB at 60 s (IQR: 60-60; p < 0.05).

Time to upright (s) for each broiler (N = 58). The x-axis represents the severity of woody breast (normal, moderate, severe) and the y-axis indicates the time each broiler took to right itself.

A linear mixed-effects model revealed a significant effect of WB severity on breast weight (Fig. 4). Normal breasts (331 g) were lighter than both moderate WB (411 g; p < 0.05) and severe WB (436 g; p < 0.05). No significant difference was observed between moderate and severe WB (p = 0.36).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that the inability of broilers to fully extend their wings, which we measured based on the presence of a partition between the glenohumeral joints of the wings, can serve as a non-invasive indicator of WB in live broilers. A profound partition (≥ 5.5 cm) can also serve as an indicator of severe WB, albeit with lower accuracy. The presence of a wing partition (moderate or profound) detected WB (moderate or severe) with high accuracy (93.1%) and sensitivity (95.5%) correctly identifying 42 of 44 cases of WB, whereas using pronounced wing partition as an indicator of severe WB was associated with relatively lower, but good accuracy (82.8%) and sensitivity (84.6%) values. Whereas others have highlighted the need for an in-vivo method for WB detection (Norring et al., 2019; Oh et al., 2024), to our knowledge, no other published study has reported a method for detecting WB in live birds with high accuracy and sensitivity (but see: Kawasaki et al., 2016).

Our study confirms and builds on preliminary observations by Kawasaki et al. (2016) who reported that broilers affected by WB were unable to fully extend their wings and displayed a partition between the wings. Whereas Kawasaki et al.’s (2016) findings were based on a small sample of birds, our study included 58 fast-growth broilers and explored whether WB severity (none, moderate, severe) influenced the presence and degree of wing extension impairment. The availability of an in vivo, on-farm method for identifying birds affected by WB enables new research into its development, contributing factors, and potential mitigation strategies, as previously noted by Norring et al. (2019). However, given that our study was conducted within 2 d of market age, research efforts must first be directed toward understanding the developmental trajectory of limited wing extension in relation to WB development. Since WB increases with age (Kuttappan et al., 2017; Caldas-Cuevas et al., 2021), we studied near-market age broilers for our study in an effort to increase the likelihood of locating affected birds in large flocks. However, macroscopic muscle alterations associated with WB have been reported as early as 18 d of age (Sihvo et al., 2017). Future research should explore how these changes to the muscle tissue corresponds with changes in wing extension over the bird’s lifetime.

Whereas the use of moderate to pronounced wing partition to detect the presence of WB in live birds was associated with high accuracy and sensitivity, the specificity was 85.7% It is, therefore, more suitable to use the presence of a wing partition to detect animals that have WB than to use lack of partition (full wing extension) to detect birds without WB. Given that only 14 of the 58 sampled broilers did not have WB, future research should further examine the relationship between the ability to fully extend the wings and absence of WB. Using pronounced wing partition as an indicator of severe WB was associated with a relatively lower sensitivity and specificity. This could be related to the cut-off used to differentiate a bird with severe WB over moderate WB. In our study, a wing partition of ≥ 5.5 cm was used to define severe WB based on visual differences in wing extension. We opted to quantify limited wing extension by measuring the partition between the glenohumeral joints in the wings and selected the cut-off value based on preliminary data which related this value to the inability to raise wings over the body. However, it is likely that the cut-off value may be affected by bird size and other factors. Future studies should consider normalizing wing partition measurements to account for variation in body size to improve accuracy.

Future research should explore whether other measures of limited extension, such as the angle of the wings relative to the back or the more descriptive measure of “ability to move wings above back” may increase the accuracy, sensitivity and specificity of severe WB detection. Our study showed that broilers with a pronounced wing partition were less likely to self-right when placed on their backs compared to broilers with a moderate partition and those able to fully extend their wings. Only 17.9% of the affected birds were able to self-right within the 60 s limit. On the other hand, all of broilers without a wing partition completed the task in under 2.5 s. Che et al. (2023) noted that most (the exact frequency was not reported) of the birds that were found on their back also had a limited wingspan suggesting that there might be an association between WB, wingspan, and ability to self-right. The effect of WB on wingspan was not directly assessed by Che et al. (2023), a gap our study addressed.

Given that WB is associated with wing partition, it is not surprising that WB also affects a broiler’s ability to self-right. In our study, the median time required to self-right increased with an increase in WB severity. Furthermore, 76.9% of broilers with severe WB were unable to self-right. Although Gall et al. (2019) did not distinguish between WB severity scores, they reported that 70% of broilers found dead in a dorsal recumbent position (on their back) were affected by WB. The inability to self-right is a welfare concern as the pressure from the breast on the heart and lungs can lead to pulmonary congestion and eventually result in death if a broiler remains on its back for a long period of time (Gall et al., 2019). WB has been previously suggested to be a potential welfare concern, but few studies have explored how WB affects the animals themselves. Those that have, have focused on the relationship between WB and activity levels. For example, Norring et al. (2019) reported that broilers with WB had poorer walking ability (higher average gait scores) compared to unaffected broilers while Ross et al. (2020) found no difference in activity levels between broilers with and without WB. We previously reported that induced activity (exercise) can reduce WB occurrence in broilers but did not find a relationship between WB presence and overall activity levels (Hisasaga and Makagon, 2024). By demonstrating that WB is associated with reduced wing mobility, which in turn impairs ability to self-right, the results of the current study support that WB poses a welfare concern. Building on these findings, future studies could investigate whether limited wingspan associated with WB affects other wing-dependent behaviors, such as accessing and balancing on perches (LeVan et al., 2000).

While our findings support the use of wing extension as an indicator of WB and help clarify the relationship between limited wing extension and a bird’s ability to right itself, several limitations should be considered and addressed in future research. With the goal of increasing external validity, we sampled from multiple flocks across three commercial farms. Visited flocks were comprised of two to three fast-growth broiler strains and were raised to different target weights. Broilers on farm C were 3 and 7 d younger than Farm A and Farm B, respectively. Because birds of different strains could not be visually discerned, the effects of strain on WB severity and wing partition limitation could not be assessed. While strain effects were outside the scope of our stated aims, this topic provides an interesting avenue for future research. Older and larger birds may show a higher incidence and severity of WB (Kuttappan et al., 2016; Caldas-Cueva and Owens, 2020; Hisasaga and Makagon, 2024). Our findings support this relationship: heavier breast weights were associated with the occurrence of WB. It is possible that breast or body size could also affect the occurrence, and perhaps the degree of wing partition. Our study only included fast-growth strains, which are more prone to WB development (Santos et al., 2021; Hisasaga and Makagon, 2024). Future studies should evaluate whether wing partition serves as an indicator of WB in slow-growth strains as it remains unclear whether WB affects wing extension to the same extent in these slower growing strains. It is important to acknowledge that all the measures were taken by the same individuals. While we included some assessment of repeatability in the preliminary work that preceded this study, future research into the relationships between WB, wing extension and inability to self-right will further add to the external validity of the current study.

In conclusion, our study aimed to determine whether the distance between the wings when extended backwards (wing partition) could serve as an indicator of WB in live market-age broilers. Our result showed that moderate to severe wing partition is an accurate indicator of WB, and a pronounced partition is moderately accurate in identifying severe cases of WB. Our findings offer a non-invasive method for identifying WB in live birds, which could help advance the experimental design of future studies. Another objective was to determine whether a pronounced wing partition associated with severe WB affects a bird’s ability to self-right. We found that birds with a pronounced wing partition were less likely to self-right, and the median time required to self-right increased with severity of WB. These findings demonstrate that WB not only affects meat quality but also the bird’s welfare. Future research should focus on understanding how WB is related to the development of limited wing extension. In addition, further work is needed to refine wing extension assessment methodologies and validate their use in both fast- and slow-growing broiler strains.
