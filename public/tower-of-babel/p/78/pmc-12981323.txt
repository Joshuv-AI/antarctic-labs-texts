Protective Effects of Hesperidin on Letrozole‐Induced Neurotoxicity: Involvement of Oxidative Stress, Apoptotic Signaling, and Inflammation
Authors: İdris Ayhan, Munevver Nazlican Kaplan, Demet Dondu Kasim, Ozlem Ozmen, Osman Ciftci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLetrozole, a widely used aromatase inhibitor for hormone receptor–positive breast cancer, has been suggested to be associated with potential neurotoxic effects; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the neurotoxic effects of letrozole and evaluate the neuroprotective potential of hesperidin, a natural flavonoid with antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties. Adult female rats received letrozole alone or in combination with hesperidin for 4 weeks. Biochemical analyses showed that letrozole significantly increased total oxidant status (TOS) and decreased total antioxidant status (TAS), indicating oxidative stress. Gene expression results revealed upregulation of pro‐apoptotic Bax and downregulation of anti‐apoptotic Bcl2 in letrozole‐treated rats, reflecting apoptotic activation. Additionally, inflammatory cytokine measurements demonstrated elevated pro‐inflammatory markers (IL‐1, IL‐6, TNF‐α) and reduced anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 following letrozole administration. Histopathological examination revealed cortical microhemorrhages, edema, and hyperemia. Importantly, co‐treatment with hesperidin attenuated oxidative imbalance, normalized apoptotic gene expression, modulated inflammatory cytokine levels towards an anti‐inflammatory profile, and ameliorated histological damage. These findings indicate that hesperidin confers neuroprotection by restoring redox balance, regulating apoptosis, and suppressing inflammation in letrozole‐induced neurotoxicity. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic applications of hesperidin in letrozole‐induced neurotoxicity.Keywords: apoptosis, hesperidin, letrozole, neurotoxicity, oxidative stress

Introduction

Letrozole, as an aromatase inhibitor, plays a critical role in the management of hormone receptor‐positive breast cancer by suppressing estrogen synthesis and thereby limiting the growth of estrogen‐dependent tumor cells [1]. Although primarily used in breast cancer therapy, letrozole's systemic suppression of estrogen may have adverse effects on other organs, including the central nervous system [2]. Given the well‐documented neuromodulatory and neuroprotective roles of estrogen, there is growing interest in the potential neurological consequences associated with aromatase inhibition. However, the mechanisms underlying letrozole‐associated neurotoxic alterations remain incompletely understood and are likely multifactorial rather than attributable to a single pathway [3, 4]. Experimental and clinical evidence suggests that processes such as oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptotic imbalance may contribute to these effects [5, 6, 7, 8].

Oxidative stress is defined as an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the capacity of the antioxidant defense system, leading to cellular damage, lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA injury. To counteract oxidative damage, natural antioxidant compounds have been widely investigated [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Flavonoids have demonstrated potent free radical scavenging activities [14, 15]. Hesperidin, a flavonoid found abundantly in citrus fruits, has gained attention due to its potent antioxidant properties [14, 16, 17]. Numerous studies have demonstrated its neuroprotective effects in various neurological disorders such as Alzheimer's disease [18, 19], Parkinson's disease [20], multiple sclerosis [21], central nervous system trauma [22] and cerebral ischemia [23]. These findings suggest that natural antioxidants like hesperidin may serve as potential protective agents against letrozole‐induced oxidative stress.

Given the role of oxidative stress and inflammation in letrozole‐induced neurotoxicity, and hesperidin's known protective effects in these pathways, it was hypothesized that hesperidin may alleviate the neurotoxic effects of letrozole. To test this hypothesis, a rat model of letrozole‐induced neurotoxicity was employed. Histological and molecular assessments, including oxidative and antioxidative parameters, cytokine levels, and the gene expression of b‐cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl2) and Bcl2‐associated X protein (Bax) were performed.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Hesperidin (HSP; sc‐205711A; CAS No: 520‐26‐3) was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc. (California, USA). Letrozole was purchased from a local commercial supplier of Novartis in Türkiye. All other reagents and chemicals used in the study were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and were of analytical grade or the highest available purity.

Animals and Treatment

The experimental procedures were approved by the Burdur Mehmet Akif Ersoy University Animal Experiments Ethics Committee (Protocol No: 20205/1590) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. A total of 28 healthy adult female Sprague–Dawley rats, aged approximately 9 months, were included in the study. Sample size was determined based on previous experimental studies evaluating neurotoxicity and antioxidant interventions in rodent models. The animals were randomly divided into four equal groups (n = 7 per group) and housed under standard laboratory conditions (∼23°C, ∼55% humidity, 12‐h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water throughout the experiment.

Group 1 (Control): Received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of 0.9% saline once weekly and sesame oil orally once daily for 4 weeks.

Group 1 (Control): Received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of 0.9% saline once weekly and sesame oil orally once daily for 4 weeks.

Group 2 (LET): Received letrozole (2 mg/kg/week, i.p., in 0.9% saline) for 4 weeks.

Group 2 (LET): Received letrozole (2 mg/kg/week, i.p., in 0.9% saline) for 4 weeks.

Group 3 (HES): Received hesperidin (100 mg/kg/day, orally, in sesame oil) for 4 weeks.

Group 3 (HES): Received hesperidin (100 mg/kg/day, orally, in sesame oil) for 4 weeks.

Group 4 (LET + HES): Received both letrozole and hesperidin at the above‐mentioned doses concurrently for 4 weeks.

Group 4 (LET + HES): Received both letrozole and hesperidin at the above‐mentioned doses concurrently for 4 weeks.

At the end of the treatment period, the rats were sacrificed, and the brain tissues (cortex and hippocampus) were collected for biochemical and molecular analyses. Samples were stored at –80°C until further evaluation.

Measurement of Oxidative and Antioxidative Status

Total antioxidant status (TAS) and total oxidant status (TOS) were measured in brain tissue homogenates. Brain tissues were excised, rinsed with ice‐cold phosphate‐buffered saline, and homogenized in cold buffer. The homogenates were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. TAS (E1710Ra) and TOS (E1512Ra) levels were determined using commercially available ELISA kits (Bioassay Technology Laboratory) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Cytokine Analysis

Brain tissue concentrations of interleukin‐1 beta (IL‐1β) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) were quantified using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Reed Biotech, PRC; IL‐1β: Cat. No. RE1074R; IL‐6: Cat. No. RE3186R), following the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves prepared for each analyte. Results were expressed as pg/mL.

RNA Extraction and Real‐Time qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from brain tissues using TRIzol reagent (Hibrigen Biotechnology, Istanbul, Türkiye), and RNA purity and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). cDNA synthesis was carried out using a reverse transcription kit (A.B.T. Laboratory Industry, Ankara, Türkiye). Gene expression analysis was carried out using a StepOnePlus real‐time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Massachusetts, USA) in combination with a 2X qPCR MasterMix kit (A.B.T. Laboratory Industry, Ankara, Turkey), in accordance with the protocol provided by the manufacturer.

Histopathological Evaluation

During necropsy, brain tissues were carefully excised and fixed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin for histological examination. Following routine tissue processing with an automated tissue processor, 5 µm‐thick sections were obtained from paraffin‐embedded blocks using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2155, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were then deparaffinized, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), cleared in xylene, and mounted. Histopathological changes were examined under a light microscope.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9, GraphPad Software Inc.). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Molecular parameters were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Oxidative Status (TAS and TOS)

Assessment of oxidative balance revealed statistically significant alterations in both total antioxidant and total oxidant parameters across the experimental groups (Figure 1). The control and hesperidin‐only groups maintained higher antioxidant capacity and lower oxidant levels, indicating preserved oxidative homeostasis. In contrast, administration of letrozole alone led to a marked decrease in total antioxidant status and a concurrent increase in total oxidant levels, suggesting enhanced oxidative stress. Notably, co‐treatment with hesperidin attenuated these changes by increasing antioxidant capacity and reducing oxidant accumulation compared to the letrozole‐only group.

Different letters (a, b, c) indicate statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05), groups sharing the same letter are not significantly different for each parameter.

Cytokine Levels

Significant differences were observed among the groups in terms of IL‐1β and IL‐6 levels (Figure 2). In the letrozole‐administrated group (LET), brain tissue concentrations of IL‐1β and IL‐6 were significantly elevated compared to the control group, indicating the induction of a pro‐inflammatory response. In contrast, co‐administration of hesperidin significantly reduced both IL‐1β and IL‐6 levels relative to the letrozole group. In the LET + HES group, the letrozole‐induced increase in IL‐1β was completely suppressed, resulting in levels comparable to the control group. Although IL‐6 levels in the LET + HES group did not fully return to control values, they were significantly decreased compared to the letrozole group.

Different letters (a, b, c) indicate statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05), groups sharing the same letter are not significantly different for each parameter.

Real‐Time qPCR Analysis

Gene expression analysis of apoptosis‐related markers demonstrated distinct patterns among the study groups (Figure 3). The expression of the pro‐apoptotic gene Bax was markedly elevated in the letrozole group, reflecting a significant upregulation of apoptotic signaling. In contrast, the control, hesperidin, and letrozole + hesperidin groups exhibited relatively lower and comparable levels of Bax expression, indicating no substantial difference among them. A parallel but inverse trend was observed in the expression of the anti‐apoptotic gene Bcl2. While letrozole treatment led to a clear reduction in Bcl2 expression, this downregulation was not evident in the control, hesperidin, or combination groups, whose Bcl2 levels remained similarly elevated. These results indicate that letrozole administration significantly alters the expression profile of apoptosis‐related genes by increasing Bax and decreasing Bcl2 levels, whereas hesperidin treatment, either alone or in combination with letrozole, maintains expression levels similar to the control group.

Histopathological Findings

Microscopic examination of brain cortex sections revealed no pathological alterations in either the Control or Hesperidin groups, with preserved normal tissue architecture. In contrast, rats treated with letrozole exhibited marked histopathological changes, including cortical microhemorrhages, vasogenic edema, and prominent hyperemia, indicating neurotoxic injury. Notably, co‐administration of hesperidin with letrozole ameliorated these changes, resulting in reduced severity of the histopathological damage (Figure 4).

(B) Hesperidin group with preserved tissue structure, similar to the control. (C) Letrozole group displaying pronounced hyperemia (arrows), microhemorrhages, and edema. (D) Letrozole + Hesperidin group demonstrating noticeable improvement in pathological findings.

Discussion

Letrozole, a commonly used aromatase inhibitor in hormone‐dependent breast cancer treatment, has been increasingly studied for its potential off‐target effects beyond endocrine regulation, including its impact on the central nervous system. Given estrogen's well‐established role in neuronal function and protection, its systemic depletion may predispose brain tissue to oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptotic damage. This study was conducted to explore the neurotoxic effects of letrozole and to investigate whether co‐administration of hesperidin—a natural flavonoid with antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties—could offer neuroprotection. Histological, biochemical, and molecular analyses collectively indicated that hesperidin administration may offer promising neuroprotective effects against letrozole‐induced brain injury.

Letrozole administration resulted in a significant increase in TOS and a concomitant decrease in TAS in rat brain tissue, indicating the presence of oxidative stress. These findings consistent with previous findings demonstrating that estrogen depletion compromises neuronal antioxidant defenses [24, 25]. In a rodent focal ischemia model, estradiol markedly attenuated NADPH oxidase activity and reduced superoxide production in the hippocampus via extranuclear signaling mechanisms [24]. Moreover, studies involving letrozole or other aromatase inhibitors, have reported similar pro‐oxidant effects in various organs, although investigations directly examining the central nervous system remain limited [26, 27]. In this context, our study contributes to a growing body of evidence linking aromatase inhibition with oxidative stress in the brain and extends these findings specifically to letrozole exposure. A key novelty of our study is the demonstration that hesperidin co‐administration attenuates letrozole‐induced oxidative imbalance. Hesperidin treatment significantly restored TAS levels while reducing TOS, suggesting its capacity to neutralize free radicals and enhance endogenous antioxidant mechanisms. While the antioxidant properties of hesperidin have been reported in models of neurodegeneration and ischemia‐reperfusion injury, its use in the context of letrozole‐induced neurotoxicity has not been previously explored to our knowledge [28, 29]. Therefore, this study presents a novel insight into the potential therapeutic role of hesperidin in preserving neural redox homeostasis under conditions of pharmacologically induced estrogen deficiency. These findings not only support hesperidin's neuroprotective profile but also open a new avenue for its application in mitigating the side effects of long‐term aromatase inhibitor therapy.

Although estrogen is recognized for its neuroprotective roles, estrogen levels were not directly measured in the present study, as the primary objective was to characterize letrozole‐associated neurotoxic alterations and the protective effects of hesperidin through established biochemical and molecular markers. Future studies integrating hormonal assessments may further elucidate the mechanistic pathways involved.

Gene expression analysis revealed that letrozole administration significantly upregulated the pro‐apoptotic Bax gene and downregulated the anti‐apoptotic Bcl2 gene, indicating activation of apoptosis‐related pathways in brain tissue. These results are consistent with earlier reports. Estradiol's modulation of Bcl2 in the brain has been documented in ischemic injury models, where it prevented injury‐induced downregulation of Bcl2 expression, preserving neuronal survival [30]. Additionally, estrogen has been shown to enhance Bcl2 expression in hypothalamic neurons of adult female rats, affirming its neuroprotective role via apoptotic regulation [31]. In contrast, co‐administration of hesperidin ‐either alone or alongside letrozole‐ resulted in Bax and Bcl2 expression levels comparable to those of the control group, suggesting restoration of apoptotic homeostasis. Similar anti‐apoptotic effects of hesperidin have been described in chemically‐induced neurotoxicity models; for example, in rat brains exposed to cadmium, pretreatment with hesperetin (a related flavonoid) reversed Cd‐induced increases in Bax and decreases in Bcl2 [32]. The ability of hesperidin to normalize apoptosis‐related gene expression in the context of letrozole exposure represents a novel observation, as no previous study has specifically examined this interaction in the brain.

Our histopathological assessments revealed that letrozole‐treated rats exhibited distinct neurotoxic alterations in the cerebral cortex, including cortical microhemorrhages, vasogenic edema, and prominent hyperemia, while control and hesperidin‐only groups maintained normal structural integrity. Importantly, co‐treatment with hesperidin ameliorated these deleterious changes, restoring histological architecture toward normal appearance. These results align with previous research indicating that hesperidin administration effectively reduces brain tissue damage caused by ischemic events [23, 28, 33]. Treatment with hesperidin has been shown to significantly improve histological outcomes in animal models of ischemia/reperfusion injury [23, 28]. Similarly, studies on hesperidin derivatives like neohesperidin reveal their capacity to protect neuronal cells by modulating apoptotic pathways [33]. These observations underscore the close relationship between oxidative stress and histopathological damage in the brain. Therefore, our data reinforce the hypothesis that hesperidin mitigates letrozole‐induced neurotoxicity by preserving histological integrity and reducing apoptotic cell death, possibly through modulation of oxidative balance.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence that letrozole induces significant neurotoxicity characterized by oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptotic dysregulation, and histopathological damage in rat brain tissue. Importantly, co‐administration of hesperidin effectively counteracts these deleterious effects by restoring redox homeostasis, suppressing inflammatory cytokine production, normalizing apoptosis‐related gene expression, and preserving neuronal histoarchitecture. These findings highlight hesperidin's potential as a neuroprotective agent against aromatase inhibitor‐induced brain injury, offering promising implications for mitigating central nervous system side effects during long‐term letrozole therapy. Further research is warranted to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms and to evaluate hesperidin's clinical applicability in patients undergoing aromatase inhibitor treatment.
