Congenital tracheobronchomegaly (Mounier-Kuhn syndrome) in a 70-year-old nonsmoking male: A rare presentation in a low-resource setting
Authors: Yousif Aboaziza, Eiman Aboaziza, Rabha E. Almahdi, Abdelrahman Aboaziza, Samira H. Belkheir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMounier-Kuhn Syndrome (MKS), or congenital tracheobronchomegaly, is a rare disorder characterized by dilation of the trachea and main bronchi due to the atrophy of elastic and smooth muscle fibers. Though often associated with recurrent infections, it may be underdiagnosed, particularly in resource-limited settings.Case reportWe present a 70-year-old Chadian male with a 3-month history of productive cough, dysphagia, and postprandial abdominal discomfort. He was a lifelong nonsmoker with no known history of chronic pulmonary disease. Imaging and bronchoscopy confirmed significant tracheobronchial dilatation consistent with MKS. Despite plans for further gastrointestinal intervention, socioeconomic factors impeded complete management.ConclusionThis case underscores the importance of considering MKS in older adults with chronic respiratory symptoms, even in the absence of typical risk factors. Early recognition is vital to avoid misdiagnosis and improve outcomes, especially in low-resource environments.

Introduction

Mounier-Kuhn Syndrome (MKS), or congenital tracheobronchomegaly, is a rare pulmonary disorder first described in 1932 [1]. It involves abnormal dilatation of the trachea and main bronchi due to atrophic or absent smooth muscle and elastic fibers of the airway wall [2,3]. To date, fewer than 400 cases globally have been documented [3]. Most cases are diagnosed in middle-aged men with a history of recurrent respiratory infections due to poor mucociliary clearance [[3], [4], [5]]. Clinical diagnosis of MKS remains challenging due to nonspecific symptoms and limited awareness, and as such, MKS may be underrecognized, especially in developing countries [6]. We report a rare case of MKS in a 70-year-old Chadian male presenting with respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms in a resource-limited setting.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old Chadian male, employed as a cleaner and a lifelong nonsmoker, presented to our clinic in April 2024 with a 3-month history of productive cough, difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), and postprandial abdominal pain. He also reported significant weight loss during this period but denied changes in bowel habits, fever, night sweats, or dyspnea. His systemic review was otherwise unremarkable.

Past medical history revealed a 2-month hospitalization at Quifia Chest Center (Libya) one year prior for presumed pulmonary tuberculosis, though no documentation was available. The patient was not on any regular medication and had no history of previous surgeries.

On examination, the patient was conscious, alert, and visibly underweight (cachectic). Chest auscultation revealed decreased breath sounds bilaterally with bilateral lower zone crepitations. Abdominal examination showed a scaphoid abdomen with no tenderness or organomegaly. Other systems were unremarkable.

The patient was empirically treated with antibiotics for a presumed bacterial lower respiratory tract infection. Spirometry performed after infection resolution showed poor patient effort, limiting interpretation.

Given the clinical and bronchoscopic findings, a diagnosis of Mounier-Kuhn Syndrome was made. The patient declined a planned referral for esophageal stenting due to financial constraints and returned to his home country.

Discussion

Mounier-Kuhn Syndrome (MKS) is characterized by abnormal and irreversible dilatation of the trachea and main bronchi caused by atrophy or absence of smooth muscle and elastic fibers in the airway wall. This congenital structural abnormality predisposes patients to ineffective mucociliary clearance, recurrent respiratory infections, and progressive airway dysfunction [[1], [2], [3]]. Smoking, though a risk factor, is not necessary for disease development. Although most reported cases occur in middle-aged men with a history of recurrent infections [4,5], our patient's advanced age and nonsmoking status emphasize that MKS can present outside the “typical” demographic profile.

The clinical presentation of MKS varies widely. Respiratory symptoms are the most common, including chronic productive cough, recurrent pneumonia, hemoptysis, and worsening shortness of breath [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. While infections are predominant presentations, some patients remain asymptomatic until the disease is advanced and discovered incidentally.

The generalized connective tissue laxity observed in MKS may theoretically extend beyond the respiratory system, affecting the integrity and function of other organ systems, including the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, due to shared embryological origins or systemic connective tissue abnormalities [5,7]. GI symptoms, such as difficulty swallowing or post-meal discomfort, are rare but have been reported, often caused by external compression of the esophagus from tracheobronchomegaly, as observed in our patient. Chronic cough and recurrent respiratory infections may indirectly affect GI function or exacerbate underlying systemic inflammatory processes. Previously reported GI findings have been structural (colonic enlargement, multiple diverticula) rather than, for example, a reproducible syndrome of chronic abdominal pain or functional GI symptoms [6,7]. When abdominal symptoms appear in case reports, they may reflect secondary consequences (e.g., venous/lymphatic congestion, cardiac sequelae) rather than primary gastrointestinal disease [8]. Thus, the direct correlation between MKS and specific gastrointestinal symptoms remains largely unexplored.

Genetic predisposition through associations with disorders such as Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, cutis laxa, and Marfan syndrome has been noted [9,10]. These findings suggest shared defects in connective tissue health. Interestingly, there are emerging reports of secondary MKS, challenging the idea that MKS is exclusively congenital [11]. Genetic testing was not feasible in our case, which limited further assessment.

Diagnostic confirmation relies on radiographic and bronchoscopic findings [12]. In adults, the CT diagnostic criteria include a tracheal diameter >30 mm and main bronchial diameters >24 mm and 23 mm for the right and left bronchi, respectively. An increase in the tracheal cross-sectional area beyond 371 mm2 in men and 299 mm2 in women supports the diagnosis [1]. Here, we show significant tracheobronchomegaly, bronchiectasis, pooled secretions, and diverticulations in the airways of our patient. Early CT and bronchoscopy provided sufficient evidence to confirm MKS; however, technical limitations prevented us from including exact measurements in some images.

There is no curative treatment for Mounier-Kuhn Syndrome, and management is mainly supportive. Airway clearance techniques, mucolytics, antibiotics, and vaccinations are the cornerstone of care, with noninvasive ventilation sometimes used to relieve airway collapse. Endobronchial stenting, tracheobronchoplasty, or even lung transplantation have been reported in severe cases and in specialized facilities [2,12]. Ultimately, treatment is individualized, with a focus on infection prevention, airway hygiene, and symptom control, while advanced interventions are considered only in select patients. The patient reported here declined esophageal stenting due to financial reasons, underscoring socioeconomic barriers to optimal care.

In any clinical setting, but especially in resource-limited areas, misdiagnosis remains a significant challenge. MKS can be mistaken for tuberculosis, chronic bronchitis, or COPD, particularly when symptoms are nonspecific and indistinguishable [11]. The similarity in symptoms can delay accurate diagnosis and proper treatment. Despite its rarity, clinicians should consider MKS in patients presenting with recurrent lower respiratory tract infections, abnormal tracheobronchial morphology, poor response to antibiotic therapy, or refractory COPD-like symptoms. Raising awareness among healthcare providers, especially in areas endemic for tuberculosis, may enhance diagnostic accuracy and lead to better patient outcomes.

Our case represents an uncommon clinical manifestation of MKS and highlights challenges in its diagnosis and management. The coexistence of chronic respiratory issues alongside gastrointestinal symptoms in nonsmoking, elderly patients should prompt clinicians to consider MKS in differential diagnoses. In our case, imaging and bronchoscopy confirmed the diagnosis. Though primary treatment modalities may be scarce in resource-poor settings, identifying and managing MKS requires both clinical suspicion and access to diagnostic and supportive care.

Conclusion

This case highlights the need to consider MKS in the differential diagnosis of chronic respiratory symptoms, especially in older patients without conventional risk factors. Early identification and tailored management strategies are crucial, particularly in resource-constrained healthcare systems.
