The role of DNA methylation and DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) as potential biomarker and therapeutic target in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)
Authors: Khairunnisa Mohd Kamal, Ahmad Rohi Ghazali, Nurul Syakima Ab Mutalib, Nadiah Abu, Eng Wee Chua, Siti Fathiah Masre
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer as the second most death cancer reported cases is becoming a major threat to the global healthcare system. With the different subtypes of lung cancer and their limited therapy options due to the lack of targetable genes, rising cases of treatment resistance further complicate the management. The majority of the reported lung cancer cases are categorised as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) which is highly associated with tobacco smoking. Tumorigenesis and cancer progression have also been associated with epigenetics. Epigenetics is responsible for cancer gene regulation and its reversible mechanisms attract the current trend of cancer management research. One of the most studied mechanisms is DNA methylation which can influence the cancer gene transcription outcomes. The enzyme, DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) play a role in regulating the whole process of DNA methylation. Thus, abnormalities in DNMTs can lead to aberrant methylation patterns which then disturb the gene regulation and cellular functions as a whole. In this review, NSCLC subtypes are discussed with the current research trend of studies involving DNA methylation mechanism as a potential diagnostic and prognostic cancer biomarker. As DNMTs expression influences the methylation pattern, our review also outlined the abnormal pattern of DNMTs and its potential therapeutic target for NSCLC to restore the aberrant gene regulation and produce a better prognosis.

Introduction

Global Cancer Statistics (GLOBOCON) 2022 listed lung cancer as the most reported cancer case with more than 2 million cases, leading the cancer fatality rate in 2022 [1,2] and the trend is expected to exponentially increase in less than 30 years if ignored [3]. Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are the two primary histological forms of lung cancer (LC), with NSCLC accounting for approximately 85 % of all cases [4,5]. NSCLC is extremely heterogeneous [6] and frequently yields a poor cancer prognosis. With the worrying trend of the reported cases, novel approaches are needed to control health issues.

Epigenetics has been studied as an alternative approach to understanding cancer. Epigenetic mechanisms mainly comprise histone modification and DNA methylation which are involved in the development, maintenance and regulation of the gene expression [7]. DNA methylation is one of the most important epigenetic mechanisms in regulating gene expression without altering DNA sequences [7,8]. It regulates essential cellular signalling and mechanosignalling pathways through the methylation of cytosine bases on CpG islands, usually located at the promoter of a gene [9]. The methylation state of the promoter is controlled by the enzymes DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), determining the fate of tumor growth. Consequently, abnormalities in the expression of DNMTs, causing either hypo- or hypermethylation, influence tumorigenesis [10,11]. DNMTs are well-known for their involvement in controlling gene transcription and repression, as they regulate crucial biological processes such as cell differentiation, gene transcription, embryo development, maintenance of genome integrity, chromosome stability, and growth of organisms [12]. DNA methylation patterns at the promoter are regulated by DNMTs which primarily function to control gene transcription [13]. The regulation of DNA methylation pattern at each CpG site is commonly mediated by DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B [14,15], which are highly concentrated in the nucleus [16]. These three types of DNMTs are the most frequently found to be highly expressed in cancer cells and tissues [9]; their substantial but reversible expression in cancers makes them one of the recent targets for alternative anticancer therapy.

As DNMT expression is often associated with tumor progression, it has also been focused as a potential therapeutic target due to the reversible gene regulation state. DNMTs and cancer progression have been extensively studied over the decades as they provide an in-depth understanding of cancer, especially in diagnosis and prognosis. Current literature is focused on investigating the role of DNMTs as a cancer biomarker and a potential cancer therapeutic target. However, there is still limited literature on the role of DNMTs especially in regulating NSCLC as the majority of the existing studies are more focused on DNA methylation, and few explored the role of DNMTs in regulating cancer gene expression at different stages.

The ultimate focus of the review is to discuss the relationship between DNA methylation and its enzyme DNMTs, with the occurrence and progression of NSCLC in both in vitro and in vivo studies. The sub-type of NSCLC and DNA methylation mechanism are briefly reviewed. Next, the trend of current NSCLC studies involving DNA methylation dysregulation and the role of their DNMTs as potential biomarkers are further discussed. In-depth discussion on DNMTs expression reported in NSCLC along with the potential therapeutic target for novel detection and therapy for NSCLC are also reviewed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the DNMTs expression with cancer gene regulation and its potential as therapeutic biomarkers.

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and its subtypes

The prevalence of lung cancer globally is ranked as the most reported cancer cases, with the highest mortality rate [1,2]. In Malaysia, lung cancer ranked as the third most reported cancer case with the highest mortality rate [17]. Lung cancer is highly related to cigarette smoking [18,19], followed by environmental factors such as air pollution [20], secondary smoking [21,22] and exposure to occupational hazards [23]. Worryingly, the estimated 5-year survival rate is the poorest at less than 15 % [24,25]. Early detection of lung cancer progression could considerably increase the chance of survival [26], as early diagnosis has been reported to improve almost 90 % of patient survival rates [27]. Nevertheless, most lung cancer patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, with only less than 20 % of cases detected at early stages with still localised tumors [28]. Late-stage detection is one of the factors leading to a poor prognosis despite the availability of advanced treatment, as metastasized tumors infiltrate other sites of organs, thus complicating treatment effectiveness and resulting in drug resistance [29]. Therefore, a novel diagnostic molecular biomarkers with an excellent ability to distinguish pre-malignant and malignant tumors should be developed to facilitate the diagnosis of lung cancer. This can be achieved through a comprehensive molecular characterization of the tumor landscape, which may reveal new molecular targets and biomarkers that can influence the course of the disease and facilitate the development of innovative treatment approaches [30].

Two major types of lung cancer are small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC); the latter is the most significant type and is estimated to occur in 85 % of lung cancer cases [4,5]. The most discussed subtype of lung cancer, NSCLC is classified into adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and large cell carcinoma (LCC) [4]. NSCLC is known for its vast heterogenicity compared to SCLC which further complicates treatment and often results in poor prognosis [31]. Adenocarcinoma is one of the most common histological subtypes of NSCLC studied. Approximately 50 % of lung cancer cases present with a general first appearance of ground glass nodules in the lung periphery under the computed tomography (CT) scan [32]. Mutations in the receptor tyrosine kinase are also commonly observed [33] with a high metastatic rate compared to lung SCC [34]. The subtype of NSCLC is also reported in non-smoker patients, especially among women. Meanwhile, lung SCC is originally an epithelial cell that lines the outer surface of many organs including the lung which proliferates abnormally, forming cancerous squamous cells [35]. SCC represents almost 30 % of NSCLC subtypes [36], commonly among smokers as it is associated with tobacco smoking [18]. Its wide heterogeneity owing to the lack of targetable gene mutations unlike adenocarcinoma [[37], [38], [39]], makes its therapeutic options to be limited and it is reported to be resistant to chemotherapy and radiation therapy [40]. As for large cell carcinoma (LCC), it represents the least common type of NSCLC (∼10 %) with high occurrence in elderly male patients [41]. The lesions of the subtype of NSCLC are observed mostly in the upper lobe region with poor differentiated histological appearance and majority of cases are diagnosed at later stage of III or IV [41].

The occurrence of cancer has been linked to epigenetic mechanisms which are responsible for the development of gene expression without altering the existing DNA sequences. Over the decades, understanding the gene regulation activity via methylation patterns has become one of the novel hallmarks of cancer development and progression. Overview of DNA methylation role and its mechanisms

The current hallmark of cancer has now shifted to focus on the epigenetic mechanisms, which involves the study of heritable genetic changes influenced by environmental changes without alteration of DNA sequences [7,8]. The molecular mechanism influences the transmission of genetic information between diverse gene expression patterns [13]. Therefore, any dysregulation of the mechanism would disrupt normal gene regulation and information transmission involving critical processes such as gene expression, cell proliferation, differentiation, and growth, which often results in cancer [7,42]. This is because the control of gene expression and the determination of genotype adaptation in mammalian tissues under varied environmental conditions are largely dependent on the epigenetic mechanism [7]. Common epigenetics mechanisms are histone modification and DNA methylation which are also responsible for the modification of gene transcription for cellular functionality. One of the first and most thoroughly researched epigenetic mechanisms is DNA methylation as it is the most significant type of epigenetic alteration of genomic DNA [13]. In the review paper, only the DNA methylation aspect will be discussed. DNA methylation is regulated by its enzyme expression, DNMTs and abnormalities in its expression are often linked to cancer.

DNA methylation involves chemical alteration through the covalent attachment of one methyl (CH3) group to the C5 cytosine ring in a cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) dinucleotide pair, yielding 5′-methyl -cytosine [26]. The chemical process involves S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) as a CH3 donor at a targeted base on the DNA sequence [43] and the enzyme DNMTs [26]. CpG dinucleotide pairs are commonly found in the promoter region of a gene whereby in mammalian genomic DNA, majority of 5′-methyl-cytosines are found in these CpG sequences [13]. Its unmethylated states are often linked to tissue-specific gene expression, especially that of housekeeping genes, which highlights the significant role of DNMTs in gene expression [13] and explains its normal cell environments as unmethylated states [44]. The methylation process, which includes the global CpG and CpG islands being hyper- or hypomethylated, is mediated by DNMTs and linked to the emergence of several malignancies. Previous studies suggest that alterations in DNA methylation patterns in the CpG sites give rise to tumor development as hypermethylation silences tumor suppressor gene while hypomethylation causes genomic instability that could lead to the activation of silenced oncogene [10,11].

Regulation of DNA methylation via DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs)

DNMTs, primarily DNMT1, DNMT3A and DNMT3B, maintain DNA methylation patterns [14]. Each of these enzymes plays a different role in regulating DNA methylation. DNMT1 methylates newly synthesized single-stranded DNA and transmits the methylation information from parental to progeny cells [[45], [46], [47]]. DNMT1 is highly expressed in active mitotic cells during the S phase of cell division [48], as it is required for the methylation of the hemi-methylated DNA strands [49]. DNMT1 plays a minimal role in the formation of new methylation sites. Such de novo methylation is regulated by DNMT3A and DNMT3B during the early stages of embryogenesis, as they identify and initiate new methylation patterns in unmethylated DNA [13,45,47]. DNMT3A is highly specific for CpG sites but has also been observed in non-CpG islands [50,51], unlike DNMT3B which methylates CpG sites exclusively [52]. As the expression of DNMTs is reversible, it has become one of the recent targets for potential alternative cancer therapy.

DNA methylation on intratumoral heterogeneity

Intratumor heterogeneity refers to the presence of distinct tumor cell populations with varying molecular and phenotypic profiles within a single tumor specimen [53]. Traditionally, this heterogeneity was attributed primarily to genetic diversity among cancer cell populations. Nonetheless, even genetically identical cells can exhibit significant morphological differences as recent research indicates that tumors exhibit heterogeneity across almost every observable trait due to both genetic and non-genetic sources of variability [54]. One key mechanism driving this heterogeneity is the epigenetic modification of the genome, which can persist through numerous cell divisions [55]. Epigenetic alterations influence the phenotypic states of cancer cells at the proteome level and contribute to the distinct identities of cell types in both healthy and cancerous tissues, despite their shared genetic background [56]. Epigenetic dysregulation has also been the primary factor in lineage plasticity in cancer. Lineage plasticity is described as the ability of cell to change its phenotype under the influence of the surrounding microenvironment, often resulting in tumor progression and also therapy resistance [57]. Regional variations in epigenetic states have been observed in various cancers and can function similarly to genetic alterations in influencing tumor progression. For example, different tumor subtypes show unique epigenetic alteration profiles in colon adenocarcinoma [58] and other cancers, including lung [59], urological [60], brain [61], and ovarian [103]. Additionally, local environmental factors such as hypoxia can lead to the expression of histone demethylases and other epigenetic regulators, affecting gene expression related to specific phenotypes, such as epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition [56]. Furthermore, inflammatory cytokines released by stromal or immune cells can modify DNA methylation and other epigenetic markers [56]. Many of these regional epigenetic variations are associated with aberrant methylation patterns in specific promoters or regulatory elements, which can lead to gene activation or silencing [[62], [63], [64], [65]]. These patterns may also help elucidate the phylogenetic relationships among different clones within tumors, such as those observed in prostate cancer [66].

DNA methylation is a key epigenetic modification that can have significant effects on intratumoral heterogeneity in lung cancer. Methylation aberrations are often non-random and closely associated with tumor progression [55]. Intratumor variability in DNA methylation is notably more pronounced at enhancer regions compared to other genomic features, such as gene promoters or CpG islands [66]. DNA methylation influences the intratumoral heterogeneity of lung cancer in several ways such as gene expression regulation which often leads to the silencing of gene transcription. The diversity in gene expression in lung cancer, arising from varying DNA methylation patterns of different tumor cells, contributes to different cell behavior including proliferation, apoptosis, and response to therapy [67]. Moreover, DNA methylation patterns influence variation between tumor subtypes, and in tandem with histological characteristics or conventional tumor staging, they can be used to define distinct subtypes of lung cancer, thus becoming the new biomarkers for early detection and prediction of prognosis [68]. In NSCLC, molecular characterization has become an additional requirement prior to deciding therapeutic strategies [69] due to the heterogenous characteristics that arise from the varied cells of origin and activated pathways [70].

Abnormal DNA methylation patterns can lead to genomic instability, which may cause further genetic alterations and contribute to the diversity of tumor cell population through the emergence of subclones with different genetic and epigenetic features within the same tumor, increasing heterogeneity [71]. This is because the different molecular subtypes of different tumor areas impacted by aberrant methylation pattern at different promoter regions contribute to different clinical implications, thus representing the intratumoral heterogeneity in lung cancer. Changes in DNA methylation can also affect the expression of genes involved in immune response, such as silencing the genes encoding immune checkpoint molecules or antigens [72]. Silencing of these genes could shield the presence of tumour antigens [73], deregulating the gene expressing the tumour antigen presentation [72] and suppressing the expression of immune checkpoint signals such as cytokines and chemokines [74], thus evading the activation of immune surveillance. The different effects of aberrant DNA methylation on tumor immune evasion contribute to the overall heterogeneity in how the tumor interacts with the immune system. As a result, these changes contribute to variability in treatment responses, therapy resistance and tumor progression [72]. Overall, DNA methylation plays a crucial role in shaping the diversity of tumor cell populations within lung cancer, affecting gene expression, tumor progression, and treatment response. Understanding these methylation patterns can offer insights into tumor biology and potentially lead to more effective and personalized therapeutic strategies.

DNA methylation status as a NSCLC biomarker

Current research on lung cancer is focused on understanding the mechanisms of disease development and progression through alternative epigenetic mechanisms. Studies on NSCLC have been conducted throughout the years to uncover the targetable genetic alterations and mutations as well as therapeutic targets by in vitro, in vivo, and human studies. In vitro studies are mostly conducted to investigate the diagnosis and prognosis of NSCLC and to understand the effects of methylation and demethylation on gene promoters using commercial NSCLC cell lines and tissue samples from patients (Table 1). Reported studies on NSCLC through epigenetics mechanism of DNA methylation via in vitro and in vivo approaches.

As DNA methylation determines the fate of gene expression at specific loci and across the genome, it is suitable and has been commonly used as a biomarker of any abnormalities in cellular functions such as cancer [[75], [76], [77]]. DNA methylation can effectively compensate for drawbacks in imaging examinations and improve the chance of early lung cancer detection, by virtue of its robustness and remarkable sensitivity [27]. Alteration of the DNA methylation plays key roles in early diagnosis, prognosis, classification of cancer stages and choices for disease treatment and management [78]. In mammals, the process of DNA methylation occurs at CpG sites within the DNA, thus CpG sites hypermethylation and genome-wide CpG islands hypomethylation are commonly associated with cancer [79] (Fig. 2). Two types of CpG dinucleotides exist in mammals; the first category is made up of widely scattered CpG dinucleotides in the genome and commonly in hypermethylated pattern. Meanwhile, the second category comprises unmethylated and clustered CpG islands at gene promoters responsible for regulating transcriptional activation and repression [13]. These methylation patterns are disturbed in tumorigenesis by aberrant DNMT expression, influencing the expression of cancer-related genes and initiating the hallmarks of cancer [13]. This is because genome-wide hypomethylation causes instability of genomic sequences, while hypermethylation in gene promoters silences the expression of tumor suppressor genes (TSGs), leading to disruption in the normal regulation of important cellular processes, including DNA repair, cell interactions, cell cycle, apoptosis, and angiogenesis [42].Fig. 2Methylation process disturbed at the CpG site and CpG island due to aberrant expression of DNMTs caused inactivation of TSG and activation of oncogene. Gene promoters reported to be highly methylated in NSCLC include CDK2NA, RASSF1A, APC, MGMT, CDH13, CALCA, and MMP-2 [[80], [81], [82], [83], [84], [85], [86], [87], [88], [89]]. Tobacco smoking, often linked to NSCLC, has been reported to downregulate p16 protein expression [87]. This protein is encoded by the CDK2NA gene, which regulates cell cycle progression [90]. Silencing of the CDK2NA gene increases the expression level of the p16 protein, which is often associated with tumor progression and can be used as a biomarker prognosis and therapeutic response [[90], [91], [92]]. Similarly, other studies have linked hypermethylation at the p16 gene promoter to poor prognosis in patients with lung SCC [81]. A previous study by Lin et al. [109] demonstrated the relationship between p53 and specificity protein (Sp1) in the methylation pattern of DNA-methylating enzymes in tumor development. Another study highlighted that methylation of matrix metalloproteinase (MMP-2) and calcitonin-related polypeptide alpha (CALCA) was observed in NSCLC which reflected poor prognosis compared to the methylation of RAS association domain family 1 (RASSF1) [93]. Furthermore, the methylation patterns at the promoters of DNA repair genes, MLH1 and MSH2, were different between genders and lung cancer subtypes, as observed in a retrospective study involving patients with adenocarcinoma and lung SCC [82]. Another study also found that DNA methylation was involved in the regulation of the ADP-ribosylation factor (ARF)-like 4C (ARL4C) gene, which is responsible for cellular migration and invasion, as hypomethylation was linked to overexpression of ARL4C in both adenocarcinoma and lung SCC [94]. Methylation levels have also been reported to affect RNA expression such as circular RNAs (circRNAs) [95] and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) [96], which are associated with carcinogenesis. In addition, a recent study discovered that demethylation of CD147 in adenocarcinoma and lung SCC suppressed the invasion and metastasis of cancer cells, thus providing insight for a novel therapeutic target in NSCLC [97].

Studies on adenocarcinoma subtypes highlighted common biomarkers observed in NSCLC due to abnormal DNA methylation include RASSF1A [81,88,89], the role of microRNA [98], immunoglobulin protein [99] and mismatch repair gene of hMLH1 and hMHS2 [100]. It has been reported that the gene is highly methylated in SCLC than NSCLC [[101], [102], [103]]; however, another study reported that methylation of RASSF1A was correlated with early stages (I and II) of NSCLC [89]. Another study highlighted the role of infiltrating B-cells and stromal-plasma cells in the tumor microenvironment and as prognostic biomarkers in NSCLC via the regulation of the expression of immunoglobulin kappa C (IGKC) [99]. Moreover, a recent study by Wu et al. (2020) reported that methylation of promoter hMLH1 and hMSH2 silenced the expression of mismatch repair genes, thus potentially inducing the proliferation of EGFR-mutated NSCLC.

In lung SCC studies, Kwon et al. [104] studied the link between DNA methylation patterns with CCDC37, CYTL1, CDO1, LMO3, and SLIT2 and found that reversing abnormal DNA hypermethylation restored the expression of these genes. Furthermore, a study by Liu et al. [85] showed that overexpression of the SNRPB gene worsened the prognosis of NSCLC by inducing tumor cell proliferation. Recent findings reported that hypermethylation in the SPAG6 promoter [105] and miR-27-3p promoter [106] supported lung SCC progression. Hypermethylation of the SPAG6 promoter reduced the expression of the gene and resulted in upregulation of the JAK/STAT pathway, immune infiltration, and increased expression of immunosuppressive genes [105]. Meanwhile, overexpression of SPAG6 was found to inhibit lung SCC malignancy in an in vitro study, thus confirming its role as a tumor inhibitor [105]. As for miR-27-3p, the downregulation of its expression via hypermethylation of the promoter region activates the Notch pathway, a regulator of tumor cell drug resistance, therefore promoting tumor progression [106]. In contrast, another study observed low levels of DNA methylation in patients with concurrent idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and lung SCC that were linked to poor prognosis and outcomes [107]. Other than the conventional in vitro and in vivo studies, a recent study by Zhu et al. [84] emphasized the functional role of methylated enhancer regions as novel biomarkers to facilitate the diagnosis of NSCLC through whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS); they demonstrated that the performance of these methylated biomarkers was comparable with that of the common biomarkers at gene promoters. The authors further reported that there were strong correlations between methylation of enhancer region of CpG site cg16434331 and gene expression of long noncoding RNA (LINC00673), the high expression level of which reflected poor prognosis [108].

In vivo studies reported so far focused on the outcomes of gene knockdown as an approach to further elucidate the pathobiology of NSCLC and to identify the potentially reversible oncogenic mechanisms through gene knockdown or the action of specific drugs (Table 1). Grasse et al. [132] demonstrated similar differential methylation patterns between primary and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) lung cancers and highlighted LDL receptor-related protein 12 (LRP12) as a potential key biomarker for NSCLC carboplatin resistance. The knockdown of culpable genes such as small nuclear ribonucleoproteins SNRPB [109] and DNMT1 [100] reduced tumor growth and proliferation. SNRPB is linked to alternative splicing including RAB26 which plays a functional role in regulating apoptosis of NSCLC cells [110]. Dysregulation of gene splicing is also known as one of the hallmarks of cancer, thus confirming the oncogenic role of SNRPB [109]. Furthermore, upregulation of DNMT1 expression induced hypermethylation at the mismatch repair genes of hMLH1 and hMH2, which promoted the tumor progression of NSCLC. The knockdown of DNMT1 could reverse these effects [100].

The methylation status of TSG is also useful for predicting therapeutic responses and resistance effects. A number of previous studies emphasized that changes in cytosine methylation patterns contributed in predicting the diagnosis, prognosis and treatment responses of lung cancer [91,[111], [112], [113]]. Gene methylation could be a predictor for therapy response; for example, better response to EGFR inhibitors was observed in patients with unmethylated plasma CHFR gene than those with methylated gene [114]. The use of 5-azacytidine, a DNMT inhibitor, was found in a study to facilitate the sensitivity of NSCLC towards PARP (poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase) inhibition and ionizing radiation therapy in a xenograft model [115]. In addition, a recent study by Zhang et al. [73] highlighted that methylation of long-coding RNA could also serve as a potential biomarker in NSCLC as observed in both in vitro and xenograft models. The use of DNMT inhibitors could be beneficial in reversing the resistance to PARP inhibitors in breast cancer gene (BRCA)-proficient non-small cell lung cancer [115]. Interestingly, a recent study by Lee et al. (2023) reported that the use of metformin suppressed DNMT activity via upregulation of miR-148/-52 family members at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels in NSCLC cells [116].

Crosstalk between DNA methylation and miRNA

Growing evidence has linked the crosstalk between DNA methylation and miRNA in regulating cellular transformation [117] as they both are important gene expression regulators, responsible for gene silencing at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels [118]. The promoters of miRNAs or the enzymes linked to them have one or more methylation sites, and the presence of methyl groups can be utilized as a biomarker for the incidence and prevalence of tumors [119]. Dysregulation of both expressions is often observed in diseases, especially cancers [42,120], as the transcriptional processes occur synergistically which marking their roles as cancer biomarkers.

Several studies have also reported that the dysregulation of microRNA is associated with dysregulation of DNA methylation in NSCLC [[121], [122], [123], [124]]. The hypermethylated state of CpG islands of the gene promoter silences the transcriptional process, thus downregulating its expression level [125]. A past study by Watanabe et al. (2011) emphasized the role of DNA methylation in regulating miR-34b and miR-126 expressions. A completely methylated miR-34b was observed in the NSCLC cell line and the methylation profile was reversed upon treatment with 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine, a DNMTs inhibitor [124]. The treatment increases the expression of miR-34b and suppresses the oncogenic c-Met expression, marking its potential as a biomarker for invasive lung cancer [124]. Meanwhile, the expression of miR-126 was silenced by the hypermethylation of its host gene, EGFL7 instead of its promoter gene [124]. A recent study by Lu et al. (2020) revealed that miR-320a was involved in the reversal of cisplatin resistance in lung adenocarcinoma; when miR-320a was successfully induced in A549 cells by the treatment with a demethylating agent, significantly increased apoptosis was observed. This is in line with a current study by Liu et al. (2022) that observed hypermethylation of miR-27-3p promoter in lung SCC cell line silence the expression of miRNA gene, leading to the activation of a tumor cell drug resistance regulator, known as Notch pathway and promotes tumorigenesis. The authors further highlighted the reversal of the methylation pattern at the miR-27-3p promoter by DNMTs inhibitor able to inhibit the Notch pathway [106].

The role of miRNA as cancer biomarkers is controlled by DNA methylation at the promoter region, and the methylation pattern is crucial in defining tumor phenotype, stages, prognosis, and treatment response [71,126,127]. It has been noted that the expression of miR-708-5p and miR-29b is inversely proportional to that of DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B, which promote the expression of metastasis suppressors and delay tumor growth [121,122]. The potential of miRNAs as cancer biomarkers is highlighted by the evidence that their expression levels can be distinguished between malignant and non-cancerous cells. Integration analysis of expression levels of miR-210 and miR-31 with methylation profile of RASSF1A and 3OST2 genes in NSCLC patients portrayed high sensitivity and specificity of early detection compared to healthy patients [128]. Another study also reported that upregulation of several miRNAs such as miR-1179, miR-572, miR-137, miR-3150b, miR-129-2, and miR-10b was seen in malignant tissue but not in healthy tissue [129]. It has also been reported that microRNA can be a biomarker for tracking NSCLC progression; for example, changes in miR-328 expression distinguish NSCLC patients with and without brain metastasis, as the microRNA promotes NSCLC cell migration [98].

DNMTs expression in NSCLC

Abnormalities in DNMT expression influence gene transcription by altering methylation patterns at gene promoters (Table 2). DNMTs are most significantly expressed in cancer cells and tissues [9,133,134], correlating with high cell proliferation rates and disrupting the normal cell cycle [47]. Aberrant DNA methylation due to overexpression of DNMTs has been reported to support tumor growth and malignancy progression, especially in NSCLC; for example, DNA methylation-based biomarkers hold diagnostic and prognostic value in lung SCC [135]. Studies on the effect of DNA methylation abnormalities at the cellular level in animal models highlighted that abrogation or impairment of DNMTs significantly impacts cellular functions and fundamental biological processes [[136], [137], [138]]. A recent study by Liang et al. (2021) highlighted the important role of DNMTs in early-stage tumor screening, detection, and prevention, especially in lung cancer patients. It is notable that the expression of DNMT1 is highly associated with tumorigenesis and cancer progression, while abnormalities in DNMT3A and DNMT3B could either be pro- or anti-cancer (Table 2). Studies on expression pattern of DNMTs with NSCLC.

DNMT1 in NSCLC

Upregulation of DNMT1 expression is widely known to be pro-oncogene and has been associated with lung cancer as the high expression silenced tumor suppressor gene (TSG) and supports cell proliferation in epidermal growth factor (EGFR)-mutated NSCLC [100]. The degree of DNMT1 expression may be favourably correlated with both the human NSCLC malignancy grade and the invasiveness of the cells in vitro [141]. It supports the initial study that highlights the association between lung cancer progression with high DNMT1 gene activity observed in stages of hyperplasia, adenomas and carcinomas [149]. Recent study by Bu et al. [141] highlighted the downregulation of DNMT1 expression resulted in the activation of anti-metastatic genes existed in Wnt signalling pathway. The pathway, mediated by DNMT1 modulates critical transduction pathways related to cell differentiation and migration [141]. Inhibition of DNMT1 suppressed Wnt signalling through downregulation of β-catenin, MMP2 and cyclin D1 expressions, which is responsible for the tumor cell migration and invasion [141]. A study by Belinsky et al. [136] reported that DNMT1 was significantly involved in lung cancer development as the reduction in genetic DNMT1 displayed 50 % decrement of tobacco carcinogen-induced lung cancer in murine model. Another study emphasized multiple TSGs were hypermethylated due to overexpression of DNMT1 gene and it disrupted the normal regulation of p53/Sp1 pathway, resulting in development of lung cancer with poor prognosis [130]. Liu et al. [140] reported overexpression of DNMT1 protein was commonly observed in multistage progression of rat lung cancer. The authors further stated overexpression of DNMT1 hypermethylated cell adhesion-related gene promoter which silenced the expression of CADM1, TIMP3 and N-cadherin, thus promoting tumor progression [140]. Current study by Wu et al. [100] demonstrated DNMT1 promoted cell proliferation in epidermal growth factor receptor-mutated (EGFR-mutated) NSCLC through hypermethylation at the gene promoter of DNA mismatch repair system gene of hMLH1 and hMSH2, thus supressing the expression of the genes [100].

DNMT3A in NSCLC

Previous studies demonstrated overexpression of DNMT3A is observed in NSCLC patients [150] and multistage lung carcinogenesis in rat [140]. Liu et al. [140] further emphasized overexpression of DNMT3A caused inactivation of cell adhesion-related gene as methylation of the gene promoter caused loss of gene expression that regulates tumor growth inhibitor and cell apoptosis in rat multistage lung carcinogenesis. It is supported by a current study which stated DNMT3A facilitated NSCLC cancer cells to evade apoptosis and induce senescent-like phenotype in vitro [144]. Deng et al. [144] further emphasized high level of DNMT3A demonstrated shortened survival rate and poor prognosis in NSCLC patients. In addition, upregulation of DNMT3A was observed among recurrent NSCLC patients compared to non-recurrent patients, thus suggesting the ability of DNMT3A to regulate tyrosine-kinase inhibitor (TKI) resistance in NSCLC therapy [144].

Nevertheless, there are studies reported on the opposite findings of DNMT3A expression with cancer progression. Recent evidence identified downregulation of DNMT3A in NSCLC [97,143], supporting similar trend observed in previous study which demonstrated the loss of DNMT3A facilitated the lung adenocarcinoma growth in mouse model [142]. Interestingly, it was demonstrated by Husni et al. [151] that high DNMT3A expression was correlated with favourable prognosis while low expression reflected poor prognosis in non-invasive lung adenocarcinoma through histological analysis from patients’ tissue sample. The authors further highlighted the characteristic of DNMT3A as independent prognostic biomarker is suitable as a potential favourable prognosis indicator [151]. It is speculated that the role of DNMT3A expression might be influenced by the histological subtype of lung cancer as previous study observed DNMT3A is not overexpressed in NSCLC but only in small cell lung cancer (SCLC) [152]. Past findings warrant the needs for further studies in the DNMT3A expression in regulating DNA methylation state, especially in the lung cancer model.

DNMT3B in NSCLC

Notably, upregulation of DNMT3B promotes cell proliferation and reflects poor prognosis in lung cancer [145,146]. This is because majority of the specific DNMT3B-targeted genes are the key factors in modulating cellular and molecular functions including basic process of development, growth, proliferation, survival and death [148]. Aberrant expression in DNMT3B targets several types of transcription factor and promoter gene which increases the methylation pattern to facilitate tumor progression. The expression of DNMT3B is influenced by FOXO3a transcription factor, whereby the association between the enzyme and transcription factor is observed to be inversely proportional [153]. Previous study by Lin et al. [139] further emphasized overexpression of DNMT3B is associated with TSG promoters hypermethylation in smoking patients. Furthermore, recent literature found overexpression of DNMT3B increased the efficiency of carcinogen-induced transformation and targeted specific genes for methylation during the transformation [148]. The authors also reported upregulation of DNMT3B methylated two common DNMT3B-target genes involving OLIG2 and MAL in lung adenocarcinoma and SCC which induced the tumor suppressor effects. DNMT3B consists of over 30 isoform [154,155] deriving different transcripts [156,157] which could influence the DNA methylation pattern and gene expression [158]. A study identified DNMT3B variants with two lacking exons were commonly observed to be overexpressed in more than half of NSCLC cell lines and primary tumors, labelled as ΔDNMT3B-del [147]. The authors further reported global methylation pattern and specific site of CpG islands hypermethylation detected in transgenic mice lung tissues upregulating ΔDNMT3B-del were similarly detected in human cancer. Recently, a study reported hypermethylation at the promoter host gene lung SCC, SPAG6 is also correlated with the overexpression of DNMT3B [105].

DNMTs as a therapeutic target in NSCLC treatment

The treatment of lung SCC faced a great challenge due to its wide heterogeneity. Current standard treatment for NSCLC comprises of surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy [159] which is chosen based on the stage of the tumor upon diagnosis. Nevertheless, the existing treatment and management remain unsatisfactory especially with relapsed cases and toxicity issues. The issue has also shifted the focus of managing the cancer to the epigenetics approach by its reversible mechanism compared to the conventional cancer management.

Mechanism of DNMTs inhibitors

The reversible mechanism of DNA methylation makes it as a new potential target in reversing cancer progression and reducing the burden effects by inhibiting the DNMTs expression. The inhibitors mainly reverse the methylation of TSG promoters by trapping the DNMTs activity which then allows re-expression of the TSG [13] (Fig. 3). Most significant DNMTs inhibitors are nucleoside analogues known as azacytidine (5-aza) and 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine (decitabine) which have been approved for its usage initially for hematological malignancies by FDA [13]. It is reported that decitabine is a more potent DNMTs inhibitor [160] due to its similar deoxyribonucleotide structure which enables direct incorporation into DNA compared to 5-aza [8]. Nevertheless, previous study reported that 5-aza poses greater inhibitory effects on DNA synthesis compared to RNA synthesis as it incorporated into both RNA and DNA synthesis with lesser extent into the latter [161]. 5-aza and decitabine share similar mechanisms of action; both are phosphorylated into 5-azacytidine- and decitabine triphosphate by uridine-cytidine- and deoxycytidine kinase [161,162]. The compounds are then incorporated into RNA and DNA (for 5-aza) or DNA only (for decitabine) and covalently bind to DNMTs as irreversible inhibitors [161,162]. The similar effects of 5-aza and decitabine towards DNMTs expression and DNA methylation still possess several distinct effects such as cell cycle, cell viability, apoptosis, DNA damage and gene expression as seen in NSCLC cell lines [81]. In terms of cell viability, 5-aza is reported to induce greater toxicity and apoptosis markers compared to decitabine in NSCLC cell lines [81]. It is believed that the key differences between the two drugs are due to the incorporation of 5-aza into both RNA and DNA while decitabine is only limited to DNA incorporation [81,163]. The demethylating effect of the TSG promoter region also suppresses the tumor cells' proliferation activity and prevents drug resistance without suppressing DNA synthesis [13,162]. However, recent studies have noted that decitabine can cause toxicity at doses exceeding 0.35 mg/kg in mice [164].

Pre-clinical studies on DNMTs inhibitors

Targeting DNMT1 with mono- and combination therapies has emerged as a promising strategy for treating NSCLC. Several growing pieces of evidence are now pursuing the study on monotherapy DNMT inhibitors, however, the efficacy remains elusive. DNMT inhibitors, such as decitabine and 5-azacytidine, have demonstrated potential in overcoming drug resistance mechanisms, with studies suggesting these inhibitors can act as resistance biomarkers and enhance treatment efficacy [106,132,139,[165], [166], [167]]. Research by Jabbour et al. (2008) highlights that DNMT inhibitors can selectively impact cancer cells without disrupting the normal cell cycle progression from the G1 to S phase. While high doses of these inhibitors can halt DNA synthesis and induce cytotoxic effects, lower doses have shown the ability to restore the activation of tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) by counteracting DNA hypermethylation, thereby increasing the sensitization towards immunotherapy and exhibiting antitumor effects [162]. A new generation of nucleoside drugs, named as zebularine is currently in pursue as the drug portrayed promising outcomes in terms of its potency in vitro and as combination agent with conventional therapy, for instance immune- radio- or chemotherapy [168]. The inhibitory effect of zebularine is due to the tight covalent complexes formed between zebularine-substituted DNA complexes and DNMT protein [169], further warranting its potential for future clinical trials [168]. Although zebularine has better stability than the previous generation in acidic and neutral pH [170], the drug requires frequent or continuous administration to sustain the DNMT inhibitory effects [171].

Currently, a combination of DNMT inhibitors with immunotherapy exhibited great promise as an alternative approach to reducing the lung cancer burden especially for NSCLC by managing the diagnosis and prognosis through reversible epigenetic mechanism (Table 3). In vitro studies portrayed the potential of decitabine in suppressing the DNA methylation process as the drug enhances the effect of gefitinib, an EGFR inhibitor in NSCLC cells [166]. Decitabine reverses the silenced EGFR expression which sensitizes the cancer cells towards gefitinib. The co-treatment of decitabine with gefitinib significantly increases the inhibitory effects and induces apoptosis of lung cancer cell lines, along with the deregulation of EGFR protein expression [166]. The hypermethylation status of the promoter gene responsible for tumor suppression and progression is responsible for the immune resistance in cancer [172,173]. It is also evident by previous literature that decitabine improved survival rate in the late stage of NSCLC by restoring expression of silenced TSG although with major side effects of hematopoietic toxicity [174]. Several studies reported that decitabine can partially reverse cisplatin resistance by inhibiting hMLH1 in human NSCLC cell line [172] and partially reverse the gefitinib resistance by demethylation of DNA promoter regions of RASSF1A and GADD45ꞵ in lung adenocarcinoma cell line [173]. Furthermore, a recent pre-clinical trial has employed DNMTi to induce aberrant DNA damage repair and sensitize NSCLC cells to PARP inhibition and radiation therapy [115]. DNMT inhibitors are thought to impact radiosensitivity through multiple mechanisms. Radiation induces DNA damage via ionization or the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which results in the death of tumor cells but may also encourage the metastasis and progression of radioresistant cancer cells [175]. As inhibitors of DNA synthesis, DNMT inhibitors also reduce the number of tumor clonogens, thereby increasing the cytotoxic effects and apoptosis on proliferative cells following radiotherapy [176,177]. It is evident that PARP inhibition induced DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) and sensitized lung cancer cells towards ionizing radiation better compared to monotherapy of chemotherapeutic or ionizing radiation [178,179]. Current studies emphasized that treatment with iDNMT1 significantly restores the methylation pattern of the miR-27-3p promoter and downregulates the activity of the Notch pathway, thus enhancing the tumor sensitivity to radiation-frequency ablation (RFA) in lung SCC cells [106]. Current clinical trials on DNMTs inhibitors

The rapid increase in lung cancer cases has forced an urgent need for novel and better treatment regimes. As DNA methylation affects cellular functions involving proliferation, differentiation, and migration, a few ongoing clinical trials combining conventional drugs and DNMT inhibitors are being pursued (Table 4). On-going clinical trials were conducted to pursue the ultimate goal of finding a comprehensive treatment and management of the type of cancer, including the combination of DNMT inhibitors with conventional drugs. The use of nucleoside inhibitors, such as 5-aza and decitabine with anti-PD1/PD-L1/CTLA-4 is also being considered. However, the outcome of the prognosis of cancer patients remains unsatisfactory. There was no significant improvement observed in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) among previously treated advanced NSCLC patients with the use of pembrolizumab and oral azacytidine in phase 2 clinical trials [181,182]. In contrast, a previous study reported that a combination of 5-aza with entinostat, a histone deacetylase inhibitor showed potential improvement in NSCLC patients survival rate with common hematological side effects, reactions at the injection site, hyperglycemia and gastrointestinal discomfort [183]. Several reports on the use of nab-paclitaxel with 5-aza as a second-line treatment for advanced NSCLC reported encouraging outcomes as it was tolerable [184] and portrayed the safety and efficacy of nab-paclitaxel in elderly patients [185]. Nevertheless, a study reported that the beneficial effects of the treatment were due to the nab-paclitaxel monotherapy, but not from the 5-aza treatment [186]. Another recent clinical study tested the use of aerosolized azacytidine and the phase I study observed tolerable with no adverse effects among patients with pre-malignant lesions [187]. Conclusion

The emergence and aggressiveness of NSCLC subtypes have revealed gaps in cancer management, especially in diagnosis and prognostication. Accurate diagnosis of NSCLC subtypes is critical in selecting the optimal cancer management and therapy regimen. A promising anticancer approach targets the epigenetic machinery, using a combination of DNA methylation status and alterations in the expression of DNMTs, the methylating enzymes, as biomarkers for both diagnosis and prognosis (Fig. 4). These epigenetic changes have been associated with NSCLC progression. However, more in-depth studies are needed to elucidate the role of DNMTs in cellular and mechanical signaling pathways and to ascertain their potential as a novel therapeutic target. DNMT inhibitors, through their ability to reverse aberrant gene expression due to altered DNA methylation patterns, have earned a place in the armamentarium of novel cancer therapies; however, more comprehensive studies are needed to ensure the safety profile of these inhibitors.
