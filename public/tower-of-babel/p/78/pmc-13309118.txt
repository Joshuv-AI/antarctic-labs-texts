Antrochoanal polyp with aberrant attachment causing airway obstruction: Presenting an anaesthesia challenge in resource-limited settings
Authors: T. Krishna Prasad, Venu V. S. Krishna, Raghul Ramesh, Kiran K. George
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Arguably, all anaesthesiologists starting their career must be well versed with difficult airway strategies. Judicious selection of a particular technique for intubation with dynamic re-adjustments while encountering unexpected challenges is required for the best attempt at securing the airway. Additionally, the need for a secondary plan of intubation, arising from failed attempts, is essential. The immediate objective is continuation of oxygen delivery while avoiding repeated instrumentation that may worsen obstruction or bleeding. Decisions such as proceeding with anaesthesia, choice of induction method, administration of neuromuscular blockade, and escalation to cricothyroidotomy or tracheostomy often rely more on clinical judgement than strong evidence as high-quality data in obstructed airway management are limited.

Airway tumours can create major technical difficulties despite minimal symptoms prior to anaesthesia.[1] We encountered a patient with airway obstruction due to a large antrochoanal polyp which prompted a review of airway rescue options in a resource-limited environment. A 24-year-old male (American Society of Anesthesiologists class I, body mass index 22 kg/m2) was scheduled for excision of a left-sided antrochoanal polyp that extended into the posterior nasopharynx through an enlarged maxillary ostium. The patient described mild swallowing and sleep-related obstruction with no significant dyspnoea. Airway assessment revealed adequate neck mobility, Mallampati class III, and complete left nasal blockage. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a complete left unilateral nasal obstruction with the tumour extending partly to the left side of the oral cavity. As the obstruction presented unilaterally and in order to accommodate an additional access to the tumour from the oral side during surgery, we planned for right nasotracheal intubation using direct laryngoscopy as our first choice. Because fibre-optic bronchoscopy was unavailable, retrograde intubation and surgical tracheostomy were prepared as contingency strategies for which prior constraints were obtained. Premedication was administered with intramuscular glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg and topical nasal decongestion using 0.1% xylometazoline 30 minutes before surgery. A 16-gauge intravenous (IV) line was secured, and standard monitoring (electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry) was applied. A nasal airway size 7.0 was inserted through the right nostril to facilitate airflow during mask ventilation. After four minutes of pre-oxygenation with 100% oxygen via a well-sealed facemask, anaesthesia was induced using fentanyl 2 μg/kg IV followed by propofol 2 mg/kg IV.

Effective mask ventilation was confirmed before administering IV succinylcholine 100 mg. Direct laryngoscopy using a Macintosh #3 blade revealed a Cormack–Lehane grade 3b view: The epiglottis was seen but partially tethered to the polyp, preventing visualisation of the glottic opening [Figure 1]. After induction, we encountered difficulty in insertion of a flexometallic tube (FMT) with a 7 mm internal diameter, probably due to the dynamic nature of the obstruction of the airway by the pedunculated tumour upon loss of tone of pharyngeal muscles from anaesthesia induction. Repeated attempts at nasal intubation were avoided, and we quickly adopted a novel plan of oral intubation using direct laryngoscopy with gentle intra-oral manipulation of the tumour position to the left side, using Magill forceps by the otorhinolaryngology surgeon, away from the path of tube introduction to successfully facilitate the passage of a smaller 6.5 mm size cuffed FMT, despite challenges of limited space in our second attempt. Apneic oxygenation was done using a nasal cannula with a flow of 4l/min to maintain oxygen saturation during this attempt. Following surgical removal of the polyp, the patient was extubated awake.

This case illustrates that upper airway lesions may not always display obvious predictors of difficult intubation. This sort of obstruction is easily identifiable on CT scans which the surgeons will have acquired before planning such surgeries. We anaesthesiologists must be familiar with the reading and interpretation of CT and magnetic resonance imaging scans as they provide valuable insights into airway anatomy and potential challenges. Besides, the ability to review and modify one’s technique instantly, due to the dynamic nature of obstruction of the tumour, as in our case, can help to avoid escalating the airway crisis situation.

Nevertheless, anaesthesiologists should acquire and maintain competence in multiple airway access methods. Retrograde intubation can provide a reliable option when conventional oral approaches are unsuccessful.[2] In distorted anatomy, even video-assisted techniques may fail.[3] Without this alternative, securing a surgical airway or awakening the patient would have been required. Adequate mask ventilation allowed us the margin of safety and preserved oxygenation and also provided the time to think and re-assess subsequent airway interventions. Comparable success using alternative airway strategies has also been described in paediatric patients.[45] Careful preparation, familiarity with alternate techniques, and avoidance of repeated traumatic attempts are particularly important in operating rooms lacking advanced equipment.[4] Maintenance of oxygenation, through apnoeic oxygenation, and thoughtful escalation of interventions remain fundamental to the safe management of unexpected difficult airways.
