Association of an almond-based low-carbohydrate diet with continuous glucose monitoring metrics in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xianming Li, Yanqin Xu, Xulin Wang, Yu Ding, Sihui Luo, Yu Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the association between an almond-based low-carbohydrate diet (LCD) and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) metrics in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsThis was a prospective randomized controlled study. Hospitalized patients with T2DM from Changshu No.1 People’s Hospital were recruited from January 2022 to December 2022. 33 patients using CGM were included and randomly divided into the low-fat diet (LFD) group or the LCD with almonds group based on the random number table. Primary CGM metrics including time in range (TIR), time in tight range (TITR), time above range (TAR), time below range (TBR) were compared between the two groups. Linear regression analysis was performed to assess the association between diet group and nocturnal glycemic metrics.ResultsCompared to LFD group, patients in the LCD with almonds group had higher nighttime TIR [98.51% (95.83, 99.88) vs. 91.67% (89.52, 98.07), P < 0.01] and TITR [94.14% (87.57, 98.41) vs. 77.58% (52.18, 90.58), P < 0.05]. Nighttime TAR [0.00% (0.00, 0.00) vs. 1.39% (0.00, 7.95), P < 0.01], HBGI [0.15 (0.01, 0.34) vs. 0.98 (0.08, 3.73), P < 0.05], and SD (15.57 ± 6.21 mg/dL vs. 23.62 ± 9.12 mg/dL, P < 0.05) were significantly lower in the LCD with almonds group. Multivariable regression demonstrated that LCD with almonds was independently associated with improvements in nocturnal TIR (β = 6.65%, P = 0.010) and TITR (β = 14.60%, P = 0.016) after adjustment for age, sex, and diabetes duration.ConclusionThe almond-based low-carbohydrate diet improved nocturnal glycemic control in patients with type 2 diabetes. These improvements in nocturnal TIR and TITR remained significant after multivariable adjustment for age, sex, and diabetes duration.Clinical trial registryThe study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (registration no. ChiCTR2600116495).Subject terms: Randomized controlled trials, Type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia [1]. Dietary management plays a central role in glycemic control, with commonly recommended strategies including low-carbohydrate diets (LCD), low-fat diets (LFD), the Mediterranean diets, the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension diets (DASH) diet, and vegetarian or vegan dietary patterns [2]. Among these approaches, LCD have attracted increasing attention for its effectiveness in promoting weight loss, improving glycemic control, and optimizing lipid profiles [3–5].

Nuts, particularly almonds, are rich in unsaturated fatty acids, plant-based proteins, fiber, and bioactive compounds, representing a typical nutrient-dense food [6]. Evidence suggests that nuts may confer metabolic benefits, including improvements in lipid profiles, reductions in inflammatory responses, and enhanced insulin sensitivity [7, 8]. As a component of LCD, almonds may replace carbohydrates with healthy fats and proteins, which not only helps control total energy intake but may also further enhance glycemic management. Previous studies have shown that incorporating nuts into LCD can effectively improve glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and fasting blood glucose (FBG) in patients with T2DM [9]. However, the mechanisms through which almonds contribute to the glycemic benefits of an LCD remain to be elucidated, highlighting the need for further investigation into their specific metabolic effects.

Traditional glucose monitoring methods, such as self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG), are limited by invasiveness and poor long-term adherence. In contrast, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) provides real-time data on glucose dynamics through subcutaneous sensors worn for 7–14 days [10]. CGM not only captures glucose fluctuations but also generates advanced metrics, enabling a more comprehensive evaluation of glycemic control.

Although numerous studies have demonstrated the benefits of LCD and nuts for glycemic control in patients with T2DM, few have specifically investigated the effects of incorporating almonds into an LCD on glycemic outcomes measured by CGM, highlighting the need for further investigation into the metabolic role of nuts. To address this gap, this study aimed to investigate the association between an almond-based LCD and CGM-derived glycemic metrics in patients with T2DM, providing evidence to guide dietary interventions for this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective randomized controlled trial enrolled 33 hospitalized T2DM patients from Changshu No.1 People’s Hospital between January and December 2022. The inclusion criteria were followed: (1) diagnosis of T2DM by an endocrinologist; (2) age 40–80 years; (3) no adjustments in oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin regimens within the two weeks prior to enrollment; (4) adequate communication skills and language comprehension; and (5) Participants who provided informed consent and voluntarily agreed to take part in this study. Exclusion criteria included: (1) fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 16.7 mmol/L; (2) Individuals who regularly consumed nuts at ≥4 bags per week; (3) nut allergies or masticatory dysfunction; (4) presence of severe systemic diseases or complications; (5) severe psychiatric disorders or cognitive dysfunction; (6) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (7) current use of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors; (8) recent episodes of diarrhea or diagnosed gastrointestinal disorders; and (9) irregular dietary patterns. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changshu No.1 People’s Hospital (Approval No. 202128) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical information were collected at baseline via structured questionnaires completed by participants. Collected information included: (1) demographic data: age, gender, height, weight, and duration of T2DM; and (2) laboratory parameters: systolic and diastolic blood pressure, FBG, and HbA1c. CGM data were provided by the participants for study analysis.

Randomization, intervention, and follow-up

Randomization and dietary intervention

Before randomization, participants underwent a nut-free washout period and wore CGM devices continuously for two weeks to establish baseline glucose metrics. After completing the washout and receiving standardized dietary education based on the Patient Education Manual for Routine T2DM Diets, participants were randomly assigned, using a computer-generated random number table, to either the LFD control group or the LCD with almonds intervention group. During the washout period, all participants abstained from consuming any nuts to avoid potential confounding effects.

The LFD group followed the Chinese dietary guidelines for T2DM, with a total energy intake of 25–30 kcal/kg/day, consisting of 50–65% carbohydrates, 20–30% fats, and the remainder from protein. Participants received individualized, one-on-one dietary education covering the principles and implementation of the diet, portion measurement, and strategies for hypoglycemia prevention.

The LCD with almonds group consumed 60 g/day of roasted, unsalted almonds, skin-on almonds (seeds of Prunus dulcis), which were provided by Changshu No. 1 People’s Hospital and pre-packaged in vacuum-sealed bags one day before distribution to ensure freshness and consistent portioning. This daily almond intake replaced 150 g of carbohydrate-rich staple foods, while the composition of other dietary components remained unchanged. Participants in the LCD with almonds group were advised to consume almonds in the interval between lunch and dinner. Education for the almond group additionally addressed the metabolic benefits of almonds, recommended consumption timing, precautions, and recognition of potential adverse effects. Participants in both groups adhered to their respective dietary regimens for 12 weeks under the guidance and supervision of the research team.

Follow-up and CGM monitoring

During the intervention, participants were regularly followed up to monitor adherence, dietary intake, and adverse events. Follow-up visits included structured dietary recalls, review of daily food logs, and assessment of any gastrointestinal or metabolic symptoms to ensure participant safety and compliance. Investigators provided personalized guidance to maintain adherence to the almond-based LCD throughout the 12-week intervention period.

At baseline, prior to the start of dietary intervention, participants wore CGM devices continuously for two weeks to establish baseline glucose metrics. After completing the 12-week intervention, participants again wore CGM devices for two weeks to assess post-intervention changes in glycemic metrics, allowing for direct comparison with baseline data.

Outcomes

Glucose profiles were obtained using CGM devices that measured interstitial glucose every 10 s and reported 5-min mean values. In accordance with the 2023 International Consensus Statement on CGM Metrics in Clinical Trials [11, 12], core clinically relevant metrics were calculated from raw CGM data.

Primary outcomes included (1) mean glucose levels; (2) time in tight range (TITR, 70–140 mg/dL, 3.9–7.8 mmol/L), time in range (TIR, 70–180 mg/dL, 3.9–10.0 mmol/L), time above range (TAR, >180 mg/dL, 10.0 mmol/L), time below range (TBR, <70 mg/dL, 3.9 mmol/L). Secondary outcomes: (1) high blood glucose index (HBGI), low blood glucose index (LBGI), standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV). CGM data includes three periods: whole day (24 h), nighttime (00:00–06:00), and daytime (06:00–24:00). Nocturnal hypoglycemic events were defined as episodes of continuous glucose values below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) or 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L) for at least 15 consecutive minutes during the nocturnal period.

Statistical analysis

Missing CGM data were minimal (<5%) and were primarily due to temporary sensor signal interruptions. Periods with missing data were excluded from the analysis, and no imputation was performed, as the remaining data satisfied recommended completeness criteria for CGM-based studies. Descriptive statistics were summarized for the study participants’ baseline characteristics. Categorical variables were summarized by frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using independent-samples t-tests for normally distributed variables and Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables. Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to identify independent factors associated with nocturnal glycemic outcomes (TIR, TITR, TAR, HBGI, and SD), adjusting for age, sex, and diabetes duration. All models were adjusted for prespecified covariates with two-tailed α = 0.05 as the significance threshold.

Sample size calculation for the key CGM-derived outcome was based on the following assumptions: a clinically significant mean difference of 0.10 mmol/L in 24-h glucose variability, and a common standard deviation of 0.10 mmol/L [13]. The calculation assumed a significance level of 5% and 80% power for a parallel-group comparison. Based on these assumptions, 33 participants were included in the final analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 33 hospitalized adults with T2DM were enrolled (15 males; mean age 57.45 ± 10.67 years; BMI 24.63 ± 2.25 kg/m²; diabetes duration 7.55 ± 7.30 years). Of these, 18 patients (54.55%) were randomized to LFD group and 15 (45.45%) to LCD with almonds group. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1 and Fig. 1. Participants in the LCD with almonds group were significantly older than those in the LFD group (61.90 ± 9.86 vs. 53.80 ± 10.10 years, P = 0.028). No significant between-group differences were observed in CGM metrics across 24-h, daytime, and nighttime periods at baseline (Table S1, P > 0.05).

Effects on glycemic control

No significant differences in daytime or 24-h CGM metrics were observed between two groups (P > 0.05).

Associations with nocturnal glycemic metrics

Multivariable linear regression (Table 3) demonstrated that LCD with almonds group was associated with significantly improved nocturnal glucose management compared with LFD group. In unadjusted models, LCD with almonds group showed positive associations with nocturnal TIR improvement (β = 7.31, 95% CI: 3.25–11.37, P = 0.001, R² = 0.29), which remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, and diabetes duration (β = 6.65, 95% CI: 1.91–11.39, P = 0.010, adjusted R² = 0.32). Significant improvements were also observed for nocturnal TITR in both unadjusted (β = 12.30, 95% CI: 2.81–21.79; P = 0.016; R² = 0.17) and adjusted models (β = 14.60, 95% CI: 3.41–25.79, P = 0.016, adjusted R² = 0.20). No significant associations were observed for nocturnal TAR, SD, and HBGI after adjustment for age, sex, and diabetes duration.

Incidence of nocturnal hypoglycemia

After 3 months of intervention, the proportion of participants experiencing nocturnal hypoglycemic events did not differ significantly in the LCD with almonds group and the LFD group (Table 4). Nocturnal hypoglycemia at the TBR < 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) was observed in 8/15 (53.33%) participants in the LCD with almonds group and 5/18 (27.78%) in the LFD group (P = 0.169), while events at the <54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L) threshold occurred in 2/15 (13.33%) and 2/18 (11.11%) participants, respectively (P = 1.000).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between an almond-based LCD and CGM metrics in patients with T2DM. The results demonstrated that this dietary intervention significantly improved multiple nocturnal CGM metrics. Notably, LCD with almonds was independently associated with a 6.65% increase in nocturnal TIR (95% CI 1.91–11.39, P = 0.010) and a 14.60% increase in TITR (95% CI 3.41–25.79, P = 0.016). Importantly, CGM was used to specifically evaluate the impact of almonds within the LCD on nighttime glucose fluctuations, highlighting their contribution to improved nocturnal glycemic regulation and stability. These findings emphasize the potential value of focusing on time-specific glycemic patterns, particularly during the nocturnal period, when glucose regulation is strongly influenced by endogenous insulin secretion and hepatic glucose output. However, after adjustment, the independent effects were mainly observed in nocturnal TIR and TITR, suggesting that the primary benefit of this dietary intervention lies in improving time-in-range metrics.

This study demonstrated that an almond-based LCD significantly improved nocturnal CGM metrics in patients with T2DM, whereas no significant changes were observed in daytime or overall 24-h measures. The advised consumption of almonds between lunch and dinner may partly explain the observed improvements in nocturnal glycemic metrics. Previous studies have shown that almond intake before or with meals can attenuate postprandial glucose excursions in individuals with dysglycemia or well-controlled T2DM and improve CGM-derived glycemic profiles [13, 14]. Therefore, consuming almonds in the late post-lunch or pre-dinner interval may have contributed to more stable evening-to-nighttime glucose patterns. Nevertheless, as the present study did not specifically evaluate the effects of different almond intake timings, this potential explanation warrants further investigation. Compared with the LFD group, participants in the LCD with almonds group showed higher nocturnal TIR and TITR and lower nocturnal TAR, HBGI, and SD in between-group comparisons. However, after multivariable adjustment, the independent associations remained significant only for nocturnal TIR and TITR. Importantly, CGM enables the characterization of nocturnal glycemic patterns, a physiologically distinct period characterized by reduced physical activity, altered hormonal secretion, and increased vulnerability to unrecognized hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia. Nocturnal glucose instability may exert an influence on fasting glucose levels and long-term metabolic outcomes, yet it is often missed by conventional monitoring approaches [15]. The use of CGM enabled continuous 24-h monitoring, allowing for a precise assessment of nighttime glucose fluctuations and highlighting the specific contribution of almonds to nocturnal glycemic stability. Previous studies have primarily focused on fasting glucose, a randomized crossover controlled-feeding trial among Chinese patients with T2DM reported that daily consumption of approximately 60 g of almonds (20% of total calories) reduced fasting glucose by 5.9% and HbA1c by 3.0% in patients with baseline HbA1c ≤ 8% (64 mmol/mol), supporting the role of almonds in ameliorating glycemic control [16]. Notably, our time-stratified CGM data suggest that improved nocturnal glucose regulation may underlie these benefits, potentially contributing to lower fasting glucose the following morning. Collectively, these findings indicate that incorporating almonds into an LCD may specifically enhance nighttime glucose stability and reduce nocturnal hyperglycemia, offering novel insights for personalized dietary strategies in clinical practice.

SD, a key indicator of glycemic variability that is not captured by traditional markers such as HbA1c, was also significantly lower in the LCD with almonds group. Growing evidence indicates that short-term glucose fluctuations and glycaemic variability are independently associated with oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with T2DM [17, 18], underscoring the clinical importance of variability-focused metrics. A cohort study confirmed that almond intake can attenuate postprandial glucose excursions and fluctuations [14]. By restricting carbohydrate intake, an LCD helps blunt sharp postprandial glucose surges, while almonds, as low-glycemic-index foods, promote satiety and support overall carbohydrate control. Moreover, Sing-Chung Li et al. showed that incorporating 60 g of almonds daily improves insulin resistance in patients with T2DM [19]. Taken together, these complementary effects suggest that an LCD supplemented with almonds not only mitigates postprandial glucose spikes but may also contribute to more stable nocturnal glucose levels, reducing nighttime hyperglycemia exposure and overall glycemic variability. Given the growing recognition that glycaemic variability independently predicts microvascular and macrovascular complications [20, 21], the observed reduction in SD further supports the clinical relevance of this dietary approach.

In the present study, although the LCD with almonds group exhibited a lower mean 24-h glucose level compared to the LFD group (114.70 mg/dL vs. 138.35 mg/dL), this difference did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, a randomized crossover trial conducted in India reported that daily almond consumption led to a significantly lower mean 24-h glucose level compared with a control diet (115.20 mg/dL vs. 124.20 mg/dL) [13]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the improvements observed in nocturnal glycemic profiles with an almond-based LCD are predominantly attributable to reductions in glycemic variability and prolonged time spent within the target glucose range, rather than changes in mean glucose levels alone.

Nuts are rich in beneficial components, including dietary fiber, magnesium, and mono- and polyunsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs and PUFAs), all of which contribute to improved insulin sensitivity and better glycemic control [22, 23]. In particular, Fermentable fiber is metabolized to short-chain fatty acids, enhancing GLP-1 secretion, delaying gastric emptying, and promoting glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. A randomized controlled trial conducted in China demonstrated that participants in the LCD with almonds group exhibited significantly higher GLP-1 levels compared to those in the LFD group [24]. Magnesium, an essential cofactor in insulin signaling, is inversely correlated with insulin resistance, and supplementation improves glycemic outcomes in T2DM [25–27]. A 12-week isocaloric intervention with whole nuts increased serum MUFAs and oleic acid, negatively correlating with HbA1c and fasting glucose, suggesting improved insulin sensitivity via membrane lipid composition [28]. Beyond these direct metabolic effects, nuts exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions, enhancing endothelial function and reducing cardiovascular risk. They also modulate gut microbiota and influence central nervous system activity via the gut-brain axis; fMRI studies revealed reduced insula reactivity to palatable food cues after nut intake, potentially decreasing consumption of energy-dense foods and improving daily energy balance [24, 29, 30]. Additionally, nuts provide a palatable, satisfying option that may improve adherence to LCDs. Collectively, these metabolic, systemic, gut-brain, and behavioral effects underscore the multifaceted role of nuts in supporting glycemic regulation and overall metabolic health in T2DM.

This study has several limitations. First, all participants were Chinese patients with T2DM, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic populations. Second, the relatively small sample size may affect the representativeness of the results. Additionally, baseline HbA1c levels in our study population were relatively well controlled, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to patients with suboptimal glycemic control or higher baseline HbA1c levels. Future studies are warranted to evaluate the effects of this dietary intervention in populations with poorer glycemic control.

Nevertheless, the study also offers multiple strengths. By utilizing CGM to capture 24 h glucose profiles, we were able to comprehensively assess postprandial glucose excursions, nocturnal glucose patterns, and subtle glycemic fluctuations that are difficult to detect with conventional monitoring methods. The study employed a multidimensional set of metrics, allowing for a more precise evaluation of glycemic control, variability, and the risk of hypo- and hyperglycemia. These findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the potential benefit of an almond-based LCD in improving glycemic outcomes among patients with T2DM and offer valuable insights for clinical management strategies in this population. From a clinical perspective, incorporating almonds into an LCD represents a simple, culturally adaptable, and well-tolerated dietary modification.

Conclusion

In conclusion, an almond-based LCD was associated with improved nocturnal glycemic control in patients with T2DM, primarily reflected by higher nocturnal TIR and TITR. These findings suggest that this dietary approach may be particularly effective in optimizing nighttime time-in-range metrics in patients with T2DM.
