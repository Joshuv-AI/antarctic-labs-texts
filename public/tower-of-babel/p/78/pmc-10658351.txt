Neuropathology on Trial
Authors: Clayton A Wiley
Published: 2020

To the Editor:

Anyone practicing medicine in the United States eventually will be dragged into the legal system. Although some specialties are more afflicted with legal repercussions than others, pathology is occasionally involved in litigation. But what about neuropathology? How often do legal cases hinge on our subspecialty?

Aside from the voluminous forensic medical legal cases, certainly there are numerous neurological sequelae of medical misadventures where neuropathological expertise is helpful in deciphering what happened, but how often is our clinical practice the focus of litigation. In other words, how often is the neuropathologist on trial?

It began one afternoon in August of 2015 with a call from an attorney. His request was fairly simple: Would I look at 3 hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides with only the clinical information available at time of intraoperative consultation and render an interpretation? This is highly reminiscent of my approach to teaching postdoctoral trainees to focus on an objective review of histopathology as a means of formulating a broad, unbiased differential. The following week the slides turned up and I was saddened to see they were minute fragments of reactive brain tissue with no distinguishing features so I was not able to render any significant diagnosis.

When I related this to the attorney, he asked if I would be surprised to know that the original pathologist had interpreted them to show a glioblastoma (GBM). I said that was impossible. No one could render such a diagnosis on these 3 slides. Certainly, there was more material and more stains. No, the attorney had requested all the material from the hospital and this was all they provided him. I reiterated it was still impossible for a GBM diagnosis to be rendered on that material and if I could see the original surgical pathology report I could help identify exactly what was done.

It was at this stage that the attorney elected to break the blind and tell me the background of the case, to see if I was willing to serve as an expert witness. He began by saying I was not the first neuropathologist he had approached, indeed he rattled off a rather long list of my colleagues before someone referred him to me. Well I never thought I was the pick of the litter, but I found the attorney’s frankness refreshing. He said no one was willing to consult on the case (indeed only 1 neuropathologist was even willing to look at the case) because the defendant institution was a highly respected academic medical center (AMC). He ended the tale by saying he and his client were approaching the statute of limitations to bring suit and just needed to know if I would help them.

The case definitely had the ring of David versus Goliath, so with the substantial caveat that there was all sorts of missing information, that I was not sure what my final opinion would be, I agreed to look at more materials on the case. I informed the attorney of my fees and said I thought I could help interpret the medical record and certainly the histopathology but that I preferred not to comment on “Standard of Care” (SOC) issues. I had recollected previous times SOC had come up and I just preferred to limit my input to what are the facts of the case and let someone else comment on SOC.

Without going into excessive detail, the case distilled to a misread brain biopsy. The patient had undergone radiation therapy for a chordoma 5 years previously and a new temporal lobe lesion was biopsied and read as GBM rather than radiation necrosis. It was an awful mistake that should not have happened but it did. At this stage to fully describe this case, I would need several pages of the case details that would prove the nature of the error, but it is not the point of this treatise to re-try the case, but rather to describe the moral dilemma and the psychological consequences of “the system.”

Before the diagnostic error had been discovered, the patient was advised of the malignant diagnosis and underwent a temporal lobe resection. Later when resection and biopsy were reviewed, there was no GBM. From my naive perspective this was so straightforward it should just be settled and the patient compensated. Isn’t this exactly the reason medical liability insurance was created? Mistakes happen. I wish they didn’t, but they do, and when a patient is harmed, they should be compensated and not expected to suffer the consequences with no recourse. Isn’t that the way all insurance (auto, fire, etc.) works?

Apparently, life, or at least the law, is not that simple. Perhaps my language is too simple or simplistic. I use the words “error” and “mistake” frequently and interchangeably. Yet in everything leading up to and including the trial, I almost never heard those words. When used they seemed to precipitate a quick frown, scowl or grimace on the attorney’s face. Why? What was freely admitted upfront was that physicians are not expected to be perfect but they are expected to be part of a process that generates quality care. And yet what every physician dealing with the health care system knows is that much of his or her time is spent steering the patient through a morass of health care trying to avoid problems.

When efficiency experts talk to physicians they frequently point to “The Toyota Model” (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Toyota_Way). I can’t be the only person who is amazed that something as complex as a car can be assembled, shipped and sold to the consumer for so little money. And with the exception of the occasional recall, the Toyota Model works flawlessly, car after car. That said it is hard to imagine anyone with a modicum of exposure to health care thinks there is anything analogous between a car assembly line and medicine. Simply with all of the construction going on around an AMC, it is a wonder the patient can get from the parking lot to the admissions lobby to begin their health care. Of course, that is no excuse for process errors, it just highlights how far we have to go to achieve the Nirvana of the Toyota model.

Over the next year I had sporadic contact with the attorney, a couple depositions to read, a few slides from the resection, but not much activity until a request for me to be deposed. Well that of course immediately precipitated a need to see all the slides (fortunately in virtual microscopy format) followed by 2 plus hours in front of a video camera describing what in my mind were a bunch of obvious pedestrian facts of the case, ending with the dreaded question about SOC. I had thought I could avoid that but this was the beginning of my greater education into the legal system.

Basically, the medical legal system is not centered on my concept of “mistake” or “error.” In the particular state this case was to be tried, for a patient to be compensated there had to be a lapse in SOC. Dance around that as much as I would like, at the end of the day if I did not use the term SOC the patient was out of luck. How had I gotten myself into this mess? Or maybe it is not me, maybe the question is how did our society get in such a mess? Why does settling a case have to be linked to defining fault? Certainly, auto insurance has wrestled with the problems generated by linking these 2 ideas, why can’t medical liability insurance find a similar mechanism? A single lapse in SOC should have very different repercussions for the practitioner versus the patient. And yet in most states the 2 are linked and frequently justified under the naïve assumption that somehow this will prevent errors from occurring. I used to believe that logic, but not anymore.

For some reason no one associated with this case could simply say a mistake was made. No health provider counsel would ever advise the physician/employee to state the obvious. If a mistake were made then someone would have to be punished, someone or some institution would be “at fault”. Therefore, while I am confident the mistake was known, the patient was never told. Instead they were left to wonder who was hiding what and why? People’s lives are at stake; this is no way to provide health care and gives the physician no means of acting ethically. So, from the patient’s perspective, how does one obtain justice in this system? Well I was going to find out.

The AMC was not interested in settling. They are world renowned, providing the highest level of SOC. How dare someone say there was a lapse. They had a team of lawyers, a troop of medical experts and more money than God to prove their expertise. What chance did a patient, a couple of lawyers and a lone medical expert have? Indeed, the AMC’s first tack would be to disqualify me as a medical expert: no medical expert, no case (see below).

Did I still have the option to participate or not? I had originally indicated my preference to not comment on SOC and this case was clearly evolving that way, (though I did not appreciate how completely focused it was in that direction). Ethically I knew with exquisite detail what had happened. It was a story the defense had no intention of allowing to be told. The patient could tell it but no one had listened to him in years. It seems clear that who controlled the story controlled the case outcome. I didn’t need a medical ethicist to tell me what to do (growing up my mom had made it abundantly clear what I would do). The trial was set along with expected dates of my testimony.

As the trial approached the tempo of phone calls and consultations with the attorneys increased beyond my wildest expectations. A script was being written, or more accurately a battle plan was being drawn. Trials are like plays or athletic games where any one performance cannot be revised and rolled out after multiple takes to select the best show. This was a script that would get aired once (or at least so I thought) and on which nuance could mean the difference between winning and losing. Like a battle there was only one chance.

As I was busy reading defendant expert and material witness depositions, my previous deposition was being aired on prime time. Actually, it was being viewed by working groups of citizens selected from the same pool from which the jurors would be selected. They were screening my testimony for its “believability.” This could not be happening. I had never thought someone would view my professional statements as believable or not. Of course, everything I said was true, hadn’t it always been? I began to see I was falling down a rabbit hole with no idea where it would end.

Much of the case centered around who knew what when. Although the electronic medical record can be particularly perverse in documenting that, with addenda and amendments obscuring the timeline of events, I had a clear idea of what happened and when. A medical error was being compounded by refusal to rethink a diagnosis as refuting tests poured in. Add to that, the flaky lines of communication between health care providers, and this was an accident waiting to happen. So, knowing the facts was not an issue, but keeping them in focus while the defense blew more smoke than a forest fire was a constant battle.

First came a litany of legal issues to dismiss the case on procedural grounds. Many of these seemed disingenuous, but I am sure the courts are filled with frivolous claims that need to be weeded out. But one of these procedural issues focused specifically on my qualifications as an expert in neuropathology. After practicing at large academic institutions for over 3 decades, reviewing high volumes of specimens, I thought this was a no brainer. However, particularly when it comes to SOC, all sorts of issues surfaced for which the unprepared expert witness could disqualify him- or herself without even realizing it. So careful thought needed to be committed to establishing my credibility to the jurors and the judge. Specifically, I needed to prove I was not some carpetbagger coming to town to earn some income at whatever cost to the community. This part of the trial requires some thick skin; no one was going to pull a punch if they could end the case with the expert on the mat prior to any further evidence.

From my perspective, the biggest challenge was how to explain the case to the jury without expecting them to be experts in neuropathology. We train rotating medical students for 4 weeks, after which we are lucky if they know which end of the microscope to use. How can one hope to communicate to a lay audience arcane histopathological detail used in the diagnosis of brain tumors? The answer is, you can’t, but you do have to convince them there is a science and process to diagnostics and if it is followed, the process works, but mistakes in process lead to mistakes in deed.

However, in a trial the defense comes second so any clear case-description I made at the beginning of the trial would be susceptible to muddling over subsequent days of testimony. When I got off the stand after 2 days, the defense would have many more days to muck up the issues beyond recognition. Additionally, they had a bevy of “expert witnesses” each willing to testify to contradictory opinions across a broad spectrum of opinion. This I found quite disconcerting and the reason I decided to write this treatise to my profession.

The original biopsy diagnosis was amended after the resection showed radiation necrosis and no tumor. Therefore, no one directly involved in the case and the patient’s treatment disputed the “facts.” Yet the defense was able to find neuropathology experts who said: 1. the tumor was there if the original neuropathologist said it was. 2. there really is a tumor in both resection and biopsy. 3. hard to say if there was a tumor present. Really? How can this be? With this sort of apparent conflict, the trial promised to be quite complex.

All these issues became moot when the defense was able to convince the judge, that it just did not matter what the diagnosis was because the temporal lobe should have been removed anyway. Is this the real world when one of our profession’s most critical decisions: radiation-induced GBM versus radiation necrosis, doesn’t matter to the patient? Wouldn’t the plaintiff have been better off with the option of conservative therapy for radiation necrosis than temporal lobectomy? Too late, trial over. Enough of these complaining patients, the doctors know best.

Now here is where the story takes an even more perverse turn. After throwing the case out of Superior Court, the State Supreme Court ruled the original judge errored. Two years later time for retrial! OMG! Reliving this nightmare for me was one thing, but what about the poor patient. This was torture! So, the tape was rewound. A new jury was selected and a new trial commenced. Same smoke, same confusion, similar result. Tough luck for the patient.

Learning of this judgment, I was dumbfounded. If malignant glioma versus no malignant glioma makes no difference, does any neuropathological diagnosis make a difference? After years of study and reflection I am left with many questions:

If asked to assist in a medical legal case involving one of our colleagues, aren’t we ethically required to lend assistance? No sane person wants to testify against a colleague, but shouldn’t every patient have access to our profession’s advice?

Are there standards to which we can hold out profession accountable? In this day and age, how can one expert say the diagnosis is whatever the other expert doesn’t say, irrespective of the facts? How can one expert say there is tumor present in the biopsy and resection when the other experts say there isn’t a tumor. Isn’t one opinion right and the other wrong?

Can the neuropathologists discern and agree on facts? Isn’t there a difference between opinion and facts? In this case the answer seems to be a resounding “No”. Whether or not we call a biopsy radiation necrosis or GBM is just an opinion with incredible consequences for the patient. Of course, everyone has a right to their own opinion but no one has a right to their own facts.

My answers to these oversimplified questions may be different from my colleagues, but if they are different, they are important enough that they should be debated. If the issues are swept under the rug that may be where our profession is headed.
