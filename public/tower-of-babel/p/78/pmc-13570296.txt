Associations Between Teenage Motherhood, Perinatal Cortical Maturation, and Risk of Psychopathology in Children: The Mediating Role of Pregnancy Complications
Authors: Andréa Paysserand, Raoul Belzeaux, Guillaume Auzias, Antoine Lefrere, Arnaud Cachia, Philippe Deruelle, Fabrizio Pizzagalli, Christine Deruelle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren born to teenage mothers (<20 years) are at increased risk for psychopathology, partially due to atypical brain development. We explored whether this risk is mediated by perinatal complications more frequent in teenage pregnancies and by their impact on perinatal brain maturation as indexed by sulcal morphometry.MethodsUsing data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (N = 5291 singletons, ages 9–11 years), we investigated the associations between maternal age, child psychopathology risks (Child Behavior Checklist [CBCL]), sulcal morphometry (sulcal surface, width, length, and mean depth), and perinatal complications through mixed modeling, mediation analysis, and structural equation modeling (SEM).ResultsMaternal age was negatively associated with CBCL scores, indicating higher risks of psychopathology in children born to teenage mothers. Severe anemia, nausea, unplanned pregnancy, tobacco/marijuana use, and urinary tract infection partially mediated this association. Consistently, SEM showed that the association between maternal age and offspring psychopathology risk was mediated by perinatal adversities that were more prevalent in teenage pregnancies. Maternal age was associated with sulcal morphometry, consistent with variations in perinatal brain maturation. In boys, perinatal adversity linked to teenage pregnancies was associated with wider frontal and subparietal sulci, which mediated higher CBCL scores in boys born to a teenage mother. In girls, maternal age was positively associated with occipitotemporal sulcus length; however, sulcal morphometry was not associated with perinatal adversity in teenage pregnancies and did not mediate psychopathology risks.ConclusionsOverall, this study suggests that teenage motherhood is linked to early brain development and psychopathology risks in offspring partially through the perinatal adversities that are more commonly associated with it.

Teenage motherhood, namely being a mother before 20 years of age (1,2), is associated with increased risks of autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia, and mood disorders, together with pervasive developmental disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and conduct disorders observed across both adult (1,3, 4, 5) and pediatric (2,6,7) populations. These psychopathological risks are commonly attributed to environmental hardship and poorer rearing practices (2,8), both known to be associated with alterations in cortical maturation during childhood (9). Supporting this view, reduced cortical volume and surface area in frontal and temporal regions partially mediate the higher psychopathology risk in children of younger mothers (6). However, recent findings indicate that the relationship between teenage motherhood and reduced cortical area and volume remained significant even after accounting for stressful family environments (2), suggesting that these effects may originate during the perinatal period rather than solely from postnatal factors. Interestingly, numerous perinatal complications more frequent among teenagers may influence fetal brain maturation (10,11). Notably, teenage pregnancies are frequently associated with higher prevalence of substance use, placental dysfunction, severe anemia and nausea, preterm birth, and obstetric hemorrhage (12, 13, 14). These complications may alter perinatal brain maturation, thereby elevating offspring psychopathology risk, although these mechanisms remain poorly understood.

Sulcal morphometry provides a valuable approach to investigate these pathways. First, sulcal patterns are stable across the lifespan; they are determined during fetal life and remain largely unchanged during postnatal development (15,16). Secondly, they are determined by genetics (17,18) and prenatal factors (19) rather than by the postnatal environment. Lastly, atypical sulcal morphology has been reported across several neurodevelopmental disorders (20, 21, 22, 23), highlighting its potential for identifying deviations in brain maturation (16). Therefore, investigating the relationship between maternal age and sulcal morphometry could illuminate mechanisms through which teenage motherhood shapes offspring neurodevelopment prenatally. Building on previous findings (2,6), we examined, for the first time, whether perinatal complications in teenage pregnancies mediate alterations in sulcal morphology and thereby contribute to higher psychopathology risks in children of teenage mothers. Analysis was conducted during the prepubertal period before the peak emergence of many psychiatric disorders to capture early manifestations of risk. Because female and male fetuses follow partially distinct prenatal developmental trajectories (24,25) and because maternal age and psychopathology risks were previously reported to display sex-specific associations (2), boys and girls were analyzed separately according to sex assigned at birth.

Methods and Materials

Participants

This study used the data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study annual release 5.1 held in the National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive. The ABCD Study is a large longitudinal study that recruited children across 21 research sites across the United States, with a total of 11,897 participants ages 9 to 11 years at baseline. More details on the participants, data collection, and preprocessing parameters of the data are provided elsewhere (26,27). Among this entire cohort, sulcal measures could be extracted from 10,069 participants. We selectively retained data from the oldest child in each family (6) and restricted the sample to singleton pregnancies and children with complete parental age information. Because the focus was not on advanced parental age, we only included children whose mothers were age ≤35 years and fathers ≤40 years, a commonly used cutoff in obstetrics (28,29) and psychiatry (30). This resulted in a refined dataset of 5291 unrelated singleton children (2735 boys) (see Figure S1). The demographic characteristics of the participants are summarized in Tables S1 and S2.

Pregnancy Complications

Perinatal complications were self-reported at baseline by mothers on the Developmental History Questionnaire (31,32). Similarly to previous investigations (33), we included any adverse event during pregnancy, any adverse obstetric events, and substance use reported by mothers. The gestational complications included were severe nausea and vomiting extending beyond the sixth month or associated with weight loss; heavy bleeding requiring bed rest or specific treatment; preeclampsia/eclampsia (toxemia); severe gallbladder attack; persistent proteinuria; rubella during the first 3 months of pregnancy; severe anemia; urinary tract infection; pregnancy-related diabetes; pregnancy-related hypertension; placenta previa, abruptio placentae, or other placental complications; accidents, injuries, or other conditions requiring medical care; alcohol, tobacco, and marijuana exposure during the first trimester of pregnancy; and unplanned pregnancy. The birth complications included were prematurity, cesarian section, cyanosis at birth, slow heart rate, absent breathing at birth, convulsions, jaundice requiring treatment, need for oxygen therapy, need for blood transfusion, and Rh incompatibility.

We computed a perinatal adversity load to assess whether a higher overall perinatal burden in teenage mothers mediated the increased psychopathology risk in offspring. This composite score followed the operationalization of Roffman et al. (33) in the ABCD cohort, where it has been associated with psychopathology risks at ages 9 to 11. This composite score was derived by summing binary indicators (0 = absent, 1 = present) of perinatal exposures, including unplanned pregnancy, substance use during the first trimester of pregnancy, cesarean section, preterm birth, gestational complications, and birth complications. Gestational and birth complications were each collapsed into single binary variables indicating the presence of at least one complication within each category (33), and all indicators were summed to yield the total perinatal adversity load. However, because this operationalization does not capture the cumulative number of complications within each domain, we also computed complementary scores for pregnancy and birth complications by summing the number of reported complications within each category to examine whether a higher overall perinatal burden in teenage mothers could account for their offspring’s increased risk of psychopathology. Substance exposures occurring after the first trimester were excluded due to low prevalence (<3%) and consistency with the original Roffman et al. (33) definition.

Offspring Psychopathology Risks

Offspring risk of psychopathology was assessed using the parent-completed Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) (34), which, although not diagnostic of current psychopathology, yields syndrome scores predictive of later diagnoses and indicative of psychopathology risk (35,36).

Eight syndrome scores can be derived from the CBCL: anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, and aggressive behavior.

Three composite scores can be computed from the syndrome scores: internalizing, externalizing, and total scores. The internalizing score is the sum of anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, and somatic complaints scores. The externalizing score is the sum of rule-breaking behavior and aggressive behavior scores. The total CBCL score is the sum of all scores.

Magnetic Resonance Image Preprocessing and Sulcal Extraction

Preprocessing was done on raw T1-weighted images from ABCD release 4.0 (https://doi.org/10.15154/1523041); as raw anatomical data are unchanged in release 5.1 (http://dx.doi.org/10.15154/z563-zd24), preprocessing was not repeated for computing and ecological concerns. Images were corrected for intensity inhomogeneities and segmented into gray and white matter tissues using FastSurfer 1.0 (https://github.com/Deep-MI/FastSurfer), a fast and accurate deep learning–based neuroimaging pipeline for the automated processing of structural MRI scans (37). To extract sulcal measures, we used BrainVISA suite version 5.1.1 which implements the algorithm fully described in (38,39). Sulci were automatically labeled according to a predefined anatomical nomenclature of 62 sulcal labels for the left hemisphere and 61 sulcal labels for the right hemisphere (39). The atlas is described in detail and freely accessible at http://brainvisa.info/web/morphologist.html and can be visualized online at http://brainvisa.info/web/webgl_demo/webgl.html. The protocol developed for this work is available at https://enigma.ini.usc.edu/protocols/imaging-protocols/. Analyzing the shape of the cortex through sulcal-based morphometry allowed us to quantify the geometry of a sulcus in terms of several distinct and complementary descriptors, consisting of length, mean depth, surface area, and width (or fold opening) of all extracted and labeled sulci. Details on these metrics can be found for example in Pizzagalli et al. (17).

Some sulci, especially the most variable and shallow ones, may not be detected in many participants. Therefore, only sulci with <5% missed detection were considered, which resulted in 80 sulci of interest (40 per hemisphere) (see the Supplement). An additional filtering was implemented to mitigate the potential impact of outliers related to mislabeling of sulci: For each sulcus, the z score of the sulcal measure being assessed was computed, and the set of participants showing an absolute z score >2.5 was discarded from current analysis (17). It corresponds to between 1% and 2% of participants for each sulcus.

Statistical Analysis

Mixed Models

To identify complications specifically associated with teenage pregnancy, maternal age was dichotomized into age ranges (<20, 20–24, 25–29, 30–35 years), with 25 to 29 years, the most prevalent group, taken as the reference group. Associations between each perinatal complication and maternal age and associations between perinatal load and maternal age were tested using logistic generalized linear mixed models with research site as a random effect. Effect sizes are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for clinical interpretability (40).

The relationship between maternal age and CBCL scores was assessed using linear mixed models with research site as a random factor, adjusting for child age, ethnicity (self-reported categories Asian, Black, Hispanic, other, and White); parental education and household income; lifetime psychopathology of each parent; and parent-reported parental monitoring and family conflict (see the Supplement). This adjustment allowed partial control for key sociodemographic, parental, and familial factors previously linked to child psychopathology risk (2,32). Marginal R2 (R2m) values (rsq package) was computed to assess effect size (41,42). To verify that there was no multicollinearity between covariates, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was computed for linear regression models with the same covariates.

We assessed the linear association between maternal age and surface, length, width, and opening of sulci using linear mixed modeling. We modeled MRI scanner serial number (29 devices) as a random effect to account for scanner-related differences, consistent with ABCD recommendations (26,43, 44, 45). Covariates were selected using causal inference principles informed by prior literature (46, 47, 48) (see the Supplement). Covariates included age, lifetime psychopathology of each parent, education level of parents, household income, and self-reported ethnicity.

Two approaches were used to control for brain size, adjustment for intracranial volume (ICV) and for hemispheric brain hull area (left and right analyzed separately) (22). Because regional and global brain measures scale nonlinearly with overall brain size, both were log10 transformed to account for allometry (49). We further modeled potential nonlinear age effects by including a quadratic age term and interactions between log-transformed ICV/brain hull area and both age and age2 (49). This resulted in 6 models in total, which we denote as ICV, hemispheric brain hull, ICV allometric, hemispheric brain hull allometric, ICV allometric + age interactions, and hemispheric brain hull allometric + age interactions. R2m values were computed to assess effect size using the rsq package (41,42).

Boys and girls were modeled as separate populations.

Due to high collinearity with maternal age, paternal age was not included as a covariate. For models with multiple comparisons, p values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method for the number of comparisons performed in each specific model. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.1.

Mediation Analysis

Partial mediation analyses tested whether perinatal complications associated with teenage pregnancy mediated the relationship between maternal age and offspring psychopathology risks. Covariates included child age, parental education and income, parental psychopathology, ethnicity, parental monitoring, family conflict, and research site.

Additional models examined sulcal morphology as a mediator, including sulci consistently associated with maternal age across the 6 linear mixed models and with MRI scanner serial number added as a covariate. Two brain-size adjustment models were tested (ICV or brain hull area).

Boys and girls were considered in separate statistical models. p Values were estimated using bootstrapping in the mediation package in R version 4.5.1 and corrected using the FDR method.

Structural Equation Modeling

To go beyond mediation analyses, which do not account for interactions between multiple mediators and outcomes, we performed structural equation modeling (SEM). SEM allows investigators to test a developmental pathway linking maternal age, perinatal adversity, sulcal morphometry, and offspring psychopathology. SEM provides fit indices (comparative fit index [CFI], Tucker-Lewis Index [TLI], root mean square error of approximation [RMSEA]) that evaluate how well the proposed theoretical model corresponds to the observed data, offering a valuable metric for assessing the plausibility of the hypothesized causal structure (50).

Using SEM, perinatal adversity associated with teenage pregnancy was modeled as a latent construct, capturing the co-occurrence and interdependence of complications (Figure S2) in relation to psychopathology (33,51). This latent factor was estimated from teenage pregnancy–associated complications using confirmatory factor analysis. This approach modeled the shared variance among these complications rather than relying on a simple summed score.

The model tested direct and indirect associations between maternal age and CBCL outcomes through latent perinatal adversity and sulcal morphology, while adjusting for scanner type, familial covariates, and sociodemographic covariates, and is described in Figure S2. Only sulci consistently associated with maternal age across the 6 previous models were included. SEM analyses were performed using the lavaan package in R version 4.5.1.

Results

Perinatal Complications Related to Teenage Pregnancy

Teenage pregnancy (<20 years) was associated with a lower overall perinatal adversity load compared with mothers ages 25 to 29 years (aOR = 0.757, pFDR < .001) (Table S3). Separate additional pregnancy and birth complication scores did not differ between teenage mothers and the reference group (Table S3).

We identified several specific perinatal complications that were significantly more frequent among teenage mothers compared with mothers ages 25 to 29 years. These included tobacco and marijuana exposure during the first trimester of pregnancy (aOR = 1.110, pFDR < .001; aOR = 1.105, pFDR < .001), severe anemia (aOR = 1.038, pFDR < .001), severe nausea (aOR = 1.042, pFDR < .05), urinary tract infection (aOR = 1.097, pFDR < .001), and unplanned pregnancy (aOR = 1.529, pFDR < .001) (Figure 1 and Table S4). Post hoc analyses indicated that these complications were associated with ethnicity, lower household education, and lower income (Figure S3), supporting adjustment for these factors in subsequent analyses. Conversely, cesarean section (aOR = 0.920, pFDR < .001), gestational diabetes (aOR = 0.961, pFDR < .001), and alcohol exposure during the first trimester of pregnancy (aOR = 0.943, pFDR = .009) were significantly less frequent among teenage mothers than in the reference group. These associations were also observed when boys and girls were modeled separately. No other complications showed significantly different aORs compared with the reference group. Post hoc analyses including low birth weight (<2500 g) and small for gestational age, defined using INTERGROWTH-21st standards as birth weight below the expected threshold for gestational age at birth, showed no significant association with teenage pregnant mothers as compared with pregnant mothers ages 25 to 29 years.

Maternal Age and Offspring Psychopathology

Among girls, maternal age was significantly negatively associated with the CBCL withdrawal/depression (pFDR = .033, t3175 = −2.681, R2m = 0.075) and rule-breaking behavior (pFDR = .005, t3175 = −3.446, R2m = 0.132) subscores, as well as with the CBCL total score (pFDR = .036, t3175 = −2.510, R2m = 0.128).

Among boys, maternal age was significantly and negatively associated with the CBCL social (pFDR = .037, t3433 = −2.316, R2m = 0.106), attention (pFDR = .023, t3433 = −2.679, R2m = 0.101), and rule-breaking (pFDR = .0004, t3433 = −4.104, R2m = 0.149) subscores as well as with the CBCL externalizing problems (pFDR = .022, t3433 = −2.816, R2m = 0.144) and the CBCL total score (pFDR = .030, t3433 = −2.474, R2m = 0.150).

The VIF was between 1 and 2, indicating no problematic multicollinearity, despite some covariates being correlated (Figure S3).

Specific complications, including urinary tract infection, tobacco and marijuana use during pregnancy, and unplanned pregnancy, mediated the associations between maternal age and rule-breaking behavior scores (Figures 2 and 3) as well as CBCL composite scores (Figure 4) in both boys and girls. No mediation effect was observed for the other CBCL syndrome scores. Structural Equation Modeling

The SEMs demonstrated good fits to the data: scaled estimates, which account for potential non-normality, yielded excellent fit indices (among girls: CFI = 0.970, TLI = 0.978, RMSEA = 0.032, 95% CI [0.029–0.034]; among boys: CFI = 0.981, TLI = 0.986, RMSEA = 0.023 95% CI [0.20–0.25]).

Among girls, SEM revealed distinct direct and indirect pathways linking maternal age to CBCL outcomes (Figure 6A). Increasing maternal age was positively associated with social problems (p < .001), somatic complaints (p < .01), and thought problems (p < .05), corresponding to higher CBCL scores in these dimensions. These associations were not observed in the mixed models and emerged only in the SEM framework. Increasing maternal age was also indirectly associated with higher internalizing symptoms through greater length of the left anterior lateral occipitotemporal sulcus. Conversely, the negative associations between maternal age and CBCL scores observed in previous mixed models were accounted for by indirect pathways involving perinatal complications that were more frequent among teenage mothers (p < .01). Among boys, SEM did not show direct associations between maternal age and CBCL scores (Figure 6B). Rather, higher CBCL scores were linked to perinatal complications that were more frequent among teenage mothers. These complications mediated associations with all CBCL scores, except depression-related scores (p < .01). SEM further suggested that maternal age and perinatal adversity were associated with sulcal morphology in opposite directions: Younger maternal age was associated with thinner sulci (p < .05), which were related to lower CBCL scores (p < .05), whereas perinatal complications more common in teenage pregnancy were associated with wider sulci (p < .01), which were related to higher CBCL scores (p < .01). SEM is consistent with higher CBCL scores among boys born to teenage mothers being mediated by a perinatal adversity more common among these mothers and its impact on sulcal morphometry, but not by maternal age itself.

Discussion

This study provides the first evidence that the association between teenage motherhood, cortical maturation, and offspring psychopathology risk is partially mediated by specific perinatal complications.

SEM demonstrated that the effect of maternal age on offspring psychopathology risk was mediated by a latent factor including tobacco and marijuana exposure, urinary tract infections, severe anemia, nausea, and unplanned pregnancies, all of which were more prevalent in teenage pregnancies. This increased risk affected all psychopathology dimensions in children ages 9 to 11 years in both sexes (Figure 6).

Importantly, these associations remained significant after controlling for family environment, suggesting that the increased psychopathology risk among offspring of teenage mothers cannot be explained solely by family hardship or parenting practices (2,8). Therefore, these findings highlight that teenage motherhood, beyond being associated with early-life adversity risks, may constitute a perinatal determinant of psychopathology risk.

This study also demonstrated for the first time that maternal age influences brain maturation during the perinatal period as reflected by sulcal morphometry, an index of perinatal brain maturation (16). While previous research linked maternal age to cortical surface area and volume in children (2,6,52), these measures primarily reflect postnatal rather than perinatal brain development (24). Our findings suggest that cortical differences observed later in childhood (6) may already originate during the perinatal period.

SEM highlighted sex-specific associations. In girls, maternal age was positively correlated with greater left anterior lateral occipitotemporal sulcus length, itself associated with higher internalizing symptoms. However, this sulcal characteristic did not mediate the relationship between teenage motherhood and CBCL scores and was not explained by perinatal adversity, suggesting that other neuroanatomical mechanisms may be involved. In boys, SEM identified perinatal pathways linking teenage motherhood to both altered sulcal morphology and elevated CBCL scores. Specifically, teenage-related perinatal adversity was associated with increased width of the right intermediate frontal sulcus, the right marginal frontal sulcus, and the right subparietal sulcus, which in turn correlated with higher anxious/depressed, social problems, and aggressive behavior scores. These findings suggest that atypical sulcal morphology partially accounts for the increased psychopathology risk observed in boys born to younger mothers. Boys also exhibited larger effect sizes, consistent with evidence of greater male vulnerability to both perinatal insults and neurodevelopmental disorders (53).

Our study further refines the specificity of the perinatal factors involved. Global adversity scores were significantly lower among teenage mothers compared with older ones (Table S3), prompting that teenage pregnancy is not associated with a higher overall perinatal burden. Instead, mediation analyses identified unplanned pregnancy, tobacco and marijuana use, and urinary tract infections as partial contributors to higher CBCL scores, particularly rule-breaking behaviors. The negative association between maternal age and offspring CBCL scores remained even when controlling for familial hardship and parental practices (2,6).

Because such behaviors predict later conduct disorder and ADHD with externalizing symptoms (54,55), our findings extend prior work linking maternal drug use, smoking, urinary tract infections, and younger parental ages to ADHD risk (56) and suggest that these complications also contribute to conduct disorders, frequently comorbid with ADHD, among children of teenage mothers (1,7,57).

Importantly, our findings identify teenage-related perinatal adversity as a key driver of the higher risk of offspring psychopathology in both boys and girls, emphasizing the need for targeted clinical monitoring beyond social interventions.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. Although analyses controlled for multiple confounders, including parental history of psychopathology, genetic liability was not accounted for. Therefore, potential interactions between teenage pregnancy–related perinatal adversity (e.g., substance use), maternal genetic predisposition, and offspring psychopathology risk, which have already been documented (58), cannot be ruled out.

Analyses were restricted to first-born singletons to reduce confounding by parity and multifetal gestation, which are associated with distinct obstetric and perinatal risk profiles. Consequently, findings do not generalize to later-born children or multiple pregnancies.

Teenage motherhood, pregnancy complications, and offspring psychopathology risk clustered within Black and Hispanic ethnic groups and families with lower socioeconomic status (Figure S3), consistent with prior work (32). Although these factors were adjusted for, they remain important (59,60) and warrant further investigation. This clustering also limits generalizability as findings may reflect a specific U.S. sociodemographic and health care context.

Finally, analyses were stratified by sex based on a theory-driven assumption of partially distinct developmental pathways (25) rather than consistent evidence of statistical interaction. Therefore, sex-specific findings should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive evidence of differential effects.

Conclusions

This study robustly highlights that specific perinatal complications that are prominent among teenage mothers partially mediate the association between young maternal age, altered cortical maturation, and increased psychopathology risks in offspring. These findings challenge previous assumptions attributing this link primarily to adverse family environments. Importantly, the perinatal adversity was not generalized but instead reflected specific complications, including substance exposure, urinary tract infections, severe anemia, severe nausea, and unplanned pregnancies.

Furthermore, we have provided novel evidence that maternal age is associated with cortical development as early as the perinatal period. In boys, psychopathology risk was partially mediated both by the increased frequency of perinatal complications among teenage mothers and by their effects on perinatal cortical maturation. In girls, although similar perinatal adversities contributed to psychopathology risk, we found no evidence that perinatal cortical maturation mediated this relationship.

Overall, these findings underscore the importance of fetal programming factors in the developmental origins of psychopathology and underscore the need for close collaborations between psychiatrists, obstetricians, and neuroscientists.
