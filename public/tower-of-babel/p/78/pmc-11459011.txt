Rule-based energy management strategies for PEMFC-based micro-CHP systems: A comparative analysis
Authors: Santiago Navarro, Juan M Herrero, Xavier Blasco, Alberto Pajares
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommercial PEMFC-based micro-CHP systems are operated by rule-based energy management strategies. Each of these strategies constitutes a different way to meet the household energy demand (following the heat demand, following the electricity demand, the maximum of the two, etc.). Previous studies demonstrate that which of them is the best —i.e. the one that manages to meet the demand at the lowest operating cost— depends on the particular scenario in which the micro-CHP system works (gas and electricity prices, annual energy demands, ability to export electricity to the grid, etc.). This paper aims to explore this dependence relationship and to deepen our understanding of it. To this end, a parametric analysis is conducted and the performances achieved by four rule-based operating strategies are compared. The parameters whose influence is studied, and through which the scenario is jointly characterized, are: (1) energy prices (electricity and natural gas), (2) feed-in tariff, (3) stack degradation, (4) climate and (5) heat to power ratio of the demand. The results show this dependence relationship in a clear and more comprehensive way, and offer a better understanding of its nature. From this improved understanding it can be inferred, among other things, that adapting the strategy to the scenario can generate annual savings of up to 14.5 percentage points. Moreover, this enhanced characterization of that dependence relationship can be useful for the design of a new operating strategy, a strategy that, without falling into the complexity that an optimal energy management approach (based on linear programming) involves, manages to exploit the savings potential of micro-CHP systems, thus facilitating their future mass commercialization.Keywords: Micro-CHP, EMS, Techno-economic model, PEMFC, Operating strategy

Introduction

Micro combined heat and power (micro-CHP) systems based on PEMFCs (proton exchange membrane fuel cells) generate electricity and heat simultaneously (cogeneration) [1]. They are used for residential energy supply (lighting, appliances, heating and domestic hot water) [2]. Although some commercial products exist, these systems are still in development. They are expected to replace the traditional supply scheme (natural gas boiler and grid connection). Evidence shows that micro-CHP systems can generate significant annual savings in the energy bill compared to the traditional supply and reduce CO2 emissions [3]. Despite these advantages, and because they are expensive equipment, mass commercialization has not yet been achieved. Over the last two decades, many technological aspects have been worked on to achieve this goal. One of them is the operating strategy or energy management system (EMS) that operates the micro-CHP system [4].

It is well known that the operating strategy that manages the micro-CHP system substantially affects the overall performance of the household energy supply, and that, consequently, its correct design is crucial if the savings potential of micro-CHP systems is to be fully exploited [5], [6], [7], [8]. This fact has motivated several investigations that should be briefly recalled here, as they constitute the background of the present work.

In the literature there are several types of EMS, which can be classified into two categories: rule-based EMSs and sophisticated EMSs. The latter include, for example, those based on linear programming or on fuzzy logic [9], [10]. The scope of this article is limited to the former. These have the advantage of being more straightforward, which greatly facilitates their implementation in commercial equipment. The most common rule-based operating strategies are heat-led strategy and electricity-led strategy [6], [11]. The first one, for example, is the one programmed in Viessmann's commercial equipment [12]. Another interesting operating strategy is the heat-and-electricity-led strategy, which was proposed in [13] and also used in [14] and in [15]. Finally, in [16], it is used a strategy that is a variant of the electricity-led, and that it is called here electricity-led-switch-off-in-summer strategy.

In [13], Hawkes and Leach studied and compared, for a particular scenario in the United Kingdom, the performance (operating cost reduction with respect to traditional supply) of three different EMSs, namely, heat-led strategy, electricity-led strategy and least cost operating strategy (optimal EMS). Their study also considered three micro-CHP technologies (Stirling engine, gas engine and solid oxide fuel cells, SOFC). They showed that which is the best strategy changes as the electricity buyback price (feed in tariff, FiT) varies. Houwing et al. [17] focused on a PEMFC-based micro-CHP system and compared two EMSs, heat-led strategy and optimal EMS. Their analysis also studied the effect that different electricity tariff structures had on the results. In the most favorable scenario, the optimal EMS achieved an operating cost saving of 13.6%, compared to the heat-led strategy. In [18], Shaneb and Taylor compared three rule-based EMSs, heat-led, electricity-led and time-led (on/off according to predetermined time intervals), operating on a PEMFC-based micro-CHP system. Their analysis included stack size as a variable and studied its effect. Results showed that the EMSs have a significant effect on operation cost. In [19], these same authors and Coates conducted a new analysis where they compared three EMSs, heat-led, electricity-led and optimal EMS (LP, linear programming). Here they took into account four factors: feed-in tariff, feed-in tariff with government incentives, carbon tax and stack size. The main result was that the optimal EMS outperformed the other two rule-based EMS in all the scenarios investigated. In [20], Napoli et al. compared four operating strategies: no modulation (stack always on), night-day modulation, time segment modulation and electricity-led. Their study included two micro-CHP technologies: PEMFC and SOFC. They also analyzed how the installation of a battery storage system affected the results. For PEMFC-based micro-CHP systems, they concluded that the electricity-led strategy achieved the greatest savings.

The general conclusion that can be inferred from the above studies, among others, is that which EMS is best1 depends on the particular situation in which the micro-CHP system is operated. There is no one operating strategy that is best in all cases. This result is already in [13]. In other words, it is known that the best strategy depends on the values taken by certain variables that jointly characterize the scenario in which the micro-CHP system is operating.

In this work, a parametric analysis is conducted and the economic performances (annual operating cost) of four rule-based EMSs, namely the four mentioned above, are compared. For the comparison, the traditional supply scheme (grid connection and natural gas boiler) is taken as a reference. The parameters whose influence is studied are: (1) energy prices (electricity and natural gas), (2) feed-in tariff, (3) stack degradation, (4) climate and (5) HPR (heat to power ratio) of the demand. In this study, only operation cost is evaluated. Other economic aspects such as capital expenditure (CAPEX), investment return and economic viability are outside the scope of this study. Furthermore, an analysis of the uncertainty of the theoretical approach is also beyond the scope of this study.

The parametric analysis and the performance comparison of the four EMSs are based on numerical results obtained from a large number of simulations, 2592 in total. In each of them, the annual energy supply of a household is simulated under certain circumstances. To carry out these simulations, a dynamic techno-economic model of a PEMFC-based micro-CHP system is used. For the development of the model, data available in the literature and the data sheets of a commercial product were used. The model was implemented in Matlab/Simulink.

The model incorporates all the phenomena that in previous studies have been shown relevant for the correct evaluation of the performance of micro-CHP systems, such as stack degradation, auxiliary energy losses and heat losses in the TES (thermal energy storage, a hot water tank), among others. Likewise, the energy demand profiles of the household have been carefully chosen so that they satisfy the requirements that have also been pointed out in previous studies, namely, sufficient temporal resolution, annual periodicity and representativeness. These two aspects (relevant phenomena in modeling and profile requirements) are explained in detail in Sections 2.1 and 3.1. The contents of these two sections are essential to fully understand the rationale of this study. They have been placed in separate sections simply to avoid a lengthy introduction.

As can be inferred from the above, this research follows the same path that those studies have opened and it aims to take a step further in the understanding of that dependence (already known) that exists between (1) the scenario in which the micro-CHP system works and (2) the best operating strategy. To achieve this, we have tried, on the one hand, to make up for the shortcomings that —in our opinion— some of the research conducted to date suffers from and, on the other hand, to apply a methodology that integrates the results that have already been sufficiently established by other studies.

Table 1 compares five previous studies (the five works listed in the fourth paragraph of this introduction) with this study. This table clearly shows why this work represents a significant contribution to the field of rule-based operating strategies for PEMFC-based micro-CHP systems. Four reasons support this statement: First, the model incorporates physical phenomena that are relevant and that other studies neglect. Second, the simulations are performed with energy demand profiles that satisfy the requirements raised in previous literature (high temporal resolution, annual periodicity and representativeness); this helps to support the validity of the results. Third, the parametric analysis surpasses those carried out to date both in number of parameters and in the number of scenarios evaluated and analyzed. Fourth, the four most important rule-based operating strategies are compared for the first time and on equal terms; in particular, this work (a) does not ignore the heat-and-electricity-led strategy, which Hawkes and Leach [13] showed to be crucial, since it provides almost as good results as the optimal strategy in certain scenarios, and (b) includes the electricity-led-switch-off-in-summer strategy. The results of this study (see for example Fig. 9) demonstrate that this last strategy is the best in various scenarios and that, therefore, it is as important a strategy as the other three. None of the previous studies, however, include it in their comparative analysis. Finally, thanks to these methodological improvements, the results of this study provide a more complete and precise picture of the relationship that exists between (1) the scenario in which the micro-CHP system operates and (2) which operating strategy is the best. This better understanding of this relationship will be useful for the design of new, more sophisticated operating strategies that, in one way or another, adapt to the scenario, thus maximizing the savings potential of micro-CHP systems. This can facilitate the mass commercialization of these systems.

Techno-economic model

Background

There are already in the literature on PEMFC-based micro-CHP systems, particularly in those works in which —as in this one— a techno-economic model is developed, several well-established conclusions on which physical phenomena, due to their importance, should be modeled in order not to compromise the validity of the results [1], [16], [21], [22], [23]. In the development of the model, these conclusions have been taken into account. Some of them are detailed below.

Fuel cell degradation is a phenomenon that must be incorporated into the model, and not doing so is known to lead to incorrect results [1], [22]. Degradation affects the electrical efficiency of the micro-CHP system, its nominal power and its thermal efficiency (these relationships are detailed in the next section), thus strongly impacting the operating cost of the supply with micro-CHP. For example, in [1], it is shown that not considering degradation can lead to an overestimation in savings (marginal value) of up to 40%.

Another important phenomenon is the energy losses, both electrical (consumption of the auxiliary elements in the micro-CHP system) and thermal (mainly due to the reformer), which are inherent to the normal operation of the micro-CHP system. These losses are called balance of plant (BoP). It is well known that if they are not incorporated into the model, the results are biased [16], [21].

A third phenomenon that should also be considered is heat losses in the hot water tank. In [23], for example, it is shown that not including them leads to an overestimation of the system's thermal efficiency.

Finally, the electrical and thermal efficiencies of the micro-CHP system depend on the operating point; to be exact, they decrease at part load, in comparison to full load. Therefore, if nominal efficiencies are assumed for the entire operating range, instead of modeling instantaneous efficiencies, the results will likely be invalid [21].

The techno-economic model developed in this work incorporates the four phenomena mentioned in the previous paragraphs. In addition, it also includes the following ones: (a) heat losses in the charge and discharge of the TES (these losses are dynamic, as are those of the TES); (b) maximum ramp limit, which models the fact that the stack cannot change its operating point instantaneously; (c) startup and shutdown times of the micro-CHP system; (d) consumption during startup and shutdown; (e) consumption of the heat dumping subsystem.

Description

In this section, the techno-economic model is described. The model of the micro-CHP system is not experimentally validated, but it is mainly based on the model that Iain Staffell developed and validated (experimentally) in [9] and on the electrical and thermal performance curves (also experimentally validated) that the same author provides in [21].

Fig. 1 shows the diagram of the micro-CHP system that has been modeled. This system consists of a PEMFC stack, a reformer, an inverter, an auxiliary boiler, a water tank (TES), a heat dumping system, and a set of auxiliary devices (pumps, valves, sensors, control systems) grouped in the diagram under the label BoP (balance of plant). The water tank can be integrated into the system or installed separately (as in the figure). The reformer converts natural gas into hydrogen. The fuel cell stack transforms the chemical energy of hydrogen into electricity (direct current) and heat. The inverter converts the direct current generated by the stack into alternating current. The auxiliary boiler converts the chemical energy of the natural gas into heat. Both the heat generated by the stack (QSYS) and the generated by the boiler (QAB) are transferred to the hot water tank, which functions as a thermal storage system (TES). The heat generated by the stack can be partially or totally dumped by the heat dumping system (Qdumped). The electrical energy generated by the micro-CHP system (PSYS) is directly consumed by the household (if there is demand) or exported to the grid (Psold). If the electricity demand (Pdem) exceeds that generated by the system, the difference is imported from the grid (Pbought). The heat demanded by the household for heating (QdemH) and hot water (QdemDHW) are taken from the TES. An energy management system (EMS) operates the micro-CHP system according to a predefined strategy. The EMS decides when to turn the system on or off and sets the operating points of the stack (PSYSsp), the auxiliary boiler (QABsp) and the heat dumping system (Qdumpedsp). For this purpose, it has several measurements: state of charge (SOC) of the TES, instantaneous electricity and heat demands, instantaneous electrical (ηeSYS) and thermal (ηthSYS) efficiencies of the micro-CHP system, etc.

Elements and flows of energy and information in the supply scheme with micro-CHP.

Heat transfers between the micro-CHP system and the TES and between the TES and the household heating system have losses. In order to take into account these losses, a charge efficiency (ηthch=98%) and a discharge efficiency (ηthdis=96%) have been incorporated in the TES model. In addition to these inefficiencies, the model also includes losses by convection with the environment (Qloss) in the TES itself. This additional loss has been modeled according to Newton's cooling law, with a heat transfer coefficient (hloss) of 0.9 W/∘C (estimate based on [24]) and an ambient temperature of 20 ∘C.

Heat dumping may be necessary when the micro-CHP system is operated according to the electricity-led strategy. Since the stack cannot generate electricity and heat independently, without heat dumping the EMS would be forced to shut down the stack when the SOC of the TES reaches 100%, which can occur during periods of low thermal demand. The heat dumping system usually consists of a small hydraulic pump and a radiator-fan that evacuates heat to the environment, both consume electrical energy to carry out their function. The model includes such consumption: 20 W per kW of dumped thermal energy.

Demand profiles

Background

Also in relation to the household demand profiles there are several well-established facts in the literature that should be taken into account to achieve realistic simulations and results, three in particular.

First, the temporal resolution of the profiles is an important aspect, especially that of the electrical demand profile. It has been demonstrated that using hourly profiles, for example, is insufficient for the simulation of micro-CHP systems, since it leads to an intolerable underestimation of the operating cost, and that a resolution of at least 10 minutes is required to achieve acceptable results [30]. This fact is well known in the literature [14], [16], [20], [31].

Second, the simulations must cover a whole year, so the demand profiles must also cover an entire year. In effect, using demand profiles of a few days and running simulations of days, for example, instead of whole year simulations, produces an additional bias and distorts the results [14].

Finally, if results are to have some general validity, it is convenient that the demand profiles used in the simulations not be restricted to those of a single household, but that they account for the diversity that exists in reality [14]. This diversity is rooted in climatic variations, differences in consumption habits, etc. Therefore, to overcome this obstacle, either many profiles from different houses are used or a set of profiles (that are representative of something like a standard household) is used. The latter is the solution adopted here, in particular the profiles established by the VDI Guideline 4655 [32], which ensures a certain degree of generality. The profiles of this guideline also fulfill the two requirements previously mentioned, i.e. temporal resolution and yearly demand profiles.

Description

VDI Guideline 4655 provides reference load profiles. These profiles are based on real measurements and constructed specifically for the design and economic evaluation of micro-CHP systems in residential buildings [32]. These profiles represent the (normalized) demands of electricity, heating and DHW for ten typical days. These typical days are characterized according to three criteria: (1) Transition/Summer/Winter day, (2) Workday/Sunday, (3) Fine/Cloudy day. A winter day is a day with an average temperature T < 5 ∘C, a transition day if 5 ∘C ≤ T ≤ 15 ∘C and a summer day if T > 15 ∘C. As for cloud amount (measured in eighths), a day is fine if its average cloud amount is less than 5/8 and cloudy if it is 5/8 or greater [32]. For summer days, no distinction is made between fine and cloudy days. Thus, the ten typical days are: TWF, TWC, TSF, TSC, SWX, SSX, WWF, WWC, WSF and WSC.

These normalized profiles must be particularized (scaled) for a concrete household in a concrete location. This particularization (scaling) is done based on the annual demands of the house (electricity, heating and DHW) according to the method described in [32]. The three types of demand profiles (electricity, heat and DHW) have a resolution of 1 minute.

The thermal energy demand for heating defined by the VDI Guideline 4655 was preprocessed before being used in the simulations. The preprocessing carried out is shown in Fig. 3. As can be seen, the original signal, which alternates between discrete power values, is not exactly a heating energy demand profile, but it represents the operation of the particular heating system of the household where the measurements were obtained, i.e. the amount of heat generated by the heating system to satisfy the demand. The true heating demand, i.e. the instantaneous heat that must be served to guarantee a particular comfort temperature inside the house, changes slowly, as this is determined by the variations in the outside temperature. Fortunately, it is possible to approximately reconstruct the true heating demand profile from the original signal. This reconstruction was performed in two steps. First, the original signal is averaged in one-hour intervals, that is to say, for each of the 8760 hours in a year, the average of the 60 values of the original signal corresponding to that hour is calculated and that number is assumed to be representative of that hour heating demand (constant for the entire hour). This procedure results in the black dotted signal in Fig. 3, below (legend: hourly average). Second, the averaged signal is interpolated, that is to say, the points of the hourly average signal (the 8760 values calculated in the previous step) are interpolated to obtain the signal QdemH. This interpolation was performed using the Matlab function interp1 (1-D data interpolation), with the interpolation method pchip (Shape-preserving piecewise cubic interpolation).

Energy management systems (EMS)

This section presents the four rule-based EMSs that are compared.

Heat-led strategy (EMS_H)

In this mode of operation, the EMS attempts to meet the instantaneous thermal demand of the household with the heat generated by the stack. If the demand exceeds the capacity of the stack, then the difference is taken from the TES, and when the TES is empty, the auxiliary boiler is activated. The auxiliary boiler is the last resort. The stack will generate the electrical power corresponding to the operating point set by the EMS. If this exceeds the electricity demand, it is injected into the grid. If it is insufficient, the difference is purchased from the grid. In this strategy, no heat is ever dumped. The pseudocode of the heat-led strategy is shown in Algorithm 1, Appendix A. This algorithm is run every minute.

Heat-led operating strategy.

Electricity-led strategy (EMS_E)

In this mode of operation, the EMS attempts to meet the instantaneous electricity demand of the household with the electrical power generated by the stack. If the demand exceeds the capacity of the stack, the difference is purchased from the grid, and if it is less than the minimum power the stack can generate (PSYSmin), the surplus is injected into the grid. The stack will generate the thermal power corresponding to the set operating point. If this exceeds the heat demand, the difference accumulates in the TES, and if it is insufficient, then the TES satisfies the difference; if the TES is empty, the auxiliary boiler supplies this deficit. Finally, if the TES is full and the heat generated by the stack exceeds the demand, the excess is dumped. In this strategy, the micro-CHP system is never shut down. The pseudocode for the electricity-led strategy is shown in Algorithm 2, Appendix A. This algorithm is run every minute.

Electricity-led operating strategy.

Electricity-led-switch-off-in-summer strategy (EMS_ES)

This strategy was chosen in [16] to analyze the economic feasibility of PEMFC-based micro-CHP systems in Germany. It is identical to the electricity-led strategy (Algorithm 2, Appendix A), with the only difference that now the micro-CHP system is switched off in summer. This “summer”, however, is not defined in a fixed way by the June solstice and the September equinox (as in [16]), but varies depending on the climate zone. In other words, it is the period of the year when a normal user would turn off the heating system. Fig. 8 shows (dashed lines) the exact days when the micro-CHP system is switched off and switched on again, for each one of the three climate zones considered in the parametric analysis.

Climate zones. CZw (W: 98, 27%; T: 144, 39%; S: 123, 34%). CZm (W: 150, 41%; T: 139, 38%; S: 76, 21%). CZc (W: 169, 46%; T: 174, 48%; S: 22, 6%). The dashed lines indicate the micro-CHP system shutdowns and startups set in EMS_ES.

Heat-and-electricity led strategy (EMS_HE)

This operating strategy is a combination of the heat-led and the electricity-led strategies. The idea is to operate the micro-CHP system to meet the greater of the instantaneous demands, electricity (Pdem) or heat (Qdem). The exact way in which this logic has been programmed is as follows. The EMS calculates at which operating point (P) it would have to set the stack to meet the thermal demand, taking into account the losses (exactly as in Algorithm 1, lines 3 and 4). If P>Pdem, then the stack set point (PSYSsp) is determined according to Algorithm 1, lines 5-10. If Pdem>P, then the stack set point (PSYSsp) is determined according to Algorithm 2, lines 3-8. The auxiliary boiler is operated so that the SOC of the TES does not fall below 0%, exactly as in Algorithm 1 and Algorithm 2. Heat dumping works as in Algorithm 2. In this strategy, like the electricity-led, the stack is never turned off. Algorithm 1, Algorithm 2 can be found in Appendix A.

Methodology

The methodology employed in the parametric analysis and the subsequent comparison of the four EMSs is as follows: A particular household (see Table 3), equipped with the micro-CHP system described in Section 2, is assumed. Then, the electrical and thermal energy supply of that house is simulated over a year. This is repeated with the 4 EMSs described in Section 4 so that the performance of these can then be compared. This “base” simulation is conducted in several different scenarios. Each scenario is defined by five parameters: energy prices, feed-in tariff, stack degradation level, climate zone and electricity demand. Each of these parameters can take different values. Methodology overview. Parametric analysis and comparison of the EMSs. Five parameters are varied, resulting in 648 different scenarios. In each scenario, the household energy supply with the micro-CHP system is simulated with 4 different EMSs and their performances are compared, i.e. how much savings they achieve with respect to the traditional supply (reference system).

The performance of the EMSs is quantified as the economic savings (cost reduction) achieved with the micro-CHP system over one year with respect to the reference system (Eq. (14)), which consists of a condensing boiler and a connection to the electrical grid (traditional supply). This is a convenient way to evaluate the performance of the EMSs, since micro-CHP systems are intended to replace the current supply systems [33], and it is, therefore, with respect to the latter that the EMS-system-micro-CHP set must be assessed.

The annual operating cost of the micro-CHP system (CμCHP) is calculated as the sum of the partial costs of the electricity purchased from the grid (Pbought), the natural gas consumed by the stack (FSYS) and the natural gas consumed by the auxiliary boiler (FAB), minus the profit obtained from the electricity sold to the grid (Psold) —in case there is feed-in tariff—. The energy prices used to calculate the operating cost are in Fig. 6 and the feed-in tariffs are explained in Section 5.1. The electricity purchased from the grid includes the heat dumping system consumption and the micro-CHP system consumption during start-up and shut-down. The annual operating cost of the reference system (CRS) is the sum of the costs of electricity and natural gas consumed.

Characterization of the scenario

In order to characterize the scenario, five variables have been chosen: energy prices, FiT, D, CZ and ED. This choice is based on two criteria. These are variables (1) that are known to affect the performance of the micro-CHP system and (2) that, in practice, will change throughout the lifetime of the system.

Fig. 6 shows electricity and natural gas prices for the domestic consumer in the European Union countries for the first half of 2021, ordered from highest to lowest. Note, first, the considerable variability in prices: from 10.03 ct/kWh (HU) to 31.93 ct/kWh (DE) for electricity and from 2.79 ct/kWh (LT) to 9.61 ct/kWh (NL) for natural gas. Secondly, it should also be noted that electricity and natural gas prices are not necessarily correlated, take as examples the cases of NL (cheap electricity, expensive natural gas) and BE (expensive electricity, relatively cheap natural gas).

The price pairs in Fig. 6 (23 in total) are also plotted, for convenience, in Fig. 7, which is a map of the energy price space (electricity and natural gas) of the European Union for the first half of 2021. For the analysis, 8 price pairs have been selected from the 23 (highlighted in red in the figure), namely, those corresponding to the Netherlands (NL), France (FR), Italy (IT), Denmark (DK), Austria (AT), Poland (PL), Germany (DE) and Belgium (BE). These have been chosen for three reasons. First, to explore the whole price space. Second, they all correspond to countries with a continental climate, the type of climate to which this analysis is limited. Third, because they are countries where the commercialization of micro-CHP systems is foreseen [33]. In that figure, the spark gap (i.e. the ratio between the price of electricity and the price of natural gas) has also been indicated, which varies between 1.33 (NL) and 5.77 (BE).

European Union energy price space (electricity and natural gas), first half of 2021. The price pairs selected for the study are highlighted in red.

The parameter feed-in tariff (FiT) represents the price at which the electricity supply company pays the consumer for the electricity that he or she injects into the grid, i.e., the electricity generated by the micro-CHP system and not consumed in the household. This parameter has units of percentage with respect to the electricity price. In the analysis, this parameter can take 3 different values, namely, 0%, 25% or 50%. This range of values is typical [9].

The parameter degradation (D) represents the stack degradation level of the micro-CHP system, Eq. (3). In this study, it takes three different values, 0% (D=0), 10% (D=0.1), and 20% (D=0.2), corresponding to BOL (begin of life), MOL (middle of life), and EOL (end of life), respectively. Throughout the lifetime of the micro-CHP system, it will successively pass through these three values [21].

The parameter climate zone (CZ) represents the climate of the house's geographical location. In this analysis, it takes three different values: warm (CZw), mild (CZm) and cold (CZc). The climate zone determines the annual thermal energy demand for heating, as well as the annual sequence of typical days. The following two sections explain how. Note, therefore, that the parameter CZ enters the simulations as annual demand profiles and that it aggregates two aspects: (1) an annual sequence of typical days and (2) an annual thermal energy demand for heating.

The annual electricity demand (ED) can take three values: ED nominal (EDn), ED low (EDl) and ED high (EDh). ED nominal corresponds to the value of the annual electricity demand of Table 3 (5250 kWh), which is the annual consumption estimated to be appropriate for a household with three occupants with normal electricity consumption habits, according to VDI Guideline 4655 [32]. ED low corresponds to an annual electricity demand 20% lower than nominal (4200 kWh) and ED high corresponds to an annual electricity demand 20% higher than nominal (6300 kWh). The performance achieved by the EMS-micro-CHP-system set will depend on the annual HPR (demand side) [14], [34]. This HPR changes depending on the climate zone in which the household is located, since its annual heating demand will vary. Moreover, in practice, this annual HPR associated with the climate zone can vary quite a lot, if the number of occupants or their consumption habits change, which is perfectly plausible over the lifetime of the micro-CHP system. The parameter ED has been included in the analysis to take this fact into account. In effect, note that when ED varies, the annual HPR varies too in the same climate zone (see Table 6).

Results and discussion

There are two aspects on which all the results presented below will focus. The first has to do with which EMS is best in each scenario, as well as how the variations of the parameters influence this and why. The second aspect refers to how much the user stops earning if the EMS is not changed when the scenario changes. The first aspect is qualitative, the second is quantitative.

Degradation, spark gap and FiT

Let us pay attention to Fig. 9. Many of the results detailed below are inferred from this figure. It compares the performances (see Eq. (14)) of the 4 EMSs studied, in 63 different scenarios, from among the 648 simulated scenarios: each group of 4 columns corresponds to one scenario. The scenarios are numbered. Above each group, there is a colored rectangle indicating which EMS is the best in that scenario. Its color, therefore, always corresponds to the color of the highest column. The 63 scenarios depicted in this figure have the same value of CZ and ED, namely CZ=CZm and ED=EDn, and they differ from each other in the parameters FiT, D, and energy prices (pair of prices associated with a country, spark gap). Note that the figure is arranged as a 3x3 matrix of graphs. In this matrix of graphs, the parameter FiT changes from left to right (FiT0, FiT25, FiT50) and the parameter D changes from top to bottom (BOL, MOL, EOL). The parameter pair of prices (country, spark gap), changes from lowest to highest spark gap within each of the 9 graphs.4

The first thing that can be appreciated in Fig. 9 is that the larger the spark gap, the greater the savings achieved by the micro-CHP system, regardless of the EMS that operates it, of whether there is FiT or not and of the level of degradation of the stack. This is logical. The micro-CHP system converts the chemical energy of natural gas into electrical energy. Therefore, the cheaper the former with respect to the latter, i.e. the higher the spark gap, the more economic savings the micro-CHP system will achieve. The convenience of a high spark gap for the economic profitability of micro-CHP systems is well known [9].

If there is no FiT (Fig. 9, left column), the best strategy is either the EMS_E or the EMS_ES. Whether it is one or the other depends on the spark gap value and the level of degradation of the stack. A lower degradation favors the best strategy to be the EMS_E, while a higher degradation favors the best strategy to be the EMS_ES. This means that given a price case (spark gap), the best strategy can change from being EMS_E to EMS_ES as the degradation level of the stack increases, as occurs for example in AT (4 → 25), PL (5 → 26), DE (27 → 48). At which precise point in the lifetime of the micro-CHP system such a change occurs (i.e. at what level of degradation) depends in turn on the price case considered, that is to say, the spark gap value. For cases with high spark gap values (e.g. DE), the best strategy will be EMS_E for most of the lifetime of the stack, while for cases with medium spark gap values (e.g. AT), the best strategy ceases to be EMS_E and becomes EMS_ES shortly after system installation. This fact, namely that the best EMS changes over the lifetime of the micro-CHP system due to stack degradation, is not restricted to the case of FiT=0%, but is also observed with FiT25 and FiT50, as illustrated in Fig. 10. This figure represents in a different way information already included in Fig. 9, namely the scenario changes 6 → 27 → 48, 12 → 33 → 54 and 18 → 39 → 60.

Best EMS as a function of the stack degradation level, for three cases. In all of them, which EMS is the best changes over the lifetime of the micro-CHP system, due to stack degradation.

Logically, it is desirable that the micro-CHP system be always operated by the strategy that provides the maximum economic savings. For this, however, it is necessary that the strategy be adapted to the scenario in some way. This is clear and the same conclusion was reached by Hawkes and Leach in [13] and Shaneb and Taylor in [18]. But how relevant is this adaptation, in economic terms? In other words, how much does the user lose if the strategy is not adapted to the scenario? And here in particular, how much does the user lose if the strategy does not adapt to the new stack degradation levels? Consider the case of AT (FiT0 column). In BOL (scenario 4) the best EMS is EMS_E. Assume that the system is installed with that EMS. If the strategy is not adapted, in MOL (scenario 25) the user will be losing 1.1% and in EOL 2.6%, since in these two cases the best EMS is no longer EMS_E, but EMS_ES. If these percentages are applied to the annual operating cost of the supply with the reference system (€2206), the results in absolute terms are €24 and €57 per year, respectively. Take these numbers as an order of magnitude estimate. These amounts will be more relevant the higher the energy bill of the household. For example, for the case DK with FiT50, scenarios 17, 38, and 59, the percentages are the same, 1.1 for MOL and 2.6 for EOL, but here these values represent €33 and €78, as the annual operating cost of the supply is higher (€2990).5,6

Regarding spark gap (Fig. 9, left column), the tendency is that high spark gap values make EMS_E the best, while low spark gap values make EMS_ES the best. The reason is that if electricity is expensive (high spark gap), it makes economic sense to consume as little as possible from the grid and, therefore, to keep the micro-CHP system running (generating electricity) even in summer, even though, as there is no heating demand, all the heat generated by the stack has to be dumped, which represents a waste of thermal energy. This tendency implies that if there is a change in electricity or natural gas prices (which is perfectly plausible over the lifetime of the system), the spark gap value will change and, consequently, which strategy is best may change also. This is evident in Fig. 11, which illustrates the case FiT25-BOL-CZn-EDn, that corresponds to the graph in the first row, second column, of Fig. 9 (scenarios 8-14). Note in particular the case of PL: a small excursion in the electricity price (of around 15%) would cause the best EMS to change from EMS_E to EMS_HE (if the price increases) or to EMS_ES (if the price decreases).

Best EMS as a function of the spark gap, in polar coordinates, for one case (FiT25, BOL, CZm, EDn). Which EMS is the best changes as a function of the spark gap (which depends, in turn, on energy prices).

Again, how relevant this fact is, from an economic point of view: how much does the user stop earning if the strategy is not adapted to the scenario? Fig. 12 shows the historical evolution of electricity and natural gas prices in Belgium. In the second half of 2014, the spark gap was 3.14 points, while in the first half of 2017, it was 5.47 points. The spark gap has gone from 3.14 to 5.47 in just two and a half years. This change in spark gap (2.33 points) is very close to that which would occur in Fig. 11 when moving from IT prices (spark gap 3.21) to BE prices (spark gap 5.77), 2.56 points. In IT, the best is EMS_ES. Assume that the system is installed with that EMS when those prices exist. If the prices were to change to BE prices and the strategy did not adapt (to become EMS_HE), the user would be stop earning 4.7% of the energy bill with traditional supply, which in that case is €2186 (scenario 14), which represents in absolute terms €103 per year.

Electricity and natural gas prices in Belgium (BE), from 2008 to 2022, left axis. As well as the value of the spark gap, right axis. The spark gap can change considerably over the lifetime of the micro-CHP system.

The best strategy depends on the spark gap. However, is worth noting that it does not depend on the electricity and natural gas prices in absolute terms. Similarly, the percentage savings achieved by the micro-CHP system with respect to the traditional supply do not depend on the electricity and natural gas prices in absolute terms, but on the spark gap. Obviously, the absolute savings do depend on the electricity and natural gas prices, since the operating cost with the traditional supply system will be higher. This result can be seen in Fig. 13.

Best EMS for three pairs of prices (AT1, AT, and AT2) with the same spark gap. Effect of a proportional change in energy prices, for one case (FiT50, MOL, CZm, EDn). Prices increase and decrease, but the spark gap value is the same. The results are not affected in any way: neither which EMS is the best nor the performances achieved by each EMS.

A look at the case FiT=FiT50 (9, right-hand column) leads to completely analogous results to those already presented above when considering the left column (FiT=FiT0), just by substituting EMS_E by EMS_HE and EMS_ES by EMS_H. To avoid making this paper too long, these results will not be repeated here.

Finally, from a comparison of the left and right columns of Fig. 9, the effect of FiT can be deduced. The tendency is that if there is FiT, the best EMS are EMS_H and EMS_HE, while if there is no FiT, the best are EMS_E and EMS_ES. The reason seems to be this: The supply strategy of EMS_H and EMS_HE is to meet —as far as possible— the thermal demand of the household with the stack of the micro-CHP system. However, the thermal efficiency of the stack is much lower than the thermal efficiency of the auxiliary boiler. Therefore, such a strategy only makes sense if there is an additional profit to be made. When there is FiT, there is indeed an additional profit, namely, the electricity generated by the stack as a by-product and fed into the grid produces an economic benefit. When there is no FiT, however, there is no additional profit, and therefore, in general it is not economically the best option: it is preferable to meet the thermal demand with the auxiliary boiler. The latter is precisely what happens with EMS_E and EMS_ES. This result is in agreement with the results that Hawkes and Leach presented in [13] for a SOFC-based micro-CHP system.

Quantitative aspect: In scenario 7 (BE, FiT0), the best EMS is EMS_E, which achieves a performance of 27.3%. However, in scenario 21 (BE, FiT50), the best strategy becomes EMS_HE, with a performance of 34.8%, while here EMS_E achieves almost the same savings as in scenario 7, namely 27.9%. This means that if in the new scenario (21) the micro-CHP system continues to be operated by EMS_E, that is to say, if the operating strategy is not adapted to the new scenario, the user will be losing 6.9%. In this scenario, the supply cost under the reference system is €2186, so the user would lose €151 per year in absolute terms. This analysis can, of course, also be looked at in reverse, i.e. assuming that the micro-CHP system was installed in scenario 21 with EMS_HE and that later, for whatever reason, the FiT disappeared. In this case, the figures would be 3.7% and €81 per year loss.

Only one last remark on feed-in tariff. It is worth realizing that the existence of FiT can be beneficial, but it is not necessarily beneficial. As can be seen in Fig. 9, the higher the FiT, the greater the savings that can be achieved, but these savings are only achieved if the strategy changes. The strategies that manage to take advantage of the existence of FiT are EMS_H and EMS_HE, which are the ones that cause the stack to generate a excess of electrical energy. With the strategies EMS_E and EMS_ES, the stack never generates an excess of electricity, so nothing is fed into the grid, and consequently, no profit is made, whether it is paid for or not.

Final remarks

So far, in order to quantify the economic relevance of adapting the EMS to the scenario, it has always been assumed that in the initial scenario the EMS that operates the micro-CHP system is the one that generates the highest savings and that afterward, when the scenario changes, the EMS remains the same. However, another case of interest can still be studied: What happens if at the installation of the micro-CHP system the EMS is not adapted to the scenario in which it is going to work for the first time? Consider scenario 7 in Fig. 9. Here the best EMS is EMS_E. If at the installation of the system, due to not taking the scenario into account, the EMS_H is programmed, the user would be losing 6.8%, which in this case means €149 per year. The same is true for scenario 21. In it the best EMS is EMS_HE. If the equipment were installed with the EMS_ES, the user would be losing 10.0%, which is €219 per year. From these results, it can be concluded that when installing a micro-CHP system it is important to consider in which scenario it will operate and to choose the EMS that is most suitable for that scenario. If this is not done, and the system comes with a fixed EMS,7 it is very likely that the micro-CHP system is not generating the full savings that it is capable of.

The most general result of the analysis presented is that the best operating strategy depends mainly on the spark gap, the feed-in tariff and the stack degradation level, and to a lesser extent on the climate zone and the annual HPR (demand side).

Adapting the operating strategy to the changing scenario generates economic savings. The savings in absolute terms that, with some examples, have been presented in this section are in the range of €18 to €151 per year, calculated with prices of the first half of 2021. As demonstrated above in Note 6, these savings would be higher with the current prices of 2023. These figures, while humble, are not negligible. The goal is to achieve mass commercialization of micro-CHP systems and, as it seems, this goal is not going to be achieved thanks to a single measure, but through many small improvements. These annual savings, therefore, despite being modest, can contribute, together with other measures, to the achievement of that objective.

The scenario can change over the lifetime of the system, many times and in multiple ways. It is worth justifying this proposition briefly. Under certain contracts electricity and natural gas prices change over time, sometimes even daily. A change of company will also cause a change in prices. The same is true for the FiT. The latter may also appear or disappear due to governmental measures. The level of degradation will increase over time. Manufacturers of micro-CHP systems offer a service whereby the stack recovers its initial electrical efficiency (e.g. Viessmann [12]), so a change from more degradation to less degradation can also occur. As already mentioned, the ED can change due to a simple change in users habits. Or consider, for example, the increase in ED that would occur by installing a supply system for an electric car (an increasingly common installation in single-family households), or the reduction in ED that would occur by installing solar panels on the roof of the household. Finally, the climate changes every day in ways that can be significant in causing an increased heating demand. In short, a change of scenario is not only possible, it is actually the normal situation.

Finally, the results confirm that the four rule-based EMSs considered are relevant, since each of the four is the best in a considerable number of scenarios (see Fig. 9). The study also shows that it is convenient, from an economic point of view, to adapt the EMS that operates the micro-CHP system to the scenario, since doing so generates economic savings (see Fig. 9). These savings can reach 14.5 percentage points (scenario FIT50, BOL, CZw, EDl). A statistical analysis performed with the numerical results of all the simulations carried out leads to these same conclusions. This statistical analysis is available in the section Supplementary Material. In this section the reader will also find the numerical results of the 2268 simulations.

Conclusion

Commercial PEMFC-based micro-CHP systems are currently controlled by rule-based EMS. These EMS implement operating strategies whose working principle consists in following the instantaneous household energy demand (heat-driven, electricity-driven, both). The main advantage of these algorithms lies in their simplicity. In this context, it is worth asking: which of these EMS is the best from an economic point of view? Or, to be more exact, which of them achieves that the energy supply with micro-CHP generates greater annual savings with respect to the traditional supply?

In order to investigate this question, in this article a parametric analysis has been performed and the performances achieved by four typical rule-based EMS have been compared. The results show that which EMS is the best depends on the particular scenario in which the micro-CHP system is working. This means that none of the considered rule-based EMS is the best for all scenarios and, consequently —since the scenario necessarily changes throughout the lifetime of the system—, that none of them fully exploits the savings potential that micro-CHP systems have with respect to the traditional supply schema.

From the previous result, the following practical corollary can be inferred: To achieve the maximum savings that micro-CHP systems can provide, the energy management strategy must be adapted to the scenario, both at the system installation and throughout its lifetime. In many cases, the additional savings that such an adaptation provides are considerable, reaching up to 14.5 percentage points. This adaptation of the EMS to the scenario, therefore, can accelerate to some extent the transition towards micro-CHP mass production.

To sum up, the results of this study agree with —but are not limited to— those published by previous researches.

The specific value and usefulness of this work, its contribution to the field, lies in that it gathers and integrates the knowledge that —in a dispersed way— existed in the literature on rule-based EMS for PEMFC-based micro-CHP systems and this knowledge is used for the design of a methodology that leads to (main finding):

■A better understanding —more comprehensive, deep and precise— of the nature of the mentioned dependence, i.e. the dependence that exists between (1) the scenario in which the micro-CHP system works and (2) which is the best operating strategy.

This improved understanding of the dependence between the scenario and the operation strategy is in itself valuable, as a theoretical result (specific to this research), and it is, in addition —and especially—, valuable and useful as a means to the proper design of a future intelligent EMS; in other words, thanks to this better understanding, it will be possible to design an intelligent EMS that, on the one hand, is capable of exploiting the savings potential of micro-CHP systems and, on the other hand, is viable from a practical point of view. Furthermore, the results of this research constitute the framework where the performance of this intelligent EMS can be appropriately compared and assessed.

Finally, this is broadly the line of future research that, in light of this study, we find of interest: First, to design an optimal EMS based on the techno-economic model developed here and to compare its performance with the four rule-based EMSs presented in this study.9 The aim of this first task is to answer the following question: is the optimal EMS, in each scenario, significantly better than the best rule-based EMS of that scenario, or is there, for every scenario, a rule-based EMS (one of the four) that is almost as good as the optimal EMS? If the latter were the case, the optimal EMS could justifiably be ruled out as a candidate for the intelligent EMS (arguing that the enormous complexity of implementation that it involves has become unnecessary), so efforts would then be directed to (second task) designing a intelligent (non-optimal) EMS that be in each scenario as good as the best of the four rule-based EMS of that scenario and that is workable in practice, i.e. that can be implemented in commercial micro-CHP systems.

The heating degree-day method is a simple procedure by which it is possible to roughly quantify the heating energy demand of a household in a particular location. The HDD value is the number of degrees Celsius that the average outdoor temperature on a given day falls from a base value. The annual HDD is the sum of the HDD of the 365 days of a given year. The base temperature represents the outdoor temperature below which a user would turn on the heating. Following [37], 15 ∘C has been taken as the base temperature for the HDD calculation. The annual HDD is a good indicator of the heating requirement of a household: the higher the annual HDD of a location, the more energy will be required to heat a house.
