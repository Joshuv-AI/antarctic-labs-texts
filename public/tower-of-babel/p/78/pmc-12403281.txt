A subgroup analysis of locally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients with small lymph nodes and large nodes using adaptive radiotherapy
Authors: Wenyan Yao, Shuhui Dong, Peixun Xu, Ziyue Zhong, Zongtai Li, Yupeng Zhou, Yongwen Fang, Zhiyue Lin, Mengxue He, Jiang Hu, Weixiong Xia, Senkui Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the anatomical and dosimetric differences in target areas and organs at risk (OARs) after adjusting the radiotherapy schedule for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC).MethodsIn total, 34 patients who underwent radiotherapy for NPC were selected. A new localization computed tomography (CT) scan was performed after 25th and before 26th fraction. The target area and organs at risk were redrawn based on the new CT, and the radiotherapy plan was redesigned for the seven remaining rounds of radiotherapy. Plan1 consists of the old image and old plan, Plan2 consists of the new image and new plan, and Plan1−2 consists of the new image and old plan. Data on the volume and dose of target areas and organs at risk were collected for two schedule sets.ResultsIn the re-scan CT, the volume of Planning Target Volume for Nodal Region Right (PTVnd (R)), Planning Target Volume for Nodal Region Left (PTVnd (L)), left parotid and right parotid decreased significantly (P < 0.05). Additionally, the centroids of the left and right parotid moved towards the centre, abdomen and head. Compared to Plan1, the D95 and V100 of PTVnd (R) and PTVnd (L) of Plan1−2 was significantly lower, also significant differences were observed in the Dmean and D50 of the left and right parotid, and the D1cc of the spinal cord (P < 0.05). In comparison with Plan2 and Plan1−2, except for the Dmean and D95 of Planning Target Volume nodal extension (PTVnx) and the D95 of PTVnd (R), significant dosimetric differences were observed in the target area and all organs at risk indicators (P < 0.05). In the re-designed plan, the exposure dose to organs at risk was reduced.ConclusionsDue to significant anatomical changes in the volume and position of both target volumes and organs at risk during the later fractions of radiotherapy for NPC, the dose coverage of lymph-node regions can no longer meet clinical requirements. Adaptive radiotherapy ensures adequate target coverage while simultaneously reducing the dose to surrounding critical structures.Keywords: Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Adaptive radiotherapy, Anatomical change, Re-scanned CT

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is highly sensitive to radiation with a high prevalence in the southern region of China [1, 2]. Intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) is commonly used for the treatment due to the irregular shape of the tumour lesion [3]. NPC Patients may experience regression of the primary tumour and metastatic lymph nodes in the neck during radiotherapy, which may result in alterations to tumour morphology, volume, and location and affecting the surrounding normal tissues’ anatomical structure [4, 5]. Moreover, weight loss and changes in body contour can also lead to the deviation of the target area and surrounding organs of risk (OAR) ’s position from before, eventually impairing the radiotherapy’s accuracy [6].

Adaptive radiotherapy refers to obtaining new images during treatment and adjusting the radiation plan parameters based on the new imaging results [7]. By monitoring changes in the volume and location of the target area and critical organs, and modifying the treatment plan as necessary, we aim to ensures adequate target coverage while simultaneously reducing the dose to surrounding critical structures [8, 9]. Niu et al. investigated the use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy and replanning IMRT for nasopharyngeal carcinoma with intracranial invasion to protect critical normal tissue [4]. Lu et al. [10] found that optimizing radiotherapy after the 25th fraction could ensure the prescribed dose to the target area while reducing the cumulative dose to certain vital organs. Lu et al. [10] found that modifying the treatment plan after the 25th fraction ensured that the prescribed dose was delivered to the target area, while sparing adjacent critical organs from excessive radiation.

However, there is currently no consensus on the timing, triggering mode, and beneficiaries of adaptive radiotherapy.

Our study assesses the morphological changes of target and OARs for patients suffering from NPC after 26 sessions of radiation therapy, to evaluate the necessity of replanning. Moreover, by comparing the dosimetric differences between the initial and re-designed plan, a reference is provided for revising the clinic radiotherapy.

Methods and materials

Patient selection

We selected 34 patients with III-IVA NPC who received IMRT at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) from January 2022 to November 2022. We retrospectively analysed 34 patients with NPC who received IMRT from January 2022 to November 2022 at the Radiotherapy Department of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. The median age of the patients was 49 years (range, 16–70 years). The inclusion criteria were: (1) histologically confirmed NPC; (2) did not suffer from other malignant tumours; (3) received neoadjuvant chemotherapy and platinum-based concurrent chemoradiotherapy; and (4) received treatment from linear accelerator (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with recurrent or metastatic NPC. and (2) patients with previous radiotherapy or chemotherapy (Table 1).

Radiotherapy and treatment planning

Before radiotherapy, all patients were immobilised with a thermoplastic head-and-neck mask and subsequently underwent CT simulation. Both the CT scanning and reconstruction layers had a thickness of 3 mm. The scanning range extended from the upper edge of the frontal sinus to 2 cm below the lower edge of the clavicle head.

The physician contoured slice-by-slice the target areas, cervical lymph-node drainage areas, and organs at risk (e.g., parotid glands, optic nerves, brainstem, spinal cord, and optic chiasm) on the CT images in the treatment-planning system (TPS). The planning target volume (PTV) forms the boundary of the clinical target volume (CTV). PTV is defined by expanding CTV boundary outward by 3–5 mm. The radiotherapy plan was calculated and optimised on the TPS, while the dose to the target volume and critical organs was evaluated using the dose and volume histogram (DVH) and cross-sectional dose distribution. The treatment plan for each patient was validated before the treatment.

The first CT scan was used for 1 st to 26th fraction, and the second set of CT scans was used for the seven last radiotherapy sessions. The second CT scan was performed according to the original position fixation and reference coordinate points. All target volumes and organs at risk were re-contoured from scratch on the re-planning CT, using the initial contours only as a reference while accounting for anatomical changes.

Data acquisition

Two CT image sets were acquired: the initial planning CT before IMRT, and a re-planning CT after the 25th fraction, both used to assess parotid-gland positional changes. The following parameters were acquired from the DVH maps: the volume of the target and organs at risk, mean dose (Dmean), dose received by 95% of the target area volume (D95), the percentage of volume receiving 100% of the prescribed dose (V100) and the dose received by 50% of the volume of the left and right parotid glands (D50). The mean dose received by 1 cm³ of the spinal cord (D1cc) and the dose received by 1% of the volume of the brainstem, right and left optic nerves, right and left temporal lobes, and optic chiasm (D1) were also acquired. Information on positional changes in the left and right parotid glands in three directions was acquired, and the similarity coefficient (DSC) of both glands was calculated as DSC = 2×VolumeAB/(VolumeA+VolumeB).

The data were classified as follows: Plan1 refers to the pre-radiotherapy CT image (old image), and the first plan (old plan). Plan2 denotes the re-optimised IMRT plan generated on the re-planning CT obtained after the 25th fraction (new images). Plan1−2 represents the use of the old plan on the new image (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

The patient volumes and doses were stated as the mean ± standard deviation (± s). The dosimetric differences between the three groups were analysed using a two-pair sample t-test. The data were analysed using IBM SPSS 27.0 computerised statistical data software. A test level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed) was used as the threshold. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Volume changes of targets and organs of risk

A paired-sample t-test was used to compare the dose–volume parameters derived from the structures that had been (re-)delineated on the initial CT (Plan1) with those re-delineated on the re-planning CT (Plan2), revealing statistically significant differences in the volumes of PTVnd (R), PTVnd (L), the left parotid gland, and the right parotid gland (P < 0.05, Table 2). The mean volume of PTVnx in Plan2 was reduced by 1.2%±4.1%, the mean volume of PTVnd (R) was reduced by 9.6%±16.7%, the mean volume of PTVnd (L) was reduced by 7%±20.3%, and the mean volumes of the left and right parotid glands were reduced by 25.7%±16.3% and 22.4%±13.1%, respectively, compared with those of Plan1. The percentage reduction in the volume of the parotid glands on both sides was analysed, and the results showed that parotid glands with a large initial volume also had a relatively large volume reduction during radiotherapy (P < 0.001).

Plan1 vs. Plan1−2

Comparing the doses to the target areas between Plan 1 and Plan1−2, the results showed significant differences at D95 and V100 only in PTVnd (R) and PTVnd (R) (P < 0.05, Table 4). Based on the comparison of the doses to the organs at risk in the two groups, the left and right parotid doses increased in Plan1−2, in which the mean dose to the left parotid was higher than that in Plan1 in 71% of patients, the left parotid D50 increased in 76% of patients, the right parotid Dmean increased in 82% of patients, the right parotid D50 increased in 85% of patients, the spinal cord D1cc increased in 65% of patients, and the differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05, Table 4).

Plan2 vs. Plan1−2

The comparison between Plan 2 and Plan 1−2 shows that the changes in dosimetric parameters in the plan reflect the advantages replanning. Based on the comparison of the dose to target areas in the two groups, no difference was observed in the PTVnx Dmean, D95 and PTVnd (R) D95, and the differences in all other indicators were statistically significant (P < 0.05). Comparing the dose of critical organs in the two groups, the dose of each index in Plan1−2 was significantly higher than that in Plan2 (P < 0.05) (Table 4). A decrease was observed in V100 (%) of PTVnd (R) and PTVnd (L), whereas all other parameters increased.

Discussion

The volumes of the NPC target area, cervical lymph nodes, and surrounding normal tissues, as well as changes in their dose and conformity, were systematically monitored throughout the course of IMRT for NPC [11–13]. Based on the analysis of rescan CT and replanning, the cervical lymph-node volumes underwent varying degrees of shrinkage, in which the mean volume of PTV for lymph nodes (PTVnd) shrunk by 7 ~ 9.6% compared with the pre-radiotherapy period. Chophy et al. reported the similar result of PTVnx and PTVnd, however, we found the mean volume of PTV for nasopharyngeal tumour (PTVnx) only shrunk by 1.2%. Maybe it was due to all patients enrolled in this study were locally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma [14]. Among the organs at risk, the parotid glands are particularly vulnerable to radiation damage due to their high radiosensitivity. The mean volume of both the left and right parotid glands was reduced by 25.7% and 22.4%, respectively. In a study by Wang et al. [15], anatomical changes were observed in 15 patients with locally progressed nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and they noted the displacement of parotid glands of both sides to the midline by an average of 4.8 and 4.3 mm, respectively. In the present study, the stroma of the left parotid gland was displaced medially and upward by 1.72 ± 1.70 and 1.32 ± 2.46 mm, respectively, while the right parotid stroma was displaced medially and upward by 1.09 ± 1.76 and 2.03 ± 1.83 mm, respectively. These displacements were similar to the above-mentioned results for the left and right parotid gland. Overall, patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma exhibit a tendency of external contour retraction during radiotherapy.

Could anatomical changes cause actual dose redistribution during radiotherapy? Our study compared the plan applied to the initial and re-scanned image, and found no statistically significant difference in the dose coverage of the nasopharyngeal tumour. However, the coverage rate of the lymph-node targets was below 95% (PTVnd(L): 93.0%, PTVnd(R):93.3%), which may increase the likelihood of cancer recurrence. If radiotherapy for nasopharyngeal cancer is not re-planned, it could lead to a noteworthy rise in the radiation dose to crucial organs, including the parotid gland, brainstem and spinal cord [3, 16, 17]. In the present study, the original plans applied to the initial and second CT images were compared. It was shown that the critical organs such as the right and left parotid gland Dmean and D50 and brainstem D1 and spinal cord D1cc in Plan1−2 were statistically significantly higher than Plan1. Although the dosimetric parameters of OARs were still within the safe threshold range, an increased risk of complications, similar to Kumar’s report [18], might be avoidable. It is worth noting that the higher radiation dose to the parotid gland is due to its reduced volume and internal contraction, but the D1cc of brainstem and spinal cord with little volume change was also significantly increased. The objective triggering conditions for adaptive radiotherapy not only consider anatomical changes, but also dosimetric changes, especially for serial organs.

In this study, PTV rather than GTV was chosen as the primary volume for dosimetric evaluation based on several considerations. First, international multicenter clinical trials such as RTOG 0615 and NRG-HN001 commonly use the PTV as the benchmark for dose coverage. The PTV typically includes a 3–5 mm margin beyond the gross tumor volume (GTV) and is generally required to receive at least 95% of the prescribed dose [19, 20]. Second, although the GTV may undergo considerable changes during the course of radiotherapy, as long as these changes occur within the original PTV, dose coverage is usually maintained and adaptive re-planning is not necessary [21, 22]. In contrast, using the GTV as the evaluation volume may increase the risk of false-positive findings.

In addition, considering the anatomical asymmetry commonly observed in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, we divided the parotid glands by both left–right laterality and ipsilateral–contralateral orientation for subgroup analysis. Both grouping strategies revealed similar trends in dose reduction [22, 23], and for consistency, we adopted the conventional left–right grouping approach in this study.

Regarding the Plan1–2 scheme, this was a simulation in which the original treatment plan was applied to the second CT scan to estimate the potential changes in dose distribution that might have occurred if adaptive re-planning had not been performed. While not implemented clinically, this simulation approach is commonly used in adaptive radiotherapy research to evaluate theoretical dose variations [21].

Finally, all patients were treated using Varian linear accelerators to reduce variability arising from equipment differences. Previous studies have demonstrated that, under comparable imaging and planning protocols, modern intensity-modulated radiotherapy platforms produce consistent and generalizable dosimetric outcomes [23]. Therefore, the findings of this study are broadly applicable.

The anatomical and dosimetric changes that occur during the course of radiation therapy are influenced by multiple underlying mechanisms. Anatomical and dosimetric changes during radiation therapy are driven by tumor shrinkage, normal tissue response, and patient-specific factors like weight loss. These changes, particularly in the nasopharyngeal region, can significantly alter dose distribution, risking underdosing of the target or overdosing of nearby tissues. The most rapid changes usually occur within the first two to three weeks, especially in radiosensitive tumors, necessitating timely treatment adjustments to maintain efficacy and minimize toxicity. Optimal re-planning should occur around the third to fourth week when most anatomical changes have stabilized but before dose deviations compromise treatment. Early re-planning may be necessary if significant changes are detected. Re-imaging with nasopharyngeal MRI, which offers superior soft tissue contrast, can enhance the delineation of anatomical changes and refine treatment plans to better protect critical structures such as the temporal lobes, brainstem, and optic nerves. Adaptive radiation therapy (ART) is valuable for managing these changes, though greater caution is needed for the nasopharynx due to its complex anatomy. Decisions on adaptation should be based on anatomical changes, their impact on dose distribution, and the risk to critical structures.

Sectional radiotherapy mode may be a good solution to correct the dose uncertainty caused by patients’ anatomical changes. It was reported that re-planning during IMRT treatment in patients with NPC resulted in a dose reduction of brainstem, spinal cord and optic nerve. These findings indicate that replanning leads to a significant enhancement in dose–volume parameters for organs at risk [24, 25]. Our study performed CT re-scan on 34 patients with NPC who underwent 26 radiotherapy treatments. We involved re-studying the plan design by comparing the new plan (Plan2) with the synthetic plan obtained by superimposing the original plan onto the new CT (Plan1-2). It was shown that the re-optimization improved the dose coverage of the lymph nodes while significantly reducing the dosimetric parameters of most OARs (Table 4), which was similar to Huang’s report [24].

It is recommended to perform CT re-scanning and treatment plan re-design before the 26th fraction of radiotherapy for patients with locally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma, particularly those at N2 and N3 stages. However, several limitations of our study need to be acknowledged. Our study did not analyse the trends of anatomical changes in the target and OARs. Additionally, this study did not assess the radiation therapy-related toxicities and side effects. Future research could address this aspect to gain a more comprehensive understanding of treatment outcomes. Moreover, compared to a unified re-scanning time, it was more reasonable to start re-planning based on individual differences of patients, such as lateral neck diameter [26].

Conclusions

Due to anatomical changes observed from fraction 26 to 33 (the last seven fractions) of radiotherapy for NPC, the overall contraction change will make the dose coverage of lymph nodes unable to meet clinical requirements. Adaptive radiotherapy can ensure the adequate dose coverage of target and reduce the dose of peripheral organs at risk.
