Prevalence of overweight and obesity and their associations with lifestyle habits among late adolescents in the Northern Border Region of Saudi Arabia: a cross‑sectional study
Authors: Lobna Mohamed Mohamed Abu Negm, Taghreed Hussien Aboelola, Fathia Ahmed Mersal, Jawaher Mohammed Alanazi, Rawan Khalid Alanazi, Reem Ali Jalawi Al-Shammari, Raghad Saad Alanazi, Manal S. Fawzy, Fatma Abdou Ahmed Eltaib
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent obesity is a growing public health problem worldwide, and Saudi data show a high and rising burden of excess weight among youth with marked regional and gender disparities. Late adolescence is a critical window in which lifestyle behaviours consolidate and track into adulthood.AimTo estimate the prevalence of overweight and obesity (primary outcome) and to examine their associations with lifestyle habits, including physical activity, sedentary behaviour, sleep, and dietary patterns (secondary outcomes), among late adolescents in the Northern Border Region of Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross‑sectional study was conducted among 352 late adolescents (18-21 years) enrolled in high schools and universities in the Northern Border Region, selected from a finite population of 51,461 using Cochran's formula. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from self‑reported weight/height, and classified according to WHO cut‑offs. Lifestyle habits were assessed using the validated Arab Teens Lifestyle (ATLS) questionnaire, which covers sociodemographic characteristics, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, sleep, and dietary habits. Associations between BMI categories and covariates were examined using Pearson's Chi-square tests and t‑tests/ANOVA, and multiple linear regression was used to identify factors independently associated with BMI.ResultsAmong participants, 54.1% of those aged 18 years or younger were overweight, while 68.8% of those >18 years were obese (p =0.004). Males predominated in the overweight group (72.1%), whereas females accounted for most obese participants (59.7%; p <0.001). Lower family income (5,000 to less than 10,000 SAR) was associated with overweight (73.8%) and obesity (57.1%; p < 0.001). High screen time (>2 hours/day) was reported by 82.8% of overweight adolescents (p <0.001). High consumption of sugary and energy drinks was associated with higher BMI, whereas frequent fruit and vegetable intake was associated with significantly lower BMI (all p <0.001). In multivariable analysis, total physical activity was independently associated with lower BMI (B = −0.16, p<0.001), whereas a higher composite dietary score was modestly positively associated with BMI (B = 0.12, p<0.001).ConclusionOverweight and obesity are common among late adolescents in the Saudi Northern Border Region and are closely linked to sociodemographic, behavioural, and dietary factors. Physical activity emerged as the key modifiable protective factor. In contrast, high screen time and frequent consumption of sugary drinks were associated with higher BMI, underscoring the need for targeted changes in food environments and opportunities for safe physical activity.

Introduction

Overweight and obesity have emerged as major public health challenges of the 21st century, with prevalence increasing steadily across age groups and world regions [1]. Obesity is defined as abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that presents a health risk [2], commonly operationalised using body mass index (BMI), with a BMI of 25.0–29.9 kg/m² classified as overweight and a BMI of ≥ 30.0 kg/m² as obesity [3]. Recent global estimates indicate that more than 390 million children and adolescents aged 5–19 years have overweight or obesity, reflecting a more than two‑fold increase in combined prevalence since 1990 [4]. This epidemic now affects low‑, middle‑, and high‑income countries alike, with the fastest relative increases observed in low‑ and middle‑income settings [5].

Saudi Arabia has undergone rapid economic development, urbanisation, and technological change over recent decades, accompanied by marked shifts in nutrition and lifestyle [6]. These shifts have been characterised by increased consumption of energy‑dense, nutrient‑poor foods, reduced habitual physical activity, greater reliance on motorised transport, and prolonged screen‑based sedentary behaviour [7]. National and regional data consistently show a high and rising prevalence of overweight and obesity among Saudi children and adolescents, with boys often exhibiting higher rates of excess weight than girls [8]. However, gender patterns may vary by age and region [9]. In particular, the Northern Border Region has experienced demographic change and increasing urbanisation, which may be driving obesogenic lifestyle patterns among young people [10].

​Adolescence is a critical period when health‑related behaviours are established and often track into adulthood. Late adolescence and emerging adulthood (approximately 18–24 years) are especially important, as individuals gain greater autonomy, transition to higher education or work, and encounter new social and environmental influences that shape diet, physical activity, and sleep behaviours [11]. In Saudi Arabia, late adolescents often face unique challenges, including irregular meal patterns, high intake of fast food and sugar‑sweetened beverages, and reduced engagement in structured physical activity, all of which have been linked to higher obesity risk [12, 13]. Concurrently, a cultural shift toward sedentary leisure pursuits, such as prolonged screen time and electronic gaming, has been associated with lower physical fitness and adverse cardiometabolic profiles among youth [14].

​A growing body of evidence from Saudi Arabia and other settings indicates that multiple modifiable lifestyle factors, such as unhealthy dietary habits, insufficient physical activity, excessive sedentary behaviour, short or poor‑quality sleep, and psychosocial stress, act synergistically to increase the risk of overweight and obesity in adolescents [9]. At the same time, obesity in adolescence is associated with a broad spectrum of short‑ and long‑term complications, including insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease, obstructive sleep apnoea, and impaired quality of life. Longitudinal studies also show that adolescents with obesity are substantially more likely to have obesity and cardiometabolic disease in adulthood than their normal‑weight peers [15–18]. Even modest weight reduction before or during puberty has been linked to a lower risk of subsequent cardiovascular and metabolic disorders, underscoring the importance of early identification and intervention [19].

​Despite the accumulation of national-level data on childhood and adolescent obesity in Saudi Arabia, there remains a paucity of region‑specific evidence on how distinct lifestyle domains, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, sleep, and dietary patterns, relate to weight status among late adolescents in the Northern Border Region. Understanding these context‑specific lifestyle determinants is essential for designing targeted, culturally appropriate interventions and informing regional public health policies [20]. Accordingly, this cross‑sectional study had two objectives. First, to determine the prevalence of overweight and obesity (primary outcome) among late adolescents (18–21 years) in the Northern Border Region of Saudi Arabia. Second, to investigate the associations of BMI with key lifestyle domains, including physical activity, sedentary behaviour (particularly screen time), sleep duration, and dietary patterns, as secondary outcomes. We hypothesised that adolescents with higher levels of total physical activity and more favourable dietary patterns (e.g., greater fruit and vegetable intake, lower consumption of sugary and energy drinks) would have lower BMI. In contrast, those reporting longer screen time and less healthy dietary habits would have higher BMI. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply the Arab Teens Lifestyle (ATLS) questionnaire to comprehensively characterise lifestyle domains and their associations with overweight and obesity among late adolescents (18–21 years) in the Northern Border Region, a socio‑demographically distinct area of Saudi Arabia where adolescent obesity remains under‑studied.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional analytical study examined the relationships among weight status, lifestyle habits, and sociodemographic factors among late adolescents in the Northern Borders Province (NBP) of Saudi Arabia. Data were collected electronically using a structured online questionnaire distributed to high schools and universities across the NBP, enabling coverage of both secondary and tertiary educational institutions.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the "Local Committee of Bioethics of Northern Border University (reference number: 121/24/H) on 22/01/2025. Before participation, all potential participants accessed a digital information sheet describing the study objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality assurances, and the voluntary nature of participation. Implied informed consent was obtained when participants indicated agreement and proceeded to complete the survey. Data were collected anonymously, stored on password‑protected devices, and handled in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants and sampling

The target population comprised late adolescents aged 18–21 years residing in the NBP and enrolled in either high school or university at the time of data collection. This population served as the sampling frame and included approximately 33,248 male and 28,213 female adolescents in the NBP (total = 51,461), according to regional administrative statistics (https://atlas.monshaat.gov.sa/en/profile/region/northern-borders). The sampling frame, therefore, comprised late adolescents who were accessible through educational institutions in the region’s main urban centres.

Inclusion criteria were: age 18–21 years, current enrollment in an educational institution within the NBP, and ability to read and understand Arabic (or English, if applicable). Participants who reported cognitive or physical impairments that could limit their ability to complete the questionnaire reliably were excluded. In addition, any respondents who did not meet the inclusion criteria (e.g. age outside 18–21 years or not currently enrolled in an educational institution in the specified region) were excluded from the analysis.

A non‑probability convenience sampling approach was used. Eligible students were invited to participate via their schools and universities and through social media channels, and all volunteers who met the inclusion criteria and provided consent during the data‑collection period were included. Because the sampling was based on voluntary response rather than random selection, we acknowledge that the sample may not fully represent all late adolescents in the NBP, particularly those not in formal education; this limitation is considered when interpreting external validity and is discussed in the Limitations section.

This approach ensured sufficient representativeness of the target population while maintaining methodological rigour and alignment with best practices for population-based cross-sectional research.

Pilot testing and instrument validity

A pilot study was conducted with 10% of the target sample (n = 27 late adolescents) from the same population to assess the clarity, comprehensibility, and feasibility of the electronic survey. Feedback from pilot participants was used to refine wording and response options where necessary, without altering the ATLS questionnaire’s core structure. The psychometric properties of the ATLS questionnaire have been established in previous studies in Arab adolescent populations, showing high test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.85) and acceptable validity coefficients (r = 0.30–0.37, p < 0.05) against pedometer‑measured step counts [23]. Pilot responses were not included in the final analysis.

Data collection procedure and recruitment

The survey link was disseminated through a two‑stage process. First, official emails were sent to high school and university administrators in the NBP, requesting their collaboration in sharing the survey link with eligible students through institutional mailing lists and learning management systems. Second, the link was circulated via student WhatsApp groups and other commonly used social media channels to enhance reach and participation, while emphasising voluntary participation and anonymity. The survey landing page included study information and a consent statement; only participants who confirmed consent could proceed to the questionnaire. Data collection was conducted over one month (e.g., March–April 2024), during which periodic reminders were sent to maximise response rates [28].

In total, 450 adolescents who met the inclusion criteria accessed the questionnaire. Of these, 352 provided complete data on key variables and were included in the final analysis, corresponding to a response rate of 78.2%. This response rate is considered acceptable for online cross-sectional surveys among adolescents and supports the adequacy of the sample size [29].

Statistical analysis

Data were exported from Google Forms into Microsoft Excel and then imported into IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Continuous variables were checked for completeness, outliers, and distributional assumptions using graphical methods (histograms and Q–Q plots) and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations (or medians and ranges, as appropriate). Given the sample size, independent‑samples t‑tests and one‑way ANOVA were considered acceptable for group comparisons of continuous variables, as these tests are robust to moderate deviations from normality.

BMI was analysed as both a continuous and a categorical variable (normal weight, overweight, obesity). Associations between BMI categories and categorical variables (e.g., sex, parental education, screen‑time categories, sleep duration categories, physical activity levels, and dietary habit categories) were examined using Pearson’s Chi‑square (χ²) test. For continuous variables (e.g., BMI and lifestyle scores), independent-samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used, as appropriate, to compare mean BMI or lifestyle scores across groups defined by dietary and physical activity patterns [25, 30]. When an ANOVA indicated a statistically significant overall difference, post‑hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test to identify which specific group means differed while controlling the family‑wise error rate. Where relevant, 95% confidence intervals were calculated and reported for mean estimates and effect sizes.

​To identify independent variables associated with BMI, multiple linear regression analysis was performed, with BMI (kg/m²) as the dependent variable. Candidate independent variables included sociodemographic factors (age, sex, parental education), total physical activity score, sedentary time (hours/day), sleep duration, and dietary habits score, selected based on a priori theoretical relevance and evidence from previous adolescent obesity studies. All selected predictors were entered simultaneously using an enter (forced‑entry) approach. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance statistics; all VIFs ranged from 1.04 to 1.33, and all tolerance values ranged from 0.75 to 0.96, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Model assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals) were assessed through residual plots and normal probability plots. In sensitivity analyses, BMI category (normal vs. overweight/obese) was also examined using logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for selected variables, analogous to contemporary adolescent obesity studies [30, 31]. Statistical significance was set at a two‑sided p‑value < 0.05 for all tests.

Results

Weight status and personal characteristics

Table 1 shows that weight status (normal BMI, overweight, obesity) was significantly associated with age, gender, father’s education, monthly family income, screen time, and sleep duration (all p < 0.05). Adolescents aged ≤ 18 years accounted for 54.1% of the overweight group, whereas those > 18 years accounted for 68.8% of the obese group (p = 0.005). Males comprised 72.1% of the overweight group, while females comprised 59.7% of the obese group (p < 0.001). Lower family income (5,000–<10,000 SAR) was reported by 73.8% of overweight and 57.1% of obese adolescents, compared with 34.0% of normal‑weight adolescents (p < 0.001). High screen time (> 2 h/day) was reported by 82.8% of overweight and 61.0% of obese participants, versus 44.4% of those with normal BMI (p < 0.001). Sleeping ≥ 8 h/day was reported by 79.1% of normal‑weight, 63.9% of overweight, and 84.4% of obese adolescents (p = 0.002).

Weight status and physical activity

Table 2 shows that the BMI category was significantly associated with all physical activity indicators (all p < 0.001). Adolescents with normal BMI consistently reported the highest prevalence of high‑frequency activity (> 3 days/week) for walking (66.7%), running (53.6%), and household activities (61.4%). Overweight adolescents had the lowest proportions of high‑frequency walking (21.3%), running (13.9%), and other sports (13.1%), yet showed the highest prevalence of high total physical activity (82.8%). Obese participants reported particularly low engagement in household activities (16.9%) but relatively high participation in other sports (50.6%).

BMI and dietary habits

As shown in Table 3, mean BMI differed across intake categories for several dietary items. Adolescents with high intakes of sugar‑sweetened beverages had higher mean BMI than those with lower intake (31.19 vs. 24.45 kg/m², p < 0.0001), and the largest difference was observed for energy drinks (34.38 vs. 24.51 kg/m², p < 0.0001). Mean BMI was also higher in the high‑intake groups for fast food, fried potatoes or chips, and cakes/biscuits/sweets (all p < 0.0001). Breakfast at home was not significantly related to mean BMI (p = 0.069), and vegetable intake showed no significant difference (p = 0.457). Milk/dairy intake and fresh fruit consumption showed smaller but statistically significant differences in mean BMI between low‑ and high‑intake groups (p = 0.001 and p = 0.011, respectively).

Discussion

Obesity in adolescence is a major global public health challenge with substantial health and economic impacts, yet most economic and epidemiologic data still originate from high‑income countries [32]. Understanding how lifestyle behaviours intersect with sociodemographic factors in specific regional contexts is therefore crucial for informing targeted prevention strategies [13, 33]. This study examined the prevalence of overweight and obesity and their associations with lifestyle habits among late adolescents in the Northern Border Region of Saudi Arabia, a setting undergoing rapid social and nutritional transition.

Age, gender, and socioeconomic context

In this study, younger participants (≤ 18 years) showed a higher prevalence of overweight. In contrast, older adolescents (> 18 years) exhibited a greater burden of obesity, suggesting progressive weight gain across the late‑adolescent transition. These findings are compatible with previous work from the Northern Borders region reporting high levels of excess weight in both adolescents and adults, and they align with national data indicating a substantial burden of overweight and obesity among Saudi youth [10, 30, 32, 34]. These age‑related patterns may reflect cumulative exposure to obesogenic environments during late adolescence, including increasing academic stress, greater autonomy over food choices, and progressive declines in structured physical education or organised sports [11, 12].

A marked gender disparity was observed: males predominated in the overweight category, whereas females constituted the majority of obese participants. This pattern may reflect gendered differences in activity types, body‑image norms, and help‑seeking behaviour, as several Saudi and regional studies have documented complex, sometimes opposing, gender trends in adolescent obesity and related behaviours [35, 36]. While some reports show higher obesity prevalence among females, others highlight higher overweight and central adiposity in males, underscoring the need for gender‑sensitive approaches that recognise distinct behavioural and sociocultural determinants [13, 33, 37]. International studies from high‑income and middle‑income settings similarly indicate that sociocultural norms around femininity, modesty, and outdoor activity can constrain girls’ participation in vigorous physical activity. In contrast, boys may be more exposed to energy‑dense diets and muscle‑building ideals, leading to different obesity phenotypes across genders [38, 39].

The association between parental education and weight status was non‑linear: university‑educated fathers were common in both normal‑BMI and obese groups, while secondary‑educated fathers were overrepresented among overweight adolescents. Evidence from the European population suggests that higher parental education generally protects against unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, and sedentary behaviour [40]. However, non‑material aspects of socioeconomic status (e.g., self‑perceived status, family dynamics) may better explain adolescent health inequalities than purely material indicators [41, 42]. In the present study, lower family income (5,000–<10,000 SAR) was strongly associated with both overweight and obesity, consistent with work indicating that socioeconomic constraints limit access to healthy foods and structured activity, and that fragmented or under‑funded obesity‑prevention initiatives struggle to reach vulnerable groups [43–45]. At the same time, other studies have shown that higher‑income families in some contexts may also exhibit elevated adolescent obesity, illustrating that the income–obesity relationship is context‑dependent and mediated by food environments, urbanisation, and cultural norms [18, 46]. From a public health perspective, these findings suggest that adolescent obesity prevention in the Northern Border Region should prioritise structural measures that reduce cost and access barriers to healthy foods and safe activity spaces for low‑income families, rather than relying primarily on individual‑level counselling [47, 48].

Screen time, sleep, and physical activity

High screen time (> 2 h/day) was particularly prevalent among overweight adolescents, supporting meta‑analytic evidence that greater screen exposure modestly increases the risk of overweight and obesity in youth [49, 50]. Beyond displacing physical activity, excessive screen time is linked to increased snacking, exposure to marketing of energy‑dense foods, and poorer sleep, all of which may contribute to positive energy balance, dysregulated appetite signalling, and weight gain [51, 52]. The present findings also align with reports that adolescents, especially girls with lower screen time, may have lower odds of obesity, highlighting the potential benefit of integrated interventions that simultaneously address sedentary behaviour, diet, and activity [49, 53, 54].

Sleep emerged as another important correlate. Adequate sleep (≥ 8 h) was common among normal‑weight and obese adolescents but less frequent in the overweight group, suggesting a non‑linear relationship between sleep duration and BMI. Recent reviews emphasise a bidirectional link between obesity and sleep disorders, mediated by hormonal dysregulation, circadian disruption, appetite regulation, and inflammation, and highlight the need to integrate sleep assessment into obesity prevention and management [40]. Experimental and observational data also suggest that improving sleep timing and duration can favourably influence weight, dietary choices, and cardiometabolic risk, reinforcing the importance of addressing sleep within multi‑component interventions [32, 55]. The apparent non‑linear pattern in our data, with shorter sleep among overweight but not obese adolescents, may indicate that reduced sleep duration is an early marker of lifestyle disruption before more severe obesity and sleep pathology become established [56–58].

Consistent with the previous literature, this study found inverse associations between domain‑specific physical activity (walking, running, household activities) and BMI, with normal‑weight adolescents reporting the highest frequency of regular activity. Interestingly, overweight participants reported the highest levels of “total” physical activity despite low engagement in specific activity types, raising questions about the composition and intensity of reported activities, potential misclassification, or compensatory low‑intensity behaviours. Similar studies have shown that insufficiently active groups exhibit higher BMI than sufficiently active peers, and that physical activity is a key determinant of physical fitness and health, independent of BMI [59–62]. These findings support the view that interventions should prioritise increasing moderate‑to‑vigorous, structured activity and reducing sedentary time, rather than relying solely on aggregate activity indices [63, 64]. They also echo international evidence that walking and other accessible aerobic activities are particularly important targets for adolescent health promotion because they can be incorporated into daily routines at relatively low cost [65].

Dietary habits and obesogenic environments

The study confirmed that higher intake of sugar‑sweetened beverages and energy drinks was significantly associated with higher BMI, with energy drinks showing the largest mean difference, in line with systematic reviews and meta‑analyses linking sugar‑sweetened beverages to weight gain and obesity in children and adolescents [66, 67]. Consistently higher BMI was also observed in high‑intake groups for fast food, fried potatoes or chips, and sweets, whereas breakfast at home and vegetable intake were not significantly associated with BMI, and milk/dairy and fresh fruit showed only modest differences; these patterns may reflect measurement limitations (e.g., frequency rather than portion size), clustering of behaviours, or residual confounding [68–71].

​At the population level, dietary patterns revealed high‑frequency consumption of ultra‑processed, energy‑dense foods (fast food, fried snacks, sweet baked goods) and sugar‑sweetened beverages, alongside inadequate intake of fruits and vegetables, mirroring previous Saudi adolescent studies [7, 13, 59]. Such patterns are characteristic of obesogenic food environments, where convenient, inexpensive industrial foods overshadow access to fresh, nutrient‑dense options [68]. Mechanistically, frequent intake of sugar‑sweetened beverages and ultra‑processed foods can increase glycaemic load, promote insulin resistance, and alter gut‑hormone responses, which together favour fat storage and may reinforce cravings for high‑sugar, high‑fat foods [72, 73]. Contemporary nutrition research emphasises that a well‑balanced diet based on minimally processed foods, rich in fruits, vegetables, and micronutrients, is critical for maintaining metabolic health and modulating low‑grade inflammation associated with obesity [74]. The present findings therefore reinforce calls for structural interventions, such as regulating marketing to youth, improving school canteens, and fiscal or policy measures targeting sugar‑sweetened beverages and ultra‑processed foods, rather than relying solely on individual education [75, 76]. In the Northern Border Region, this could include revising school canteen offerings, implementing front-of-pack labelling, and restricting the promotion of energy drinks targeted at adolescents [77–79].

Gender patterns and multivariable determinants

An interesting feature of this study is that females had substantially lower BMI than males, but reported higher physical activity and lower dietary habit scores. In contrast, males reported healthier dietary habits yet exhibited higher BMI. This contrasts with some literature suggesting females are more vulnerable to body‑image concerns, emotional eating, and obesity‑related neural and psychological vulnerabilities, even when their measured BMI is similar or lower than that of males [80, 81]. The divergence observed here may reflect gender differences in reporting accuracy, food choices not captured by the score (e.g., portion size, snacking), or distinct social pressures influencing diet and activity in the Northern Border Region. It also suggests that gender‑specific mechanisms, including social media influences, body dissatisfaction, and mental health, should be examined more closely in future work [35, 82]. It may also point to gender‑specific coping strategies, whereby some male adolescents respond to stress with increased intake of energy‑dense foods, while some females engage in restrictive dieting and higher levels of activity driven by body‑image concerns. These patterns have been described in regional and international adolescent populations [83, 84].

​In the multivariable model, higher levels of physical activity were independently associated with lower BMI, reinforcing the central role of physical activity as a modifiable protective factor in youth [60]. Findings from paediatrics indicate that physical activity mediates the relationship between BMI and physical fitness, with active children demonstrating better motor performance and health outcomes across BMI categories [61, 85]. By contrast, the positive association between the composite dietary score and BMI in this sample suggests that the scoring algorithm may insufficiently distinguish between protective and obesogenic elements, or that adolescents with higher BMI may be attempting to adopt healthier patterns, a form of reverse causation frequently noted in cross‑sectional diet-obesity research [32, 86]. This behavioural compensation could lead to an apparent paradox in which higher scores on “healthier” lifestyle indices are observed among those already living with overweight or obesity. Sex remained independently associated with BMI, while age was not, within this narrow late‑adolescent range, consistent with the notion that behavioural and socioeconomic factors play a more prominent role than chronological age in this developmental window [36, 87].

Implications, strengths, and limitations

Collectively, the present findings underscore the importance of addressing multiple lifestyle domains, including physical activity, sedentary screen time, sleep, and diet, in tandem, rather than focusing on single behaviours in isolation. The findings also highlight that composite lifestyle indices, which combine several behaviours into a single score, can sometimes obscure important differences between specific domains (for example, physical activity versus diet) and may yield apparently paradoxical associations, such as higher “healthy lifestyle” scores among adolescents who already have overweight or obesity. The study adds region‑specific evidence from the Northern Border Region of Saudi Arabia using a validated lifestyle instrument, contributing to the limited literature on late adolescents in this setting [13, 22].

For policy‑makers and practitioners in the Northern Border Region, the results imply that effective strategies will need to integrate school‑based programmes, community‑level changes to food and physical activity environments, and tailored clinical counselling, rather than relying solely on short‑term educational campaigns. By documenting lifestyle patterns in late adolescents, our data can also inform national Saudi initiatives and global non‑communicable disease prevention agendas that prioritise adolescence as a key window for lifelong obesity risk reduction.

However, interpretation should consider several limitations, including the cross‑sectional design (which precludes causal inference and restricts conclusions to associations), reliance on self‑reported anthropometrics and lifestyle data (susceptible to recall and social‑desirability bias), and the use of frequency‑based dietary measures without portion‑size estimation. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs, objective measures of physical activity and sleep, and more granular dietary assessment, while also integrating psychological and environmental variables. Such approaches would better clarify the causal pathways linking lifestyle behaviours to obesity in late adolescence and support the development of comprehensive, context‑specific interventions in the Northern Border Region and similar settings.

Conclusion

This study shows that overweight and obesity are highly prevalent among late adolescents in the Northern Border Region of Saudi Arabia and are linked to sociodemographic, behavioural, and dietary factors. Overweight was more common among younger males, whereas obesity predominated among older adolescents and females, and higher adiposity was observed among adolescents from lower‑income families and those with fathers educated to the secondary level. High screen time, low engagement in walking and running, and greater consumption of sugar‑sweetened beverages and energy drinks were associated with higher BMI, whereas higher total physical activity and more favourable dietary patterns were associated with lower BMI. The positive association between the composite dietary score and BMI, together with divergent patterns across lifestyle domains, highlights the limitations of aggregate lifestyle indices and supports the need for domain‑specific, structurally oriented interventions that modify food environments and opportunities for safe physical activity, rather than relying solely on individual‑level counselling.

Recommendations and clinical implications

Based on the study findings and in line with current adolescent obesity prevention guidance, several practice‑oriented recommendations can be drawn (summarised in Fig. 3).

Summary of the proposed evidence-based recommendations of the present study [88–92]

Shift from individual blame to environmental support

Clinicians should move beyond generic advice such as “eat less, move more” and help adolescents and families connect with concrete local resources, including safe walking routes, youth sports facilities, and community programmes that facilitate regular activity and healthier food choices. As part of routine care, health professionals can also support advocacy for policies that reduce marketing of sugary drinks and ultra‑processed foods to youth and improve access to affordable, healthy foods in schools and neighbourhoods.

Prioritise physical activity (especially walking)

Routine care should prioritise prescribing regular physical activity, particularly brisk walking, as a core component of weight management and cardiometabolic risk reduction. Practical goals (for example, accumulating at least 60 min per day of moderate‑to‑vigorous activity, including walking to and from school or campus) should be individualised and monitored over time, in line with WHO recommendations.

​Tailored screening and counselling

Clinical assessments should incorporate age‑ and gender‑specific risk profiling, with attention to overweight in younger males and obesity in older adolescent females, alongside systematic evaluation of screen time, sleep duration, and physical activity. Targeted counselling should explicitly address reducing consumption of sugar‑sweetened and energy drinks and promote domain‑specific activities such as walking, running, and structured sports, which, in this study, were clearly associated with more favourable BMI profiles.
