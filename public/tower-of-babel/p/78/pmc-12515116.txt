Pharmacokinetics of UGN‑102, an investigational mitomycin‑containing reverse thermal gel for the treatment of non-muscle invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Sandip M Prasad, Michael J Louie, Brent Burger, Victoria Tsurutis, Nikky Ugwuoke, Dalit Strauss-Ayali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the pharmacokinetic properties of UGN-102, a mitomycin-containing reverse thermal gel.MethodsTwelve patients with NMIBC received 6 once-weekly intravesical instillations of UGN-102 (BL004: 120 mg mitomycin [n = 6]; BL005: 75 mg mitomycin [n = 6]). Plasma samples for determination of pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters were collected up to 6 h following instillation.ResultsIn BL004, mean Cmax was 20.39 ng/mL, and median was 16.10 ng/mL (range 4.00–40.40), 100-10-fold lower than reported myelosuppression toxic level (RMTL); and median Tmax was 1.5 h. Mean AUC0-6 was 56.23 ng·h/mL and mean apparent terminal half-life (t1/2) was 49 min. The highest observed Cmax (40.40 ng/mL) was 59-fold and 13-fold lower than Cmax following IV 30 mg or 10 mg mitomycin, respectively, and 10-fold lower than the RMTL. Maximum measured urine concentration was 635 µg/mL. In BL005, mean Cmax was 2.27 ng/mL, 181-fold lower than the RMTL; mean Tmax was 1.95 h and mean AUC0–6 was 5.69 ng·h/mL. At 4–6 h post intravesical instillation, mitomycin concentrations in urine were either below or approaching the lower level of quantification (< 0.250 µg/mL) in 5/6 patients. In the remaining patient, the measurable urine concentrations over the 6-hour period suggest dwell time was > 6 h post-instillation, possibly due to the tumor location restricting urine flow. However systemic exposure was < 4 ng/mL, 100-fold lower than the RMTL.ConclusionsIntravesical instillation of UGN-102 results in low-level systemic absorption of mitomycin, with Cmax values considerably lower than those following IV administration and those associated with myelosuppression.Study registrationBL004: NCT02307487; BL005: NCT03558503.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00280-025-04821-5.Keywords: UGN-102, Mitomycin, Pharmacokinetics, Non-muscle invasive bladder cancer, NMIBC

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the 6th most common cancer in the USA, accounting for about 4% of all cancers in the USA, with approximately 83,000 new cases diagnosed annually [1]. Approximately 90% of bladder cancers are confined to the bladder wall, of which about 75% are non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and 25% muscle invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) [2]. The American Urological Association (AUA) and the Society of Urologic Oncology (SUO) guidelines define intermediate risk (IR) NMIBC as a low-grade (LG) T1 tumor; a solitary high-grade (HG) Ta < 3 cm non-recurrent tumor; or an LG Ta tumor with specific features, i.e. recurrent within 1 year, large tumor size (>3 cm), or multifocal in nature [3, 4]. LG-IR-NMIBC is often multifocal in nature, with a high risk of recurrence but a low risk of progression to muscle-invasive bladder cancer [5, 6]. NMIBC has a high risk of recurrence and progresses to muscle invasive bladder cancer in about 6–17% of cases [3, 4]. High grade NMIBC can progress to MIBC within 5 years at a rate of 50% [7], with a high mortality rate [8].

The standard of care for low-grade intermediate-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (LG-IR-NMIBC) is transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) under general anesthesia, with or without adjunctive intravesical chemotherapy (IVCT) [4]. Intravesical administration of aqueous cytotoxic agents (such as mitomycin, gemcitabine and Bacille Calmette-Guerin [BCG]) have been used for chemoablation and/or as an adjunct therapy to TURBT in LG-IR-NMIBC, to reduce the risk of recurrence, and is supported by previous studies [9]. Mitomycin has also been used as an aqueous formulation, and dose-intense regimens of 3 instillations per week for 2 weeks have been shown to be effective [10]. Other agents used as adjuvants to TURBT include gemcitabine and BCG, the latter also having been shown to reduce recurrence if used as a maintenance therapy [11]. A recent systematic Cochrane review concluded that the utility of gemcitabine as an intravesical therapy was higher than aqueous mitomycin and better than BCG in patients that have previously had BCG and experienced recurrence [12]. However, due to normal urine flow, the dwell time of aqueous chemotherapy in the bladder is low and limits exposure of the tumor to the cytotoxic agent. In addition, the use of adjuvant intravesical agents is low in the US [13].

Mitomycin is an antibiotic which inhibits the synthesis of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), and at high doses can cause myelosuppression; cardiac, renal and pulmonary toxicity and can cause hemolytic anemia [14]. The systemic exposure associated with myelosuppression is 400 ng/mL [15]. Mitomycin administered by IV or as an aqueous intravesical administration, cleared from the circulation with a t1/2 range of 23–78 min (IV dose range 5–60 mg/m2, intravesical dose 20–40 mg) [16–20] and intravesical administration results in negligible systemic exposure [16, 18, 21–23].

Recent studies suggests that many patients with LG-IR-NMIBC can be successfully treated with UGN-102, a reverse thermal gel containing mitomycin administered via intravesical instillation via urinary catheter in the outpatient setting, without the need for surgery [24, 25]. UGN-102 is administered under chilled conditions as a liquid and forms a localized drug depot in the bladder at body temperature. Patients reported visible gel in urine for up to 24 hours (median 5 hours) after instillation [14]. In the ATLAS study, 142 LG-IR-NMIBC were randomized to UGN-102 ± TURBT and 140 to TURBT-alone. Complete response at 3 months was achieved in 92 (64.8% [95% confidence interval [CI], 56.3–72.6]) patients who received UGN-102 and 89 (63.6% [95% CI, 55.0–71.5]) patients who received TURBT. Of the patients who achieved complete response (CR) at the 3-month evaluation, the Kaplan-Meier estimate of duration of response (DoR) at 12 months post-CR was 79.7% (95% CI, 69.3–86.9) in the UGN-102 arm and 67.7% (95% CI, 55.8–77.1) in the TURBT arm. The between-arms hazard ratio for DoR in ATLAS was 0.46 (95% CI 0.24, 0.86), favoring the UGN-102 arm.

In the ENVISION study of 240 LG-IR-NMIBC patients treated with UGN-102, 191 patients achieved CR at 3 months (79.6%; 95% CI 73.9, 84.5). For these patients, complete response was durable, with a probability of remaining in response 12 months after CR of 82.3% (95% CI 75.9, 87.1) according to the Kaplan–Meier analysis [25]. UGN-102 has recently been FDA approved for the treatment of adults with recurrent LG-IR-NMIBC [26].

In this report, we document the pharmacokinetics of mitomycin observed when administering UGN-102 intravesically, for completeness we report the results from a study using 120 mg mitomycin (BL004), as well as from a study (BL005) using the dose subsequently selected for clinical development (75 mg). In study BL004 dose escalation above 120 mg mitomycin was planned, the study was terminated early as data from another study (BL003) suggested that 75 mg had a favorable safety profile and similar efficacy (CR rate) compared with the 120 mg dose used in BL004. Study BL003 compared the efficacy and safety of 37.5 mg and 75 mg doses of UGN-102 with an aqueous dose of 40 mg mitomycin in 39 patients with NMIBC and found superior efficacy with the 75 mg UGN-102 dose. The CR rates achieved were: 89.4% with 75 mg UGN-102; 68.2% with 40 mg mitomycin; and 41.2% with 37.5 mg UGN-102.

Methods

Pharmacokinetic (PK) data were generated during 2 studies using different intravesically administered doses of UGN-102 in patients with NMIBC (BL004, NCT02307487; and a sub study of BL005/OPTIMA II, NCT03558503 [27](Supplementary Table S1).

Study designs, drug dosages, and treatment plans

Study BL004

BL004 was an open-label multicenter study to assess the safety of pre-TURBT intravesical instillations of escalating doses of UGN-102 in patients with NMIBC. The study was performed between February 16, 2015, and March 21, 2017, at 2 sites in Israel. A total of 14 patients were treated with 6 once-weekly intravesical instillations of UGN-102 (120 mg mitomycin in 60 mL hydrogel) and 6 patients were evaluated for plasma mitomycin concentrations before (t = 0) and 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4.5, and 6 h after the 6th instillation. In addition, urine was collected before the 6th instillation and during the 6 h following instillation.

Study BL005

BL005 was a single-arm multicenter study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of UGN-102 as a primary treatment in patients with new or historic LG-IR-NMIBC. The study was performed between October 15, 2018, and October 21, 2020, at 20 sites in 2 countries (18 sites in the USA and 2 sites in Israel). Patients received 6 once-weekly intravesical instillations of UGN-102 (75 mg mitomycin in 56 mL hydrogel) in an outpatient setting. Plasma samples for determination of mitomycin concentrations were collected in 6 patients before (t = 0) and 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 h after the first instillation.

Both studies were conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the designated ethics committees and institutional review boards at each study site. All participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

Study BL004

Key inclusion criteria for BL004 were age ≥ 18 years; willing and able to sign an informed consent form (ICF); LG or HG NMIBC; no active urinary tract infection; and, if female and of childbearing potential, using 2 acceptable and effective methods of contraception until 6 months post-treatment.

Key exclusion criteria were: tumor located in prostatic urethra (for male patients) or in a bladder diverticulum, prior to or requiring pelvic radiotherapy; systemic chemotherapy within 1 year prior to screening; pregnant or breastfeeding female patient; treatment of bladder cancer with Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) within the last 12 months prior to screening, except for patients who had BCG treatment within 6–12 months prior to screening visit and were not symptomatic; treatment with intravesical chemotherapy within the 3 months prior to screening visit; contraindication or known sensitivity to mitomycin; and known current urinary retention requiring intermittent catheterization to empty the bladder.

Study BL005

Key inclusion criteria for BL005 were: age ≥ 18 years; willing and able to sign an ICF; newly diagnosed or historic LG-NMIBC (Ta) histologically confirmed by cold-cup biopsy at screening or within 6 weeks of screening; at intermediate risk of recurrence, defined as having 1 or 2 of (a) presence of multiple tumors, (b) solitary tumor > 3 cm, and/or (c) recurrence (≥ 1 occurrence of LG-NMIBC within 1 year of the current diagnosis); negative voiding cytology for high-grade disease at or within 6 weeks of enrollment; and willing to use 2 acceptable forms of effective contraception from enrollment through 6 months post-treatment if the participant was female or the female partner of a male participant and was of childbearing potential.

Key exclusion criteria were: history of carcinoma in situ on preliminary cystoscopy within 5 years of enrollment; BCG treatment for urethral cancer (UC) within previous 2 years; history of high-grade papillary UC in the past 2 years; known allergy or sensitivity to mitomycin, urethral stricture that would preclude passage of a urethral catheter; history of pelvic radiotherapy; history of (a) neurogenic bladder, (b) active urinary retention, and/or (c) any other condition that would prohibit normal voiding; past or current muscle invasive (i.e. T2, T3, and T4) or metastatic UC or concurrent upper tract UC; participation in a study with an investigational agent or device within 30 days of enrollment; and history of prior treatment with an intravesical chemotherapeutic agent, with the exception of a single dose of chemotherapy immediately after any previous TURBT.

Blood and urine sampling and preparation

Blood samples were collected prior to instillation of UGN-102 (t = 0) and at time points post-instillation as previously described for individual studies. At each time point, 3 mL of blood was collected into K2EDTA tubes and placed immediately in ice, in a closed container protected from light. Samples were centrifuged in a refrigerated centrifuge to separate plasma. The plasma was then frozen at −700C, and shipped to the bioanalytical lab (AIT Bioscsience) for analysis, and assessed for PK parameters. In study BL004, urine was also collected before the 6th instillation at baseline and during the 6-hour period following instillation from all spontaneous urination. Urine samples were collected in Eppendorf tubes and immediately stored at −700C, and shipped to the central lab for analysis. Nothing was added to the samples prior to storage. Plasma or urine was used for analysis.

Pharmacokinetic analyses

Prior to sampling, calibration and quality control (QC) samples were removed from the freezer, thawed at room temperature and then vortex-mixed for 10 s. A validated bioanalytical method was based on liquid–liquid extraction followed by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry instrumental analysis (LC-MS/MS). It covered a measurement range from 0.100 to 100 ng/mL for mitomycin, using mitomycin C–13C–15N2 as the internal standard. A sample volume of 50.0 µL was aliquoted into a 1.2 mL 96-well plate and mixed with 50.0 µL internal standard solution (1.0 µg/mL mitomycin C-13C-15N2 in PBS (10 mM, pH 7.4)) and 600 µL of ethyl acetate.

The plate was covered and the mixtures were vigorously shaken, vortexed to mix, and centrifuged. A 450 µL aliquot of the organic phase was transferred, evaporated to dryness under nitrogen at 35°C, and reconstituted in 500 µL of [ethanol: ammonium bicarbonate (5.0 mM, pH 7.0)] (10:90). The plate was once again covered, vigorously shaken, vortexed to mix, and centrifuged. A Tomtec Quadra96 liquid handler is then used to transfer 25.0 µL from each well to a final plate containing 600 µL ammonium bicarbonate (5.0 mM, pH 7.0). The column used was Phenomenex Luna 100 × 2 mm, 3 μm, 100A, P.N. 00D-4251-B0. The gradient used consisted of A: acetonitrile/4mM ammonium formate, 5/95 + 0.1% formic acid and B: acetonitrile/4mM ammonium formate, 95/5 + 0.1% formic acid, according to Table 1. Raw mass spectrometer data were acquired using Thermo Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) TSQ Module (v.1.0). Data were processed using Thermo Scientific Watson Laboratory Information System™ (v.7.4 SP4) for regression analysis and computation of sample concentrations.

PK parameters that were assessed included observed maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), time to maximum plasma concentration (Tmax), area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time 0 to 6 h post-instillation (AUC0–6), area under the plasma concentration–time curve extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf), elimination rate constant (λz),terminal half-life (t½), total body clearance for extravascular administration/fraction of dose absorbed (CL/FF); volume of distribution based on the terminal phase (V)/fraction of dose absorbed (F); Vz/F. The data were estimated by non-compartmental PK methods using Phoenix® WinNonlin® version 6.4 (Pharsight, Mountain View, CA).

Urine pharmacokinetics

In BL004, patient urine samples were collected into a tube for up to 6 h after the 6th instillation. Urine volume was measured and mitomycin concentration was determined by LC-MS/MS conducted by AIT Bioscience, Indianapolis, IN, USA. The lower limit of quantitation was 0.250 ng/mL.

Safety and toxicity analyses

The safety of UGN-102 was assessed by the following: evaluation (frequency, seriousness, severity, and type) of adverse events (AEs); changes from baseline in laboratory values and incidence of measurements defined as potentially clinically significant; and clinically meaningful changes in physical examination findings, including vital signs. AEs (recorded from signing the ICF to the end of the study) were coded to a system organ class and preferred term using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, version 21.0. AEs are reported for the overall populations of BL004 and BL005. Laboratory assessments included complete blood count, blood coagulation, liver and renal function tests, urinalysis, and urine culture.

The mean total systemic exposure to mitomycin up to 6 h post-instillation (AUC0-6) was 56.23 ng·h/mL (range 8.31–139.62) (Table 3). The mean apparent t1/2 following intravesical instillation of UGN-102 was 0.82 h (49 min), which is longer than the t1/2 of mitomycin following a 30 mg IV bolus injection (mean t1/2 value of approximately 17 min) [14]. The apparent t1/2 demonstrates that UGN-102 most-likely dissolved gradually, resulting in prolonged exposure of mitomycin following intravesical instillation. The VZ/F observed was relatively high at 5357.12 L (standard deviation [SD]: 7183.62), indicating that mitomycin present in the plasma was rapidly cleared, and the clearance was 4486.84 L/hour (SD 5413.10).

The maximum measured urine concentration was 635 µg/L, and at 4–6 h post intravesical instillation, mitomycin concentrations in urine were either below the level of quantification (< 0.250 µg/mL) or approaching the level of quantification in 5 out of 6 patients. In the remaining patient, the measurable urine concentrations over the 6-hour period suggest that the dwell time of this patient was longer than 6 h post-instillation. Evaluation of the patient’s medical history did not explain this; however, the patient’s large tumor size near the bladder neck area may have potentially resulted in residual urine after each micturition and led to the apparent prolonged dwell time in this patient. Despite the prolonged urine exposure, there was minimal systemic exposure, with plasma mitomycin concentrations ≤ 4 ng/mL up to 6 h post-instillation in this patient, 100-fold lower than the mitomycin plasma concentration known to be associated with myelosuppression (400 ng/mL) [15].

In summary, available urine mitomycin data support the observed minimal systemic exposure to mitomycin in patients with NMIBC who were intravesically instilled with UGN-102 (120 mg mitomycin). All patients were exposed to minimal or no systemic mitomycin levels at 6 h post-instillation.

Instillation of UGN-102 into the bladder resulted in low systemic exposure to mitomycin while achieving prolonged bladder exposure. Analysis of mitomycin plasma concentration versus time profiles in this subset of 6 patients showed that at 6 h post-instillation, systemic exposure was limited, and the mean Cmax was well below the concentration known to be associated with myelosuppression.

Discussion

PK data generated in 2 studies of different doses of UGN-102 administered intravesically in patients with NMIBC demonstrate that intravesical instillation of UGN-102 results in low systemic absorption of mitomycin. The observed Cmax values were considerably lower than those following IV administration. Mean Cmax (range) was 20.4 ng/mL (4.00–40.40) at the highest dose level tested (120 mg in study BL004). The highest observed individual Cmax value of 40.40 ng/mL was 59- and 13-fold lower than the Cmax after IV injection of 30 or 10 mg mitomycin (2,400 and 520 ng/mL, respectively) [10] and 10-fold lower than the mitomycin plasma concentration known to be associated with myelosuppression (400 ng/mL) [15]. In addition, the volume of distribution and clearance rates observed in BL004 indicate a low bioavailability, which is in line with the PK of mitomycin administered in an IV form [14]. The variability observed in the PK parameters collected may have been due to the small sample size of the studies. In both studies, 1 of the 6 patients had higher exposure to mitomycin (although still below the level needed for myelosuppression), in BL004 this was attributed to possible longer dwell time due to the tumor location restricting urine flow, in BL005 the cause was unknown.

Previous studies have shown that with IV and aqueous intravesical administration, mitomycin is cleared from the circulation with a t1/2 range of 23–78 min (IV dose range 5–60 mg/m2, intravesical dose 20–40 mg) [16–20]. Renal or hepatic impairment appears to have limited effect on mitomycin clearance overall [17–20], and intravesical administration results in negligible systemic exposure [16, 18, 21–23].

Intravesical chemotherapy has a potentially ablative effect for well-selected patients with NMIBC [28], and recent studies have shown promising CR rates for treatment of LG-IR-NMIBC with UGN-102 [24, 25]. Long-term studies are underway to confirm the beneficial effects of the use of UGN-102 as a treatment option in LG-IR-NMIBC.

This study has a number of limitations, including the small sample size, and different patient demographics included in each study. Only 6 patients were included in each study, due to the burden of frequent blood tests. To minimize risk, sites were encouraged to insert a catheter to collect all blood samples where possible. Patients in BL005 were selected randomly, and this could have introduced bias toward healthier patients. Nonetheless, data from these 2 clinical trials demonstrate that intravesical instillation of UGN-102 results in low systemic absorption of mitomycin, with Cmax values considerably lower than those following IV administration, achieved approximately 2 h after instillation.
