Tension pneumothorax and pneumoperitoneum following shoulder arthroscopy in a single-lung patient: A case report
Authors: Byunghyuk Yu, Inhwan Yeo, Changho Kim, Yeonjoo Cho, Jae Yeong Yang, Hyo-Won Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Surgical procedures in patients with severely compromised pulmonary function, particularly those with single-lung physiology following pneumonectomy, pose significant perioperative challenges. Swyer–James syndrome is a rare, acquired lung disease characterized by unilateral hyperlucency and hypoplasia secondary to post-infectious bronchiolitis obliterans [1,2]. In advanced cases, surgical intervention such as pneumonectomy may be required, leaving patients with reduced pulmonary reserve and increased susceptibility to anesthetic complications, barotrauma, and hypoxemia [3,4].

Although shoulder arthroscopy is generally considered a low-risk procedure, it carries specific risks related to patient positioning, positive-pressure ventilation, and fluid extravasation, particularly in high-risk patients [5,6]. The lateral decubitus position, commonly used in shoulder arthroscopy, has been associated with increased rates of pneumothorax during other procedures, such as CT-guided lung biopsies, especially when the dependent lung is absent or compromised [7,8].

We report a rare and life-threatening case of a 57-year-old man with a history of left pneumonectomy for Swyer–James syndrome who developed intraoperative hypoxia progressing to pulseless VT arrest during arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. After successful resuscitation, the cause of the pulseless VT arrest was identified as tension pneumothorax accompanied by massive subcutaneous emphysema, pneumomediastinum, and pneumoperitoneum.

This case underscores the need for heightened perioperative vigilance, individualized ventilatory strategies, and early recognition of tension pneumothorax as a reversible cause of intraoperative cardiac arrest in patients with significant pulmonary comorbidities.

This case report has been reported in line with the 2025 SCARE checklist [9].

Case

Patient profile

A 57-year-old man with a medical history of Swyer–James syndrome, diagnosed 33 years previously, underwent left pneumonectomy 28 years prior because of a lung abscess.

Clinical history

One week before surgery, the patient presented to a local orthopaedic hospital with complaints of right shoulder pain persisting for 1 week. He reported difficulty lifting the right arm following a fall down the stairs. Given his history of left pneumonectomy, preoperative chest radiography was performed and revealed a normal right lung and airway (Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed a full-thickness tear of the subscapularis tendon and a mid-substance full-thickness tear of the supraspinatus tendon (Fig. 2A, B). Surgical intervention and postoperative complications

Anesthesia was induced using remimazolam 6 mg and rocuronium 50 mg. A continuous interscalene nerve block was administered. Mechanical ventilation was maintained using volume-controlled settings with a tidal volume of 300 mL (5 mL/kg), respiratory rate of 15 breaths/min, peak airway pressure of 12–15 cm H₂O, and end-tidal carbon dioxide levels of 34–35 mmHg. Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) was not used.

The patient underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair under general anesthesia in the left lateral decubitus position. Shoulder arthroscopy was performed using the standard four-portal approach (anterior, posterior, mid-lateral, and anterolateral portals). An intermittent fluid infusion pump system was used for fluid management. This system intermittently infused fluid into the glenohumeral joint and subacromial bursa space at a pressure of 60 cmH₂O. The volume of infused fluid was balanced by simultaneous suction to maintain constant intra-articular and subacromial pressures throughout the procedure. The surgical techniques included double-row repair of the subscapularis tendon and single-row repair of the supraspinatus tendon, supplemented with side-to-side sutures (Fig. 2C, D). Intraoperatively, the vital signs of the patient were stable: systolic blood pressure (SBP) 90–110 mmHg; diastolic blood pressure (DBP) 50–60 mmHg; mean blood pressure 70–73 mmHg; heart rate, 60–80 beats/min; oxygen saturation (SpO₂), 100 %; and bispectral index, 40–50.

Approximately 90 min after the induction of general anesthesia, near the end of the procedure, the patient developed hypoxia with a drop in SpO₂ to 85 %. Manual ventilation was initiated using an Ambu bag. After surgery and repositioning to the supine position, the patient developed pulseless ventricular tachycardia. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), including defibrillation, was performed for 25 min, resulting in the return of spontaneous circulation. The patient was transferred to our hospital under manual bag ventilation without regaining consciousness.

Initial assessment, radiologic findings, and laboratory results

Upon arrival at our hospital, the patient exhibited seizure-like activity and was administered lorazepam 2 mg twice. Vital signs were as follows: SBP 106 mmHg; DBP, 74 mmHg; heart rate, 105 bpm; and SpO₂, 100 %. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 5. Chest radiography revealed a right-sided pneumothorax and extensive subcutaneous emphysema (Fig. 3). An arrow catheter was inserted in the right chest by an emergency physician. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed diffuse pneumomediastinum and marked subcutaneous emphysema involving the entire chest and trunk (Fig. 4A, B). Abdominal CT revealed widespread subcutaneous and intramuscular emphysema extending into the abdominopelvic region, with right periureteric infiltration. The patient also had extensive pneumoperitoneum, which is an unreported complication of shoulder arthroscopy (Fig. 4C and D). Initial laboratory tests showed marked leukocytosis with a white blood cell count of 39,710/μL. Hemoglobin and platelet counts were within normal limits at 15.3 g/dL and 255,000/μL, respectively. The liver function test results were elevated, with aspartate aminotransferase at 193 U/L and alanine aminotransferase at 212 U/L. Serum creatinine was mildly elevated at 1.33 mg/dL and blood glucose was significantly elevated at 265 mg/dL. Pancreatic enzymes revealed a markedly elevated serum amylase level of 500 U/L, whereas lipase levels were within the normal range at 37 U/L. Arterial blood gas analysis indicated acidemia with a pH of 7.203, hypercapnia with a partial pressure of carbon dioxide of 57.5 mmHg, and adequate oxygenation (partial pressure of oxygen 124 mmHg, oxygen saturation of the arterial blood 98 %). The serum lactate level was slightly elevated to 1.93 mmol/L, thus suggesting mild tissue hypoperfusion.

ICU management and recovery

Sedation was initially induced with midazolam and fentanyl, and mechanical ventilation was initiated. Due to the comatose state of the patient, a target temperature management (TTM) of 34 °C was implemented. Sedation was later changed to remifentanil and dexmedetomidine, and a low-dose norepinephrine infusion was administered to maintain a mean arterial pressure > 65 mmHg. On the day after surgery, the patient exhibited spontaneous movements in the morning and showed partial verbal responsiveness. Based on these neurological improvements, the TTM was discontinued. The patient was successfully extubated and switched to nasal oxygen therapy. His level of consciousness continued to improve throughout the day, with a GCS score of 15 and a Richmond agitation–sedation scale score of 0. No evidence of neurological complications was found, and the multiorgan dysfunction continued to improve. The arrow catheter placed to manage the right pneumothorax was left to drain passively without applying negative pressure (natural drainage only).

Follow-up chest and abdominal CT scans were performed 5 days after surgery (Fig. 5). The images show marked resolution of the previously observed right pneumothorax, pneumoperitoneum, pneumomediastinum, and diffuse subcutaneous and intramuscular emphysema. The patient reported no recollection of the cardiac arrest episode and did not report any discomfort other than chest pain attributable to chest compressions during CPR. He was transferred back to the referring hospital on 6 days after surgery for wound care and rehabilitation therapy and was subsequently discharged 14 days after surgery without any neurologic sequelae.

Discussion

This case highlights a rare but critical complication of arthroscopic rotator cuff repair: intraoperative cardiac arrest due to tension pneumothorax presenting with ventricular tachycardia (VT). Tension pneumothorax leads to rapidly increasing intrathoracic pressure, obstructed venous return, decreased cardiac output, and severe hypoxemia, which can precipitate malignant arrhythmias such as VT [3,4,10]. Under general anesthesia, classic signs may be absent, and early indicators such as hypotension, hypoxemia, or arrhythmias may be nonspecific, delaying recognition [5,10]. Therefore, the possibility of pneumothorax must be considered when unexplained oxygen desaturation or hemodynamic instability occurs during shoulder arthroscopy [10].

A notable feature of this case is that ROSC was achieved after CPR, with imaging revealing extensive subcutaneous emphysema, pneumomediastinum, and pneumoperitoneum. We hypothesize that the increased intrathoracic pressures during CPR allowed air to dissect into extrathoracic and extraperitoneal spaces, transiently relieving the tension physiology and restoring venous return [4,11]. While this redistribution of air may reduce intrathoracic pressure sufficiently to allow ROSC, it does not definitively resolve the underlying pathology. This partial decompression can complicate clinical assessment by masking ongoing intrathoracic pathology and delaying definitive diagnosis and treatment [4,11].

Several risk factors in this patient may have contributed to the development of tension pneumothorax. First, the patient had undergone a left pneumonectomy for Swyer–James syndrome, resulting in single-lung physiology. In such patients, the remaining lung is at increased risk of barotrauma during mechanical ventilation. Lung-protective strategies—tidal volumes of 4–6 mL/kg predicted body weight and PEEP of 5–8 cmH₂O with driving pressures <15 cmH₂O—are recommended to minimize ventilator-induced lung injury [12]. Despite using these recommended settings, tension pneumothorax developed in this case, suggesting that even standard protective ventilation may be insufficient in single-lung patients and that individualized ventilator strategies should be considered [12]. Also, along with protective lung ventilation, intensive intraoperative monitoring should consist of auscultation and chest expansion assessment, use of lung ultrasound to detect atelectasis or pneumothorax, and continuous capnography and pulse oximetry to detect early gas-exchange abnormalities.

Second, the patient was positioned in the left lateral decubitus position, placing the remaining right lung in the non-dependent (upper) position. This positioning is known to increase pneumothorax risk during mechanical ventilation, with higher rates observed during CT-guided lung biopsies in the non-dependent lung [8]. Furthermore, mechanical ventilation in the lateral decubitus position has been reported to increase pneumothorax risk in patients with single-lung physiology [7]. The combination of prior pneumonectomy, single-lung physiology, and surgical positioning likely increased the risk of tension pneumothorax in this case, underscoring the importance of thorough preoperative evaluation and tailored intraoperative precautions. Therefore, intraoperatively, maintain neutral head-and-neck alignment in the beach-chair or lateral decubitus position, ensuring adequate padding under the torso to prevent focal rib or pleural compression, and apply only the minimal distraction force needed for visualization [13].

Additionally, the observed pneumomediastinum and pneumoperitoneum may have been secondary to tension pneumothorax or related to arthroscopic insufflation. There was no evidence of tension pneumoperitoneum or visceral perforation contributing to cardiac arrest. However, case reports have described diaphragmatic air migration from tension pneumothorax into the peritoneal cavity, acting as a decompressive mechanism and transiently improving hemodynamics [11]. This decompression mechanism occurs through two main pathways: Anatomically, excess air within the pleural cavity can enter the abdominal cavity through natural openings in the diaphragm, such as the esophageal hiatus, aortic hiatus, or caval opening. Additionally, air can descend along the mediastinal connective tissue and diffuse into the abdominal cavity through the soft tissues beneath the diaphragm [14]. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for tension pneumothorax as a reversible cause of cardiac arrest, employing vigilant monitoring with chest auscultation, airway pressure tracking, and point-of-care ultrasound for early diagnosis. Definitive management with needle decompression or chest tube placement remains essential to prevent recurrence and improve outcomes [3,5,6].

Unlike the present case, which was characterized by intraoperative cardiac arrest due to tension pneumothorax, previously reported cases of pneumothorax following shoulder arthroscopy have generally occurred in otherwise healthy individuals without significant pulmonary comorbidities and have presented with only mild to moderate symptoms requiring standard chest tube drainage. A comprehensive literature review by Tan et al. analyzed 20 cases of pneumothorax after shoulder arthroscopy, but no findings in that review were comparable to, or predictive of, the circumstances observed in our case. [15].

Importantly, our search of PubMed and other databases yielded no previously reported cases resembling the present one. Specifically, none described intraoperative cardiac arrest secondary to tension pneumothorax during arthroscopy. Furthermore, no cases have been reported of contralateral pneumothorax occurring during extra-thoracic or thoracic surgery in single-lung patients who had undergone pneumonectomy; most previously reported cases were spontaneous [16]. This striking absence underscores the exceptional rarity of our case and highlights the need for heightened vigilance in patients with altered pulmonary physiology undergoing shoulder arthroscopy.

Conclusions

This case illustrates that even procedures generally considered low-risk, such as arthroscopic shoulder surgery, can result in life-threatening complications including tension pneumothorax leading to cardiac arrest with pulseless VT, particularly in patients with significant pulmonary comorbidities. When multiple risk factors—such as prior pneumonectomy, single-lung physiology, lateral decubitus positioning, and mechanical ventilation—coexist, the risk of severe complications increases markedly. Therefore, thorough preoperative assessment, individualized ventilatory strategies, and proactive intraoperative planning are essential. In these high-risk patients, a sudden decrease in SpO₂ or hemodynamic instability should immediately prompt evaluation for tension pneumothorax, and timely decompression should be considered to prevent VT, cardiac arrest, and improve outcomes.
