The Natural Quassinoid Brusatol Enhances Venetoclax Efficacy and Overcomes Resistance in Myeloid Leukemias
Authors: Shin‐ichiro Kawaguchi, Kazuya Sato, Junko Izawa, Norihito Takayama, Hiroko Hayakawa, Ken Suzaki, Susumu Goyama, Chiyoko Kato, Eiji Sakashita, Kaoru Tominaga, Hitoshi Endo, Yoshinobu Kanda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile venetoclax‐based combinations have shown promising results in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), the remission duration is generally short, warranting strategies to further improve efficacy and overcome resistance. Here, we show that the natural quassinoid brusatol induces cell‐cycle arrest and apoptosis in multiple AML cell lines while enhancing venetoclax efficacy irrespective of inherent or acquired resistance. Mechanistically, brusatol increased p53 protein expression, leading to upregulation of its target genes/proteins, including CDKN1A (p21) and BBC3 (PUMA). Genetic deletion of TP53 attenuated brusatol‐induced apoptosis and its synergy with venetoclax, supporting p53 activation as a central mechanism underlying the anti‐leukemia response. Furthermore, the combination synergistically decreased mitochondrial membrane potential and respiratory activity, causing accumulation of reactive oxygen species in AML cells. Although brusatol and venetoclax exhibited limited effects individually, their combination markedly reduced leukemia burden and significantly prolonged survival in three independent cell line‐derived xenograft models, including venetoclax‐resistant and ‐refractory models. Notably, brusatol increased normal leukocyte and platelet counts while reducing leukemic infiltration in both bone marrow and extramedullary sites. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the synergistic effects of the brusatol‐venetoclax combination, supporting further evaluation of this therapeutic strategy in myeloid leukemias.Keywords: acute myeloid leukemia, brusatol, CDKN1A, mitochondria, p53, venetoclax

Introduction

Since the introduction of the selective BCL‐2 inhibitor venetoclax into clinical practice, therapeutic strategies for acute myeloid leukemia (AML) have been markedly transformed, particularly for elderly patients or those ineligible for intensive chemotherapy. Venetoclax shows only modest activity in AML when used as a single agent, achieving complete response (CR) or CR with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi) rates of 19% [1]. However, when combined with hypomethylating agents or low‐dose cytarabine, venetoclax has demonstrated substantially improved efficacy, with CR/CRi rates of 61% and 54%, respectively [2, 3]. Despite these advances, several challenges remain, such as limited remission duration, severe and sometimes prolonged myelosuppression, and poor responses in patients harboring certain genetic mutations [4]. To address these limitations, efforts are underway to optimize venetoclax dosing and to develop more effective combination strategies.

Natural quassinoids have been shown to possess a broad range of biological activities, with increasing attention to their potential applications in cancer treatment. Among these, brusatol exerts potent anti‐tumor activities against various types of malignancies while also overcoming resistance and potentiating the efficacy of other anticancer agents and radiotherapy [5]. However, the precise mechanism by which brusatol induces cell death remains unclear and may vary depending on cell types. One potential target of brusatol is antioxidant transcription factor NF‐E2 related factor‐2 (NRF2) pathway [6, 7], with its inactivation leading to reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction [5, 8, 9]. Other cell signaling pathways may also be involved in the anti‐tumor effect of brusatol, such as the PI3K/AKT/mTOR [10, 11, 12], ERK/JNK/p38 MAPK [12, 13], and NF‐κB [13, 14]. Nevertheless, most of the current evidence stems from research on solid tumors, with only limited data available for myeloid malignancies.

In the present study, we demonstrate that brusatol induces apoptosis and sensitizes myeloid leukemias to venetoclax, irrespective of inherent or acquired resistance, both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, translational activation of p53 and its downstream targets, including p21 and PUMA, plays a crucial role in brusatol‐induced apoptosis. Furthermore, brusatol in combination with venetoclax synergistically induces mitochondrial dysfunction in AML cells, including mitochondrial depolarization, ROS accumulation, and reduced oxidative phosphorylation.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Human AML cell lines used in this study are listed in Table S1. Generation of venetoclax‐resistant cell lines was described elsewhere [15]. Briefly, MOLM‐14 cells were intermittently cultured in increasing concentrations of venetoclax for short intervals, with the medium being replaced with fresh medium to allow the cells to recover. Finally, we generated venetoclax‐resistant (VEN‐R) MOLM‐14 cells that were stably viable in the medium containing 4 μM venetoclax (Figure S1A). Consistent with previous reports [16, 17], VEN‐R cells exhibited elevated MCL‐1 and MYC protein levels, whereas BCL‐2 expression remained unchanged relative to the parental MOLM‐14 cells (Figure S1B).

Cell Proliferation Assay

The median inhibitory concentration (IC50) of the drugs tested in AML cell lines was determined using the Premix WST‐1 Cell Proliferation Assay System (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). Briefly, AML cell lines (4.0 × 104 cells in 100 μL medium) were seeded in 96‐well plates in quintuplicate and cultured with various concentrations of drugs for 24 h. During the last 4 h, 10 μL of Premix WST‐1 reagent was added to each well (1:10 final dilution), and absorbance at 440 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer (Spark 10M, Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). Combination effects and synergy scores were analyzed using the SynergyFinder web application (https://synergyfinder.org/) [18].

Flow Cytometry

Reagents for mitochondrial analysis, including CellROX Deep Red, MitoTracker Red CMXRos, and MitoTracker Green, were purchased from Thermo Fisher. Cell‐cycle status and DNA double‐strand breaks were simultaneously assessed using DAPI and a phospho‐histone H2AX (Ser139) monoclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher) [19]. All events were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC).

Western Blot Analysis

Cells were lysed with M‐PER mammalian protein extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with cOmplete protease inhibitor and PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche). Samples were separated using polyacrylamide gels and transferred using the iBlot dry transfer system (Thermo Fisher). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST and then incubated with primary antibodies (Table S2) overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with the corresponding HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The blots were imaged on the GeneGnome chemiluminescence imaging system (Syngene International Ltd.) after incubation with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher).

CRISPR/Cas9 Genome Editing

NRF2 and TP53 were knocked out by CRISPR/Cas9 technology using lentiviral vectors coding for gene‐specific single‐guide RNAs, hCas9, and puromycin resistance gene (Table S3). Scrambled gRNA lentiviral vector with no known target in the human genome was used as a reference. Stably transfected cells were selected by 5 μg/mL puromycin.

Cell Line‐Derived Xenograft Mouse Models

Female NOD/Shi‐scid‐IL2rγnull (NOG) mice were purchased from In Vivo Science Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) and housed in the animal facility of our institute. Mice were allowed to acclimate to the facility for at least a week and used at ages 7–9 weeks. To establish cell line‐derived xenograft (CDX) models, NOG mice were given 250 cGy of total body irradiation using a gamma irradiator with a Cesium137 source, followed by intravenous injection of 0.2 million AML cells after an interval of 4 to 24 h.

Statistical Analysis

Two‐tailed unpaired t‐tests or one‐way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni's post hoc comparisons tests were performed using Prism (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) or EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), the latter of which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) [20]. More precisely, it is a modified version of R Commander designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics.

Results

Brusatol Exhibits Potent Anti‐Leukemic Activity Across Human AML Cell Lines

We first investigated the anti‐leukemic effects of brusatol in seven distinct human AML cell lines treated with increasing concentrations of the compound for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the WST‐1 assay. As shown in Figure 1A–C, brusatol inhibited cell proliferation in a dose‐dependent manner across multiple AML cell lines. MOLM‐14 cells were the most sensitive, followed by MV4‐11 and OCI‐AML3, whereas HL60 and KG‐1a showed intermediate sensitivity. THP‐1 and U937 were comparatively less sensitive but still showed IC50 values below 100 nM. Sensitivity correlated with TP53 status, with higher efficacy in TP53 wild‐type (TP53 wt) cells (Table S1, Figure 1D).

Despite their different sensitivities, Annexin V positivity and caspase‐3/7 activity significantly increased in both TP53 wt MOLM‐14 and TP53 null (TP53 null) U937 cells in a dose‐dependent manner upon brusatol treatment (Figure 1E,F, and Figure S2A,B). Cell viability was largely recovered by a pan‐caspase inhibitor, Z‐VAD‐fmk, suggesting that apoptosis is the predominant form of brusatol‐induced cell death in leukemia cells (Figure 1G). Supporting these observations, nuclear fragmentation and chromatin aggregation became more prominent with increasing concentrations of brusatol (Figure 1I). Furthermore, colony‐forming assays demonstrated that brusatol led to a pronounced reduction in both colony number and size in MOLM‐14 cells (Figure 1H,J, and Supporting Information Doc S1). Importantly, brusatol had a lesser effect on the colony‐forming capacity of human CD34+ hematopoietic progenitors: whereas almost no colony formation was observed in MOLM‐14 cells at a concentration of 10 nM, approximately 60% of colony‐forming capacity was preserved in CD34+ cells (Figure S2D,E). Cell cycle analysis revealed that a sublethal dose of brusatol reduced the proportion of cells in the S phase (9.1% ± 1.3% vs. 48.5% ± 5.2%, p < 0.0001), while substantially increasing the G0/G1 population (72.3% ± 1.0% vs. 36.0% ± 4.1%, p < 0.0001) (Figure 1K,L, and Figure S2C). At a higher concentration, cells exhibited a prominent sub‐G1 peak (48.4% ± 4.7% vs. 2.2% ± 0.5%, p < 0.0001), indicating DNA fragmentation associated with apoptosis.

Brusatol Synergistically Induces Cell Death in Combination With Venetoclax in AML Cells

Studies have shown that TP53 status is also a major factor that determines the sensitivity to venetoclax in AML cells [21, 22, 23, 24]. Indeed, we observed that venetoclax dose‐dependently induced cell death in TP53 wt MOLM‐14 cells within the low‐nanomolar concentration range (Figure 2A). Although 2.5 nM brusatol alone did not induce cell death in MOLM‐14 cells (Figure 1A,E), its combination with venetoclax markedly enhanced cytotoxicity, reducing the IC50 from 16.8 to 2.4 nM. Consistent with this, apoptotic morphology was more frequently observed with the combination treatment than with either agent alone (Figure 2B). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that the combination decreased leukemia cell viability (Figure 2C), accompanied by increased Annexin V positivity (Figure 2D) and caspase‐3/7 activation (Figure 2E).

Conversely, TP53 null U937 cells were highly refractory to venetoclax treatment as expected (Figure 2F). However, co‐treatment with 20 nM brusatol restored venetoclax sensitivity, reducing the IC50 from undetectable levels to 13.8 nM. Synergistic effects of the drug combination were confirmed using both the Highest Single Agent and Bliss independence models (Figure 2G). Similar results were observed in MOLM‐14‐derived venetoclax‐resistant (VEN‐R) cells, which had been established through prolonged drug exposure. Notably, the addition of 2.5 nM brusatol, the same concentration used for TP53 wt MOLM‐14, also restored venetoclax sensitivity of VEN‐R MOLM‐14 cells (Figure 2H). Furthermore, brusatol enhanced the cytotoxicity of S55746, another BCL‐2‐selective inhibitor, in MOLM‐14 cells (Figure 2I), suggesting that this synergy is mediated, at least in part, through the inhibition of BCL‐2. Colony‐forming assays also revealed that the combination treatment markedly suppressed the clonogenic growth of MOLM‐14 cells compared with each single agent (Figure 2J,K), suggesting sustained cytotoxicity and impaired self‐renewal.

Brusatol Upregulates p53‐Target Genes in AML Cells

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the anti‐leukemia effects of brusatol and its synergy with venetoclax, RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq) was performed in MOLM‐14 cells. Cells were treated for 24 h with DMSO (control), venetoclax, brusatol, or the combination, followed by transcriptomic analysis (Supporting Information Doc S1). Principal component analysis revealed that the venetoclax group clustered closely with the control group, likely due to the minimally effective concentration of venetoclax used in this experiment, whereas the brusatol and combination groups exhibited distinct transcriptional profiles (Figure 3A). In total, 278 genes were differentially expressed between brusatol‐treated and control groups, of which 212 were upregulated and 66 were downregulated (significance cut‐off: |log2 fold change| > 1, adjusted p ≤ 0.05) (Tables S4 and S5). Venetoclax treatment led to the downregulation of 34 genes, whereas only one gene was upregulated compared with control (Table S6). Across all six pairwise comparisons, a total of 735 unique DEGs were identified (Tables [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link]), and these were used for downstream visualization and clustering analyses. A heatmap of these DEGs is shown in Figure 3B, and the genes were classified into 11 distinct clusters based on their expression patterns (Figure 3C, Tables S12 and S13).

Notably, there was a stark contrast in cluster profiles between the brusatol and venetoclax groups (Figure 3B,D,E). The majority of DEGs (436 genes, 59.3%) were classified into Cluster 1, characterized by increased expression with brusatol but reduced expression with venetoclax. Among these, several genes associated with p53‐dependent apoptosis, including BBC3, CDKN1A, MDM2, TP53INP1, and TP53I3, were upregulated, whereas TP53 itself showed no significant change in the brusatol group (Figure 3D). Another p53 target, TNFRSF10B, which encodes the extrinsic apoptosis receptor DR5, was also highly upregulated in the brusatol group. Meanwhile, the cell‐cycle promoter CCND1 was downregulated, consistent with the cell‐cycle analysis results showing G0/G1 arrest following brusatol treatment (Figure 1K,L).

p53 Activation Is a Central Mechanism Underlying the Anti‐Leukemia Response of Brusatol and Its Synergism With Venetoclax

Consistent with the NGS results, time‐course analysis revealed that BBC3, CDKN1A, MDM2, TP53INP1, and TP53I3 were upregulated as early as 4 h and remained elevated for 24 h following brusatol treatment (Figure 4A, left). Immunoblotting showed increased expression of p21, PUMA, and MDM2 between 8 and 24 h after brusatol treatment in TP53 wt AML cell lines, including MOLM‐14, VEN‐R MOLM‐14, and OCI‐AML3 cells (Figure 4A). Notably, p53 protein levels were also elevated in these TP53 wt AML cell lines upon brusatol treatment, whereas TP53 mRNA expression remained unchanged (Figure 4A, right). Levels of phosphorylated γ‐H2AX, a sensitive marker of DNA double‐strand breaks, were not altered by brusatol treatment, suggesting that p53 activation does not result from DNA damage (Figure 4C). Neither global protein translation nor p53‐MDM2 interaction, assessed by puromycin incorporation and BRET assay, respectively, was impaired by brusatol (Figure S3A–C and Supporting Information Doc S1). Canonical p53‐activating pathways, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR and ERK/JNK/p38 MAPK, were also not altered at either the phosphorylation or total protein level (Figure S3D).

Although venetoclax treatment slightly reduced the expression of p53 and its target proteins p21 and PUMA, their expression levels increased substantially when combined with brusatol (Figure 4D). To further examine the role of p53 activation in brusatol activity, we generated TP53‐knockout MOLM‐14 cells using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing (Figure S3E). Genetic knockout of TP53 attenuated the anti‐leukemia effects of brusatol (Figure 4E) and its synergistic interaction with venetoclax (Figure 4F), supporting the notion that p53 activation constitutes a major mechanism underlying brusatol‐induced apoptosis.

Brusatol Promotes Oxidative Stress and Mitochondrial Damage Through NRF2‐Independent Mechanisms

Deletion of TP53 reduced the brusatol‐induced apoptosis but did not fully restore cell viability to control levels. Additionally, brusatol induced apoptosis in AML cells with TP53 deletions, albeit to a lesser extent than in TP53 wt AML cell lines, suggesting that brusatol can also exert anti‐leukemic effects through a p53‐independent mechanism. Given that brusatol has been shown to induce cell death through oxidative stress in cancers [6, 7], we next examined its effects on mitochondrial function and ROS accumulation in leukemia cells.

To assess mitochondrial function, MOLM‐14 cells were stained with two mitochondrial probes: MitoTracker Green, a mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)–insensitive dye that reflects mitochondrial mass, and MitoTracker Red, an MMP–sensitive dye that labels functionally active mitochondria (Figure 5A). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that brusatol or venetoclax alone modestly reduced MMP, whereas their combination significantly increased the proportion of depolarized cells compared with either agent (Figure 5B,C). Similarly, while brusatol or venetoclax each increased cellular ROS levels, their combination further enhanced this effect (Figure 5D–F). Seahorse analysis demonstrated that brusatol reduced both basal and maximal OCR in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 5G–I, Supporting Information Doc S1). Single‐agent venetoclax had no effect on mitochondrial respiration at this concentration; however, its combination with brusatol significantly decreased these mitochondrial respiration parameters. Antioxidants, including N‐acetyl cysteine (NAC) or glutathione, did not significantly reduce either ROS levels or apoptosis induced by brusatol (Figure S4A). Cellular NRF2 levels were decreased upon brusatol treatment, consistent with previous reports [6, 7]; however, NRF2‐knockout MOLM‐14 cells exhibited a similar sensitivity to brusatol as their wild‐type counterparts (Figure S4B,C). These observations suggest that ROS accumulation is not caused by dysfunction of antioxidant defense systems, but rather by impaired mitochondrial integrity.

The Combination of Brusatol and Venetoclax Demonstrates Potent Anti‐Leukemic Activity in Human AML CDX Models

With the promising findings obtained from our in vitro experiments, we next evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of the combination of brusatol and venetoclax in different AML CDX models. The experimental design and treatment schedule are shown in Figure 6A. To clearly assess the clinical relevance of the combination therapy, we deliberately employed a suboptimal dosing schedule of venetoclax. Compared with continuous daily administration, this intermittent schedule led to only a modest extension of survival that did not reach statistical significance in the MOLM‐14 xenograft model (Figure 6B and Figure S5A,B). Notably, brusatol significantly prolonged survival as a single agent (median survival: 24.5 days vs. 31 days, respectively), whereas the combination therapy more than doubled survival duration, with a median survival of 51 days (Figure 6B). Bioluminescence imaging demonstrated a slight reduction in leukemia burden in the venetoclax group, whereas almost no luminescence was observed in the mice treated with brusatol or its combination therapy (Figure 6C). Compared with MOLM‐14, the U937 xenograft model demonstrated a more aggressive disease course (Figure 6D). Neither venetoclax nor brusatol prolonged survival, and all mice died before the completion of treatment. However, venetoclax and brusatol combination still significantly improved survival. Furthermore, this combination also exhibited in vivo activity in the VEN‐R xenograft model, with median survival extended from 25 days to 34.5 days (Figure 6E). Reduction in leukemia burden was confirmed by a substantial decrease in spleen size (Figure 6F) and histological examination (Figure 6G and Figure S5C) on day 21 post‐transplantation.

Leukemia cells were almost completely cleared from the peripheral blood and significantly reduced in the spleen and bone marrow in the brusatol and its combination groups (Figure 7A–F). Strikingly, the combination resulted in a > 2‐log reduction in leukemia cells in the spleen and bone marrow compared with brusatol alone (Figure 7G,H), suggesting that a deeper therapeutic response may contribute to the sustained disease control, thereby leading to prolonged survival. Annexin V positivity in leukemia cells was significantly increased in the brusatol and its combination groups, consistent with the observations from our in vitro experiments (Figure 7I). Of note, brusatol monotherapy even improved leukocytopenia and thrombocytopenia observed in the control and venetoclax groups (Figure 7J–L). Furthermore, its combination with venetoclax did not result in additional cytopenia, suggesting that brusatol has a minimal impact on normal hematopoiesis while effectively eliminating leukemia cells.

Discussion

While chronic lymphocytic leukemia is almost entirely dependent on BCL‐2 for survival and can therefore achieve complete remission readily with BH3 mimetics, AML is generally less susceptible to BCL‐2 inhibition due to its moderate and variable BCL‐2 expression and relatively higher levels of MCL‐1 [25]. Nevertheless, cells suffer sublethal injury caused by mitochondrial depolarization and impaired energy production upon BCL‐2 inhibition, thereby increasing their susceptibility to additional stressors. This provides a rationale for combination therapy to maximize apoptotic cell death [25]. Our study demonstrates that the combination of brusatol and venetoclax synergistically induces apoptosis in AML cells, leading to a marked reduction in leukemia burden and prolonged survival in multiple mouse models. Notably, synergistic effects were also observed in AML cells with inherent or acquired resistance to venetoclax. Our series of experiments identified two major mechanisms underlying the synergistic effect: one is activation of the p53‐dependent apoptotic pathway, and the other is mitochondrial dysfunction (Figure 8).

Schematic illustration of brusatol‐induced apoptosis and enhancement of venetoclax efficacy.

Given that BCL‐2 family proteins and p53 signaling serve as central nodes of apoptotic pathways in cancers [26, 27, 28], enhancing p53 activity is a rational strategy to improve the efficacy of and overcome resistance to BH3 mimetics. Although TP53 gene mutations are observed in only ~8% of de novo AML cases, p53 function is nevertheless frequently suppressed in AML with TP53 wt, primarily due to MDM2/4 overexpression [29, 30]. One recent study analyzing AML patient samples harboring TP53 wt showed that leukemia cells with low‐quartile TP53 expression were far less sensitive to venetoclax [23], supporting the therapeutic relevance of TP53 activation. Similarly, CDKN1A was significantly downregulated in venetoclax‐resistant leukemia cells [31], and even short‐term exposure to venetoclax appeared to suppress the p53/p21 axis in BCL2‐rearranged lymphoma cells, indicating a potential crosstalk between BCL‐2 signaling and the p53 pathway [32].

Accumulating evidence indicates that venetoclax induces apoptosis not only through cytochrome c release and caspase activation but also by impairing mitochondrial metabolism and electron transport chain activity [33, 34, 35]. Loss‐of‐function CRISPR/Cas9 screens have identified TP53 and mitochondrial function as key determinants of venetoclax response in AML, suggesting potential strategies to overcome resistance [23]. One such example supporting this notion is azacitidine: single‐agent azacitidine induces NOXA to prime AML cells for venetoclax‐mediated apoptosis, while its combination with venetoclax disrupts energy metabolism by impairing ETC complex II activity in leukemia stem cells [35, 36]. Consistently, we previously reported that pharmacological inhibition of glutathione sensitizes AML cells to venetoclax by inducing oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction [37]. These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for the combination of brusatol with BH3 mimetics.

Our data demonstrate that brusatol induces mitochondrial dysfunction, as evidenced by mitochondrial depolarization, ROS accumulation, and impaired respiratory activity. Similar to our findings, a recent study demonstrated that idasanutlin, a selective p53‐MDM2 inhibitor, not only functions as a p53 activator but also exhibits anti‐tumor activity in a p53‐independent manner by inducing ROS accumulation [38]. Oxidative stress can lead to p53 activation [39, 40, 41], and conversely, activated p53 can promote ROS accumulation, either directly or indirectly [42, 43, 44], potentially forming a self‐amplifying feedback loop upon brusatol treatment. Given that neither TP53 mRNA expression nor global protein translation was altered, nor was the MDM2–p53 interaction disrupted, p53 stabilization induced by brusatol is likely mediated at the post‐translational level. Candidate upstream regulators involved in p53 activation were extensively investigated; however, the mechanism underlying p53 stabilization could not be identified, representing a limitation of this study. Further studies are warranted to clarify the mechanisms of p53 stabilization, including the potential involvement of ribosomal stress and deubiquitination, as well as AMPK activation triggered by cellular energy depletion.

In summary, our study provides novel evidence that brusatol acts as a p53 activator while simultaneously disrupting mitochondrial integrity, leading to apoptosis in myeloid leukemias. Although no clinical trials have been conducted to date, potent anti‐leukemic effects and minimal impact on normal hematopoiesis in our animal models highlight brusatol as a promising therapeutic candidate, especially when combined with venetoclax. While specific data are lacking, several side effects such as gastrointestinal symptoms and hypotension have been reported in patients receiving bruceantin, a structural analog of brusatol [45]. For future clinical application, optimization of dosing and treatment duration will be required to maximize brusatol efficacy while minimizing toxicity.
