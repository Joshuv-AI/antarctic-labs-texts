INTRODUCTION

Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is a focal epilepsy syndrome that is frequently associated with drug‐resistant seizures and may often require surgical intervention to achieve seizure control. 1 However, the long‐term cognitive consequences of epilepsy surgery, particularly in the domains of verbal and visuospatial memory, remain a subject of ongoing debate. 2 , 3 , 4

Although long‐term cognitive outcomes in patients with epilepsy may vary depending on demographic and disease‐specific factors, seizure control plays a critical role in maintaining cognitive stability, particularly in focal drug‐resistant epilepsies such as TLE. In such cases, cognitive functioning typically remains stable if seizures are well‐controlled through medication or surgery. Moreover, some studies suggest that patients with drug‐responsive mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) may exhibit better‐preserved memory functions compared to those undergoing surgical treatment. 5

In TLE, anterior temporal lobectomy (ATL) offers a more extensive resection, which may improve seizure control compared with amygdalohippocampectomy (SAH). 6 Conversely, ATL can lead to cognitive deficits, particularly in verbal memory. 7 SAH aims to preserve cognition by minimizing neocortical damage while providing effective seizure control. 8 Prior work shows that surgery in the dominant temporal lobe carries a risk of impaired verbal memory, 9 whereas interventions performed in the nondominant hemisphere can preserve verbal memory but impair visuospatial memory. 10 These findings highlight the multifactorial interplay between seizure control, surgical technique, and underlying pathology in determining cognitive outcomes.

The effects of epilepsy and its treatments on memory are central to research in this field. Mesial temporal sclerosis (MTS), a common pathology in TLE, disproportionately affects verbal and visuospatial memory, and preoperative hippocampal volume has been linked to postoperative cognitive decline. 11 Recent studies suggest that mesial TLE may be associated with progressive structural and functional changes over time; however, this notion remains debated and evidence is still inconclusive. Importantly, MTLE may also engage adaptive neuroplastic and compensatory mechanisms that influence postoperative cognitive recovery. 12 , 13 , 14

The aim of this study was to characterize long‐term cognitive outcomes in patients with TLE undergoing different surgical and pharmacological treatment approaches.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Study design and participants

This retrospective longitudinal study investigated the long‐term cognitive outcomes of patients with MTLE who underwent either ATL or SAH. A nonoperated TLE group of age‐ and education‐matched patients with drug‐resistant MTLE and radiologically confirmed MTS was included for comparison.

The final cohort consisted of three groups

ATL group (n = 10): Patients who underwent standard ATL, including resection of the hippocampus and amygdala.

SAH group (n = 26): Patients who received selective resection of the amygdala and hippocampus via a transsylvian approach, sparing lateral neocortical regions.

Nonoperated TLE group (n = 12): Patients with drug‐resistant MTLE and MTS who did not undergo surgical intervention.

All participants underwent standardized neuropsychological evaluations at baseline (either preoperative or initial) and after a minimum follow‐up period of 10 years. Demographic variables, epilepsy duration, seizure outcomes (Engel classification), and lateralization of the epileptogenic focus were recorded for all subjects.

Neuropsychological assessment

Verbal memory was assessed using the Turkish version of the Verbal Memory Processes (Sözel Bellek Süreçleri Testi; SBST), 15 which evaluates total learning capacity across 10 acquisition trials (a 15‐word list) followed by delayed recall, free delayed recall, and recognition tasks, all administered 30 min after the initial presentation. The SBST provides indices for total learning and long‐term retention.

Nonverbal memory was evaluated using the visual reproduction subtest of the Turkish‐adapted Wechsler memory scale (WMS), which includes both immediate and delayed recall components, assessing visuospatial memory performance. 16

Neuropsychological results are presented as raw test scores. Because the primary aim was to evaluate longitudinal within‐subject change, analyses focused on modeling change over time rather than cross‐sectional normative comparisons.

Follow‐up timeline

Neuropsychological evaluations were conducted at two time points: baseline and long‐term follow‐up. For operated patients (ATL and SAH groups), baseline refers to the presurgical neuropsychological assessment performed as part of the standard preoperative work‐up, typically within 3–6 months prior to surgery. For the nonoperated TLE group, baseline corresponds to the initial neuropsychological evaluation conducted during surgical candidacy assessment.

The follow‐up evaluation was conducted approximately 10 years after the baseline assessment in all groups. All participants included in the final analyses had complete neuropsychological data at both time points; therefore, the sample size remained stable across baseline and follow‐up assessments (ATL n = 10, SAH n = 26, nonoperated TLE n = 12).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) for descriptive statistics. Inferential linear mixed‐effects model (LMM) analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.3 using the lme4 and lmerTest packages. Within‐group paired comparisons were conducted descriptively to illustrate pre‐to‐post changes within each treatment group and were not used for inferential conclusions. Post hoc contrasts following significant LMMs results were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction.

LMMs were fitted using the lme4 package in R with restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML). Each model included time (PRE, POST), group (ATL, SAH, nonoperated TLE), and their interaction (time × group) as fixed effects, with subject ID specified as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements within individuals. Fixed effects were evaluated using type III tests with Satterthwaite's approximation for degrees of freedom as implemented in the lmerTest package. When a significant time × group interaction was observed, estimated marginal means were obtained using the emmeans package, and pairwise contrasts were adjusted using Bonferroni correction. Model adequacy was assessed through residual inspection and likelihood‐ratio comparison with reduced models. Sensitivity analyses additionally included age or disease duration as covariates. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

To evaluate individual‐level reliable change, we calculated a control‐based reliable change index (RCI). 17 Change scores were computed as the difference between follow‐up and baseline performance for each participant and standardized relative to the variability observed in the nonoperated TLE group, which served as an estimate of expected test–retest variability and practice effects. RCI values exceeding ±1.96 were interpreted as statistically reliable improvement or decline, whereas values within this interval indicated no reliable change. RCI categories were used descriptively to characterize individual change beyond measurement error and were not interpreted as clinical outcome classifications.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki and approved by institutional ethics committee of the corresponding institution (approval number: E‐10840098‐202.3.02‐1895; date: March 7, 2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

Verbal total learning (SBST total)

For SBST total learning, the model demonstrated significant main effects of time (F(1,44) = 10.03, p = 0.003) and group (F(2,44) = 6.89, p = 0.003). Importantly, a significant time × group interaction was observed (F(2,44) = 7.45, p = 0.001), indicating differential longitudinal change across groups.

At baseline, the nonoperated TLE group demonstrated higher scores than both surgical groups. The SAH group scored 19.05 points lower (β = −19.05, p = 0.020), and the ATL group scored 29.12 points lower (β = −29.12, p = 0.004) than the nonoperated TLE group.

Over time, the nonoperated group showed a significant decline (β = −22.62, p = 0.002). In contrast, both surgical groups exhibited significantly more favorable trajectories relative to the nonoperated TLE group (time × SAH: β = 28.16, p = 0.001; time × ATL: β = 36.82, p < 0.001), with the largest relative longitudinal improvement observed in the ATL group (Figure 1).

Delayed verbal recall (SBST‐USB)

For delayed verbal recall, the model demonstrated a significant main effect of Group (F(2,45)= 10.58, p < 0.001), whereas the main effect of Time was not significant (p = 0.923). The overall Time × Group interaction did not reach statistical significance (F(2,45) = 2.54, p = 0.079).

At baseline, both surgical groups scored significantly lower than the nonoperated TLE group (SAH: β = −3.49, p < 0.001; ATL: β = −5.12, p < 0.001).

The nonoperated TLE group showed no significant longitudinal change (β = −0.08, p = 0.923). Although the overall time × group interaction did not reach statistical significance, planned contrasts suggested that the SAH group demonstrated a more favorable trajectory compared with the nonoperated TLE group (time × SAH: β = 2.28, p = 0.030), whereas the corresponding contrast for the ATL group did not reach statistical significance (time × ATL: β = 2.18, p = 0.089).

Visual immediate recall (WMS immediate)

For visual immediate recall, the model revealed a significant main effect of Time (F(1,45) = 9.24, p = 0.0039), while the main effect of Group was not significant (p = 0.237). A significant Time × Group interaction was observed (F(2,45) = 6.06, p = 0.0047).

Fixed‐effect contrasts demonstrated that the interaction was significant for the ATL group (time × ATL: β = 3.40, p = 0.004), whereas the SAH group did not differ significantly from the nonoperated TLE group (p = 0.735). The nonoperated TLE group showed no significant longitudinal change.

Adjustment for age did not alter the significance of the time or time × group effects.

Visual delayed recall (WMS delayed)

For visual delayed recall, the model demonstrated a significant main effect of time (F(1,45) = 5.13, p = 0.028), while the main effect of group was not significant (p = 0.438). Importantly, a significant time × group interaction was observed (F(2,45) = 8.20, p < 0.001).

Interaction contrasts indicated significant positive effects for both surgical groups relative to the nonoperated TLE group (time × SAH: β = 2.50, p = 0.021; time × ATL: β = 5.20, p < 0.001), with the largest longitudinal improvement observed in the ATL group.

Sensitivity analyses including age and disease duration as covariates did not materially alter these findings.

RCI analysis

RCI analysis was conducted to determine whether individual‐level changes in neuropsychological performance exceeded measurement error and could be considered statistically reliable. Lateralization‐specific patterns of reliable change were observed.

In the left ATL group, statistically reliable improvement in verbal learning was observed in 83.3% of patients, while 16.7% showed no reliable change. In the right ATL group, no statistically reliable improvement in verbal learning was detected; however, statistically reliable improvement in immediate recall was observed in all patients.

In the left SAH group, reliable decline in verbal learning was observed in 35.7% of patients, whereas 42.9% demonstrated reliable improvement in long‐term recall. In the right SAH group, performance was generally stable, although 41.7% showed reliable improvement in immediate recall.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that long‐term cognitive trajectories in MTLE differ according to surgical approach, memory domain, and hemispheric lateralization. The linear mixed‐effects analyses revealed significant group × time interactions in verbal total learning and visual memory measures, indicating differential longitudinal change across treatment groups. The most pronounced improvement in verbal learning was observed in the ATL group, particularly among left‐sided resections, whereas the nonoperated TLE group showed a decline over time. The SAH group demonstrated an intermediate pattern of change. These findings provide important context for understanding domain‐specific and lateralization‐dependent cognitive outcomes following epilepsy surgery.

Verbal memory and neuroplasticity after ATL

The significant group × time interaction observed for verbal total learning indicates that longitudinal change differed across treatment groups. The most pronounced increase was observed in the ATL group, particularly among left‐sided resections, consistent with the observed lateralization‐dependent effects. At the individual level, RCI analysis further demonstrated statistically reliable improvement in verbal learning in 83.3% of patients with left ATL. These findings are consistent with the existing literature, which suggests that resection of epileptogenic foci may reduce abnormal network activity in surrounding and contralateral cortical areas, potentially facilitating neuroplastic reorganization. 18 , 19 , 20 In line with this, recent evidence indicates that neuroplastic mechanisms may contribute to functional adaptation following resection, particularly in younger patients or those with significant hippocampal atrophy before surgery. Some studies have proposed that pre‐existing atrophy may be associated with compensatory reorganization processes. 20 , 21 After ATL resection, functional network remodeling has been reported, reflecting compensatory activation patterns that may support postoperative memory stability or recovery. 22

Importantly, improvement was not restricted to verbal learning, as visual memory measures also demonstrated significant longitudinal change in the ATL group. These domain‐specific patterns may reflect distributed network adaptation rather than isolated hippocampal effects.

The dissociation between significant improvement in total learning and the more limited effects observed in delayed recall may suggest differential recovery across memory components. While learning performance improved over time, delayed recall showed more modest change, possibly reflecting the involvement of broader hippocampal–cortical networks. This pattern may indicate domain‐specific cognitive adaptation rather than uniform recovery across all memory processes.

Lateralization and domain‐specific cognitive change

The laterality of the surgical intervention was associated with distinct patterns of memory change. Reliable improvement in verbal learning was most prominent in left ATL patients, whereas right ATL and right SAH patients demonstrated improvement primarily in visual immediate recall at the individual level. Specifically, RCI analysis indicated statistically reliable improvement in visual immediate recall in 100% of right ATL patients and in 41.7% of right SAH patients.

This dissociation is consistent with established models of hemispheric specialization, in which the left hemisphere predominantly supports language and verbal memory functions, whereas the right hemisphere contributes more strongly to visuospatial and nonverbal memory processes. 17 The observed domain‐specific pattern may reflect functional specialization and differential network adaptation following surgery rather than uniform cognitive change across hemispheres. 23

Cognitive outcomes after SAH

Despite achieving effective seizure control in approximately 80% of cases (Engel Class I), the SAH group demonstrated a more selective, domain‐specific pattern of cognitive change compared to ATL. While gains in verbal total learning were less pronounced than those observed in the ATL group, linear mixed‐effects analyses indicated significant longitudinal interaction effects in the delayed memory domains. These findings suggest that cognitive change following SAH appears to be domain‐specific rather than global in nature.

RCI analysis further highlighted the heterogeneity of outcomes within the SAH group, with 35.7% of LSAH patients demonstrating declines in verbal learning, whereas 42.9% showed statistically reliable improvement in delayed recall.

The comparatively narrower resection scope of SAH may be associated with more localized patterns of structural and functional adaptation. 14 While sparing lateral neocortical tissue may preserve certain cognitive functions, the extent and distribution of postoperative network adaptation may differ from that observed following ATL. Prior studies have linked SAH to accelerated long‐term forgetting (ALF) and persistent episodic memory difficulties in some patients, 24 , 25 highlighting the potential importance of structural and functional changes in determining long‐term cognitive trajectories.

Nonoperated TLE patients

Importantly, nonoperated TLE patients demonstrated a significant longitudinal decline in verbal total learning, contrasting with the relative stability or improvement observed in the surgical groups. These findings underscore the progressive nature of cognitive dysfunction in medically refractory MTLE and are consistent with evidence suggesting ongoing subclinical epileptiform activity and chronic hippocampal dysfunction. 21

The absence of surgical intervention in these patients may allow continued degradation of memory‐related networks, highlighting the potential importance of timely and appropriate treatment planning. Furthermore, although a direct causal link between seizure frequency and cognitive decline has not been firmly established, 26 accumulating evidence suggests that MTLE may involve not only progressive structural and functional changes but also adaptive neuroplastic responses. 12 , 14 Moreover, metabolic changes in hippocampal structures observed after surgery 13 further support the notion that functional reorganization may contribute to cognitive recovery despite the underlying degenerative process.

Clinical implications and surgical strategy

Taken together, these findings underline the importance of personalized surgical planning in MTLE. Left ATL was associated with more pronounced improvements, particularly in verbal memory recovery. In contrast, right ATL and SAH were associated with more domain‐specific gains and may require careful evaluation in patients with preexisting memory compromise. 9 , 10 , 18

Furthermore, the observed cognitive deterioration in nonoperated patients highlights the potential risks of expectant management, even when seizures are not overtly worsening. 27 , 28

Postoperative memory outcomes were not directly associated with seizure freedom, reinforcing the notion that cognitive recovery and seizure control are partially dissociable processes. 4 , 26

Strengths

This study has several notable strengths. Compared to the systematic review by Alexandratou et al., 29 which summarized 11 long‐term studies with heterogeneous cohorts and variable follow‐up periods, our study provides one of the relatively rare 10‐year longitudinal follow‐ups of cognitive outcomes after epilepsy surgery, offering valuable insight into the durability of memory changes.

Second, it directly compares two major surgical approaches (ATL vs. SAH) alongside a nonoperated control group, allowing differentiation between treatment‐specific effects and disease‐related cognitive trajectories.

Third, integrating longitudinal LMMs with individual‐level RCI analyses provides a complementary analytic framework for examining both group‐level trends and statistically reliable individual change. LMMs appropriately account for repeated measurements and unequal group sizes, thereby accommodating interindividual variability and improving the precision of long‐term outcome estimation.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was modest, particularly for the ATL subgroup, which limits the generalizability of our findings and reduces statistical power to detect small effect sizes. Second, group sizes were unbalanced, with more patients undergoing SAH than ATL, which may have influenced between‐group comparisons. Third, the study was conducted at a single center, and results may not fully represent broader patient populations or surgical practices.

Fourth, although we applied appropriate statistical methods, including LMM and RCI analysis, the small sample size, group imbalance, and potential test–retest effects remain possible sources of bias. While LMMs can accommodate incomplete observations under certain assumptions, missing data may still influence the precision of estimates.

Finally, cognitive outcomes were assessed using a specific set of verbal and visual memory tests; therefore, the results may not capture the full spectrum of postoperative cognitive changes.

The long follow‐up interval also represents an important limitation. Although the 10‐year duration enables the assessment of long‐term cognitive trajectories following epilepsy surgery, subtle or transient cognitive changes occurring in the early postoperative period may not have been captured. Furthermore, cognitive outcomes at this time point may reflect influences beyond surgical intervention, including aging, disease progression, and long‐term use of antiseizure medications. These factors should be considered when interpreting the present findings.

Future perspectives

In recent years, advanced imaging techniques have provided crucial insights into predicting cognitive outcomes after surgery. Functional connectivity and hippocampal volumetric analyses have revealed how changes in hippocampal–cortical networks affect postoperative memory functions. Moreover, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have been applied to characterize the long‐term effects of surgery on brain networks. 27 Future research should integrate multimodal neuroimaging with longitudinal cognitive assessments to refine predictive models of surgical outcomes. Incorporating emerging approaches, such as machine learning‐based network analyses and multimodal biomarkers, may further enhance patient stratification. Ultimately, these strategies could help identify individuals most likely to benefit from ATL versus SAH, guiding personalized surgical decision‐making. In addition to neuroimaging‐based prognostic approaches, structured cognitive rehabilitation protocols have also been proposed to enhance recovery after TLE surgery. 30

Overall, our findings emphasize that timely and individualized surgical intervention in MTLE not only achieves seizure control but may also contribute to durable cognitive stabilization or recovery through mechanisms of neuroplasticity and functional reorganization.

CONCLUSION

ATL was associated with significant longitudinal improvements across multiple memory domains, particularly verbal total learning and visual memory. In contrast, SAH was associated with more selective, domain‐specific effects, while nonoperated TLE patients demonstrated a longitudinal decline in memory performance over the follow‐up period.

Overall, this 10‐year longitudinal study provides longitudinal evidence that surgical approach and memory domain are important determinants of long‐term cognitive outcomes in MTLE. Our findings highlight the importance of sustained follow‐up and comprehensive preoperative neuropsychological assessment, as well as the potential value of individualized surgical planning and adjunctive rehabilitation strategies to optimize cognitive outcomes after epilepsy surgery.
