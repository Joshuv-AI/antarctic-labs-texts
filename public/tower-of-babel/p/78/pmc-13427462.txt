Integrated metabolomics and molecular docking reveal fermentation-dependent flavonoid transformation and bitterness attenuation in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea
Authors: Jinfeng Zhou, Haocheng Liu, Xueke Sun, Jiguo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study was conducted to elucidate the fermentation-dependent remodeling of sensory attributes, non-volatile metabolites, flavonoid profiles, and bitterness-related mechanisms in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea processed at different fermentation degrees, including non-fermented tea (NFT), slightly fermented tea (SFT), fungal flowered tea (FFT), and prolonged fermentation tea (PFT). Fermentation was found to markedly reshape the taste profile of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea, with bitterness, sweetness, saltiness, and complexity being progressively reduced, whereas sourness and umami were enhanced, particularly in PFT. A total of 1799 non-volatile metabolites and 102 flavonoid metabolites were identified. Slightly fermented stage favored the accumulation of flavonoids, with the total flavonoid content reaching the highest level in SFT (4703.77 ± 15.75 mg/kg), representing increases of 188.26%, 52.13%, and 137.58% compared with NFT, FFT, and PFT. Molecular docking analysis indicated that catechin, (−)-epicatechin, isoorientin, and hesperidin may interact with the T2R14 receptor with relatively strong predicted binding affinities.Keywords: Non-volatile metabolites, Flavonoid, KEGG, Bitter taste

Introduction

Eucommia ulmoides Oliver is a medicinal and edible plant that has been widely consumed in East Asia and has recently attracted increasing attention as a promising raw material for functional beverages (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2020). Its leaves are rich in bioactive phytochemicals, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, lignans, and iridoids, which contribute to their antioxidant activity and other health-promoting properties (Wang, Li, et al., 2022). Consequently, Eucommia ulmoides tea has been recognized as a functional plant-based infusion with considerable nutritional value. However, its relatively intense bitterness, astringency, and medicinal-like taste have remained major obstacles to consumer acceptance and the broader development of related tea products.

In tea processing, fermentation is a key determinant of final quality because it continuously reshapes the composition of non-volatile metabolites responsible for taste formation (Guo et al., 2025; Wang, Bai, et al., 2025). Previous studies have demonstrated that fermentation markedly alters flavonoids, polyphenols, amino acids, sugars, lipids, and organic acids, thereby modifying bitterness, astringency, umami, sweetness, and overall mouthfeel (Chen et al., 2021). Among these metabolites, flavonoids are particularly important because they contribute to both the functional properties and sensory quality of tea (Wen et al., 2024). During fermentation, flavonoids may undergo oxidation, hydrolysis, and polymerization, making flavonoid metabolism a key biochemical process underlying taste evolution in fermented tea (Ye et al., 2022). Although fermentation-associated metabolic changes have been widely investigated in conventional tea systems, a systematic evaluation of fermentation-induced taste remodeling in Eucommia ulmoides tea remains limited, especially with respect to the coordinated interpretation of global non-volatile metabolites, flavonoid transformation, and bitterness-related sensory responses (Wang et al., 2021).

In the present study, a standardized fermentation-gradient model was established of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea, including non-fermented, slightly fermented, fungal flowered, and prolonged fermentation samples, to systematically investigate fermentation induced bitterness remodeling. Widely targeted metabolomics was used to characterize the overall changes in non-volatile metabolites, targeted flavonoid metabolomics was applied to resolve the dynamic redistribution of flavonoid subclasses, electronic tongue analysis was performed to evaluate taste variation across fermentation stages, and molecular docking was conducted to assess the potential interactions between representative bitter flavonoid metabolites and the T2R14 receptor. By querying the BitterDB database (Dunkel et al., 2020), catechin, (−)-epicatechin, isoorientin, and hesperidin are all documented as reported bitter compounds, with bitterness threshold values within the ranges of 16–500 μmol/L, respectively. These existing sensory data indicate that the above compounds possess intrinsic bitterness perception properties. By integrating sensory, metabolomic, and receptor-level evidence, this study aimed to elucidate the metabolic basis and potential molecular mechanism underlying bitterness attenuation and taste optimization in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea, thereby providing theoretical support for quality-oriented processing and the development of functional Eucommia ulmoides dark tea products.

Material and methods

Materials

Fresh Eucommia ulmoides leaves with uniform maturity and free from visible mechanical damage, disease, and insect infestation were selected as the experimental materials and were randomly assigned to four treatment groups with different degrees of fermentation. Catechin, (−)-epicatechin, isoorientin, and hesperidin were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Eucommia ulmoides tea samples preparation

Eucommia ulmoides dark tea used in this study was provided by Guangdong Jia Bake Food Co., Ltd. (Guangdong Province, China), and all samples representing different fermentation stages were processed under standardized manufacturing conditions by the same company. Moreover, all Eucommia ulmoides dark tea samples were produced by the same manufacturer in a single production batch using identical processing equipment and standardized operating procedures to minimize batch-to-batch variation and ensure the reproducibility of subsequent metabolomic analyses.

The basic processing procedure was adapted from the method reported by Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2025) with slight modifications, and included withering, fixation, rolling, primary drying, pile fermentation, fungal flowering treatment, and final drying. Specifically, freshly harvested Eucommia ulmoides leaves were first withered until the moisture content decreased to 65%–70%. Fixation was then performed using a drum-type fixation machine, and the moisture content of the leaves was subsequently controlled at 55%–60%. While the leaves remained moderately warm, rolling was carried out, and deblocking was performed once the strip formation rate exceeded 80%. After rolling, the moisture content decreased to approximately 30%–35%. The rolled leaves were then subjected to primary drying at 140–180 °C until the moisture content decreased to 25%–30%. The sample obtained at this stage was designated as non-fermented tea (NFT). Using the NFT as the starting material, Eucommia ulmoides dark tea samples with different fermentation degrees were further prepared by controlling the extent of pile fermentation. Slightly fermented tea (SFT) was obtained by subjecting NFT to natural pile fermentation for 2 d. Fungal-flowered tea (FFT) was produced by further applying fungal flowering treatment to the SFT sample for 9 d. Prolonged-fermentation tea (PFT) was obtained by extending the fermentation of the FFT sample for an additional 9 d. Finally, all samples were subjected to the same standardized final drying procedure using the same drying equipment and operating conditions. The drying temperature was gradually adjusted within the range of 30–50 °C according to the moisture status of the tea leaves, and the drying process was continued for 5–12 d until the moisture content of each sample decreased to below 8%.The samples were then stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Widely targeted metabolomics analysis

A freeze-dried Eucommia ulmoides leaf powder sample (50 mg) was extracted with 1.0 mL of methanol/acetonitrile/water (1:2:1, v/v/v), followed by vortexing (30 s), homogenization (45 Hz, 10 min), sonication in an ice-water bath (10 min), and incubation at −20 °C for 1 h. After centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C), 300 μL of the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and transferred into an LC–MS vial for analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes (10 μL) of each extract to monitor instrument stability and analytical reproducibility.

Widely targeted metabolomic analysis was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class PLUS system coupled with a SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. Metabolites were separated on a Waters HSS-T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) using water containing 0.1% formic acid and 5 mM ammonium acetate (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase B). Gradient elution was performed as follows: 0–1.5 min, 98% A; 1.5–5.0 min, 50% A; 5.0–10.0 min, 2% A; and 10.0–11.0 min, 98% A, followed by 3 min of equilibration. The flow rate, column temperature, and injection volume were 0.35 mL min−1, 50 °C, and 4 μL, respectively. The ESI source was operated in both positive and negative ion modes with an ion source temperature of 550 °C and spray voltages of +5500 V and − 4500 V. Curtain Gas, Ion Source Gas I, and Ion Source Gas II were set at 35, 50, and 55 psi, respectively. Data were acquired in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode and processed using Analyst (v1.7.2) and Sciex OS (v2.0.1) (Yu et al., 2025).

Targeted flavonoid quantification

Approximately 20 mg of sample was extracted with 1.0 mL of methanol–water (7:3, v/v) in the presence of steel beads, followed by vortexing (30 s), homogenization (45 Hz, 15 min), and sonication in an ice-water bath (30 min). After centrifugation (12,000 r/min, 15 min, 4 °C), 200 μL of the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane for analysis. A mixed flavonoid standard solution (10 μmol/L) was prepared by serial dilution of a 10 mmol/L stock solution to generate calibration curves.

Targeted flavonoids were analyzed using a Waters ACQUITY I-Class UPLC system equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm). Water containing 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid were used as mobile phases A and B, respectively. The column temperature, autosampler temperature, and injection volume were maintained at 35 °C, 10 °C, and 2 μL, respectively. Quantitative analysis was performed on a SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an IonDrive Turbo V ESI source operating in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The ion source parameters were as follows: Curtain Gas, 35 psi; IonSpray Voltage, +5500 V/−4500 V; source temperature, 550 °C; Ion Source Gas 1, 50 psi; and Ion Source Gas 2, 55 psi. Data were acquired and processed using Analyst (v1.7.2) and Sciex OS (v2.0.1), respectively (He, Cheng, et al., 2025).

Molecular docking

To elucidate the potential interactions between the identified bitter flavonoid metabolites and human bitter taste receptors in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea at different fermentation degrees, molecular docking simulations were conducted. Candidate bitter compounds were first screened from previously reported metabolites of Eucommia ulmoides by querying the BitterDB database (http://bitterdb.agri.huji.ac.il/dbbitter.php), FooDB database (https://foodb.ca/), DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com/), and ChemTastesDB Version 2.1 (https://zenodo.org/records/15051366) (Dunkel et al., 2020). The three-dimensional structures of the identified bitter flavonoid metabolites were obtained from the PubChem database. Ligand information for the human bitter taste receptor (T2R14) was retrieved from BitterDB following the method described by Cai et al. (Cai, Hong, & Cui, 2025). The human bitter taste receptor T2R14 was selected as the docking target based on the following considerations. First, T2R14 is one of the most broadly tuned bitter receptors, capable of recognizing structurally diverse plant-derived bitter ligands, particularly flavonoids (Ziaikin et al., 2025). Second, according to the BitterDB database (http://bitterdb.agri.huji.ac.il/dbbitter.php), all four candidate bitter compounds (catechin, (−)-epicatechin, isoorientin, and hesperidin) have been documented as known or predicted ligands of T2R14, whereas other TAS2R receptors (e.g., T2R7, T2R10, T2R39) show only partial coverage among these compounds. Third, previous experimental studies have confirmed that T2R14 responds to catechin and (−)-epicatechin, making it the most relevant receptor for the flavonoid metabolites identified in this study (Roland et al., 2013; Soares et al., 2013). For these reasons, T2R14 was selected as the representative receptor for molecular docking. Fourth, while T2R14 was selected as the docking receptor because it is the broadest-spectrum receptor known to interact with the flavonoid compounds identified in this study, we acknowledge that bitterness perception in humans is mediated by a family of more than 25 TAS2R receptors, and different compounds may activate multiple receptors with varying affinities. For example, T2R7 and T2R10 have also been reported to respond to certain catechins. Therefore, the current docking analysis may not fully capture the complex receptor–ligand interaction network underlying the bitterness of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea. Future studies should extend the molecular docking analysis to include other relevant TAS2R receptors (particularly T2R7, T2R10, and T2R39) and, ideally, validate these predictions using cell-based functional assays.

Prior to molecular docking, the receptor and ligand structures were prepared using AutoDock Tools 1.5.7. The preparation procedure involved the addition of polar hydrogen atoms, the assignment of Gasteiger charges, and the removal of water molecules and other unnecessary heteroatoms. Docking simulation was subsequently performed using AutoDock Vina, and binding affinity was assessed according to the calculated binding energy (kcal/mol). A binding energy threshold of −7.0 kcal/mol was used to identify stable receptor–ligand complexes (Cai, Mou, et al., 2025). The docking conformations were then visualized and analyzed using PyMol 1.7.2.1 (Schrödinger, New York, NY, USA) and Discovery Studio 2019 (Dassault Systèmes BIOVIA, California, USA).

Electronic tongue analysis

Taste characteristics were evaluated using an ASTREE II electronic tongue system (Alpha MOS, Toulouse, France) equipped with an autosampler and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The sensor array consisted of seven cross-selective sensors, including AHS (sourness), ANS (sweetness), SCS (bitterness), CTS (saltiness), NMS (umami), and two universal tastes be response (PKS and CPS), which collectively provided comprehensive information on the taste profile of tea infusions.

Tea infusions were prepared by accurately weighing 2.00 g of Eucommia ulmoides leaves into a sealed vessel and extracting them with 100 mL of boiling water. The mixture was maintained in a 60 °C water bath for 30 min to facilitate the release of taste-active substances. The infusion was subsequently filtered through gauze into a glass beaker and allowed to cool to room temperature for 1 h prior to analysis. Before measurement, the sensor array was conditioned and calibrated according to the manufacturer's instructions. The sensors were immersed in ultrapure water and 0.01 M HCl solution to stabilize the electrode potentials, and system performance was verified using the standard calibration solution. For each analysis, 100 mL of tea infusion was transferred into the sample cup and measured under the following sequence: sample acquisition (120 s), aftertaste acquisition (40 s), and sensor cleaning (10 s). The potentiometric differences between each sensor electrode and the Ag/AgCl reference electrode were continuously recorded during measurement. The average response values obtained during the final 10 s of the acquisition period (110–120 s) were used for subsequent statistical analysis (Zan et al., 2025).

After each measurement, the sensor electrodes were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water and regenerated using 0.01 M HCl solution until stable baseline potentials were achieved. Six biological replicates were analyzed for each fermentation stage, and the sensor response data were processed using AlphaSoft software (version 12.0, Alpha MOS, Toulouse, France).

Quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA)

QDA was conducted in a sensory evaluation room maintained at 21 ± 1 °C to evaluate umami, sweetness, sourness, bitterness, astringency, and kokumi. Six trained panelists (three males and three females, aged 22–45 years) received more than 20 h of training based on the sensory evaluation criteria shown in Table S1. Tea infusions were prepared as described in section 2.6, and approximately 5 mL of each sample was assessed using a 10-point intensity scale (0–2, very weak; 2–4, weak; 4–6, moderate; 6–8, strong; and 8–10, very strong) (Ma et al., 2025). All samples were evaluated in triplicate at different tasting temperatures, with water rinsing performed between successive evaluations.

Omission experiments

To evaluate the sensory contribution of bitter compounds to the overall bitterness perception of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea at different fermentation stages, a series of Omission experiments were performed by selectively removing individual bitter compounds. Three samples, including one omission sample and two reconstituted samples, were randomly coded with three-digit numbers and placed in 5 mL black glass bottles. The three samples were simultaneously presented to each panelist. The panelists were instructed to taste the samples and identify the one that exhibited a significantly different taste from the other two samples. According to the triangle test method (GB/T 12311–2012), the sensory differences caused by the omission of specific compounds were evaluated. When the removal of an individual compound resulted in a significant change in the bitterness intensity of the reconstituted sample, the compound was considered a key contributor to bitterness in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea (He, Xiao, et al., 2025).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Raw data were collated using Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 20.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). For pairwise comparisons in metabolomic analyses, Student's t-test was performed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using R version 3.6.1. Hierarchical clustering heatmap and K-means clustering analyses were performed using R version 4.2.0. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate the reproducibility and similarity of non-volatile metabolite profiles among samples. Prior to Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), missing values were processed, followed by log₂ transformation to improve data normality. The processed data were subsequently normalized using unit variance scaling (UV), in which each variable was mean-centered and divided by its standard deviation to assign equal variance to all variables. OPLS-DA and 200-permutation tests were then performed using R version 3.5.1. Variable importance in projection (VIP) values were calculated by cross-validation. Differential metabolites were screened according to the following criteria: VIP > 1, p < 0.05, and fold change (FC) > 2 or < 0.5. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differential metabolites was performed using a hypergeometric test, and pathways with p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Results and discussion

Sensory evaluation

As shown in Fig. 1A-B, the electronic tongue analysis revealed a clear fermentation-dependent transformation in taste attributes across NFT, SFT, FFT, and PFT. NFT was characterized by relatively high bitterness (SCS), saltiness (CTS), sweetness (ANS), and two universal tastes be response (PKS and CPS), whereas comparatively low sourness (AHS) and umami (NMS) responses were observed. By contrast, SFT and FFT exhibited markedly increased NMS responses together with reduced PKS, CTS, CPS, ANS, and SCS signals (p<0.05), indicating that partial and FFT weakened the intense and sharp taste attributes and shifted the overall taste pattern toward a more mellow and coordinated profile. PFT displayed the highest AHS and NMS responses, but the lowest responses for SCS, CTS, CPS, ANS, and PKS, suggesting that PFT further suppressed bitterness- and stimulation-related taste signals and produced a softer, more mellow, and more typical fermented taste profile.

PCA and hierarchical clustering further supported the taste evolution during fermentation (Fig. 1C–D). PC1 and PC2 explained 93.00% and 6.52% of the total variance, respectively, indicating that the first two principal components effectively captured the major variation in taste attributes. NFT exhibited high responses for SCS, PKS, CPS, CTS, and ANS but low responses for NMS and AHS, reflecting a taste profile dominated by bitterness-related characteristics. In contrast, PFT showed increased NMS and AHS responses accompanied by reduced SCS, PKS, CPS, CTS, and ANS signals, indicating a shift toward a milder, less bitter, and more mellow taste profile. These results were consistent with those reported by Pei et al. (Pei et al., 2025).

As shown in Fig. 1E–F, the taste characteristics of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea were markedly altered during fermentation, exhibiting an overall transition from a bitterness- and astringency-dominated profile to a profile characterized by enhanced umami and reduced bitterness. In NFT, the highest intensities of bitterness and astringency were observed. Compared with NFT, both bitterness and astringency were significantly reduced in SFT (p < 0.001), whereas umami and kokumi were significantly enhanced (p < 0.001). The FFT stage represented a critical turning point in taste quality development. Compared with NFT, umami and kokumi increased by 115.80% and 207.10%, respectively, whereas bitterness and astringency decreased by 42.00% and 46.94%, respectively. In PFT, umami and kokumi reached their highest levels, while bitterness and astringency declined to their lowest levels.

PCA based on the QDA results revealed that the first two principal components collectively explained 96.43% of the total variance, with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 89.86% and 6.57% of the variance, respectively, indicating that the model effectively captured the taste differences among samples (Fig. 1G). As shown in Fig. 1H, the hierarchical clustering heatmap indicated that Bitterness and Astringency were grouped into the same cluster and exhibited the highest standardized intensities in NFT, followed by a continuous decline throughout fermentation, reaching their lowest levels in PFT. Conversely, Kokumi and Umami showed highly similar variation trends, increasing progressively from negative standardized values in NFT to their highest levels in PFT. Notably, FFT served as a critical turning point in taste transformation, during which Bitterness and Astringency were markedly reduced, whereas Umami and Kokumi were substantially enhanced. These clustering patterns were consistent with the QDA and PCA results.

To assess the consistency between electronic tongue measurements and sensory evaluation, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between the electronic tongue sensor responses and QDA taste attributes. Significant correlations were observed between all electronic tongue sensors and QDA attributes (|r| = 0.71–0.90, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1I). Specifically, AHS was positively correlated with Sourness (r = 0.76), NMS with Umami (r = 0.83), and ANS with Sweetness (r = 0.84), whereas SCS exhibited the strongest positive correlation with Bitterness (r = 0.89). These results further confirmed the strong agreement between the electronic tongue responses and QDA sensory evaluation.

Full classification analysis of non-volatile metabolites

To characterize the global variation in non-volatile metabolites among Eucommia ulmoides dark tea samples at different fermentation stages (NFT, SFT, FFT, and PFT), PCA was performed. The first two principal components explained 68.41% of the total variance (PC1: 52.55%; PC2: 15.86%), indicating that the major metabolic variation was effectively captured. QC samples clustered tightly, demonstrating satisfactory analytical stability and reproducibility (Fig. S1A). NFT and SFT were closely clustered, indicating similar metabolite profiles during the early fermentation stage, whereas FFT and PFT were clearly separated, reflecting substantial metabolic remodeling with increasing fermentation.

Spearman rank correlation analysis further supported these results. Strong intra-group correlations (r > 0.98) confirmed excellent biological reproducibility, whereas inter-group correlations gradually decreased along the fermentation process. NFT showed high similarity to SFT (r ≈ 0.95) but substantially lower correlations with FFT (r ≈ 0.75) and PFT (r ≈ 0.71). Although FFT and PFT remained moderately correlated (r ≈ 0.89), they were clearly differentiated from the early-stage samples (Fig. S1B). Together, these results demonstrated a fermentation-dependent remodeling of non-volatile metabolites.

A total of 1799 non-volatile metabolites were identified across all Eucommia ulmoides dark tea samples and classified into 18 chemical categories (Fig. 2A). Lipids constituted the largest proportion, accounting for 263 metabolites (14.62%), followed by terpenoids (219, 12.17%), sugars (200, 11.12%), flavonoids (195, 10.84%), and organic acids (181, 10.06%). Together, these five dominant classes represented more than half of the detected metabolome, indicating that lipid metabolism, terpenoid biosynthesis, carbohydrate transformation, and phenolic-related pathways were major contributors to the chemical composition of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea. Moderately abundant classes included amino acids (6.34%) and polyphenols (5.06%), which are closely associated with taste formation and bioactivity. Alcohols (3.95%), alkaloids (3.67%), and coumarins (2.89%) were detected at intermediate levels, suggesting additional contributions to aroma precursors and functional properties. Minor metabolite groups, including nucleotides (1.89%), quinones (1.78%), phenylpropanoids (1.67%), nucleosides (1.56%), steroids (1.39%), lignans (1.17%), and vitamins (0.50%), collectively accounted for a smaller fraction of the metabolome but may play important roles in biochemical regulation and fermentation-associated transformations.

The compositional distribution of non-volatile metabolites in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea was compared across four fermentation stages (NFT, SFT, FFT, and PFT) (Fig. 2B–E). Similar numbers of metabolites were detected in all groups (NFT: 1671; SFT: 1674; FFT: 1695; PFT: 1699), indicating that fermentation primarily affected metabolite abundance rather than chemical diversity. Lipids (14.99–15.47%) consistently represented the largest metabolite class, followed by terpenoids (11.83–12.63%), sugars (11.27–11.73%), flavonoids (10.63–10.89%), and organic acids (9.40–10.01%), suggesting that the overall metabolic framework remained largely conserved throughout fermentation. Only minor compositional changes were observed from NFT to SFT, whereas progression to FFT was accompanied by increased organic acids and slightly decreased amino acids, indicating intensified metabolic remodeling. In PFT, organic acids and polyphenols were further enriched, while the proportions of most other metabolite classes remained relatively stable. Minor metabolite categories, including alkaloids, coumarins, nucleotides, phenylpropanoids, quinones, nucleosides, steroids, lignans, and vitamins, also exhibited consistently low and stable abundances, indicating that fermentation mainly altered metabolite abundance rather than overall metabolite composition.

The relative abundance of non-volatile metabolite classes in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea was further visualized by hierarchical clustering analysis (Fig. 2F). NFT and SFT clustered closely and were enriched in sugars, amino acids, flavonoids, coumarins, nucleotides, and organic acids, reflecting metabolite profiles characteristic of primary metabolism. In contrast, FFT was characterized by increased abundances of terpenoids, vitamins, steroids, lignans, and phenylpropanoids, indicating intensified metabolic transformation. PFT exhibited higher levels of polyphenols and lipids, accompanied by reduced abundances of several primary metabolites, including sugars, amino acids, and nucleotides.

K-means clustering further revealed dynamic changes in non-volatile metabolites during fermentation (Fig. 2G). Six metabolite clusters with distinct accumulation patterns were identified. Sub classes 1 and 2 decreased progressively from NFT to PFT, suggesting continuous consumption or transformation during fermentation. Sub class 3 showed transient enrichment in SFT before declining, indicating the formation of intermediate metabolites during early fermentation. Sub class 4 reached its highest abundance in FFT and declined in PFT, whereas sub classes 5 and 6 increased progressively, particularly in FFT and PFT, reflecting the gradual accumulation of fermentation-associated metabolites.

Differential non-volatile metabolites screening

To further characterize metabolic differences among fermentation stages, OPLS-DA was performed using the widely targeted metabolomics dataset (Fig. S2A—C). Clear separation was observed for NFT vs SFT, SFT vs FFT, and FFT vs PFT, indicating progressive stage-dependent remodeling of the non-volatile metabolite profiles during fermentation. The corresponding permutation tests yielded R2Y/Q2 values of 1.000/0.979, 1.000/0.998, and 1.000/0.997, respectively, demonstrating excellent model robustness and predictive performance without overfitting. Hierarchical clustering analysis further supported the OPLS-DA results (Fig. S2D).

To elucidate the effects of fermentation on Eucommia ulmoides dark tea, differential metabolites were identified using the criteria of p < 0.05, VIP > 1, and FC < 0.5 or > 2.0. A total of 109, 654, and 415 differential metabolites were detected in NFT vs SFT, SFT vs FFT, and FFT vs PFT, respectively (Fig. 3A–B, Fig. S2E–G, and Table S2). During early fermentation (NFT vs SFT), amino acids, organic acids, lipids, alkaloids, and terpenoids were generally upregulated, whereas flavonoids and polyphenols, including quercetin, catechins, and gallic acid, were significantly downregulated. The transition from SFT to FFT exhibited the greatest metabolic remodeling, characterized by further accumulation of lipids, organic acids, terpenoids, and amino acid derivatives, accompanied by a marked reduction in flavonoids and phenolic acids. Similar trends persisted during prolonged fermentation (FFT vs PFT), indicating continuous flavonoid transformation and secondary metabolic reprogramming, which were likely associated with the progressive reduction in bitterness and astringency and the improvement of overall taste quality.

As shown in Fig. 3C, multiple significantly enriched pathways related to metabolic processes were identified (p < 0.05). The enriched pathways were primarily associated with phenolic compound degradation, amino acid metabolism, microbial metabolism, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and energy-related processes, indicating a coordinated metabolic reprogramming driven by fermentation. In the NFT vs SFT, pathways related to degradation of flavonoids (ko00946), degradation of aromatic compounds (ko01220), and aminobenzoate degradation (ko00627) were significantly enriched. These results suggested that phenolic compounds underwent rapid oxidative transformation and breakdown at the onset of fermentation. The enrichment of these pathways suggested the initiation of phenylpropanoid-derived metabolite catabolism during fermentation, leading to the transformation and degradation of key bitter and astringent metabolites, including flavonoids and phenolic acids. However, because microbial community dynamics were not investigated in the present study, the specific biological drivers responsible for these metabolic changes could not be identified. Future studies integrating metabolomics with microbial community analyses will be conducted to elucidate the microorganisms and molecular mechanisms underlying these metabolic transformations. In the FFT vs PFT, pathways such as degradation of flavonoids (ko00946), aminobenzoate degradation (ko00627), phenylalanine metabolism (ko00360), and ubiquinone and other terpenoid-quinone biosynthesis (ko00130) were enriched. This indicated that phenolic compounds continued to undergo transformation. The sustained degradation of flavonoids and aromatic compounds suggested a progressive reduction of bitterness and astringency. Notably, degradation of flavonoids (ko00946) and aminobenzoate degradation (ko00627) were consistently enriched across multiple fermentation stages, suggesting that these two pathways were closely involved in the metabolic reprogramming process during fermentation.

From a mechanistic perspective, these observations were consistent with the established transformation pathways of tea flavonoids (Wang, Li, et al., 2022). During fermentation, flavonoids such as catechins and flavonols undergo oxidative polymerization, degradation, and structural rearrangement, ultimately forming smaller phenolic compounds or polymeric pigments such as theabrownins (Yun et al., 2025). This dynamic balance between degradation and transformation contributes to the reduction of bitterness and astringency, as well as the formation of a smoother taste profile (Wang, Xu, et al., 2025). Collectively, these pathway enrichment results indicated that flavonoid-related metabolic pathways were extensively remodeled during fermentation. Nevertheless, because the present study focused on metabolomic profiling rather than microbial community characterization, the KEGG pathway annotations should be interpreted as metabolite-associated pathway enrichment rather than direct evidence of microbial metabolic activity. Future studies integrating metabolomics with microbiome or metatranscriptomic analyses will be necessary to identify the microorganisms and enzymes responsible for these metabolic transformations.

Targeted flavonoid metabolomics analysis

To investigate flavonoid dynamics during fermentation, qualitative and quantitative analyses were performed using UPLC–MS/MS. A total of 102 flavonoid metabolites were identified, with polyphenols (35.29%), flavonones (14.71%), flavones (12.75%), and flavonols (12.75%) representing the predominant subclasses and collectively accounting for more than 75% of all identified metabolites (Fig. S3A and Tables S3–S4). PCA revealed clear stage-dependent differences in flavonoid profiles, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 57.61% and 32.31% of the total variance, respectively (89.92% cumulative variance) (Fig. S3B). NFT was distinctly separated from the fermented groups, whereas SFT, FFT, and PFT were also clearly differentiated. Tight clustering of biological replicates demonstrated good analytical repeatability and within-group consistency.

OPLS-DA was performed to compare the flavonoid metabolite profiles among adjacent fermentation stages of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea (Fig. S3C–E). Clear separation was observed for NFT vs SFT, SFT vs FFT, and FFT vs PFT, indicating progressive remodeling of flavonoid metabolism during fermentation. Component 1 explained 95.10%, 79.30%, and 85.60% of the total variance, whereas Component 2 explained 1.12%, 6.60%, and 5.20%, respectively. The corresponding R2Y/Q2 values were 1.000/0.999, 1.000/0.992, and 1.000/0.995. Biological replicates clustered closely within each group, and 200-permutation tests confirmed that all models were robust and free from overfitting.

As shown in Fig. 4A, the number of flavonoid metabolites gradually decreased during fermentation, with 87, 65, 64, and 63 metabolites identified in NFT, SFT, FFT, and PFT, respectively. Polyphenols remained the predominant subclass, followed by flavonones, flavones, and flavonols. Flavones showed relatively stable abundance, whereas flavonols continuously declined. Flavonones decreased sharply in SFT and partially recovered thereafter. In contrast, chalcones increased from two to four metabolites after fermentation, whereas biflavone was detected only in NFT. Despite the reduction in flavonoid diversity, the total flavonoid content reached its maximum in SFT (4703.77 ± 15.75 mg/kg) before declining in FFT and PFT, although it remained significantly higher than that in NFT (1631.76 ± 36.80 mg/kg; p < 0.001) (Fig. S3F). These findings suggested that fermentation predominantly reshaped flavonoid abundance rather than overall flavonoid diversity.

(D) Sankey-bubble plot of KEGG enrichment analysis for NFT vs SFT. (E) Sankey–bubble plot of KEGG enrichment analysis for SFT vs FFT. The UpSet plot analysis further illustrated the shared and unique distribution patterns of flavonoid metabolites among the four fermentation stages of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea (Fig. 4E). A total of 46 flavonoid metabolites were commonly detected in NFT, SFT, FFT, and PFT, indicating the presence of a relatively stable core flavonoid framework throughout processing. In contrast, NFT contained the largest number of unique metabolites (31), whereas SFT had only one unique metabolite (gambogenic Acid), and no unique flavonoid metabolites were observed in FFT or PFT.

Based on the screening criteria of VIP > 1 and p < 0.05, 36 differential flavonoid metabolites were selected for heatmap and hierarchical clustering analysis (Fig. 4C). During the transition from NFT to SFT, marked accumulation was observed in multiple subclasses, particularly flavonols, flavonones, and polyphenols. For example, astragalin, rutin, and nicotiflorin in the flavonols subclass increased from 673.52, 221.15, and 159.08 mg/kg to 1858.97, 1056.63, and 305.94 mg/kg, respectively, while representative flavonones, including flavanomarein, kaempferol 3-glucorhamnoside, and prunin, increased from 1.13, 1.96, and 16.66 mg/kg to 15.05, 16.83, and 46.08 mg/kg, respectively. At the same time, several polyphenols, such as baimaside and (−)-epicatechin, increased from 3.69 and 100.17 mg/kg to 54.81 and 330.88 mg/kg, respectively. In contrast, only a limited number of metabolites decreased at this stage, including nobiletin, hesperidin, and isorhamnetin-3-O-glucoside. From SFT to FFT, many previously enriched flavonols, monophenols, and others declined markedly, such as rutin (1056.63 to 211.92 mg/kg), nicotiflorin (305.94 to 44.88 mg/kg), quercimeritrin (433.33 to 267.93 mg/kg), catechin (48.60 to 13.56 mg/kg). Meanwhile, selected flavonones, polyphenols, isoflavones, and chalcones increased, including eriodictyol (1.18 to 1.90 mg/kg), (±)-naringenin (2.35 to 8.03 mg/kg), naringenin chalcone (1.94 to 7.07 mg/kg), syringaldehyde (6.70 to 12.93 mg/kg), and hyperoside (256.08 to 606.84 mg/kg). During the transition from FFT to PFT, this redistribution pattern became more pronounced. Several flavonones and polyphenols continued to accumulate, including eriodictyol (1.90 to 11.35 mg/kg), taxifolin (1.22 to 4.75 mg/kg), (±)-naringenin (8.03 to 27.44 mg/kg), naringenin chalcone (7.07 to 23.42 mg/kg), daidzin (3.68 to 8.29 mg/kg), and hesperidin (2.44 to 4.91 mg/kg), whereas major flavonols, monophenols, and several glycosylated metabolites declined further, including astragalin (1250.22 to 566.31 mg/kg), rutin (211.92 to 31.76 mg/kg), nicotiflorin (44.88 to 7.95 mg/kg), catechin (13.56 to 5.44 mg/kg), and (−)-epicatechin (21.54 to 4.18 mg/kg).

As shown in Fig. 4D–F, differential flavonoid metabolites were primarily enriched in six KEGG pathways, including flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941), flavone and flavonol biosynthesis (ko00944), degradation of flavonoids (ko00946), biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids (ko01061), biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110), and metabolic pathways (ko01100). Flavonoid biosynthesis showed the highest enrichment (Rich Factor ≈ 1.0) across all comparisons, whereas metabolic pathways and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites exhibited broad enrichment. During NFT vs SFT, flavonol, flavanone, polyphenol, and chalcone related metabolites were predominantly enriched, indicating enhanced flavonoid accumulation during early fermentation (Zhou et al., 2025). In SFT vs FFT, similar pathways remained enriched, but metabolite profiles shifted from accumulation to selective redistribution, consistent with flavonoid transformation through oxidation and hydrolysis (Liu et al., 2024). Flavonoid biosynthesis- and degradation-related pathways remained significantly enriched in FFT vs PFT. Collectively, these results indicated that fermentation dynamically reprogrammed flavonoid metabolism, with early-stage accumulation followed by progressive transformation and remodeling.

Transformation pathways of key flavonoid metabolites

Pearson correlation analysis was performed between 1799 non-volatile metabolites and 102 flavonoid metabolites, using |r| > 0.80 and p < 0.05 as the significance thresholds. A total of 23,220 significant correlations were identified (Table S5a). The correlation network revealed that several flavonoid metabolites, including kaempferol, nicotiflorin, liquiritin, naringenin chalcone, (±)-naringenin, isoliquiritigenin, daidzin, baicalein, and eriodictyol, exhibited extensive associations with non-volatile metabolites. Most of these metabolites belonged to the flavonol, flavanone, and chalcone subclasses, which represent major branches of the biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids (ko01061) pathway. Notably, Kaempferol exhibited the largest number of significant correlations, with more than 1000 associated metabolites, suggesting that it may represent a central metabolic hub within the flavonoid metabolic network of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea.

Based on the identified flavonoid metabolites, a putative metabolic conversion pathway of key flavonoids during the fermentation of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea was constructed (Fig. 5). According to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, six flavonoid-related metabolic pathways were screened, including flavonoid biosynthesis, flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, degradation of flavonoids, biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and metabolic pathways. These pathways provided the metabolic basis for the interconversion and dynamic remodeling of flavonoid metabolites during fermentation. Specifically, the proposed pathway mainly involved glycosylation/de-glycosylation, hydrolysis, hydroxylation/dehydroxylation, dehydrogenation/hydrogenation, and O-methylation reactions, which collectively drove the transformation of flavonoid metabolites (Krawczyk-Lebek et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020). Naringenin chalcone was isomerized to form (±)-naringenin, which was subsequently hydroxylated to generate eriodictyol and dihydrokaempferol (Cao et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024). Dihydrokaempferol could be further converted into taxifolin and kaempferol, and then glycosylated to form derivatives such as astragalin and nicotiflorin (Tartik et al., 2023). Meanwhile, the apigenin/luteolin branch was also markedly remodeled, in which apigenin was further converted into cosmosiin, whereas luteolin gave rise to cynaroside and isoorientin (Pei et al., 2020).

Transformation pathways of key flavonoid metabolites in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea across different fermentation stages.

From the dynamic variation patterns, in NFT vs SFT stage was mainly characterized by marked enrichment of glycosylated flavonols, polyphenols and chalcones metabolites. In NFT, the three most abundant flavonoid metabolites were astragalin (673.52 ± 12.01 mg/kg), rutin (221.16 ± 15.70 mg/kg), and hyperoside (160.92 ± 4.58 mg/kg). After slightly fermented stage, the dominant metabolites shifted to astragalin (1858.97 ± 36.87 mg/kg), rutin (1056.63 ± 23.02 mg/kg), and quercimeritrin (433.33 ± 6.00 mg/kg). Among the selected high-abundance flavonoids, the three largest increases from NFT to SFT were observed for astragalin, rutin, and quercimeritrin, followed by (−)-epicatechin and nicotiflorin. No pronounced decreases were observed among these major flavonoids in this transition, and kaempferol remained essentially unchanged. These results suggested that the slightly fermented stage favored the release and accumulation of glycosylated flavonoids and catechin-type metabolites.

In SFT vs FFT, the flavonoid network shifted from early enrichment toward pathway branching, oxidative conversion, and structural redistribution. In FFT, the three most abundant metabolites were astragalin (1250.22 ± 12.91 mg/kg), hyperoside (606.84 ± 9.03 mg/kg), and kaempferol (437.67 ± 6.59 mg/kg). The largest increases in this stage were observed for kaempferol and hyperoside, indicating that FFT strongly promoted the accumulation of this aglycone and selected flavonol-related metabolites. In contrast, the three largest decreases were found for rutin, astragalin, and (−)-epicatechin, followed by nicotiflorin and quercimeritrin. These results suggested that, in addition to oxidation and redistribution, enhanced de-glycosylation and other fermentation-associated biochemical transformations may have contributed substantially to kaempferol enrichment at this stage.

In FFT vs PFT stage, prolonged fermentation further strengthened the late-stage reorganization of the flavonoid network. In PFT, the three most abundant metabolites were kaempferol (737.47 ± 4.59 mg/kg), astragalin (566.31 ± 18.31 mg/kg), and hyperoside (240.52 ± 1.18 mg/kg). The most pronounced increase was observed for kaempferol, confirming that prolonged fermentation continuously favored the accumulation of this aglycone. By contrast, the three largest decreases were detected for astragalin, hyperoside, and rutin, followed by quercimeritrin and nicotiflorin. At the same time, catechin, (−)-epicatechin, and prunin also continued to decline. These results indicated that prolonged fermentation promoted the sustained depletion of major glycosylated flavonoids, a trend consistent with that reported by Li et al. (Li et al., 2025).

Pearson correlation analysis was performed between the selected flavonoid metabolites and both SCS and bitterness. The results demonstrated that most flavonoid metabolites were significantly correlated with both bitterness-related indicators (p < 0.01), with generally high correlation coefficients (|r| = 0.76–0.99), indicating a close association between flavonoid metabolic variation and the evolution of bitterness in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea (Table S5b). As fermentation progressed, the abundance of several flavonoid metabolites changed markedly, which was accompanied by a gradual reduction in bitterness and simultaneous enhancement of umami and kokum. These changes may have been associated with fermentation-induced flavonoid transformation, including oxidation, glycoside hydrolysis, polymerization, or other structural modifications, thereby contributing to the improvement of taste quality during the fermentation process (Ye et al., 2022).Slightly fermented stage mainly promoted the accumulation of flavonoid glycosides and catechin-related metabolites, whereas FFT and PFT favored the depletion of high-abundance glycosides together with the pronounced enrichment of kaempferol, thereby jointly shaping the flavonoid composition and flavor quality of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea at different fermentation stages.

Omission experiments were further performed to validate the contribution of candidate bitter flavonoids to the overall bitterness perception of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea (Table S7). Among the four evaluated compounds, (−)-epicatechin exhibited the strongest and most consistent bitterness contribution across all fermentation stages, with highly significant sensory differences observed after omission in NFT, SFT, FFT, and PFT samples (p < 0.01). Catechin also showed stable bitterness contributions throughout fermentation, whereas isoorientin exhibited an increased contribution during advanced fermentation stages. Hesperidin showed stage-dependent effects, contributing significantly in NFT, FFT, and PFT (Table S6). These results confirmed that fermentation dynamically altered the contribution of flavonoid metabolites to bitterness perception.

Molecular docking analysis

To further clarify the bitterness-associated flavonoid metabolites in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea at different fermentation stages, bitter flavonoid metabolites were retrieved from four bitter taste databases and screened using the criteria of VIP > 1, p < 0.05, and FC > 2 or < 0.5. To identify the most appropriate bitter taste receptor for molecular docking, four representative flavonoid metabolites (catechin, isoorientin, (−)-epicatechin, and hesperidin) were first docked against four human bitter taste receptors (T2R10, T2R39, T2R7, and T2R14). T2R14 exhibited consistently lower binding energies than the other receptors for all representative flavonoids, suggesting stronger binding affinity and more stable ligand–receptor interactions (Fig. S4A). T2R14 showed the strongest overall interaction with an average binding energy of −8.08 kcal/mol, followed by T2R7 (−7.49 kcal/mol) and T2R10 (−6.90 kcal/mol), whereas T2R39 exhibited relatively weaker interactions (−5.52 kcal/mol) (Fig. S4B). Among the tested compounds, hesperidin displayed the strongest interaction with T2R14 (−9.70 kcal/mol), indicating a potentially stable receptor–ligand complex. Binding energies ≤ −7.0 kcal/mol were considered indicative of strong predicted binding affinity. Based on these preliminary screening results, T2R14 was selected for the subsequent molecular docking analysis. In addition, to investigate the potential receptor–ligand interactions of the candidate bitter flavonoid metabolites, T2R14 was selected as the docking target. BitterDB database queries revealed that T2R14 is the receptor with the broadest coverage among the four candidate compounds, with documented or predicted interactions for all of them (Table S7). By contrast, T2R7 covers only catechin and (−)-epicatechin, while T2R10 and T2R39 show even more limited coverage. This receptor coverage analysis supports the selection of T2R14 as the most representative target for the initial docking investigation. Four representative flavonoid metabolites, including hesperidin, isoorientin, catechin, and (−)-epicatechin, were identified and subsequently subjected to molecular docking analysis to visualize their interaction patterns with the T2R14 receptor (Tan et al., 2026). The binding energies of hesperidin, isoorientin, catechin, and (−)-epicatechin toward T2R14 were determined to be −9.7, −7.4, −7.8, and − 7.4 kcal/mol, respectively, suggesting strong binding affinities between the receptor and these bitter flavonoid compounds. Subsequently, the three-dimensional and two-dimensional interaction patterns were further characterized, and the results are presented in Fig. 6. As shown in Fig. 6A–C, the binding of hesperidin to T2R14 was mediated by multiple interaction forces, including conventional hydrogen bonds with SER265 and GLN266, a carbon hydrogen bond with GLN255, an unfavorable receptor–receptor interaction with SER177, Pi-alkyl interactions with VAL180 and LEU257, and an unfavorable donor–donor interaction with GLN266. As shown in Fig. 6D–F, the interaction between isoorientin and T2R14 was dominated by conventional hydrogen bonds involving PRO73, TRP89, SER265, and GLN266, while an additional Pi–Pi stacked interaction was formed with PHE76. As shown in Fig. 6G–I, the binding of catechin to T2R14 was stabilized by conventional hydrogen bonds with GLU79, ASN162, and ASN171, together with Pi–Pi stacked interaction with GLY158, Pi–Sigma interaction with ASN171, and amide–Pi stacked interaction with TYR159. As shown in Fig. 6J–L, the interaction of (−)-epicatechin with T2R14 was found to be primarily governed by hydrogen bonding, including conventional hydrogen bonds with PHE82, THR86, ILE179, VAL180, and SER183, as well as a carbon hydrogen bond with SER183. In addition, hydrophobic interactions further contributed to ligand binding, mainly through Pi–Pi stacked interactions with PHE82 and TPR89, and Pi-alkyl interactions with LEU85 and VAL180. Furthermore, the BitterDB database, which integrates experimentally reported bitter compounds and their sensory characteristics from peer-reviewed literature, was consulted for additional validation. Catechin (BitterDB id:809), (−)-epicatechin (BitterDB id:813), isoorientin (BitterDB id:2264), and hesperidin (BitterDB id:2211) are all catalogued as known bitter compounds with reported bitterness thresholds (catechin: 250–350 μmol/L; (−)-epicatechin: 300–400 μmol/L; isoorientin: 16 μmol/L, and hesperidin: 75 μmol/L). These existing sensory data, derived from aqueous or standardized test systems, provide independent experimental evidence supporting the bitterness properties of these compounds, complementing our computational docking predictions. Collectively, these results indicated that the binding of the four representative bitter flavonoid metabolites to T2R14 was jointly stabilized by multiple hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions, thereby providing a structural basis for their potential contribution to bitterness perception in Eucommia ulmoides dark tea. Combined with the BitterDB-documented bitterness thresholds, these computational predictions support the identification of catechin, (−)-epicatechin, isoorientin, and hesperidin as candidate bitterness-related metabolites, although their actual sensory contributions in the complex tea matrix require further experimental verification.

Interactions of four representative differential flavonoid metabolites (VIP > 1, p < 0.05, and FC > 2 or < 0.5) with the bitter taste receptor T2R14.

By integrating the molecular docking results with the dynamic changes in metabolite abundance, the stage-dependent contribution of representative bitter flavonoid metabolites to the bitterness of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea could be further elucidated. Their actual contribution to bitterness was likely co-determined by both receptor affinity and metabolite abundance. During the transition from NFT to SFT, catechin and (−)-epicatechin increased markedly, while isoorientin emerged from an undetectable level and hesperidin was nearly depleted. Given the relatively strong receptor-binding capacities of catechin and (−)-epicatechin together with their substantial accumulation, these two metabolites were likely the major contributors to the enhanced bitterness of SFT. From SFT to FFT, the contents of catechin, (−)-epicatechin, and isoorientin decreased markedly, whereas hesperidin reappeared at a detectable level, suggesting that the bitterness contribution shifted from catechin-dominated enhancement to a weaker and more composite receptor-binding pattern. During the transition from FFT to PFT, catechin, (−)-epicatechin, and isoorientin declined further, whereas hesperidin increased to its highest level.

The exclusive use of T2R14 in this study warrants further justification. T2R14 is one of the most broadly tuned bitter taste receptors in the human TAS2R family, with an exceptionally wide ligand spectrum that encompasses diverse chemical scaffolds including flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, and synthetic bitter compounds (Karaman et al., 2016). Structural studies have revealed that T2R14 possesses a relatively large and flexible binding pocket, enabling it to accommodate structurally distinct ligands (Yu et al., 2022). Importantly, among the four flavonoid candidates identified in our study, T2R14 is the only receptor that has documented or predicted interactions with all of them, whereas other receptors such as T2R7, T2R10, and T2R39 show limited and incomplete coverage. This receptor-ligand correspondence makes T2R14 the most rational and representative choice for our initial docking analysis. However, we acknowledge that bitterness perception is inherently polypharmacological, and different compounds may activate distinct receptor combinations. Further studies involving multiple receptors (e.g., T2R7, T2R10, and T2R39) and functional validation will be necessary to fully elucidate the receptor-level mechanisms.

Conclusion

In this study, the fermentation-dependent changes in sensory attributes, non-volatile metabolites, flavonoid profiles, and bitterness-related mechanisms of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea were systematically investigated. Fermentation significantly reshaped the taste profile, characterized by reduced bitterness and astringency and enhanced umami and kokumi, particularly at the PFT. Widely targeted metabolomics identified 1799 non-volatile metabolites, while targeted flavonoid analysis revealed 102 flavonoid metabolites with dynamic stage-dependent redistribution.

Flavonoid metabolism was markedly remodeled during fermentation. SFT promoted flavonoid accumulation, whereas FFT and PFT induced progressive transformation and degradation of flavonoid metabolites through pathways associated with flavonoid biosynthesis, flavonoid degradation, and phenylpropanoid metabolism. Correlation analysis further demonstrated strong associations between flavonoid metabolites and bitterness-related sensory responses. Molecular docking combined with metabolite profiling suggested that catechin, (−)-epicatechin, isoorientin, and hesperidin may contribute to bitterness perception through interactions with the T2R14 receptor, although further functional validation is required. These findings provide new insights into the chemical basis of bitterness attenuation and offer a theoretical foundation for optimizing fermentation processes to improve the sensory quality and functional value of Eucommia ulmoides dark tea.
