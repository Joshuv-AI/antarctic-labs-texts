Combined Arthroscopic Assisted Lower Trapezius Tendon Transfer and Achilles Tendon Allograft plus Superior Capsular Reconstruction using Long Head Biceps Autograft for Massive Irreparable Rotator Cuff Posterosuperior Tears: The “Pull-Over” Technique
Authors: Athanasios Kalogeropoulos, Timnit Tekie, Ali Hassan, Richard Dimock, Mohamed Imam, A. Ali Narvani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Massive, irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears, particularly those involving the infraspinatus, remain challenging to manage. In recent years, superior capsular reconstruction (SCR) and lower trapezius transfer (LTT) have been two techniques that have gained popularity. Superior capsular reconstruction aims to restore superior glenohumeral stability, but on its own, it may not restore the dynamic anteroposterior force couple, particularly in cases with significant infraspinatus dysfunction. Lower trapezius tendon transfer provides dynamic external rotation and replicates the posterior force couple by providing a line of pull that mimics native infraspinatus, but it may not restore superior stability, especially in early abduction. We describe a combined technique integrating arthroscopically assisted LTT with SCR using the long head of the biceps tendon as a biological graft. This method seeks to combine the static stability benefits of SCR with the dynamic rotational advantages of LTT, in a cost-effective and reproducible approach.

Despite significant advancements in surgical techniques and arthroscopic technology, certain massive rotator cuff tears remain irreparable, presenting a considerable challenge.1, 2, 3 Current treatment strategies, including anterior deltoid rehabilitation, debridement with or without long head of the biceps (LHB) tenotomy, tuberoplasty, partial repair, interval slides, tendon transfers, superior capsular reconstruction (SCR), and patch augmentation, all have inherent limitations and variable outcomes.1, 2, 3

Introduced by Mihata et al.4 in 2012, SCR aims to restore superior glenohumeral stability using a fascia lata autograft. While effective, it is associated with donor site morbidity. To mitigate this, acellular dermal matrix (ADM) grafts were later adopted, particularly in Western practice.5,6 However, ADM use presents challenges, including high cost and inconsistent graft integration.1,5,7,8

The LHB tendon has emerged as an alternative autologous graft, offering several advantages: no additional donor site, cost-effectiveness, and retained vascularity when left attached to the glenoid, potentially improving biological incorporation and graft healing.9,10

While SCR effectively restores superior stability, it may not address deficient posterior force couples, particularly in cases with infraspinatus dysfunction or advanced fatty infiltration.11,12 This may partially explain the variable graft healing observed in imaging-based studies.1,7,8

Arthroscopically assisted lower trapezius tendon transfer (ALTT), described by Elhassan et al.,13 offers a dynamic external rotation vector closely replicating the infraspinatus. However, ALTT alone may not reliably restore superior stability in early abduction. Recent biomechanical studies have illustrated that SCR is more effective than ALTT at 0° and 30° of abduction in improving range of motion, minimizing superior humeral head translation, and reducing subacromial peak pressures.11,12 When combined, SCR and ALTT appear to provide complementary static and dynamic stabilization, yielding superior biomechanical performance compared to either procedure alone.11,12,14,15

In this report, we describe a combined technique integrating ALTT with SCR using the LHB as a biological graft. This method seeks to combine the static stability benefits of SCR with the dynamic rotational advantages of ALTT in a cost-effective and reproducible approach.

Patient Positioning and Setup

The procedure is performed under general anaesthesia with an interscalene block. The patient is positioned in the beach-chair orientation. A wide sterile field is prepared, exposing the posterior-lateral shoulder, medial scapular border, spine, and inferior angle for tendon harvest. The operative arm is placed in a standard pneumatic limb positioner to allow controlled adduction when harvesting the graft and external rotation at the end when suturing the allograft to the lower trapezius (Fig 1).Fig 1Patient positioning for right shoulder surgery showing the operative arm secured in a pneumatic arm holder. Lower Trapezius Harvest

Anatomic landmarks are marked on the skin, including the medial border of the scapula, the lower trapezius origin (T4-T12), and insertion (along the medial 2-3 cm of the scapular spine). The operative arm is positioned in approximately 60° of forward flexion to improve access and facilitate identification of the lower trapezius. A horizontal incision measuring 5 to 6 cm is made roughly 1 cm inferior to the scapular spine, starting from the medial border of the scapula and extending laterally (Fig 2). Dissection is carried through the subcutaneous layers and fascial plane to expose the lower trapezius. The lateral edge of the muscle is typically identifiable by a small triangular fat pad situated medially. Blunt dissection is used to mobilize the muscle belly, allowing for visualization and isolation of the tendon. The tendinous portion is followed to its insertion on the medial aspect of the scapular spine and is sharply released from the bone. Dissection is then extended medially along the superior border of the tendon to define the interval between the lower and middle trapezius. Caution is exercised in this region to protect the spinal accessory nerve, which lies within the deep fascial layer approximately 2 cm medial to the scapular border. While superficial separation between the trapezial layers is typically safe, deeper dissection requires meticulous technique to avoid neural injury. Once the tendon is adequately mobilized, 2 No. 2 ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (FiberWire 2; Arthrex) sutures are placed in Krakow fashion along the free end. Mobility and excursion of the tendon are confirmed with gentle traction (Figs 3, 4). Arthroscopy and Debridement

Standard posterior, anterior, anterosuperior, and lateral portals are created. Diagnostic arthroscopy of the glenohumeral joint is performed. A bursectomy is carried out, and if required, acromioplasty is performed. The greater tuberosity (GT) footprint is prepared.

Superior Capsule Reconstruction With the LHB

With the scope in the posterior port, after confirming structural integrity of the LHB, No. 2 ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (FiberWire 2; Arthrex) sutures are passed through it using a ratchet-loop technique approximately 4.5 to 5 cm distal to its insertion. A tenotomy is performed roughly 1 cm distal to the distal-most suture. All 4 suture limbs are then secured into a 5.5-mm ReelX STT anchor (Stryker) and implanted at the center of the GT footprint (Fig 5). The anchor’s incremental tensioning mechanism, which involves its expansion, allows for adequate tensioning.Fig 5Anterolateral schematic view of the right shoulder showing transposition of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) for superior capsular reconstruction (SCR). The LHBT is detached approximately 4 cm distal to its glenoid insertion and fixed into the center of the greater tuberosity using a suture anchor to restore superior glenohumeral stability.

Allograft Preparation

The Achilles tendon allograft is prepared concurrently on a sterile back table during lower trapezius harvest. The thick distal end is reinforced with two No. 2 ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene sutures in a Krakow whipstitch, serving as the primary fixation for subacromial anchoring. One surface is marked as dorsal to ensure correct superior orientation during implantation (Fig 6). Two different-colored Krakow sutures are used to differentiate medial and lateral graft sides and prevent twisting.Fig 6Preparation of the Achilles tendon allograft on the back table. Arthroscopic Allograft Passage and Fixation

The infraspinatus fascia is incised through the medial incision to expose the muscle and establish a graft passage. A large clamp is inserted via the anterolateral portal and advanced posteromedially toward the medial wound.

With the scope in the lateral port, the Krakow sutures from the thick end of the graft are retrieved using the grasper in the anterosuperior port and pulled over (“pullover technique”) into the joint (Figs 7, 8). Tension is maintained on the sutures through the anterolateral portal, guiding the graft into the subacromial space. Graft orientation is confirmed using the marked dorsal surface and color-coded sutures to prevent rotation. With the scope in the posterior port, the graft is fixed to the GT using 2 knotless 4.5-mm anchors (ReelX; Stryker) inserted anteromedially and anterolaterally (Fig 8). The anchors’ incremental tensioning mechanism aids the passages and the “pulling over” of the graft into the desired location. Additionally, the 2 limbs of No. 2 ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene sutures are first passed through the Achilles tendon allograft using a standard rotator cuff suture passer. The suture limbs are then secured through a knotless 4.5-mm anchor (ReelX; Stryker), which is inserted posterolaterally into the GT to provide additional stability to the allograft (Fig 8).Fig 7Posterior schematic view of the right shoulder illustrating advancement of the prepared Achilles tendon allograft into the subacromial space. Lower Trapezius–Allograft Attachment

With the arm positioned in 30° of external rotation and neutral abduction (Fig 9), the proximal end of the Achilles tendon allograft is secured to the mobilized lower trapezius. The thinner portion of the graft is longitudinally split into 2 limbs (Fig 10). These limbs are passed through a preprepared slit in the lower trapezius, then turned back on themselves and independently sutured to the superior and inferior borders of the muscle. The limbs are then further secured to the more lateral portion of the allograft, effectively enveloping the lower trapezius between the 2 limbs and ensuring balanced soft tissue fixation (Figs 11, 12). Rehabilitation

The shoulder is immobilized in a custom external rotation brace with a 30° abduction pillow for 6 to 8 weeks. At 6 to 8 weeks, patients begin active-assisted range-of-motion exercises. Return to full activity is permitted around 6 months postoperatively, based on functional recovery and clinical evaluation.

Discussion

Despite advances in arthroscopic techniques, irreparable rotator cuff tears remain a significant clinical challenge. Two surgical strategies that have gained traction over the past decade are SCR and arthroscopically assisted lower trapezius tendon transfer (LTT).16 SCR was first introduced by Mihata et al.4 in 2012 using a fascia lata autograft to restore superior glenohumeral stability. Although effective, this method is limited by donor site morbidity. To mitigate this, Hirahara et al.5 and Burkhart et al.6 popularized the use of ADM grafts. However, ADM use introduces concerns regarding cost and inconsistent healing at the graft-bone interface.7,8 Healing outcomes remain variable, with graft failure rates reported between 0% and 55%.17,18

SCR alone may be insufficient in cases with infraspinatus dysfunction, where disruption of the transverse force couple leads to unbalanced joint forces and compromised graft integrity. Biomechanical studies also suggest that SCR may be less effective at 60° of abduction due to graft laxity and reduced resistance to superior migration.12

Tendon transfers, such as LTT, provide dynamic restoration of the posterior force couple and have shown biomechanical superiority to latissimus dorsi transfer in replicating the native infraspinatus line of pull.13,19 Elhassan et al.20,21 introduced an Achilles tendon–augmented LTT technique that has shown promising clinical outcomes. However, isolated LTT may not fully restore superior stability, particularly in early abduction.

Recent biomechanical evidence supports a combined approach, showing that SCR and LTT together improve glenohumeral stability, reduce subacromial contact pressures, and enhance range of motion more effectively than either technique alone.12 Clinical series report high graft healing rates and improved patient-reported outcomes with the combined construct.14

In this technique, we integrate arthroscopically assisted LTT with SCR using the LHB tendon as an autograft. The technique requires a structurally intact LHB and reparable subscapularis tendon. If the LHB is absent or severely degenerated, or if the subscapularis is irreparable, the procedure is contraindicated. Additionally, careful tenotomy and rerouting of the LHB are needed to mitigate the risk of persistent anterior shoulder pain.

This report outlines a reproducible technique combining SCR and LTT using the LHB, aiming to restore both static and dynamic stability in patients with massive, irreparable posterosuperior cuff tears.
