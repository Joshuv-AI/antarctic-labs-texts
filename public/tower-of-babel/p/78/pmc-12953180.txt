Managing High‐Risk Drug Interactions With Nirmatrelvir/Ritonavir: Experience From a Dedicated Pharmacology Advice Service
Authors: Youssef Libiad, Bénédicte Franck, Camille Tron, Sébastien Lalanne, Christelle Boglione‐Kerrien, Fabrice Taïeb, Marie‐Clémence Verdier, Florian Lemaitre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNirmatrelvir boosted with ritonavir is a key antiviral against COVID‐19, yet ritonavir's potent CYP3A4 inhibition exposes patients to numerous drug–drug interactions. To make prescribing safer, a national proactive system providing expert pharmacology advice was implemented in France. This retrospective study reports the experience of the pharmacology department at Rennes University Hospital, which provided these expert services from February 2022 to December 2024. We evaluated 455 consecutive patients (median age 75 years, median eight co‐medications per patient, 3813 prescription lines). Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir was ultimately contra indicated in 26 patients. Among the 429 eligible patients (3582 prescription lines), 86.7% (372/429) had at least one clinically relevant drug interaction; 60.8% (261/429) had ≥ 2; 30.4% (130/429) had ≥ 3. Among the 831 interacting medication lines, the main drug classes involved: statins (17.7%, 147/831), immunosuppressants (12.0%, 100/831), and direct oral anticoagulants (10.5%, 87/831), with the drug mix evolving over time from transplant‐related immunosuppressants in 2022 to statin agents in 2023–2024. Expert review identified options to navigate initial contraindications in 13.5% (58/429) of patients. Despite a very high prevalence of potential clinically relevant DDIs, the service supported prescribers’ decision‐making to consider nirmatrelvir/ritonavir in most evaluated patients, primarily via conservative co‐medication management: continue unchanged 77.2% (2764/3582), temporary interruption 13.8% (493/3582), dose adjustment 5.8% (208/3582), alternative therapy (117/3582); and via targeted monitoring: clinical monitoring 45.9% (197/429) and therapeutic drug monitoring 14.2% (61/429). As a retrospective service evaluation, these findings are hypothesis‐generating and emphasize a scalable model of proactive clinical pharmacology support to secure interaction‐prone therapies in aging, polymedicated populations.Keywords: anti‐infective, drug–drug interaction, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir is a protease‐inhibiting antiviral indicated for the treatment of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection in patients at high‐risk of progressing to a severe form of the disease [1]. Nirmatrelvir, the active component, is combined with ritonavir, which acts as a cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A) inhibitor and slows its metabolism [2], thereby allowing twice‐daily administration. This therapeutic combination received marketing authorization in Europe on January 28, 2022, based on a pivotal trial that showed an 89% reduction in the risk of progressing to a severe form of COVID‐19 compared with placebo [3]. Subsequent studies confirmed the efficacy and favorable safety profile of this treatment [4, 5].

However, because ritonavir acts as a potent, time‐dependent inhibitor of CYP3A4, it raises an increased risk of drug–drug interactions (DDIs). Indeed, it can promote the accumulation of numerous co‐medications metabolized by CYP3A4, potentially leading to severe adverse effects [1]. In addition, ritonavir also inhibits other enzymes such as CYP2D6 and membrane transporters like P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), while it can induce other CYP subtypes (including CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19) and some UDP‐glucuronyl transferases [2]. Conversely, the presence of strong CYP3A inducers can significantly lower nirmatrelvir concentrations, resulting in a risk of reduced virologic response. Moreover, the risk of DDIs is even higher in patients taking multiple medications (polypharmacy). Notably, patients at increased risk of severe COVID‐19 often present with comorbidities requiring various drugs to manage their underlying medical conditions [6]. Consequently, clinicians were concerned about using nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, increasing the risk that this drug, despite its proven effect on hard clinical endpoints (hospitalization and death), would be under‐prescribed, thereby representing a loss of chance for high‐risk COVID‐19 patients.

Therefore, the French Society of Pharmacology and Therapeutics (SFPT) has published guidelines to assist with this treatment prescription [7]. Nonetheless, managing these interactions remains complex, especially in patients taking multiple medications, and requires an in‐depth assessment that often calls on clinical pharmacology expertise. In accordance with the product's summary of characteristics, a multidisciplinary approach involving physicians and pharmacology experts is strongly recommended to evaluate DDI risks and propose appropriate therapeutic adjustments [1]. To that end, in France, the General Directorate of Health (DGS), in collaboration with the SFPT, the network of regional pharmacovigilance centers, and the network of pharmacology laboratories, has implemented a proactive system providing expert advice to facilitate and secure the prescription of nirmatrelvir/ritonavir [8]. In this retrospective descriptive analysis, we report the experience of the pharmacology department at Rennes University Hospital, which provides these expert services nationwide and offers expert opinions on managing DDIs related to nirmatrelvir/ritonavir.

Materials and Methods

We conducted a retrospective observational service evaluation of all expert opinions regarding DDIs involving nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, issued by the pharmacology department of the University Hospital of Rennes in France between February 2022 and December 2024. This period corresponds to the introduction and widespread use of nirmatrelvir/ritonavir for the treatment of COVID‐19 in patients at high‐risk of developing a severe form of the disease. All patients for whom a pharmacology expert opinion was provided concerning a prescription review of nirmatrelvir/ritonavir were included. The requests for expert opinions were made by telephone, email, or via a tele‐expertise platform. Data were systematically collected and documented by clinical pharmacologists responsible for assessing prescriptions, then recorded in a secure file. The collected data included: demographic and clinical characteristics (age, sex, renal function estimated by the glomerular filtration rate, major comorbidities), prescription information (treatment start date, intended dosage, name of the requesting department or healthcare professional), concomitant medications (pharmacological class, dosage, mode of administration), and the expert opinions provided by the pharmacologists.

Healthcare professionals requested these opinions before initiating treatment, allowing clinical pharmacologists either to contraindicate the antiviral in cases of major risk or to propose therapeutic recommendations to manage co‐medication and limit potential adverse effects. The recommendations issued were then categorized as follows: continue without modification (no significant risk), dosage adjustment (dose modification), temporary discontinuation (temporary cessation of a high‐risk concomitant treatment), or change of treatment (replacement by a therapeutic alternative). Close clinical monitoring and/or therapeutic drug monitoring could also be recommended. To assess potential DDIs with nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, pharmacologists drew upon scientific publications, the product's summary of characteristics, guidelines from the SFPT [7], and their own expertise to propose the most individualized recommendations possible. Results

A total of 455 patients were identified for whom a nirmatrelvir/ritonavir prescription review was performed, corresponding to 3813 prescription lines analyzed. The median age was 75 years [65–85], the female‐to‐male ratio was 1.27, and the median glomerular filtration rate was 77 mL/min [55–90]. The median number of concomitant medications per patient was 8 [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Among these patients, 26 (approximately 6%) were deemed to be ineligible to receive the antiviral: 19 (73.1%) because of major non‐mitigable DDIs (i.e., anticancer and antiepileptic drugs), 5 (19.2%) due to severe renal impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min), and 2 (7.7%) because of the absence of an indication for treatment (symptom onset > 5 days).

Of the 429 patients ultimately deemed eligible (corresponding to 3582 prescription lines), 86.7% (372/429) were likely to have at least one clinically significant DDI with nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, of whom 60.8% (261/429) had at least two DDIs and 30.4% (130/429) had at least three. Among the 831 interacting medication lines, the most frequent interactions involved statins (17.7%, 147/831), followed by immunosuppressants (12.0%, 100/831) and direct oral anticoagulants (10.5%, 87/831) (Table 1). However, the distribution varied by year: in 2022, immunosuppressants, particularly tacrolimus and mycophenolic acid, were predominant, whereas in 2023 and 2024, statins (especially atorvastatin) were most frequently involved (Table S1). Furthermore, at the patient level, 13.5% (58/429) had ≥ 1 initial contraindication. Across the 64 contraindication events in these patients (including four patients with two and 1 with three), expert review addressed issues most commonly involving simvastatin (23.44%, 15/64), amiodarone (12.50%, 8/64), and silodosin (7.81%, 5/64) (Table 2).

Across co‐medication lines, expert recommendations were as follows: continuation of treatment without modification in 77.2% (2764/3582) of cases, temporary interruption in 13.8% (493/3582), dosage adjustment in 5.8% (208/3582), and treatment change in 3.3% (117/3582). Among the concomitant medications requiring temporary interruption, atorvastatin was the most frequently affected (18.7%, 92/493), followed by tacrolimus (8.9%, 44/493) and mycophenolic acid (8.5%, 42/493). Dosage adjustments mainly concerned bisoprolol (27.9%; 58/208), amlodipine (17.3%, 36/208), and risperidone (13.5%, 28/208), while treatment change was particularly recommended for apixaban (50.4%, 59/117), rivaroxaban (15.4%, 18/117), and oxycodone (12.8%, 15/117) (Table S2). In addition, 45.9% (197/429) of patients were recommended for close clinical monitoring, and therapeutic drug monitoring was advised for 14.2% (61/429) of cases.

Regarding the sources of requests for expert opinions, most originated from hospital specialists in 2022 (70.8%, 87/123), whereas an increasing proportion of general practitioners requested this service in 2023 (45.2%, 101/224) and 2024 (50.0%, 54/108), reflecting a shift in the nature of requests (Figure 2). Lastly, the use of a tele‐expertise platform introduced in 2023 rose from 5.8% (13/224) that year to 24.1% (26/108) in 2024.

Discussion

This retrospective service evaluation provides real‐world, hypothesis‐generating evidence that a dedicated on‐demand clinical pharmacology advisory service can make prescribing nirmatrelvir/ritonavir feasible for most high‐risk polymedicated patients. Despite the high‐risk of DDIs, 94% of evaluated patients were deemed eligible to receive nirmatrelvir/ritonavir after expert review, indicating the feasibility of identifying a prescribing pathway in most cases. A small subset of patients remained ineligible, reflecting boundary conditions where prescribing was not pursued within our service. Apart from severe renal impairment and the absence of an indication for treatment, ineligibility cases involved complex situations: concomitant medications that could not be interrupted or modified (i.e., anticancer drugs), prolonged enzymatic induction by certain antiepileptics, or the presence of multiple significant DDIs rendering prescription management unfeasible.

Among the patients ultimately deemed eligible, potential clinically significant DDIs were identified in 86.7%, one‐third of whom had three or more interactions, and we provided management recommendations intended to mitigate these risks. These figures are consistent with those of a cohort study reporting that 70% of patients had more than one interaction, 82% of whom were older adults [9]. The high rate of DDIs observed can be explained by a predominantly older, polypharmacy‐prone population. Indeed, in a prospective cohort of hospitalized older adults taking five or more medications, the prevalence of a potential DDI mediated by hepatic cytochrome enzymes reached 80%, rising to 100% for patients taking 20 or more medications [10].

Statins, particularly atorvastatin, were most frequently implicated in DDIs, consistent with North American data indicating that this is the medication most likely to interact with nirmatrelvir/ritonavir among the 100 most prescribed concomitant treatments [11]. Immunosuppressants, such as tacrolimus and mycophenolate, also generated a large number of requests. Global pharmacovigilance data, including data from France, show that tacrolimus is most often involved in serious or even fatal DDIs with nirmatrelvir/ritonavir [12, 13]. Our expert opinions managed these interactions by suspending the immunosuppressant, with a priori dose adjustments followed by a posteriori readjustments based on therapeutic drug monitoring [14, 15]. Requests concerning these medications predominated in 2022 but became fewer in 2023 and 2024, likely due to the dissemination of specific recommendations from the SFPT regarding immunosuppressants and the acculturation process of transplant specialists to manage these DDIs independently of a clinical pharmacologist [16].

Moreover, among eligible patients, 15% had initial contraindications for which expert review suggested management options, outlining a feasible prescribing pathway for nirmatrelvir/ritonavir in patients at risk for severe disease if untreated. These observations echo those of another retrospective study, in which 14.6% of patients who received a nirmatrelvir/ritonavir prescription had contraindications related to DDI [17]. The most frequently contraindicated drugs during nirmatrelvir/ritonavir treatment were simvastatin and amiodarone. Given the short duration of antiviral therapy, these medications can likely be discontinued temporarily without harming patients. Although some authors recommend substituting simvastatin with other statins to limit interactions, data indicate a non‐negligible risk of increased plasma levels with other statins such as atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, or pravastatin [18]. Amiodarone is one of the situations for which nirmatrelvir/ritonavir was initially contraindicated because of toxicity risks related to its cardiac effects and very long half‐life. However, an experience suggests that this interaction could be managed by discontinuing amiodarone during antiviral treatment, provided close monitoring is performed [19].

The shift in where expert opinion requests originated, particularly the notable increase in inquiries from general practitioners in 2023 and 2024, suggests that information is gradually spreading beyond tertiary centers, allowing more prescribers to integrate interaction analysis into their practice. This trend also reflects growing trust in the expert opinion system and the need for structured support to avoid complex medication‐related complications that are often difficult to manage in primary care. Access to the tele‐expertise platform has played a key role in this shift: by providing the option of quickly obtaining a specialized opinion remotely, general practitioners are more inclined to prescribe nirmatrelvir/ritonavir safely. This is particularly relevant as prescribing nirmatrelvir/ritonavir often takes place in an outpatient setting initiated by the primary care physician. According to the FDA, 74% of nirmatrelvir/ritonavir prescriptions were made by primary care physicians [20].

One of the major strengths of this specialized service is the individualized support it provides, allowing recommendations to be tailored to each patient's characteristics (comorbidities, concomitant treatments, etc.). This service context‐driven approach goes beyond more standardized tools such as automated DDI checkers, which, although useful, remain static tools that require independent interpretation by healthcare professionals. Comparative studies have shown significant variability among different DDI checkers, with low concordance regarding the detection and severity rating of interactions [21, 22]. Moreover, several studies emphasize that these tools can generate numerous low‐relevance alerts, potentially leading to alert overrides [23]. To address the well‐known inconsistency of standardized DDI checkers, our service standardizes recommendations through a fixed evidence backbone (SmPC, SFPT, primary PK/PD literature) and a structured template with an operational severity‐to‐action mapping. The experience gained in managing DDIs related to nirmatrelvir/ritonavir highlights the importance of a proactive strategy to support prescribing, especially as managing these interactions can be complex and may involve multiple actions on multiple drugs. Our study underscores the critical role of pharmacologists’ core expertise in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics for managing complex DDI scenarios on a patient‐by‐patient basis. It also paves the way for a new position for clinical pharmacologists at the interface between the pharmacology department and frontline clinical services. Building on this foundation, an expert opinion service could be usefully extended to other high‐risk drugs prescribed to polymedicated patients, particularly as new complex molecules are introduced to the market. For replication and scale‐up, Box 1 summarizes core workflows and operational safeguards of the centralized DDI advice service. Finally, it would be worthwhile to expand these services in the form of occasional consultations with specialized pharmacologists, via a tele‐expertise platform accessible remotely, allowing any physician or pharmacist, whether in a hospital or community setting, to request expert opinion that would help ensure safer treatment management and promote continuity of care.

Our study has limitations inherent to retrospective observational designs: (i) unmeasured confounding and case‐mix differences; (ii) selection bias toward clinicians who sought expert advice; (iii) potential misclassification of concomitant medications or interaction severity across “prescription lines”; (iv) missing data for some clinical variables; (v) temporal bias as indications, variants, and prescriber acculturation evolved from 2022 to 2024; (vi) limited generalizability outside similar healthcare systems; and (vii) the absence of longitudinal follow‐up and a control group precludes inference about the impact of our recommendations on DDI or adverse event incidence, or other clinical outcomes. Our quantitative findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating and as evidence of feasibility.
