Synergistic integration of flexible bioelectroactive nanofibrous conduits and electrical stimulation for accelerated peripheral nerve regeneration
Authors: Rong Cheng, Meng Li, Jianming Wang, Lu Han, Wei Cui, Yanfeng Xi, Lingli Guo, Yukun Luo, Ride Wang, Shengbo Sang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRepair of peripheral nerve injuries remains a major clinical challenge, motivating the development of bioelectroactive nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) capable of reconstructing the structural and electrophysiological microenvironment required for effective regeneration. Here, we report flexible bioelectroactive nerve guidance conduits (CPC-NGCs) that integrate mechanical compliance with efficient bioelectrical signal transmission, address challenges associated with the repair of peripheral nerve defects. These conduits are fabricated from chitosan, polyethylene oxide, and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) via electrospinning, forming a conductive nanofibrous network with a biomimetic fibrous architecture that supports peripheral nerve regeneration. By systematically regulating CNTs content, the hydrophilicity, conductivity, and mechanical properties of the conduits were optimized to achieve stable electrical performance and tissue adaptability. In vitro studies demonstrated that CPC-NGCs promoted PC12 cell viability, neurite extension, and neuronal differentiation. Electrical stimulation (ES) further enhanced neurite alignment and maturation-related marker expression. Moreover, the combination of CPC-NGCs and ES promoted Schwann cell elongation and maturation while modulating macrophage polarization toward a pro-regenerative M2 phenotype, thereby establishing a favorable microenvironment for nerve repair. In a 10 mm rat sciatic nerve defect model, electrically activated CPC-NGCs significantly enhanced axonal regeneration and remyelination, resulting in improved electrophysiological performance, sensory recovery, and motor functional restoration. Histological analyses further confirmed the reconstruction of structurally mature nerve tissue with reduced fibrosis. These findings demonstrate that the synergistic integration of biomimetic conductive conduits and electrical stimulation effectively promotes peripheral nerve regeneration through coordinated regulation of neuronal differentiation, Schwann cell maturation, and immune microenvironment remodeling.Keywords: Peripheral nerve regeneration, Nerve guidance conduit, Electrospun nanofibers, Conductive materials, Electrical stimulation

Introduction

Nerve injury repair remains a persistent challenge in the field of regenerative medicine, particularly in cases of nerve defects where endogenous repair capacity is severely limited, resulting in incomplete functional recovery [1,2]. Although autologous nerve grafting is currently regarded as the clinical standard, it is associated with inherent limitations [3,4]. Factors such as donor site morbidity, limited availability of donor nerves, and geometric mismatch significantly compromise its clinical applicability and long-term efficacy [5]. These limitations underscore the need for engineered strategies capable of creating a permissive microenvironment for guided and functional nerve regeneration [6,7].

Tissue-engineered nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) have emerged as a promising alternative by providing physical guidance and spatial confinement for regenerating axons [8,9]. Among the design parameters of effective NGCs, biomimetic micro/nano-topographical cues have been recognized as critical regulators of axonal elongation and Schwann cell migration, reflecting the complex fibrillar architecture of native peripheral nerves [10,11]. Interwoven micro- and nanostructures can mimic the fibrillar architecture of native nerve tissue, guiding axonal elongation and Schwann cell migration [12,13]. Electrospinning, in particular, enables the fabrication of fibrous matrices with controlled alignment and scale, closely resembling the extracellular matrix (ECM) while offering structural continuity and high surface-area-to-volume ratios conducive to nerve repair [14,15]. Nevertheless, while topographical guidance effectively directs axonal growth, it alone is insufficient to recapitulate the complex functional microenvironment required for robust and synchronized nerve regeneration [16,17].

Beyond structural organization, peripheral nerves are intrinsically electrically active tissues, in which bioelectric signals play a central role in axonal outgrowth, myelination, and functional maturation [[18], [19], [20]]. Electrical stimulation (ES) has been shown to enhance neurite extension and accelerate nerve regeneration, particularly when delivered through conductive substrates that enable localized signal transmission [21]. In addition, ES can further enhance nerve repair by remodeling the regenerative microenvironment through mechanisms such as promoting Schwann cell maturation and remyelination, as well as inducing macrophage polarization toward the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype [22]. Accordingly, the incorporation of electrical conductivity into NGCs has attracted increasing attention as a means to couple structural guidance with functional stimulation [23]. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs), owing to their one-dimensional morphology and exceptional electrical properties, offer an attractive strategy for constructing conductive networks within polymeric scaffolds [24]. However, integrating CNTs into soft nerve conduits remains challenging, as excessive loading or improper dispersion may compromise mechanical compliance and cytocompatibility, highlighting the necessity for precise compositional and structural control [25,26].

The mechanical flexibility of NGCs is a prerequisite for successful clinical translation [27]. Peripheral nerves experience continuous tensile, compressive, and bending deformations during physiological movement [28]. Rigid or mechanically mismatched conduits can induce chronic inflammation, impede axonal regeneration, or result in conduit failure [29,30]. Thus, an effective NGC must simultaneously exhibit sufficient flexibility to accommodate dynamic deformation and adequate radial strength to maintain lumen patency, preserving a stable regenerative niche [[31], [32], [33]].

Herein, we report a flexible bioelectroactive nerve guidance conduit designed to promote peripheral nerve regeneration through the synergistic integration of biomimetic nanofibrous architecture, electrical conductivity, and exogenous ES (Fig. 1). This system was fabricated by electrospinning a homogeneous chitosan/polyethylene oxide/carbon nanotube (CS/PEO/CNTs) composite to generate a highly interconnected conductive network with favorable mechanical compliance. By systematically optimizing CNTs content, an appropriate balance among structural flexibility, cytocompatibility, and electrical performance was achieved. We further investigated the biological effects of the This system combined with ES on neuronal differentiation, Schwann cell maturation, and macrophage polarization. In vivo evaluation using a rat sciatic nerve defect model demonstrated that the electrically activated CPC-NGCs significantly enhanced axonal regeneration, remyelination, and functional recovery. These findings suggest that the bioelectroactive conduit not only provides structural guidance and electrical signaling support, but also promotes the establishment of a pro-regenerative cellular microenvironment, highlighting its potential as a promising strategy for peripheral nerve repair.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of CS/PEO/CNTs nanofibrous membranes

Chitosan (CS; MW = 85,000, >95% deacetylation) and polyethylene oxide (PEO; MW = 100,000) were dissolved in a 2% (v/v) acetic acid solution to yield a base solution of 2 wt% CS and 5 wt% PEO. Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, Macklin, China; diameter: 20 ± 3 nm, length: 3 ± 2 μm) were subsequently dispersed into this base solution via ultrasonication to prepare electrospinning precursors with CNTs concentrations of 0, 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 wt%. Subsequently, the homogeneous solutions were loaded into a syringe and electrospun at an applied voltage of 18 kV with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/h and a tip-to-collector distance of 15 cm in an environment maintained at 35 °C and 18-25% relative humidity. The prepared nanofiber membranes were named CPC 0, CPC 1, CPC 3 and CPC 5.

Fabrication and characterization of CPC-NGCs

Electrospun nanofibrous membranes were manually wrapped around silicone mandrels with a fixed diameter of 2 mm to form tubular structures. The wrapped constructs were then immersed in a 0.2% (w/v) gelatin solution and incubated at 25 °C for 12 h to allow partial surface fusion at the membrane seam. Subsequently, the constructs were freeze-dried to secure the tubular geometry and form a continuous, closed conduit. After lyophilization, the silicone templates were carefully removed to obtain self-supporting nanofibrous CPC-NGCs.

Morphology observation

The macroscopic and microscopic morphologies of the CPC-NGCs were characterized using digital photography and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S4800, 5 kV), respectively. Average fiber diameters were quantified using ImageJ (n ≥ 100). Additionally, the structural stability of the fibrous network was evaluated via SEM following a 3-day aqueous immersion.

Hydrophilicity

The contact angle was measured by goniometer (Shanghai Zhongchen Digital Technology Apparatus Co., Ltd., China). 1 μL of deionized water was placed on the surface of the CPC-NGCs, and the contact angle values were measured.

Nanostructures characterization

The chemical structure of the CPC-NGCs was characterized using a Bruker V70 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer(FTIR) from 500 to 3500 cm−1 with a 4 cm−1 resolution. Crystalline properties were further evaluated via a Rigaku Ultima III X-ray diffractometer(XRD) over a 2θ range of 5-50° at 10°/min and a Renishaw RM-1000 Raman spectrometer with 532 nm excitation from 300 to 3200 cm−1.

Mechanical property

Uniaxial and cyclic (100 cycles) tensile tests were conducted using a universal testing machine (Norwood 5556 Research Institute, USA) equipped with a 10 N load cell at a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min. The tensile modulus was calculated from the slope of the stress-strain curve within the linear region (0%-10% strain).

Electrochemical and conductivity

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) were performed on a CS2350 electrochemical workstation (CORRTEST, China) using a standard three-electrode configuration (CPC-NGCs working, Pt counter, Ag/AgCl reference electrode). CV was recorded from −0.6 to 0.6 V at 10 mV/s, and EIS was conducted at open circuit potential with a 5 mV amplitude from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz. The conductivity of CPC-NGCs was measured using four-point probe method (Keithley 2400, Tektronix, USA), with current-voltage (I-V) scans ranging from −1 V to 1 V.

Gene expression of neural markers

Total RNA from PC12 cells cultured on different NGCs for 3 and 7 days was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and the RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit, Takara, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using a SYBR Green-based master mix (TB Green Premix Ex Taq, Takara) on a real-time PCR system (ABI 7500, Applied Biosystems, USA). Target genes included GAP43, NGFR (nerve growth factor receptor), TUBB (β-Tubulin gene), and Nestin (a marker of neural stem/progenitor cells), with GAPDH as the housekeeping gene. All reactions were run in triplicate, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Optimization of ES intensity

To determine the optimal ES intensity for promoting neurite outgrowth and neural differentiation, PC12 cells were seeded onto CPC 3-NGCs and subjected to continuous direct-current (DC) ES using a parallel electrode configuration. Two sterile platinum electrodes were placed in parallel at both ends of the nerve conduit to generate a uniform electric field along its longitudinal axis. ES was delivered using a dual-channel electrophysiology system (RM6240, Chengdu Instrument Factory, China) operated in a voltage-controlled mode. DC electric fields of 100, 150, 200, and 250 mV/cm were applied for 1 h per day over 7 days. The electrode spacing was maintained at 10 mm throughout all experiments. The electrode spacing was maintained at 14 mm throughout all experiments. The biocompatibility of the different stimulation intensities was assessed using Live/Dead staining and CCK-8 proliferation assays on days 1, 4, and 7. To evaluate the effect of voltage on cell motility, a scratch wound healing assay was performed, and cell migration into the wound area was monitored at 0, 12, and 24 h. Following the 7-day stimulation period, FITC-phalloidin staining was conducted to visualize cell morphology, and ImageJ software was used to quantitatively analyze nuclear elongation, the percentage of neurite-bearing cells, and the average neurite length under different stimulation conditions. Neural differentiation was further evaluated via immunofluorescence staining for specific markers and expression analysis of neural-related genes. Additionally, the fluorescence intensity of the immunostained images was quantitatively analyzed.

In vitro Schwann cell behaviors and maturation assessment

Rat Schwann cells (RSC96, Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) were employed to evaluate the potential of the bioelectrical conduits combined with ES in supporting Schwann cell behavior and maturation. RSC96 cells were seeded onto the CPC-NGCs, and an exogenous DC voltage of 200 mV/cm was applied for 1 h daily over the culture period for the CPC 3+ES group. To observe cytoskeletal organization, filamentous actin (F-actin) and DAPI staining were performed on days 3 and 7 to visualize cell morphology, and ImageJ software was used to quantitatively analyze the average cell process length from at least 100 randomly selected cells per condition. Schwann cell maturation was further evaluated on days 3 and 7 via immunofluorescence staining for S100 calcium-binding protein β (S100-β) and myelin basic protein (MBP) markers, with the fluorescence intensity of the fluorescence images quantitatively analyzed using mean gray values.

In vitro immunomodulatory

RAW 264.7 cells (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) were seeded onto the CPC-NGCs and stimulated with lipopolysaccharide, LPS, at 100 ng/mL to simulate an inflammatory microenvironment. The identical daily ES protocol of 200 mV/cm for 1 h per day was implemented for the CPC 3+ES cohort. After 3 days of co-culture, the expression of inflammatory and polarization-related cytokine proteins, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-10 (IL-10), was analyzed using immunofluorescence staining, with the corresponding mean gray values quantified via ImageJ software. Additionally, the relative mRNA expression levels of M1/M2 phenotype markers, CD86 and CD206, were quantified using qRT-PCR analysis based on the 2−ΔΔCt method.

In vivo evaluation of nerve regeneration

Animal model

Adult male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (8 weeks old, 200-250 g) were used to establish a sciatic nerve defect model. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Taiyuan University of Technology in China (Approval No. 110322251100744827). Animals were housed under standard conditions and were randomly divided into five groups (n = 12 per group), an autograft group, a blank group, a CPC 0 group, a CPC 3 group, and a CPC 3+ES group.

Surgical procedures were performed under anesthesia using 2% (w/v) sodium pentobarbital solution (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). Under aseptic conditions, a 10 mm section of the sciatic nerve in the right hind limb was removed. For the NGC groups (CPC 0, CPC 3, and CPC 3+ES), the proximal and distal stumps were inserted 2 mm into each end of a 14 mm long nanofibrous NGC and secured to the epineurium using 8-0 nylon sutures. In the Autograft group, the excised 10 mm nerve segment was reversed 180° and reimplanted between the proximal and distal stumps using 8-0 nylon sutures to serve as the positive control. For the CPC + ES group, electrical stimulation was delivered using a dual-channel electrophysiological stimulation system. Needle electrodes were inserted and fixed at the proximal and distal ends of the implanted conduit to deliver electrical stimulation across the regenerating nerve segment. Based on the in vitro optimization results, stimulation parameters were set as follows: a frequency of 20 Hz, a pulse width of 0.2 ms, and an output voltage of 200 mV (device setting). Electrical stimulation was initiated immediately after surgery and subsequently applied every other day during the first two postoperative weeks, with each session lasting 30 min. This period corresponds to a critical window for early axonal sprouting and nerve bridging [19,34].

Walking track analysis

Gait analysis was performed at 4, 8, and 12 weeks post-implantation of the NGCs to assess functional recovery in all animals. The motor function of rats was evaluated using the CatWalk XT gait analysis system (Version 10.6, Noldus Information Technology, Netherlands). Each rat was allowed to freely traverse a glass walkway without external interference, and a minimum of three uninterrupted runs with consistent speed were recorded for each animal.

Electrophysiological evaluation

To evaluate nerve conduction and muscle reinnervation at 4, 8, and 12 weeks post-surgery, compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) of the gastrocnemius muscle were recorded under anesthesia. Following proximal electrical stimulation (10 mV) of the regenerated sciatic nerve, CMAP peak amplitude and latency were quantified utilizing a dual-channel electrophysiology system (RM 6240, Chengdu Instrument Factory, China).

Evaluation of muscle atrophy

To evaluate muscle atrophy at 4, 8, and 12 weeks post-surgery, bilateral gastrocnemius muscles were harvested and weighed. For histological analysis, the surgical-side muscles were transversely sectioned (5 μm, Leica, Germany) and stained with H&E and Masson's trichrome (Solarbio, China). Myofiber integrity and the degree of fibrosis were assessed by quantitatively analyzing the percentages of muscle fiber and collagen areas from light microscopy images utilizing ImageJ software.

Hot plate latency analysis

The recovery of sensory function in rats was studied using the hot plate latency (HPL) test 4, 8, and 12 weeks after surgery. The rats with wounded limbs were placed on a hot plate (56 °C) and timed until they licked their paws or leapt. Their reaction time was limited to 12 s.

Histological evaluation of regenerated nerves

To evaluate nerve regeneration at 4, 8, and 12 weeks post-surgery, the distal regenerated nerves were harvested and sectioned (5 μm). General morphology and fibrosis were assessed via H&E and Masson's trichrome staining. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining against MBP, NF-200, GAP43, and β-Tubulin was performed to evaluate remyelination and axonal regeneration, with fluorescence intensities quantified utilizing ImageJ software.

Evaluation of systemic biosafety

At 12 weeks post-surgery, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were harvested from rats in the four experimental groups for HE staining. Corresponding tissues from healthy, untreated rats were collected as the normal control group to evaluate potential systemic toxicity induced by the implanted nerve conduits.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using Student's t-test for two-group comparisons and one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's post hoc test for multiple group comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at* p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and****p < 0.0001. Each condition of the study was repeated in a minimum of three independent experiments (n = 3).

Results

Characterization of NGCs

A biomimetic CPC-NGCs were fabricated with a stable, hollow tubular structure. The dimensions (14 mm length, 2 mm inner diameter) closely match those of rat sciatic nerves [36], demonstrating favorable structural compatibility (Fig. 2A). SEM analysis revealed that the nanofibers were smooth, uniform, and randomly oriented without CNTs aggregation (Fig. 2B). The fiber diameter increased with higher CNTs content, providing sufficient surface area to facilitate cell infiltration and tissue formation within the 3D scaffold. Furthermore, the conduits exhibited favorable stability, retaining their fibrous texture and integrity even after immersion in water for 3 days (Fig. S1).

Regarding surface properties, the incorporation of CNTs resulted in a gradual decrease in hydrophilicity due to their intrinsic hydrophobic nature (Fig. 2C). Moderate hydrophobicity is crucial as it simulates the neural extracellular matrix microenvironment, thereby promoting neurite formation and extension [37]. This surface characteristic modulates protein adsorption conformation to favor specific cell adhesion [38]. While excessive hydrophobicity may inhibit early adhesion, the contact angles observed in this study (60-80°) are considered favorable for the adhesion, spreading, and differentiation of PC12 cells.

Spectroscopic analyses further confirmed the molecular interaction between the components. FTIR spectra indicated the formation of hydrogen bonding and π-π interactions between CNTs and the polymer chains, accompanied by a reduction in PEO crystallinity (Fig. 2D) [39]. The observed peak shifts and splitting in the XRD patterns demonstrated the insertion of polymer chains between CNT layers (Fig. 2E). Moreover, Raman spectroscopy confirmed the good dispersion of CNTs within the composite (Fig. 2F). This intercalation effectively prevented CNTs agglomeration and promoted a uniform nanostructure, ensuring the mechanical performance and structural integrity of the fibers.

Appropriate mechanical properties, balancing sufficient flexibility with adequate tensile strength, are critical for nerve conduits to withstand physiological stresses and support regeneration [40]. Tensile tests conducted on the composite CPC-NGCs showed that the tensile modulus increased with the CNTs content, reaching a peak value of 28.5 kPa for the CPC 3 group (Fig. 2G and H). This enhancement is attributed to the effective dispersion of CNTs, which reinforces the polymer matrix through physical entanglement and hydrogen bonding [41]. However, a slight reduction in modulus was observed at higher concentrations (CPC 5), likely due to nanotube aggregation creating microstructural defects [42]. Beyond tensile strength, all CPC-NGCs demonstrated favorable flexibility, withstanding large-angle bending without structural damage (Fig. S2). Furthermore, cyclic compression tests confirmed their outstanding mechanical durability and fatigue resistance, as the conduits maintained structural integrity without delamination under repeated deformation (Fig. S3), ensuring long-term functionality in dynamic in vivo environments.

To assess the potential of the scaffolds for electrical signal conduction, the electrochemical properties of the CPC-NGCs were evaluated. CV studies showed that CNTs incorporation significantly enhanced electrochemical activity, with increased current response and loop area, unlike the negligible activity of CS/PEO NGCs (Fig. 2I). The enhancement in charge transfer kinetics was further validated by Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS). As shown in the Nyquist plots, the addition of CNTs markedly reduced the impedance semicircle radius, indicating a significant decrease in interfacial resistance and superior ionic/electronic transport capabilities (Fig. 2J). The electrical conductivity of the NGCs increased from 1.3 mS cm−1 (CPC 0) to 6.9 mS cm−1 (CPC 5) with higher CNTs loading, creating effective pathways for endogenous signal propagation and exogenous electrical stimulation (Fig. 2K). Notably, the composite conduits demonstrated favorable electromechanical stability, maintaining sufficient conductivity to power LED bulbs even when bent at a 60° angle (Fig. S4). In conclusion, the CPC-NGCs combine biomimetic dimensions, robust mechanical properties, and superior conductivity, providing a solid foundation for their application as effective alternatives to autologous grafts in peripheral nerve repair.

In vitro biocompatibility

The fundamental biological safety of the composite NGCs was first established through hemocompatibility and cytotoxicity assessments. Hemocompatibility assays demonstrated that all CPC-NGCs exhibited hemolysis rates below the generally accepted safety limit, indicating minimal damage to red blood cells (Fig. 3A). With the increasing incorporation of CNTs, the hemolysis rate demonstrated a continuous upward trend, reaching its peak in the CPC 5 group due to the higher nanomaterial content. Nevertheless, the hemolysis rates across all groups remained below the 2% threshold for non-hemolytic materials, confirming excellent hemocompatibility (Fig. 3C). To evaluate cellular responses, cytotoxicity tests using Live/Dead staining were performed, revealing a predominance of viable (green) cells with negligible dead (red) cells across all groups (Fig. S5). By day 7, cell viability remained high, and CCK-8 assays further demonstrated that the incorporation of CNTs, particularly in the CPC 3 group, significantly promoted PC12 cell proliferation and metabolic activity compared to the blank control (Fig. 3B and D). These results confirm that the conductive CPC-NGCs provide a biocompatible substrate suitable for cell growth.

Assessment of neural differentiation potential

Neuronal differentiation was assessed by observing cell morphology. SEM images revealed that PC12 cells adhered well to the surface of the CPC-NGCs and aligned along the fiber direction (Fig. S6). Compared to the blank control group, cells on conductive NGCs displayed more developed cytoskeletal networks and evident axon elongation as early as day 4, with significant neural network formation observed by day 7 (Fig. 3B, and S7). Quantitative analysis showed that both differentiation rate and average axon length were significantly higher in conductive NGC groups than in blank or CPC 0 groups (Fig. 3E and F). Notably, the CPC 3 group showed the strongest effect, eliciting the most pronounced neurite outgrowth. This quantitative data confirms that the optimized conductivity effectively accelerates axonal extension.

At the molecular level, immunofluorescence staining was used to evaluate neuronal differentiation. As shown in Fig. 3G–I, PC12 cells cultured on conductive NGCs (CPC 3 and CPC 5) displayed pronounced positive staining for neuron-specific markers. Expression of NF200 and β-Tubulin indicated enhanced cytoskeletal organization associated with neuronal differentiation, while GAP43 reflected increased neurite growth potential, collectively supporting enhanced neuron-like differentiation of PC12 cells [43,44]. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity revealed a clear dependence of protein expression on CNTs content. As shown in Fig. S8, the CPC 3 group exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity across all markers, with a peak observed on day 7. These results indicate that a CNTs concentration of 0.3% provides an optimal conductive cue for promoting neuro-related protein expression.

These results were further validated by qRT-PCR analysis, which revealed a significant upregulation in the transcription levels of GAP43, NGFR, TUBB, and Nestin in the conductive groups (Fig. 3J). The elevated expression of Nestin and NGFR suggests that the cells maintain a neural progenitor state that is responsive to neurotrophic signals, thereby establishing a molecular foundation for subsequent maturation [45]. Collectively, these results position 0.3% CNTs as the optimal concentration for promoting differentiation, while highlighting potential inhibitory effects at 0.5% CNTs.

Antibacterial activity

Given the critical role of infection control in the long-term success of implantable devices, the antibacterial activity was evaluated against E. coli and S. aureus. The composite CPC-NGCs demonstrated a dose-dependent antimicrobial effect, where increasing CNTs content from 0% to 0.5% progressively inhibited bacterial colony formation (Fig. 3K and L). This enhanced performance is attributed to the synergistic action of the physicochemical antimicrobial properties of CNTs and the cationic charge interactions of CS, providing a safe biological environment to prevent infection during neural repair [46].

Optimization of ES parameters for neuronal differentiation

To investigate the synergistic effects of ES and conductive NGCs, PC12 cells were seeded on CPC 3-NGCs and subjected to varying voltage gradients (100-250 mV/cm) (Fig. 4A). This approach aims to mimic physiological electrical cues and may involve the activation of voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs), which have been reported to participate in regeneration-related cellular processes [47]. Before evaluating functional differentiation, the biocompatibility of ES was thoroughly assessed. Live/Dead staining images showed that PC12 cells maintained high viability across all voltage groups without significant cell death (Fig. 4B). Consistently, the stable increase in OD values recorded by the CCK-8 assay over 7 days indicated the absence of voltage-induced cytotoxicity (Fig. 4D). Regarding cell morphology and migration, cytoskeletal staining provided visual evidence of differentiation. As observed in Fig. 4b, cells in the 100 mV/cm group appeared with shorter, disorganized neurites. In contrast, the 200 mV/cm group displayed a distinct spindle-like morphology with visibly longer and denser neurite networks. Notably, the 200 mV/cm group significantly outperformed others, exhibiting the highest percentage of neurite-bearing cells and average neurite length (Fig. 4E and F). However, excessive voltage (250 mV/cm) led to a decline in these metrics. Similarly, scratch wound assays visually showed rapid closure of the wound area over 24 h (Fig. 4C), where the calculated migration area revealed that 200 and 250 mV/cm significantly enhanced motility compared to lower voltages (Fig. 4G).

Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that ES markedly enhanced the expression of the neuronal differentiation markers NF200, β-Tubulin, and GAP43, with the strongest fluorescence observed in the 200 mV/cm group (Fig. 4H–J). Quantitative fluorescence analysis further confirmed that the expression levels of these markers reached their maximum at 200 mV/cm (Fig. 4K). These findings indicate that ES promoted neuron-like differentiation and neurite outgrowth of PC12 cells. This trend was further validated by qRT-PCR analysis at the transcriptional level, where the mRNA expression of GAP43, NGFR, TUBB3, and Nestin was significantly highest in the 200 mV/cm group (Fig. S9). Collectively, these results identify 200 mV/cm as the optimal intensity for maximizing neurite outgrowth and differentiation, likely associated with ion channel activity and related electrophysiological responses [48].

In vitro evaluation of Schwann cell maturation and neuro-regenerative potential

Schwann cells play a crucial role in peripheral nerve regeneration by supporting axonal growth and remyelination [49]. To evaluate the effects of the bioelectrical conduit combined with ES on Schwann cell behavior, cytoskeletal organization and maturation-related markers were examined in RSC96 cells on days 3 and 7. F-actin staining showed that all groups exhibited good adhesion and spreading on day 3 (Fig. 5A). By day 7, RSC96 cells cultured with CPC 3 and CPC 3+ES displayed a more elongated spindle-like morphology and formed interconnected cellular networks compared with the control and CPC 0 groups. Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that conduit treatment significantly increased cell process length, with the CPC 3+ES group showing the highest value among all groups (Fig. 5D).

Immunofluorescence staining of S100-β and MBP revealed a time-dependent increase in the expression of these Schwann cell maturation markers (Fig. 5B and C). Consistently, quantitative analysis confirmed that conduit-treated groups exhibited higher S100-β and MBP expression than the control group, with the CPC 3+ES group displaying the strongest fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5E). These findings suggest that the synergistic integration of the bioelectrical conduit and ES promotes Schwann cell elongation and maturation-like phenotypes, thereby supporting a regenerative microenvironment for peripheral nerve repair.

In vitro evaluation of immunomodulatory properties

Macrophage-mediated immunomodulation is essential for establishing a favorable regenerative microenvironment following peripheral nerve injury [50]. To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of the bioelectrical conduit combined with ES, LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages were evaluated for inflammatory and polarization-related markers. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that LPS stimulation increased TNF-α expression while reducing IL-10 expression (Fig. 5F). In contrast, CPC-treated groups exhibited reduced TNF-α levels and enhanced IL-10 expression, with the CPC 3+ES group showing the lowest TNF-α and highest IL-10 fluorescence intensity. Quantitative analysis of the mean gray values further supported these observations (Fig. 5G).

The polarization state of macrophages was further assessed by qRT-PCR analysis of CD86 and CD206 expression (Fig. 5H). Compared with the LPS and conduit-only groups, the CPC 3+ES group exhibited lower CD86 expression and higher CD206 expression, indicating a shift from the M1 phenotype toward the M2 phenotype. These results suggest that the combined application of the bioelectrical conduit and ES promotes a pro-regenerative macrophage phenotype and contributes to the establishment of an immunological microenvironment conducive to peripheral nerve regeneration.

Overall, the CPC 3+ES treatment not only promoted Schwann cell elongation and maturation, but also modulated macrophage behavior by suppressing pro-inflammatory responses and enhancing M2 polarization. These findings suggest that the bioelectrical conduit combined with electrical stimulation contributes to the establishment of a pro-healing microenvironment and may facilitate peripheral nerve regeneration.

In vivo evaluation of nerve regeneration

To evaluate the peripheral nerve regeneration capacity, a 10 mm sciatic nerve defect model was established in rats and repaired using the fabricated CPC-NGCs (Fig. 6A and B). Throughout the 12-week observation period, the implanted CPC-NGCs remained structurally intact without inducing neuroma formation or inflammation, indicating favorable biocompatibility.

Functional recovery was first assessed through gait analysis. In the early stages (4 weeks), most groups exhibited severe motor deficits. However, by week 12, the autograft and CPC 3+ES groups exhibited distinct toe separation and substantially restored gait patterns, indicating improved motor function recovery (Fig. 6C and S10). In contrast, the CPC 0 and blank control groups retained a dragging gait with clustered toes, indicating persistent motor loss. Corresponding SFI measurements paralleled these morphological observations. The autograft group showed the strongest functional recovery. Importantly, the CPC 3+ES group achieved significantly higher SFI scores than the CPC 3, CPC 0, and blank groups (Fig. 6D), with values approaching those of the autograft group [51]. These findings indicate that conductive CPC-NGCs combined with electrical stimulation contribute to enhanced peripheral nerve regeneration and functional recovery.

To verify the quality of reinnervation, electrophysiological properties were examined by recording CMAPs. Representative waveforms showed that the CPC 3+ES group displayed robust signal recovery, in sharp contrast to the negligible signals in the negative control (Fig. 6E). The CPC 3+ES group achieved CMAPs amplitudes significantly higher than the CPC 3 and CPC 0 groups (Fig. 6F). Correspondingly, NCV measurements aligned with the amplitude data, with the CPC 3+ES group demonstrating conduction speeds markedly faster than non-stimulated groups and approaching the levels of the autograft group (Fig. 6G).

The efficacy of neural recovery was further corroborated by assessing the structural integrity of the target gastrocnemius muscle. Gross observation of wet weight ratios indicated that the CPC 3+ES treatment significantly minimized muscle atrophy compared to the severe wasting observed in the blank and CPC 0 groups (Fig. 6H and K and S11A). Histological assessment via H&E staining revealed that by week 12, the CPC 3+ES group maintained a nearly normal muscle architecture with uniform fiber diameter and organized alignment, resulting in a muscle fiber area percentage that was statistically superior to other experimental groups (Fig. 6I and L and S11B). Furthermore, Masson's trichrome staining demonstrated that the combined therapy effectively inhibited fibrosis. The CPC 3+ES group exhibited significantly lower collagen deposition compared to the extensive scarring seen in the injury groups, thereby preserving tissue integrity (Fig. 6J and M and S11C).

Sensory recovery was assessed via hot plate latency tests to evaluate nociceptive restoration. The Blank control consistently failed to respond within the cutoff time, indicating permanent sensory loss. In contrast, by week 12, the CPC 3+ES group exhibited rapid withdrawal reflexes, showing no significant difference compared to the Autograft group, while the CPC 3 and CPC 0 groups remained significantly slower (Fig. 6N and S11D). Collectively, these findings confirm that the synergistic combination of conductive scaffolds and ES accelerates both motor and sensory regeneration to levels comparable to autologous nerve grafting.

Histomorphological assessment

The functional regeneration and structural maturity of the sciatic nerves were evaluated through histological staining of transverse sections. H&E staining revealed that while the autograft group displayed the most mature architecture, the CPC 3+ES group exhibited the most favorable outcomes among the experimental groups. By week 12, it presented a well-organized structure characterized by distinct fascicular contours, minimized inflammation, and densely distributed axons, closely mimicking the morphology of the autograft group, which stood in sharp contrast to the disorganized, sparsely distributed fibers seen in the blank and CPC 0 groups (Fig. 7A and S12). Furthermore, Masson's trichrome staining visually demonstrated the extent of fibrosis. The blank group showed extensive blue-stained scar tissue surrounding the nerve. Conversely, the CPC 3+ES group exhibited minimal collagen deposition comparable to the autograft group, with fibrosis confined mainly to the outer perineurium, indicating that the combined strategy effectively inhibits scar formation (Fig. 7B and S13). This is critical because the conduit serves as a physical bridge, and the effective inhibition of scar formation is a prerequisite for ensuring that axons can successfully traverse the defect and grow directionally toward the distal stump.

To further investigate axonal regeneration and myelination, immunofluorescence analysis was conducted. As shown in the representative images (Fig. 7C and S14-S17), the CPC 3+ES group exhibited strong expression of key neural markers. MBP staining revealed widespread, uniform myelin structures in a characteristic ring-like pattern, while NF200 staining highlighted large, well-organized bundles of mature axons, closely resembling the maturation observed in the autograft group. Additionally, β-Tubulin and GAP43 staining indicated ongoing early axonal sprouting and significant regenerative plasticity within the CPC-NGCs [52]. As depicted in Fig. 7D, the CPC 3+ES group showed markedly higher expression of all markers compared to the CPC 3, CPC 0, and blank groups at every time point. Notably, by week 12, the CPC 3+ES group achieved expression levels comparable to those of the autograft group, confirming that the combination of conductive NGCs and ES effectively promotes structural reconstruction and protein-level regeneration.

In vivo systemic biosafety assessment

To evaluate the long-term systemic impact of the implanted nerve conduits, H&E staining was performed on major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) at 12 weeks post-surgery (Fig. S18). Histological observations revealed that the normal and autograft groups maintained intact tissue architecture and orderly cellular arrangement, serving as a healthy physiological baseline. In the blank and non-stimulated groups (CPC 0, CPC 3), mild inflammatory infiltration, slight congestion, or alveolar expansion were occasionally noted. These minor alterations were likely attributed to surgical trauma or chronic responses to the defect, yet the overall structural integrity remained preserved without necrosis or distinct fibrosis. Notably, the CPC 3+ES group displayed tissue morphology indistinguishable from the normal and autograft groups, with no significant abnormalities detected. These results collectively suggest that the CPC- NGCs and their degradation products do not induce systemic toxicity in distant organs, demonstrating a favorable biosafety profile for peripheral nerve regeneration applications.

Discussion

In recent years, a variety of NGCs have been developed to improve peripheral nerve regeneration. Biomimetic conduits based on nanofiber, microgrooved structures, and extracellular matrix-mimicking architectures have demonstrated remarkable capability in guiding axonal extension and Schwann cell migration by recreating the native topographical features of peripheral nerves [[53], [54], [55]]. However, these systems mainly provide structural guidance and often fail to reconstruct the electrophysiological microenvironment required for efficient nerve regeneration. To address this limitation, conductive NGCs incorporating conductive polymers, carbon-based nanomaterials, or metallic components have been developed to facilitate electrical signal transmission and enhance neuronal activity [56,57]. Although these conductive platforms have shown promising regenerative outcomes, challenges remain regarding the homogeneous dispersion of conductive fillers, potential cytotoxicity at high loading levels, and the trade-off between electrical conductivity and mechanical compliance. More recently, piezoelectric and wireless stimulation-based conduits have emerged as attractive alternatives because they can generate electrical cues without external wiring [58,59]. Nevertheless, their electrical output is often highly dependent on mechanical deformation, resulting in signal instability during long-term implantation. Furthermore, despite the growing sophistication of NGC design, many current strategies primarily focus on promoting neuronal growth while overlooking the coordinated regulation of Schwann cell maturation and macrophage-mediated immune responses, both of which are critical determinants of functional nerve regeneration in vivo [60,61].

Here, we developed a flexible bioelectroactive CPC-NGC composed of chitosan, polyethylene oxide, and carbon nanotubes through a simple and scalable electrospinning strategy. The CPC-NGC was designed to simultaneously integrate biomimetic architecture, electrical functionality, mechanical adaptability, and immunoregulatory capacity within a single platform. In terms of structural design, the electrospun nanofibrous network closely mimics the topological features of the native extracellular matrix, thereby constructing a biomimetic three-dimensional microenvironment. This architecture not only endows the material with excellent flexibility to accommodate the dynamic mechanical environment of peripheral nerves, but also provides a contact-guidance interface favorable for cell adhesion and migration. Accordingly, cells exhibit enhanced adhesion and alignment within this microenvironment, accompanied by oriented neurite extension along the fiber direction. This phenomenon may be attributed to the contact guidance effect induced by the nanofibrous architecture, which enhances protein adsorption and integrin-mediated focal adhesion formation, thereby promoting cytoskeletal reorganization and directing cellular migration and axonal outgrowth [62,63].

At the level of electrical functionality, the uniformly embedded CNTs form a continuous conductive network that significantly enhances electrical signal transmission within the conduit, enabling the establishment of a stable and homogeneous electroactive microenvironment under external ES. Within this microenvironment, neurons exhibit enhanced differentiation capacity, markedly extended neurite length, and accelerated axonal growth. This promotive effect can be attributed to an electrical signal amplification cascade triggered by ES. ES induces membrane depolarization, which activates voltage-gated Ca2+ channels and promotes intracellular Ca2+ influx, subsequently triggering the cascade activation of CaMKII and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways. These calcium-dependent signaling events ultimately regulate gene expression programs associated with neuronal differentiation and axonal extension [64,65].

Meanwhile, this electroactive microenvironment further regulates surrounding supporting cells and immune responses. The enhanced maturation of Schwann cells and the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype collectively indicate a shift toward a pro-regenerative local microenvironment. This coordinated regulatory effect may result from sustained modulation of the MAPK/ERK signaling axis by electrical stimulation, thereby promoting Schwann cell maturation and myelin-associated phenotypic expression. In addition, downregulation of the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α and upregulation of anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 contribute to the restoration of inflammatory balance, ultimately establishing an immunoregulatory microenvironment favorable for nerve regeneration [66,67].

Overall, the synergistic combination of biomimetic CPC conductive nerve guidance conduits and electrical stimulation promotes neuronal differentiation, Schwann cell maturation, and immune microenvironment remodeling, ultimately achieving functional nerve regeneration. However, a limitation of the present study is that the therapeutic efficacy of CPC-NGCs was evaluated only in a rat sciatic nerve defect model. Given the increased complexity of clinically relevant long-gap peripheral nerve injuries, further studies in larger-gap defects and large-animal models are required to comprehensively assess the translational potential of this bioelectrical regeneration strategy.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a flexible and bio-electroactive nerve guidance conduit (CPC-NGCs) composed of chitosan, polyethylene oxide, and carbon nanotubes via electrospinning. By integrating mechanical compliance, biomimetic fibrous topography cues, and electrical conductivity within a single platform, the conduit provides a favorable microenvironment for peripheral nerve regeneration. The construction of a biomimetic conductive nanofibrous network enables the conduit to integrate mechanical compliance with efficient bioelectrical signal transmission, facilitating the reconstruction of key structural and electrophysiological features of native peripheral nerve tissue. Systematic modulation of CNTs content enabled the optimization of hydrophilicity, electrical conductivity, and mechanical properties within a cytocompatible range, achieving a balanced material profile suitable for dynamic physiological environments. In vitro studies demonstrated that the biomimetic topography significantly enhanced PC12 cell viability and promoted neurite extension. ES further guided neurite outgrowth along the fiber axis and promoted neuronal differentiation. Importantly, the combined bioelectrical strategy also facilitated Schwann cell elongation and maturation and promoted macrophage polarization toward a pro-regenerative phenotype, contributing to the establishment of a favorable regenerative microenvironment. In vivo, electrically activated CPC 3-NGCs promoted axonal and myelin regeneration in a rat model of sciatic nerve defect, improving the recovery of motor function in rats. Histological analysis further confirmed the regeneration of structurally intact nerves, enhanced myelination, and upregulated expression of neuron-specific proteins. Collectively, these findings suggest that the synergistic integration of conductive biomimetic conduits and ES may promote peripheral nerve regeneration through coordinated regulation of neuronal, glial, and immune responses, providing a potentially promising strategy for functional nerve repair.
