Fast rehabilitation does not worsen clinical, radiological, and arthroscopic outcomes after medial meniscus posterior root repair: A retrospective comparative study
Authors: Masanori Tamura, Takayuki Furumatsu, Yusuke Yokoyama, Yuki Okazaki, Koki Kawada, Toshifumi Ozaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background/ObjectiveThe main surgical treatment for medial meniscus posterior root tear (MMPRT) has shifted from meniscectomy to meniscus repair; however, there is no clear consensus regarding the optimal postoperative management strategy after MMPRT repair. This study aimed to perform a comparative analysis of patients who received conventional rehabilitation or fast rehabilitation following MMPRT repair.MethodsThe current retrospective cohort study compared clinical, radiological, and arthroscopic outcomes after conventional rehabilitation (group A, January 2020 to April 2020, 24 patients) with those after fast rehabilitation (group B, May 2020 to August 2020, 24 patients) in patients who underwent pullout repair for MMPRT. Partial weight-bearing and range of motion exercises were allowed 2 weeks postoperatively in group A and 1 week postoperatively in group B. In patients with an average weight of approximately 60 kg, full weight bearing was allowed 4 weeks postoperatively in group A and 3 weeks postoperatively in group B.ResultsAt 12 months postoperatively, the clinical scores, including International Knee Documentation Committee and Japanese Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score, significantly improved in both groups (p < 0.01). Although meniscus healing was achieved in both groups, medial meniscus extrusion significantly progressed by 0.9 mm in group A and 0.8 mm in group B (p < 0.01, compared with preoperative extrusion). There were no significant differences in clinical scores, arthroscopic meniscal healing status, or medial meniscus extrusion progression on magnetic resonance images between the groups.ConclusionA fast rehabilitation protocol can be safely implemented without compromising patient outcomes after pullout repair for MMPRT.

Introduction

A medial meniscus (MM) posterior root tear (MMPRT) is a severe degenerative tear commonly occurring in middle-aged or older people. The overall incidence of MMPRTs has increased recently because of the aging of the population and the rising prevalence of obesity.1 MMPRT can induce progressive pathological medial meniscus extrusion (MME) with time, leading to rapid progression of knee osteoarthritis and subchondral insufficiency fractures of the knee.2, 3, 4, 5 A paradigm shift has occurred in the surgical treatment of MMPRTs: previously, partial meniscectomy was a common surgical procedure; however, MMPRT repairs, such as a transtibial pullout repair, have been increasingly performed in recent years, aiming to restore hoop tension of the meniscus, slow the progression of osteoarthritis, and prevent arthroplasty conversion compared with partial meniscectomy or conservative treatment.6, 7, 8, 9

The goals of surgical treatment are to prevent secondary complications, avoid arthroplasty conversion, and facilitate early recovery of muscle strength and proprioception. Accelerated rehabilitation after meniscus repair reportedly does not lead to a higher failure rate for some meniscus tears, such as longitudinal or bucket handle tears.10 However, there is no consensus on the optimal postoperative rehabilitation protocol for MMPRT repair. Postoperative rehabilitation must balance the risk of healing failure due to early mobilization with the risk of complications resulting from prolonged immobilization, such as adhesions and muscle atrophy.

Previously, prolonged postoperative restriction on partial or full weight-bearing was enforced; partial weight-bearing typically starts at 2–6 weeks postoperatively and full-weight bearing at 6–10 weeks postoperatively, with or without knee immobilizer use.4,11, 12, 13, 14 However, over-protective rehabilitation may harm meniscal healing; as immobilization for more than 4 weeks could decrease collagen formation.15 Moreover, over-protected weight-bearing and range of motion (ROM) could exacerbate postoperative muscle weakness, which may require 6–12 months of intense rehabilitation to normalize.16 A recent retrospective study revealed that postoperative quadriceps muscle strength is associated with clinical scores and postoperative MME progression after MMPRT repair.17 There is still a lack of evidence for whether the initiation of weight-bearing and ROM exercises could be safely accelerated.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous clinical studies have compared the clinical outcomes of conventional or accelerated postoperative weight-bearing and ROM. This study aimed to perform a comparative analysis of patients who received conventional rehabilitation (group A) versus fast rehabilitation (group B) following MMPRT repair. In the conventional rehabilitation program, partial weight-bearing and ROM exercises were allowed 2 weeks postoperatively, whereas in the fast rehabilitation program, they were allowed 1 week postoperatively. Weight-bearing and ROM exercise protocols were accelerated carefully and adjusted weekly to minimise confusion for both patients and rehabilitation therapists. We hypothesized that the fast rehabilitation regimen would not compromise meniscal healing or clinical outcomes, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings, when compared with the conventional regimen.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Okayama University (Ref. No. 1857), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients before study commencement.

Patients

Between January and August 2020, 55 consecutive patients underwent pullout repair for MMPRT. All patients were diagnosed with complete radial MMPRT (LaPrade’s classification type 2) based on characteristic MRI findings after a sudden, painful popping episode. All the patients met the following surgical criteria: femorotibial angle ≤180° (normal knee alignment), radiographic Kellgren–Lawrence grade 0–2 without subchondral insufficiency fractures, mild cartilage lesions (Outerbridge grade I or II), and body mass index <35 kg/m2. The exclusion criteria were previous knee surgery on the injured side, concomitant ligament injury, and partial tear. Of the 55 patients, 7 were excluded, and 48 were finally enrolled. Patients were sequentially categorized into two groups: group A (January 2020 to April 2020, n = 24) and group B (May 2020 to August 2020, n = 24) to compare the clinical efficacy of two different postoperative rehabilitation regimens (Fig. 1). No patients were lost during the 1-year follow-up period. All patients underwent MRI evaluations at 3 months and 1 year postoperatively, and repeat arthroscopy was performed for meniscal healing evaluation and screw removal at an average of 1 year postoperatively. Surgical techniques

All surgical procedures were performed by a well-experienced surgeon (T.F.). The two simple or cinch stitches with additional posteromedial pullout repair18 were performed (Fig. 2) according to the surgical time: two simple stitches were performed in group A and two cinch stitches were performed in group B (Table 1). Ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene sutures—SutureTape (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA), Ultratape (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA), or Ultrabraid (Smith & Nephew)—were passed through the meniscus using a suture passer. An all-inside meniscal repair device (JuggerStitch; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) was used for additional posteromedial pullout repair.18 In both groups, a 4.0-mm tibial tunnel was created at the anatomical attachment of the MM posterior root using a dedicated aiming device. Postoperative rehabilitation

The two different postoperative protocols are shown in Fig. 3. In group A, patients initially maintained non-weight-bearing in the knee immobilizer for 2 weeks. ROM exercises were initiated 2 weeks postoperatively, starting at 30° flexion, increasing to 90° at 4 weeks, and 120° at 5 weeks postoperatively. Partial weight-bearing of under 20 kg was initiated 2 weeks postoperatively, and weight-bearing was increased by 20 kg weekly to full weight-bearing according to the patient’s weight. In contrast, patients in group B utilized the knee immobilizer for 1 week, and ROM exercises and partial weight-bearing were initiated 1 week postoperatively. ROM exercises were initiated 1 week postoperatively starting at 30° flexion, subsequently increasing to 90° at 3 weeks postoperatively, and 120° at 4 weeks postoperatively. Partial weight-bearing of under 20 kg was permitted 1 week postoperatively, and weight-bearing was increased by 20 kg weekly to enable full weight-bearing according to the patient’s weight. For patients with an average weight of approximately 60 kg, full weight-bearing was allowed 4 weeks postoperatively in group A and 3 weeks postoperatively in group B. If the patient’s body weight exceeded 80 kg, full weight-bearing was allowed 6 and 5 weeks postoperatively in group A and group B, respectively (Fig. 3). In both groups, the knee immobilizer was removed during daytime after a partial weight-bearing was started and utilized during sleep until the patients were able to bend their knees to a 90-degree angle.Fig. 3Postoperative rehabilitation protocols. In both groups, ROM exercise was initiated using open chain activities, and partial weight-loading was controlled by a weighing scale. Most patients remained under hospital care until they could walk freely without external aid. After returning home, supervised rehabilitation was recommended twice per week until 2–3 months postoperatively. Knee hyperflexion in weight-bearing situations, such as squatting, was avoided permanently to prevent the repaired meniscus root from impinging the tibia and femoral medial condyle.

Methods of assessment

Clinical scores were evaluated and compared between the groups at the primary surgery and during the second-look arthroscopy, performed 1 year postoperatively, on average, using the Lysholm knee score, International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) score, Japanese Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), and a pain score using a visual analogue scale.

MRI evaluations were performed preoperatively, 3 months postoperatively, and 1 year postoperatively using an Achieva 1.5 T system (Philips, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) and an Oasis 1.2 T system (Hitachi, Medical, Chiba, Japan) with 10° of knee flexion in a non-weight-bearing position. MME and medial meniscus body width (MMBW) were evaluated at the midpoint of the anterior-posterior length of the MM (Fig. 2).4 Osteophyte was excluded for MME evaluation. Relative MME was calculated as 100 × MME/MMBW (%). Radiological data, as well as the pre-and postoperative MME, were compared between the groups.

The tunnel position was assessed using postoperative three-dimensional computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 2). The CT images were obtained with a Multislice CT system (Toshiba Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan), and the three-dimensional images of the tibial condyles were reconstructed using a three-dimensional volume-rendering technique (AZE Virtual Place software; AZE Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The virtual anatomic centre of the MM posterior root was determined as the centre of a virtual circle that joined the three sides (anterior border of the posterior cruciate ligament tibial attachment, lateral margin of the medial tibial plateau, and retro-eminence ridge), as previously reported.19 If the centre of the bone tunnel was found to be within 5 mm of the virtual anatomic centre of the MM posterior root attachment, it was considered an anatomical repair; otherwise, it was considered a non-anatomical repair.

During second-look arthroscopy, the meniscal healing score (0 [worst]–10 points [best]), comprising the anteroposterior width of bridging tissues (0–4 points), stability (0–4 points), and synovial coverage (0–2 points),20 was evaluated. Arthroscopic evaluation of the meniscus healing was assessed by a surgeon (T.F.) during surgery and reassessed by an assistant (M.T.) postoperatively using arthroscopic records. The healing scores were compared between the groups (Fig. 2).

All radiographic measurements were performed independently by two blinded investigators. Measurements were conducted twice over an interval of 8 weeks.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables, such as age and MME, between groups. Fisher’s exact test was employed to evaluate categorical variables, such as sex. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to evaluate the clinical scores and meniscus extrusion of each group pre- and postoperatively. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Easy R software (Saitama Medical Centre, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan) was used for statistical analyses. Intra-observer and inter-observer correlations were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients. For the sample size calculation, the non-inferiority test for two groups using clinical scores revealed a maximum of 24 patients were needed to detect significant between-group differences, referencing the minimal clinically important difference of the IKDC (12.3) and KOOS subscales (symptoms, 12.3; pain, 11.8; activities of daily living, 11.4; sport and recreation, 16.7; and quality of life, 16.9) reported previously.21

Results

There were no demographic differences between the two groups in terms of patients' sex, age, body mass index, radiographic femorotibial angle, Kellgren–Lawrence grade, and duration from injury to operation; however, the duration from surgery to return-to-home was shorter in group B than in group A (Table 1). In the comparison of pre- and postoperative clinical scores, all the clinical scores significantly improved postoperatively in both groups (p < 0.01), with IKDC score and KOOS subscales improving beyond the minimal clinically important difference listed above (Table 2). Additionally, all the clinical scores were similar between the two groups (Table 2). The rate of anatomical bone tunnel drilling was also similar between the groups (Table 3). No significant differences were found in arthroscopic meniscal healing scores 1 year postoperatively (Table 3). MME markedly progressed 1 year postoperatively in both groups (p < 0.01), and the pre- and postoperative MME, MMBW, and relative MME were similar between the groups (Table 3). The inter- and intra-observer reliabilities for the evaluations of MME were 0.92 and 0.88, respectively. Discussion

The most important finding of the current study was that early weight-bearing and knee ROM exercises initiated 1 week postoperatively (group B) did not have a negative effect on postoperative clinical scores, postoperative MME progression, and arthroscopic meniscal healing status 1 year postoperatively compared to conventional rehabilitation (group A). Our results suggest that a fast rehabilitation regimen is clinically safe to perform.

Previously, only a few studies reported an arthroscopic meniscus healing status after MMPRT repair with different rehabilitation protocols. For instance, following MMPRT pullout repair, immediate partial weight-bearing was allowed for the first 6 weeks postoperatively. Of the 30 patients included, second-look arthroscopy identified loss of healing in two (6.7 %).22 In another report of 13 cases following MMPRT pullout repair, non-weight-bearing crutches were applied postoperatively for 6 weeks. Only one knee, in a patient who was uncooperative with postoperative care, failed to heal.23 These reports might indicate that unrestricted postoperative weight-bearing may lead to healing failure. A recent systematic review also revealed that although toe-touch or low-level weight bearing was utilized immediately after surgery in some reports, most studies recommended toe-touch or non-weight bearing until 2–6 weeks following operation.24 However, how long partial or full weight-bearing restriction is needed was unclear due to the relatively small number of patients and non-comparative characteristics of these studies. In the current study, no case of poor meniscus healing (healing score ≤4 points) was found in either group. Additionally, the subscale of meniscus healing status (width, stability, synovial coverage) was similar between groups. Therefore, both postoperative weight-bearing protocols implemented in the present study can be safely performed, and progressive increases in weight-bearing load starting at 1 week postoperatively (group B) might not deteriorate meniscus healing.

In this study, ROM exercises were carefully performed. The starting point was 2 (fast) or 3 (conventional) weeks after the operation, with full ROM allowed at 8 (fast) and 12 (conventional) weeks. Clinically, ROM exercises might be started immediately after the operation, and in some reports, ROM exercises were performed without restrictions.24 However, previously, abnormal posteromedial meniscus extrusion of MMPRT in knee flexion position was reported,25 and the reduction of postoperative posterior meniscus extrusion correlated with the postoperative clinical scores after MMPRT repair.26 Therefore, we carefully accelerated the ROM protocol in this study.

Rehabilitation concepts could be based on biomechanics, physiological responses of tissue healing, and muscle adaptation. Biomechanically, MM root tension reaches around 30–60 N under femorotibial contact forces of 500 N.27 The contact force of 500 N can occur during the open kinematic chain without additional resistance or a postoperative toe-touch weight-bearing period.27 The ultimate load-to-failure strength of the pullout sutures passed through the meniscus is reportedly around 120–180 N.28 Thus, restricted partial weight-bearing or ROM exercises might be safely performed initially after MMPRT repair. Furthermore, considering the biomechanical research, there is little rationale for patients weighing 50 and 100 kg to follow the same rehabilitation protocol. It may be reasonable to adjust the timing of weight-bearing for each patient based on their body weight, as adjusted in the current study. However, the femorotibial force exceeds three times that of the patient’s body weight while walking or ascending/descending in daily life,29 and over-stress may place too much tension on the repaired site, which could lead to meniscus extrusion or healing failure. Hence, returning to daily activities must be suspended until the meniscus has healed. That said, the initial tissue healing process has yet to be evaluated in a biomechanical study, and safe and early rehabilitation protocols should be established through clinical trials.

Although the clinical scores were significantly improved in both groups in the present study, MME progressed over time in both groups within the first postoperative year. The MME has the potential to reflect cartilage degeneration and meniscus hoop tension.3 The postoperative MME progression is related to several factors: meniscal loose healing; knee varus malalignment; tibial tunnel malposition; and, especially, older age.30 In both groups, although the appropriate width of the bridging tissue was obtained in all cases, the perfect stability subscale (4 points) was rarely obtained (0/24 knees in group A and 3/24 knees in group B). Although different suture material and suture configuration might limit the comparison of different reports, the meniscus healing status of our technique is compatible with that in a previous report utilizing the modified Mason–Allen suture, or two simple stitches using various non-absorbable strong sutures.13 Furthermore, a similar progression of MME following MMPRT repair, utilizing partial weight-bearing initiated at 4–6 weeks postoperatively and full weight-bearing at 6–8 weeks postoperatively, was also reported.4,31 Although a longer follow-up is necessary to evaluate the efficacy of pullout repair, we assume early weight-bearing did not deteriorate meniscus healing and MME progression compared to that in previous reports.

The novelty of this study is that patients' compliance was ensured during the hospital stay. Most patients stayed under hospital care until they could walk freely without external aid at that time, although we do not believe that extending the hospital stay after meniscus surgery is necessary. Although the supervised rehabilitation was continued until 2–3 months postoperatively in this study, in the future, a home-based rehabilitation may be a good alternative,32 after the accumulation of evidence for the safety of early rehabilitation programs and patient education.

The present study has some limitations. First, the sample size was small, and the retrospective nature of the study may have led to selection bias. Second, the knee flexion angle while weight-bearing (such as half-squatting training), was not restricted in this study. A biomechanical study revealed that MM movement in MMPRT while weight-bearing was greater in a flexed knee position than that in a straight knee position,33 indicating that half-squatting quadriceps training or stepping motion could over-stress the meniscus and repair sutures. A knee immobilizer, utilized while weight-bearing during initial rehabilitation, may be beneficial for preventing meniscal dynamic movement and improving meniscal healing. Third, two different configurations were utilized in this study. We assume the difference had little effect on the clinical results considering that none of the suture configurations can achieve the strength of a naïve meniscus root (359–678N),34,35 and a recent systematic review of the biomechanical study revealed that no one suture configuration is superior to another.36 Fourth, we could not reveal a concrete advantage of early weight-bearing in this study. However, improved muscle strength testing, reduced time to return to daily activities and lower medical cost could be expected merits of early weight-bearing, and further studies to confirm these benefits are needed.

Conclusions

Early weight-bearing and ROM exercises can be safely performed after transtibial pullout repair for MMPRT as the fast rehabilitation program employed herein demonstrated similar clinical scores, arthroscopic meniscal healing scores, and MME progression on MRI 1 year after surgery compared to conventional rehabilitation.
