3D imaging reveals robustness and plasticity of cell division in rice early embryogenesis
Authors: Moeno Tezuka, Takunori Kitta, Haruka Kumakura, Moeko Sato, Naoya Kamamoto, Satoshi Naramoto, Hiroyuki Tsuji, Takashi Okamoto, Atsuko Kinoshita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmbryogenesis is an essential process involving a series of formative cell divisions that contribute to establishing the plant’s body axis. In many dicotyledons, the asymmetric cell division of the zygote gives rise to two daughter cells, which develop into two distinct cell lineages. In contrast, the fate of the two daughter cells and their contribution to the body axis formation remain poorly understood in the monocots. To address this question, we developed a method for three-dimensional imaging of early rice embryos. Our observations demonstrated that both an egg cell and two synergids are polarized prior to fertilization and are anchored to the micropylar end of the ovule via a cell wall-like structure stained with SR2200. Upon fertilization, the zygote undergoes an asymmetric cell division with a ventrally tilted division plane. The following cell divisions are not strictly synchronized between the apical and basal lineages, exhibiting non-stereotypic patterns up to the globular stage of embryogenesis. Furthermore, we examined the role of auxin signaling in rice embryogenesis using the auxin response sensor DR5rev::NLS-3xVENUS. The reporter activity was first detected at the center of the globular embryos and subsequently extended along the apical–basal axis as embryogenesis progressed. Our results highlight the importance of the progressive establishment of the body axes within cell populations during early embryogenesis.Keywords: 3D imaging • auxin • body plan • cell division • embryogenesis • rice

Introduction

Embryogenesis is a fundamental developmental process in multicellular organisms in which the body plan, including the body axis, is established from a single zygote. The body axis formation during embryogenesis is extensively studied in Arabidopsis, revealing that cell polarity and directional cell growth in the zygote or 1-cell stage proembryo lead to the formation of the apical-basal or radial axes, respectively (Mansfield and Briarty 1991, Jürgens 2001, Tanaka et al. 2024). The cellular polarities are transmitted to the tissue-level patterns through formative asymmetric cell divisions (Laux et al. 2004, Ueda and Berger 2019). The first asymmetric cell division of the zygote gives rise to two daughter cells with distinct fates, initiating the apical-basal axis formation. At the 8-cell stage, periclinal asymmetric divisions generate the inner cell mass and the outer layer, which serve as a base for the radial axis. These divisions are accompanied by various molecular events, including subcellular and intercellular signaling.

Recent advances in genomics and comparative molecular genetics have revealed both conserved and divergent aspects of embryogenesis across plant species. In Arabidopsis, the mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinase pathway is essential for zygote elongation and induces the expression of WUSCHEL-RELATED HOMEOBOX 8 (WOX8) downstream (Lukowitz et al. 2004, Ueda et al. 2017). Following the first zygotic division, WOX8/9 is specifically expressed in the basal cell, while another WOX family member, WOX2, is confined to the apical cell (Haecker et al. 2004). These distinct expression patterns of WOX genes in the 1-cell stage embryo reflect the early establishment of cell fate and polarity in Arabidopsis. Orthologs of WOX8/9 are expressed in the zygote of maize and rice and are proposed to regulate the apical-basal patterning (Nardmann et al. 2007, Chen et al. 2017, Ishimoto et al. 2019, Tang et al. 2024). In contrast, WOX2 orthologs appear to be expressed at a later developmental stage, suggesting delayed apical identity specification in these two species. In rice, the MAP kinase cascade contributes to the specification of basal identity, although its role in zygote elongation or asymmetric division remains unclear (Ishimoto et al. 2019). Instead, it may be involved in radial patterning via L1 layer specification (Yi et al. 2016).

Auxin is another key regulator of cell polarity and fate specification in Arabidopsis embryos. Auxin biosynthesis is upregulated in ovule integuments post-fertilization, and PIN7-mediated polar auxin transport from the basal cell leads to auxin accumulation in the apical cell (Friml et al. 2003, Robert et al. 2018). In the subsequent embryogenesis, spatiotemporal regulation of auxin biosynthesis and transport generates local auxin maxima, contributing to the pattern formation in the early embryo (Möller and Weijers 2009). These correlations have positioned auxin signaling as a central regulator of embryogenesis from its earliest stages. On the other hand, two independent studies demonstrated that the conventional DR5 reporter activity was not detected in the early-stage embryo of maize and Brachypodium (Chen et al. 2014, Hao et al. 2021). Thus, it is still unknown whether the role of auxin signaling in early embryogenesis is conserved in monocots.

Beyond molecular regulations, nuclear positioning and division plane orientation are key visible indicators of polarity and asymmetric cell division. The orientation of cell division in plants is often explained by phenomenological rules based on cell geometry. One widely recognized principle is Errera’s “shortest wall” rule, which predicts that cells divide at the plane that minimizes surface area (Errera 1888). This concept has been further refined to incorporate the inherent stochasticity of cell division, offering a general model of division plane orientation (Besson and Dumais 2011). While these geometric predictions fit well with symmetric division patterns, deviations from the shortest wall rule are frequently observed during formative asymmetric divisions. Notably, auxin signaling has been implicated in overriding such geometric regulations during embryogenesis. For example, auxin induces deviation from the shortest wall rule during periclinal division at the 8-cell stage in Arabidopsis embryos (Yoshida et al. 2014, Vaddepalli et al. 2021), suggesting a mechanistic link between molecular signaling and oriented cell division. Indeed, auxin plays a central role in many asymmetric cell divisions throughout plant development, influencing cell polarity and division orientation (Zhang et al. 2023).

Although the molecular mechanisms underlying asymmetric cell division and body axis formation are well characterized in Arabidopsis, their conservation across species remains unclear. Particularly, monocotyledonous grass species exhibit distinct embryonic patterns and develop unique organs such as the scutellum, coleorhiza, and epiblast. Of note, grass embryos establish a distinct dorsal-ventral axis, with the shoot apical meristem located ventrally and the root apical meristem (RAM) centrally (Itoh et al. 2005, Zhao et al. 2017, Radoeva et al. 2019). This fundamental difference in body plan suggests that monocots may have evolved independent mechanisms for body axis formation. Indeed, zygotes of maize and rice undergo asymmetric division without extensive cell elongation, which is the critical feature of apical-basal axis formation in Arabidopsis. Furthermore, the impact of the asymmetric cell division on subsequent embryogenesis remains unclear in monocots due to challenges in cell lineage tracking.

To investigate how geometric cell division patterns contribute to early embryogenesis in monocot species, we established a three-dimensional (3D) imaging method for early rice embryos. This approach enabled us to visualize and quantify cellular features such as cell number, volume, and spatial arrangement in 3D, allowing for the reconstruction of cell lineages up to the 8-cell stage. Furthermore, by combining this imaging technique with reporter-based expression analysis, we examined the contribution of auxin signaling to early embryogenesis in rice.

Results

3D imaging of rice ovules

To enable detailed observation of the early embryogenesis in rice, we developed a whole-mount 3D imaging method by combining tissue clearing with cell wall staining. For this analysis, the whole ovules were isolated from unpollinated/pollinated flowers (Fig. 1a). Tissue clearing with ClearSee improved the transparency of fixed ovules, enabling the deep imaging of the embryo sac (Fig. 1b). The rice ovule is thick in size, in which the egg apparatus is located at the basal, ventral end (Fig. 1c and d). Therefore, confocal imaging was conducted either from the left/right side (L-R axis) or the ventral side (V-D axis) to obtain high-resolution images (Fig. 1a). To visualize cell structure and nuclear position, the ovules of the transgenic rice plant ubiquitously expressing histone H2B protein fused with green fluorescent protein (H2BGFP) were treated with a cell wall staining dye, SCRI Renaissance 2200 (SR2200).

In the ovules of unpollinated flowers, the nuclear H2B-GFP signal from an egg cell and two synergid cells was successfully detected at the basal end of the embryo sac (Fig. 2a and g). As previously reported, the egg cell is located on the apical and the ventral side of the two synergids, with a nucleus polarly localized close to the maternal integument (Fig. 2a and g; Sato et al. 2010). While the nucleus of the egg cell was approximately spherical, those of the synergids were flat and located near the cell periphery, suggesting that not only the egg cell but also the two synergids are polarized prior to fertilization (Fig. 2a and m, Supplementary Fig. S1b). Although the heteromorphism of synergid nucleus is not universal, a similar elongated nucleus was reported previously in Crepis capillaris (Kuroiwa 1989). Interestingly, both egg and synergid cells were partially stained by SR2200 at their basal ends (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Fig. S1a and b), which likely corresponds to the egg apparatus wall (Russell 1993). In addition, we observed the ovule of plants expressing a cell membrane marker RCI2A-tdTomato under the egg cell-specific promoter (pECA1::tdTomato-RCI2A). In this transgenic plant, the plasma membrane and the intracellular vesicle compartments were labeled in the egg cell. Again, intracellular polarity of the egg cell along the apical-basal axis was confirmed, as indicated by the localization of the vacuole and nucleus at the apical and basal ends, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1c and d). By staining with SR2200, we confirmed that the egg apparatus wall is associated with the egg plasma membrane, covering approximately one-third of the dorsal side of the egg cell (Supplementary Fig. S1e–g).

Following pollination, the entry of a pollen tube into a synergid triggers its degeneration, resulting in the loss of H2B-GFP fluorescence from the synergid nucleus (Fig. 2b). Occasionally, a faint GFP signal, which can be sperm nuclei, was observed within the degenerated synergid (Supplementary Fig. S2a). On the other hand, the nucleus of the intact synergid remained polarized at the cell periphery (Fig. 2b and n, Supplementary Fig. S2b and c). SR2200 staining delineates the entire zygote and synergids, enabling clear visualization of their cell outlines within the embryo sac (Fig. 2b, c, h, and i). Interestingly, intense SR2200 staining was observed surrounding the degenerated synergid (Supplementary Movies 1 and 2). This pattern might not only be the cell wall but also include its degradation products and/or pollen tube contents that diffused into the apoplast.

Asymmetric division of the rice zygote

We previously demonstrated that the first division of the zygote is asymmetric, giving rise to a small apical cell and a larger basal cell (Sato et al. 2010). Consistent with this observation, the 3D confocal imaging revealed nuclear migration from the basal and the ventral region toward the apical and the dorsal region prior to division (Fig. 2g–i). In addition, the division plane was oriented either parallel or slightly oblique to the surface adjacent to the synergid, as reported previously (Fig. 2d–f and j–l; Sato et al. 2010). Intriguingly, this division plane does not correspond to the simulated division plane estimated by the minimal area passing through the geometric center of the zygote (global minimum division plane: GMDP), suggesting that the first zygotic division does not follow the “shortest wall” principle in rice (Fig. 2o–q). To assess the geometry of zygotic cell division, we compared the normalized area as well as the angle between the actual division plane (ADP) and the GMDP with those observed in root cells, where the “shortest wall” principle is typically applicable. Both parameters were significantly higher in the zygote, indicating a deviation from geometric rules in the first zygotic cell division (Fig. 2s). To evaluate the potential effect of Z-axis resolution of confocal microscopy on the GMDP estimation, we performed the same analysis by changing the Z-axis scale, which resulted in no substantial changes to our conclusions (Supplementary Fig. S3e). Consistent with the previous observation (Ishimoto et al. 2019), the volume between the two daughter cells was also significantly different, confirming that the first zygotic cell division is indeed asymmetric (Fig. 2t). The GMDP is typically perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of a cell, which in the zygote aligns with the micropylar–antipodal axis and is angled relative to the apical–basal axis of the ovule (Supplementary Fig. S3a). Interestingly, we observed one case in which the first division plane of the zygote is formed perpendicular to the apical-basal axis of the ovule, suggesting that the ovule polarity itself may influence the zygote polarity (Supplementary Fig. S3b–d).

In addition to their size disparity, the apical and basal cells exhibited distinct differences in cellular characteristics. The basal cell maintains direct contact with maternal tissues via its basal end and interfaces dorsally with the intact and degenerated synergids. In contrast, the apical cell is oriented predominantly toward the elastic endosperm, with a notably thinner cell wall on its ventral side (Fig. 2j–l, Supplementary Movie 3, Supplementary Fig. S4). Beyond these structural distinctions, the basal cell typically appeared to undergo division earlier than the apical cell, suggesting that the cell cycle progression is not synchronized between the two daughter cells (Fig. 2k and l).

Cell division and lineage tracking of the rice early embryo

By extending the 3D segmentation approach to later-stage embryos, we analyzed a total of 82 embryos ranging from the 2-cell to the 233-cell stage (Fig. 3a and b, Supplementary Table S1). While the individual cell volume decreased dramatically, the total embryo volume remained relatively constant from the 2-cell to the 32-cell stage. Subsequently, the overall embryo volume increased, whereas individual cell volume became constant (Fig. 3c and d). This phase transition resembles a characteristic feature of animal embryogenesis, wherein the zygote undergoes successive cleavage division to form a blastomere-stage embryo without an increase in overall size, and subsequently enters a phase of volumetric growth and tissue patterning beyond the blastomere stage (Płusa and Piliszek 2020).

To investigate the kinetics of cell number during early embryogenesis, we analyzed embryos collected at 24, 31, 38, 48, and 72 h after artificial pollination (HAP). As anticipated, the number of cells increased exponentially over time. While embryos with the doubling number of cells—such as 2-, 4-, and 8-cell stages—were frequently observed at 24 and 31 HAP, a substantial fraction of embryos exhibited non-canonical cell numbers, indicating pronounced variability in division timing. Notably, this variation became more prominent at later time points (38, 48, and 72 HAP), reflecting increasing heterogeneity in cell division dynamics as embryogenesis progressed (Fig. 3e).

Tracking cell division patterns during early rice embryogenesis has been challenging, mainly due to the technical limitations of 2D observation. By employing a 3D segmentation approach, we successfully traced the cell lineages of the apical and basal cells up to the 21-cell embryo (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. S5). Notably, the number of cells derived from the basal lineage was often higher than that from the apical lineage, indicating that cell division is not strictly synchronized between the two lineages (Fig. 4b). This difference in cell cycle progression suggests a functional asymmetry between the apical and basal cells. Furthermore, the size of cells in the basal lineage remained significantly larger than those in the apical lineage up to the 16-cell stage, although this difference became less pronounced as cell size decreased (Fig. 2t).

To further investigate the cell-lineage-specific division profiles, we analyzed the orientation of division planes up to the 8-cell stage (Supplementary Movie 4). After the first asymmetric division (P1), both apical and basal cells underwent a second cell division, with the division planes intersecting the first plane (Fig. 5a and c). In the apical cell lineage, the division plane was oriented transversely in most (91%) of the embryos (A1), while 9% of the embryos exhibited longitudinal division planes. In the latter case, both daughter cells divided transversely, forming the quadrant cells (Supplementary Fig. S6c). In the former case (A1), the upper cell (A1a) was predominantly divided in the longitudinal direction (A2, 95%), while the lower cell (A1b) was divided both longitudinally (A3, 60%) and transversely (A4, 40%) (Supplementary Fig. S6a and b). Collectively, the apical lineage was categorized into two major patterns: four-quadrant or T-shaped (Fig. 5b). Similarly, the basal lineage was classified into the same two patterns, although more variation in division angle was observed compared to the apical lineage (Fig. 5c and d). Overall, our lineage tracking analysis suggested that the transverse division was dominant in both the apical and the basal lineages (A1 and B1). Furthermore, in each lineage, the robust longitudinal division was observed in the upper cell (A2 and B2), while the division pattern was more stochastic in the lower cell (A3, A4, B3, B4, B5).

Radial pattern formation began around the 16-cell stage, although the timing varied among individual embryos. Notably, our observation revealed that the initial periclinal division occurred in the basal lineage in two independent embryos (Fig. 4c and d, Supplementary Fig. S7). In Arabidopsis embryogenesis, asymmetric formative cell divisions, including protodermal periclinal division, often deviate from the geometrical rule (Yoshida et al. 2014, Vaddepalli et al. 2021). To investigate whether a similar regulatory mechanism exists in rice embryogenesis, we compared the ADP and the GMDP across various cell categories. In the apical lineage, the basal cell division observed in the T-shaped pattern (Fig. 5a and b; A4) did not follow the shortest wall rule, exhibiting a large angle of deviation between ADP and GMDP (Supplementary Fig. S8a). During telophase in the basal cell of the two-cell embryo, the cell plate was formed at an oblique angle relative to the GMDP (Fig. 2f and r). Later in the basal lineage, the most basal cell, which is directly associated with maternal tissue, occasionally divides in a manner that deviates from the GMDP (Supplementary Fig. S8b). At later stages, outermost cells frequently underwent divisions that substantially differed from the GMDP, particularly during the initiation of epithelial layer formation (Supplementary Fig. S8c). In these cases, the normalized cell wall area (NCA) did not necessarily deviate from the GMDP, and the ADP corresponded to the plane with local area minima. These data suggest that, depending on the mother cell shape, the stochastic selection of division plane orientation underlies cell division pattern during rice embryogenesis.

Auxin signaling and axis development in rice embryogenesis

Extensive research on Arabidopsis has demonstrated the multiple roles of auxin in early embryogenesis, including the establishment of apical-basal polarity and the formation of radial patterns. However, the role of auxin during early embryonic development in rice remains to be elucidated. To address this question, we utilized our 3D imaging approach and visualized the auxin response marker DR5rev::NLS-3xVENUS in rice. In ovules, DR5 promoter activity was detected in the integument, with the strongest signal detected at the basal end (Supplementary Fig. S9a). In contrast, DR5 promoter activity was absent in the early embryo up to one day after pollination (Fig. 6a). The first DR5 signal was detected at the center of the embryo on two days after pollination (DAP), and it extended to the basal region according to the progress of embryogenesis (Fig. 6b and c, Supplementary Fig. S9b and c). On the fourth day, when a notch appeared on the ventral side, the DR5 activity was detected along with the apical-basal axis, and this pattern was maintained until the shoot and root meristem were established (Fig. 6d and e). In accordance with the observed shift in DR5 reporter activity from the central to the basal region of the embryo, the basipetal localization of PIN1b-GFP was first detected in the cells at the center of the globular embryo (Fig. 7a–c), suggesting the auxin flow from the center to the basal region of the embryo at this stage (Fig. 7c and f). In the later stage embryos, the PIN1b-GFP signal was detected in both shoots and RAMs as well as vascular bundles, implying that PIN1b is one of the major auxin efflux carriers that are responsible for the establishment and maintenance of the body axis during rice embryogenesis (Fig. 7d and e).

Previous research in Arabidopsis has demonstrated a link between auxin response and protoderm formation (Yoshida et al. 2014, Vaddepalli et al. 2021). Based on our observation of characteristic cell division patterns during the initiation of epithelial layer formation, we hypothesized that auxin signaling may also be involved in the formation of the protoderm in rice. Although asymmetric DR5 promoter activity was occasionally detected in the cells undergoing periclinal divisions at the basal region of the globular embryos, such activity was not observed in the apical region (Supplementary Fig. S9b). Therefore, it remains unclear whether auxin signaling directly affects the formation of the epithelial layer in rice. Further studies on auxin-related mutants and additional molecular markers will be essential to clarify the involvement of auxin in rice protoderm formation and to assess the extent of conservation and divergence of this mechanism among angiosperms.

Discussion

Research on model organisms with invariant cell lineages has uncovered fundamental mechanisms for body axis formation and cell differentiation during embryogenesis. On the other hand, recent studies have highlighted the stochastic variations that underlie developmental plasticity and robustness. In this study, we examined early embryogenesis in rice at the cellular level and detected considerable variation in cell division patterns and orientations. These divisions appear to be stochastic rather than random, with multiple patterns observed in both apical and basal lineages. Despite this variability, the apical-basal and radial axes were gradually established within the cell populations, implying that robust axis formation can emerge from variable cellular behaviors. Our findings provide evidence for the progressive body axis formation during early embryogenesis, which will contribute to understanding the regulatory mechanisms for robust developmental regulations within complex systems.

One of the characteristic features of angiosperm embryogenesis is that the egg cell is attached to the maternal tissue prior to fertilization. Our observations suggest that this attachment is at least partially mediated by the cell wall component that can be stained by SR2200. SR2200 is known to label β-1,4- and β-1,3-glucans (Nicholas et al. 1994, Musielak et al. 2015), indicating that polysaccharides such as cellulose or callose are likely the components of this structure. Previous electron microscopy studies have also detected the Periodic acid-Schiff stain-positive cell wall at the micropylar end of the egg apparatus, suggesting that this structure is widespread in many angiosperms (Willemse and van Went 1984). Furthermore, our observations revealed stronger SR2200 staining at the basal end of the two-cell embryo compared to the apical end (Fig. 2j–l). Notably, the location of intense staining at the basal side coincided with the SR2200 signal observed in unfertilized egg cells (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Fig. S4). These observations suggest that internal polarity of the unfertilized egg may be partially inherited by the two-cell embryo through the cell wall and that mechanical properties derived from the cell wall differ between the apical and basal cells at the two-cell stage.

The first zygotic division in rice is oriented, resulting in the formation of apical and basal cells, located on the ventral and dorsal sides, respectively. Our 3D analysis demonstrated that this division plane deviates from the shortest wall, indicating that its orientation is not solely determined by geometric properties (Fig. 2o–q). Several factors, such as maternal signal or intrinsic cell polarity, may contribute to this division plane orientation. Mechanical constraints acting on the zygote may also play a role, potentially via differences in cell wall thickness or mechanical stress originating from the surrounding cells/tissues. The oblique cell division of the basal cell (Fig. 2f and r, Supplementary Fig. S8b) may also reflect the effect of mechanical constraints on cell division pattern.

Although it remains unclear whether the directional division of the zygote is linked to the apical–basal or dorsal–ventral axis in the mature embryo, it is plausible that such asymmetric division contributes to functional divergence between the two daughter cells, potentially guided by structural and positional cues. Indeed, our lineage tracking analysis revealed that the mitotic progression is faster in the basal cell lineage than in the apical lineage. This result contrasts with the Arabidopsis embryogenesis, in which the cell-cycle duration is correlated with the cell size (Gooh et al. 2015). Such differences may reflect the divergence in body plan between the two species, as the dorsal and basal regions of the mature rice embryo give rise to the monocot-specific embryonic organs, including the scutellum and coleorhiza.

In contrast to the robust, oriented zygotic division, subsequent division patterns exhibited larger variability. Our 3D observations revealed several representative division patterns in both apical and basal lineages. These patterns were particularly predictable in the apical lineage, with over 95% of the embryos categorized into two major patterns. Notably, the Quadrant pattern can result from two distinct division sequences (Supplementary Fig. S6a and c), indicating the presence of a compensatory mechanism that ensures developmental robustness. Given that the apical cell wall is thin and can contribute to stochastic variations in cell shape, the observed diversity in division patterns may function to buffer such heterogeneity. Interestingly, a recent study on Arabidopsis embryogenesis proposed a similar compensation mechanism underlying variability in division plane orientation in later stages (Laruelle et al. 2022). The study also demonstrated that the variation in division pattern may be influenced more by mother cell geometry than by a fixed division rule. Due to limitations in sample size and the use of fixed specimens, it remains unclear whether a similar mechanism underlies rice embryogenesis. However, the similarity between two phylogenetically distinct species suggests that compensation mechanisms in cell division patterns may represent a fundamental nature of angiosperm embryogenesis.

As embryogenesis progresses, successive cell divisions gradually attenuate the intrinsic polarity of the zygote, resulting in a morphologically homogeneous population of cells. This raises a fundamental question: how do individual cells perceive positional cues and initiate body axis formation? Our analysis suggests that auxin response plays a pivotal role in this process. Following several rounds of cell division, a part of the embryonic cells lost direct contact with external structures (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. S7a). We observed that the auxin response is first activated within these internalized cells (Fig. 6b), implying that acquisition of an “inner” identity may serve as a primary positional cue for spatial organization. Subsequently, the auxin response expands towards the basal side, which is in contact with maternal tissues, leading to the establishment of the apical–basal axis. These observations suggest that interactions with maternal tissues provide an additional, essential cue for embryonic axis formation. Strong DR5 reporter activity in the integument implies maternal supply of auxin, as in Arabidopsis, although the responsible transporter is still unidentified. On the other hand, the polarized PIN1b localization was observed at the center of the embryo, which supports the basipetal auxin transport from the “inner” tissue. Interestingly, similar expression patterns of PIN1 proteins were reported in Brachypodium (Hao et al. 2021), suggesting that this mechanism of axis formation is likely conserved throughout the Poaceae family. A recent study in rice reported a broader auxin response from the early stages of embryogenesis (Tang et al. 2024). This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in cultivar or the growth condition. Nevertheless, the consistent observation of a continuous auxin response along the apical–basal axis across studies reinforces the central role of auxin in axis establishment.

On the other hand, auxin response was not likely involved in the periclinal divisions in the outermost layer of the embryo. This observation contrasts with previous insights from Arabidopsis, where radial division at the 16-cell stage is regulated by auxin signaling (Yoshida et al. 2014, Vaddepalli et al. 2021). However, it remains possible that other PIN proteins or auxin responses below the detection threshold may contribute to the regulation of the periclinal divisions. Notably, the timing of these divisions—2 to 3 DAP—coincides with the onset of endosperm cellularization, suggesting that physical and/or molecular interactions from surrounding tissues may influence this characteristic division pattern. Indeed, a strong auxin response has been detected in the endosperm at a similar developmental stage in maize, which is proposed to affect embryogenesis (Chen et al. 2014). Our observation also implies active auxin signaling in the endosperm in later stages (Supplementary Fig. S9c).

Overall, our 3D imaging approach has significantly advanced the understanding of early embryogenesis in rice. It enables quantitative analysis of mutant phenotypes during early stages in future studies. Furthermore, combining related marker lines, such as PIN1b and DR5, will allow investigation of their functional interactions and spatiotemporal dynamics. Nonetheless, several technical limitations remain. First, chemical fixation may cause artifacts or alter cellular structures, particularly during the earliest stages of development. Second, the limited resolution along the z-axis in confocal laser scanning microscopy can affect the accuracy of quantitative analyses. In addition, high-quality cell wall signals are essential for reliable 3D segmentation; however, these signals are substantially weaker in embryos than in ovules due to imaging depth. We validated our results by observing living egg cells and by digitally adjusting the z-scale, thereby confirming the robustness of our analyses despite these technical limitations (Supplementary Figs. S1c and d and S3e). Future improvements, such as implementing live-cell imaging and advanced microscopy techniques, will further enhance the reliability and utility of rice embryogenesis as a model for complex developmental systems.

Materials and Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

All plants used in this study were Oryza sativa L. cv. Nipponbare background and were grown in an environmental chamber (K30-7248; Koito Industries, Yokohama, Japan) at 26°C under a 13-h light/11-h dark photoperiod. Transgenic rice plants expressing the histone H2B-GFP fusion protein were previously described (Abiko et al. 2013).

Plasmid construction and plant transformation

For pECA1::tdTomato-RCI2A transgenic rice, the 3-kb upstream region of OsECA1-like1 (Os03g0296600), the coding sequence of tdTomato, and AtRCI2A were amplified by PCR and cloned into the entry vectors pGGA000, pGGB000, and pGGC000, respectively, using the restriction enzyme BsaI (New England Biolabs; Massachusetts, United States) and the T4 DNA ligase (TOYOBO; Osaka, Japan). The entry vectors were subcloned into the intermediate vector pGGM000 and then into the destination vector pGGZ001, together with other entry vectors (pGGD002, pGGE005, and pGGF005), as previously described (Lampropoulos et al. 2013). The plasmid kit used for generating plant transformation constructs was kindly provided by Jan Lohmann (Addgene kit #1000000036). For DR5rev::NLS-3xVENUS, we first created the construct pENTR DR5-NLS-3xVenus by linking the DR5rev promoter to a gene encoding a nuclear localization signal (NLS) fused with three tandem copies of the Venus fluorescent protein. This DR5:NLS-3xVenus fragment was subsequently transferred into the binary vector pGWB501 (Nakagawa et al. 2007) via the Gateway LR recombination reaction (Sato et al. 2025). For pPIN1b::PIN1b-GFP, a 3521-bp upstream to 1545-bp downstream region of OsPIN1b (Os02g0743400), predicted to have the highest expression in rice embryos, was amplified by PCR using PrimeSTAR GXL DNA polymerase (TaKaRa) and cloned into pDONR221 (Invitrogen) through a BP reaction. Subsequently, GFP was inserted at 654 bp downstream of OsPIN1b using the In-Fusion cloning kit (TaKaRa). The resulting construct was transferred into the binary vector pGWB1 via an LR reaction to generate the OsPIN1b-GFP fusion. These plasmids were then introduced into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain pEHA101 or EHA105 to transform into rice calli (cv. Nipponbare) as described previously (Toki et al. 2006). Transformed calli were selected on a medium containing hygromycin. The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Microscopy and imaging

Ovules at different developmental stages were dissected under a stereo microscope and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde under vacuum. The fixed samples were washed twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), then cleared with ClearSee (Kurihara et al. 2015) for 5 to 14 days at room temperature, with the cell-wall staining dye, SCRI Renaissance 2200 (Musielak et al. 2015). The cleared samples were embedded in ClearSee with/without 2% (w/v) low-melt agarose and observed on a glass-bottom imaging dish (Zell-Kontakt; Nörten-Hardenberg, Germany) using either a C2 or AX confocal microscope equipped with a water-immersion lens (CFI APO LWD Lambda S 40x WI) or a silicon oil (CFI Plan Apo Lambda S 40XC Sil) lens (Nikon; Tokyo, Japan). The excitation and detection wavelengths were at 405 nm and 420–470 nm for SR2200, or at 488 nm and 500–550 nm for GFP/VENUS, respectively. The Z intervals of sections for 3D segmentation were 0.207–0.5 μm. The images were denoised using the Denoise.ai function of NIS.ai (Nikon) or adjusted for brightness and contrast using FIJI (https://fiji.sc/), if necessary.

Image processing and analysis

The 3D segmentation and quantitative analyses were performed using the MorphoGraphX software (https://morphographx.org/) as previously described (Yoshida et al. 2014, Strauss et al. 2022). In Brief, the cell surface mesh was generated from the SR2200 signal using automatic segmentation, followed by manual correction. Nuclear meshes were created from the H2B-GFP signal after applying Gaussian filtering. Meshes corresponding to tissues outside the embryo were manually removed. Cell lineages were traced manually, from younger to older stages of the embryo. Based on the geometric organization of the cells and nuclei, sister cells were paired to minimize discontinuities in the wall of reconstructed parent cells (Supplementary Fig. S5). Undetermined cases were excluded from the analysis. Statistical analysis

Data visualization and statistical analyses were performed with RStudio, including Welch’s t-test and Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD, using the packages “ggplot2” and “ggpubr.” Significant differences were determined at P < 0.05.
