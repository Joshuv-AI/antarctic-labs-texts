Epidemiology of Human Metapneumovirus (HMPV) in India: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Rizwan Suliankatchi Abdulkader, Neetu Vijay, Varsha Potdar, Joshua Chadwick, Jitendra Narayan, Sabarinathan Ramasamy, Devika Shanmugasundaram, Selvavinayagam Thirumalaicheri Sivaprakasam, Manoj Murhekar, Nivedita Gupta, Virus Research and Diagnostic Laboratory Network and the Model for Integrated Influenza Surveillance in Tamil Nadu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe Department of Health Research, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, India, established the Virus Research and Diagnostic Laboratory Network (VRDLN) in 2014 to strengthen viral diagnostic capabilities and provide a system for routine virological surveillance. In 2022, we also initiated the Model for Integrated Influenza Surveillance in Tamil Nadu (MIST) to enhance surveillance of virologic and clinical characteristics of severe acute respiratory infections (SARI) cases in selected sentinel tertiary care hospitals. This study investigates the epidemiology of Human Metapneumovirus (HMPV) in India by analysing data from these distinct but related surveillance systems.MethodsWe analysed VRDLN data from 2019 to 2024 to describe HMPV cases based on time, place, and personal characteristics. We presented a comparison of two periods—2019 to 2023 and 2024—to compare historical trends with the current scenario. We also examined detailed clinical and laboratory profiles of HMPV-positive SARI cases collected under the MIST project from 2022 to 2024 (a case series). Hospitals under the VRDLN conducted testing of any physician-referred patient, while MIST exclusively tested SARI patients who met a standardised case definition. While the VRDLN provided an overall picture of the testing and positivity trends, MIST provided details on laboratory parameters, and clinical course and outcomes of positive cases. All laboratories adhered to standardised quality-assured testing protocols and reported data to a centralized database.FindingsBetween 2019 and 2023, the VRDLN tested 20,625 patients for HMPV, of whom 1030 (3.2%) were positive. In 2024, 11,155 patients were tested, with 367 (3.3%) confirmed positive. Across both periods, age-stratified analysis indicated that the majority of tests were conducted among children. The highest positivity was observed in those aged 1–2 years, with 4.5% (128/2864) testing positive in 2019–2023 and 4.6% (70/1508) in 2024. Most HMPV-positive cases presented with Acute Respiratory Infection (ARI)/Influenza-like illness (ILI), accounting for 85.4% [566/17,090] of cases in 2019–2023 and 40.3% [148/1697] in 2024, as most testing was carried out among these patients. Fever and cough were the most common symptoms, reported in 70.3% of cases from 2019 to 2023 and 79.6% (292/8398) in 2024, and in 60.5% (401/12,059) and 60.8% (223/5977) of cases, respectively. Under the MIST project, we tested 3599 SARI patients between 2022 and 2023, identifying 28 (0.8%) HMPV-positive cases. Patients experienced a median illness duration of 11 days (interquartile range [IQR]: 7.5–14.5) and stayed in the hospital for a median of 7 days (IQR: 5–9.5). Eight cases required intensive care for a median of 3.5 days (IQR: 2.5–6), while 12 cases needed oxygen support for a median of three days (IQR: 3–5.5). Among the 28 cases, 25 (89.3) showed clinical improvement at discharge, and 22 (84.6) were healthy at a one-month follow-up. As of April 2025, 12 patients remained healthy, one experienced recurrent lung complications, and six were lost to follow-up.InterpretationThe increased testing and higher positivity rates in 2024 highlight the growing recognition of HMPV as a significant respiratory pathogen in India. However, we noted no recent change in the epidemiological features of HMPV. Strengthening surveillance networks remains critical to understanding the burden of such emerging viral pathogens and guiding public health policies. Although HMPV detection in our surveillance population indicates a potential association with illness, it should not be regarded as definitive evidence of causation. Moreover, hospitalisation duration should be interpreted in the context of potential unmeasured co-infections and comorbidities, rather than being solely attributed to HMPV infection.FundingFunded by the Department of Health Research, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India.Keywords: Human metapneumovirus, HMPV, Epidemiology, Severe acute respiratory infections, VRDLN, MIST, Surveillance, India

Research in context

Evidence before this study

India bears a substantial burden of respiratory infections, which are a leading cause of death among children under five, resulting in approximately 400 thousand deaths annually. Despite this, the understanding of Human Metapneumovirus (HMPV) in India remains limited compared to more prevalent pathogens such as influenza and Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV). In India, one study reported an HMPV positivity rate of 9.6%, while others conducted in Chennai and Uttar Pradesh observed rates of around 4%. Evidence suggests that HMPV primarily affects children under two years of age, contributing to 5–10% of paediatric hospitalisations due to acute lower respiratory tract infections. In late 2024, China experienced a marked increase in hospital admissions, potentially linked to HMPV, whereas India reported no unusual surge in respiratory illnesses during the same period. Typically, such infections in India peak during the winter and early spring months. In this context, we analysed data from 2019 to 2024 to examine HMPV testing and positivity in India, focusing on temporal, geographical, and demographic characteristics.

Added value of this study

This study presents a comprehensive analysis of HMPV surveillance data in India. Our results contribute to the existing evidence regarding HMPV, including positivity rates, at-risk groups, co-infections, and infection patterns. The study indicates that although children represented the largest proportion of cases, the overall positivity rate was low. This suggests stable epidemiological trends, with higher positivity observed during the monsoon and winter months. Furthermore, we found that Severe Acute Respiratory Infection ARI/ILI was the most common presentation among the positive cases. We emphasize the need for targeted diagnostics and preventive strategies in addressing HMPV.

Implications of all the available evidence

The increased testing and higher positivity rates in 2024 highlight the growing recognition of HMPV as a significant respiratory pathogen in India. However, we noted no recent change in the epidemiological features of HMPV. Strengthening surveillance networks remains critical to understanding the burden of such emerging viral pathogens and guiding public health policies.

Introduction

Human metapneumovirus (HMPV) is a respiratory pathogen causing illnesses ranging from mild upper respiratory tract infections to severe lower respiratory tract conditions such as bronchiolitis and pneumonia, particularly affecting young children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals.1,2 First identified in 2001 in the Netherlands, HMPV belongs to the Paramyxoviridae family and shares clinical and epidemiological features with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV).3 Globally, HMPV accounts for approximately 3%–10% of hospitalisations among young children (<5 years) due to acute lower respiratory tract infections.4 A meta-analysis of 75 studies estimated that HMPV prevalence among hospitalised acute respiratory infections (ARI) cases is around 6%.5 The absence of specific antiviral treatments or vaccines underscores the importance of surveillance and early diagnosis to mitigate the impact of HMPV infections.6

India bears a substantial burden of respiratory infections, significantly contributing to global ARI cases. ARIs remain a leading cause of mortality among children under five in India, accounting for approximately 400 thousand deaths annually.7, 8, 9 Despite this burden, epidemiological understanding of HMPV in India remains limited, as surveillance and public health initiatives have predominantly focused on pathogens such as influenza and RSV. Only a few studies have described the incidence, seasonal trends, and clinical outcomes associated with HMPV infections, highlighting a clear need for more comprehensive research.10, 11, 12, 13 This gap is largely attributable to limited diagnostic infrastructure, inconsistent reporting, and the absence of integrated surveillance frameworks. Since the first report of HMPV in India from Pune in 2004, subsequent cases have been documented in Lucknow, Vellore, Hyderabad, and New Delhi.14, 15, 16, 17 Nevertheless, regional variations in circulation patterns, age-specific risk factors, and clinical manifestations remain inadequately characterised.

Recognising the significance of viral infections and their potential for outbreaks, the Government of India established the Virus Research and Diagnostic Laboratory Network (VRDLN) in 2014 under the Department of Health Research, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare.18,19 The VRDLN aims to enhance diagnostic capabilities, enable early outbreak detection, and facilitate robust nationwide viral surveillance.18,20 By 2024, this network had expanded to include more than 160 laboratories performing routine viral diagnostics using standardised protocols, contributing valuable data to a centralised database for tracking trends in viral infections. To complement existing surveillance systems, the Model for Integrated Influenza Surveillance in Tamil Nadu (MIST) was launched in 2022, specifically targeting Influenza-like illness (ILI) and Severe Acute Respiratory Infections (SARI).21,22 Unlike the broader diagnostic coverage of VRDLN, MIST employs a focused approach by integrating clinical and laboratory data from sentinel tertiary care hospitals using standardised case definitions, diagnostic methods, and data collection procedures.

In late 2024, China reported a large increase in the number of hospital admissions, possibly due to HMPV infections.23,24 In the post-COVID era, health systems have been under heightened awareness to detect any deviations in established trends of respiratory infections or the detection of novel pathogens. However, India reported no unusual surge in respiratory illnesses during the same period, noting that such infections typically peak during the winter and early spring months.25 With this background, we wanted to examine if the HMPV epidemiology has undergone any recent change in 2024 as compared to the previous years (2019–24) in India. Surveillance systems such as the VRDLN and the MIST offer a unique opportunity to study the epidemiology of HMPV in India by integrating extensive testing data with detailed clinical insights. Although many studies have been published in India, the clinical profile of HMPV cases, including disease severity, progression, and outcomes, has not been adequately established. In this context, the present study investigates the temporal, geographical, and demographic characteristics of HMPV testing and positivity in India from 2019 to 2024 and compares trends across two distinct periods, 2019 to 2023, and the year 2024, to identify potential recent shifts in positivity rates and demographic patterns.”

Methods

Study setting

The VRDLN is a nationwide surveillance network of about 160 laboratories.18 They were established to enhance the capacity for diagnosis and surveillance of viral pathogens in tertiary care and teaching hospitals across the Indian states. These laboratories cater to both in-patient and out-patient facilities, providing a robust platform for routine viral diagnostic surveillance. In 2024, 38 of these laboratories actively tested and reported HMPV data. We implemented the MIST project in four selected sentinel tertiary care hospitals representing four districts in Tamil Nadu starting in 2022. We chose these sites based on their high patient load, geographic representation, and diagnostic capabilities, ensuring comprehensive SARI surveillance. MIST was aimed at establishing a robust virological surveillance with detailed clinical case characterisation.

Study population

For the VRDL network, the study population included symptomatic patients presenting to healthcare facilities, who underwent viral testing as recommended by clinicians based on their clinical presentation. There were no restrictions based on age or sex; all individuals who presented and underwent testing were included. For the MIST study, the population comprised hospitalised patients who met the WHO definition for Severe Acute Respiratory Infection (SARI), defined as an acute respiratory infection with measured fever (≥38 °C or 100.4 °F) and cough, with symptom onset within the past 10 days.26

Study design

We conducted a record-based descriptive cross-sectional analysis of the VRDLN data from 2019 onwards. HMPV testing was systematically introduced in the network in 2019, enabling consistent data collection for analysis. The MIST project was a continuously enrolled cohort study of patients meeting the World Health Organization (WHO) case-definition for SARI since September 2022 with a follow-up at one-month post-discharge and one additional follow-up done till April 2025.

Sample size and sampling

The VRDLN collected data from 24,163 patients tested for HMPV between 2019 and 2023, with an additional 9714 patients tested in 2024. Samples were collected sequentially based on clinical suspicion, with treating physicians ordering viral tests based on presenting symptoms. In MIST, a total of 3599 SARI patients were enrolled and tested in the study, among whom 28 cases tested positive for HMPV during September 2022–October 2024.

Study procedures

In the VRDLN, we collected socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex, residence) and some clinical details, including the presenting syndrome and symptoms, duration of illness prior to visit, in-patient or out-patient status, sample type, and type of test performed. In MIST, researchers used a detailed case report form (CRF) to collect demographic, clinical, and laboratory data, as well as treatment and outcome information. They gathered follow-up data via telephonic interviews one-month post-discharge and one additional follow-up in April 2025. We defined an HMPV positive case as one with a laboratory-confirmed HMPV infection through molecular testing Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR). More details of the MIST study procedures are published elsewhere.21 In both systems, researchers collected all the relevant information in pilot-tested paper-based a case report form and trained data entry operators entered them electronically into a centralised database. Influenza A, Influenza B, SARS CoV-2, RSV, PIV1, PIV2, PIV3, PIV4, Adenovirus, Rhinovirus, Enterovirus, and HMPV are routinely tested. The influenza A positive sample will further be subtyped for A (H3N2) and A (H1N1) pdm09, and influenza B positive samples for Yamagata and Victoria (B/Y, B/V).

RT-PCR protocol for HMPV detection

Following nucleic acid extraction using the Gens2Me MagRNA Viral RNA-II Extraction Kit, real-time RT-PCR was performed using the SuperScript III One-Step qRT-PCR kit (Invitrogen, USA). Each 25 μL reaction contained 10 μM each of forward and reverse primers, 5 μM TaqMan probe, 12.5 μL of 2× reaction buffer, 0.5 μL SuperScript III enzyme, and 5 μL of extracted RNA template. Thermal cycling conditions included reverse transcription at 50 °C for 30 min, initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s (denaturation), 50 °C for 15 s (annealing), and 55 °C for 30 s (extension). Fluorescence signals collected during the 55 °C extension step were used to detect HMPV. The primers and probes utilised in this protocol are based on previously published sequences.26,27

Data management and analysis

This study involved secondary analysis of existing surveillance data and so we did not perform a formal sample size calculation. Data from the VRDLN covering the period 2019–2024 were analysed to characterise the temporal, geographical, and demographic patterns of HMPV testing and positivity. Trends were compared between two distinct timeframes (2019–2023 and 2024) to identify potential shifts. Descriptive statistics summarised categorical variables as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges. Temporal trends and geographical distribution of case positivity were visualised graphically, including district-level maps of India. To identify deviations in monthly testing volume and positivity rates, thresholds based on the 90th percentile from historical data (minimum of three data points per laboratory–month combination) were established, using a color-coded system: green indicated acceptable limits, red indicated exceedance of thresholds, and white indicated insufficient data. Analysis of MIST data further provided insights into clinical presentations, risk factors, disease progression, management, and outcomes among HMPV-positive SARI cases. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2) and STATA/MP (version 17.0), while spatial visualisations were generated using QGIS (version 3.40.4). Missing age information was imputed using the median value, and missing district was replaced with the lab district. There were no other missing variables in the dataset.

Quality control and assurance of testing

All participating laboratories in both systems adhered to standardised protocols. The apex laboratory at the ICMR—National Institute of Virology (NIV) conducted External Quality Assurance (EQA) periodically to ensure consistent and accurate testing across the network.

Ethics approval

We used de-identified routine surveillance data from VRDLN, which did not require individual patient consent. The Institutional Human Ethics Committee (IHEC) of ICMR-NIE and all participating institutes/colleges approved the study (NIE/IHEC/201606-01 dated 12-08-2025). The MIST project obtained ethical approval from participating hospitals' ethics committees and ICMR-NIE's IHEC, with written informed consent secured from patients or their legal guardians before enrolment (NIE/IHEC/202110-01 dated 18-05-2022).

Role of funding source

VRDLN and MIST are funded by the Department of Health Research, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India. The funders had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation of results, or preparation of the manuscript.

Results

HMPV testing and positivity trends in VRDLN (2019–2024)

Between 2019 and 2024, VRDLN laboratories tested 31,780 patients for HMPV, with an overall positivity rate of 3.2% [1030/31,780]. Case positivity increased from 3.2% [663/20,625] during 2019–2023 to 3.3% [367/11,155] in 2024. Monthly testing remained low (<400 patients) from 2019 to 2022, with peak positivity rates observed in early 2019. There was reduced testing during the pandemic years of 2020–2022. Testing volumes surged in 2023, with 800–2500 tests done monthly. In 2024, testing increased further (400–1600 monthly), with a peak positivity of 10.2% in January, declining to 2.3% by March and stabilising at 0.5–2.0% during May–December (Fig. 1). Between 2019 and 2023, we found that over 50% of the reported positivity cases came from the states of Haryana, Delhi, Tamil Nadu, Odisha, and West Bengal. In 2024, the cases were additionally noted in Bihar, Himachal Pradesh, and Karnataka, highlighting the geographic spread and prevalence of HMPV across various regions in India (Fig. 2).

Positivity proportions were comparable across genders, 3.2% [402/12,274] in females vs. 3.3% [191/6286] in males in 2019–2023 and 3.6% in males and 3.0% in females in 2024. We did not notice any major shift in positivity proportions across the two time periods in any of the variables examined. The threshold analysis for HMPV testing across 41 labs in 2024 did not identify any significant deviations in the number of tests performed or positivity rates across most of the examined lab–month combinations. However, some variability in test volumes was observed in Pondicherry and Kolkata due to higher testing activity (Supplementary Figures S1-S3).

Co-infections were relatively infrequent but varied across pathogens and study periods. Rhinovirus was detected in 8.0% [53/663] of HMPV-positive cases from 2019 to 2023, increasing to 14.2% [24/367] in 2024. Adenovirus was found in 8.0% [53/663] of cases during 2019–2023, declining to 2.3% [5/367] in 2024. Parainfluenza virus was identified in 4.5% [30/663] of cases during 2019–2023, with a slight increase to 6.7% [17/367] in 2024. Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) co-infection was observed in 3.5% [23/663] of cases from 2019 to 2023 and decreased to 1.6% [6/367] in 2024. Influenza A virus was detected in 2.3% [15/663] and 1.9% [7/367] of cases during these respective periods, while Influenza B was detected in 0.9% [6/663] and 1.4% [5/367], respectively (Supplementary Table S1).

Impact of the covid pandemic on the testing results

We conducted a supplementary analysis comparing key characteristics across three distinct timeframes: pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic periods. Our findings indicate a notable increase in testing volume from 1746 samples pre-pandemic to 1974 during the pandemic and substantially rising to 27,056 in the post-pandemic period. Although the overall positivity showed a slight decline (from 3.7% [65/1746] pre-pandemic, 3.5% [74/1974] during the pandemic, to 3.2% [864/27,056] post-pandemic), the number of positive cases significantly increased post-pandemic due to expanded surveillance coverage. Age-wise, children aged 1–2 years consistently exhibited the highest positivity across all periods (5.5% [13/236] pre-pandemic, 5.1% [13/253] pandemic, and 4.5% [170/3804] post-pandemic). Gender-wise differences were minimal, with positivity slightly higher among males, pre-pandemic (4% [43/1075]) and among females during the pandemic (4.2% [31/744]). In the post-pandemic period, positivity was nearly equal between males (3.1% [511/16,255]) and females (3.3% [353/10,801]. Clinically, Acute Respiratory Illness (ARI/ILI) showed a consistent HMPV positivity around 3.9% across pre-, during, and post-pandemic periods, whereas Severe Acute Respiratory Infections (SARI) notably increased in the post-pandemic with a positivity of 3.6% [99/2768] from 2.2% [6/271] during the pandemic period (Supplementary Table S2).

Case series of HMPV positive cases from MIST (2022–2023)

The MIST project tested 3599 SARI patients and identified 28 (0.8%) HMPV-positive cases (one co-infection with Influenza A H3N2). Among these, children aged 0–5 years represented the largest group (13 (46.4%) cases), followed by adults aged 19–60 years with 8 (28.6%) cases, reflecting the higher proportions of testing done among children. Unequal representation was observed between urban 18 (64.3%) and rural 10 (35.7%) residences, with LPG being the primary cooking fuel in nearly all households (n = 1527). The median household size was 4 (IQR: 3–5), and most households had a separate cooking area (n = 1426) (Table 2). Detailed clinical assessments revealed elevated heart rates (median 120 bpm) and respiratory rates (median 25 breaths/min). 12 (42.9%) out of the 28 patients required supplemental oxygen therapy. Treatment regimens frequently included antibiotics (e.g., ceftriaxone, Oseltamivir) and bronchodilators (e.g., salbutamol). Complications were rare, with only one case of hyperglycemia and cardiac ischemia reported. The median hospital stay was seven days, with 8 (28.6%) patients requiring Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission, and the duration of ICU stay was a median of 3.5 days, with 2.5–6 days. Laboratory findings showed elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) (median: 40.8 mg/L, IQR: 8.7–80), and chest X-rays indicated diffuse infiltration in fourcases. Computed Tomography (CT) scans were performed in seven patients out of 28. Clinical diagnoses included acute respiratory infection/acute bronchitis (12 (42.9%) cases), acute febrile illness (6 (21.4%) cases), and other conditions such as acute pulmonary edema (2 (7.1)) and tuberculosis reactivation (1 (3.6%)). The median duration of illness from onset to discharge was 11 days (IQR: 7.5–14.5) (Table 3).

At discharge, 25 (89.3%) cases showed clinical improvement, with one requiring transfer to another facility. At one-month follow-up, 22 (84.6%) were healthy (4 could not be traced), and as of April 2025, 12 (54.5%) were healthy, while 3 (13.6%) experienced ongoing complications; other cases could not be contacted after multiple attempts. 20 (71.4%) cases were unvaccinated, while eight had received one dose, six had two doses, and one had three doses of the COVID-19 vaccine. Among 13 cases aged less than five years, 7 (53.9%) were partially immunised, and 6 (46.2%) were fully immunised. Antibiotic use within two weeks prior to admission was reported by 17 (60.7%) patients, and a majority (n = 22%) had no pre-existing medical conditions (Supplementary Table S3). Exposure history, one often undertook long-distance intercity travel for work, and one had pets at home. Exposure to birds, poultry, or wild animals was reported by two patients, while three had attended social gatherings within 14 days prior to illness onset. Regarding lifestyle factors, one patient was a past smoker; there were no current smokers. Additionally, two patients had a family member who smoked (Supplementary Table S4).

Comparision of diseases severity between HMPV positive and negative cases

Cardiac ischemia and hyperglycemia were both observed more frequently in HMPV-positive patients (each 3.6%) compared to negative cases. Additionally, the need for oxygen support was higher among HMPV-positive patients (42.9%) than among negative patients (32.1%). Oral rehydration therapy was also more frequently required by HMPV-positive cases (39.3%) compared to negative cases (17.7%). However, the median duration of oxygen support was similar between groups (3 days [IQR: 3–5] for HMPV-positive vs. 4 days [IQR: 3–6] for HMPV-negative cases), with no significant difference identified. Length of hospital stay (IPD/ICU) was also comparable between groups. Post-discharge outcomes at one month indicated slightly higher proportion of complications and deaths in the negative group (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

Our analysis provides a comprehensive examination of HMPV testing trends in India, drawing on data from the VRDLN and the MIST project. The overall HMPV positivity rate was 3.2%, showing a slight increase from 2019 to 2023 to 2024. Children exhibited high positivity proportions, underscoring the propensity of HMPV infections to occur in the younger age groups. This study revealed higher positivity rates during the monsoon and winter months, suggesting potential seasonal influences. Our study found that HMPV is closely associated with SARI and ARI in children, and the average hospital stay for HMPV-positive patients was around a week. However, it is important to note that HMPV detection in our surveillance population reflects a potential association with illness and should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of causation.

Our study reports that the overall HMPV positivity rate was 3.2%, showing a slight increase from 2019 to 2023 to 2024. This result aligns with several global studies reporting varying estimates. For instance, a multi-centre prospective study in China found a 5.3% prevalence in hospitalised children,26 while a study in the United States identified prevalence ranging from 2.2% to 10.5%.27 Indian studies show similar variability. For example, one study conducted from November 2022 to March 2023 reported a 9.6% positivity,10 whereas studies conducted in Chennai and Uttar Pradesh found a 4% positivity.11,28 Similarly, a multi-site surveillance study for influenza in India reported that the HMPV was detected in 3.4% of ARI cases and 2.6% of SARI cases.29 A meta-analysis of 75 articles reported a global HMPV prevalence of approximately 6% among patients hospitalised with ARI.5 These variations may reflect differences in study design, population characteristics, and testing methodologies. However, most studies, including ours, report HMPV positivity below 10%. Our study found that the highest incidence occurred amongst children <2 years, accounting for 5–10% of paediatric hospitalisations for acute lower respiratory tract infections. Children under six months are three times more likely to be hospitalised compared to those aged 6 months to 5 years.30,31 This aligns with observations from other studies, showing the highest incidence among children under two years (7.6%), and incidence declined with increasing age.32

This study revealed that the positivity was higher in January, suggesting seasonality. This result aligns with observations of many global studies that report that HMPV infections exhibit distinct seasonal patterns. One study reported peak infections in late winter and early spring in the northern hemisphere,31 while a cohort study observed distinct biennial seasonality.33 Similarly, the studies from Canada and Croatia reported peak incidences in January–April and winter or spring months, respectively.34,35 The WHO notes that HMPV typically spreads in late winter and spring alongside other respiratory viruses.6 Further, studies in China and India documented peak incidences between December and April, with one Indian study reporting a February 2013 peak.23,36 Similarly, a retrospective analysis of surveillance data reported that HMPV circulated from the monsoon to winter seasons, peaking between September and December.12 These findings align with our data showing elevated positivity during monsoon and winter months.

Our study found that HMPV is closely associated with SARI and ARI in children. An Egyptian clinic-based study highlighted HMPV's significant clinical impact on infants and children.37 A study in China reported a 7.1% HMPV prevalence among SAsRI patients.38 Similarly, an Indian hospital-based study found HMPV to be the predominant virus among 17 detected in SARI cases, with a positivity of 72.9%.23 Our study corroborates these findings, indicating HMPV's strong association with fever, cough, and SARI cases. Hospitalisation due to HMPV can result in significant healthcare burdens. Our study found that the average hospital stay for HMPV-positive patients was 6.5 days. The possibility of bacterial co-infections contributing to disease severity and prolonged hospitalisation is an important consideration. Although the surveillance network primarily focuses on viral respiratory pathogens and does not systematically test for bacterial pathogens across all sites, we acknowledge this limitation. A similar study in Canada reported an average stay of 4.5 days, with one-third of patients hospitalised for over seven days, highlighting the severity of HMPV infections.39 Similarly, a study in Vietnam found a median hospitalisation duration of five days.40 A systematic review estimated that HMPV accounts for approximately 5% of SARI hospitalisations or severe pneumonia cases in Africa.41 These observations align with our results and highlight the need for enhanced clinical management to mitigate the burden of HMPV on healthcare systems. In the PERCH multi-country case–control study, human metapneumovirus (HMPV) was identified as one of the leading viral pathogens associated with severe pneumonia in children aged 1–59 months. HMPV was detected in approximately 6.9% of pneumonia cases compared to 2.3% of healthy community controls, indicating a significant association with disease but also indicating that its presence alone does not confirm causation.42 In the Drakenstein Child Health Study, HMPV was often found along with other viruses in children with pneumonia. This makes it hard to say if HMPV alone caused the illness. Co-detections were common, suggesting that the presence of HMPV may not directly imply causality.43

While our study provides valuable insights into HMPV epidemiology in India, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our analysis relies on data collected through the VRDLN and MIST surveillance networks, which may not capture all HMPV cases nationally, particularly in regions with limited diagnostic infrastructure. Also, the absence of healthcare utilisation data from the study areas restricts our ability to fully assess the representativeness and generalisability of the findings. Future studies would benefit from incorporating healthcare utilisation surveys to better understand health-seeking behaviors, referral biases, and the extent of community-level disease burden. Therefore, our findings might not be fully representative or generalisable across the broader Indian population. Having said that, in the absence of any alternative facilities for HMPV testing, this laboratory-based surveillance strategy offers the best available estimate of the community-level HMPV burden. Although a key limitation, this does not preclude us from drawing valid inferences about the population that was tested. Second, the slight increase in positivity observed between the periods 2019–2023 and 2024 may reflect changes in testing practices, healthcare access, or reporting standards rather than a genuine epidemiological shift. Additionally, our study overrepresents the pediatric population; consequently, data for other age groups, particularly elderly, immunocompromised, and community-dwelling individuals, are limited. As a result, the actual burden of HMPV infection, especially among asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic individuals who are unlikely to seek testing, may be underestimated. Third, our findings are based on a relatively small number of positive cases, potentially limiting statistical power and generalisability. However, the consistency of testing practices and numerators over the analysed period provides a robust basis for identifying meaningful trends and deviations. Finally, detection of HMPV alone does not confirm causality—especially in young children—as highlighted by large studies like PERCH and the Drakenstein Child Health Study (42, 43). It is also important to recognise that laboratory-based surveillance inherently cannot yield population-level estimates of disease burden, yet it remains highly valuable for detecting temporal trends and shifts in disease epidemiology.

Our study has some design-related limitations. Mainly, we did not include a control group, so we cannot compare how common HMPV is in people with symptoms vs. those without symptoms. Additionally, bacterial infections were not tested in our network, so we could not fully understand how bacterial co-infections might affect how serious the illness is.

Conclusions

Our findings highlight that HMPV testing is predominantly done in children and HMPV positivity is high in them. The slight increase in positivity from 2019 to 2023 to 2024 suggests stable epidemiological trends, with higher positivity observed during monsoon and winter months. The results underscore the critical role of surveillance networks like VRDLN and MIST in tracking respiratory virus trends. Despite relatively low positivity, the clinical severity of HMPV cases, particularly among paediatric patients, emphasises the need for targeted diagnostics and preventive strategies. Strengthening global partnerships, investing in research, and developing innovative healthcare solutions will be vital to mitigate the impact of HMPV and other emerging pathogens.
