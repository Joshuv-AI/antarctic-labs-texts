Is the neighbourhood environment associated with indicators of health in children and adolescents? Developing and testing a new proof-of-concept Healthy Environments Index for Children in Taranaki, New Zealand
Authors: Jesse Whitehead, Melody Smith, Yvonne Anderson, José G B Derraik, Niamh Donnellan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe describe the development of a comprehensive proof-of-concept index of environmental exposures for children based on evidence-informed connections to health behaviours– the Healthy Environments Index for Children (HEIC) - with two sub-indices relating to the food environment (HEIC-FE) and physical activity environment (HEIC-PA) in Taranaki, New Zealand. Associations between this theory-informed index and health outcomes in a cohort of children and adolescents identified with overweight or obesity and enrolled in a community-based healthy lifestyle programme and randomised controlled trial were examined.MethodsThe HIEC was developed using Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and 15 variables selected from a series of systematic literature reviews identifying environmental factors associated with childhood obesity. Activity spaces around each participant’s residential address, and the route to their nearest school were created and used to estimate environmental exposure. Health data from the Whānau Pakari randomised controlled trial (n = 179 at baseline, 121 at 12-months, 95 at 24-months) was integrated to test associations between HEIC and health outcomes. Statistical analyses included spearman rank correlations, multinomial linear regression, and geographically weighted regression.ResultsHigher HEIC scores (indicating health-promoting environments) tended to be clustered within the cities and towns, while rural areas had low HEIC scores. Strong and consistent associations were not identified between HEIC indices and health outcomes in our study population. However, higher HEIC food environments were associated with increased water intake and decreased sweet drink intake at 24-months.ConclusionsThe theory-informed HEIC and its two subindices may be useful tools for policy and practice aiming at improving child health outcomes. However, they require validation in larger studies in other areas of New Zealand.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12942-025-00409-7.

Background

Where a child lives can have a significant impact on their health [1]. Associations between the housing environment and childhood health have been identified [2], and evidence exists for the impact of neighbourhood environments (especially the role of street design and outdoor recreation settings) on children’s physical fitness and activity [3]. A wealth of cross-sectional evidence demonstrates clear associations between children’s physical activity and residing in “walkable” neighbourhood environments (i.e. those with better connected streets, a greater mix of land uses and higher population density) [3–5]. Neighbourhoods with infrastructure that allows safe walking and wheeling, and that have access to places to be active (e.g., parks, greenspaces, and playgrounds) are also important to support children’s health-promoting levels of activity [4]. [6] [7].

Similarly, causal evidence exists for the impact of the food environment on enabling or hindering healthy eating, particularly in terms of exposure to advertising and volume of ultraprocessed foods from fast food outlets and convenience stores [8]. Importantly, evidence suggests inequity exists in food environment exposure. For example, studies have shown that neighbourhoods (including areas around schools) with higher household deprivation [9–11] have comparatively more fast food, takeaway, and convenience outlets than less socioeconomically deprived areas. The same pattern by socio-economic status has been reported for unhealthy food marketing around schools [11]. [12]

Ultimately, environments that make it more difficult for children to be physically active and eat more healthily will likely foster suboptimal health outcomes, through contributing to unhealthy body weight and associated physical and psychological comorbidities, including reduced quality of life [13–15]. Accordingly, when weight status (or related comorbidities) is the outcome of interest, it is important to consider both the physical activity and nutrition environments, otherwise studies are not adequately considering children and young people’s context. For instance, it is possible that environments that support active travel modes (e.g., walking or biking– and thus are “health promoting”) could increase exposure to unhealthy food environments and thus could negate the benefits of increased physical activity [16]. Research using participatory mapping with children has shown that parks with multiple options for engagement are important physical and social settings for children, as are shopping areas where children are reportedly more likely to consume unhealthy food and drinks [17]. While home environments [18], the behaviours and decisions of parents, [19] and school-based programmes [20] are important areas for intervention, understanding the wider built environment and social context is also key [21]. Pedrick-Case et al., [22] in their study protocol, emphasise the need for research to understand the complex and interacting factors in the built environment that influence childhood non-communicable diseases such as obesity. The importance of simultaneous consideration of factors associated with health was also highlighted by Marek et al. [23] who demonstrated co-occurrence of factors that supported or hindered health in their Healthy Living Index for Aotearoa New Zealand (hereafter NZ). However, this index did not have a child-centred focus, including for example, alcohol and gambling outlets as important parameters. When considered together, the evidence suggests there are negative health impacts for children residing in areas where environmental opportunities for physical activity are low and access to suboptimal nutrition environments is high (i.e. “obesogenic” or obesity-promoting environments). In a large county in the southeastern United States, Hughey and colleagues [24] developed a multicomponent built environment measure for youth using kernel density, and detailed audit of data on public parks, food stores, and restaurants. Findings showed that health-promoting built environments were associated with lower weight status in youth.

It is noteworthy that there are conflicting findings on these relationships, likely due, in part at least, to heterogeneous studies including varying approaches to measurement of exposures and outcomes [14]. [25] Calls to improve the evidence base have been made, for example through increased use of anthropometric outcome measures [8], consensus and consistency in defining and measuring environmental factors and weight-related outcomes [14], [26] improved reporting of study methods [27], improved sensitivity and specificity in approaches being used (e.g., measures appropriate to the population of interest) [28], and use of more “complete” datasets (e.g., via ground-truthing using in-person [29] or virtual audits [30]. Advances have been made in generating objective child-specific measures that are more appropriate for children than the earlier adult-centric approaches. For instance, Kaczynski et al. [31] developed a national childhood obesogenic environment index for the United States (also see Pearce et al.’s work in this area [32]). The child-specific school walkability index [28] and Neighbourhood Destination Accessibility Index– Child [33] are two pertinent examples of child-centric geographic information systems(GIS)-based measures of physical activity environments. The use of Google Street View [12] and passive photography [34] have also yielded important insights on children’s exposure to food (and other health-related) environments, which can also be integrated with GIS [30]. However, no child-specific measure has been generated that simultaneously considers both the food and physical activity environment.

In this study, we describe the development and proof-of-concept of a comprehensive index of environmental exposures for children developed from evidence-based connections to health behaviours– the Healthy Environments Index for Children (HEIC). We then examine associations between the index and accelerometer-derived physical activity, self-reported or parent proxy dietary intake, health-related quality of life, and weight status in a cohort of children and adolescents identified with overweight or obesity enrolled in a community-based healthy lifestyle programme. We conduct preliminary testing of associations with health outcomes.

Setting

Any approach to measuring food and physical activity environments should be context-specific and relevant. This research was undertaken with data from the Taranaki region, on the West coast of NZ’s North Island, which is a mixed urban-rural population of approximately 25,000 children aged 0–15 years, of whom 19.8% identify as Māori (NZ’s Indigenous population) at the time of this study [35]. The Taranaki region has historically experienced high levels of childhood obesity [36], and was therefore prioritised as a setting for this study. The city of New Plymouth had approximately 84,400 residents in 2020 [37], accounting for 69% of the Taranaki population, and is essentially urban. On the other hand, other areas of Taranaki such as Stratford and South Taranaki districts consist of much smaller rural towns, with different nutritional and physical activity environments, and levels of infrastructure. Thus, Taranaki’s urban-rural geographic setting and diverse demographic composition makes the region an optimal setting to undertake this study.

Whānau Pakari

Whānau Pakari is a multidisciplinary healthy lifestyle programme for children and adolescents affected by overweight and obesity in Taranaki [38]. It aims to provide a community-based, family-focused approach to providing support for healthy lifestyle change. A randomised clinical trial was embedded within the programme and its outcomes were reported elsewhere [39]. [40] Briefly, from 2012 to 2016, baseline, 6-, 12-, and 24-month data were collected, including clinical information such as body mass index (BMI) standard deviation score (SDS), dietary intake, and physical activity [38]. This programme aimed to reach and engage with those most affected by obesity in the region, namely Māori and those living in the areas of highest socioeconomic deprivation. This was achieved by ensuring culturally appropriate and acceptable service provision [39]. Baseline data from the Whānau Pakari cohort (98% with a BMI ≥ 98th percentile, and 2% with a BMI 91st -98th percentile but with weight-related comorbidities at entry) found a higher prevalence of suboptimal dietary behaviours and significant differences in dietary intake when compared with national counterparts [41]. Low levels of physical activity were also identified, with the vast majority not meeting national physical activity recommendations [42].

Stage 1: conceptualising the HEIC

A total of 15 variables for the HEIC were selected based on a comprehensive series of systematic literature reviews led by Jia [15], which identified basic environmental factors that are associated with childhood obesity. Each systematic review also indicated the overall strength and direction of associations between environmental factors and weight status. This information was used to group factors into three categories; those with a with a positive influence (i.e., reduced risk of childhood obesity); those with a negative influence (i.e., increased risk of obesity); and those where the association is unclear. Environmental factors with a clear association were ranked by importance according to the strength and volume of evidence supporting the association with childhood obesity. Given the study context, priority was given to research from NZ. A ranking of 1 represents strong and repeated findings, 2 represents consistent findings; 3 represents weaker and less consistent findings; 4 represents weak and inconsistent findings. These rankings were then used to allocate weights that would be applied to each factor when developing the HEIC. Weights of 1.0 were applied for positive factors with a ranking of 1, with a 0.1 reduction in weighting for each step down the rankings to 4 (with a weighting of 0.7). Factors with an overall unclear association according to the systematic literature review were not ranked but were given a low positive or negative weighting based on strength of evidence (taking into consideration the number of studies demonstrating consistent associations, geographic spread, and individual study characteristics). It is important to note that these weightings are not intended to be concrete and conclusive settings for a definitive or validated HEIC. The purpose of this paper is to outline the development of the HEIC, and it is expected that the precise weightings used could vary with different contexts to reflect the importance of various environmental factors in different settings. In a series of reviews, Jia reported that some natural environmental factors were linked to health outcomes. For example, temperature was associated with reduced physical activity and high altitude with increased BMI [55] but these were excluded from the HEIC because they are not modifiable features of the built environment. Full-service restaurants [56], neighbourhood aesthetics [57], and urban sprawl [58] were found to have unclear associations with childhood obesity. Since the evidence surrounding these factors was insufficient, mixed, or changing over time, they were excluded from the HEIC.

Developing the HEIC and linking it to participants involved three further steps: (2a) GIS analysis to create the HEIC; (2b) Creating activity spaces for Whānau Pakari participants; and (2c) Linking HEIC scores to Whānau Pakari data.

(2a) GIS analysis to create the HEIC

First, a kernel density estimate (KDE) analysis of point and line features (factors 2, 5–8, and 11–15) was performed in ArcGIS 10.7 [64]. For each KDE analysis the cell size was set to 10 m, with a search radius of 800 m. While there is no agreed-upon optimal buffer size for us with children, a Finnish study where children mapped places of importance showed 47% of meaningful places were outside the study’s 500 m street network buffer employed [65]. Additionally, a systematic review of GIS-based approaches to measuring children’s physical activity geographies have shown buffer distances of approximately 800 m around children’s homes performed optimally for identifying associations with physical activity outcomes [26]. To estimate street connectivity (factor 1), street networks were converted to points indicating junction locations (and representing connectivity) following the most common GIS approach identified by Jia et al. [5] The density of these connections was then expressed using KDE analysis. Similarly, for factor 2, data on greenspace were first converted to points before KDE was carried out. A land use mix measure (factor 3) was calculated based on entropy [66] following an approach described by Donnellan [67], whereby land use zoning data provided by councils were re-classified into the following six categories: commercial, residential, retail/industrial, institutional, open space, and other (e.g., vacant land). Entropy is a representation of land-use mix which is important for understanding how the built environment influences active transport. The land use mix for each meshblock area unit [68] in the Taranaki region was then calculated using the entropy index described by Mavoa et al., [69] allowing local-level land use mix relevant to walkability to be examined. The sum of meshblock land area was used to calculate the proportion of land within each meshblock attributed to a particular use. The resulting entropy values range from 0 (indicating homogenous land uses) to values closer to 1 (indicating greater heterogeneity of land use). Once calculated, the entropy index was converted into Raster format, so that it could be incorporated within the HEIC. The development of a KDE of traffic calming features (factor 9) included equal weightings of the following three features: (1) child- and active-transport-specific traffic signs; (2) speed bumps; and (3) pedestrian crossings. Speed limit data (factor 10) was converted to a raster layer of speed limits across the Taranaki region, which was then integrated into the HEIC in the next step.

A python script was developed to convert each KDE into Z-scores, allowing them to be meaningfully combined [70]. [71] Z-scores for each environmental feature were weighted according to Table 1 and combined into a single index using the Raster Calculator function in ArcGIS. This output was then normalised to a scale from 0 to 10 (representing the least and most healthy environments for children respectively).

‘Sub-scores’ were also created to represent two components of the HEIC– the physical activity environment (HEIC-PA), and the food environment (HEIC-FE). The same approach for developing the HEIC– as outlined above– was followed, except at the final stage z-scores for environmental features 1–10 were combined into the ‘HEIC Physical Activity’ sub-score. This was normalised, on a scale from 0 (physical activity constraining) to 10 (physical activity promoting) environments. Similarly, environmental features 11–15 were combined into the ‘HEIC Food Environment’ sub-score which represents health promoting and constraining food environments.

(2b) creating activity spaces

Child specific activity spaces were developed for each participant in the current study. Participant residential addresses were geocoded and an 800 m road network buffer around children’s home addresses and their nearest school was created. While a zoning system means that children generally attend their nearest school, this is not always the case, and a limitation of this research is that we did not have information of which school children attended. A 200 m buffer around the estimated route between home and school was also created and included in the activity space to link the home and school environment. This approach is based on recommendations that environmental exposure be considered at multiple locations [72], previous work combining home, school, and daily transport environments [73], and research suggesting that the ideal size of an activity space for adults is an 800 m to 1 km road network buffer [74]. A systematic search and scoping review on children’s physical activity geographies also outlined the trade-off between larger buffer sizes being more likely to identify relationships, while smaller buffers increase geographic variability between children [26]. Due to residential mobility across the trial period, three sets of activity spaces were created based on home addresses provided at baseline (n = 235), 12-month (n = 157), and 24-month (n = 136) assessments.

(2c) linking HEIC scores to Whānau Pakari data

Each participant in the Whānau Pakari study was assigned a HEIC score based on their estimated average exposure to environmental features that positively or negatively impact physical activity and dietary intake. Since each 10 m cell in the study area has a unique HEIC value, the Zonal Statistics as a Table function in ArcGIS was used to calculate an average score based on HEIC values located within each activity space boundary.

The average value of HEIC scores within each activity space was then linked to each participant. This was repeated for baseline, 12-month, and 24-month activity spaces, as well as for the HEIC-PA and HEIC-FE sub-scores. This information was subsequently linked to children’s socio-demographic variables (age at assessment, sex, ethnicity (coded as Māori and non-Māori), and area-level socioeconomic deprivation [75]), potential confounding variables (season of assessment, trial randomisation group, and siblingship), and our outcome variables of interest as outlined in Table 3 were as follows: BMI SDS (i.e., adjusted for age and sex); ‘Physical activity’– activity expressed in minutes per day, derived from a children’s physical activity questionnaire (C-PAQ) [76]; and ‘Physical fitness’– time taken (minutes) to complete the 550-m walk/run test [77]. Self-reported outcomes included: servings of fruit and/or vegetables per day; consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages (ml per day); water intake (ml per day); health-related quality of life was reported by both children and their caregivers, using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) [78].

An initial review of the dataset revealed a normal distribution of HEIC, and HEIC-PA scores across the study sample. However, the HEIC-FE scores were highly skewed (see Supplementary Fig. 1) and a cluster of scores between 5.8 and 6.0 was identified. Further investigation revealed that these scores were associated with participants living in rural areas of Taranaki where the food environment is relatively homogenous. It was decided that, to ensure a robust statistical investigation, analysis would be limited to urban participants living in small (1,000–9,999 residents) and large urban areas (30,000–99,999 residents) [79]. Therefore 56 participants living in rural areas were excluded at baseline. The challenges of developing an HEIC for rural areas are detailed in the discussion section. It was also decided that, at each study period, only participants with complete data would be included. The statistical approach used complete case analysis to ensure that models were fitted on a complete and comparable study population, and multiple imputation for missing data was deemed unsuitable. Therefore, a further 58 and 26 participants were subsequently excluded at 12 and 24 months, respectively, due to incomplete data.

Stage 3: statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

The key predictors of interest were the overall HEIC, the HEIC-PA, and HEIC-FE. However, examination of the data on all participants with baseline data (n = 235) indicated that HEIC was almost entirely a reflection of the HEIC-PA values, and the two indices were very strongly correlated (Bonferroni adjusted Spearman rank correlation, r = 0.95, p < 0.001). As a result, analyses focused on the two subsets, since the overall HEIC would provide redundant results.

Spearman rank correlations

A spearman rank correlation matrix (with Bonferroni adjustment) between all variables of interest was calculated at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months in R Statistical Software. Age at assessment and area-level socioeconomic deprivation were included as potential confounding variables.

Multinomial linear regression

A series of linear models (estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression) were fitted in R to predict each outcome variable of interest, using HEIC-PA, HEIC-FE, as well as all other outcome variables plus age at assessment, sex, and area-level socioeconomic deprivation as predictor variables. These models were created separately at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months. For instance, Model 1 was fitted to predict BMI SDS at baseline, the following predictor variables were included: HEIC-PA (at baseline); HEIC-FE (at baseline); age at assessment; area-level socioeconomic deprivation; sex; physical activity levels; physical fitness; fruit and vegetable intake; water intake; sweet drink intake; Child PedsQL score; and caregiver PedsQL score.

Geographically weighted regression

The HEIC and participants’ outcome data include spatial information, and we considered that therefore, both outcome variables (e.g., body size, physical activity, and diet) and predictor variables (e.g., HEIC, socioeconomic status) may have varied spatially. Furthermore, the influence of predictor variables on outcome variables may also have varied spatially. In these situations, geographically weighted regression (GWR) models can be useful for dealing with the spatial autocorrelation and multicollinearity of variables [80]. To determine whether a GWR approach was appropriate for this research, we first tested whether any outcome variables were spatially autocorrelated, but the only finding with evidence of statistically significant clustering (at p < 0.05) related to fast-food servings at 24-months. The input variables of ethnicity, socioeconomic deprivation, and the HEIC-PA, and HEIC-FE subscores displayed clustering at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months. The HEIC showed clustering at baseline and 12 months (see supplementary Table 6 for results related to GWR testing). Since some spatial autocorrelation appeared to be present, we then tested for multicollinearity between variables. Most variables included within a test OLS model showed very little collinearity (all variables had a variable inflation factor (VIF) < 5). The exceptions were child and adult ‘health-related quality of life’ measures, and the HIEC and HEIC sub-scores. This was to be expected as child and parent/caregiver-reported PedsQL measures are likely to be related, while the HEIC is comprised of the HEIC-PA and HEIC-FE sub-scores. Finally, we tested whether GWR models had better explanatory power compared to linear models, finding no improvement with identical r2 values in each model. Therefore, our GWR results are not reported here.

Correlation analysis results

Neither the HEIC-PA nor the HEIC-FE indices were correlated with outcome variables of interest at baseline or 12 months. Further, no statistically significant associations between HEIC-PA and any outcome variables of interest were identified at 24 months. However, a higher HEIC-FE index was correlated with increased water intake at 24 months ( ρ= 0.25, p < 0.01), reduced sweet drink intake at 24 months (ρ=-0.22, p < 0.01), and higher area-level socioeconomic deprivation at baseline ( ρ= 0.19, p < 0.05). It was also noted that, at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months, the physical activity and food environment sub-indices of HEIC were negatively and significantly correlated (ρ=-0.39, -0.42, and -0.47, respectively, all p < 0.01), indicating that children living in areas with healthier physical activity environments tended to also live in areas with less healthy food environments and vice versa. At baseline, BMI SDS and physical activity levels were also correlated with area-level socioeconomic deprivation (for both, ρ= 0.20, p < 0.01), while various other statistically significant correlations between outcome variables were noted and are reported in supplementary Tables 1–3.

Multinomial linear regression results

Model 1 (baseline BMI SDS) explained a moderate proportion of variance (r2 = 0.27, F(12, 112) = 3.50, p < 0.001, adj. r2 = 0.19). However, neither HEIC-PA nor HEIC-FE were predictive of BMI SDS at baseline. Physical fitness was negatively associated with BMI SDS (b = -0.38, 95% CI [-0.60, -0.16], t(112) = -3.46, p < 0.001; β = -0.31, 95% CI [-0.49, -0.13]), but positively associated with male sex (b = 0.34, 95% CI [0.15, 0.54], t(112) = 3.43, p < 0.001; β = 0.59, 95% CI [0.25, 0.93]). However, all other variables were non-significant predictors (See supplementary Table 4 for full results of Model 1).

Discussion

Statement of principal findings

We have outlined the development and proof-of-concept of a novel, theoretically informed index– the Healthy Environments Index for Children (HEIC) - for examining environmental factors that are known to be important for child health. This included the utilisation of novel geospatial process to develop the HEIC and two sub-indices. The HEIC-FE represents the food environment, while the HEIC-PA represents the physical activity environment, with the latter found to heavily influence the overall HEIC score. Although further investigation into the reasons for this is required, the strong influence of the HEIC-PA sub-index may be an artefact of Taranaki’s mixed rural-urban setting. Zoning rules mean that variations in the food environment are effectively limited to certain neighbourhoods within urban areas and small settlements. This highlights some of the challenges around developing an index that effectively balances aspects of both the physical activity and food environments in a mixed rural-urban setting. Future research should examine a replication of the HEIC in other areas, including major cities where greater spatial heterogeneity can be found. It also highlights the challenges around attempting to resolve the conflict between needing context-specific food and physical activity measures, as well as the need for broad and consistent measures that can be compared across studies. In addition, further work could also examine whether different weightings for each of the factors in the index may improve results.

Strong or consistent associations were not identified between either of the HEIC indices and health outcomes in a relatively small sample of children and adolescents from a randomised control trial. However, at the 24-month assessment, a 'better' Food Environment index was associated with greater water intake, lower sweet drink intake, and both child and caregiver PedsQL. In other words, children whose activity spaces included areas with higher HEIC-FE scores (i.e., that had ‘healthier’ food environments) were more likely to consume more water and less sweetened beverages.

Meaning of the study: possible mechanisms and implications for clinicians or policymakers

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

The HEIC is a demonstration of a proof-of-concept for an evidence-informed child specific index, which to our knowledge is the first child-focused healthy environments index developed for the NZ context. Once further validated across other contexts, the HEIC is a potentially useful tool for clinicians, policy makers, and communities, which could help whānau (extended family, family group), schools, communities, clinicians and policy makers to better understand the health-relevant features of environments that children live in.

These types of tools have potential use amongst policy makers and public health advocates to identify geographic areas and communities that are most affected by unhealthy food and physical activity environments. In addition, local councils could use this information to support their planning and decision making relating to long-term strategic plans, the built-environment and infrastructure projects, and licensing decisions. While licensing is more commonly considered by councils in relation to alcohol outlets, there is potential for the HEIC to inform council decisions to approve or reject fast-food outlet or unhealthy food/drink licensing applications. For example, a UK review highlighted the legal impacts at a local authority level on (un)healthy food access, including through licencing and local planning. Examples included preventing the sale of unhealthy food near a school, and use of planning policies to “combat the proliferation of fast-food retailers” [81]. Interviews with senior representatives from state/territory governments, statutory authorities and non-government organizations in Australia showed appetite for limiting the density of fast-food outlets, but also the need for evidence of effectiveness and strategies to overcome a push for deregulation and private sector opposition [82].

Some limitations of this work should be noted. While Kaczynski et al. [31] have completed similar work at a national level in the United States, this was not possible for the NZ context given data constraints and the scope of the wider study. The decision to convert greenspace to multiple points likely amplified the influence of greenspace in the final index. Many of the features included in the HEIC are associated with urbanicity (e.g., land use mix, residential density, and lower speed limits), thereby resulting in low HEIC scores for many rural areas in the study region. As the HEIC is an evidence-informed index, with components included due to findings from systematic literature reviews, this could reflect a bias towards urban built environments in the current research literature. Further research is urgently needed to understand whether rural environments are ‘less healthy’ than urban environments, or whether a different approach needs to be taken to create rural indices of healthy environments more suitable for representing the reality of children in rural and mixed-urban-rural settings. A lack of high-quality accurate data about the environments that children residing in rural and remote areas are exposed to is an important data equity issue.

Challenges for developing environmental indices in rural areas include that: public environments are largely homogenous in rural areas; it is difficult to account for physical activity and food environments on private land when basing environmental indices on public data; exposure may not be as accurately estimated compared to urban environments; private vehicle reliance is common in rural communities, and are often required to access, food, education, and public recreation opportunities. An additional limitation is that the weightings applied to features in the index were not based on ‘child-voice’ of what they consider important. Future research is needed to assess whether these environmental features are what children consider as important features in their environments and for their health [83]. Furthermore, weightings could be improved with an alternative method (e.g. looking at which individual features were associated with key outcomes) however this was not feasible for the current study and care must be taken to avoid tautological approaches. The main purpose was to demonstrate proof-of-concept of a comprehensive index of environmental exposures for children. Based on evidence-informed connections to health behaviours for the Taranaki region, the index can be tested in other areas in future research and potentially refined or modified to suit different local, regional, or national contexts by applying appropriate weightings.

While activity spaces and HEIC exposures were estimated separately for residential location at baseline, 12-months and 24-months, meaning that residential mobility is somewhat accounted for in our analysis, we did not have information about the school each child attended. Therefore, we were unable to account for any possible school changes other than by continuing to assume that children attended the school closest to their home.

It is important to note that the limited number of statistically significant results was likely a result of our relatively small sample size, which might have limited our statistical power to detect other associations. In addition, the characteristics of the study sample do not reflect the overall population. Future research could expand the HEIC index to a national scale (depending on data availability) and then link HEIC scores to other, larger datasets, to better understand the potential associations between the HEIC and child health outcomes. Finally, our HEIC index and both PA and FE subindices are static measures of the food and physical activity environments, which have changed over time [84] and will likely continue to change into the future. Consequently, indices such as the HEIC require regularly updating to accurately reflect the environment.

Conclusion

This paper has described the development and proof-of-concept of a composite Healthy Environments Index for Children (HEIC), and two sub-indices relating to the food and physical activity environments. We examined associations between the indices and the health outcomes of children and adolescents living with overweight or obesity enrolled in a community-based healthy lifestyle programme. While analysis did not identify clear associations with child health outcomes, the HEIC food environments sub-index was significantly associated with increased water and decreased sweet drink intake. When further validated, the HEIC and the two subindices may be potentially useful tools for clinicians, community, whānau, policymakers and local councils wanting to improve child health outcomes.
