ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDarier disease (DD) is a rare, autosomal dominant genodermatosis caused by pathogenic variants in the ATP2A2 gene, encoding the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase. DD is characterized by chronic, recurrent cutaneous lesions and variable extracutaneous manifestations, resulting in significant impairment of quality of life. Disease onset typically occurs after puberty, with environmental triggers and variable expressivity.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to provide practical, evidence- and consensus-based recommendations for the management of patients with DD, particularly for healthcare professionals in non-expert centers, and to highlight emerging therapeutic avenues.MethodsRecommendations were formulated on the basis of a systematic review of the literature and expert consensus from the European Reference Network for Rare and Undiagnosed Skin Disorders (ERN-Skin). Evidence from case reports, case series, and mechanistic studies was considered to provide guidance on symptomatic and advanced targeted therapies.Results and RecommendationsThe panel agreed on a total of 68 recommendations.Diagnosis is primarily clinical, supported by family history, histopathology, cytodiagnosis, and, whenever possible, ATP2A2 genetic testing. Genetic counseling should be offered to patients and at-risk relatives.First-line management entails trigger avoidance, skin care using topical antiseptics, emollients, and anti-inflammatory agents, alongside symptomatic control. Key Summary Points

Darier disease (DD) is a genetic, chronic, and complex disorder with a high burden and significant impact on quality of life. Diagnosis relies on clinical features, family history, histopathology, and genetic testing. Genetic testing and counseling should be offered to all suspected patients and their relatives.Owing to its genetic etiology, permanent remission is currently not achievable. Treatments are symptomatic, aiming to control inflammation, reduce hyperkeratosis, and prevent secondary infections, thereby improving patient quality of life.Avoidance or minimization of environmental triggers is essential. Standard-of-care symptomatic therapy—including topical antiseptics, emollients, keratolytic and anti-inflammatory agents—should be implemented in all patients.Consider systemic therapy in patients with severe or refractory disease. Choice of agent should be individualized on the basis of clinical context (keratosis, infection, inflammation, lesion extent, comorbidities). Retinoids should be considered as first-line therapy. For other drugs, evidence is limited to case reports and series, and response may vary between patients. Individualized monitoring and adaptable treatment are essential.Clinical trials and multicenter registries are needed to evaluate efficacy, compare therapies, study combination strategies, and identify biomarkers or patient stratification criteria.

Introduction

Darier disease (DD), also known as dyskeratosis follicularis, Darier–White disease, or ATP2A2-nEDD (OMIM#124200; Orphanet ORPHA:218), is a genetic disorder characterized by both cutaneous and extracutaneous manifestations. The disease typically begins after puberty and follows a chronic course with recurrent exacerbations. DD has been reported worldwide, with a prevalence ranging from approximately 1:12,000 to 3.8:100,000 individuals [1–4].

The inheritance pattern is autosomal dominant with almost complete penetrance in adults but variable expressivity. DD results from monoallelic loss-of-function variants in the ATP2A2 gene, which encodes the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA2) protein. SERCA2, a calcium pump located in the endoplasmic reticulum, plays a pivotal role in maintaining calcium homeostasis in keratinocytes. The pathogenesis of DD is complex, involving the interplay between SERCA2 haploinsufficiency and various environmental factors [5–7].

The current therapeutic options are diverse but nonspecific and of limited efficacy, underscoring the need for improved strategies for patients, who often experience a substantial disease burden. In the era of molecular disease characterization and the emergence of targeted therapies, ongoing research efforts are increasingly directed toward developing and validating pathogenesis-based and corrective treatment approaches to improve patient outcomes [8]. Although several reviews have been published in recent years [8–11], the European Reference Network (ERN)-Skin (https://ern-skin.eu/) and an international expert task force have recognized the need for practical recommendations to support healthcare professionals (HCP) in the diagnosis and management of DD. Here, we aim at synthetic recommendations for diagnostic and treatment for HCP from non-expert centers to assist in the management of DD.

Methods

Literature Research

The recommendations are based on a systematic review of the literature combined with expert consensus reached within the ERN-Skin network and its associated international task force. The process followed established ERN methodological standards for guideline development, including evaluation of available evidence, assessment of clinical relevance, and formulation of practical statements to guide real-world care.

The systematic literature research was performed using the PubMed, Embase, and Medline databases from 2002 to 2023. The following key words were used: “Darier Disease OR Darier-White Disease OR Keratosis Follicularis” and “clinical presentation OR case series OR diagnostic OR physiology OR mutation OR (phenotype AND (gene OR genotype)) OR treatment OR therapy OR therapeutic OR management”. Altogether 2293 publications were identified, of which 621 dealt with DD. Exclusion criteria were: preclinical studies, articles without abstracts, and articles in a language other than English, French, and German. A total of 558 publications were screened, of which 471 were included in the assessment (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additional relevant publications from 2023 until September 2025 on “Darier disease and therapy” were also integrated. Each publication was assessed by two reviewers.

Expert Consensus: Development and Digital Implementation of the Delphi Method

To obtain expert consensus on the recommendations, the Delphi method [12] was applied within the members of expert centers of the ERN-Skin and the international task force. The Delphi method was implemented using a purpose-built digital platform designed to preserve its core principles of expert anonymity, iterative feedback, and controlled convergence. To support structured and asynchronous participation by an international panel of medical experts, the platform was custom-developed on a robust open-source framework (PHP DevShell 3.5, LGPL license).

The system architecture mirrors the methodological structure of the Delphi process and is organized into projects, successive rounds, and evaluated items. Experts were invited by email to rate items on a standardized 1–9 relevance scale and could provide optional qualitative comments, with the possibility to complete questionnaires over multiple sessions.

A dedicated back-office interface enabled coordinators to monitor participation, automatically aggregate responses, and generate descriptive statistics (medians, percentiles, and distribution graphs) to inform decisions between rounds. Expert responses were fully anonymized, nonresponders were excluded from subsequent rounds to maintain panel consistency, and all communications complied with General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) requirements.

Overall, this digital implementation transformed a traditionally labor-intensive Delphi process into a scalable, reproducible, and methodologically rigorous workflow while preserving analytical precision and methodological integrity.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. Consent was obtained for publication of patient photographs.

Results

On the basis of the literature review summarized below (detailed review available on request) and their expert opinion, the panel agreed on a total of 68 recommendations divided into seven groups (Fig. 1). These recommendations are intended to assist HCPs, particularly those working in nonexpert or general dermatology centers, in recognizing and appropriately managing patients with DD.

Clinical Manifestations

DD is characterized by multiple, symmetrically distributed, crusted, keratotic papules, primarily affecting seborrheic areas (Fig. 2). These may coalesce into large plaques which, especially in intertriginous areas, may become hypertrophic, fissured, eroded, and malodorous, often complicated by secondary infections and poor treatment response [13–16]. “Classical” lesions include mucocutaneous keratotic papules, palmar pits, wart-like acral papules, and nail abnormalities (Fig. 2). “Nonclassical” lesions, observed in about one-third of patients, include acral keratoderma, leukodermic macules, giant comedones, keloid-like vegetations, and acral hemorrhagic blisters [17]. These cutaneous manifestations may characterize rare and underrecognized clinical variants and may sometimes coexist in the same patient or family, contributing to the clinical heterogeneity of DD. The cutaneous extent of the disease is highly variable. In severe or untreated cases, the disease can rarely progress to erythroderma, scarring alopecia, leonine facies, and palmoplantar keratoderma [18–21]. Approximately 10% of patients exhibit localized, asymmetric forms, which include the segmental, unilateral, zosteriform patterns, reflecting type 1 or type 2 genetic mosaicism [16, 22–35]. DD runs a chronic and unpredictable course as flares can be triggered by a wide variety of factors such as heat, sweating, friction, UV exposure, stress, poor personal hygiene, hormonal fluctuations, major surgery, infections, vaccinations, and common medications such as lithium, oral contraceptives, calcium antagonists, azathioprine, and interferon [13, 36–47]. Infections, both systemic (e.g., coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)) and localized, are also recognized as exacerbating factors [40, 48–52]. Mucocutaneous lesions are frequently susceptible to infections by pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, Candida albicans, herpes simple virus (HSV), human papillomavirus, varicella-zoster virus, and poxvirus [10, 13, 53–58]. Most frequently, these infections are self-limited and should be suspected in case of rapidly worsening or treatment-resistant lesions, especially when they become painful, present with vesiculopustules, confluent erosions, or whitish exudates or if systemic signs and symptoms arise, such as lymphadenopathy, fever, and malaise [13, 59, 60] (Fig. 2). Disseminated infections may occasionally progress to Kaposi’s varicelliform eruption, erythroderma, sepsis, or fatal multiorgan failure [13, 19, 59–68].

Recent evidence has strengthened the association between DD and keratinocyte carcinomas on mucocutaneous DD lesions, both squamous and basal cell types [1]. These findings align with previous case reports [42, 62, 69–71] and molecular studies suggesting that SERCA2 haploinsufficiency may contribute to oncogenesis in human keratinocytes [14, 42, 69]. Additional malignancies occasionally described in association with DD include adnexal tumours, Paget’s disease, melanoma, and pulmonary small-cell squamous carcinoma [42, 72, 73].

Noninvasive Diagnostic Methods

New noninvasive imaging techniques are valuable tools in the early diagnosis of DD, in detecting recurrences, and in supporting differential diagnosis. Among these, dermoscopy is the most accessible and typically shows a central yellowish–brown area corresponding to a compact keratotic mass, surrounded by a whitish halo of acanthotic epidermis, often with a reddish background and dotted or linear vessels. Plaque-type lesions may display parallel pink–reddish furrows and characteristic “tire tread-like” or “cracked riverbed-like” patterns [74, 75].

In vivo confocal microscopy further enhances diagnostic accuracy by visualizing corps ronds, grains, and suprabasal clefts in real time, correlating well with the histological hallmarks of abnormal keratinization, acantholysis, and dyskeratosis [74]. Similarly, line-field confocal optical coherence tomography (LC-OCT) provides high-resolution vertical and horizontal images, revealing an epidermis of variable thickness with localized hyper-reflective stratum corneum, irregular hyporeflective suprabasal spaces, and scattered bright round cells with a targetoid appearance, while the dermal–epidermal junction and dermis remain unremarkable. These features closely mirror the histopathological pattern of DD, including hyperparakeratosis, suprabasal acantholytic clefts, and dyskeratotic cells such as grains and corps ronds [76].

Laboratory Diagnostics

Histopathology

Typical histopathological features include suprabasal clefts in the epithelium, accompanied by acantholysis and the presence of dyskeratotic cells known as “corps ronds” and “corps grains” (Fig. 3a). “Corps ronds” are larger structures typically found in the granular layer and contain irregular eccentric, and sometimes pyknotic nuclei. The underlying papillary dermis is covered by a single layer of basal cells and is forming villi extending into these clefts. Subcellular compartmentalization of stromal interaction molecule 1 (STIM1) may distinguish DD from Hailey–Hailey disease (HHD) [77]. If a skin sample is not available, cytodiagnosis (i.e., Tzanck test) may be helpful in indicating the presence of dyskeratotic and acantholytic cells [78, 79].

Genetic Testing

As over 300 pathogenic ATP2A2 variants without mutagenic hotspots, including missense, nonsense, splice-site mutations, and small indels, are associated with DD and novel diverse pathogenic variants are constantly reported, DD genetic testing should include comprehensive sequencing to capture a broad spectrum of variants [11, 42, 80–86]. Sanger sequencing or next-generation sequencing of ATP2A2 are commonly used methods [87–90]. For patients without ATP2A2 pathogenic variants, deep intronic variants or variants affecting transcription or splicing and epigenetic alterations may remain undetected. The incidence of sporadic cases is reported as approximately 40–50%, with a high penetrance of greater than 95% [11].

Mosaic DD can be confirmed by Sanger or NGS sequencing of ATP2A2 or WES from lesional skin and blood DNA, especially when blood DNA shows no ATP2A2 variant [26]. Type 1 mosaic DD is caused by a post‐zygotic mutation that manifests as affected dermatomes along Blaschko’s lines surrounded by normal skin [22, 31, 34, 91, 92]. Type 2 mosaic results when an individual with a germline mutation develops a second-hit somatic mutation, resulting in more severe segmental lesions superimposed on a milder generalized DD [26].

Comprehensive sequencing poses value for diagnostic confirmation, genetic counseling, and for expanding understanding of genetic diversity and its impact on clinical phenotype. Moreover, it enhances the accuracy of diagnosis and enables personalized management of the disease (Table 3).

Treatment of Cutaneous Manifestations of DD

The large number of therapeutic approaches reported in the literature reflects the complexity and challenges of managing the cutaneous manifestations of DD (Fig. 3b). First-line management focuses on preventive and symptomatic measures, including the avoidance of triggering factors, and the regular use of topical antiseptics, emollients, and keratolytic and anti-inflammatory agents (Fig. 4). These measures aim to reduce secondary infection, reduce keratosis, control inflammation, and restore skin barrier function, thereby minimizing disease exacerbations. Systemic therapies are recommended for disseminated lesions that are not responsive to topical therapies. Physical and procedural treatments are reserved for localized, treatment-refractory lesions or as adjuncts to medical therapy. The proposed approach is not intended as a step therapy model, but rather as an individualized therapeutic strategy tailored to the predominant clinical manifestations and extent of lesions, consistent with the management principles applied across dermatologic diseases.

Antiseptic Therapy

Regular use of antiseptics containing octenidine or chlorhexidine can reduce microbial colonization on compromised skin and thereby lower the risk of secondary infections [10, 110].

Topical Antibiotic Treatment

Short-course treatment with topical fucidic acid 2% cream is effective for localized secondary bacterial infections, particularly those caused by Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus species [35, 110]. A case report described a 49-year-old woman in whom daily application of topical metronidazole 1% gel to the affected areas resulted in complete resolution of malodor despite persistence of the underlying skin lesions [111]. Prolonged or repeated use of topical antibiotic therapy should be avoided to reduce the risk of antimicrobial resistance [112].

Topical retinoids demonstrate therapeutic benefits (Supplementary Table 1). Adapalene 0.1%, applied once daily for 2 months, leads to a significant reduction in lesions with good tolerability and no notable side effects [103, 113–117]. Tretinoin cream (0.01%) and tazarotene (0.01–0.1%) gel have shown similar efficacy, particularly in short-contact applications, and showed no side effects [107, 116]. Early local adverse effects may be reduced through photoprotection, avoidance of extreme temperatures, and regular use of emollients [118].

Topical Corticosteroids

During acute inflammatory flares, moderately to highly potent topical corticosteroids are often required. Class II–III agents can be used temporarily, while class IV steroids are reserved for short-term use in acute exacerbations [10, 104, 119].

Calcineurin inhibitors have also proven effective (Supplementary Table 2). Tacrolimus 0.1% ointment, especially beneficial in patients with coexisting atopic dermatitis, is typically used twice daily initially, followed by maintenance therapy with tacrolimus 0.03% [105]. Pimecrolimus 1% cream offers comparable results and safety, with no significant adverse effects reported. Both agents are preferred for use in intertriginous areas owing to their favorable safety profile.

Topical Vitamin D Analogs

Vitamin D analogs are well recognized for three main actions: keratinocyte hyperproliferation inhibition, epidermal differentiation regulation, and cutaneous inflammation inhibition [25]. Few case reports/series report either substantial or variable improvement of DD lesions [25, 94, 99] (Supplementary Table 3).

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU)

Special considerations should be taken into account when using 5-FU as topical treatment in patients with DD (Supplementary Table 4) [120–123]. In patients who require large body surface areas treatment, as well as patients diagnosed with cardiac, digestive, and hematological diseases, 5-FU treatment should be used with caution, and moreover, screening for dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase deficit is advised [124]. When considering the effects of topical 5-FU in pregnancy, the experience of using it in pregnant patients with human papillomavirus (HPV) infections show no complications related to pregnancy, delivery, or the health of newborns/infants [125]. However, this drug represents a potentially teratogenic drug (former Food and Drug Administration (FDA) pregnancy category X drug), with topical usage being linked to ventricular septal defects and increased risks of miscarriage [126]. Regarding the usage of topical 5-FU during breastfeeding, if applied accurately, avoiding direct contact with the breast or nipple, it poses minimal risks; however, the risk of drug excretion in breastmilk is considerable and the manufacturer guidelines advise on discontinuing either breastfeeding or the treatment after careful risk–benefit ratio assessment [126].

Topical Diclofenac Sodium

The mechanism of action in DD involves specifically cyclooxygenase (COX)-2 inhibition, leading to reduced activity of prostaglandin E2, which subsequently results in the downregulation of the ATP2A2 gene, leading to reestablishment of normal SERCA2 levels in keratinocytes [100, 108] (Supplementary Table 5 [127]).

In pregnancy, there is no data regarding the safety of topical diclofenac sodium usage, as studies are lacking; however, reproductive studies in animals have not shown any fetal harm or risk for this topical treatment [109]. On the other hand, the use of topical diclofenac gel in breastfeeding mothers is not anticipated to determine side effects in infants [109].

Treatment of Mucosal Lesions

In the oral cavity, the use of nonalcoholic mouthrinses is beneficial, as the coalescence of papules on the mucosa leads to the accumulation of organic material, which in turn causes unpleasant odor and promotes microbial growth [128]. Patients with the severe and generalized form of the disease are usually treated with systemic and topical retinoids; however, oral lesions often persist despite such therapy [129].

Systemic Therapies

Oral retinoids are well-established treatments that have revolutionized the management of keratinization disorders. They should be considered as first-line options for the treatment of DD, but are highly teratogenic, causing severe birth defects, and require strict pregnancy prevention programmes for post-menarchal adolescent females.

Acitretin has shown efficacy in DD, with case series [130] and comparative studies with etretinate [131–133] reporting significant but suspensive improvement. Although safety data specific to DD are limited, findings from studies in congenital ichthyosis—which share overlapping treatment considerations—are largely reassuring. One adverse effect more specific to DD is the occurrence of hemorrhagic blistering lesions on acral sites, typically secondary to mechanical trauma during acitretin treatment [134, 135].

Isotretinoin has been evaluated in DD primarily through a retrospective series of 7 patients [130], a few clinical case reports [136, 137], and most notably, an open-label study involving 104 patients conducted by Dicken et al. [138]. In this study, isotretinoin demonstrated substantial efficacy, with approximately 70% of patients showing clinical improvement within 1–4 weeks of treatment. However, 11 patients discontinued therapy owing to adverse effects. Long-term follow-up data for isotretinoin in DD remain limited. Nonetheless, insights can be drawn from its use in congenital ichthyosis, where long-term administration has been associated with bone toxicity [139].

Alitretinoin use in DD has been reported in several case studies, primarily involving young women in whom acitretin was ineffective or contraindicated owing to childbearing age. Most of these reports describe positive outcomes with alitretinoin, either alone or in combination with topical 5-fluorouracil [140]. A dosage of 30 mg/day was typically used, with clinical improvement seen within weeks. Relapse was common upon dose reduction or discontinuation, although one patient-maintained disease control with alternate-day dosing. Follow-up durations varied, with some extending beyond 1 year. Adverse effects were generally mild and included hypercholesterolemia, dry eyes, hair thinning, and transient headaches. One case, however, reported treatment failure and notable adverse events, including aseptic cellulitis of the breast and enlargement of pyogenic granulomas [141, 142].

Bexarotene is a synthetic retinoid analog approved for the treatment of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma. A clinical case reported a significant improvement in DD [143]. However, typical side effects such as hypothyroidism and hyperlipidemia were observed.

Naltrexone

Several studies have reported clinical success of low-dose naltrexone, often combined with magnesium supplementation (given the magnesium dependency of the SERCA2 pump) in the treatment of patients with HHD [144, 145]. In DD, one clinical case reported a global improvement of the condition with low-dosage (1.5–12.5 mg/day) naltrexone treatment [146], whereas a case series of six patients found no significant effect [147]. Another clinical case described the efficacy of naltrexone in combination with oral isotretinoin [148].

Oral Magnesium

On the basis of reports of treating HHD with magnesium chloride [149], this therapy was administered to an 11-year-old boy with DD [150]. The author noted significant improvement after 1 month; however, the patient discontinued treatment after 4 months owing to its unpleasant taste.

Intravenous Immunoglobulin

Intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIg) are considered for treatment owing to skin barrier abnormalities that predispose patients to bacterial infections. One case report [151] described significant clinical improvement after four infusions of IVIg administered at a substitutive dosage of 0.4 g/kg every 3 weeks. The patient’s positive response has been sustained for over 2 years.

Doxycycline

Tetracyclines have the ability to chelate calcium and facilitate its transport across cellular membranes. This mechanism may help correct the cellular calcium imbalance observed in DD. Two clinical case reports describe the use of doxycycline 100 mg/day in adult DD patients, showing improvement after a few weeks of treatment [152, 153]. However, no data on long-term follow-up are available.

Other Therapies

Anecdotal clinical cases reported reduction of pruritus in vesiculobullous DD with the use of cetirizine 10 mg daily [154], improvement in a woman with perimenstrual exacerbations following treatment with oral contraceptives [155], or improvement of extensive eczematized DD under cyclosporine [156, 157].

Targeted Anti-inflammatory Therapies

Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) and biologics target proinflammatory cytokine signaling pathways that may contribute to the downregulation of ATP2A2 expression in DD [158]. In addition to their anti-inflammatory effects, JAK inhibitors may also alleviate pruritus by modulating sensory nerve activity. The efficacy of baricitinib (4 mg daily) was reported in an adult with DD, with noticeable improvement observed after 1 month of treatment. A relapse occurred upon discontinuation, but symptoms improved again when the treatment was restarted. Clinical efficacy was sustained over a 9-month follow-up, with good tolerability and no reported adverse effects [158]. In a single case report, a combination of abrocitinib and acitretin resulted in rapid symptom resolution without recurrence in an 11-week follow-up [159]. Another case report showed a beneficial effect of the monoclonal antibody against human interleukin (IL)-6R in the treatment of summer exacerbations of DD [160]. Biologics against type 2 inflammation, IL-4/IL-13 antibodies were reported to improve symptoms in DD [161, 162].

In a small case series, three patients with DD were treated with biologic therapies against type 3 inflammation: one received an IL-23A inhibitor, and two received an IL-17A inhibitor [163]. All three patients experienced a rapid and sustained improvement in skin inflammation and lesion severity, with clinical benefits maintained for at least 1 year. The treatments were well tolerated, and the clinical improvement was accompanied by a normalization of the inflammatory gene expression profile, suggesting a targeted anti-inflammatory effect. A report on a series of five cases underscores the challenges of targeted systemic anti-inflammatory therapies in DD: despite Th17 inflammatory profiles in lesional skin, the therapeutic responses to anti-IL-17/IL-23 antibodies were heterogeneous and transitory [10].

These data suggest the involvement of complex and compensatory inflammatory pathways in the pathogenesis of DD. Although current clinical evidence for anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory treatments remains limited, the reported responses indicate therapeutic potential that warrants further systematic investigation to establish formal evidence and guide evidence-based recommendations.

Emerging Targeted Therapies

Recent research provides mechanistic support for MEK inhibition as a potential therapeutic strategy in DD [164, 165]. In particular, a case report described a patient with severe, therapy-refractory DD who experienced marked clinical improvement under treatment with trametinib (2 mg daily), a selective MEK inhibitor approved for advanced BRAF V600-mutant melanoma in adults [165].

In addition, dantrolene sodium, a ryanodine receptor antagonist that inhibits calcium release from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), has shown promising preclinical effects. In experimental models, dantrolene enhanced ER calcium retention, improved cell–cell adhesion, reduced ER stress, and suppressed keratinocyte apoptosis [6]. By specifically targeting the calcium homeostasis defect underlying DD, dantrolene represents a mechanism-based therapeutic candidate warranting further clinical evaluation [6]. Expansion of the HSV-1-based gene therapy platform to treat DD is under development [166, 167].

Physical Therapies for DD

Physical therapies for DD represent an alternative to more established topical and systemic treatments. Surgical approaches, whether partial (such as dermabrasion, partial removal of the epidermis and papillary dermis, or electrosurgery) or involving wide excisions, can be curative and are associated with a low recurrence rate according to the available literature [168] (Supplementary Table 6 [169–172]). However, it is important to note that these interventions, in addition to being linked to hypo- or hyperpigmented sequelae, should be proposed only for localized forms that are resistant to standard treatments or as an adjunct to them [168, 173, 174]. An additional physical modality is cryotherapy, which has demonstrated a favorable clinical response in cases reported in the literature [170, 175].

The main alternative to surgical treatment is the use of ablative laser therapy (Supplementary Table 7), particularly fractional and nonfractional CO2 laser [168, 173, 174]. Studies report a clear clinical benefit in 83% of treated patients, even in extensive disease, with significant symptomatic improvement and long remission periods of up to 2 years [141, 171, 176–182]. Similar results have been documented with other ablative lasers, such as the erbium:yttrium aluminum garnet (YAG) laser [173, 176]. The extent of the treated areas, however, can influence the occurrence of more significant side effects, including erythema and edema.

Nonablative alternatives, such as the pulsed dye laser, have also been reported in the literature. In this context, the underlying inflammation is modulated through direct action on blood vessels, leading to good clinical responses in the majority of patients. Nonetheless, typical adverse events of this therapy, such as purpura and occasional infectious reactivations, have been observed [168, 172, 173, 176, 183, 184].

Several reports have described therapeutic responses in patients with DD undergoing radiotherapy for oncological indications [173] (Supplementary Table 8). The techniques employed vary depending on the extent of the disease and the required depth of penetration [184–189]. Clinical responses appear to vary according to disease severity, with some patients showing early relapse or consequent complete remission. Nevertheless, significant side effects, including sclerosis and ulceration have been documented. Moreover, the potential for malignant transformation with repeated exposure is not negligible. As a result, radiotherapy is generally limited to small, localized areas.

Photodynamic therapy also represents a therapeutic option (Supplementary Table 9). Although data are limited, more than 50% of patients appear to achieve clinical remission or marked improvement, with only the known mild adverse events of this treatment reported [173, 178, 190–193].

Lastly, considering the role of sweating in disease exacerbation, several authors have explored the use of botulinum toxin (Supplementary Table 10) [194]. Approximately 71.4% of patients report complete or satisfactory responses after injection. However, transient pain at the injection site, bleeding, and mild tingling sensations have been noted [53, 173, 178, 194]. Given the approximately 6-month duration of action of botulinum toxin, its long-term use remains limited, particularly in more extensive disease (Table 4).

The data presented in this manuscript may not fully reflect current clinical practice in all countries or centers; however, they are based on the available literature and the consensus of authors from 13 different countries. ERN-Skin has made substantial efforts to collect and critically evaluate the available worldwide evidence and to integrate the experience of predominantly European expert centers caring for large numbers of patients. These recommendations are therefore based on both published evidence and expert consensus and likely represent the best guidance currently available.

Conclusions

DD is a chronic, genetically determined disorder that profoundly impacts patients’ quality of life and currently lacks a curative therapy. DD carries a huge stigmatizing effect along with a high infection risk. Our practical recommendations aim to guide diagnosis, symptomatic management, and ongoing follow-up of affected individuals. We acknowledge the limitations and have striven to provide the most accurate statements possible. Many of the therapies discussed remain unapproved in numerous countries, reflecting a common challenge in rare disorder management. The ongoing risk of therapy denial by payers owing to insufficient evidence persists. Continued collection of real-world data, as well as publication of case reports and case series, will be essential to further refine and strengthen future recommendations. We emphasize the urgent need for robust clinical trials, disease registries, and biomarker-driven research to advance therapeutic strategies and foster the discovery of novel, mechanism-based treatments. These recommendations should be regularly updated as new research emerges.
