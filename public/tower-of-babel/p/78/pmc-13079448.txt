Highlight: A gift from a great-grand-fly—transgenerational epigenetic inheritance of heat shock response
Authors: Shady A Kuster
Published: 2026

As global temperatures rise, understanding how organisms mitigate environmental heat stress becomes crucial. Long-term evolutionary responses are traditionally considered to be associated with heritable changes in DNA sequence, while non-genetic mechanisms, such as gene expression plasticity or epigenetic modifications, help individuals cope with environmental fluctuations by facilitating temporary adjustments in physiology. Whether these epigenetic modifications are inherited, environmentally induced, and subject to evolutionary selection is currently contentious.

Recent evidence documenting heritable, environmentally induced epigenetic changes has renewed attention to the historically popular idea that a parent's exposure to a given environment benefits future generations subjected to the same environment. Biologists have frequently described epigenetic modifications that last for only one or 2 generations after environmental exposure. Effects that persist to the third generation or further, referred to as transgenerational epigenetic inheritance, have less support, particularly in animals (Bošković and Rando 2018; Fitz-James and Cavalli 2022). One of the few well-documented examples comes from laboratory strains of Drosophila, where environmental perturbations resulted in heritable chromatin interactions (Ciabrelli et al. 2017). Caenorhabditis elegans has also shown consistent histone modifications across generations following heat shock (Wan et al. 2021).

Can heritable effects of environmental exposure occur in natural populations that are exposed to heterogeneous climate-related selection pressures? “The epigenome clearly evolves, as selection favors patterns of gene regulation with adaptive advantages,” Dr. Ewan Harney, a molecular ecologist currently at the University of Liverpool, shared. “However, whether evolution is influenced by environmental sensitivity in the epigenome remains a contentious issue.” Harney addressed this controversial topic while working at the Institute of Evolutionary Biology in Barcelona, Spain, where he collaborated with Dr. Josefa González, an evolutionary biologist currently at the Botanical Institute of Barcelona. In their research, now published in Molecular Biology and Evolution (Harney and González 2026), the 2 researchers asked whether environmental acclimation is associated with evolutionary change in natural populations.

Under high temperatures, epigenetic silencing mechanisms can be weakened, increasing chromatin accessibility; one possible consequence is the increased replication of transposable elements (TEs) (Rey et al. 2016). Thus, Harney and González hypothesized that the interaction between TEs and the epigenome's response to heat shock may have diverged in populations with frequent heat exposure. “Genes that are proximate to novel insertions may be caught up in the remodeled chromatin landscape, allowing TE sequences to influence broader regulation patterns. TEs could also be co-opted by the host to play a more active role in gene regulation,” González described.

For their experiment, Harney and González analyzed 2 fruit fly populations: one from the warm, arid environment of Manzanares in Spain, and the other from the cold Akaa region of Finland. The flies were brought to the lab to produce fly lines derived from either the warm or cold populations without the confounding effects of parental environmental exposure (Fig. 1). The third generation of each line was then heat-shocked, and their patterns of gene expression and chromatin accessibility were profiled and compared to a non-heat-shocked control group. The authors then bred the heat-shocked and control flies for 3 more generations and repeated gene expression and phenotypic measurements. In addition, control flies were used to assess TE variability between the two populations.

Flies from the Manzanares D. melanogaster population were collected and classified by the teachers and students of the IES Azuer as part of the citizen science project “Melanogaster: Catch the Fly.” Photos by IES Azuer students, image composition and permission to use obtained by Roberto Torres.

Although the cold-sourced flies mounted a larger transcriptional response to heat shock, a greater number of upregulated genes in warm-sourced flies were found in more accessible chromatin regions. Moreover, most of the differentially expressed genes in the warm-sourced group were shared with their cold-sourced counterparts, indicating that although the core physiological response to heat shock was the same, the warm population may have tighter upregulation of the heat stress response. When researchers repeated the gene expression measurements in the sixth generation—3 generations following the heat shock—they found that offspring of the warm-sourced group shared a large proportion of differentially expressed genes with their heat-shocked great-grandparents: 132 out of 292 differentially expressed genes (relative to the control group) were shared between them. As with their grandparents, the expression of these genes were more often concordant with chromatin accessibility. In contrast, sixth-generation cold-sourced flies had 20 differentially expressed genes, and only 5 of these were shared with their heat-shocked ancestors. These findings could indicate an evolved transgenerational effect of heat shock responses. “To see such a clear correlation in transcription between the third and sixth generation flies in critical developmental genes was really exciting, as these genes could provide a putative mechanism for the phenotypic results,” Harney shared.

Do these transgenerational changes in gene regulation translate to adaptive phenotypes? The answer seems to be yes. For up to 4 generations following heat shock, the offspring from heat-shocked warm-sourced females showed shorter developmental times compared to offspring of the warm-sourced control group—an effect that was not observed in the cold-sourced groups. Reduced development times are expected to improve fitness, as the heat-exposed, rotting fruit where larvae grow in natural conditions can become too hot for viability. “Our results suggest that the Manzanares population has evolved a distinct transgenerational response to having frequently experienced heat stress,” González summarized.

Harney and González initially hypothesized that TEs could be linked to the evolution of transgenerational effects. Indeed, TE insertions specific to the warm-sourced flies were associated with higher chromatin accessibility. In contrast, the cold-sourced population showed TE presence to be associated with decreased gene expression.

These findings could reflect how frequent exposure to hot environments affects the selective consequences of TE activity. Increased chromatin accessibility associated with frequent heat shocks might facilitate the insertion of TEs, increasing the chance that some TEs will land near genes relevant for the stress response. TEs can drive expression in nearby genes, so these insertions could, for example, enhance the response to heat shock. Further validation of specific candidate genes and TEs identified in this study will be necessary to clarify their roles in the transgenerational inheritance of gene regulation.

Although the role of the epigenome in evolutionary responses to environmental change is becoming recognized, this relationship is still viewed with understandable skepticism. The authors themselves mention that “identifying the epigenetic mechanisms that are labile enough to respond to environmental change but stable enough to be transmitted over multiple generations remains a critical gap in our understanding of how transgenerational inheritance might affect evolutionary change.”
