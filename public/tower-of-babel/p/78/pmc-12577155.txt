Incidence and risk factors for recurrent venous thromboembolism, recurrent stroke and bleeding in Chinese patients with acute ischemic stroke and cancer : a real-world study
Authors: Yuan Zhang, Huan-yu Wang, Hai-xia Ren, Yi Lu, Xiao-chen Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and cancer are at exceptionally high risk for recurrent thromboembolism (RTE), which includes venous thromboembolism (VTE) and arterial thromboembolism (ATE). Yet contemporary data from Chinese patients remain scarce.MethodsIn this retrospective, observational study conducted at Tianjin First Central Hospital (June 2023-December 2024), consecutive adults with AIS and cancer were enrolled. The primary outcomes during 6 months follow-up were recurrent VTE།composite VTE (symptomatic and incidental VTE requiring treatment), recurrent stroke [cerebral infarction, transient ischemic attack (TIA), and systemic embolism (SE)], and bleeding (major bleeding and clinically significant non-major bleeding). Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of each endpoint.ResultsAmong 218 eligible patients, 49 (22.5%) had baseline VTE, of whom 45 (91.8%) were asymptomatic. Baseline VTE was independently predicted by cryptogenic stroke subtype (OR 2.70; 95% CI 1.101–6.621; P = 0.030) and D-dimer > 1.2 µg/mL (OR 3.619; 95% CI 1.505–8.703; P = 0.004). During follow-up, 25 patients (11.5%) experienced composite VTE, 71 patients (32.6%) experienced recurrent stroke, and 21 patients (9.6%) experienced bleeding. Multivariable analysis revealed that D-dimer > 1.2 µg/mL independently predicted recurrent VTE (OR 3.501; 95% CI 1.012–12.112; P = 0.048), whereas cancer metastasis portended recurrent stroke (OR 3.155; 95% CI 1.423–6.995; P = 0.005), and anticoagulant therapy was associated with an increased bleeding risk (OR 9.458; 95% CI 1.158–77.267; P = 0.036).ConclusionsIn this Chinese cohort of patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and cancer, routine screening revealed venous thromboembolism (VTE) in roughly one-fifth of participants at baseline, most of whom were asymptomatic. Exploratory multivariable analyses indicated that cryptogenic stroke subtype and D-dimer > 1.2 µg/mL were associated with prevalent VTE, while D-dimer > 1.2 µg/mL also predicted recurrent VTE. These findings suggested that D-dimer guided VTE screening might be considered at the time of stroke presentation, even in the absence of symptoms. Cancer metastasis showed an association with recurrent stroke, whereas anticoagulant therapy was linked to a higher bleeding risk.Keywords: Acute ischemic stroke, Cancer, Venous thromboembolism, Recurrent stroke, Bleeding, Risk factors

Introduction

Cancer-related hypercoagulability arises from a triad of clotting cascade, platelet function, and endothelial integrity, yielding a ≥ 4-fold increase in venous thromboembolism (VTE) compared with the non-cancer population [1, 2]. Symptomatic ischemic stroke complicates the clinical course of roughly 7% of all patients with cancer [3], and conversely, 4–20% of individuals presenting with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) harbor an occult or known cancer [4]. Within the first 6 months after a cancer diagnosis, 4.7% develop an arterial thromboembolic (ATE) event, conferring a 2-fold excess risk relative to matched controls [5]. Indeed, thrombosis has become the second leading cause of death among oncology patients [6]. Concomitant cancer in AIS patients amplifies early neurological deterioration, short-term mortality, and stroke recurrence [7]. In a single-centre retrospective cohort study, 31% of patients with active cancer experienced a thromboembolic event within 3 months; recurrent ischemic stroke accounted for 13% of these episodes, which is nearly triple the rate observed in non-cancer populations [8, 9]. Incident cancer likewise doubles the risk of recurrent thromboembolism (RTE) in the 6 months following an index AIS [8]. As the global population of cancer survivors expands and survival lengthens, the burden of cancer-associated stroke is poised to escalate, underscoring the urgent need for evidence-based prevention strategies.

Nevertheless, prior investigations have largely excluded patients in whom cancer is newly diagnosed at, or immediately after, the index stroke-a group that constitutes 28–42% of AIS cases with active cancer [10]. Consequently, reliable predictors of long-term outcomes in this time-sensitive setting remain undefined, and clinically relevant endpoints such as bleeding have seldom been captured. Rigorous data on the incidences and determinants of recurrent VTE, recurrent stroke, and bleeding among Chinese patients with AIS and cancer are particularly scarce. To address these gaps, we undertook a comprehensive retrospective analysis of cancer patients presenting with AIS to delineate their clinical phenotypes and to identify independent risk factors for recurrent VTE, recurrent stroke, and bleeding.

Methods

This retrospective observational study conducted at the Department of Neurology of Tianjin First Central Hospital in China, between June 2023 and December 2024. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tianjin First Central Hospital according to the Declaration of Helsinki on reviewing and publishing information from patient’s records. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for the use of the medical records for research purposes.

Patients

Subjects were eligible for this study if they were at least 20 years of age, diagnosed with AIS within 14 days following symptom onset, had previously diagnosed cancer within 5 years prior to AIS onset or newly diagnosed cancer at the time of AIS onset, and underwent lower extremity venous ultrasonography after admission and at 6 months following AIS onset for VTE screening. Active cancer was defined as proven cancer that was either treated within the past 6 months before admission, untreated, or metastatic [11]. All included patients displayed various signs and symptoms of neurological deficits, and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) confirmed the presence of AIS [11]. Patients were excluded if they were did not have AIS or cancer, had hemorrhagic stroke, hematological cancer, or double cancer, were deemed inappropriate for inclusion or difficult to follow up, or lacked complete clinical and radiological data [12]. AIS was diagnosed based on the American Heart Association criteria [13].

Data collection

We systematically reviewed all relevant clinical and laboratory records. Clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking [14], drinking [15], traditional stroke risk factors, antithrombotic regimens initiated after stroke onset, stroke subtype, infarct location on neuroimaging, deep venous thrombosis or pulmonary embolism (DVT/PE) documented at the time of AIS, and use of recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA). Cancer-related parameters included cancer type, cancer metastasis, and cancer treatment status. Neurological severity was quantified using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), and functional outcome was assessed using the modified Rankin scale (mRS) [12].

Blood samples were collected immediately after admission, before any treatment was initiated. All patients underwent standard laboratory testing, which included a complete blood count, prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), international normalized ratio (INR), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), albumin (ALB), alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), serum creatinine (Scr), calculated creatinine clearance (CLcr), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). LDL-C classification was based on guideline-defined risk-stratified targets [16]. D-dimer levels was measured and categorized as > 1.2 µg/mL or ≤ 1.2 µg/mL, according to previously reported cut-offs predictive of non-VTE status [17]. The institutional reference range is 0 to 0.5 µg/mL. Carotid ultrasonography, transcranial Doppler (TCD), brain imaging (computed tomography [CT] or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]), 24-hour electrocardiographic monitoring, transthoracic echocardiography, and computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) were performed as clinically indicated.

The stroke subtype was classified according to the Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) criteria: (1) large artery atherosclerosis (LAA), (2) small-vessel occlusion (SVO), (3) cardioembolism, (4) other rare etiologies, and (5) cryptogenic stroke [18]. Patients with cryptogenic stroke were categorized as having no apparent cause of ischemic stroke other than malignancy [18]. All strokes and stroke etiologies in this study were diagnosed in consultation with a neurologist.

Follow-up duration and outcomes

Outcomes were evaluated at baseline and throughout follow-up. Baseline outcomes included the prevalence of VTE and its associated risk factors. Follow-up outcomes included the incidence of recurrent VTE, recurrent stroke, and bleeding events, along with the identification of risk factors for each of these complications.

Recurrent VTE (Composite VTE) comprised symptomatic or incidental (requiring treatment) events. Recurrent stroke was defined as the occurrence of cerebral infarction (confirmed by imaging), transient ischemic attack (TIA, defined as a neurological deficit lasting < 24 h), or systemic embolism (SE, characterized by abrupt vascular insufficiency associated with clinical or radiological evidence of arterial occlusion in the absence of other likely mechanisms such as atherosclerosis) [19]. Bleeding events were categorized into major and clinically relevant non-major bleeding. Major bleeding was defined as clinically overt bleeding associated with a fatal outcome, involvement of a critical anatomic site (intracranial, spinal, ocular, pericardial, articular, retroperitoneal, or intramuscular with compartment syndrome), a decrease in hemoglobin concentration of > 20 g/L, or transfusion of > 2 units of whole blood or packed red blood cells. Clinically relevant non-major bleeding was defined as overt bleeding that did not meet the criteria for major bleeding but required medical intervention, resulted in unscheduled contact (visit or telephone) with a physician, led to temporary interruption of study drug (i.e., delayed dosing), caused pain, or impaired daily activities [11, 19]. To diagnose intracranial hemorrhage, clinicians should assess for hallmark manifestations: headache, nausea, vomiting, altered consciousness of varying degrees, and progression of motor deficits [20]. When these signs are present, prompt confirmation by head CT or MRI is mandatory.

Patients were followed for 6 months from the date of AIS diagnosis through telephone interviews, outpatient visits, and systematic review of electronic medical records. During this period, we documented the occurrence of recurrent VTE, recurrent stroke, and bleeding events.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. All data are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables, or as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. Inter-group differences were assessed for significance using independent-sample t-tests or the Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables, or using the chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Uni- and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify independent predictors of recurrent VTE, recurrent stroke, and bleeding in Chinese patients with AIS and cancer. The estimated odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for each parameter were calculated. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 on the univariate analysis, as well as a priori variables that were clinically relevant, were entered into the forward stepwise multivariate logistic regression model. All tests were two-sided, and p-value < 0.05 were considered significant. No formal calculation of sample size was performed due of the characteristics of AIS and cancer.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 264 patients diagnosed with AIS and cancer were admitted to the Department of Neurology, Tianjin First Central Hospital from June 2023 to December 2024. Of these, 218 individuals were retained for final analysis, including 49 (22.48%) patients with VTE at baseline and 169 (77.5%) patients without VTE at baseline (Fig. 1). Baseline VTE was documented in 49 patients (22.48%), most of whom had asymptomatic DVT (Table 1). Of these 218 patients, 115 (52.75%) had active cancer. The most common primary cancer sites were respiratory tract (n = 63, 28.9%), urogenital system (n = 60, 27.5%), and gastrointestinal tract (n = 55, 25.2%). Baseline characteristics according to VTE status are summarised in Table 2. The study cohort had a median age of 72.1 years (IQR 65–78.25) and included 136 men (62.4%). Compared with patients without VTE at baseline, those with VTE at baseline were more likely to have a BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 (P = 0.032), cancer metastasis (P = 0.029), cryptogenic stroke subtype (P < 0.001) and D-dimer > 1.2 µg/mL (P < 0.001). They also exhibited higher NIHSS and mRS scores, elevated white blood cell and neutrophil counts, prolonged PT, INR, and hs-CRP levels, whereas hemoglobin, red blood cell count, and ALB were lower (all P < 0.05). Oral anticoagulant use was more prevalent in patients with VTE at baseline. No significant between-group differences were observed for sex, age ≥ 65 years, smoking, drinking, AST, ALT, CLcr, platelet or lymphocyte count, APTT, LDL-C, cancer type, infarct location, cancer treatment within the preceding 6 months (diagnosis, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy), recent surgery within 2 months, or traditional stroke risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, prior stroke, prior cerebral hemorrhage, intracranial vascular stenosis) (Table 2). Multivariable analysis identified cryptogenic stroke (OR 2.70; 95% CI 1.101–6.621; P = 0.030) and D-dimer > 1.2 µg/mL (OR 3.103; 95% CI 1.301–7.405; P = 0.011) as the only independent predictors of baseline VTE (Table 3).

Study outcomes

Incidence of events during follow-up

During the 6-month follow-up period, 25 patients (11.5%) developed recurrent VTE (composite VTE), comprising 16 patients (7.4%) with de-novo VTE and 9 patients (4.1%) with extension or progression of pre-existing VTE. Symptomatic VTE occurred in 4 patients (1.83%), while incidental VTE requiring treatment was detected in 21 patients (9.6%). Recurrent stroke was documented in 71 patients (32.6%), including 70 cases of cerebral infarction and 1 case of TIA. Clinically relevant non-major bleeding was observed in 21 patients (9.6%), comprising intracranial hemorrhage (n = 3), gastrointestinal bleeding (n = 9), genitourinary bleeding (n = 4), hemoptysis (n = 3), conjunctival hemorrhage (n = 1), and epistaxis (n = 1). According to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), all reported bleeding events were Grade ≤ 2. The incidence of these outcomes was stratified according to post-stroke antithrombotic strategy: no therapy, antiplatelet therapy, or anticoagulant therapy. The corresponding event rates are detailed in Table 4. Antithrombotic strategy was significantly associated with the risk of recurrent stroke. Patients who received no therapy had a higher rate of recurrent stroke than those treated with antiplatelet therapy (50% vs. 25.6%; F = 8.565, P = 0.003). No statistically significant difference in recurrent stroke was observed between no therapy and anticoagulant therapy (50% vs. 34.9%; F = 2.601, P = 0.107) or between antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy (25.6% vs. 34.9%; F = 1.717, P = 0.190). In contrast, bleeding events were driven primarily by anticoagulant therapy. The incidence of clinically relevant non-major bleeding was comparable between patients who received no therapy and those on antiplatelet therapy (2.6% vs. 6%; F = 0.658, P = 0.417). However, bleeding events were significantly more frequent in patients receiving anticoagulant therapy than in those receiving no therapy (20% vs. 2.6%; F = 6.434, P = 0.011) or antiplatelet therapy (20% vs. 6%; F = 8.901, P = 0.003). Importantly, the incidence of recurrent VTE did not differ among the three antithrombotic strategies (F = 0.129, P = 0.937) (Table 4).

Predictors of outcomes during 6-month follow-up

Multivariable logistic regression (Table 5) showed that D-dimer > 1.2 µg/mL was independently associated with recurrent VTE (OR 3.501; 95% CI 1.012–12.112; P = 0.048). Cardioembolism stroke subtype, cancer metastasis, elevated AST, and prolonged APTT demonstrated ORs of > 1, but none reached statistical significance.

Discussion

The bidirectional interplay between cancer and thrombosis is well established: cancer induces a systemic hypercoagulable state, and thromboembolism, comprising both VTE and ATE, has emerged as the second leading cause of mortality in patients with cancer, surpassed only by the cancer itself. Beyond direct lethality, incident thromboembolism frequently necessitates the interruption or delay of essential oncologic therapies, thereby adversely affecting oncologic prognosis [6]. Against this background, the concomitant rise in the prevalence of cancer and AIS has rendered their intersection an increasingly critical area of clinical and public health concern. To date, however, robust, prospectively collected data characterizing the incidence and determinants of recurrent VTE, recurrent stroke, and bleeding among Chinese patients with AIS and cancer remain scarce. The present study was therefore designed to bridge this evidence gap by providing a comprehensive, real-world evaluation of antithrombotic outcomes in this high-risk population.

Lower extremity venous ultrasonography identified VTE at baseline in 49 of 218 participants (22.5%). The majority were clinically silent (asymptomatic 20.64%; symptomatic 1.83%). Gastrointestinal (36.7%), respiratory (26.5%), and urogenital (22.4%) primaries predominated. This observed prevalence exceeds that reported in single cancer cohorts (5.9% or 8.5%) [21, 22], but is consistent with a pooled estimate across all cancers (≈ 15%). The discrepancy reflects our inclusion of heterogeneous solid organ cancers, a higher proportion of active disease (52.8%), and over-representation of gastrointestinal and pulmonary primaries-both recognized to confer heightened thrombotic risk [23].

Multivariable analysis identified cryptogenic stroke subtype (OR 2.70; 95% CI 1.101–6.621; P = 0.030) and D-dimer >1.2 µg/mL (OR 3.103; 95% CI 1.301–7.405; P = 0.011) as independent predictors of baseline VTE. Elevated D-dimer levels were particularly pronounced among cryptogenic strokes, supporting occult cancer-related hypercoagulability as a unifying mechanism [24–26]. During follow-up, 11.47% of patients experienced recurrent or progressive VTE; only 1.8% were symptomatic. These rates fall within the range reported in contemporary cancer-associated thrombosis registries (1.1–12%) [27], with most events occurring within the first six months, underscoring the value of systematic screening at cancer diagnosis [27].

Recurrent stroke events occurred in 71 patients (32.59%), comprising 70 cerebral infarctions and 1 TIA. This incidence exceeds that observed in a recent tertiary-care cancer cohort (17.8%) [7], plausibly reflecting older age and a greater burden of traditional vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, prior stroke, intracranial vascular stenosis) in our population. Recurrent stroke is strongly associated with poor functional outcomes and increased mortality; consequently, effective secondary prevention is essential to minimize neurological disability in patients with AIS and cancer [28, 29].

Cancer metastasis was the sole independent determinant of recurrent stroke (OR 3.155; 95% CI 1.423–6.995; P = 0.005), reflecting its propensity to generate cardioembolism and large artery occlusion. Metastases are also a well-established predictor of mortality in patients with concomitant ischemic stroke and cancer. In a retrospective Chinese cohort, cancer metastasis was positively and independently associated with AIS, underscoring its pivotal role in cerebrovascular events among patients with cancer [30]. Moreover, cancer metastasis poses a significant risk of systemic embolization, rendering stroke a severe manifestation of the disease [31, 32].

Anticoagulant therapy in cancer carries a markedly higher bleeding risk than in those without cancer [33, 34]. In our cohort of 218 Chinese patients with AIS and cancer, 38 received no therapy, 117 received antiplatelet therapy, and 63 received anticoagulant therapy. Anticoagulation, although theoretically advocated for cancer-associated stroke, substantially increased the rate of clinically relevant non-major bleeding (20% vs. 2.6% in no therapy and 6% in antiplatelet therapy; P < 0.05) without reducing recurrent VTE (11.1% vs. 13.2% in no therapy and 11.1% in antiplatelet therapy; P >0.05) or recurrent stroke (34.9% vs. 50% in no therapy and 25.6% in antiplatelet therapy; P >0.05). By contrast, antiplatelet therapy conferred an absolute 24.4% reduction in recurrent stroke compared with no therapy (25.6% vs. 50%; F = 8.565, P = 0.003) without increasing bleeding risk (6% vs. 2.6%; F = 0.658, P = 0.417). These findings align with a prior retrospective study of 172 similarly affected patients that reported comparable thrombotic outcomes but higher bleeding rates with anticoagulation, mostly low-molecular-weight heparin [32]. Collectively, the evidence supports antiplatelet therapy as the most pragmatic first-line strategy for Chinese patients with AIS and cancer, providing the most favorable risk-benefit profile [32, 35]. Prospective trials evaluating cancer-directed antithrombotic regimens, such as tailored direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC), are urgently needed to reconcile thrombotic and hemorrhagic risk.

This study had several limitations. First, the follow-up period was limited to six months, precluding evaluation of longer-term thrombotic and bleeding outcomes. Second, the retrospective, single-centre design and the exclusive enrolment of Chinese patients restrict the external validity of our findings to other ethnicities, healthcare systems, or stroke populations across Asia. Third, the antithrombotic therapy regimen at the time of enrollment, including the decision to withhold treatment, was determined at the discretion of the attending physician rather than by protocol, introducing potential selection bias. The median age (70 years) was higher than in several prior cohorts; consequently, silent or paroxysmal atrial fibrillation may have emerged during follow-up and influenced treatment choices. Anticoagulants could have been prescribed for newly detected atrial fibrillation, incident asymptomatic VTE, or secondary stroke prevention, a therapy attenuating event rates. We stratified outcomes only according to anticoagulant use at baseline and did not capture dosage adjustments, interruptions, or drug switches during follow-up, preventing a time-varying exposure analysis. Fourth, ascertainment of recurrent stroke, recurrent VTE and bleeding relied on patient or proxy recall supplemented by electronic medical records. Asymptomatic events may therefore have been under-ascertained. Finally, although multivariable models were adjusted for key confounders, residual confounding, particularly from unmeasured cancer-related factors, cannot be excluded. Prospective, multicenter studies with longer follow-up, protocolized antithrombotic regimens, systematic event adjudication, and inclusion of diverse populations are warranted to confirm and extend these observations.

Conclusions

In this Chinese cohort of patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and cancer, routine screening revealed venous thromboembolism (VTE) in roughly one-fifth of participants at baseline, most of whom were asymptomatic. Exploratory multivariable analyses indicated that cryptogenic stroke subtype and D-dimer > 1.2 µg/mL were associated with prevalent VTE, while D-dimer > 1.2 µg/mL also predicted recurrent VTE. These findings suggested that D-dimer guided VTE screening might be considered at the time of stroke presentation, even in the absence of symptoms. Cancer metastasis showed an association with recurrent stroke, whereas anticoagulant therapy was linked to a higher bleeding risk.
