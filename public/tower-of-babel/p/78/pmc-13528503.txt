Chemoenzymatic Synthesis of Well‐Defined α(2,8)‐ and α(2,9)‐Linked Oligosialosides
Authors: Jelle A. Fok, Keshav Kishor, Mehman I. Bunyatov, Gerlof P. Bosman, Geert‐Jan Boons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA chemoenzymatic strategy is described as a method that can readily provide well‐defined α(2,8)‐ and α(2,9)‐linked oligosialic acids of different lengths. It employs the recombinant bacterial sialic acid polymerases, NmC‐PST and NmB‐PST, in combination with cytidine 5’‐monophosphate N‐acetylneuraminic acid (CMP‐Neu5Ac) derivatives that have a benzoyl ester at C‐9 or thioethyl ether at the C‐5 acetamide of Neu5Ac. It results in products that lack glycosyl accepting properties and hence prevent polymerization. A rationale for the blocking behavior of the C‐5 modification is provided by molecular dynamics simulations. The benzoyl ester can be removed by mild basic hydrolysis, and the thioethyl ether by reductive desulfurization to give natural Neu5Ac than can be extended in the next cycle of enzymatic sialylation and unmasking. The resulting oligosialosides are attractive materials for the development of bacterial vaccines and as probes to investigate the roles of oligosialosides in human physiology.

Introduction

Sialic acids are a family of nine‐carbon acidic monosaccharides with N‐acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac) being the most common one. Neu5Ac is a common terminal moiety of glycoproteins and glycolipids and plays a role in many biological and diseases processes [1, 2, 3]. Neu5Ac can also occur as α(2,8)‐ or α(2,9)‐linked oligomers or polymers (Figure 1a). For example, α(2,8)‐linked polymeric Neu5Ac (polysialic acid) is a posttranslational modification of several glycoproteins, including neuronal cell adhesion molecule (NCAM) [4], neuropilin‐2 (NRP2) [5], and E‐selectin ligand 1 (ESL‐1) [6, 7]. Polysialylation of these proteins is regulated in a developmental manner and is associated with disease [8]. During embryonic development, NCAM is modified by polysialic acid, which is critical for neuronal development by providing motility to neurons. Its expression diminishes rapidly, however, during the early postnatal phase [4, 9].

(c) Transfer of modified donors to an oligosialoside acceptor “stops” further elongation by a PST. Recent findings indicate that polysialic acid can function as a ligand for growth factors, chemokines, and cell‐surface receptors [7]. For example, it can engage with sialic acid‐binding immunoglobulin‐type lectin (Siglec) 11 [10], which is an anti‐inflammatory receptor expressed by macrophages and microglia [10, 11], contributing to brain immune homeostasis [12, 13, 14]. Dysregulation of polysialic acid expression is associated with several diseases [8], including cancer [15], autoimmunity [16], and schizophrenia [17]. Polysialic acid may find application as a therapeutic agent, and for example, polysialic acid with an average degree of polymerization (DP) of ∼20 can bind to Siglec 11 and act as an anti‐inflammatory agent [13, 18, 19]. It can inhibit lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐induced tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α production. Moreover, it prevents the oxidative burst of human macrophages triggered by neural debris or fibrillary amyloid‐β1–42, and its neuroprotective properties have been demonstrated in a human macrophage‐neuron co‐culture system [13]. Based on these anti‐inflammatory properties, polysialic acid nanoparticles are currently under clinical investigation for treatment of age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) [19, 20].

Several pathogenic bacteria express capsular polysaccharides composed of α(2,8)‐ or α(2,9)‐linked polysialic [21]. For example, the human pathogens Neisseria meningitidis serogroup B (NmB) [22] and Escherichia coli K1 [23] express an α(2,8)‐linked polysialic acid containing capsule, while N. meningitis serogroup C (NmC) is encapsulated by α(2,9)‐linked polysialic acid [22]. The capsular polysialic acids of these bacteria are important virulence factors contributing to invasive diseases such as meningitis and urinary tract infections [21]. Therefore, polysialic acids are important for the development of bactericidal agents [24, 25] and vaccines [26, 27, 28]. For example, human vaccines for meningococcal group C are based on glycoconjugates of isolated α(2,9)‐polysialic acid conjugated to the carrier protein diphtheria or tetanus toxoid [28].

There are indications that the length of a polysialic chain can determine biological properties [7]. For example, the preferred ligand of Siglec 11 is α(2,8)‐linked oligosialic acid with a DP of 2–3 [10], but the anti‐inflammatory effect induced by engaging Siglec 11 requires longer chains with a DP of ∼20 [13]. Whether this effect is due to multivalent interactions of the longer polymers or whether binding affinity is length‐dependent is not known. A lack of insight into the precise molecular mechanisms of poly‐oligosialic acid function is in part due to a lack of oligomers of defined length.

Although elegant chemical approaches have been reported for the preparation of well‐defined α(2,8)‐ and α(2,9)‐linked oligosialic acids [29, 30, 31], these methods are very demanding due to the intrinsic properties of sialic acid that make them poor glycosyl donors [32]. Fermentation in E. coli can provide α(2,8)‐linked polysialic acid in a scalable manner; however, the resulting polymers are very heterogeneous, having an average DP of 100–800 [33, 34]. Acid hydrolysis can provide shorter polysialic acid chains; however, it also results in heterogeneous mixtures of compounds [35, 36, 37]. Enzyme‐catalyzed glycosylations can streamline oligosaccharide assembly as it forgoes the use of protecting groups and generally proceeds with complete stereo‐ and regioselectivity [38]. In this regard, polysialyltransferases (PSTs) of NmB [39] and NmC [40] are attractive biocatalysts for the synthesis of α(2,8)‐ and α(2,9)‐linked oligosialic acids, respectively. These enzymes are, however, polymerases that produce mixtures of sialosides of different length [41, 42].

Here, we report a chemoenzymatic methodology for the facile synthesis of α(2,8)‐ and α(2,9)‐linked oligosialic acids of defined length. It is inspired by a stop‐and‐go chemoenzymatic methodology for N‐glycan synthesis in which non‐canonical monosaccharides are employed to temporarily block specific sites from enzymatic modification [43]. In this study, we set out to identify chemically modified cytidine 5’‐monophosphate (CMP)‐Neu5Ac derivatives (1–5, Figure 1b) that are readily accepted by a PST to form an α(2,8)‐ or α(2,9)‐linked sialoside. Once installed, the chemical modification of Neu5Ac prevents further glycosylation (“stop”) thereby blocking polymerization. Next, the artificial chemical entity can be removed under mild conditions (“go”) to give natural Neu5Ac that can be employed for a next elongation cycle (Figure 1c). We explored two types of chemically modified CMP‐Neu5Ac derivatives having an ester at C‐9 (compound 1–4, Figure 1b) or thioethyl ether modified acetamide at C‐5 of Neu5Ac (5, Figure 1b). The ester modification can easily be removed by base, whereas the thioethyl moiety can be unmasked by hydrogenation over Raney Nickel (RaNi, Figure 1c).

Results and Discussion

Chemoenzymatic Synthesis

Both NmC‐PST and NmB‐PST require a disialoside as a minimal acceptor [39, 40], and therefore we first enzymatically prepared disialylated primer 11 carrying an anomeric linker for conjugation purposes (Scheme 1). Thus, lactosyl derivative 10 was treated with CMP‐Neu5Ac in the presence of recombinant bacterial sialyltransferase from Pasteurella multocida (PmST1 M144D) [44] to install an α(2,3)‐linked sialoside. This intermediate was further extended by an α(2,8)‐linked sialoside using recombinant bacterial sialyltransferase from Campylobacter jejuni (CST‐II) [45] and CMP‐Neu5Ac to give primer 11.

Chemoenzymatic synthesis of a disialylated primer as minimal acceptor for NmC‐PST and NmB‐PST.

Initially, we focused on synthesis of α(2,9)‐linked sialosides using CMP‐Neu5Ac derivatives having an ester at C‐9 (1–4, Scheme 2a). It was expected that successful transfer by an α(2,9)‐polymerase would provide a product that cannot be further extended at the C‐9 position. The ester can, however, be removed by mild base treatment, which would give an acceptor for further enzymatic elongation. Thus, Neu5Ac was treated with four different trimethyl orthoesters in presence of catalytic p‐TsOH in DMSO, providing C‐9 acetyl, propyonyl, butyryl, and benzoyl modified Neu5Ac derivatives 6–9, respectively (Scheme 2a) [46]. The monoester‐protected Neu5Ac derivatives could enzymatically be converted into the corresponding nucleotides 1–4 by incubation with cytidine 5'‐triphosphate (CTP) in the presence of recombinant CMP‐sialic acid synthase from N. meningitis (NmCSS), which has relaxed substrate specificity [47]. After purification by successive silica gel and size exclusion chromatography (SEC), CMP‐Neu5Ac derivatives 1–4 were obtained in good yield.

(a) Chemoenzymatic synthesis of C‐9 acylated CMP‐Neu5Ac derivatives 1–4. (b) Chemoenzymatic synthesis of α(2,9)‐linked oligosialosides.

We evaluated whether the 9‐O‐acylated donors 1–4 are appropriate substrates for NmC‐PST for the controlled synthesis of α(2,9)‐linked oligosialosides (see also Scheme S1a). Donors 1–4 and primer 11 were incubated in the presence of NmC‐PST in Tris buffer at pH 7.0 to minimize ester hydrolysis. After an incubation time of 18 h, electrospray ionization‐mass spectrometry (ESI‐MS) analysis of the reaction mixtures indicated that the esters of donors 1 and 2 had hydrolyzed, as formation of a mixture of oligosialosides of different chain length was observed. Gratifyingly, the bulkier esters of donors 3 and 4 were sufficiently hydrolytically stable and full conversion of 11 into the corresponding modified trisialosides was observed, demonstrating that NmC‐PST tolerates donors with relatively large modifications at C‐9 of Neu5Ac. Since donors 3 and 4 performed equally well, we proceeded with the benzoylated donor 4, as the aromatic group aids detection by UV absorption.

Trisialoside 12 was prepared on a sufficiently large scale for further synthesis and isolated by SEC using Bio‐Gel P‐6. The benzoyl ester of 12 was hydrolyzed by treatment with 10% aqueous NH3 for 18 h. After evaporation of the base, the reaction mixture was lyophilized and the residue purified by SEC to provide trisialoside 13. The sequence of enzymatic elongation and deprotection by aqueous NH3 was repeated six times (Scheme 2b) providing octasialoside 23 with an average yield per cycle of 62%. Each oligosaccharide (12–23) was isolated as a single entity, as demonstrated by LC‐MS and 1H‐NMR (Figure 2), confirming installation of a single modified Neu5Ac residue per cycle and proper deprotection. During the first reaction cycle, several additions of enzyme and donor were required to achieve full conversion. Although the same reaction conditions were employed for the subsequent reaction cycles, only one addition of enzyme and shorter reaction times were required to achieve full conversion of the starting material into product, which is in agreement with a previous report that NmC‐PST has a preference for elongated sialosides [40]. We also found that larger oligosialosides co‐eluted with protein during SEC purification. Therefore, oligosialosides with five or more Neu5Ac residues (i.e., compounds 16–23) were purified by anion‐change chromatography using a diethylaminoethyl (DEAE) resin. After loading the reaction mixture on the resin, impurities such as protein, salts, and excess donor were washed away with pure water and then 50 mM aqueous NH4HCO3. The product was eluted by increasing the buffer concentration to a maximum of 200 mM. Although each reaction proceeded to completion, only an average yield of 62% per cycle was achieved which mainly is due to loss of compound during the purification steps. The aminolysis of the benzoate esters involves ammonia, which can easily be removed by evaporation. Benzamide is the only by‐product that is formed, which should not interfere in a subsequent enzymatic transformation. Thus, it is likely that SEC purification after the aminolysis can be excluded potentially improving the overall yield per cycle.

1H NMRs of the ester‐modified and unmasked α(2,9)‐linked oligosialosides. A downfield shift of the C‐9 proton signals of is characteristic for 9‐O‐benzoylation and the region of these shifted peaks is shaded in red.

Next, we set out to develop a similar strategy for the chemoenzymatic synthesis of α(2,8)‐linked oligosialic acids using recombinant NmB‐PST [39]. Initially, the 9‐O‐acylated donors 3 and 4 were evaluated as substrates for NmB‐PST (Scheme S1b). These donors were, however, poorly accepted by this enzyme. We considered Neu5Ac donors having a modification at the C‐8 hydroxyl. However, such donors would probably not be accepted given the enzyme's noncompliance with the C‐9 modified donors. Moreover, the synthesis of such donors would be challenging as C‐8 esters are highly prone to migration [48, 49, 50]. Instead, we focused our attention on CMP‐Neu5Ac donors having a temporarily stopping modification at the C‐5 position. It has been reported that NmB‐PST tolerates modified donors in which the C‐5 acetamide is replaced by other linear amides, while recognition of the resulting oligosialoside as acceptor substrate progressively decreases with increasing amide bulk [51]. Strikingly, only a single sialic acid unit is installed when the C‐5 acetamide of CMP‐Neu5Ac is replaced by a pentylamide. Such a modification is, however, permanent, and thus not suitable for the envisaged stepwise extension approach. We hypothesized that substitution of a single carbon atom in the acyl chain with sulfur could resolve this issue. The resulting S‐ethylthioglycolamide modification of Neu5Ac in derivative 5 (Scheme 1b) is isosteric to pentylamide and therefore was expected to block polymerization by NmB‐PST. Importantly, the thioethyl ether can be removed by reductive desulfurization [52], yielding natural Neu5Ac, making 5 an appropriate substrate for controlled synthesis of α(2,8)‐linked oligosialic acids.

Sialyl donor 5 was synthesized in two steps starting from mannosamine (Scheme 3a). First, acylation of the amine with N‐succinimidyl S‐ethylthioglycolate gave derivative 24, which was converted in a one‐pot two‐step enzymatic procedure into CMP‐Neu5Ac derivative 5 using E. coli sialic acid aldolase and NmCSS. As anticipated, this donor is an appropriate substrate for NmB‐PST (Scheme S1c), and modified trisialoside 25 was readily formed when using disialoside 11 as acceptor, as observed by ESI‐MS. The reaction proceeded to full conversion and no larger oligosialosides were detected.

(a) Chemoenzymatic synthesis of 5‐N‐modified CMP‐Neu5Ac derivative 5. (b) Chemoenzymatic synthesis of α(2,8)‐linked oligosialosides. 25: R = N3, 26–34: R = NH2.

The sulfide modification at the C‐5 amide of 25 was removed by Raney Nickel catalyzed reduction under a H2 atmosphere. This reductive desulfurization procedure provided the unmodified trisialoside 26, releasing only volatile byproducts (i.e., ethane and H2S). The azide of the anomeric linker was also reduced to a primary amine. After filtration to remove the catalyst, only purification over a short size exclusion column using Bio‐Gel P‐6 was applied to remove salts and other impurities that may have leached from the catalyst. Similarly to the use of C‐9 modified CMP‐Neu5Ac donors, purification after the unmasking step is only required to obtain high quality analytical data and can in principle be omitted to streamline the overall synthetic procedure. The sequence of enzymatic elongation and reductive deprotection was repeated five times to provide heptasialoside 34 in an overall yield of ∼55% for each cycle. Similarly to NmC‐PST, it was observed that NmB‐PST favors longer oligosialoside acceptors, which is in agreement with the reported processive character of this enzyme [41]. Similar purification procedures were employed as for the α(2,9)‐linked oligosialosides, namely SEC for compounds containing fewer than five Neu5Ac residues (25–28) and anion exchange chromatography for larger compounds (29–34). The progress of the of elongation and deprotection of α(2,8)‐linked oligosialosides was monitored by matrix assisted laser desorption/ionization time‐of‐flight (MALDI‐TOF) MS by treating samples first on‐target with 0.5% H3SO4, resulting in lactonization thereby stabilizing the oligosialosides [53]. This analytical technique is especially advantageous for larger oligosialosides, as analysis by ESI‐MS became increasingly challenging for compounds with a higher charge. The same analytical procedure was not compatible with α(2,9)‐linked oligosialosides, probably due to incomplete lactonization. We also found that the C‐5 modified donor 5 served as a chain terminating donor for NmC‐PST (Scheme S1d), in a similar fashion as for NmB‐PST, thus offering an alternative method to prepare α(2,9)‐linked oligosialosides (Scheme S2, Figure S2).

In Silico Analysis of Substrate Binding

While the blocking effect of the C‐5 modification is striking, a mechanistic and structural rationale cannot easily be inferred from the limited knowledge of the structural features of bacterial PSTs. Therefore, we performed in silico modelling of the binding of the natural and modified acceptor and donor to NmB‐PST. All known bacterial PSTs, including NmB‐PST, belong to the GT38 family of glycosyltransferases and share a common GT‐B architecture consisting of two Rossmann‐fold domains connected by a flexible hinge region [54]. Despite moderate sequence similarity (Figure S3), these enzymes share strictly conserved catalytic and substrate‐binding residues, making structural information from one bacterial PST applicable to others. Previously, Freiberger et al. [55]. proposed a binding model and catalytic mechanism for NmB‐PST based on Saturation Transfer Difference (STD) NMR and homology modelling. It appeared that the N‐terminal Rossmann domain can accommodate at least six Neu5Ac residues within the A1 subsite positioning the C‐8 hydroxyl of the terminal Neu5Ac toward E153, which was the proposed catalytic base (Figure 3b). Lizak et al. reported a structural foundation of the reaction mechanism of the bacterial PST from Mannheimia haemolytica serotype A2 (Mh‐PST) by solving the x‐ray crystal structure, revealing a GT‐B fold with a positively charged groove that spans both Rossmann domains and positions the donor and acceptor for catalysis [54]. Specifically, E153 of the conserved D/E‐D/E‐G motif acts as the catalytic base to abstract a proton from the C‐8 hydroxyl of the acceptor. Furthermore, H291 (H278 in NmB‐PST) of the HP motif acts as the catalytic acid, stabilizing the negatively charged CMP leaving group, assisted by S339 and T340 (S328 and S329 in NmB‐PST).

We generated a 3D structure of NmB‐PST using AlphaFold2 [56], which was validated by backbone alignment of a Swiss homology model built on the Mh‐PST template [57] (PDB: 5WCN; RMSD ∼1.2 Å), confirming that the predicted fold recapitulates the GT‐B architecture (Figure 3a) [54]. Binding poses of natural CMP‐Neu5Ac and chemically modified donor 5 were obtained by docking into the C‐terminal domain nucleotide cleft. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the most populated pose using Desmond [58] for 200 ns yielded for both the natural and modified donor (5) a binding mode consistent with the previously reported STD NMR study of NmB‐PST [55] with CMP‐Neu5Ac (Figure 3c, left) [56]. In both complexes, the cytidine moiety is anchored in the nucleotide‐binding cleft and sialic acid adopts a pose appropriate for catalysis (Figure 3c). H278, S328 and S329 stabilize the cytidine and phosphate moieties of the donor through hydrogen bonding. The C‐5 thioether substituent of 5 is accommodated in a pocket within the donor‐binding site without dislocating the sialic acid moiety from its canonical position, indicating that the modification is tolerated, which is consistent with the experimental observation that donor 5 is efficiently transferred by NmB‐PST.

The trisialoside 35a (Figure 4) was modelled using the GLYCAM Web carbohydrate builder [59], which generates solution‐phase conformations using the GLYCAM force field [60]. The model of C‐5 modified trisialoside 35b (Figure 4) was generated manually in Maestro with the thioether substituent introduced at C‐5 of the non‐reducing terminal Neu5Ac and the geometry optimized prior to docking. These structures served as inputs for all subsequent docking and co‐prediction workflows. Acceptor binding poses were obtained through two independent docking approaches. Vina‐carb [61] was used to dock the unmodified (35a) and C‐5 modified trisialoside (35b) into both the isolated N‐terminal Rossmann domain (RD1) and the full AlphaFold2 NmB‐PST model. Both simulations returned consistent binding geometries, with the trisialoside occupying the extended acceptor groove and the non‐reducing terminal Neu5Ac positioned near E153, in agreement with STD NMR‐derived binding model.

Having established the individual binding modes of the donors and acceptors, starting geometries for ternary complex MD simulations were generated by subjecting donor 5 and C‐5 unmodified (35a) or modified (35b) acceptor to AlphaFold3‐style [62] co‐prediction of ternary complexes. Five models were returned for each system, and pose selection was based on two geometric criteria reflecting the requirements for SN2‐competent catalysis. These were the distance between E153:Oε and the C‐8 hydroxyl of the non‐reducing terminal Neu5Ac moiety of the acceptor, and the distance between O‐8 of acceptor and the anomeric C‐2 of the donor. Gratifyingly, the selected poses were consistent with those obtained by Vina‐carb docking with respect to trisialoside orientation and subsite occupancy (see above), providing confidence that the starting geometries reflect genuine binding modes. The C‐5 thioether modification was parameterized using Desmond FFBuilder, which assigned OPLS4 [63] parameters to the non‐standard sulfur‐containing moiety. The ternary complexes were subjected to MD simulations in three independent replicates of 500 ns for the C‐5 modified acceptor (35a) and two independent replicates of 500 ns for the unmodified acceptor (35b).

Unmodified 35a adopted a stable binding geometry within the acceptor groove throughout the 500 ns trajectories (Figure 5b, left; Figure S5). It made a contact with anchoring residue R246 (>94% occupancy) and sustained interactions with Y134, T67, E44, K280, and the catalytic residue E153 (∼85% occupancy). These contacts lock the conformation of the terminal Neu5Ac of the acceptor in such a way that its C‐8 hydroxyl is positioned within reach of E153, as reflected by a mean E153:Oε – HO8 distance of 4.2 ± 1.4 Å (Table 1, Figure 5a) consistent with a geometry competent for proton abstraction. The corresponding exocyclic sidechain torsion ω7 and ω8, all adopt well‐defined, narrow conformational distributions (Table 1) consistent with the terminal Neu5Ac residue being locked in a catalytically productive conformer (Figure 5d).

The MD simulations of the ternary complex with the C‐5 modified trisialoside acceptor 35b exhibited a very different behavior (Figure 5a, right). While initially the modified acceptor occupied a similar position in the acceptor groove, the contact network is lost over the course of the simulation (Figure 5b, right; Figure S5). The protein backbone remained structurally stable across all three independent replicates (protein RMSD 1–2.5 Å throughout, Figure S4). The modified acceptor showed, however, increased ligand RMSD values reaching up to 10 Å in some replicates (Figure S4), indicating large‐scale repositioning of the acceptor within the binding groove. The dominant R246 contact was maintained (>95% occupancy; Figure 5b, right), anchoring the terminal Neu5Ac carboxylate to the groove through an interdomain ionic interaction equivalent to R259/K293 in Mh‐PST [54]. The contacts with Y134, T67, E44, and critically E153 were, however, lost (Figure 5b, right). E153 does not appear in the sustained contact map (>30% occupancy threshold) in any of the three replicates, indicating a failure of catalytic base engagement.

Analysis of the conformational space of the terminal Neu5Ac of acceptor 35b revealed the structural basis for a lack of catalytic activity (Figure 5d). We evaluated the conformational preferences of the exocyclic torsions ω7 (O7–C7–C6–O6), and ω8 (O7–C7–C8–O8), which dictate the positioning of the critical C‐8 hydroxyl group. In unmodified trisialoside 35a, both torsions are rigid and locked into a single conformation, with ω7 and ω8 maintaining gauche population majorities of 99.9% and 96.6%, respectively (Table 1). Conversely, the C5‐modified trisialoside 35b exhibits a large change in rotamer population, indicating substantial conformational flexibility. For ω7, the gauche population drops to 72.3% as it samples the ‐gauche conformation (25.3%). For ω8, it samples the anti (26.2%) and ‐gauche (8.5%) conformations. This data demonstrates that the unnatural C‐5 modification induces free rotation around these exocyclic bonds. As a result, the mean E153:Oε –HO8 distance increases to 13.1 ± 5.8 Å (Figure 5c), which is far beyond the range for productive proton abstraction. Furthermore, the nucleophilic attack geometry (acceptor O‐8 to donor C‐2) deteriorates to 11.0 ± 6.1 Å. These observations are consistent with an earlier computational study on the conformation of N‐acyl analogues of α(2,8)‐linked trisialosides, which showed that unnatural C‐5 substituents affect the exocyclic torsions ω8 and ω7 depending on the nature of the N‐acyl substituent, with bulkier analogues populating additional ω7 rotameric states not accessible to the native N‐acetyl structure [64].

Conclusion

In conclusion, a chemoenzymatic strategy is described for the preparation of well‐defined α(2,9)‐ and α(2,8)‐linked oligosialosides of different length without requiring complex chemical α‐sialylations. It is based on the use of the recombinant bacterial sialic acid polymerases, NmC‐PST and NmB‐PST, in combination with easily accessible modified CMP‐Neu5Ac donors. It provides products that temporarily lack glycosyl acceptor properties and hence prevent polymerization. This property can be achieved either by direct blocking the acceptor site (compound 4), or through the incorporation of thioethyl moiety at the C‐5 position of the donor (compound 5). The MD simulation provided a rationale for the tolerance of a C‐5 thioether of the sialyl donor. It also uncovered the molecular mechanism by which this modification of sialoside acceptors terminates chain elongation. The modified terminal Neu5Ac of an acceptor remains tethered to the acceptor groove through its carboxylate R246 ionic contact, but loses other important protein‐ligand contacts. In particular, ones that constrain ω8 in a productive rotameric state are lost, allowing the C‐8 OH arm to rotate away from E153, resulting in a catalytically incompetent geometry. This molecular explanation accounts for the stop‐and‐go behavior observed in the chemoenzymatic synthesis. It is to be expected that the chemoenzymatic methodology presented here can be combined with emerging automation technologies for chemoenzymatic synthesis of polyanionic oligosaccharides [65], thereby reducing time‐consuming purification steps to provide products in higher overall yields. The resulting compounds are of great interest for biochemical and biotechnological research. To date, biological evaluation has focused on of polysialoside chains of high DP, which are accessible through bacterial fermentation, leaving the biological significance and potential technological application of shorter oligosialosides understudied [66]. There are indications that such compounds can have important biomedical applications and, for example, it has been shown that a polysialoside of DP12 can rescue synaptic plasticity and cognitive function in models of tauopathy and amyloidosis associated with Alzheimer's disease [67]. A major area of interest of α(2,9)‐linked sialosides is the development of synthetic glycoconjugate vaccines [68]. It is known that chemically synthesized α(2,9)‐linked oligosialosides of low DP that are conjugated to a carrier protein can elicit anti‐NmC IgG antibody titers in mice comparable to licensed vaccines based on isolated polysaccharides [69, 70]. Conversely, α(2,8)‐linked oligosialosides are of low antigenicity [71], but of great value to investigate the roles of oligosialosides in human physiology. For example, such oligosialosides can be used in binding assays to elucidate the ligand preference of Siglec 11 and other Siglecs that bind α(2,8)‐linked Neu5Ac [72, 73]. Furthermore, chemoenzymatically synthesized oligosialosides can be linked to a multivalent scaffold to provide materials for anti‐inflammatory drug development [74].
