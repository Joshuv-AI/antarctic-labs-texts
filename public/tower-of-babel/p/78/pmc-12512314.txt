Does public indebtedness matter in the effect of public health expenditure on human longevity in Sub-Saharan Africa countries? Evidence from dynamic panel threshold regression
Authors: Jacques Boundioa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe third Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) aims to enable everyone to live in good health and to promote the well-being of all. However, despite the various efforts made to achieve this goal, sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is still characterised by low human longevity. To increase longevity, authors have stressed the importance of public spending on health. However, continuous borrowing and servicing of public debt deprive the SSA of funds that could be devoted to public spending on health. In this context, this research provides evidence on how public debt mitigates the effect of public health spending on human longevity in SSA. Using public indebtedness as a transition variable, this paper uses dynamic panel threshold regression to analyse the effects of public health expenditure on human longevity in 27 SSA countries covering the period 2010–2020. The results reveal a nonlinear relationship between public health spending and human longevity. This research shows that a level of public debt greater than 35.1348% of gross domestic product leads to a reduction in public spending on health and a decrease in human longevity. In view of these results, the governments of SSA countries must strengthen prudent budgetary measures to reduce public debt and increase the mobilisation of domestic resources to finance the health sector.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13561-025-00673-0.Keywords: Public debt, Public health expenditure, Human longevity, SSA, Dynamic panel threshold regression

Introduction

Health plays an important role in sustaining economic development, as it is both a prerequisite and an outcome of economic development [1]. It thus contributes to achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Its importance is further demonstrated in SDG3, which promotes a holistic approach to health. This goal must “enable all people to live in good health and promote well-being for all at all ages”. The amount of resources in the health sector, as measured by public health expenditure, is a potentially important factor in improving human longevity. Indeed [2], suggested that, to achieve higher life expectancy in a country, it may be necessary to prioritise the allocation of public health expenditure and per capita income. Public spending on health enables the development and maintenance of health infrastructure, the provision of essential medical supplies and equipment, and the recruitment and training of health professionals [3]. Theoretical arguments such as health demand theory explain the relationship between health spending and human longevity. Indeed, this theory postulates that people invest in their health via several methods, such as the use of healthcare, to achieve the desired state of health [4].

With this in mind, African leaders have pledged to increase public health expenditures (Abuja Declaration of 2001). In fact, domestic general government health expenditures (% of current health expenditures) increased from approximately 37.24% in 2010 to approximately 36.024% in 2015 and 41.15% in 2020 [5]. Despite this steady increase in public health expenditure in sub-Saharan Africa, it remains well below the commitment made by African states to devote at least 15% of their budgets to healthcare, as set out in the Abuja Declaration of 2001. These values are also lower than those in other regions. For example, in 2020, domestic general government health expenditures (% of current health expenditures) in Latin America and the Caribbean, East Asia and the Pacific, the World average, Europe and the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) were 54.55%, 50.13%, 63.54%, 65.75% and 62.75%, respectively [5].

In addition to the low level of public health expenditure, SSA has a low level of human longevity, characterised by low levels of human longevity, compared with other regions of the world. While life expectancy worldwide rose from 67.70 years in 2000 to approximately 73 years in 2020, life expectancy in SSA rose slowly, from approximately 51.26 years in 2000 to approximately 61 years in 2020 [5]. Additionally, for other regions, such as North America, East Asia and the Pacific, Latin America and the Caribbean, North Africa and the Middle East, the life expectancies in 2020 were 77.46, 76.75, 73.03 and 71.94 years, respectively.

High levels of public debt in these countries could be one of the factors limiting public spending on health [6] and could therefore reduce human longevity [7]. “Debt overhang” hypothesis states that public spending tends to fall as the debt burden increases beyond a certain threshold.

In the empirical literature, some authors have shown that public debt has a negative effect on public spending on healthcare [8–10]. Indeed, countries with high levels of public debt apply economic policies based on fiscal austerity measures, including spending cuts or tax increases, which could have a negative impact on the financing of healthcare systems [9, 11]. Reductions in the public budget due to debt servicing are also likely to have an impact on sectoral budget al.locations, including healthcare [8]. Debt servicing reduces available public resources and therefore diverts funds away from public services, such as healthcare. Economic policies to reduce debt often reduce spending on healthcare, an essential item in the public budget, and affect the efficiency of healthcare systems as a whole [12]. In Europe [13], have shown that high levels of public debt are associated with lower levels of spending on health and health system preparedness to address the COVID-19 pandemic crisis, generating a high mortality rate. Recently [14], reported that debt has a positive effect on public spending on health.

The SSA has seen an increase in public debt over the years. Public debt as a percentage of GDP rose from 25.35% in 2010 to 37.11% in 2015 and 56.27% in 2020 in SSA [15]. Additionally, from 2010 to 2021, public and publicly guaranteed debt services as a percentage of national income (GNI) more than tripled from 0.73% in 2010 to 2.00% in 2015 and 2.3% in 2020 [5].

While some studies have considered the effect of indebtedness on public health expenditures [16], research on the relationship between public indebtedness and human longevity is limited. The few studies that have investigated the relationship between indebtedness and human longevity include [17–67]. In addition, despite growing interest in the impact of health expenditures on life expectancy [21–26], limited research has examined how this relationship may vary depending on a country’s level of public debt. To fill that gap, this paper asks: Does public indebtedness moderate the effect of public health expenditure on human longevity in sub-Saharan Africa?

To answer that question, this study, uses the dynamic panel threshold model [27, 28], for 27 SSA countries from 2010 to 2020, to analyse the threshold effect of public debt on the relationship between public health expenditure and human longevity. The dynamic panel threshold model (DPTR), which is based on the generalised method of moments (GMM) in first difference and the bootstrap test for the existence of the threshold effect, addresses endogeneity by allowing the regressors as well as the threshold variable to be endogenous.

The remainder of the article is structured as follows: the literature review is discussed in the next section, followed by an overview of the methodological approach used. This is followed by a presentation and discussion of the results and a conclusion with policy implications.

Literature review

Theoretical foundations (Health demands and debt Theories)

Theoretical arguments have been proposed within the framework of health demand theory and human capital theory to explain the relationship between health expenditure and health capital. Health demand theory postulates that people invest in their health via a number of methods, such as the use of healthcare, to achieve the desired state of health [4]. It forms the basis of health investments made by individuals and governments [29]. Thus, the stock of health at the individual level is determined not only by household investments (e.g., private health expenditures) but also by health risk behaviours (e.g., excessive smoking). Investment thus helps keep individuals in good health. At the global or macroeconomic level, to improve the health of populations and consequently increase their productivity, the State invests in terms of public spending on health [30, 31]. However, public debt could play a role in the allocation of public resources to healthcare. Indeed, for some authors, public debt makes it possible to compensate for the shortfall in financial resources in developed and developing economies [32], and this financing tends to produce higher levels of public expenditure [33].

On the other hand, others have argued that the enormous debt burden tends to generate a sharp reduction in public expenditure and/or adjustments in components of public expenditure such as education and health expenditure, among others [68, 32, 34, 35]. In fact, high levels of public debt have negative effects on economic systems [36] and can decrease a government’s ability to respond to emergencies and social problems [37, 38]. Countries with high public debt levels apply economic policies based on fiscal austerity measures, including spending cuts or tax increases, which could negatively impact the funding for healthcare systems [11, 39].

Beyond these contradictory points of view, some authors, including [34], have emphasised that the potential effect of public debt on public spending is not linear. For the latter, beyond a specific limit or threshold, the continuous increase in the stock of public debt would lead to a decrease in productive and nonproductive spending. This argument is in line with [7] “Debt overhang” hypothesis, which asserts that public (investment) spending tends to decline following an increase in the debt burden beyond a certain threshold [9]. show that countries with high public debt apply fiscal austerity measures, including cuts in public spending [40]. attributed the fall in public spending, following a continuous increase in outstanding debt above a certain threshold, to the abandonment of some (or all) components of public spending in relation to the relative share of interest payments.

Public health expenditures and health outcomes

Empirical studies have shown that public spending on health has a positive effect on life expectancy at birth. The studies are disparate in terms of data, samples and methods. Concerning the data utilised, some studies relied on time series data for specific countries, whereas others adopted panel regressions on many nations. These studies covered both Africa and other continents. In addition, various methodologies have been utilised, including two-stage least squares, fixed effects, random effects and the generalised method of moments (GMM). Indeed [25], analysed the effect of public health spending on life expectancy in 42 African countries in the sub-Saharan region for the period 2000–2022. Using fixed effects and two-stage least squares as methodologies, their results revealed that health spending has a positive and significant effect on life expectancy [41]. , using the method of generalised moments, obtained similar results in the same region of the SSA. In Nigeria [22], , using an autoregressive model with a distributional lag in Nigeria between 1990 and 2022, also reported a positive effect. Positive relationships were also reported by [26, 42, 43] in Nigeria. On the European continent [23], studied the relationship between public health expenditures and health outcomes in developing countries of the European Union. Using the ordinary least squares method, they reported that public health spending significantly increased life expectancy at birth. On the same continent [21], studied the impact of health spending on health outcomes. Using the generalised method of moments (GMM) for thirty-eight countries from 1996 to 2020, they reported a positive effect on life expectancy at birth.

In Asia [44], examined the relationships between private, public and total health expenditures and three health outcomes, including life expectancy at birth, in the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation-Association of South East Asian Nations (SAARC-ASEAN) region. Using fixed and random effects models, they reported that both public and private health expenditures improved life expectancy. Later [24], , with random effects and the generalised least squares method, showed that public health spending contributes to improving life expectancy at birth.

It appears that all these studies, despite using different estimation methods (dynamic and static) with both panel data and time series, arrive at the same result.

Public debt and public spending on health

In one of the first studies on the effect of debt on public health spending in sub-Saharan Africa [45], used a random effects model. He used data from 35 sub-Saharan African countries for the period 1975–1994. The debt service ratio had a negative impact on public spending on health, whereas official development assistance had a positive influence. Similar results were obtained in a contemporary study covering the sub-Saharan African region: the debt burden suppressed public health spending [69]. The generalised method of moments was applied to data from 43 countries for the years 2000–2014 [69]. used three indicators of indebtedness, namely, the debt/exports ratio, the debt service/exports ratio and the debt/GDP ratio. The coefficient of each of these indicators was negative and statistically significant, demonstrating the negative effect of the debt burden on government health spending. The positive determinants of health spending in the study were institutional quality, official development assistance and GDP per capita [70]. applied Arellano and Bond’s estimator to panel data for 50 countries and reported that higher debt ratios dampened social spending.

A study including debt service as a determinant of social spending (including public health) in a sample of 84 developing countries reported an inverse relationship [10]. The coefficient of debt servicing was negative and statistically significant, indicating that debt reduces healthcare financing. Using data on a sample of Asian countries for the period 1980–2010 [68], concluded that debt servicing had a negative effect on social spending.

In contrast to the majority of studies that have confirmed the negative effects of the debt burden on healthcare financing, a recent study based on a sample of 59 countries established that healthcare spending increased with debt servicing [14]. The results suggest that, despite the debt burden, governments have prioritised the health sector and that there has been no crowding-out effect. Another study investigated whether debt relief led to an increase in budgetary allocation to healthcare [71]. He reported that the multilateral debt relief granted to African countries between 1989 and 2003 had a positive effect on healthcare funding. However, this was only the case in countries with better institutions. The authors recommended that institutional change be considered a precondition for debt cancellation.

Finaly, the conflicting findings could be explained by the difference in methodology. Indeed, ARDL regression represents long-term regression, while GMM captures short-term dynamics [46]. Another point is that in GMM, the stationarity of variables tends to be ignored because these models are generally limited to short time series.

Public debt and life expectancy

With respect to the effect of debt on life expectancy at birth [18], used the distributional lag autoregressive panel method for empirical analysis of seven emerging Asian countries over the period 1991–2019. They find that debt has a negative effect on life expectancy at birth. Similar results were obtained in a recent study of SSA countries [16] [19]. analysed the effect of external debt on longevity in developing countries, particularly in West Africa, from 1981 to 2020. Estimators from the scaled lag augmented autoregressive model, dynamic correlated common effects and Driscoll and Kraay were used. Using life expectancy as a proxy for longevity, they reported a negative effect. On the other hand [20], , with an autoregressive model, find that external debt is positively associated with life expectancy at birth in Turkey. They explained their result by the fact that the increase in life expectancy in Turkey led to an increase in population, and the increase in population led to an increase in the construction of new buildings. Additionally, in the 20 years prior to their study, they explained that Turkey had begun to renew its infrastructure while constructing new buildings for the growing population. As a result, it needed funds to finance its investments and may have resorted to external debt to finance these investments. Furthermore, ARDL deals with the long-term relationship between variables.

Research gap and contribution

The above review of the empirical literature raises a number of questions. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have analysed the nexus between public indebtedness-public health expenditure and human longevity. Specifically, the analysis of the threshold effect of public debt on the relationship between public spending on health and life expectancy at birth has not been the subject of an empirical study. This research fills this gap by using a new technique, the dynamic panel threshold model [27, 28], for 33 SSA countries from 2010 to 2020. This technique offers more advantages than the threshold techniques provided by [47–49] and [50]. Indeed, these techniques are useful only for static models and do not consider endogeneity issues (by treating the regressors and/or transition variables as exogenous).

Following the economic literature [25, 52, 57] and to reduce variable omission bias, we control our model by including several variables that have been identified as relevant determinants of life expectancy at birth. The first variable is the human capital index. It is used as a proxy for education and is assumed to positively influence life expectancy at birth [57].

The second variable is the proportion of the urban population to the total population. The effect of urbanisation on life expectancy at birth is ambiguous. Urbanisation can encourage the development of health and medical services, which has a positive influence on life expectancy at birth. On the other hand, a high rate of urbanisation increases pollution and greenhouse gas emissions [58], which has a negative impact on people’s health. Another variable is private health expenditure, which is measured by domestic private health expenditures (% current health expenditures). The effect of this variable is also ambiguous. Indeed, an increase in private health expenditure (direct payments in particular) can limit access to healthcare because people do not have the means to access health centres. However [59], find a positive effect on life expectancy at birth. The fourth variable is gross domestic product in purchasing power parity. It has been shown in the literature that income measured by gross domestic product improves life expectancy at birth [60].

Data sources

In this article, a panel of 27 SSA (see Table A1 in the appendix) countries1 covering the period 2010–2020 is used. Life expectancy at birth is used as the dependent variable to measure human longevity. The datasets for life expectancy at birth, private health expenditure, urbanisation public health expenditure and gross domestic product per capita were obtained from the World Bank database. Public debt as a percentage of GDP and education was sourced from the International Monetary Fund’s World Economic Outlook database and the Penn World Table, respectively (see Table A3).

Estimation method

Following [28], Eq. (6) is estimated via the dynamic panel threshold (GMM) estimator. This empirical methodology, which is based on the first-difference GMM estimator developed by [61], allows the threshold variable and/or the explanatory variables to be endogenous2. The default instruments for the exogenous independent variables are the independent variables themselves (as in the Arellano–Bond estimator). In addition, the dynamic panel threshold model (DPTR), which is based on the generalised method of moments (GMM) in first difference, reveals the true pattern of the relationship, whether kinked (i.e., a discrete change in slope or a fold in the relationship) or discontinuous, when the public debt variable reaches a threshold level. In other words, this model is estimated by adopting the dynamic panel threshold method with and without the imposition of a kink to determine the most appropriate model for elucidating the role of public debt in the relationship between public health spending and human longevity.

Furthermore, before analysing the estimation results of the threshold model, we perform a locally weighted ordinary least squares (LOWESS) analysis for public health expenditure on life expectancy at birth on the one hand and public debt on public health expenditure on the other. LOWESS is a non-parametric regression technique that makes no assumptions about the shape of the relationship between two variables and allows for an initial assessment of the existence of a non-linear relationship [65]. Figure 2 shows the LOWESS between public health expenditure and life expectancy at birth. The graph shows that the effects of public health expenditure on life expectancy are linear. However, Fig. 3, which shows the LOWESS between public debt and public health expenditure, changes sign after an initial phase of positive correlation. This leads us to believe that there is a non-linear relationship between public debt and public health expenditure. This non-linear relationship between these two variables could influence the relationship between public health expenditure and life expectancy at birth. Indeed, when public debt is low, it contributes to an increase in public health expenditure, which could translate into an increase in life expectancy. On the other hand, after reaching a certain level, public debt leads to a decrease in public health expenditure and therefore negatively influences life expectancy at birth.

Furthermore, the negative signs of these coefficients indicate the moderating effect of debt on the relationship between public spending on health and life expectancy at birth. This result explains the increase in the level of indebtedness of SSA countries and the inability of most of these countries to fall below a debt threshold or not to exceed it. The table also shows that the threshold level of the transition variable (indebtedness) is statistically significant. The maximum debt (public debt as a percentage of GDP) that guarantees a significant effect of public spending on health on life expectancy at birth is 35.1348%. This threshold divides the observations into two regimes, namely, those below the threshold value, which represents a low level of indebtedness, and those above the threshold value, which represents a high level of indebtedness.

In this research, more than 50% of the observations (countries) fall into the second regime (Table A6). This means that the majority of SSA countries operate above the threshold. Indeed, on average, public debt as a percentage of GDP over the period in the SSA region was 43.565% (Table A3); this value is higher than the threshold determined. This explains the negative and significant effect of public spending on health on life expectancy at birth in this research. When public debt is higher than the threshold of 35.1348%, it has a negative influence on the effect of public spending on health on life expectancy at birth in SSA countries. For example, an increase in the stock of public debt could lead various governments to react by reducing total public spending and increasing revenue to meet debt service payments. In such a situation, public spending on health is affected by the decrease in total public spending. Thus, public debt would lead to a reduction in public spending on health by reducing the room for manoeuvring for additional indebtedness.

Furthermore, in developing countries in general, loans from multilateral organisations are often subject to discipline in terms of public spending. This discipline may often involve reducing the budget deficit, capping the wage bill and the number of civil servants, and many other measures. All of these measures could have a negative effect on the health system by reducing the budgetary room for investment and operation in the health sector and by shortages or inadequacies in the health workforce [66]. have shown that, over the period 1995–2014, International Monetary Fund (IMF) loan conditionalities reduced per capita public spending on health by 0.248% in 16 West African countries. Finally, when the cost of servicing debt increases as a result of rising public debt, more public resources are devoted to interest payments, crowding out scarce funds needed to provide healthcare. Our result corroborates that of [54].

With respect to the control variables, the level of education has a positive and significant effect on life expectancy at birth at the 1% threshold. An increase in the number of years spent at school leads to an increase in life expectancy at birth in SSA countries. This result could be explained by the fact that education makes it possible to acquire knowledge and information about healthy behaviour in terms of health risks. The information and knowledge acquired could thus enable people to recognise the symptoms of illnesses and use medical services. Finally, a higher level of education provides knowledge of how to use information and communication technologies (icts), such as the internet, to obtain medical information and possibly benefit from professional health consultations. This result confirms that reported by [72].

The results also show that urbanisation has a significant negative effect on life expectancy at birth at the 1% threshold. In fact, an increase in the urban population results in a decrease in life expectancy at birth. This result is justified in the case of developing countries in general and those of SSA in particular. These countries are experiencing rapid urbanisation, which often leads to inadequate health services, higher crime rates and increased pollution and greenhouse gas emissions. All these factors could reduce life expectancy at birth. The inadequate supply of medical infrastructure and equipment resulting from population growth could make access to medical treatment more difficult and therefore adversely affect health. Pollution is also considered a factor that negatively influences public health [58, 73]. Our results are similar to those of [74] and [75]. Gross domestic product has a positive and significant effect on life expectancy at birth in SSA. An increase in income measured by GDP translates into an increase in life expectancy at birth, all other things being equal. When the gross domestic product increases, this translates into easier access to health services for the population, as well as access to other services that improve health (housing, drinking water, etc.) [60, 76]. reported similar results.

Robustness check

Three robustness tests were used to validate our main results. First, an alternative indicator for measuring public spending on health is used. In a second robustness test, an alternative indicator of public debt is tested. Finally, the two alternative indicators (public debt and public spending on health) were used together for the last robustness test.

The lagged value of the dependent variable also influences the present value of life expectancy at birth. Using an alternative indicator of public debt

In this section, instead of a stock variable (public debt % GDP), we use a flow variable (public and state-guaranteed debt service % GNI). As debt servicing is a flow variable, it highlights the diversion of available resources to meet the debt, which can have a negative effect on public spending on health. It is an indicator of vulnerability. Vulnerability refers to a country’s ability to service its debt on the basis of its national income. The results presented in Table 3 in column 2 confirm the existence of a public debt threshold in the relationship between public spending on health and life expectancy at birth. The optimal debt service threshold is set at 1.4660%. Public spending on health has a negative effect on life expectancy at birth. Indeed, for the stock, on average, debt servicing over the period in the SSA region was 1.638% (Table 1), which is borne at the determined threshold, hence the negative effect. Furthermore, the slope coefficient is also negative and significant at the 1% level, confirming the moderating effect of debt servicing on the relationship between the two variables. The difference in thresholds can be explained by the fact that 1.4660% reflects debt service flows, while 35.1348% refers stock effects. Control variables such as private expenditures on health, education, urbanisation and gross domestic product remain significant. These results once again confirm the robustness of the main findings of this analysis.

Conclusions and economic policy implications

This article aimed to analyse the effect of public indebtedness on the relationship between public health expenditure and human longevity in sub-Saharan Africa. The research focused on a panel of 27 countries over the period 2010–2020. A dynamic panel threshold model based on the first difference generalised method of moments estimator was applied. The results reveal a nonlinear relationship between public health expenditure and human longevity with a given threshold level of public debt. This research shows that a level of public debt greater than 35.1348% of gross domestic product leads to a reduction in public spending on health and a decrease in human longevity.

This study shows that, on average, countries in the region operate above this threshold, which explains the negative effect. Public debt is a constraint on the ability of SSA countries to devote the necessary resources to health. Debt distorts the positive effect of public health expenditure on life expectancy. In terms of economic policy implications, the various governments of SSA countries must keep public debt below 35.1348%. SSA governments could reduce the effect of public debt on health through public health expenditures by improving domestic revenue collection and expenditure efficiency. This objective could be achieved by promoting digital taxation through the introduction of electronic e-identification, e-declaration and e-payment of taxes. This could be achieved by setting up platforms along the lines of countries such as Burkina Faso (“FASO ARZEKA”, launched in 2025) and Nigeria (“taxpro-Max Platform”, launched in 2021). The introduction of these platforms could be accompanied by training programs for taxpayers in the use of these platforms, as well as awareness campaigns on the potential benefits of this new form of payment.

In addition, African countries could apply for a tax on digital services along the lines of Senegal, Kenya, Nigeria, Burkina Faso, etc. Governments should work together to create a harmonised continental strategy and policy on the taxation of the digital economy, as recommended at the International Conference on Taxation of the Digital Economy in Africa held in Dakar on 15 and 16 April 2025. It would also be necessary to extend the tax net to all sectors of activity to include property taxes. In addition, the informal sector is highly developed in this SSA, and to increase its contribution, it needs to be identified and integrated more fully into the digital world. Thus, a portion of these resources could be returned to the health sector. However, to reduce the healthcare sector’s dependence on public funding, countries could move towards operationalising health insurance, drawing inspiration from Rwanda’s experience. This would involve involving the entire population in its financing, while taking into account the different social strata in the contributions.

Furthermore, policies aimed at improving income and the level of education could help improve life expectancy at birth. Allocating more resources to the education system at all levels would therefore be necessary to guarantee access to quality education. Finally, an increase in healthcare provision or an appropriate healthcare policy should accompany urbanisation to anticipate and remedy the health problems associated with urbanisation. Indeed, an increase in the supply of health infrastructure and equipment at the same time as an increase in the urban population would make it possible to avoid problems of overcrowding in health centres and, consequently, medical treatment.

This study has certain limitations, particularly in terms of methodology. These limitations, include the relatively small size of the panel and the failure to incorporate a post-test for endogeneity in the xthenreg command. Future research could use alternative methods (e.g., the R’s ‘pdR’ package).
