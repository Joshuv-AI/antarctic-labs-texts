Cryptococcal Meningitis: Comparison of Short- (≤ 1 Week) Versus Longer-duration (> 1 Week) Liposomal Amphotericin B Induction Therapy and Factors Associated With Clinical Outcomes in HIV and Non-HIV Patients
Authors: Sun O Park, Matthew A Stack, Ty C Drake, Rodrigo Hasbun, Luis Ostrosky-Zeichner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShorter induction regimens for cryptococcal meningitis (CM) are being proposed, but data from resource-rich countries are limited. We compared clinical outcomes between short- (≤ 1 week) and longer-duration (> 1 week) of liposomal amphotericin B (L-AmB) induction therapy and investigated factors associated with clinical outcomes at a United States medical center.MethodsIn this retrospective study, cases of CM were identified by a positive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) culture for Cryptococcus, cryptococcal antigen (CrAg), or India ink stain during 2016–2023. Clinical characteristics and outcomes including Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) at discharge, in-hospital mortality, and 90-day readmission between short-duration and longer-duration of L-AmB induction therapy were compared. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify predictors of GOS at discharge and 90-day readmission.ResultsOf 129 patients, 20 were in the short-duration (11 patients with HIV) and 109 in the longer-duration L-AmB group (72 with HIV). The short-duration group was less severely ill at baseline. Unfavorable GOS at discharge was 50.0% versus 61.5% (P = .34), in-hospital mortality 5.0% versus 5.5% (P = .93), and 90-day readmission 25.0% versus 38.5% (P = .24) in the short-duration and longer-duration groups, respectively. In multivariable analyses, higher CSF CrAg titer, acute kidney injury, and absence of photophobia were associated with unfavorable GOS at discharge, whereas L-AmB duration was not significantly associated. Association between L-AmB duration and 90-day readmission was inconsistent.ConclusionsShort-duration L-AmB may have comparable outcomes to longer-duration in this cohort; further studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Cryptococcosis is one of the major fungal infectious diseases predominantly affecting immunocompromised hosts and was designated as a critical fungal priority pathogen by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2022 [1]. The incidence of cryptococcosis increased significantly during the HIV epidemic of the 1980s and 1990s [2]. By the early 21st century, the broad availability of antiretroviral therapy (ART) led to a progressive decline in the prevalence of cryptococcosis among individuals with HIV [2, 3]. However, the incidence of cryptococcosis in non-HIV immunocomprom.ised hosts, such as individuals receiving immunosuppressive agents, has been rising and now exceeds that in HIV at some centers in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand [2–4].

The propensity of Cryptococcus to invade the central nervous system poses unique challenges [2]. The management of cryptococcal meningitis (CM) involves 3 major components: (1) antifungal therapy to eradicate Cryptococcus, (2) control of elevated intracranial pressure, and (3) immune modulation tailored to patient's immune status [2, 5]. Disease resulting from weakened immunity and high fungal burden may require immune augmentation, whereas excess immune response such as immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome or postinfectious immune response syndrome may benefit from immunosuppressive therapy [2, 5].

Clinical studies of antifungal therapy against CM have centered on the optimal use of amphotericin B, flucytosine, and fluconazole [2]. Furthermore, liposomal amphotericin B (L-AmB) demonstrated reduced nephrotoxicity, fewer infusion-related reactions, and potentially improved fungicidal effect compared with amphotericin B deoxycholate (AmB-d) [6]. In resource-rich settings, 3–4 mg/kg/day of L-AmB plus flucytosine (100 mg/kg/day) for 2 weeks is the preferred induction therapy for CM in both HIV and non-HIV patients based on earlier clinical trial data, early fungicidal activity (EFA), a more favorable side effect profile compared with AmB-d, and accessibility [6–9]. In resource-limited settings, major clinical trials have informed treatment guidelines. The Advancing Cryptococcal Meningitis Treatment for Africa (ACTA) trial among HIV patients demonstrated similar mortality between 1-week and 2-week AmB-d induction therapy when combined with a partner drug [10]. Subsequently, the 2018 WHO recommended 1 week of AmB-d plus flucytosine, followed by 1 week of high-dose fluconazole (1200 mg/day for adults) as the preferred induction therapy in resource-limited settings [11]. Most recently, Ambisome Induction Optimization for cryptococcal meningitis (AMBITION-cm) trial demonstrated that a single 10-mg/kg dose of L-AmB combined with flucytosine and fluconazole was noninferior to the 2018 WHO recommended regimen, leading to its adoption as the first-line therapy in the 2022 WHO and other international guidelines for HIV-associated CM in resource-limited settings [8, 12].

The findings of these pivotal trials (ACTA and AMBITION-cm), which were conducted in resource-limited settings, have not been widely adopted in resource-rich settings for several reasons. First, AMBITION-cm compared a single high-dose L-AmB combined with 2 weeks of fluconazole plus flucytosine with a comparator regimen that is not a standard regimen in resource-rich settings [7, 13, 14]. Second, the use of high-dose fluconazole (1200 mg/day) in the induction phase in these trials may pose barriers for transplant recipients because of drug-drug interactions with calcineurin inhibitors and for individuals with liver cirrhosis because of concerns for hepatotoxicity [8]. Nonetheless, accumulating evidence suggests that the current regimen in resource-rich settings may warrant updating. For example, a phase II trial and a prospective cohort study from Africa suggested that 5–7 days of AmB-d achieved EFA, with rates comparable to those reported with 2-week courses [15, 16]. Additionally, a phase 2 AMBITION-cm trial showed that a single 10-mg/kg dose of L-AmB was noninferior to 14 days of 3 mg/kg/day of L-AmB, based on EFA, which is independently associated with clinical outcome [13, 17]. Updates to current CM treatment guidelines are hindered by limited clinical data from resource-rich settings.

In this context, we retrospectively compared the clinical characteristics and outcomes of short-duration (≤ 1 week) versus longer-duration (> 1 week) L-AmB induction therapy at a tertiary medical center in the United States. We examined factors associated with clinical outcomes, including the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) and 90-day all-cause hospital readmission to capture in-hospital functional recovery and postdischarge outcomes and healthcare utilization, respectively [18–20]. Our cohort included patients with HIV, solid organ transplantation (SOT), liver cirrhosis, and other conditions (Figure 1), reflecting a real-world clinical setting.

METHODS

Study Design and Study Population

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study. Patients aged ≥ 18 years who presented to 1 of 14 hospitals within the greater Houston metropolitan area and were diagnosed with CM between January 2016 and September 2023 were included.

Case Definition and Data Collection

Cases of CM were identified by a positive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) culture for Cryptococcus, or a positive CSF cryptococcal antigen test or India ink stain, using the TheraDoc Clinical Surveillance Software (Premier, Inc.). For the patients identified multiple times, only the first occurrence was included. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes diagnostic criteria.

Electronic medical records of CM patients during the hospital course were reviewed, and the following data were extracted—demographics (age, sex, race), Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), underlying conditions (HIV, SOT, liver cirrhosis, or other), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) at presentation, symptoms at presentation (fever, headache, neck stiffness/pain, nausea/vomiting, blurry vision, and photophobia), abnormal brain computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging at presentation, laboratory results (CSF or blood culture for Cryptococcus, CSF or serum for cryptococcal antigen [CrAg], CSF opening pressure [OP], CSF studies including gram stain, India ink, white blood cell count, glucose, and protein), total length of hospital stay (LOS), intensive care unit (ICU) admission, septic shock, AKI, need for ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt or lumbar drain placement, L-AmB duration, in-hospital mortality, GOS at discharge. CCI, a validated weighted score that quantifies overall comorbidity burden, was used to compare baseline comorbidity between treatment groups [21]. In the underlying conditions, conditions with fewer than 5 cases or unknown risks were categorized as “other.” Other conditions included CD4 lymphopenia, diabetes mellitus, sarcoidosis, Addison disease, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, COVID-19, Mantle cell lymphoma, end-stage renal disease, lupus, multiple myeloma, multiple sclerosis, and no apparent immunosuppression (Figure 1). To mitigate time-dependent bias, analyses were restricted to patients who survived and received at least 7 days of any antifungal agents, regardless of L-AmB duration. The GOS was dichotomized into unfavorable outcomes (scores 1–4; 1 = death, 2 = persistent vegetative state, 3 = severe disability, 4 = moderate disability) and favorable outcomes (score 5 = good recovery).

Measures and Statistical Analysis

SPSS version 29 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. A 2-sided P value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-squared test. Continuous variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The linearity assumption was examined for continuous variables. Continuous variables that did not meet linearity assumption were categorized.

Clinical characteristics between short-duration (≤ 1 week) and longer-duration (> 1 week) L-AmB induction therapy were compared. The association between L-AmB duration and clinical outcomes was assessed using bivariate logistic regression to estimate crude odds ratios (ORs). Adjusting for age, sex, race, underlying conditions, L-AmB treatment duration, CSF CrAg, and variables that were significant from bivariate analyses, multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate adjusted ORs (aORs) and to identify independent predictors of GOS at discharge and 90-day readmission. Given the small number of in-hospital deaths, multivariable regression was not performed for the in-hospital mortality outcome. LOS between short- and longer-duration L-AmB groups was compared as a measure of real-world resource utilization; however, LOS was not included for multivariable analysis because of a potential for time-dependent bias. Sensitivity analyses were performed using logistic regression adjusted for the propensity score, which was estimated using baseline characteristics including age, underlying condition, GCS, and CSF CrAg titer.

Variables with significant conceptual overlap were not included simultaneously in the multivariable analysis to minimize multicollinearity. Multicollinearity was further assessed using the variance inflation factor, and model fit was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow test.

RESULTS

Clinical Characteristics and Outcomes of Patients based on the Duration of L-AmB

A total of 157 cases of CM were identified. Twenty-three duplicate cases and 5 patients who did not complete at least 1 week of any antifungal therapy (4 due to death and 1 due to elopement) were excluded. After exclusion, 129 patients were included in the study (Figure 1). Of these, 20 patients received short-duration and 109 patients received longer-duration L-AmB, with a higher proportion of patients with HIV in the longer-duration group (11/20 vs 72/109). Of 129 CM cases, only 1 was caused by Cryptococcus gattii, and the rest by Cryptococcus neoformans. Most patients (96%) received combination therapy during the induction phase, and there were no significant differences in the induction regimens between the 2 groups: L-AmB plus flucytosine (89% vs 90%) or L-AmB plus fluconazole (5.0% vs 8.3%) in the short- and longer-duration groups, respectively. In nearly all cases (98%), L-AmB was administered at a dose of 3 mg/kg/day. Several baseline characteristics differed between short- and longer-duration L-AmB treatment groups (Table 1). Patients in the short-duration L-AmB group more often had a GCS score of 15 at presentation (89.5% vs 60.6%), lower initial median CSF CrAg titers (1:40 vs 1:640), lower initial median serum CrAg titers (1:160 vs 1:1280), fewer positive CSF cultures (40.0% vs 74.3%) or gram stains (10.5% vs 39.3%), and lower rates of nausea/vomiting (10.0% vs 47.7%) (all P < .05). Other baseline characteristics were not statistically different between the short- and longer-duration groups (all P ≥ .05) (Table 1); for example, median CCI scores (5.5 vs 6.0), positive CSF India ink stains (23.5% vs 48.0%), positive blood cultures with Cryptococcus (10.5% vs 36.0%), septic shock (5.0 vs 15.0%), need for a VP shunt or lumbar drain (5% vs 19.4%), and ICU admission (10.0% vs 27.5%). Although the difference was not statistically significant, AKI was diagnosed more often in the short-duration group (65.0% vs 59.0%, P = .6).

Rates of unfavorable GOS at discharge were 50.0% versus 61.5% (P = .34), in-hospital mortality was 5.0% versus 5.5% (P = .93), and 90-day readmission was 25.0% versus 38.5% (P = .24) for the short- and longer-duration L-Amb groups, respectively, with no evidence of differences between the groups. The median LOS was significantly shorter in the short-duration group than the longer-duration group (11 days vs 20 days, P < .001) (Table 1).

Outcome: Glasgow Outcome Scale at Discharge

In bivariate analyses (Table 2), a GCS score of 15 and presence of headache, neck stiffness, or photophobia at presentation were associated with favorable GOS at discharge. In contrast, older age, a CSF CrAg titer > 1:320, AKI, ICU admission, septic shock, and VP shunt or lumbar drain placement were associated with unfavorable GOS at discharge.

In multivariable analysis (Table 2), a CSF CrAg titer > 1:320 and AKI remained independently associated with unfavorable GOS at discharge (aOR = 3.37, P = .046; aOR = 3.36, P = .03, respectively) and presence of photophobia at presentation remained independently associated with favorable GOS at discharge (aOR = 0.21, P = .049). L-AmB duration was not significantly associated with GOS at discharge, although there was a trend toward unfavorable GOS at discharge with longer-duration L-AmB (aOR for longer-duration = 2.40, P = .29). In the propensity score-adjusted sensitivity analysis, the association between GOS at discharge and treatment duration remained non-significant, with an attenuated effect estimate (OR for longer-duration L-AmB = 1.26, P = .70). Overall, underlying conditions were not significantly associated with GOS at discharge (P = .15). However, compared with HIV, other conditions were associated with higher odds of unfavorable outcome (aOR = 6.02; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.07–33.70).

Outcome: 90-day All-cause Readmission

In bivariate analyses, no variables were significantly associated with 90-day all-cause readmission.

In multivariable analysis, longer duration L-AmB was significantly associated with 90-day readmission (aOR for longer-duration = 4.53, P = .048). However, propensity score-adjusted sensitivity analysis did not show a significant association between L-AmB duration and 90-day readmission, although the effect estimates were in the same direction (OR for longer-duration L-AmB = 2.43, P = .2). There was no significant association between underlying conditions and 90-day all-cause readmission (overall P = .26). However, in comparison with patients with HIV, there was a trend toward higher odds of 90-day readmission among patients with SOT, liver cirrhosis, or other conditions (aOR = 4.22, 95% CI, .91–19.7; aOR = 3.35, 95% CI, .54–20.58; aOR = 1.69, 95% CI, .50–5.14, respectively).

DISCUSSION

There were no significant differences in the outcomes including GOS at discharge, in-hospital mortality, and 90-day readmission between short- and longer-duration L-AmB groups, although there was a trend toward favorable outcomes in the short-duration group. The short-duration L-AmB group was generally less severely ill at baseline measured by a higher proportion of a GCS score of 15 at presentation and lower fungal burden with lower initial median CrAg titers or CSF culture positivity than the longer-duration L-AmB group, although AKI was more common. These findings suggest that clinicians may have preferentially used short-durations of L-AmB in patients with more favorable baseline profiles and may have discontinued L-AmB earlier in response to treatment-related toxicity, such as AKI. The propensity score-adjusted sensitivity analysis, which was performed given this potential bias, consistently showed no association between L-AmB duration and GOS at discharge, with the same trend toward unfavorable GOS at discharge in the longer-duration group. However, significant association between longer-duration L-AmB and 90-day readmission was no longer observed in the sensitivity analysis, although the direction of estimates was consistent. Taken together, our findings suggest L-AmB duration was not a major factor of these clinical outcomes.

Before the HIV epidemic, the combination of AmB-d (0.3 mg/kg/day) and flucytosine (150 mg/kg/day) for 4–6 weeks was considered the standard therapy [9]. The trial conducted by Mycoses study group and AIDS clinical trial group established the current 3-phase treatment framework for CM, which includes 2 weeks of induction therapy with AmB-d (0.7 mg/kg/day) and flucytosine (100 mg/kg/day), followed by 8 weeks of consolidation therapy with fluconazole, and then maintenance therapy [9]. More recent randomized trials conducted in Africa such as ACTA and AMBITION-cm have further informed clinical practice, particularly in resource-limited settings [10, 12, 17]. Important inferences can be drawn from prior studies. Both the ACTA and a randomized trial conducted in Thailand showed similar mortality between 1-week and 2-week durations of AmB-d as induction therapy for CM [10, 22]. Fungal clearance between 1-week and 2-week AmB-d groups was similar as well [10]. These prior studies suggest that a 1-week duration of amphotericin B induction therapy may achieve similar clinical outcomes to a 2-week duration for CM. Similarly, we did not find significant differences in clinical outcomes between short- (≤ 1 week) and longer-duration (> 1 week) L-AmB induction therapy.

In the phase 2 AMBITION-cm randomized trial among HIV patients, a single 10-mg/kg dose of L-AmB was noninferior to 14 days of L-AmB 3 mg/kg/day in EFA, with no statistical difference in 10-week mortality [17]. This may be attributable to the prolonged tissue half-life of L-AmB and its effective penetration into brain tissue [12]. As noted in a Commentary article, AMBITION-cm regimen demonstrated fungicidal activity comparable to that of 14 days of L-AmB plus flucytosine regimen, while also being associated with fewer adverse events, including anemia and AKI, shorter hospital stays, more line-free days, and reduced use of L-AmB [13].

Treatment recommendations for CM in resource-rich settings have not been updated, mainly because of the lack of recent randomized controlled trials conducted in these settings. However, emerging evidence from resource-limited settings may be underused. Factors beyond the practical challenges of using high-dose fluconazole (1200 mg/day) in patients with multiple comorbidities [8] that limit generalizability to resource-rich settings have not been clearly defined. Additionally, alternative dosing strategies or L-AmB duration depending on clinical parameters such as CSF CrAg titer or persistence of positive CSF culture may need to be investigated further. The 2010 Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines recommend an individualized approach for patients who failed to improve after induction therapy [23]. For example, extension of induction therapy or intensification of antifungal therapy using the most fungicidal drug combination when CSF cultures continue to be positive may be a logical strategy. Selection of an effective partner drug for induction therapy is critical, and the use of flucytosine as a partner agent has shown improved outcomes [9, 24]. A randomized trial conducted in Vietnam among patients with HIV-associated CM demonstrated 2 weeks of AmB-d plus flucytosine induction therapy improved survival and yeast clearance from CSF compared with 4 weeks of AmB-d monotherapy, whereas 2 weeks of AmB-d plus fluconazole did not confer a survival benefit compared with AmB-d monotherapy [24]. In combination with AmB-d, flucytosine was superior to fluconazole, and 1 week of AmB-d plus flucytosine group had the lowest 10-week mortality in the ACTA trial [10]. Furthermore, given concerns about drug interactions and hepatotoxicity associated with high-dose fluconazole, the optimal fluconazole dose may need to be reevaluated [8].

Our study conducted in resource-rich settings, including patients with HIV, SOT, non-HIV, non-transplant, provides preliminary insights. Further clinical studies addressing the management of CM in SOT and non-HIV, non-transplant populations, who experience higher mortality than HIV patients [3], are needed. Finally, although prior studies suggest that outcomes of cryptococcosis in non-HIV patients may be poorer than those in patients with HIV, our study did not demonstrate a significant difference, although the direction of effect was similar [25–29]. With epidemiology of cryptococcosis shifting away from HIV as the predominant risk factor since the widespread availability of ART, liver cirrhosis has emerged as a major risk factor for cryptococcosis, accounting for up to 36% of non-HIV-associated cryptococcosis [30]. Mortality from cryptococcosis in patients with end-stage liver disease (ESLD) has been reported to be as high as 75% to 80% in several studies [28, 31]. Deficiencies in the immune system, dysfunction of the reticuloendothelial cells, delay in diagnosis because of low clinical suspicion for cryptococcosis, and antifungal toxicity are considered plausible contributors to the high mortality observed in ESLD patients [28].

Our study has several limitations. Given the small sample size, especially in the short-duration L-AmB group, our findings may not be generalizable. Data were retrospectively collected from an electronic database, which are susceptible to selection bias, missing record, and misidentification of diagnoses or clinical parameters. Exclusion of patients who received <7 days of any antifungal therapy may have introduced selection bias; however, their inclusion could also introduce time-dependent bias, such that the associations between L-AmB duration and outcomes may not reflect a true effect. The study is subject to confounding by indication, as decisions regarding L-AmB duration may have been influenced by the patients' disease severity rather than random allocation. Although propensity score-adjusted sensitivity analyses were performed, residual confounding may persist, particularly given the small sample size of the short-duration group. Although multivariable analyses were performed, unmeasured confounding may remain. Additionally, inclusion of multiple covariates may have resulted in overfitting of the multivariable logistic regression model [32]. Outcomes that occurred outside our hospital system could not be captured, and postdischarge deaths or readmission to other institutions may have been missed. Some may have been admitted for CM-associated immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome or postinfectious immune response syndrome, for which management may differ from that of CM without these conditions. Nonetheless, our cohort reflects CM cases in real-world, resource-rich settings and may help inform clinicians practicing in similar contexts.

In conclusion, induction therapy with L-AmB for 1 week or less may be associated with clinical outcomes comparable to those of longer-duration therapy, at least in selected populations that match the baseline characteristics of our short-duration group. The traditional paradigm of a minimum 2 weeks of L-AmB for induction therapy may warrant reevaluation, even in resource-rich settings. There are significant unmet needs in resource-rich settings. Phase II trials to evaluate optimal duration and dosing of L-AmB and antifungal regimen and multicenter phase III trials to confirm the efficacy and safety in HIV and non-HIV populations, including SOT recipients and patients with ESLD, would be invaluable for optimizing CM therapy and informing guidelines.
