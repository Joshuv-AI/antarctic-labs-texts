A Case of Liver Hemorrhage Uncontrollable by Transarterial Embolization after Percutaneous Liver Biopsy in a Patient with Intravascular Large B-cell Lymphoma
Authors: Yuki Himoto, Yasuyuki Onishi, Yuito Michiwaki, Yuichi Tsuji, Tsuyoshi Ohno, Hironori Shimizu, Hiroshi Arima, Chisaki Mizumoto, Shigeharu Nakano, Yuki Masano, Yuji Nakamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntravascular large B-cell lymphoma is a rare, aggressive lymphoma characterized by intravascular growth. The liver is a common site of involvement. Although liver biopsy provides sufficient tissue for diagnosis and treatment planning, it carries a risk of severe hemorrhage. This report describes the case of a woman in her 50s with intravascular large B-cell lymphoma who developed liver hemorrhage that was uncontrollable by transarterial embolization after undergoing ultrasound-guided percutaneous liver biopsy, necessitating laparoscopic hemostasis. Pathological findings revealed markedly dilated liver sinusoids filled with tumor cells, suggesting direct hemorrhage from these vessels. This case highlights the potential for increased hemorrhage risk in intravascular large B-cell lymphoma and underscores the necessity for careful consideration of alternative biopsy techniques, such as the transjugular approach or percutaneous biopsy with tract embolization.Keywords: intravascular large B-cell lymphoma, percutaneous liver biopsy, hemorrhage

Introduction

Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma (IVLBCL) is a rare and aggressive lymphoma characterized by predominant intravascular growth, without formation of solid tumors [1, 2]. Clinically, IVLBCL presents with nonspecific symptoms such as fever of unknown origin, making diagnosis challenging [1]. The most commonly affected sites include the skin, liver, spleen, bone marrow, and central nervous system [1, 2]. Liver biopsy has been reported as a valuable diagnostic tool for IVLBCL, complementing random skin and bone marrow biopsies [2-4]. However, liver biopsy carries a considerable risk of hemorrhage [5, 6]. This report describes a case of IVLBCL complicated by post-biopsy liver hemorrhage that could not be controlled by transarterial embolization, highlighting the potential increased risk of peritoneal hemorrhage in IVLBCL.

Case Report

A woman in her early 50s with no prior medical history was referred to the Department of Hematology with fatigue, abdominal distention, and leg edema persisting for 1-2 months. Hypothyroidism, hypogonadism, thrombocytopenia (59,000/μL), and coagulation abnormalities were observed. Contrast-enhanced CT showed hepatosplenomegaly with ascites, mild pleural effusion, and subcutaneous edema (Figure 1A). The patient's condition deteriorated, necessitating admission to the intensive care unit. After steroid pulse therapy and continuous hemodiafiltration (CHDF), the patient recovered from metabolic acidosis. Regarding thrombocytopenia, the platelet count remained below 50,000/μL (ranging from 37,000 to 43,000) for several days after treatment initiation without transfusion, and subsequently stabilized at approximately 60,000/μL. FDG-PET/CT revealed marked hepatosplenomegaly with FDG uptake, indicating spleen and liver involvement (Figure 1B). The lack of FDG uptake suggested the absence of tumor involvement in other organs. Although random skin and bone marrow biopsies confirmed IVLBCL, the obtained tissue samples were inadequate for comprehensive diagnostic testing, including immunohistochemical studies, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and treatment planning. Consequently, an ultrasound-guided liver biopsy was planned after confirming safe conditions: resolution of ascites following treatment, platelet count of 61,000 (>50,000)/μL, and an adequate coagulation profile (prothrombin time international normalized ratio of 1.14, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) of 53.8 s, fibrinogen level of 336 mg/dL, D-dimer level of 15.9 μg/mL, and antithrombin III activity of 85.3%). The APTT was mildly prolonged relative to the standard reference range because of continuous heparin infusion during and after CHDF to prevent deep vein thrombosis. The infusion was discontinued 6 hours before the biopsy. Three liver punctures were performed using an 18-G biopsy needle (Monopty Disposable Core Biopsy Instrument; Becton, Dickinson and Co., Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) through the right intercostal space, targeting the anterior liver segment. The puncture routes were carefully planned to avoid vessels visualized using Doppler ultrasound, in accordance with our standard safety protocol (Figure S1). Immediately after the biopsy, a Doppler ultrasound was carefully performed to confirm the absence of major bleeding. A faint signal corresponding to the biopsy tract was observed, but it gradually attenuated.

Contrast-enhanced CT reveals hepatosplenomegaly (A) with ascites and subcutaneous edema (ascites had resolved by the time of liver biopsy following therapy). FDG-PET/CT after steroid pulse therapy and continuous renal replacement therapy shows increased FDG uptake in the liver and spleen (B), indicating their involvement. No abnormal FDG uptake suggestive of tumors in other organs is detected.

The patient developed upper right abdominal pain and dyspnea 15 minutes after the liver biopsy, accompanied by hypotension (blood pressure, 82/55 mmHg), tachycardia (94 bpm), and decreased peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (94%). Dynamic contrast-enhanced CT revealed extravasation at the liver surface (Figure 2A). Angiography confirmed hemorrhage from the biopsy tract (Figure 2B and C). The anterior segment branch of the right hepatic artery was embolized using 1-mm gelatin sponge particles (Gelpart; Nippon Kayaku Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) to achieve hemostasis. However, after returning to the ward, she experienced right back pain and a second episode of hypotension (blood pressure 68/58 mmHg). Doppler ultrasound revealed a nonpulsatile flow signal extending from the puncture site to the liver surface (Figure 3A), suggesting persistent bleeding. Although the second angiography exhibited no extravasation, CT arterioportography, performed by injecting contrast media through a diagnostic catheter placed in the superior mesenteric artery, detected extravasation of the contrast media at the liver surface near the biopsy site (Figure 3B). Given the preoperative diagnosis of a portal vein injury, laparoscopic hemostasis was performed. During the procedure, injury to the liver capsule and venous bleeding were observed (Figure S2). They were subsequently controlled by cauterization. Her postoperative course was uneventful.

Pathological findings of the liver biopsy revealed that the liver sinusoids were filled with tumor cells. Based on the pathological, immunohistochemical, and FISH findings, the final diagnosis was IVLBCL, non-germinal center B-cell-like and double-expressor lymphoma (C-MyC and Bcl-2 positive), allowing for subtype-specific diagnosis and treatment stratification. Following the diagnosis and assessment of tumor aggressiveness, and after initiating dose-adjusted etoposide, prednisone, vincristine, cyclophosphamide, and doxorubicin plus rituximab (DA-EPOCH-R) therapy, treatment was switched to polatuzumab vedotin, rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone (Pola-R-CHP) therapy. The treatment was completed successfully according to the prescribed course. Complete remission was achieved.

Discussion

We encountered a case of IVLBCL complicated by liver hemorrhage that was uncontrollable by transarterial embolization, necessitating laparoscopic hemostasis. This case highlights the potential for an increased risk of hemorrhage during percutaneous liver biopsy for IVLBCL.

Recent therapeutic advances have created a need for large tissue samples for immunohistochemical studies and FISH. In the case described herein, skin and bone marrow biopsies did not yield adequate samples. A liver biopsy was performed instead because strong FDG uptake was observed in the liver on PET/CT. Percutaneous liver biopsy, a relatively safe procedure [7], carries a risk of massive hemorrhage, usually of arterial origin [8]. Portal or venous hemorrhage requiring intervention is rare [9], possibly due to spontaneous hemostasis.

Comprehensive data regarding the safety of liver biopsy in IVLBCL are lacking. Terjung et al. [6] reported malignant lymphoma as a risk factor for biopsy-related bleeding. However, no specific risk associated with IVLBCL has been reported. Although some reports have described successful cases and the utility of liver biopsy [4, 10], Aoki et al. [5] reported an autopsy case of fatal hemoperitoneum following ultrasound-guided liver biopsy. Based on pathological findings in the liver, they presumed that massive blood extravasation originated directly from the markedly dilated liver sinusoids, which were filled with intermingled tumor cells and red blood cells [5]. Markedly dilated sinusoids caused by infiltrating tumor cells were observed in IVLBCL [3], as in our case. Although bleeding caused by tissue laceration was also considered, persistent bleeding from the sinusoids was regarded as the likely cause in our case, suggesting a high risk of hemorrhage following percutaneous liver biopsy in IVLBCL. Evaluating the safety of percutaneous liver biopsy in IVLBCL requires the collection of data from earlier cases of IVLBCL. In recent years, FDG-PET/CT has been regarded as useful for diagnosing IVLBCL, for determining the extent of disease progression, and for selecting biopsy sites [4]. FDG-PET/CT findings, such as diffuse liver involvement as observed in this case, might assist in risk stratification for liver biopsy in IVLBCL. A retrospective evaluation of earlier cases must be conducted.

Considering the higher risks of massive liver hemorrhage in IVLBCL and the frequent occurrence of pleural and peritoneal effusions, thrombocytopenia, and coagulopathy, safer alternatives might be transjugular liver biopsy (TJLB) and percutaneous liver biopsy with tract embolization. Particularly, TJLB can minimize the bleeding risk, especially in patients with coagulopathy. In some case reports, IVLBCL was diagnosed successfully using TJLB in coagulopathic patients [3, 11]. For percutaneous liver biopsy with tract embolization, a coaxial introducer is used. After obtaining the specimen, the tract is embolized with materials such as a gelatin sponge during withdrawal of the introducer [12]. It is noteworthy that TJLB can be technically challenging. Moreover, it tends to produce smaller or fragmented tissue samples and requires specialized equipment.

Conclusion

This report described a case of IVLBCL that required laparoscopic hemostasis after liver biopsy. IVLBCL might entail a heightened risk of hemorrhage post-biopsy, suggesting that TJLB or percutaneous liver biopsy with tract embolization might be a safer alternative for obtaining adequate tumor tissue.
