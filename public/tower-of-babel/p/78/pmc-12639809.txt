Nitromethane-Induced Assay Interference Mimicking Severe Acute Kidney Injury
Authors: Ting-Yun Lin, Szu-Chun Hung
Published: 2025

Introduction

Serum creatinine remains the cornerstone for diagnosing acute kidney injury (AKI) because of its accessibility and cost-effectiveness. However, it is prone to analytical interference, particularly with the Jaffé method—a colorimetric assay in which creatinine reacts with alkaline picrate to form a red-orange chromophore. Numerous exogenous and endogenous substances can mimic this chromogenic reaction, leading to spurious elevations or reductions in measured creatinine levels and resulting in potential misdiagnosis and inappropriate management.1 We report a case of apparent severe AKI following solvent inhalation, ultimately identified as assay interference through clinical correlation and confirmatory biomarker testing.

Case Presentation

A 45-year-old man with well-controlled hypertension on valsartan and bisoprolol was found collapsed in a poorly ventilated bathroom after using a commercially available adhesive remover purchased from Taobao.com. Although the product label listed no ingredients, information from the manufacturer's official website indicated the presence of nitromethane. His wife, who had been in the same enclosed space assisting with the task, also developed symptoms of dizziness and nausea, raising immediate concerns for acute solvent inhalation toxicity affecting both individuals.

Upon arrival at the emergency department approximately 1 hour later, the patient was alert and oriented (Glasgow Coma Scale E4V5M6), with stable vital signs as follows: blood pressure of 121/68 mm Hg, heart rate of 70 bpm (regular rhythm), and SpO2 of 95% on room air. He complained of dizziness but denied chest pain, dyspnea, vomiting, or neurologic deficits. A comprehensive physical examination was unremarkable. Laboratory evaluation revealed a striking elevation in serum creatinine to 16.35 mg/dl (baseline: 0.92 mg/dl, measured during a routine health check-up just 20 days earlier), measured using the Jaffé assay on an auto analyzer (ROCHE ANALYTICS; Roche Professional Diagnostics, Penzberg, Germany). In contrast, the blood urea nitrogen was only 15 mg/dl. Additional blood tests, including a complete blood count, demonstrated no anemia, thrombocytopenia, or leukocytosis suggestive of infection or inflammation. Liver function tests, including alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, bilirubin, and ammonia, were all normal. The electrolyte panel showed normal levels of sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium. Venous blood gas analysis revealed a pH of 7.38 with normal bicarbonate (24 mmol/l), indicating no acid-base disturbance. Renal ultrasound showed no abnormalities.

Given the discrepancy between the patient’s benign clinical presentation and the marked creatinine elevation, pseudo-AKI because of analytical interference was suspected. Serial creatinine values showed a rapid decline from 13.85 mg/dl at 8 hours to 11.08 mg/dl by the next morning. Serum cystatin C, retrospectively measured using a residual sample drawn at admission, was 0.65 mg/l (reference: 0.6–1.0 mg/l), corresponding to an estimated glomerular filtration rate > 90 ml/min per 1.73 m2. The patient received supportive care with observation and hydration and was discharged the next day without complications. Discussion

This case highlights a clinically significant pitfall of the Jaffé assay—pseudo-AKI because of analytical interference by nitromethane. The Jaffé method for creatinine measurement relies on a reaction between creatinine and picric acid in an alkaline solution, producing a colored compound (chromophore) that can be measured spectrophotometrically.2 Nitromethane, a volatile solvent found in various commercial products, can react with alkaline picrate to produce chromophores indistinguishable from those of creatinine (Supplementary Figure S1). This results in an overestimation of creatinine concentration, often by several-fold, despite preserved kidney function.3 Unrecognized interference may lead to unnecessary interventions, including dialysis.

The differential diagnosis for this presentation included true AKI from solvent toxicity. For example, toluene, another solvent commonly found in adhesive removers, can induce distal renal tubular acidosis and AKI through mechanisms such as rhabdomyolysis and acute tubular necrosis, typically manifesting with metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia, and possibly elevated muscle enzymes or myoglobinuria.4 However, key clinical indicators that pointed toward interference rather than authentic AKI in this patient included the sudden onset of extreme creatinine elevation without preceding risk factors (e.g., hypovolemia, nephrotoxic medications, or sepsis); the lack of corroborative findings such as azotemia (elevated blood urea nitrogen), oliguria, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, or imaging abnormalities; and the swift exponential decline in measured creatinine levels mirroring the clearance kinetics of the interfering substance rather than the slower recovery typical of resolving AKI. Moreover, the normal cystatin C value strongly supported preserved glomerular filtration rate, because this biomarker is independent of the Jaffé reaction, muscle mass fluctuations, diet, or age-related changes, making it a superior alternative in cases where serum creatinine results are discordant with the clinical picture.

The Jaffé method, used in this case, is employed by many widely available clinical analyzers, including those from Beckman Coulter, Roche Cobas, Siemens, and Abbott. Despite the availability of enzymatic alternatives, the Jaffé assay remains commonly used across both resource-rich and resource-limited settings because of its simplicity, speed, and cost-effectiveness.5 Nitromethane is among several agents known to cause positive interference in the Jaffé assay. Others include cephalosporins, ascorbic acid, dopamine, and ketones. Conversely, substances such as bilirubin, hemolysis, or paraproteins, may cause falsely low readings (Supplementary Table S1).6 Although nitromethane interference with the Jaffé assay is not entirely novel, this report adds value by describing a modern, real-world scenario involving inhalation exposure from a common household solvent in a nonoccupational setting, contrasting with most previous reports of ingestion from nitromethane-containing fuels.7 The continued underrecognition of this phenomenon, despite longstanding awareness, may reflect evolving formulations in consumer products that introduce unrecognized solvents, the reduced availability of enzymatic creatinine assays in resource-constrained settings, and limited clinician education on assay limitations in everyday toxicology contexts. To mitigate such diagnostic pitfalls, preventive strategies could include advocating for routine disclosure of ingredients in consumer products, implementing laboratory alerts for potential interferents during toxicology screens, promoting wider adoption of interference-resistant enzymatic assays, and enhancing clinician training on correlating laboratory data with clinical presentation.

Contemporary clinical practice supports a multimodal approach to AKI diagnosis, particularly in diagnostically challenging situations.8 Cystatin C, as used in this case, provides a robust, muscle-mass–independent estimate of glomerular filtration rate. Additional biomarkers such as neutrophil gelatinase–associated lipocalin or TIMP-2 × IGFBP7 (NephroCheck) can aid in distinguishing true tubular injury. Enzymatic assays for creatinine, which are less prone to interference, may also reduce misclassification.

In conclusion, analytical interference in creatinine assays remains an underrecognized cause of pseudo-AKI, particularly in the setting of solvent exposure. This case underscores the need for clinical vigilance and corroborative testing when laboratory data conflict with the clinical picture. Incorporating alternative biomarkers such as cystatin C can clarify renal status, improve diagnostic precision, and prevent unnecessary procedures.
