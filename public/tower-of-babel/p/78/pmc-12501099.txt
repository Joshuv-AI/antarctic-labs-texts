Secondary traumatic stress and burnout in healthcare professional: systematic review and a meta-analysis based on correlation coefficient
Authors: Sümeyye Ulaş, İsmail Seçer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe challenging conditions faced by healthcare professionals (HCPs) during the pandemic have been extensively discussed in the literature, particularly concerning Secondary Traumatic Stress (STS) and Burnout (BO). This study systematically compiled studies meeting the inclusion criteria and examining the relationship between STS and BO between 2019 and 2024 in the Web of Science and PubMed databases, conducting a correlational meta-analysis. While the PRISMA was adhered to in all stages of this manuscript, the Quality Assessment and Validity Tool for Correlational Studies was adhered to in evaluating the articles that met the inclusion criteria. This analysis included 61 publications involving 33.906 HCPs. When raw r coefficients were transformed into Fisher’s z values, the correlation coefficients ranged between 0.1820 and 1.1881, with a 100% positive direction, and the weighted correlation coefficient was 0.6305 (95% CI: 0.5888 to 0.6721). The results indicate a strong positive relationship between the levels of STS and BO among HCPs during the pandemic. The validation of the strong relationship between STS and BO during the COVID-19 period underscores the critical need for the development of information dissemination, resources, support, or policies to strengthen HCPs against STS and BO in the face of future epidemics, pandemics, or situations that could negatively impact the functioning of the healthcare system. In other words, it can be suggested to develop training and awareness programs for HCPs in terms of STS and BO, strengthen support systems, improve workload and working conditions, ensure continuity in monitoring and evaluating HCPs in terms of BO and STS, etc.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-06950-6.Keywords: Secondary traumatic stress, Burnout, Pandemic, Healthcare professionals, Correlational meta-analysisSubject terms: Psychology, Health care

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic emerged as a global threat to government operations, economic stability, and public health worldwide1. In addition to its biological impact, the pandemic also brought extensive and prolonged changes in daily life, resulting in psychosocial adversities at both individual and societal levels2. Healthcare professionals, who were on the frontlines of diagnosing and treating COVID-19, were identified as the primary risk group for these negative psychosocial impacts3,4. Those working directly with patients often had to cope with a wide range of mental health issues, including obsessive–compulsive disorder, stress, anxiety, depression5–7, burnout (BO)8, and secondary traumatic stress (STS)9, as they witnessed patient suffering and distress10,11. Several factors contributed to the increased risk levels for healthcare workers during the pandemic in comparison to other populations. While governments globally imposed restrictions on social activities and life in general to protect public health, healthcare workers were actively and directly engaged in managing the crisis on the frontlines. Therefore, they were forced to work under more intense and exhausting conditions. Besides the increased workload, they also experienced high levels of stress because of the need to plan and manage treatment processes for a disease with an uncertain prognosis2. Moreover, insufficient personal protective measures, equipment shortages, a higher risk of contracting the virus, the possibility of transmitting it to family members, and a higher risk of death created significant stress factors for healthcare workers. Furthermore, healthcare workers confronted with patients’ physical pain, psychological distress, and death during the pandemic were reported to have a high likelihood of developing secondary traumatic stress12. The prevalence of STS among healthcare workers during the pandemic was found to be 74% in Asia, 59% in North America, and 53% in Europe, with an overall prevalence rate of 65%13. Comparisons of STS levels in healthcare workers before and after the pandemic indicated a higher risk of developing STS after the pandemic. STS is defined as a condition characterized by the physical and emotional symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder, originating from intense empathic interactions with individuals who have experienced trauma14. It was reported that 80.6% of healthcare workers involved in the treatment process of COVID-19 patients and engaged in intense empathic interactions exhibited moderate levels of STS symptoms15. The core symptoms of STS in healthcare workers include fatigue, loss of appetite, sleep disturbances, attention deficits, fear, hopelessness, and irritability2. Factors such as increased workload during the pandemic, lack of protective equipment against infection, quarantine measures impacting daily life, uncertainty of the situation, inadequate social support, and decreased psychological resilience are considered risk factors for the emergence of STS13,16–19. The literature suggests that the levels of STS among healthcare workers during the pandemic are associated with depression, stress, anxiety5,20,21, compassion satisfaction22,23, emotion regulation24,25, psychological resilience9,26,27, and burnout22,23,28,29.

It can be considered common for healthcare workers to experience burnout under conditions of prolonged high stress, limited access to treatments, and rising case numbers30. STS symptoms can affect individuals’ empathic tendencies towards potential events or situations, potentially leading to interpersonal difficulties due to a changed worldview14. Wilkinson et al.31 reported a positive relationship between the empathic tendencies of healthcare professionals and their burnout levels. In other words, with the excessive level of empathic tendencies of healthcare professionals, healthcare professionals may be negatively affected emotionally by the suffering of their patients, and it is evaluated that this situation may pave the way for them to experience burnout in case of continuity. On the other hand, in cases such as pandemics, large-scale traumatic experiences, or disasters, the public turns to healthcare professionals for information about the process. At this point, healthcare professionals may feel that they are primarily responsible for managing the crisis situation related to this challenging process and may be limited in recognizing and managing their own emotions, worries, and concerns32. This can be fueled by being dedicated to managing their patients’ situations, and as a result, healthcare professionals may not be able to use their own emotional regulation processes functionally. For this reason, it can be considered that it may be a normal result for healthcare professionals to feel burned out in the face of both intense empathic tendencies towards patients and resorting to suppressing their own emotions for patients. The results of the conducted with citizens support this result and show that families, in particular, have significant expectations from healthcare professionals and the healthcare management system to ease their caregiving burden33.

This condition is linked to healthcare professionals becoming desensitized and feeling emotionally exhausted. Burnout (BO) is defined as a prolonged response to chronic emotional and interpersonal stressors experienced in the workplace and is comprised of three components: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a diminished sense of personal accomplishment. Burnout impairs both the social and psychological functioning of employees34. A study by Tan et al.35 found that during the pandemic, 43.9% of healthcare workers experienced burnout, particularly in the dimensions of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization. Wang et al.15 reported that 81.4% of healthcare workers experienced moderate levels of burnout during the pandemic. Key factors influencing high burnout levels among healthcare workers include psychological factors (stress, anxiety, coping style, psychiatric problems), social factors (family life, stigma), work-related factors (workload, insufficient staffing or equipment, working conditions, inadequate support services at the workplace), and COVID-19-specific factors (fear of COVID-19, exposure to COVID-19 patients, traumatic situations, being COVID-19 positive, a relative or colleague contracting COVID-19, witnessing COVID-19-related deaths, etc.)36. All these factors play a significant role in healthcare workers’ experiences of both secondary traumatic stress and burnout.

The relationship between STS and BO among HCPs since the pandemic has been examined in many cross-sectional studies. Given that these two concepts have the potential to directly impact healthcare workers’ quality of life and the quality of care they provide, it is very important to compile existing literature to generalize the relationship between these variables to the relevant population. The pandemic period has not only affected all humanity psychologically but has also been an extraordinary source of stress for HCPs. The results of the cross-sectional studies show that STS and BO are more prevalent in HCPs during this period. In this context, these variables are considered to be important for HCPs because they can directly affect both individual health and the quality of healthcare services provided to the community. Therefore, examining the relationships between these variables is important in terms of determining general trends in literature. Therefore, this study aims to examine the relationship between STS and BO levels among healthcare workers during the pandemic using correlational meta-analysis and to determine the overall effect size. In this context, the research question sought to be answered in this study is “What is the strength and direction of correlation between STS and BO level among HCPs during the COVID-19 pandemic?” By identifying the direction and magnitude of the total relationships between STS and BO, which have the potential to directly affect the quality of life of healthcare workers and the quality of care provided, the results can guide policymakers internationally and contribute to raising awareness among healthcare workers about potential risks to their mental health. These possible contributions can be considered as the practical purpose of this study.

Methods

Design

In this study, a correlational meta-analysis was conducted based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

Data selection criteria

Studies included in the meta-analysis were selected based on the following criteria: a) Studies conducted using cross-sectional, descriptive, or relational research designs; b) Studies involving healthcare professionals from any age group; c) Studies conducted during the pandemic period; d) Studies that calculated the raw correlation coefficient (r) between secondary trauma and burnout; e) Studies published in English.

Data search strategy

The Web of Science and PubMed databases were searched using the keywords “secondary trauma” and “burnout” or “secondary trauma” and “emotional exhaustion,” according to the inclusion criteria. Two researchers independently conducted the searches. The search process was completed on August 25, 2024. The PRISMA flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1.

Data abstraction/extraction

Studies with full-text access were downloaded, and those fitting the scope were independently reviewed by both researchers for preliminary screening. For data considered discordant, both researchers (SU and İS) jointly made the decision and excluded them. For studies considered suitable for inclusion, details such as authors, publication years, study design, country of conduct, healthcare professional group involved, age, sample size, scales used, correlation coefficient between secondary trauma and burnout, p-values, and quality assessment scores were extracted and are presented in Table 1.

Quality assessment

The quality of the studies included as data sources for the meta-analysis was evaluated using the Quality Assessment and Validity Tool for Correlational Studies37,38. This tool comprises components related to design, sample, measurement, and statistical analysis. This assessment tool consists of 13 items in total. While 12 items are scored as yes (1 point)—no (0 points), the item addressing the internal consistency of the measurement tool is also scored as yes 2 points and no 0 points when this coefficient is above 0.70. During the general validity assessment, the total score is used. Accordingly, studies that received a score between 0 and 4 indicate low validity, studies that received a score between 5 and 9 indicate medium validity, and studies that received a score between 10 and 14 indicate high validity. An example item in the assessment tool is “If a scale was used for measuring the dependent variable, was the internal consistency ≥ 0.70?”39. When the authors encountered disagreements, they continued the review and evaluation process until a consensus was reached. The quality scoring results of the included studies range from 7 to 13, and the quality score result of each study is given in the Study Quality Score column in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were performed by using the Jamovi 2.3.28 and SPSS 29.02.0 statistical software. In this process, the raw correlation coefficients from 61 studies were transformed into Fisher’s z values, which were then used for further analysis. To evaluate the heterogeneity of the included studies, Q statistics and I2 values for variance were examined. Depending on the heterogeneity test results, a decision was made to apply either a fixed-effect or random-effect model, and additionally, publication bias was tested using Begg and Mazumdar’s Rank Correlation, Egger’s Regression tests, and funnel plot examination. The meta-analysis results, based on the random effect size model, are presented in the results section. Furthermore, meta-regression analysis was conducted to determine the effect of possible heterogeneity due to the measurement tools used on the variance.

Table 1 shows that all included studies were conducted between 2019 and 2024 using a cross-sectional design. The sample sizes of the studies ranged between 68 and 10,305, with a total of 33.906 participants. The participants’ age range was between 18 and 74 years, and the majority of the studies were carried out on nurses. The studies represented 25 countries, and approximately 20% of them were carried out in China and 16% in the USA. The correlation coefficients between STS and BO measurements varied from 0.018 to 0.83.

Results

A total of 61 studies were considered suitable for the meta-analysis addressing the relationship between STS and BO among healthcare professionals during the pandemic period (details of the selection process are provided in Fig. 1, with additional information in Table 1, reference information of the studies included in the meta-analysis is provided in supplementary material). For model selection during the meta-analysis, heterogeneity was assessed using the Q test and the I2 index. The Q test value was 539.1691 (p < 0.001), and the I2 index was 89.53% (p < 0.001), indicating a high level of heterogeneity among the studies40,41. Consequently, a random-effects model was used in the meta-analysis. The observed Fisher r-to-z transformed correlation coefficients ranged from 0.1820 to 1.1881, with all estimates being positive (100%). A forest plot of the results is provided in Fig. 2. The estimated average Fisher r-to-z transformed correlation coefficient based on the random-effects model was 0.6305 (95% CI: 0.5888 to 0.6721). Thus, the average outcome significantly differed from zero (z = 29.6514, p < 0.0001). Fisher z value was converted to Perason’s r coefficient using the formula Pearson’s r = (e2Z-1)/( e2Z + 1). While “z” represents Fisher z coefficient, the natural logarithm base value “e” was taken as 2.718. In the calculations, the Pearson’s r conversion value of the Fisher z coefficient value of 0.6305 was found to be 0.559.

Forest plot.

Meta-regression analysis was conducted to investigate whether the relationship between BO and STS was affected by the measurement tools used in the studies. In the meta-regression, firstly, the variance values ​​of Fisher z were calculated for the effect size. The online calculation tool “https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/calculator/r-r“ was used for this process. Then, a comparison was made between the studies that used the ProQOL measurement tool and those that did not. As a result of the analysis, it was found that the measurement tool used was not a significant predictor of the overall effect size (β = -0.047, 95% CI = − 0.141 to 0.047, p > 0.05).

To test for publication bias, Begg and Mazumdar Rank Correlation and Egger’s Regression were calculated, and a funnel plot was drawn. Begg and Mazumdar Rank Correlation yielded tau: 0.037, p > 0.05, and Egger’s Regression yielded t: 0.309, p > 0.05, indicating no significant statistical bias in the publications. The funnel plot, with the y-axis representing the standard error and the x-axis representing Fisher’s z Transformed Correlation Coefficient, is shown in Fig. 3. Therefore, there was no evidence of bias in the publications.

Discussion

It is evident that the pandemic period introduced numerous psychosocial stressors for healthcare workers. However, understanding the relationship patterns between secondary traumatic stress (STS) and burnout (BO)—both significant outcomes of the pandemic for this population—necessitates a comprehensive review of the literature through meta-analysis, aiming to expand the perspectives on these issues. The relationship between STS and BO levels among healthcare workers during the pandemic was extensively examined, with varying magnitudes of correlation coefficients reported between these two variables42–44. It indicates that the studies included in the meta-analysis exhibit a high degree of heterogeneity, which may originate from differences in sample sizes and measurement tools. Meta-analysis findings derived from 33.906 healthcare workers across 25 countries reveal a strong positive relationship between STS and BO levels during the pandemic, indicating that healthcare professionals are at significant risk for severe mental health issues.

However, in the literature, there are studies reporting no relationship between STS and BO among healthcare professionals45. It is important to consider the diversity among healthcare professionals, including doctors, nurses, emergency responders, physiotherapists, psychologists, and radiotherapists, among others. Undoubtedly, the extent of direct patient contact varies by job position. Hadian et al.46 reported in their study on the COVID-19 pandemic that healthcare professionals, especially those in the position of nurses and paramedics providing emergency medical services, suffered from a number of psychiatric problems due to lack of equipment and workload, and that BO and STS were among the most important problems, and these two conditions were discussed under the theme of human resources in the study. Similarly, in a qualitative study conducted with nurses, nurses stated that they were physically and emotionally exhausted during the COVID-19 process47.

The studies included in the meta-analysis predominantly involved healthcare workers actively engaged on the front lines of the pandemic, suggesting a discrepancy in findings. Healthcare workers, especially nurses and doctors who directly interacted with COVID-19 patients—a virus responsible for 7,059,612 deaths worldwide to date48—are clearly at risk for STS and BO. Working with critically ill patients or assuming greater responsibilities has been identified as a key determinant of burnout syndrome among healthcare professionals49,50. Studies comparing healthcare workers who were directly involved in COVID-19 clinics with those who had no contact reported that those in contact experienced higher levels of burnout51. Furthermore, the uncertainty associated with a disease with an unpredictable course, ongoing and rapid changes in public health policies, the onset of economic downturns, and the inadequacies in protocols designed to prevent healthcare workers from becoming infected, all contributed significantly to the stress leading to burnout during the pandemic4. In addition, considering the fact that healthcare workers might neglect their own psychological and physical well-being to serve their patients52, the compatibility of their working conditions with their overall physical and psychological needs may serve as a significant protective factor against burnout. In a study carried out by Maddigan et al.53, it was revealed that there are negative correlations between the level of satisfaction with work arrangements or working conditions and Secondary Traumatic Stress (STS) and Burnout (BO). Similarly, Moreno-Jiménez et al.54 reported that workload and fear of contagion during the pandemic were predictive variables for STS. Additionally, Orrù et al.12 reported that perceived stress, witnessing patient deaths, and emotional exhaustion are also predictive variables for STS. These outcomes originate from the intense empathy, powerlessness, and frustration experienced by healthcare workers, especially in response to patient treatment failures or deaths55. The recurrence of such negative emotions and experiences increases the likelihood of developing STS56. Given the ongoing nature of the pandemic, which continually exposed healthcare workers to deaths and suffering in intensive care units, it is clear that they were in a high-risk position for STS.

Shaqiqi and Abou El Soun57 reported that secondary traumatic stress directly influences burnout levels among healthcare workers. Lee et al.,58 after diversifying variables, reported that healthcare workers with high job satisfaction, high compassion satisfaction, and low secondary traumatic stress were less likely to experience burnout in comparison to their colleagues. Scott et al.59 also identified secondary traumatic stress as a risk factor for burnout among healthcare workers. Therefore, strong relationships between STS and BO are evident, with both conditions directly influencing each other. Recognizing the complex and strong relationships between psychological variables rather than strict causality explains the variability in the predictive roles of STS and BO. Both the literature and the results of this meta-analysis make it clear that there is a strong and positive relationship between STS and BO levels among healthcare workers. In conclusion, during crisis periods, healthcare workers are at risk for STS and BO, and there is a need for individual or organizational interventions, training, or policies addressing these processes.

Limitations and future studies

This study has several limitations. First, there are constraints related to database access; while the researchers had access to Web of Science and PubMed, they did not have access to APA-affiliated databases. Moreover, the data obtained were limited by the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria. This study is also limited by the use of raw correlation coefficients between STS and BO. Despite these limitations, examining the relationships between STS and BO among 33.906 healthcare workers from 25 countries underscores the significance of this study. For future studies, especially in order not to suffer from the language limitation in this study, a meta-analysis study covering world languages ​​can be conducted by expanding the research team. In addition, a correlational meta-analysis study was conducted in this meta-analysis study and the results are descriptive. Future studies are recommended to conduct meta-analyses incorporating moderator variables. Therefore, it is suggested that in future studies, meta-analysis studies should be conducted on intervention approaches used in the intervention of STS and BO levels of HCPs, and that approaches with high effect sizes for STS and BO can be disseminated and that field experts can be aware of their use.
