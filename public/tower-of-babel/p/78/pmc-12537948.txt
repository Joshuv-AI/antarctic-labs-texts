Redox and magma recharge controls on excess sulfur build-up at Mount Samalas, 1257 CE
Authors: Shuo Ding, Marc-Antoine Longpré, Rita Economos, Yasmin Jackson, Céline-Marie Vidal, Jean-Christophe Komorowski, Brian Monteleone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The 1257 CE eruption of Mount Samalas (Indonesia) caused the largest stratospheric sulfur injection of the Common Era, as recorded by Greenland and Antarctic ice cores, and led to strong climate perturbations. About 80% of the eruption’s sulfur output likely came from vapor stored in the magma reservoir. However, the factors that controlled prodigious excess sulfur buildup at Samalas remain enigmatic. To address this problem, we analyzed sulfur and iron speciation, along with sulfur isotopes, in plagioclase-hosted melt inclusions and matrix glasses from 1257 pumice samples, and we modeled magma reservoir processes. Our results indicate that intermediate redox conditions, with sulfur as both sulfate and sulfide, and progressive magma recharge maximized pre-eruptive sulfur accumulation at Samalas. Redox-controlled sulfur speciation in the magma and magma reservoir growth via incremental recharge may thus modulate the sulfur emissions and climate impacts of large explosive volcanic eruptions.

Introduction

Explosive volcanic eruptions can inject massive amounts of sulfur (S) gases into the stratosphere and cause global cooling on timescales of months to years1–4. Extreme climate following large volcanic eruptions may yield to crop failure, known to have contributed to famine, epidemics and even regional social unrest in the past5,6. Volcanic sulfur emissions may thus have adverse effects, and their quantification has long been an integral part of volcano monitoring. Remote sensing observations for the 1991 Pinatubo eruption (Philippines) revealed that the directly measured stratospheric SO2 emissions were two orders of magnitude larger than the amount of S that could have been released by syn-eruptive degassing of the magma, as determined by a comparison of melt inclusions and matrix glasses7–9. Similar discrepancies have frequently been observed for other volcanic eruptions, such as Mount St. Helens in 1980 (USA)10, El Chichón in 1982 (Mexico)11, and Redoubt in 1989 (USA)12, and are commonly referred to as the “excess S problem”. Several hypotheses have been invoked to explain the excess S problem, including the decomposition of anhydrite and sulfide during or shortly after the eruption13,14, the supply of S-bearing vapor from unerupted magmas9,15,16, and/or the contribution of an exsolved gas phase present in the magma before eruption7,17,18. Resolving the cause of the excess S in volcanic eruptions requires detailed investigation of the distribution of S in different phases—melt, vapor and S-bearing minerals—in magmatic systems during the pre-eruptive stage16,19.

For eruptions preceding satellite technology, the petrologic method constrains the total sulfur yield20 while sulfate records from ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica provide key estimates of stratospheric S injections21. Both methods have their limitations22. The caldera-forming eruption of Mount Samalas, on Lombok, Indonesia (Fig. 1a), in 1257 CE was recognized as responsible for the largest sulfate spike of the Common Era recorded by Arctic and Antarctic ice cores, equivalent to a 119 Tg stratospheric SO2 emission23 with strong climatic impacts globally24–27 (Fig. 1b). Vidal et al.16 reconstructed a detailed S budget for the 1257 eruption using a mass balance approach and concluded that a pre-eruptive S-bearing vapor phase contributed ~80% of the total SO2 output. However, the potential factors controlling the highly effective sequestration of sulfur to the pre-eruptive vapor, such as vapor–melt partitioning and the redox conditions, have not been constrained. Moreover, magma recharge and mixing in the Samalas magmatic system were identified by a previous petrological study28, but were not considered in the mass balance approach16. Though redox reactions driven by magma mixing were previously proposed as the source of sulfur released at Pinatubo9, magma recharge has typically been overlooked in many recent studies estimating volcanic sulfur release, despite its long-recognized importance in large volcanic systems29–31. The Rinjani-Samalas volcanic complex is located in the eastern Sunda arc, associated with the northward subduction of the Indo-Australian plate beneath the Eurasian plate32 (Fig. 1a). Previous work proposed that the trachydacite magma of the 1257 eruption was derived from a high-alumina basalt parental magma through fractional crystallization in the lower crust, followed by pre-eruptive decompression-induced crystallization due to H2O saturation of the magma as magma migrated upwards, and, finally, in-situ crystallization under water-saturated conditions at shallow depth (90–120 MPa)28. In addition, the textural heterogeneity and zoning patterns of plagioclase phenocrysts16,28 suggest that magma mixing and recharge also likely occurred at Samalas.

Reference 16 reported that melt inclusions from the 2550 B.P. trachydacite eruption, an explosive eruption that preceded the 1257 eruption, match the whole rock composition of 1257 pumice in both major and trace elements. Therefore, the H2O and S concentrations, 3.9 ± 0.1 wt.% and 870 ± 20 ppm, respectively, in the least degassed melt inclusions from the 2550 B.P. samples were interpreted as the initial volatile contents at the onset of in-situ crystallization in the pre-1257 trachydacitic system16. The same authors then used H2O/Th systematics—assuming a perfectly incompatible behavior of Th—to argue that 3.5 wt.% H2O exsolved to a pre-eruptive vapor during ~45% in-situ crystallization. Sulfur yield was obtained from the difference between the assumed initial sulfur concentration (870 ± 20 ppm) and that of the degassed whole rock (80 ± 20 ppm) from the 1257 pumice. This loss of sulfur of 790 ± 30 ppm, when scaled to an erupted magma mass of 1.00 ± 0.08 × 1014 kg (dense rock equivalent—DRE—magma volume and density of 40 ± 3 km3 and 2500 kg/m3, respectively) translates to 79 ± 6 Tg S (ref.16). Although Fe-sulfides, as well as apatite, are observed as inclusions in phenocrysts (primarily within amphibole), their composition and mass balance calculations suggest that only a negligible amount of S ( ~ 50 ppm) is sequestered by these minor (mode ~0.01%) Fe-sulfide phases16. On the other hand, ref. 16 argued that an additional 13 ± 6 Tg S could have been contributed from the 1.5 wt.% fluid degassed from unerupted high-alumina basalts underplating and potentially recharging the magma reservoir. One critical underlying assumption of the mass balance approach is that all 5 wt.% exsolved fluid/vapor remained in the system prior to the eruption. However, shallow magma reservoirs, such as that which fed the Samalas eruption, may not always function as closed systems regarding S degassing, as proposed by a recent reassessment33 of sulfur release during the “Millennium Eruption” of Changbaishan (Paektu) volcano, whose magmatic system likely experienced pre-eruptive vapor loss. Moreover, the estimated S concentration in the pre-eruptive vapor (0.85 ± 0.15 wt.%) and the maximum S concentration measured in the trachydacitic melt (870 ± 20 ppm) from ref. 16 suggested a sulfur partition coefficient of 13. Previous experiments34 show that, in the absence of sulfide or sulfate phases, intermediate to felsic magmas exhibit such low S partition coefficients when S exists solely as S6+ in the melt. Therefore, it remains unclear if the S partitioning behavior and the closed-system degassing required to satisfy the model of ref. 16 are feasible under the pre-eruptive redox, pressure and temperature conditions relevant to the 1257 eruption.

The objective of this paper is to identify the redox conditions and magmatic processes that favored the prodigious excess sulfur emissions at Samalas and, by extension, that may be important for other climate-impacting eruptions. We present sulfur and iron speciation measurements performed by X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy, supported by analyses of major and volatile elements (by electron probe micro-analysis—EPMA), as well as S isotopes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta }^{34}S$$\end{document}δ34S, by secondary ion mass spectrometry—SIMS) in plagioclase-hosted melt inclusions and matrix glasses to track the redox evolution of the magma reservoir that erupted catastrophically in 1257 CE. We also evaluate the effects of magma recharge on S degassing via a box model (Methods). Our results demonstrate that a mixture of S6+ and S2− in the magma maximizes the sulfur degassing efficiency to the pre-eruptive vapor. Model calculations show that continuous magma recharge enhances the accumulation of vapor in the magma reservoir and the effective concentration of S in this pre-eruptive vapor, which could have eventually triggered this VEI 7 eruption and the release of ~80 Tg S into the atmosphere. We propose that S speciation in the magma and magma recharge dynamics may be key factors determining the S emissions and climate impacts of large explosive volcanic eruptions.

Results

Major and volatile elements

The composition of the investigated melt inclusions and matrix glasses is fully listed in Supplementary Data 1. Plagioclase-hosted melt inclusions show trachydacitic compositions, with 66.1 to 70.6 wt.% SiO2 and 1.1 to 0.54 wt.% MgO (on an anhydrous basis), overlapping with previous data16 (Fig. 2). As MgO contents decrease in the melt inclusions, Al2O3 and Na2O concentrations remain relatively constant, while CaO, FeOT and TiO2 decrease, and SiO2 and K2O increase (Figs. 2, S1). In comparison, matrix glasses exhibit SiO2 contents ranging from 67.3 to 69.9 wt.%, partially overlapping with the most evolved melt inclusion compositions, though some inclusions record even more evolved melts (Fig. 2). Iron speciation and oxygen fugacity

The Fe3+/ΣFe ratios in the melt inclusions are generally high (0.25–0.52%), decreasing from 0.52 to ~0.30 as MgO declines from 1.1 to 0.7 wt.%. As MgO continues decreasing to 0.5 wt.%, Fe3+/ΣFe ratios rise slightly to 0.35–0.40 (Fig. 4b). Ratios measured in matrix glasses cluster ~0.30, similar to the lowest values observed in melt inclusions with ~0.7 wt.% MgO. Overall, the melt inclusions with high S concentration generally have higher Fe3+/ ΣFe, while the low-S melt inclusions and the matrix glasses display lower Fe3+/ΣFe (Figs. 3a and 4b). Measured Fe3+/ΣFe ratios are equivalent to oxygen fugacity (fO2) values of 0.87 to 2.8 log units above the fayalite-magnetite-quartz (FMQ) buffer35–37 at the temperature of 950 °C and pressure of 120 MPa28,38, with the matrix glasses recording slightly more oxidized (FMQ + 0.95–1.3) conditions than the most reduced melt inclusion (FMQ + 0.87) (Fig. 4b).

As a point of comparison with the XANES data, the FeTiMM oxybarometer39, based on Fe and Ti partitioning between titanomagnetite microphenocrysts and the melt, may also provide constraints on fO2. Matching the average measured titanomagnetite composition to a range of matrix glass compositions gives an fO2 of FMQ + 0.80 ( ± 0.03) (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Data 3), very close to the lowest fO2 (FMQ + 0.87) recorded by the melt inclusions. Fe-Ti oxides are known to re-equilibrate rapidly to changing temperature–fO2 conditions39; therefore, fO2 values derived from them may reflect later-stage magmatic conditions compared to those recorded by melt inclusions. On the other hand, fO2 estimates obtained using the FeTiMM oxybarometer are 0.1–0.5 log units lower than the lowest fO2 values inferred from Fe-XANES analyses of matrix glasses, which potentially captures the final redox evolution upon eruption.

Plagioclase composition

Plagioclase phenocrysts hosting the melt inclusions studied herein show a bimodal compositional distribution (Fig. 4d), with a mode at high anorthite content (An = 100 × molar Ca/(Ca+Na+K) = 78–81) and another at low anorthite content (An40–62) (Supplementary Data 2), consistent with previous investigations16,28. S concentration in melt inclusions positively correlates with the anorthite content of the adjacent plagioclase (Fig. 4d). Published data28 and analysis of 46 backscattered electron images (selected images in Fig. 5a–d) show that most (Type 1, 38 out of 46 crystals—83%) plagioclase phenocrysts display conspicuous normal zoning, with high-An cores and low-An rims (Fig. 5e). However, some crystals (Type 2, 8 out of 46—17%) have low-An cores (Fig. 5f). Discussion

The relatively constant H2O concentrations and decreasing S and Cl concentrations with decreasing MgO (Fig. 3) in melt inclusions, as well as the presence of H2O-rich fluid inclusions16, all suggest that the pre-1257 magmatic system was vapor-saturated during in-situ crystallization, with S and Cl strongly partitioning into the pre-eruptive vapor phase. To test the hypothesis of efficient S extraction by pre-eruptive vapor proposed by ref. 16, we first construct an in-situ crystallization and degassing framework by calculating the mass fractions of vapor, residual melt and crystals at each crystallization step using Th systematics and mass balance of H2O (see Methods). Then, assuming an initial S concentration of 870 ppm16, we use experimentally constrained sulfur partition coefficients to track the sulfur evolution in the melt during in-situ crystallization and pre-eruptive degassing of the Samalas trachydacite. Foremost, the newly measured S and Fe speciation in the melt inclusions enables us to investigate the relationship between S degassing and redox evolution of the magma.

Sulfur degassing has been proposed to drive a decrease in oxygen fugacity observed in many mafic volcanic systems, usually revealed by concomitantly decreasing Fe3+/∑Fe, sulfur concentration, and, when measured, S6+/∑S in the melt41–44. Interestingly, as S decreases in the Samalas melt inclusions, we observe a broad decrease in Fe3+/∑Fe accompanied by an increase in S6+/∑S, at odds with previous findings. Though the potential contribution of Fe3+ from the plagioclase host may have caused an overestimation of Fe3+/∑Fe (Fig. S4), it is unlikely to be responsible for the overall decreasing trend of Fe3+/ΣFe as S decreases. Nevertheless, with the established decreasing Fe3+/ΣFe, we test whether sulfur degassing could have induced iron reduction in the Samalas magma by computing a redox budget of the system before and after S degassing (Methods, Table 1, Supplementary Data 4). We assume that the system starts with magma only and stays closed for redox budget during degassing and crystallization. In the final state, the melt and vapor phases contain similar numbers of excess electrons, with those in the melt derived entirely from Fe²⁺ and those in the vapor from SO₂ and H₂S. Considering the presence of ~0.1 wt.% iron sulfide (Fe2+S2−) in the mineral assemblage16, FeS could account for only a small portion of the redox budget. Our calculations (Table 1 and Supplementary Data 4) indicate that S degassing alone is insufficient to explain the observed reduction of Fe in the melt while preserving the redox budget. Crystallization (excluding FeS) must have contributed substantially to the redox budget (~50%), likely via Fe-bearing silicates and oxides, with a Fe3+/ΣFe ratio of 0.56 in the crystal cargo (Methods). Furthermore, though S has a stronger reducing/oxidizing power per mole than Fe, our analysis indicates that changes in Fe redox states via crystallization should dominate the redox budget in felsic systems with relatively low S contents (Table 1). Nevertheless, the inverse relationship between S6+/ΣS and Fe3+/ΣFe in the melt inclusions remains enigmatic (Figs. S2b and 4b). It has been suggested that the shift in sulfur speciation as Fe3+/ΣFe changes is a function of temperature46, FeO contents47, pressure48 and H2O contents. Notably, experiments49,50 have shown that, at constant fO2, Fe-free soda lime glasses display much higher S6+/ΣS than Fe-bearing glasses. Therefore, in the case of the Samalas magma, heating due to magma recharge and/or a decrease in melt FeO concentration could have contributed to the observed inverse correlation between sulfur and iron speciation.

Having excluded the significant impact of S degassing on fO2 of the Samalas trachydacitic system, we next investigate how, in turn, S degassing can be affected by fO2. Previous experiments on intermediate to silicic magmas have shown that S degassing efficiency strongly depends on fO2, S speciation in the melt, and melt composition34,51–54. In essence, these results underscore the critical influence of sulfur speciation on degassing efficiency in large magma reservoirs capable of driving explosive, climate-impacting eruptions.

In oxidized magmas where sulfur exists predominantly as S6+, pre-eruptive degassing can only be moderately effective in extracting sulfur because of the low vapor–melt partition coefficient for sulfur. This scenario may apply to the 1815 Tambora eruption, for which titanomagnetite in matrix glasses indicates an fO2 of FMQ + 1.8 to +2.2 (ref. 58), ~1 log unit higher than that inferred from Samalas matrix glasses. This elevated oxidation state may help explain Tambora’s overall lower estimated SO2 release (~34–147 Tg59,60), despite a similar eruptive volume. We note, however, that if the upper end of the estimated SO2 release59 for Tambora is correct, then—given the comparable erupted volumes and the relatively homogeneous compositions through the whole eruptions (with whole-rock SiO2 varying only ~1–2 wt.% for Tambora61)—the difference between the two eruptions becomes less pronounced. Tighter constraints on magmatic fO2 and further refinement of SO2 emission estimates for the Tambora eruption may be needed for a more robust comparison.

Under reduced conditions where sulfur is present only as S2−, most sulfur in the magma partitions to sulfide melt or cumulate minerals due to the low SCSS in felsic magma57. In such cases, only about one-third of the total sulfur is available to degas into the pre-eruptive vapor. The “Millennium Eruption” of Changbaishan (Paektu) volcano33 presents a case in which reducing conditions and sulfide sequestration may have caused very low S release.

On the other hand, in intermediate scenarios—such as in the parental Samalas magma, which contained both S6+ and S2− (~40% S2−)—even moderate accumulations of pre-eruptive vapor (e.g., ~2.5 wt.% of the magma) can efficiently extract massive quantities of S, ultimately enabling large excess S degassing during eruption. Our model results emphasize the importance of tracking changes in S redox state throughout magma differentiation to better assess the SO2 degassing potential of magmatic systems. Though the discussion above demonstrates the importance of S speciation in controlling the efficiency of pre-eruptive S degassing, several compositional features of the 1257 samples are difficult to reconcile with a crystallization-only scenario. For instance, compatible element (e.g., MgO, Al2O3 and FeO) concentrations in melt inclusions are lower than predicted by isobaric crystallization models using rhyolite-MELTS62 (Fig. S1), although this may reflect the significant role of amphibole at Samalas for which rhyolite-MELTS is not calibrated. On the other hand, significant Th enrichment in melt inclusions implies 54% in-situ crystallization of the 1257 magma reservoir, which is inconsistent with the reported crystallinity28 (6–17%) in pumices. Fractional crystallization and storage of crystals in an unerupted mush could potentially account for this mismatch between expected and observed crystallinities. Most importantly, however, the 1257 matrix glass shows a somewhat variable composition in both major and volatile elements, with an average falling within the range of melt inclusions, rather than representing the most evolved endmember (Figs. 1 and 2). All these discrepancies call for an important role for magma recharge and mixing28,63. Inspection of plagioclase phenocrysts reveals systematic, progressive reverse zoning from ~An45 to ~An55 in the outermost ~300 µm of thick overgrowth rims (Fig. 5), strongly suggesting protracted compositional and/or thermal rejuvenation of the system prior to the 1257 eruption32. Such rejuvenation—assuming a recharging magma compositionally similar to the least evolved melt inclusions, with ~4 wt.% H2O and a S2−/∑S ratio of ~0.5—would have increased the amount of pre-eruptive vapor and resulted in a higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{kd}}_{\sum S}^{{vapor}/{melt}}$$\end{document}kd∑Svapor/melt, thereby enhancing S extraction into vapor phase.

When the system is fluid saturated (Mfluid >0), due to the large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{kd}}_{\sum S}^{{vapor}/{melt}}$$\end{document}kd∑Svapor/melt(fixed at 90, average of the high-MgO and low-MgO \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{kd}}_{\sum S}^{{vapor}/{melt}}$$\end{document}kd∑Svapor/melt), S behaves as a compatible element and its concentration in the melt first decreases, then reaches a steady state. However, if the fluid proportion is much smaller than that of the crystals, or the system is fluid-undersaturated (shown as the case of recharge/crystallization ratio of 2), sulfur eventually becomes incompatible. Comparison between the modeled results and our matrix glass composition shows that the model can only reproduce all the investigated elements with recharge/crystallization ratios of 1–1.2 and numbers of magma reservoir overturns of 1–1.5. These scenarios lead to the accumulation of ~3.8–5.2 wt.% fluid in the magma reservoir (Fig. 7e), with S concentrations of ~2–2.2 wt.% in the fluid (Fig. 7f). Since 5–6 wt.% fluid is thought to represent a threshold for overpressure-induced eruption triggering66–68, this level of volatile accumulation could plausibly have contributed to initiating the 1257 eruption. Scaling to the mass of erupted magma once again—this time using 3.8–5.2 wt.% fluid with ~2.0–2.2 wt.% S —yields a buildup of 78–91 Tg S in pre-eruptive vapor that could have been released to the atmosphere upon eruption. Again, the modeled mass fraction of fluid is notably sensitive to assumptions on initial H2O contents. For instance, increasing initial H2O from 4.0 to 4.3 wt.% allows similar combinations of recharge/crystallization ratios and magma reservoir overturns—still satisfying all geochemical constraints— to yield up to 80–110 Tg S upon eruption.

Therefore, simultaneous magma recharge and crystallization at similar rates can explain all observed geochemical features of the 1257 Samalas eruption products (Fig. S5). Particularly, our model allows enrichment of highly incompatible elements (e.g., Th) in the melt without over-depleting the compatible elements (e.g., Al2O3, MgO, CaO)63, in contrast to a crystallization-only scenario (Fig. S1). Our analysis indicates that lower recharge/crystallization ratios, implying either lower recharge rates and/or more efficient cooling, require fewer overturns to reproduce the plausible compositional range of the 1257 magma, and result in lower pre-eruptive fluid accumulation. Across all modeled scenarios, however, the total magma reservoir mass either remains constant or decreases slightly over time, suggesting that large amounts of unerupted cumulates were generated and stored—equivalent to 1–1.5 times of the original magma reservoir mass.

In terms of degassing and S release, the sulfur yield estimates of 78–91 Tg resulting from the interplay between recharge and crystallization could be even higher if a more H2O-rich (>4 wt.%) magma had been emplaced at low pressure. Conversely, the total sulfur released upon eruption may have been lower if some vapor had escaped prior to the 1257 eruption. Nonetheless, our model results demonstrate that the combined effects of magma recharge and crystallization generate more than twice the amount of pre-eruptive vapor compared to crystallization alone. This enhanced vapor accumulation not only has the potential to trigger explosive eruption but also represents a key mechanism for massive S release into the atmosphere.

In this study, we present measurements of sulfur and iron speciation, S isotopes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta }^{34}S$$\end{document}δ34S), and major and volatile elements in plagioclase-hosted melt inclusions and matrix glasses from the 1257 Samalas eruption. Based on these data and modeling, we identify two critical factors that favored prodigious excess sulfur emissions at Samalas and that are likely to control the buildup of eruptible sulfur in the lead up to other climate-impacting eruptions. First, we show that redox conditions strongly influence the proportion of available sulfur that transfers from the melt to the pre-eruptive vapor phase. S speciation analysis and experimentally derived S partition coefficients under different redox conditions allow us to compare the S degassing efficiencies in magmas dominated by S2−, S6+ and a mixture of both sulfur species. We demonstrate that parental magmas with both S6+ and S2− yield high S partition coefficients (compared to the S6+ dominant scenario) without sulfide precipitation (as opposed to the S2− dominant scenario) and, therefore, have the highest S degassing efficiency. Second, we outline how magma recharge, which is likely at the origin of several compositional features of the 1257 eruptive products, may enhance the buildup of eruptible sulfur in large magma reservoirs. Model calculations show that concomitant magma recharge and crystallization in the magma reservoir yield more than twice the amount of pre-eruptive vapor and, thus, sequestrate more S, compared to crystallization alone. Magma recharge and pressure buildup due to the accumulation of >5 wt.% pre-eruptive vapor may have also eventually destabilized the system, triggering the VEI 7 eruption.

Methods

Samples

The samples analyzed in this study were collected during fieldwork in 2013 by J.-C. Komorowski and C.-M. Vidal. Details regarding permissions and collaboration are provided in Vidal et al. (2015).

Unlike the deposits of some large explosive eruptions characterized by pronounced compositional zoning (e.g., the transition from rhyodacite to andesite in the 6845 ± 50 BP climactic eruption of Mount Mazama69), the products of the 1257 Samalas eruption appear remarkedly homogeneous32. Indeed, previous work has shown that bulk pumice compositions remain consistent throughout the eruptive sequence comprising four phases (P1–P4)32 and that matrix glasses from each phase exhibit similarly low S concentrations16. Although P3 and P4 have different pumice textures (with more microlites that grew during magma ascent and degassing32) from P1, melt inclusions from P1 and P3 show comparable compositions16. Therefore, based on available data, we select pumice lapilli (RIN1307A, previously collected and reported by ref. 16) from unit P1 (accounting for ~1/8 of the total erupted volume) at the type stratigraphic section, as a representative sample of the 1257 eruption16,28,32. We note, however, that a stratified Samalas magma reservoir cannot be excluded, and further in-depth analyses of pyroclastic density current deposits are needed to fully test the compositional homogeneity of the 1257 products.

The 1257 trachydacite pumice contains 7–16 wt.% phenocrysts, including plagioclase displaying different zoning patterns, amphibole, orthopyroxene, clinopyroxene, magnetite, iron sulfide and apatite16,28. Though plagioclase antecrysts are not uncommon in the pre-caldera and post-caldera products of Samalas system, previous calculations on Mg partitioning between plagioclase and melt28 demonstrate that plagioclase in the 1257 products are unlikely to be antecrysts. In addition, previous study16 shows that plagioclase-hosted melt inclusions encompass the full compositional range of the 1257 Samalas magma, while amphibole- and pyroxene-hosted melt inclusions record more evolved trachydacite compositions approaching that of the matrix glass (~68 wt.% SiO2). Therefore, we focus on plagioclase-hosted melt inclusions, 40–100 µm in size, and matrix glass adhered to phenocrysts in RIN1307A. All investigated melt inclusions are glassy. Most melt inclusions include vapor bubbles occupying ~<1–8% of the inclusion’s volume (Supplementary Data 1).

Analytical methods

Sulfur and Fe X-ray absorption near-edge structure spectroscopy

S-XANES at the S K-edge and Fe-XANES at the Fe K-edge of the melt inclusions and matrix glasses were conducted at the GSECARS 13-ID-E beamline of the Advanced Photon Source (APS), Argonne National Laboratory, USA, following the methods established by previous studies70–74. We conducted S-XANES measurements in all the samples before other analytical procedures to avoid potential beam damage due to exposure to the electron/ion beam during EPMA and SIMS. A high spatial resolution micro-focused 2 × 1 μm (μ-XANES) beam at beamline 13-ID-D allows in-situ S-XANES measurements in small melt inclusions (~20 µm). The energy of the Si (111) channel-cut monochromator was calibrated to the 2481.8 ( ± 0.2) eV white line of the spectrum for clear double-sided adhesive tape. In the measurements of our samples and standards, the energy range of 2437 to 2550 eV was scanned with a step size of 0.2 eV at the S K-edge (2467 to 2487 eV) and 1 eV for the pre- and post-edge regions. A 2-s scan duration per energy step was used to achieve higher S X-ray counts required for high-quality spectra, especially in low-S matrix glasses (e.g., <200 ppm S). Beam damage estimate73 and the procedure to quantify the S6+/∑S ratio are available in the supplementary materials.

Fe-XANES measurements were performed in fluorescence mode with a cryogenically cooled double-crystal Si (311) monochromator and a Vortex ME4 silicon-drift diode detector array coupled to a high-speed digital spectrometer system (Quantum Xpress3). Samples were positioned so that the normal to the sample surface was 45 degrees to the incident X-ray beam. Spectra were collected from 7050 to 7105 eV in 2.5 eV steps with 0.75 s dwell time; from 7105 to 7120 eV (pre-edge region) in 0.1 eV steps with 0.75 s dwell time and from 7120 to 7346 eV (XAFS region) in 2 eV steps with 0.75 s dwell time. Seven rhyolitic standards and 14 basaltic standards with pre-determined Fe3+/∑Fe ratios from the Smithsonian Museum72,75 were measured and processed to serve as the internal calibration. The Fe3+/ΣFe ratios of the unknown Samalas samples were calculated using the calibration derived from combined rhyolitic and basaltic standards (Fig. S2). More details on monitoring beam damage, Fe3+/ΣFe calibration and potential Fe3+ contamination assessment are available in the supplementary materials.

Electron probe micro-analysis

Major and volatile (Cl, F, S) elements in melt inclusions and matrix glasses were analyzed with a CAMECA SX-100 electron microprobe at the American Museum of Natural History in New York City. An acceleration voltage of 15 kV and a beam diameter of 15 μm were used. Na, K, Fe and F were analyzed with a beam current of 2 nA to avoid Na loss; the rest of the major elements, S and Cl, were analyzed with a beam current of 10 nA. S and Cl were counted for 240 s to lower the detection limits. The S peak was calibrated using barite. S-bearing secondary standards, NBS 620, NBS 621 (synthetic soda lime glasses with NIST standard values) and a synthetic alkali basalt glass AB36CIA-176 were measured repeatedly to check the accuracy of S measurements during our sessions. S concentration for these three secondary standards from our analyses are 1099 ± 60 ppm, 505 ± 20 ppm and 159 ± 63 ppm respectively, consistent with the published values (112077, 520, and 163 ppm). Cl concentration for AB36CIA-1 from our analyses is 3644 ± 75 ppm, also in good agreement with the published value of 3690 ppm76. Accuracy and precision of the major element analyses were also checked against secondary standards VG-2 (a natural MORB glass from Smithsonian78) and PCD, a synthetic rhyolitic glass79, measured at the beginning of the analytical session.

Plagioclase was analyzed using an accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a focused beam at a current of 20 nA. For crystal hosting melt inclusions, we acquired two points next to the melt inclusions and two points in each major zone (core, mantle and rim) of the crystals, when obvious zoning was present.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Backscattered electron images of plagioclase crystals were acquired on a Hitachi S-2600N SEM equipped with a Bruker Quantax 400 energy-dispersive X-ray (EDS) detector at Queens College, City University of New York.

Major element contents (Si, Al, Fe, Ti, Mg, and Mn) were measured from 12 titanomagnetites that were adjacent to glass within the 1257 pumice sample using a ThermoFisher EDS detector mounted on a JEOL JSM-IT500HR field-emission scanning electron microscope at Southern Methodist University. All data and standardization routines were processed in the ThermoFisher Pathfinder software. Given the consistency in the major element compositions—with all measured oxides varying by less than 1 wt.% across all data points—only one or two analyses were performed per titanomagnetite grain. The compositions from grains with multiple analyses were subsequently averaged. Additionally, major element contents of the adjacent glass (Si, Al, K, Ca, Ti, Mn, Fe, Na, and Mg) were obtained to represent the composition of the melt. The analytical potential was set to an accelerating voltage of 15–20 kV, with a beam current of 2.73 nA. To measure the titanomagnetites, the instrument was calibrated to the following standards: pure magnetite (for Fe), pure rutile (for Ti), quartz (for Si), and the Chicago ilmenite (for Mn, Mg, and Al). For the adjoining glass, the instrument was calibrated to the following standards: the 1959 Kīlauea glass (for Fe, Ti, Mg, Mn, and Ca) and VG568 (for Si, Al, K, and Na)80. The compositions of measured titanomagnetites were then paired with their host matrix glass compositions. To ensure equilibrium between the titanomagnetite-melt pairs, we calculated a Mn-Mg exchange coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{{Mn}-{Mg}}^{{mgt}/{melt}}$$\end{document}DMn−Mgmgt/melt) for each pair. Pairs in disequilibrium are easily identifiable with this method since the partition coefficients for Mn and Mg similarly rely on melt composition, but their absolute concentrations will drastically vary with magmatic differentiation39. All titanomagnetite-glass pairs gave \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{{Mn}-{Mg}}^{{mgt}/{melt}}$$\end{document}DMn−Mgmgt/melt values between 1 and 4, which fulfill the criteria for equilibrium.

Secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS)

Sulfur isotope (δ³⁴S) measurements of melt inclusions and matrix glasses were conducted using the Cameca IMS 1280 SIMS at the Northeast National Ion Microprobe Facility (NENIMF) at Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution. Prior to analysis, carbon coats were removed, and samples were pressed into indium mounts, which were then gold-coated. The analyses use a 10 μm Cs+ primary beam, 10 kV accelerating voltage, and 1–1.5 nA beam current74. To remove surface contaminants, samples were pre-sputtered for 180 seconds using a 25 × 25 μm raster area, followed by analysis using a smaller 20 × 20 μm raster area to minimize surface contamination. Secondary ions of ³⁴S and ³²S were separated with a mass resolution of ~5000 and counted in mono-collection mode using a pulse-counting ETP electron multiplier for each mass. Each SIMS analysis included 40 measurement cycles over 15 minutes, with ³²S measured for 3 seconds and ³⁴S for 8 seconds per cycle. Measurements were corrected for dead-time and time-interpolated to account for non-simultaneous measurements of 34S and 32S within each cycle in mono-collector mode. Instrumental mass fractionation and beam stability were monitored by repeated measurements of the STAP standard, a subglacial basalt from Iceland with ~530 ppm S and δ³⁴S of −1.10 ± 0.66 ‰81.

Sulfur background was minimized by applying 1000 × 1000 µm secondary field aperture, blocking transmission of magnified secondary ions from outside of the innermost 12.5 × 12.5 µm of the sputtering sample crater. Background sulfur levels were monitored by measuring a sulfur-free Suprasil quartz glass among sample measurements, and these data were used to apply corrections to the data. These corrections were minor in most cases, averaging at 0.08‰ absolute and with a total range of −0.83 to −0.54‰. Reported uncertainty is propagated from the standard error (1SE) of each measurement and the standard deviation of the mass fractionation factor based on STAP repeat measurements. The ³²S ion yields ranged from 3–80×10⁴, depending on sulfur concentration in the glasses. Analyses of Samalas glasses generally had δ³⁴S uncertainties of ± 0.7–1.9‰ (± 1SE) due to their relatively low S contents. A few analyses with larger uncertainties were discarded from further analysis.

Redox budget (RB) calculations

RB is defined as the moles of electrons that need to be added to or given off by the system to reach a given reference state83. In a closed system, during the initial and final stages of degassing, electrons are given or received by different elements in different phases, while the RB of the system remains the same. Based on the degassing framework described above, we assigned the initial stage when MgO = 1.3 wt.%, and the final stage, when MgO = 0.55 wt.%, the lowest measured value in melt inclusions. At the initial stage, the system starts with melt only (100 g) while at the final stage, the system has 50% (50 g) melt, 48% (48 g) crystals, and 2% fluid (2 g). 0.1 wt.% FeS (0.048 g) is assumed in the crystals16. We assume that S and Fe are the only two elements with redox capacity and take S6+ and Fe3+ as the reference states. RB of the Samalas system at the initial state is calculated using the initial S (870 ppm) and highest FeO (4 wt.%) concentrations, along with the lowest S6+/ΣS (0.42) and highest Fe3+/ΣFe (0.52) measured in the melt inclusions. At the final state after crystallization and degassing, RB is calculated accounting for S from both melt (70 ppm, lowest measured in melt inclusions) and vapor, and FeO in the melt (1.8 wt.%, lowest measured in melt inclusions) and crystals. S concentration in the vapor at the final state, ~1.6 wt.%, is calculated using sulfur mass balance, assuming 0.1 wt.% FeS in the crystals16. At the average fO2 derived from magnetite–melt equilibrium (~ FMQ + 0.8 at 950 °C) representing a late-stage fO2, this ~1.6 wt.% sulfur consists of 80% H2S and 20% SO2 in the final vapor. RB calculations are presented in Supplementary Data 4.

With given magma crystallization, recharge, evacuation and crustal assimilation rates, the concentration change of any element can be solved numerically using Eqs. 8 and 9.

In our model, the parental magma is assumed to contain 65.8 wt.% SiO2, 16.5 wt.% Al2O3, 1.3 wt.% MgO, 1.75 wt.% Fe2O3, 8 ppm Th, 4 wt.% H2O and 500 ppm S, approaching the composition of the least evolved melt inclusion from the 1257 eruption (pl5mi from ref. 16) within EPMA uncertainties. Though the recharging magma composition is not specifically constrained, it is reasonable to assume that the magma reservoir is recharged with the same magma as the parental magma based on the maxima of ~An55 in contact with matrix glass from reversed zoned plagioclase. Since there are no stratigraphy records showing any trachydacitic magma evacuation between the 2500 BP and 1257 eruptions28, and no reports on crustal contamination of the 1257 Samalas magmas, we only consider the fractional crystallization and recharge. Therefore, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{d{M}_{{cc}}}{{dt}}$$\end{document}dMccdt and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{d{M}_{e}}{{dt}}$$\end{document}dMedt are 0. Using the original REFC model63, we simulate the evolution of Al2O3, MgO, CaO and Th. With the same recharge/fractional crystallization rates ratio, the main controlling factors for compatible elements like Al2O3, MgO and CaO and highly incompatible element like Th are their bulk partition coefficients. Here we used DAl2O3 = 1.1, DMgO = 2.2, DCaO = 2 (averaged from MELTS modeling results, Supplementary Data 5), DTh = 0.002.

For H2O and S, we modified ref. 63’s approach in modeling sulfide saturation and chalcophile elements. We treat the vapor phase as part of the cumulates, analogous to sulfide in ref. 63. H2O is handled like S in ref. 63, with its concentration buffered by solubility and fixed at 4 wt.% (as in the fractional crystallization model above). Sulfuris treated similarly to chalcophile elements in ref. 63, its behavior is controlled by the vapor saturation and bulk partition coefficient. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{S}^{{bulk}}$$\end{document}DSbulk is calculated by the fraction of vapor in the vapor+cumulates and the partition coefficient of sulfur between vapor and silicate melt. In our model, we use the average sulfur partition coefficient between vapor and silicate melt (~90) of high- and low-MgO melts. Varying the partition coefficients of S, MgO, CaO, Al2O3 and Th within the reasonable range changes the absolute values of each element; however, it does not change their behavior patterns as recharging goes on. The python code adapted from ref. 63 to calculate the recharge, crystallization and degassing process is provided on Github (https://github.com/sdecho/REFC_modified).
