Hyperprolactinemia as a manifestation of multiple sclerosis attack
Authors: Sahand Golparvar, Saba Naghavi, Iman Adibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Multiple Sclerosis is a chronic inflamatory disorder. The prevalence of hyperprolactinemia in patient with MS is 6.7 %. Method: Case report. Result: This case report describes a 31-year-old woman with multiple sclerosis (MS) who presented with galactorrhea, a rare symptom of MS, along with other neurological manifestations. She had elevated serum prolactin levels and a normal-sized pituitary gland with no evidence of adenoma or lesions. The elevated serum prolactin levels were normalized after treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone. No dopaminergic drugs were used to treat her galactorrhea, which also resolved after receiving intravenous methylprednisolone. Conclusion: This case illustrates the possible association between MS and hyperprolactinaemia and if the etiology is MS there is no need for treatment of hyperprolactinemia.

Introduction

Prolactin is a hormone responsible for lactation and other actions needed to maintain homeostasis. The anterior pituitary synthesises and secretes prolactin, which is regulated by dopamine-mediated hypothalamic control. However, the central nervous system, immune system, uterus and mammary glands are also capable of producing prolactin. Inappropriately elevated levels can lead to galactorrhea in non-breastfeeding females or males, which can be caused by different diseases and requires different treatments.1

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory disorder involving the central nervous system. Some of the symptoms of MS include loss of balance, numbness, double vision, muscle spasms, depression and, rarely, galactorrhea. The prevalence of hyperprolactinemia in patients with MS is 6.7%.2 Serum prolactin levels are elevated in relapsing-remitting patients with MS during the course of the illness, with half of them showing a rise in the acute stage of relapse and a decrease during the recovery and remission phases.3 4 The reason behind high prolactin levels in these patients is unclear; however, observations suggest it may be part of hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysregulation due to neurodegeneration or demyelination.5 It is not recommended to treat galactorrhea in patients with MS with dopaminergic drugs; by treating the acute relapse of MS, hyperprolactinemia and galactorrhea are typically resolved.

In this case report, we present a 31-year-old woman with left-sided paresthesia, urinary retention and bilateral galactorrhea due to a new relapse of MS. Hyperprolactinemia and galactorrhea were resolved with intravenous methylprednisolone.

Case presentation

A 31-year-old married woman with three children was referred to the Kashani clinic in Isfahan, Iran, for left-sided paresthesia, urinary retention, optic neuritis and galactorrhea, which had been present for 1 week. The patient was sexually active and had regular menses, with menarche at age 11. She had a previous medical history of bipolar disorder and was under treatment with sertraline and trifluoperazine.

On physical examination, the patient was found to have bilateral nipple discharge on stimulation. At the time of referral, her prolactin level was 70.39 ng/mL (reference range: <20 ng/mL). Thyroid-stimulating hormone(TSH) was normal at 3.79 μIU/mL (reference range: 0.4–4 μIU/mL), and the pregnancy test was negative. MRI showed multiple small bilateral white matter hyperintense lesions, including in the periventricular area, subcortical and juxtacortical regions and cerebellum, which were compatible with demyelinating disease (figure 1). The pituitary gland was normal in size, with no evidence of adenoma. Based on the 2017 revised McDonald criteria, the patient was diagnosed with relapsing-remitting MS.6

A high dose of intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg/day) was started for 3 days, which resulted in relief of all her symptoms. The serum prolactin level declined to 26.3 ng/mL (reference range: <20 ng/mL), which is within the normal range. Notably, no dopaminergic agents were used to treat her galactorrhea, which improved considerably. The patient underwent follow-up and maintenance treatment with dimethyl fumarate, a disease-modifying therapy for relapsing MS. During the 6-month follow-up period, the patient did not have a recurrence of galactorrhea.

Discussion

In this case report, a new patient with MS was referred to our clinic with galactorrhea and hyperprolactinemia, which were cured with methylprednisolone. It is probable that demyelination of neurons in the pituitary gland and hypothalamus caused the surge in prolactin.

Prolactin contributes to hundreds of physiological functions, but its two primary responsibilities are milk production and the development of mammary glands within breast tissue. Causes of prolactin excess include the loss of dopamine inhibition under the influence of antipsychotic drugs or destruction of the hypothalamus/hypophyseal tract.1

In other studies, pituitary adenoma has been shown to raise prolactin levels or even trigger an MS attack, but in this case, no adenoma was found on MRI.7 8 Drugs, particularly antipsychotics, can also increase prolactin levels.9 In this case, the patient was taking trifluoperazine, an antipsychotic drug, but her prolactin level decreased after treating the MS relapse without changing her antipsychotic medication.

Currently, prolactin is believed to have a dual effect on the central nervous system. It may support system repair by providing regenerative signals for neurons, oligodendrocytes and adult neural stem/progenitor cells, while also stimulating peripheral immune cells, which might promote unusual immune responses and negatively impact the disease.10 11

During MS relapse, serum prolactin levels are elevated, but they decrease in the recovery stage, indicating that MS exacerbation may contribute to elevated prolactin levels. Similar to our case, a recent report12 found that after treatment of an MS relapse, serum prolactin levels returned to the normal range. A similar observation was reported in a cohort study involving 255 patients with MS, where approximately 38% exhibited mild elevations in serum prolactin without evidence of pituitary pathology, supporting a potential association between MS activity and prolactin imbalance.13

In this case report, after receiving intravenous methylprednisolone during the acute stage of MS, serum prolactin declined to within the normal range, and there was no need for further treatment of the elevated prolactin. The patient’s antipsychotic medication (trifluoperazine) was maintained throughout the course of treatment. Despite the known potential of this medication to elevate prolactin levels, serum prolactin decreased from 70.3 ng/mL prior to treatment to 26.3 ng/mL following intravenous methylprednisolone. A follow-up measurement 1 month later showed a stable level of 22.7 ng/mL. The sustained reduction in prolactin despite ongoing antipsychotic therapy suggests that the hyperprolactinemia was primarily related to MS-associated neuroinflammation rather than medication effects, and that treatment of the relapse effectively normalized prolactin levels.

Overall, when a patient presents with hyperprolactinemia and galactorrhea, it can have several etiologies. If the etiology is MS, there is no need for specific treatment of hyperprolactinemia, as treating the MS relapse will normalize prolactin levels.
