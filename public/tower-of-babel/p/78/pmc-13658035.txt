Oil crop supply chains drive rising global biodiversity loss and outsource impacts to the tropics
Authors: Shuntian Wang, Livia Cabernard, Martin Bruckner, Maulana Permana Ajie, Stephan Pfister
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oil crop expansion threatens global biodiversity, yet its supply chain impacts remain poorly quantified. Here we analyse biodiversity impacts along oil crop supply chains from 1995 to 2020 using spatially explicit assessments, a hybridized multi-regional input–output framework, enhanced supply chain mapping and structural decomposition analysis. By 2020, oil crop cultivation caused the potential long-term loss of 1.5% of global species (plants and terrestrial vertebrates), with oil palm, coconuts and soybeans responsible for three-quarters of this effect. Tropical regions, occupying just under half of harvested area, bear almost four-fifths of impacts. More than half of this loss was outsourced through international trade, as major importers displace biodiversity burdens to tropical regions. Biodiversity impacts increased by four-fifths since 1995, statistically attributed primarily to rising per capita consumption. Our findings identify production hotspots and consumption-side contributions, supporting targeted interventions to mitigate biodiversity impacts along oil crop supply chains.

Main

Biodiversity loss is one of the most pressing global challenges, threatening ecosystem services, food security and climate resilience and is largely driven by agricultural land use (that is, land occupation, land transformation and intensification). The global oil crop cultivation is a major contributor to ongoing land-use changes and has thus emerged as a critical driver of biodiversity loss. For instance, oil palm cultivation replacing natural habitats in Southeast Asia causes substantial deforestation and species extinction1–3. By 2020, oil crops occupied approximately 330 million hectares, representing 22% of the world’s cropland, and their total area had increased by approximately 58% since 19954. Oil crop cultivation is expected to continue expanding in response to growing global demand for vegetable oils, biofuels and other by-products used in the food and non-food industries5,6. Although numerous studies have assessed the region-specific biodiversity impacts of individual oil crops such as oil palm2,5,7 and soybeans8,9, these assessments lack a comprehensive global perspective across all oil crops and their full supply chains.

Due to the globalization of supply chains, biodiversity impacts caused by oil crop cultivation in production regions can be driven by demand from other parts of the world10–12. For example, palm oil consumption in the European Union (EU) has led to extensive deforestation in Indonesia, resulting in a substantial increase in local species loss13. To trace and quantify these outsourced environmental impacts across the global economy, multi-region input–output (MRIO) analysis is commonly employed14–17. However, existing MRIO frameworks face substantial challenges when it comes to accurately capturing the biodiversity impacts of specific oil crops. These challenges stem from coarse sector and regional classifications, incomplete global economic coverage18–20 and a lack of detailed, region-specific data on the biodiversity impacts of various crops. Moreover, classic MRIO analysis typically focuses on the direct links between production and consumption, overlooking the intermediate stages within the supply chain18. As a result, although classic MRIO analysis can identify the final consumer countries and products such as cosmetics that ultimately drive oil palm cultivation, it fails to provide detailed insights into how biodiversity impacts are transferred at each stage, from oil palm cultivation and palm oil refining to the final consumer goods.

Here we address the above knowledge gaps by (1) quantifying spatially explicit biodiversity impacts at grid cell level (0.05°) to identify hotspot areas (grid cells with high impact) and critical regions (countries or regions with disproportionately high contributions to biodiversity impacts) linked to oil crop cultivation, (2) tracking biodiversity impacts along the full supply chain from production to consumption (including intermediate processing steps) and (3) decomposing the socio-economic contributors of biodiversity impacts over time. First, we synthesized the global spatial distribution of oil crops in 2020 through the integration of multiple datasets, including Food and Agriculture Organization Corporate Statistical Database (FAOSTAT)4, Spatial Production Allocation Model (SPAM)21, CROPGRIDS22 and remote-sensing-based global crop distribution maps23,24. Crop-specific land-use intensity (LUI) was calculated using global nitrogen and phosphorus application datasets25,26 and irrigation datasets where applicable27. Second, we combined the spatial distribution and LUI of oil crops with global species loss factors (LFs) to calculate potential global species loss (PSLglo). We use PSLglo as a key indicator of biodiversity impacts because it is a robust, globally comparable metric recommended by the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) that directly quantifies potential global species extinctions caused by land use28,29. Third, we hybridized the food and agriculture biomass input–output model (FABIO)20, which has detailed classifications of oil crops and their by-products, with Resolved EXIOBASE3 (REX3) MRIO19, which provides detailed classifications of remaining non-food final uses. This hybridized MRIO framework enables comprehensive analysis by providing both detailed sector classification and extensive economic coverage across the global oil crop supply chains. Finally, we linked PSLglo to the hybridized MRIO, applying enhanced supply chain mapping15,18,30,31 and structural decomposition analysis (SDA)32,33 to systematically track the biodiversity impacts and its socio-economic contributors along oil crop supply chains. Our findings identify hotspot areas and critical regions and reveal the transfer mechanisms of biodiversity loss along global oil crop supply chains, providing insights to guide targeted cross-border policy coordination and responsible sourcing strategies.

Oil crop cultivation drives substantial biodiversity impacts, reaching a PSLglo of about 1.52% in 2020, indicating that the associated land use has generated a global extinction risk equivalent to the potential loss of 1.52% of global terrestrial species (plants and terrestrial vertebrates). However, these impacts are highly concentrated: tropical regions (23.5° S–23.5° N), which represent only 43% of the harvested area, account for a disproportionate 78% of the total biodiversity impacts (Fig. 1c,d). Among individual crops, oil palm occupies about 9% of the harvested area yet contributes approximately 34% of total PSLglo, whereas coconuts, covering less than 4% area, account for about 23% PSLglo. By contrast, soybeans, despite claiming 39% of the harvested area, account for only 18% of PSLglo. The disparity also exists within the same oil crops cultivated in different regions. For example, while the USA accounts for 26% of the world’s soybean harvested area, it contributes only 7% of the biodiversity impacts caused by soybean cultivation, whereas Brazil, with 29% share of harvested area, is responsible for a disproportionate 60% of the associated biodiversity impacts. Figure 1e identifies hotspot areas, defined as grid cells with high biodiversity impact (>10−6% PSLglo), corresponding to approximately the 90th percentile (top 10%) of impacted grid cells. These hotspots reveal critical regions with disproportionately high contributions to global species loss, including oil palm and coconut cultivation in Southeast Asia (notably Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines), soybean production in South America (especially Brazil), olive cultivation in Mediterranean countries (for example, Spain, Italy, Tunisia) and rapeseed cultivation in China.

Figure 2f–j illustrates that global biodiversity impacts associated with oil crop cultivation increased by approximately 80% (0.67% PSLglo) from 1995 to 2020 (relative to the 1995 baseline, covering most regions with traceable supply chains for food and non-food final uses). According to the planetary boundaries (PB) framework, biosphere integrity has been identified as a core PB that is already transgressed globally34–36. Recent studies have proposed using the annual change rate of PSLglo as an alternative indicator for tracking biosphere integrity PB, with a suggested threshold of 0.001% PSLglo per year (refs. 37–39). Our findings show that oil crop cultivation alone contributed an annual species loss rate that strongly surpassed this threshold, indicating a substantial contribution of oil crop expansion to the transgression of the biosphere integrity PB. This PSLglo increase was predominantly driven by rising impacts from oil palm and soybeans. Oil palm alone accounted for 60% of the total increase in biodiversity impacts, with Latin America, Indonesia and other regions contributing 25%, 18% and 17%, respectively. Soybean cultivation contributed an additional 25%, primarily due to expansion in Brazil (21%). From the production regions perspective, rapid biodiversity impact increases occurred predominantly in tropical regions such as Southeast Asia and Latin America, whereas impacts in temperate regions (for example, USA, EU and China) showed minimal growth (<4%). From the consumption regions perspective, developing economies drove most of the global increase: China’s biodiversity impacts rose approximately 3.3-fold, Africa’s increased by 2.3-fold and Latin America’s by 2.3-fold (all relative to 1995). Notably, China’s growth was largely driven by imports, whereas Africa’s increase predominantly resulted from expanding domestic production.

From the intermediate product perspective, increased demand for vegetable oils contributed the most, accounting for over 64% of the overall growth in PSLglo. Palm oil alone represented approximately 51% of this increase. Coconut oil was the only vegetable oil category showing a decline in biodiversity impacts; however, this reduction was offset by substantial growth from other vegetable oils. Among non-vegetable oil intermediate products, soybean cake made the largest contribution to the total increase (approximately 14%), followed by coconut fruits (10%).

From a final-use perspective, global biodiversity impacts associated with food consumption rose by approximately 77% between 1995 and 2020, whereas those associated with non-food consumption nearly doubled (+89%). Growth was most pronounced in China, where food-related impacts increased by 196% and non-food-related impacts increased by 438%. In contrast, the EU showed a different pattern: food-related impacts rose only marginally (+6%), whereas non-food impacts more than doubled (+115%), largely fuelled by increased consumption of bio-based materials. Whereas the EU’s shift towards renewable bio-based energy and materials has contributed to reducing carbon emissions, it has simultaneously intensified biodiversity loss by driving increased demand for oil crops cultivated in biodiversity-rich tropical regions.

Outsourced biodiversity impacts along the global oil crop supply chains

International trade played a critical role in displacing biodiversity impacts from production to consumption regions along global oil crop supply chains. In 2020, the outsourced share of total impacts reached 54% (Fig. 2b,c). Outsourcing shares differed substantially across food and non-food consumption: biodiversity impacts associated with food final uses showed an outsourced share of 42% (Fig. 3b,c), whereas impacts related to non-food final uses reached as high as 79% (Fig. 4b,c). Three major importing regions exhibited notably high proportions of outsourcing: China outsourced 77% of its consumption-based impacts, the EU outsourced 79% and North America outsourced 86%. Collectively, these three regions contributed nearly 60% to global outsourced biodiversity impacts, with China and the EU each accounting for 24% and North America for 12%.

The outsourced biodiversity impacts fell disproportionately upon biodiversity-rich tropical production regions. In Southeast Asia, Indonesia (21%) and the Philippines (13%) bore substantial portions of these outsourced impacts, while in Latin America, Brazil alone accounted for 20% of global outsourced biodiversity impacts. In total, these tropical regions absorbed approximately 70% of globally outsourced biodiversity impacts. Specifically, China’s outsourced biodiversity impacts were primarily driven by soybean imports from Brazil, whereas the EU’s outsourced impacts largely stemmed from tropical oil crops, particularly palm oil imported from Southeast Asian countries such as Indonesia. Consequently, international trade has facilitated a substantial transfer of biodiversity impacts from temperate consumption regions to tropical production regions, thereby increasing ecological pressures in these vulnerable regions.

Between 1995 and 2020, biodiversity impacts outsourced via international trade increased approximately 2.4-fold (a 133% increase), accounting for roughly two-thirds (68%) of the total global increase in biodiversity impacts along oil crop supply chains. When categorized by final use, outsourced biodiversity impacts represented 58% of the biodiversity impact growth attributed to food final uses, whereas this proportion reached 91% for non-food final uses. Among consumption regions, China’s growing demand emerged as one of the major drivers of outsourced biodiversity impacts, increasing nearly 11-fold and accounting for 34% of the global growth in outsourced impacts. The EU was the second largest contributor, with an 87% increase, contributing 17% to global growth. Taken together, these large consumption regions in both emerging (for example, China) and developed economies (for example, EU) jointly drive the observed rise in outsourced biodiversity impacts along global oil crop supply chains.

Per capita consumption accounts for the largest share of biodiversity impact growth

Using SDA, we statistically attribute the observed increase in biodiversity impacts from global oil crop supply chains between 1995 and 2020 primarily to changes in per capita consumption, which accounted for a contribution equivalent to ~74% of the total net increase (Fig. 5a). Population growth was the second largest contributor (~54%). These contributions were partly offset by a negative contribution from reduced land-use area per tonne of production of approximately −44%, consistent with yield improvements and shifts in the oil crop mix towards higher-yielding crops. Regionally, China emerged as the country where rising per capita consumption made the largest contribution to the increase in consumption-based biodiversity impacts. Between 1995 and 2020, China’s per capita consumption nearly quadrupled and thus contributed 119% of the net increase in its biodiversity impact. Other emerging economies, including India, Brazil and Indonesia, also exhibited considerable growth in biodiversity impacts, statistically attributed primarily to increased per capita consumption. By contrast, per capita consumption contributed less to the net increase in most developed economies over the same period. Nevertheless, despite rapid consumption growth in emerging regions, biodiversity impacts per capita in developing economies remain substantially lower than those in developed regions such as the EU and the USA; for example, in 2020, China’s consumption-based biodiversity impact per capita from the global oil crop supply chain was still about 63% lower than that of the EU. In Africa, SDA assigns a substantial positive contribution to increases in land-use area per tonne of production, reflecting declining oil crop agricultural efficiency. Our supply chain analysis indicates that more than 80% of Africa’s oil crop consumption is locally sourced, suggesting that investing in and adopting sustainable agricultural practices and improved farming methods would be particularly relevant in Africa. Except for Africa, most regions experienced reductions in land-use area per tonne of production due to improvements in agricultural practices and the adoption of more efficient crop varieties. However, increased reliance on high-yield oil crop cultivation (for example, oil palm), often located in biodiversity-rich regions, has geographically shifted biodiversity impacts. A more detailed analysis of the SDA results is provided in Supplementary Results 3.

Discussion

Our study integrates spatially explicit biodiversity impact assessments that explicitly incorporate LUI with a hybrid MRIO framework and detailed supply chain impact mapping. These methodological improvements, particularly the differentiation of LUI levels and the application of spatially explicit LFs, result in biodiversity impact estimates for global oil crop cultivation of 1.52% PSLglo in 2020, almost three times higher than previously reported (0.56% PSLglo)39, which did not account for the spatial distribution of oil crops and variation in LUI and therefore substantially underestimated their biodiversity impacts. However, to enable a consistent global comparison, our framework necessarily simplifies (1) the spatio-temporal representation of crop expansion and land-use change (including the focus on land occupation rather than explicit land transformation), (2) biodiversity responses through generalized land-use classes and ecoregion‑average loss factors and (3) certain supply chain and sectoral distinctions (for example, biofuels). Whereas our SDA results identify the relative contributions of consumption and efficiency, it is important to note that SDA is a mathematical accounting framework rather than a causal model. It treats factors such as per capita consumption and technical efficiency as independent, whereas in reality, these variables are often interdependent. For example, improvements in agricultural efficiency can lower production costs and prices, which may subsequently stimulate higher demand through the rebound effect. Consequently, these results should be viewed as a statistical attribution of historical trends rather than a prediction of how these variables would interact in a dynamic economic system. We discuss these considerations and limitations in Supplementary Discussion 1 and outline priorities for future work. Looking ahead, integrating temporally explicit land‑use change and more granular supply chain information and complementing the endpoint footprint with finer‑resolution, spatially explicit biodiversity indicators (linked to globally comparable endpoint metrics)40,41, could further improve the accuracy and decision relevance of biodiversity impact assessment of global agri‑food supply chain analysis.

Our findings offer critical policy insights to mitigate the biodiversity impacts of oil crop cultivation and promote more sustainable production and consumption patterns. Sustainable cultivation in pan-tropical regions, notably Southeast Asia and Latin America, is vital given their disproportionate biodiversity impacts despite modest harvested area shares. There is an immediate need for more stringent deforestation restrictions42,43 and the reinforcement of certification systems44 such as the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil (RSPO)45 to mitigate these impacts.

Beyond production hotspots, our analysis shows that major importing economies, particularly China and the EU, account for nearly half of the global outsourced biodiversity impacts along global oil crop supply chains. This highlights the need for these regions to promote sustainable sourcing through increased consumer awareness46,47, stricter import standards48 and the creation of incentives for responsible suppliers49. Such measures can drive shifts towards sustainable dietary patterns via demand reduction. However, their effectiveness depends on broader market feedback and price-demand elasticities not explicitly modelled in our accounting framework. Meanwhile, dietary shifts in the global south also require attention, as improved nutrition driven by higher consumption of vegetable oils and animal products has inadvertently increased biodiversity impacts. Therefore, policy interventions should address the environmental implications of dietary transitions while ensuring nutritional security.

Furthermore, policy focus should differ for food and non-food final uses. Food consumption is the main driver of current biodiversity impacts (~70%), primarily from vegetable oils and animal feed, requiring policies that promote sustainable diets while balancing global nutritional needs. Non-food uses (~30%), though smaller currently, are growing faster (+89%) and rely heavily on trade outsourcing (82%). Their drivers (for example, biochemicals, bioplastics) are often linked to climate objectives. This requires a careful assessment of the environmental footprint of bio-based alternatives to ensure alignment between climate and biodiversity policies, particularly regarding imported feedstocks.

Moreover, robust international supply chain traceability systems are fundamental to ensuring environmental compliance across borders. Enhanced transparency in oil palm and soybean supply chains (potentially leveraging technologies such as blockchain) could substantially improve the monitoring and enforcement of sustainability standards.

Finally, our results indicate that decreases in land-use area per tonne of production (reflecting yield gains within specific crops and shifts towards higher-yielding crops) substantially counteracted increased consumption-side contribution to biodiversity impacts over 1995–2020. This indicates that agricultural productivity improvement served as a critical leverage point historically. Moving forward, policy efforts might benefit from targeted investments in agricultural research and technological innovations focused on sustainably intensifying production on existing farmland. However, achieving this without triggering rebound effects requires complementary measures to ensure that yield gains genuinely alleviate pressure for expansion into biodiversity-rich habitats, rather than inadvertently stimulating new demand, as highlighted in the land-sharing/sparing debate50.

In summary, addressing biodiversity impacts from global oil crop cultivation requires integrated policies targeting sustainable production, responsible consumption and enhanced supply chain transparency, while accounting for potential feedback loops and rebound effects. Future research should focus on improving data collection, analytical methods and evidence-based assessments to inform integrated strategies addressing production efficiency, biodiversity conservation and consumer behaviour. Such an approach will support policymakers in promoting sustainable and ecologically responsible oil crop supply chains.

Methods

Harmonized global spatial distribution of oil crop cultivation

To quantitatively assess biodiversity impacts associated with global oil crop cultivation, we constructed a detailed spatial distribution map covering 19 major oil crops (Supplementary Table 1). Given the importance of reliable datasets for accurate assessment, we prioritized data sources by reliability, using direct observational data (for example, remote sensing) where available, followed by indirect observational data and land-use statistics. Specifically, high-resolution remote sensing data provided distributions for closed-canopy coconuts23 and oil palm plantations24, whereas the global spatially disaggregated crop production statistics data for 2020 (SPAM 2020)21 provided distributions for coconuts, soybeans, groundnuts, sunflower, rapeseed, sesame and seed cotton. For the remaining oil crops, we used CROPGRIDS22 data, a global gridded dataset providing spatially explicit distribution for numerous crops.

To harmonize the diverse input datasets, we first resampled all spatial layers to a uniform 0.05° spatial resolution (approximately 5.5 km at the equator), consistent with CROPGRIDS. Subsequently, we calibrated our preliminary maps to align with the national harvested area statistics reported by FAOSTAT (2020). This calibration involved three steps: (1) aggregating our preliminary, pre-calibration mapped harvested area estimates to the country level using standardized administrative boundaries from Natural Earth51; (2) deriving country-specific calibration factors, calculated as the ratio of FAOSTAT-reported national harvested area to our aggregated harvested area estimates; and (3) systematically applying these calibration factors back to the original harvested and cultivated area maps, thereby adjusting their absolute magnitudes within each country while preserving their inherent relative spatial distribution patterns. Harvested area refers to the total area from which crops are gathered annually, potentially including multiple harvests per year on the same land. In contrast, cultivated area represents the physical extent of land dedicated to crop production, encompassing areas including fallow and rotated plots, which better reflects the annual land occupation. Although the calibration factors were derived from harvested area discrepancies, we applied them to cultivated area maps under the assumption that any systematic country-level bias affects both metrics similarly. This methodological workflow resulted in a globally harmonized spatial distribution for oil crop cultivation in 2020, serving as the spatial baseline for our temporal reconstruction.

To enable temporal analysis, we reconstructed annual historical distributions of oil crop cultivated area (1995–2019) by combining the 2020 maps with the HYDE 3.5 historical cropland dataset52, following the method of Mialyk et al.53. Specifically, for each 0.05° grid cell, we calculated the fraction of each oil crop relative to the total HYDE cropland in 2020. In cells where total oil crop area exceeded reported HYDE cropland, we instead calculated the share relative to the total oil crop cultivated area. We then applied these 2020-derived fractions to annual HYDE maps to scale oil crop cultivated areas proportionally to historical cropland changes (truncating negative values to zero). This procedure produced a consistent set of annual maps at 0.05° resolution. These datasets assume that the within-grid crop mix remains constant at 2020 patterns while total cropland area evolves according to HYDE 3.5 data, providing the basis for the temporal biodiversity impact assessment.

Spatially explicit biodiversity impact assessment of global oil crop cultivation

To quantify biodiversity impacts due to oil crop cultivation, we used state-of-the-art global species loss factors (LFs) developed by Scherer et al.29. These LFs cover plants and terrestrial vertebrates (amphibians, birds, mammals and reptiles) and offer a more refined and updated assessment than the benchmark LFs currently recommended by UNEP28. By incorporating land-use intensity (LUI) and habitat fragmentation, the LFs estimate the per hectare global potential species loss (PSLglo) due to land occupation at the level of terrestrial ecoregions. The LFs integrate a global extinction probability weighting, which scales local species losses according to each species’ global range and degree of endemism. These per hectare PSLglo values indicate the potential for long-term global species loss resulting from ongoing land-use activities28,29. These losses extend beyond immediate extinctions to account for extinction debt due to habitat degradation and fragmentation28,29, with the metric ranging from 0 to 1 (here expressed as 0–100%, where 1 or 100% indicates complete extinction risk for all terrestrial species). The LFs provide spatially explicit factors across five broad land-use types (cropland, pasture, plantations, managed forests and urban land) at three intensity levels (minimal, light and intense). For this analysis, we applied the aggregated LFs for plants and terrestrial vertebrates as provided by Scherer et al. These LFs were calculated by first averaging the four terrestrial vertebrates (amphibians, birds, mammals and reptiles) to obtain a kingdom-level factor for Animalia, then averaging this with the plant kingdom factor for each land-use type and intensity level to represent the eukaryotic domain29. This aggregation yields an integrated assessment of biodiversity impacts across plants and terrestrial vertebrates, capturing their combined response to land use.

We classified each oil crop according to its corresponding land-use type: tree crops (oil palm, coconuts, olives and so on) were assigned to plantations, whereas annual crops (soybeans, rapeseeds, sunflower, groundnuts and so on) were assigned to cropland. Detailed mappings for all 19 oil crops are provided in Supplementary Table 1. We then calculated the spatial and temporal distribution of LUI for the different oil crops from 1995 to 2020. For tree crops, the industrial oil palm plantations were classified as intense land use, whereas smallholder plantations were classified as light land use. Closed-canopy coconut plantations were categorized as intense land use, whereas other coconut plantations were classified as light land use. For oil palm and coconuts, we computed the within-pixel shares of area under intense versus light LUI based on the 2020 distribution and assumed that these relative shares remain constant over 1995–2019, whereas the total cultivated area per pixel evolves over time. For major annual oil crops with crop-specific nitrogen fertilizer information (soybeans, rapeseeds, sunflower, groundnuts and sesame), we classified LUI based on crop-specific nitrogen fertilizer application data25. Nitrogen use of less than 10 kg ha−1 was classified as minimal land use, between 10 and 100 kg ha−1 as light land use and more than 100 kg ha−1 as intense land use. For all remaining crops, we followed Scherer et al.’s method29, to construct a general LUI time series by combining area equipped for irrigation (AEI) with annual maps of nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizer application. AEI data were taken from the global dataset developed by Mehta et al.27, which provides snapshots at 5‑year intervals up to 2015. We resampled these maps to the 0.05° grid using an area-preserving procedure, linearly interpolated between the 5-year snapshots and linearly extrapolated from 2015 to 2020 to obtain an annual AEI time series for 1995–2020. Nitrogen and phosphorus application rates were derived from the global crop-specific fertilization dataset compiled by Coello et al.26, by aggregating across all crop groups and converting reported P2O5 to elemental P, before resampling to 0.05° resolution to extract annual maps. Using the 2020 global distributions of AEI, nitrogen and phosphorus application rates, we calculated the first and third quartiles (Q1 and Q3) for each indicator and used these fixed thresholds for all years to ensure temporal comparability of LUI classes and avoid shifts in class boundaries: pixels where all three indicators were ≤ Q1 were classified as minimal land use, pixels where at least one indicator exceeded Q3 were classified as intense land use and all others as light land use. After obtaining the spatial and temporal distribution of oil crop LUI, we multiplied the cultivated area of each oil crop by the corresponding land-use type and LUI specific LFs to derive grid cell level PSLglo attributable to oil crop cultivation for each year from 1995 to 2020.

To identify hotspot areas of biodiversity impacts, we first calculated PSLglo for each 0.05° grid cell. We then classified grid cells according to their PSLglo values and defined hotspot areas as cells with PSLglo exceeding 10−6%, which in our dataset corresponds approximately to the 90th percentile (top ~10%) of impacted grid cells (PSLglo > 0). These hotspots represent areas with high biodiversity impacts from oil crop cultivation. At the regional scale, we further identified critical regions as countries or regions with particularly high biodiversity impacts. Ultimately, the differences in global species loss from land occupation by various oil crops are driven by the combination of their typical geographic distribution (ecoregions/global extinction probability), land-use type and LUI. For instance, oil palm plantations in Southeast Asian tropical rainforest ecoregions typically cause greater biodiversity impacts per hectare than olive groves in Mediterranean ecoregions due to the higher baseline endemic biodiversity of tropical rainforests.

As a robustness test of our dynamic biodiversity impact time series, we additionally applied a simplified, nationally aggregated approach based on FAOSTAT harvested area statistics (fixed-2020)4. In contrast to our spatially explicit reconstruction, which allocated national crop areas within countries to grid cells and time‑varying LUI, this additional test reconstructed temporal dynamics solely from national statistics (FAOSTAT) while holding the national‑average impact per harvested hectare for each crop in 2020 constant. First, we computed for each country and oil crop a national average PSLglo per hectare by dividing the total 2020 PSLglo attributable to that crop by its harvested area in 2020. Second, we multiplied these national average LFs by the annually reported harvested areas from FAOSTAT to obtain country‑ and crop‑specific biodiversity impact time series for 1995–2020. Third, for countries where FAOSTAT-reported harvested areas for a given oil crop, but no spatial distribution information was available, we used the national average LFs of the corresponding land‑use type (cropland or plantation) as a proxy. Comparing this nationally aggregated reconstruction with the dynamic spatially explicit estimates allowed us to assess the robustness of global and regional temporal trends to assumptions about within‑country spatial allocation of oil crops and LUI changes. The relevant comparisons are provided in Supplementary Results 4.

Hybridized FABIO and REX3

To analyse biodiversity impacts embodied in global oil crop supply chains, we developed a hybrid MRIO model14,16 combining the FABIO20 and REX3 databases19,30, covering the period 1995–2020. FABIO v1.2 serves as our foundation, providing balanced physical MRIO tables covering 192 countries and 123 agricultural and food products from 1986 to 2020. FABIO offers detailed sectoral resolution, including 10 primary oil crop cultivation sectors, 11 vegetable oil sectors and 9 oil cake sectors but lacks coverage of non-food sectors. REX3, a high-resolution global MRIO database derived from EXIOBASE3 and enhanced with detailed country-level data from Eora26 and other international statistics19,30, covers 189 countries and 163 sectors for the period 1995 to 2022. REX3 is specifically designed to improve the spatial resolution in regions previously aggregated as ‘Rest of the World’, thereby offering more accurate assessments of environmental impacts linked to global supply chains. We selected REX3 for the hybridized MRIO with FABIO, primarily due to its finer sectoral and spatial resolution and its close alignment with FABIO’s country-level granularity. The hybridized FABIO and REX3 enables comprehensive tracking of oil crops and their by-products from production through intermediate processing to final consumption.

The hybrid MRIO model14,16,54 consists of four quadrants: (1) the physical MRIO table of FABIO, (2) the FABIO-to-REX3 linkage matrix, (3) the REX3-to-FABIO linkage matrix and (4) the monetary MRIO table of REX3. While the third quadrant is zero, assuming no downstream linkages from REX3 to FABIO, we mapped the flows of agricultural commodities from FABIO to the regions and sectors of REX3 in the second quadrant of the integrated hybrid MRIO model. We constructed the FABIO-to-REX3 linkage matrix by distributing FABIO’s final demand category ‘other uses’ across REX3 manufacturing sectors based on use shares from REX3 and using a country concordance matrix (Supplementary Table 2). The monetary transaction matrix was computed using supply and use filter matrices (Supplementary Table 8). We then derived the row-stochastic use-share matrix by normalizing these transactions. For each country, the linkage matrix was constructed by multiplying the use shares by the ‘other uses’ demand. Finally, we assembled the full hybrid MRIO matrix, incorporating both physical and monetary flows and computed the hybrid Leontief inverses matrix via blockwise inversion. Supply chain impact mapping

To trace tele-connected biodiversity impacts from global oil crop cultivation through the entire supply chain, we applied the Supply Chain Impact Mapping (SCIM) methodology18 to oil crop cultivation and its downstream intermediate product and final-use sectors. SCIM is an MRIO-based approach that extends standard Leontief analysis by systematically tracking environmental impacts through distinct supply chain stages—from production, through intermediate processing, to final consumption. Whereas conventional consumption-based MRIO analysis directly links upstream production impacts to final demand, SCIM explicitly captures intermediate transformation steps. For instance, rather than directly linking biodiversity impacts from Brazilian soybean cultivation to Chinese meat consumption, SCIM tracks impacts through intermediate stages such as soybean cake processing in China. This framework has been applied to study greenhouse gas emissions in global plastics supply chains30 and biodiversity impacts in agri-food39 and mining supply chains15. The stage-wise mapping enables identification of strategic intervention points for targeted mitigation strategies.

This structure leverages the comprehensive agri-food supply chain detail inherent in the FABIO database. Statistics and reproducibility

This study is a global modelling analysis based on a hybridized MRIO framework with spatially explicit land-use data; no experimental sampling, randomization or blinding is involved, and no data were excluded. Full input datasets and analysis code are publicly available or available upon reasonable request to ensure reproducibility.
