Autonomous codesign and fabrication of multistimuli-responsive material systems
Authors: Liwei Wang, Alexander L. Evenchik, Jared M. Yang, Ryan L. Truby, Wei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Responsive materials offer solutions to complex engineering challenges by enabling systems to adapt their shapes or properties in response to external stimuli. To fully harness the potential of responsive materials, inverse design methods that integrate multiple types of stimuli and manufacturing processes are necessary. We present a unified, autonomous codesign framework that simultaneously optimizes structure, manufacturing, materials, and stimuli for responsive material systems, achieving target shape morphing under multiple stimuli without relying on human heuristics or expertise. It integrates generalized topology optimization with hybrid data-physics differentiable simulations to achieve flexible, manufacturing-aware designs for network-like responsive material systems. We showcase our framework with a multimaterial three-dimensional printing process with high material tunability, which we use to fabricate liquid crystal elastomer systems that morph into different forms in response to heat and light. The exceptional flexibility and efficiency of our method will advance shape-morphing applications spanning soft robotics to drug delivery.

INTRODUCTION

Biological organisms change their forms to dynamically adapt to their environment (1–6). In contrast, engineered systems are typically constrained to fixed configurations and functionalities. The emergence of responsive material systems marks a major step in closing this performance gap. Responsive materials alter their properties or exert mechanical forces to achieve shape morphing in response to external stimuli (7). However, creating such material systems requires not only innovations in materials and manufacturing for diverse tunable responsiveness but also design methods to program the overall shape-morphing process. Existing studies predominantly focus on manufacturing innovations rather than design methods (8–10), which have made considerable progress in finding new responsive materials or mechanisms (11–17), enhancing fabrication capabilities (18–21), and developing corresponding simulation models (22–29). These efforts have led to material systems that respond to various environmental changes or external stimuli, such as heat (30, 31), light (32–34), humidity (35), voltage (36, 37), magnetic fields (38), and pH (39). Continued improvements in manufacturing capabilities have driven enhanced programmability in morphing capabilities, enabling responsive material systems to perform a wide range of tasks such as minimally invasive surgery (40), drug delivery (39), human body monitoring (41), mechanical computing (42, 43), and environmental remediation (35).

Despite advances in manufacturing, many responsive material systems are still obtained without an inverse design process, relying on human expertise or heuristics to produce relatively regular material or structural patterns. While effective for proof-of-concept demonstrations in manufacturing-focused research, they offer limited flexibility for achieving more complex behaviors that demand an inverse design process capable of producing customized and potentially nonintuitive solutions. To address this limitation, several pioneering efforts aimed at autonomous inverse design have produced major breakthroughs through both iterative optimization (44–48) and machine learning approaches (49–51). Among these efforts, topology optimization (TO) stands out as a particularly promising method due to its flexibility and generalizability (52–55). TO has long been applied to study several aspects that are both relevant and critical to responsive material systems (52), such as mixed-variable problems (56–58), multiphysics actuators (59, 60), nonlinear deformation (61–64), multimaterial integration (65–70), nonlinear mechanical response programming (61, 71), tensegrity structures (72), compliant mechanisms (73), and design for additive manufacturing (74–78). A comprehensive introduction to TO can be found in (55). Recently, these TO methods have also been introduced to the inverse design of morphing material systems. For example, multimaterial TO has been combined with nonlinear thermomechanical constitutive models to enable the inverse design of responsive materials systems composed of multiple polymers (45, 79) or liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) (45, 80), achieving distinct behaviors with or without thermal actuation. In another study, multimaterial TO was applied to design edgewise distributions of two LCE materials within predefined truss structures, enabling various shape-morphing behaviors under multiple temperatures (81).

However, existing inverse design approaches have primarily been developed for systems actuated by a single stimulus, typically thermal stimuli, at varying intensity levels rather than accommodating different stimulus types. They focus mainly on multimaterial layouts and often do not directly consider or optimize the manufacturing process (82). However, recent advances in fabrication have expanded the range of responsive materials and actuation mechanisms, enabling the integration of multiple stimuli with distinct physical principles, such as heat and light, within the same system (9, 83–86). These advances also provide finer control over manufacturing parameters, allowing more precise tuning of responsive behavior beyond material selection alone (7, 19, 87). Together, these manufacturing developments present new design challenges that demand approaches capable of simultaneously optimizing materials, structural geometry, and manufacturing parameters to achieve programmable shape morphing under multiple, distinct stimuli. However, an autonomous design framework that meets these emerging requirements is still notably lacking (88).

In this study, we build upon previous TO methods and present a unified design and manufacturing framework that simultaneously and autonomously optimizes all aspects of creating multistimuli-responsive material systems. As shown in Fig. 1, our artificial intelligence (AI)−driven, autonomous codesign framework and accompanying fabrication platform, which together account for the structure, stimuli, material placement, and manufacturing process, enable the rapid realization of complex shape-morphing systems. Our framework is general enough to accommodate multistimuli-responsive behaviors driven by different underlying physical or chemical processes, such as thermomechanical and photomechanical effects. Central to our approach is the integration of TO with a hybrid data-physics differentiable simulation model for network-like materials. We build upon TO and extend it to codesign by unifying multiple sources and types of design variables, including continuous structural variables, discrete numbers of printing paths, categorical material types, and combinatorial printing parameters, into a single continuous design space. To account for manufacturing effects, during the iterative optimization, neural networks (NNs) trained on experimental data act as differentiable surrogates to relate location-specific processing parameters to location-specific responsive material properties in a quasi-explicit simulation. This data-physics hybrid model can be seen as a generalized NN, allowing it to leverage automatic differentiation and the computational power of graphics processing unit (GPU) parallelization—key techniques that underpin AI development—to enable efficient physical simulation and provide gradient information for codesign. As a result, print-ready material designs can be automatically generated within several minutes, achieving programmed target shape-morphing behaviors without the need for human heuristics or design expertise.

While this framework is generalizable to various classes of responsive materials, we demonstrate its effectiveness on three-dimensional (3D) printed, multistimuli-responsive LCEs. We select LCEs due to their capacity for large, contractile shape changes, multistimuli responsiveness through thermo- and phototropic actuation, and their ease of fabrication with 3D printing. To experimentally validate material designs, we develop ink formulations and a multimaterial 3D printing process for LCEs (movie S1). These advances enable the direct fabrication of multimaterial shape-morphing networks responsive to both heat and ultraviolet (UV) light. In all cases, the method demonstrates exceptional design flexibility and efficiency, accommodating diverse network topologies, target shape-morphing processes, and multiple stimuli. In addition, our method provides valuable physical insights and design principles beneficial to general morphing systems, uncovering how initially homogeneous edges evolve into a heterogeneous network of specialized actuators and structural units. Our methods enable a wide range of material systems to be designed beyond the limitations of human intuition. They open opportunities for the accelerated production of autonomous and robotic materials (89), material systems for mechanical computing (90, 91), biocompatible morphing structures for biomedicine (28, 92), and more.

RESULTS

Design framework

Our autonomous codesign framework begins by defining an initial design together with shape-morphing targets specific to respective stimuli of interest (Fig. 1A). Through iterative optimization (Fig. 1B), our framework generates the optimized distribution of materials with selective stimuli-responsiveness, structural topology design, and detailed edge-level manufacturing instructions (Fig. 1C) needed to create a network that achieves the specified morphing objectives (Fig. 1D). In this work, we focus on network-like structures made from responsive materials. Our framework integrates two key components: a hybrid data-physics differentiable simulation and an extended TO that integrates variables of different types and sources.

The first component of our framework, the differentiable simulation, models the responsive behaviors of network edges as generalized nonlinear springs with initial strains (Fig. 1B and fig. S1). Before actuation, the spring has an unactuated length l with no forces or strain applied. Upon actuation, the spring is assigned a stimulus-induced strain, generating stress that drives the spring to its actuated rest length lr. Because the springs in the network interact as each moves toward its own actuated rest length, simulating the actuated configuration of the network is equivalent to determining the equilibrium of the prestrained spring system. Accurately modeling the responsive behaviors of each edge is crucial to finding the true equilibrium of the network. However, edge behaviors are highly nonlinear and influenced by various factors, such as edge structure, stimuli, materials, and manufacturing parameters (Fig. 1B). Therefore, a flexible model is required to account for this complexity and variability. To address this, we use an NN due to its strong fitting capability, training it on experimental data to establish the constitutive relation of the materials under different processing conditions (Fig. 2A). The trained NN is integrated into the generalized spring model to predict the force on a spring as it experiences different deformations. We then combine this NN-based spring model with a symplectic Euler integration scheme to iteratively update the nodal locations in the network (93). This forward simulation continues until equilibrium is achieved, corresponding to the actuated configurations under external stimuli (see text S1 for details). The simulation was implemented using Taichi, a differentiable programming language that enables GPU-accelerated parallel computing with high efficiency (93, 94). In addition to accelerated simulation, we use automatic differentiation to compute the gradients of the final nodal positions with respect to all variables of interest in the materials network. These gradients quantify how small variations in each variable, such as material choices or edge thickness, influence the equilibrium positions of the nodes after actuation. This gradient information is used to guide subsequent optimization to achieve target actuated shapes.

The second component of our framework is a TO process built onto the differentiable simulation. The objective of our optimization problem is to minimize the discrepancy between the network’s actuated shapes and the prescribed target shapes. The primary challenge of this process lies in the entangled and mixed-type variables from different sources (i.e., structures, materials, stimuli, and manufacturing), which hinder the differentiation of the simulation and the realization of an effective codesign process. Specifically, the design space includes continuous nodal positions, binary connectivity, categorical variables for material selection, discrete variables for the number of printing paths, and combinatorial printing parameters. Similar discrete variable problems, such as multimaterial design, have long been studied in TO (56, 65–68), while combinatorial variables have been relatively rare. Here, we integrate existing TO methods and extend them to incorporate combinatorial variables, constructing a design representation and property interpolation scheme that maps mixed-type variables into a unified and continuous design space (see text S2, fig. S2, and table S1). It accommodates nearly all variable types encountered across various digital fabrication methods. Manufacturing constraints are also integrated into the design process to ensure printability. This allows us to transform the original mixed-variable network design problem into a constrained continuous optimization problem that we can solve iteratively using the method of moving asymptotes (see details in text S2) (95). We simultaneously optimize all variables based on gradients obtained from the differentiable simulation, which autonomously and efficiently generates on-demand network designs for specified morphing tasks. Unlike existing methods that typically focus only on the distribution of a limited set of materials, our method also provides optimized network geometry, topology, and edge-specific manufacturing parameters, thereby offering greater design flexibility and practicality, exploiting heterogeneity to achieve superior design performance.

In our framework, we choose networks over continuous solids as the architectural form for shape morphing, which is motivated by considerations of morphing flexibility and manufacturability. Specifically, solid continua often require large local deformations to redistribute material and alter their shapes (96). In contrast, network architecture, with voids between discrete edges, relaxes the constraints of a continuum, allowing for greater shape variation with lower requirements on materials, such as stretchability and induced strain. Moreover, the network architecture facilitates adjusting printing parameters and thereby controlling actuation performance across different edges during manufacturing. In contrast, achieving similar spatial variations in a continuous solid would pose practical challenges. Furthermore, as shown in previous studies (91, 97, 98), a network system has a vast combinatorial design space and complex interactions between nodes, providing substantial design freedom. These desirable features make a network an excellent choice for morphing material systems.

Manufacturing platform and material modeling

We demonstrate the capabilities of our framework with multistimuli-responsive LCE networks. LCEs exhibit large, reversible, and anisotropic actuation and can be chemically modified to respond to multiple stimuli (85–87). For this work, we focus on two LCE formulations that respond to two orthogonal stimuli, i.e., heat and UV light. Our first LCE formulation is a main-chain, azobenzene-functionalized LCE (azoLCE) that reversibly actuates in response to both heat and UV light. Thermal actuation occurs because of global changes in mesogen ordering once the nematic-isotropic transition temperature (TNI) is surpassed (Fig. 2A, left). Photomechanical actuation occurs as azobenzene undergoes trans-cis isomerization in response to UV light exposure, which drives contraction via local mesogen domain disordering (99). We also use a second LCE formulation, termed neatLCE, that does not contain azobenzene and only responds to thermal stimuli. Integrating both azoLCE and neatLCE allows the fabrication of heterogeneous, multistimuli-responsive networks that can spatially respond to heat, light, or both.

A distinct advantage of our framework is that, in addition to codesigning network geometry and edgewise material/stimuli selection, it also simultaneously optimizes the manufacturing parameters. These manufacturing parameters are crucial for tuning the material properties that govern the network’s overall performance and enhancing design granularity in complex morphing tasks. We use 3D printing in this study because it offers a straightforward approach for both spatially distributing material types and leveraging numerous processing parameters to fine-tune material properties. To use our general design framework for 3D printing multistimuli-responsive networks, we first identify key print parameters and characterize how they affect material stiffness and actuation.

Both azoLCE and neatLCE are 3D printed as viscous, shear-thinning inks (fig. S3) via multimaterial high−operating-temperature direct ink writing (HOT-DIW) (fig. S4) (100). LCE mesogen domains are aligned along the network edges during the extrusion process, i.e., along the printing direction (87). The inks are photopolymerized into LCE networks using green light (525 nm) both during and after printing, which preserves network topology, prevents azobenzene trans-cis isomerization, and maintains the desired degree of mesogen alignment (movie S1). We identified printing speed, pressure, and the number of printing paths as key print parameters that enabled edgewise variation of material properties. Specifically, we tune the cross sections of each edge by varying the number of printing paths from three to six in both the width and thickness directions. In addition, for each LCE ink, we allow 12 unique combinations of edgewise print parameters based on four printing pressures and three printing speeds. These variables provide programmability over both the cross-sectional geometry and active response of each edge, thereby controlling edge stiffness and actuation strains.

The stiffness of printed LCEs is dictated by the dimensions of the edge and the stress-strain behavior of the material present. To quantify this relationship, we measured a wide range of single-pass widths and thicknesses, with low-pressure, high-speed combinations producing thinner edges (fig. S5). We also performed dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) on printed samples to analyze their stress-strain behavior as a function of ink type, printing parameters, and applied stimulus (i.e., no stimuli, above TNI, or after UV exposure) (Fig. 2A, right, and figs. S6 to S11).

Adjusting print speed and pressure also enables tunable control over LCE actuation, especially for azoLCE. As shown in Fig. 2B, the actuation strains of azoLCE increase with print speed, whereas the relationship between print speed and actuation strain for neatLCE is less clear (fig. S12). We observe a broad range of thermotropic actuation for azoLCE, from 40 ± 2.5% contraction to 21 ± 1% expansion (Fig. 2B and fig. S13). The azoLCE demonstrates a reduced range of strains during photoactuation, from a maximum of 8 ± 1.3% contraction to nearly no actuation. For neatLCE, while slow print speeds result in smaller actuation, print speeds of 5 and 10 mm/s show similar actuation, likely due to the higher viscosity of neatLCE leading to consistent mesogen alignment beyond a critical print speed (fig. S3) (87). In general, lower print pressures tend to result in higher actuation strains for both azoLCE and neatLCE (fig. S12), although this trend is more pronounced with speed than pressure. On the basis of these results, which show a greater degree of actuation tunability for azoLCE compared to neatLCE, we used azoLCE ink for printing single-stimulus morphing materials and included neatLCE only when printing multistimuli-responsive composites.

These mechanical and actuation data can then be used to explicitly link manufacturing parameters to material performance. Although constitutive models for general thermally responsive materials exist (79, 81), capturing the influence of process parameters and accurately modeling photothermal-responsive behavior remain substantial challenges. A key contribution of this work is addressing these gaps using our experimental data to develop reliable data-physics hybrid models. To do this, we trained our NNs used in the generalized spring model on our experimentally measured stress-strain curves (Fig. 2A, right, and figs. S14 and S15). With the trained model, starting from the measured actuation strain, we trace the NN-fitted stress-strain curves for each edge to determine the forces exerted by the material, as it transitions from its unactuated length l to its actuated rest length lr (Fig. 2A, right; see text S1 and figs. S14 and S15 for details). By incorporating other edgewise features, such as single-path dimensions and the number of printing paths, we calculate the forces at both ends of each spring and iteratively determine the actuated states of the entire network system. This manufacturing-informed simulation model is then integrated into extended TO, allowing us to cooptimize nodal locations, topology, material types, print speeds, pressures, and the number of printing paths. The detailed ranges and sources of the design variables are provided in table S1 in the Supplementary Materials. All variables are normalized within the range of 0 to 1 through projection and scaling to facilitate the optimization process. Comprehensive manufacturing instructions obtained from the optimization, as demonstrated in Fig. 2C, enable the rapid fabrication of complex shape-morphing systems that would be nearly impossible to design manually.

Programming thermoresponsive shape morphing

To demonstrate our framework’s capabilities, we begin with the design of morphing azoLCE networks that achieve target shapes under thermal actuation. Figure 3 provides several examples, showcasing the design flexibility of our framework. For these thermal shape-morphing tasks, the optimization objective is to minimize the average shortest distances from the boundary points of actuated networks to the target shapes (text S2.3 to 2.4). Our framework simultaneously optimizes the network’s topology, structure, edgewise print speed, print pressure, and cross-sectional geometry controlled by the number of printing paths in each design produced. It provides superior flexibility to accommodate different initial network topologies and geometries, a variety of regular and irregular actuated shape targets, and freely chosen unactuated and actuated states (Fig. 3). The design process is also highly efficient with GPU-enabled parallel computing. For the cases shown in Fig. 3, there are 25 to 49 nodes, connected by dozens to hundreds of edges. Each node is associated with a 2D coordinate, while each edge carries multiple design variables of various types and sources (table S1). Together, this results in hundreds of mixed-type design variables in the systems. The algorithm completed one iteration in 0.11 to 0.15 s on an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU (equipped with 48-GB GDDR6 graphics memory and 18,176 CUDA cores), including ~0.1 s for simulation and 0.05 s for variable update. This represents a slight improvement compared to 0.17 to 0.22 s per iteration on four AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 7975WX CPUs. Nonetheless, both implementations achieve fast performance for the current problem size. The optimization completed in 250 iterations, resulting in a total computational time of less than 1 min.

Figure 3A and fig. S16A show examples of shape-morphing materials with varying nodal density that morph from squares to circles. Our design method iteratively optimized the topology by eliminating edges that impede shape morphing, adjusting nodal locations to reshape internal interactions, and programming edgewise manufacturing parameters to create heterogeneous stiffness and strain distribution (fig. S17). This optimized nodal interaction, combined with the nonuniformity of actuation and stiffness, enables each network to successfully morph into the target circular shape (movie S2). Across these cases, we observe an interesting trend where two groups of edges emerge: one group consists of radial edges extending from the center (darker edges in Fig. 3A), while the other consists of mostly circumferential edges (lighter edges in Fig. 3A). The radial edge group is assigned low print speeds, high print pressures, and large cross-sectional areas, resulting in reduced actuation strain and increased stiffness (see detailed distribution in fig. S17). In contrast, the circumferential edge group is assigned higher speeds, lower pressures, and smaller cross-sectional areas, enhancing contraction while reducing stiffness. As a result, the radial edges form a structural skeleton-like network that supports and maintains roundness, while the circumferential edges act as contracting actuators that drive morphing. This mirrors the interplay between bones and muscles in biological systems. This trend consistently appears across all three initial topologies in Fig. 3A, despite their vastly different starting configurations.

Our framework can also accommodate a wide range of target shapes, including irregular shapes without analytic expressions (Fig. 3B and movie S2). We designed materials that morph into ellipsoidal targets with two axes of symmetry, heart-shaped forms with one axis of symmetry, and chiral-shaped targets with handedness. For the heart and chiral transformations, we need to evaluate the discrepancy between the actuated state and the target shape without relying on analytical expressions to construct the objective function in optimization. We accomplish this by first computing the L2 distance field of the target contour, which represents the shortest distance for any given point in the space to the target. We then fit an NN to parameterize this L2 distance field, using spatial coordinates as input and outputting the corresponding L2 distances (fig. S18 and text S2.3). By applying the NN to each boundary point and aggregating the results, we obtain the average distance to the target contours as the overall shape-matching error and use it as the objective function. Because both the NN and our simulation are differentiable, they can be seamlessly integrated, enabling backpropagation throughout the whole simulation process to obtain gradient information for optimizing the design variables. With this modified optimization, each case in Fig. 3B started from the same initial design—a 5 × 5 square grid with diagonal edges—but still achieved final topologies with different symmetry types that align with the targets. In addition to the structure and topology, the manufacturing parameters were also optimized to create nonuniform stiffness and strain distribution (fig. S19). In general, the contracting but softer edges generate actuation forces, while the stiffer edges provide structural support and guide the actuation into directional deformation, allowing the network to conform to the target shape.

Our framework also allows for a flexible selection of shapes both before and after actuation (Fig. 3C, figs. S16B and S20, and movie S2). For example, as shown in Fig. 3C, we can morph from a circle or an ellipse (fig. S16B) into a square, reversing the transformations shown in Fig. 3 (A and B). In addition, a circle can transform into an ellipse, or conversely, an ellipse into a circle. In all these cases, we first map a 5 × 5 square grid onto the desired preactuation shape via conformal mapping and use this mapped grid as the initial topology in the optimization process. Notably, for each example in Fig. 3, the entire network was automatically generated without the need for human heuristics or trial-and-error adjustments, despite the vast mixed-variable codesign space.

Figure 4 provides several examples of azoLCE material systems 3D printed using the print parameters generated by our framework. Figure 4A and movie S3 show the 5 × 5 square-to-circle shape change. With this design, we aim to demonstrate our ability to fabricate and realize a difficult-to-achieve but classic shape morphing. Results from nodal locations before and after actuation, tracked via black dots placed on the nodes of the printed structure by the high-precision gantry used for fabrication, show that the printing process and subsequent shape morphing closely align with simulation results (Fig. 4A). From a node-by-node perspective, the experiment achieves coefficient of determination (R2) > 0.99 in both the unactuated and actuated states. Beyond evaluating the alignment of individual nodal positions through R2 values and root mean square distances (RMSD; summarized in table S2), we also assess the overall fit of our experimental results to the target shape by analyzing key geometric parameters that define the target shape. For the square-to-circle shape change, we identify the ratio of experimentally realized to computationally predicted square side length or circle diameter as a key geometric parameter (in the unactuated and actuated states, respectively). Our designed and experimental systems are well matched in this comparison, with ratios of 1.01 and 1.02 for the unactuated square side length and actuated circle diameter, respectively. This indicates that, in addition to matching the actual location of specific nodes, our validation also matches the overall shape of the target objective with less than 2% error.

Figure 4B shows an example of a printed material system that morphs into an irregular shear-like shape with two axes of symmetry (movie S4). We chose this design to explore not only irregular shape morphing but also systems that undergo marked contraction during actuation. The initial network topology was a 5 × 5 square with diagonals. Notably, after optimization, the structure retains only the right-tilted diagonal edges, discarding the other diagonal edges. These right-tilted diagonal edges, with higher stiffness but lower contraction in comparison to the other printed edges, resist overall deformation in the right diagonal direction, while the horizontal and vertical edges drive the overall contraction. This ensures that the entire structure adopts a shearing shape along the right diagonal when activated. In addition to good nodal agreement in the unactuated and actuated states (R2 values of 0.9997 and 0.9767 for the unactuated and actuated states, respectively) (table S2), we again identify geometric parameters of interest, specifically the ratios of the long and short diagonals between the experimental and designed systems, to assess overall geometric validity. In the actuated state, the ratio of the experimental to predicted long diagonal is 0.84. The ratio for the short diagonal is 0.94. These ratios suggest a good overall match, but the experimental system slightly overactuated along the long and short diagonals. Insights into the framework’s printing parameters (fig. S19) and experimental images indicate that the slight out-of-plane buckling of the right-tilted diagonal edges is the likely cause. During the optimization process, we imposed various constraints to minimize the risk of out-of-plane and in-plane buckling. Specifically, for out-of-plane buckling, we performed preliminary experiments on simplified geometries to determine the minimum thickness and width of printed features that did not buckle out-of-plane during thermal actuation. From these experiments, we selected three printing passes as the minimum width and thickness necessary to prevent buckling and set them as lower bounds for our manufacturing process (table S1). To reduce the chance of in-plane buckling, we incorporated linear buckling constraints into our optimization framework (text S2.4). Despite these measures, material nonlinearity, nonuniformity in material properties and geometries during manufacturing, residual stress after fabrication, and heat gradients during actuation could still result in buckling (101, 102). Future efforts that build on previous research in nonlinear buckling constraints (103), robust design (104), and strategies to improve material and actuation uniformity could help mitigate this issue, or, alternatively, leverage buckling as a tool for intentional shape transformation (31).

Last, we fabricated and experimentally demonstrated a circle-to-ellipse morphing material in Fig. 4C (movies S5 and S6). The printing process yields a material system that programs the directional responsiveness of each edge, despite the increased complexity of the preactuation shapes generated by our design algorithm. In addition to good nodal agreement (table S2), the overall geometric agreement is also very high. Comparing the ratio of the major or minor axis between the actuated printed and designed systems gives 1.02 and 0.94 for the major axis and minor axis, respectively. A value of 1 would show 100% agreement between the designed and experimental systems, so these values support the validity of our computational approach.

To assess the importance of manufacturing parameters on accurate shape morphing, we also fabricated and tested a control network that used the same circle-to-ellipse design but without the printing parameters provided by the framework (movie S7). As shown in Fig. 4D, the network did not morph into the target ellipse. Instead, we observe an isotropic shrinkage into a smaller-diameter circle. From a nodal perspective, the error is much larger compared to the design in Fig. 4C, with R2 values of 0.8505 and 0.9952 for the circle-to-ellipse shape morphing that did not or did take manufacturing instructions into account, respectively (table S2). Normalized RMSD is also approximately four times larger when manufacturing instructions are ignored (table S2). In addition to a larger nodal error, the global shape error also increases when printing parameters are not accounted for. Using the same major and minor axis comparison as in Fig. 4C, we now attain ratios between the experimental and designed major/minor axis of 0.62 and 0.68. This result highlights the crucial interactions between different entities, especially between manufacturing and geometry, and emphasizes the importance of heterogeneous codesign of all parameters as enabled by our design framework.

Given the reversible nature of LCEs, our networks can achieve repeatable performance over multiple cycles (movie S6). Figure S21 displays the nodal locations of the thermoresponsive shape-morphing networks from Fig. 4 in their actuated states over 5 cycles. For all samples, there is good agreement in nodal location across all cycles. The samples exhibit variable ability to fully return to their unactuated shapes due to several factors, including friction leading to element buckling during cooling. Although outside the scope of this study, future research could explore accurately modeling and predicting the onset of buckling and cooling-rate−dependent performance of thick versus thin elements to allow for reversible unactuated states.

Programming photoresponsive shape morphing

We further demonstrated the flexibility of our framework by exploiting the photomechanical responsiveness of azoLCEs. Unlike thermal actuation, which involves global mesogen disordering above TNI, UV-induced actuation is driven by local mesogen disordering. As a result, azoLCEs generate lower contractile forces during actuation than is predicted by tracing their stress-strain curves, as was done with thermotropic actuation. To account for these differences, we directly measured the actual photomechanical stress during UV actuation (fig. S22) and scaled the NN spring models accordingly. Using these new fitted spring models for photoactuation, we applied the codesign framework to create materials capable of achieving target shape morphing under UV light, as shown in Fig. 5, fig. S23, and movie S8. Because azoLCEs generate weaker contractile forces under UV than thermal actuation, we designed a rectangular network with a high length-to-height ratio to amplify deformation, using the initial topology shown in Fig. 5A. In the first case (Fig. 5A), we designed two distinct networks to either minimize or maximize the distance between the nodes at the right end, corresponding to a closed gripper and an open gripper. For the closed gripper, the design takes on a fish-like shape, with highly actuated, soft edges mostly at the center (light blue) and less-actuated, stiffer edges along the outer rim (dark blue). The mismatch between the contracting center and the passive outer rim pulls the right-end nodes closer together, closing the gripper (Fig. 5A, top row). In contrast, the opening gripper exhibits an almost reversed strain and stiffness distribution, with a stiffer, less-actuated center and more flexible outer rims, causing the right-end nodes to separate (Fig. 5A, bottom row).

In the second case shown in Fig. 5B, we designed a system aimed at maximizing outward bending at both ends of the gripper. The optimization process removed all the edges along the horizontal centerline and the two vertical edges at both ends. This minimizes the bonding between the top and bottom layers, reducing resistance to opening. The optimization process again generated a nonuniform distribution of strain and stiffness by varying the printing parameters (see fig. S23). Stiffer edges with minimal actuation strain form several X-shaped scissor mechanisms in the middle of the design, converting the photo-induced contractions along the top and bottom edges into outward-expanding movements (movie S8).

We fabricated and experimentally validated this design in Fig. 5B by illuminating our sample with UV light after printing (movie S9). Images of the actuated state were taken after the sample had cooled to room temperature to remove any effect of photothermal actuation, which was not directly included in the design method. The darker coloring of the system after UV exposure is indicative of the trans-cis azobenzene isomerization that causes actuation. The experimental deformation is slightly smaller than the simulated results, likely due to friction between the material system and substrate that is comparable to the force of photomechanical actuation but not accounted for in the simulation. Despite this, most nodes ended in their predicted locations, with a high R2 value (0.9985) and low normalized RMSD compared to the other designs (table S2). Cyclability is difficult to ascertain given the limited recovery of the original shape after cis-trans azobenzene isomerization due to element buckling, as discussed for the thermally actuated systems. Overall, the experimental results display the correct shape-morphing behavior and generally agree with the simulation, demonstrating the flexibility and generalizability of our method in accommodating diverse actuation mechanisms and physical principles.

Programming multiresponsive shape morphing

We demonstrate the design of multistimuli-responsive behaviors in LCE composites that react to both heat and UV light (Fig. 6). The networks are 3D printed from neatLCE, which responds only to heat, and azoLCE, which responds to both heat and UV light. Combining these two materials enables the printed networks to undergo one shape morphing with UV light and a fundamentally different morphing when heated. The edgewise selection of these materials is integrated into the codesign framework by aggregating the objective functions corresponding to thermal and UV actuation. In this work, we sum the two objectives for the aggregation due to their comparable scales (see text S2.5). However, for tasks with differing objective priorities or magnitudes, alternative aggregation strategies or full multiobjective optimization may be needed to explore trade-offs and achieve a more comprehensive Pareto solution space.

We first applied the proposed method to design a network that has a square preactuation shape but morphs into different target contours under thermal or UV light (Fig. 6A and movie S10). The design process began with a 5 × 5 square grid with diagonals as the initial topology, identical to the one shown in Fig. 3B. After optimization, the nodal positions and topology were modified, and the edges automatically evolved into two groups: those composed of azoLCE and those composed of neatLCE. The manufacturing parameters—printing speed, pressure, and the number of printing paths—were optimized for both the neat- and azoLCE groups (fig. S24). When exposed to UV light, the azoLCE group exhibits spatially varying induced strain, while the neatLCE group experiences negligible actuation (Fig. 6A and fig. S25). As a result, the unactuated neatLCE group serves as a skeleton that resists the contraction imposed by the photoactuated azoLCE. Notably, the topology of the neatLCE indicates that this skeleton is stiffer in the horizontal direction where most edges are aligned but more compliant in the vertical direction. This leads to inward contraction along horizontal boundaries matching the designed objective, while vertical boundaries remain unchanged. In contrast, both the neat- and azoLCE groups are activated under high temperatures, resulting in a different shape where all boundaries contract inward.

In the previous case, the shape-morphing tasks required different responsive movements along horizontal and vertical boundaries. The design framework addressed this by forming two weakly coupled LCE groups, each primarily responsible for morphing either the horizontal or vertical boundaries. To push the limits of our method, we explored more challenging cases where it is difficult to decouple the movements along different boundaries. In addition, these cases present conflicting morphing targets for the two stimuli (Fig. 6, B to D), forcing interaction between the two LCE groups and increased coupling between the stimuli. For all these cases, we use the same initial topology from Fig. 5A.

For the case in Fig. 6B, we designed a network that bends inward under UV light but exhibits outward bending under thermal actuation (figs. S26 and S27 and movie S10). Again, two groups emerge: the neatLCE forms mirrored C-shapes, while the azoLCE fills the middle. Under UV light, the azoLCE acts as the sole actuator, causing inward bending. When heated, both groups actuate, but the neatLCE’s greater strain dominates, resulting in outward bending. For comparison, we designed another network with the same settings but switched the targets for the two stimuli. As shown in Fig. 6C, figs. S28 and S29, and movie S10, while the topology remains largely unchanged, the group distribution is nearly reversed: the neatLCE moves to the inner part, and the azoLCE occupies the top and bottom. As the objective for each stimulus is swapped, the physical distributions of active material also reverse.

These two contrasting cases demonstrate that when conflicting targets exist for different stimuli, the two groups alternate as the major actuators. Under UV light, the azoLCE group dominates to achieve the target, while under thermal actuation, the neatLCE group, with its larger induced strain, overrides the azoLCE to be the major actuator, compensating for the opposing effects of the two targets.

As a control study, we designed a network where the two targets no longer compete but instead reinforce each other, i.e., with outward bending as the goal for both stimuli (figs. S30 to S32). In this case, the overall topology and material distribution (fig. S31) remain similar to that of the previous case with competing targets in Fig. 6C. However, the printing parameters for the neatLCE group are adjusted to reduce its heat-induced strain, preventing it from overriding the azoLCEs even under thermal actuation. As a result, the azoLCE group dominates the deformation under both UV and thermal stimuli, achieving outward bending in both cases. This demonstrates that both structure and manufacturing are critical in achieving multistimuli-responsive behaviors, further emphasizing the importance of codesigning them, as enabled by our method.

We validated the design in Fig. 6C with fabricated samples and compared the experimental results with the simulations (Fig. 6D). The printing process (movie S1) well matches the initial design, with neat- and azoLCE distributed according to the simulation. During thermal actuation, the system exhibits substantial closing of the outer units; it matches the target design by achieving more than 80% of the predicted actuation strain (Fig. 6E; red boxes indicating region of interest). During photoactuation, the outer elements move outwards as designed, albeit to a lower extent than the simulation (i.e., 32% of the predicted strain; Fig. 6E). There are a few reasons for this lower-than-predicted experimental strain. The first, as discussed in the purely photomechanical system, is the impact of friction in reducing actuation stress. However, another effect, not present in the single-stimulus system, is the photothermal heating of neatLCE during UV illumination. This heating raises the temperature of the material above TNI (fig. S33) and causes a degree of contraction that works against—and limits—photomechanical actuation. Although the sample was cooled before analyzing photomechanical morphing, the neatLCE prevented the azoLCE from reaching its full actuation. This effect is amplified by the fact that the thermal and photo objectives are directly opposing. Any actuation of the neatLCE elements, which produce greater force overall during actuation, directly counteracts that of the azoLCE. Countering this effect will require future innovations in materials or composites with no cross-talk between stimuli. Nevertheless, as shown in Fig. 6D, the simulation and experimental outcomes do align. This demonstrates the ability of our method to integrate multiple actuation mechanisms into a single framework, realizing multiresponsive behaviors that were previously unattainable.

DISCUSSION

We present a unified integrated design and manufacturing framework for multistimuli-responsive materials with network-based structures. Our framework is based on a nonlinear spring model that integrates physical principles and NNs to simulate actuated shapes. It facilitates GPU-enabled parallel computing and automatic differentiation, advancements driven by recent AI developments, allowing the entire optimization process to be completed within minutes. It incorporates mixed-variable TO to generate networks with designed nodal interactions, spatially varying stiffness, and actuation strains to achieve desired morphing behaviors. Unlike existing methods that focus on single-actuation mechanisms or do not include manufacturing parameters, our approach is flexible enough to account for the diverse physics of multistimuli-responsiveness while explicitly considering the impact of the manufacturing process. This enables designers to simultaneously optimize topology and structure, tune material responsiveness to multiple stimuli, and program the manufacturing process, offering unparalleled codesign flexibility that surpasses existing partial-design approaches. We validated our framework with thermo- and photoresponsive LCE composites that we 3D printed via multimaterial DIW. We demonstrated through simulations and experiments that our methods effectively created materials capable of morphing between complex target shapes in response to either thermal or UV light stimuli.

In this study, we focus primarily on modeling axial interactions using NN-based spring elements informed by experimentally measured axial responses. We acknowledge that this simplification has limitations in capturing bending, shearing, and, in some cases, local buckling behaviors within the network. To address these effects, nonlinear beam elements (105, 106) or solid elements could serve as more comprehensive alternatives. However, implementing such formulations requires a rigorous, high-fidelity constitutive model, as demonstrated in recent studies of manufacturing platforms and material systems (45, 80). This constitutive model would need to incorporate the effects of manufacturing parameters and account for the distinct physics and mechanisms induced by multiple stimulus types. Developing such a model would require further advancements in both modeling and experimental techniques, making it an active area of ongoing research (102, 107, 108). We see this as a critical and promising direction for future work.

Although our entire design process was fully automated without any human heuristics or instructions, interesting patterns emerged. We found that our algorithm automatically categorizes edges within the material networks into two distinct groups. The first group consists of low-stiffness edges with high actuation strains, serving as the network’s actuators, while the other group is stiffer with low actuation strain, acting as the skeleton or mechanisms that translate the actuators’ motion into the target deformation. Edges in the actuation group exhibit varying levels of actuation strain depending on the type of stimulus. As a result, different actuator subgroups can either reinforce or counterbalance each other to accommodate complementary or competing demands for stimulus-specific tasks. To achieve distinct shapes under different stimuli, the roles of actuating and structural edges can also switch as stimuli change. These patterns can be seen as an analog to musculoskeletal systems or cellular cytoskeleton networks, where different active groups (i.e., muscles or molecular motors) work with supporting structures (i.e., bones or cytoskeletal filaments), enabling a range of motions and configurations. We also observed that the algorithm often converged on similar designs for the same morphing requirements, even when starting from different initial configurations. This suggests that the optimization process can reliably find optimized designs across different scenarios. Another exciting future avenue is exploring the relationship between shape-morphing capabilities and network features, such as topology, nodal degrees of freedom, and network size. Similar research has been conducted in the study of passive disordered mechanical metamaterials to achieve unique mechanical properties or enable mechanical computing (91, 97, 98).

We envision that our methods can be broadly adopted to establish design principles—some of which have already been discussed in the context of our multistimuli-responsive materials—and frameworks for programmable material systems. Our framework is not constrained to single materials or stimuli but is generalizable and applicable to broad materials and stimuli types. It enables design engineers to fully leverage the capabilities of responsive materials and advanced manufacturing processes to create morphing material systems where heterogeneities are optimized for complex tasks. We anticipate our approach enabling new avenues for multistimuli-responsive materials designed for soft robotics (7, 39, 40, 109), soft electronics (41), mechanical computing (42, 90, 97, 98), and more.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

LCE ink synthesis

neatLCE and azoLCE inks were prepared via a catalyst- and solvent-free, aza-Michael addition reaction (87). All chemicals were used as received without further purification. 1,4-bis[4-(6-acryloyloxyhexyloxy)benzoyloxy]-2-methylbenzene (Ambeed; 95%) and n-butylamine (Sigma-Aldrich; 99.5%) were added to a 20-ml scintillation vial in a 1:1 mol ratio. For the azoLCE ink, 10 wt % photoresponsive 4,4′-bis[6-(acryloyloxy)hexyloxy]azobenzene (AccelaChem; 95%) was also added. A total of 3 wt % bis(2,6-difluoro-3-(1-hydropyrrol-1-yl)phenyl)titanocene (Irgacure 784, Ambeed; 98%) served as the visible wavelength photoinitiator for both inks. The mixture was melted at 90°C while stirring at 550 rpm. Once melted, the reaction temperature was lowered to 70°C, and the vial was allowed to sit overnight in an oil bath for 18 hours. The azoLCE ink was stored for 2 days at −10°C before printing; the neatLCE ink was printed the day it finished oligomerization.

Multimaterial printing

All materials were 3D printed via multimaterial HOT-DIW (fig. S4) using a custom, high-precision gantry (AGS15000 Gantry, Aerotech Inc., Pittsburgh, PA, USA) (87). The three-axis gantry is equipped with four independent z-stages and operated by user-generated G-code and AeroBasic code (A3200, Aerotech). Ink extrusion is pneumatically controlled with digital pressure regulators (Ultimus V, Nordson EFD). Before 3D printing, LCE inks are loaded into custom-machined aluminum syringes. A 250-μm stainless steel nozzle (Tecdia, A-250350100SN-A124) was attached to the syringe via a Luer lock fixture. The syringe was loaded into a high-pressure adaptor (Nordson EFD, 7023590) and placed inside a custom-machined aluminum heating block. This block was mounted onto one gantry z-stage and maintained at 50°C by a PID temperature controller (CNi16, Omega) equipped with two cartridge heaters (Omega, CSH-102100/120V) and an RTD probe (PR-10L-3-100-1, Omega). The LCE ink was preheated at 50°C for at least 30 min before printing. For multimaterial printing, independent heating systems were set up for each material system (fig. S4), and offsets between nozzle tips were found using a manual, camera-aided process. LCE inks were 3D printed onto cleaned glass slides and photocrosslinked during printing using two 525-nm lamps (PR160L-525, Kessil Lighting) at an intensity of 58 klux. azoLCE elements were printed at pressures of 210, 280, 350, or 420 psi and speeds of 1, 5, or 10 mm/s. neatLCE elements were printed at pressures of 385, 455, 525, or 595 psi and speeds of 1, 5, or 10 mm/s. Nodal locations were marked on printed LCE networks using a gantry-mounted Sharpie marker to ensure location accuracy. 3D-printed LCE networks were photocrosslinked for at least 15 additional minutes on each side after printing.

Print path generation

A custom-written Python script was used to convert the optimized LCE network, containing node locations, topology, material information, and manufacturing parameters, into printable G-code files. Edges were grouped by print speed, and material was deposited layer by layer to reduce collisions between the nozzle and previously printed material.

Materials characterization

Printing characterization

The dimensions of LCEs printed with different printing speeds and extrusion pressures were characterized (fig. S5). Samples were printed three passes wide, three layers thick, and with a target length of 10 mm. Sample lengths and widths were measured using computer vision programs (OpenCV, Python) and verified by hand calculations (ImageJ). Their thickness was measured using calipers. Averages and SDs from five samples at each print parameter combination were then calculated and used in the computational model.

Differential scanning calorimetry

LCE inks were heated at 200°C to erase any thermal memory, cooled at −50°C, and then reheated at 200°C, all at a rate of 10°C/min (DSC250, TA Instruments).

Rheology

azoLCE and neatLCE inks were characterized using a controlled-strain rheometer (MCR 302, Anton Paar) at both room temperature and 50°C. The rheometer was equipped with an 8-mm parallel plate set at a 0.4-mm gap. Before testing, LCE inks were presheared at a constant shear rate of 0.5 s−1 for 5 min in the opposite direction of testing to prealign mesogens. Flow tests were performed to measure apparent viscosity over shear rates from 0.001 to 10,000 s−1. Oscillatory amplitude sweeps were performed at a constant frequency of 1 Hz over shear stresses from 0.1 to 100,000 Pa.

Dynamic mechanical analysis

Mechanical characterization of 3D-printed LCE samples was performed via DMA (RSA G2, TA Instruments). HOT-DIW samples were tested to gather stress-strain curves for the various print parameters and stimuli used to design shape-morphing systems. The 12 unique print pressure and print speed combinations used in the design process were tested for each ink. The stimuli tested were at room temperature, above TNI (150°C), and after UV illumination. These stress-strain curves were then fitted with NNs and used in the material spring models.

For all testing, LCE samples were 20 mm long, three passes wide, and one, two, or three layers thick for samples printed at 1, 5, and 10 mm/s, respectively, to match sample thicknesses across print speeds. The width was fixed regardless of print speed at three passes to best mimic a typical element width. Samples were clamped in square fixtures and stretched at a strain rate of 1 s−1. This strain was selected because it approximated the strain rate during thermal actuation.

Samples tested at 150°C were clamped and then enclosed in a heating chamber. They were rapidly heated to 150°C and allowed to equilibrate for 5 min before testing. UV samples were suspended in oil and exposed to 370-nm light (PR160L-370, Kessil Lighting) for 15 min before testing. Samples were stretched to failure. All print parameters and stimulus combinations were tested in triplicate.

Actuation strain measurements

3D-printed LCE samples (20 mm long, three passes wide, and three layers thick) were removed from their glass slides, covered in oil, and placed on a new glass substrate. The samples were photographed before and after thermal or photoactuation, and their pre- and postactuation lengths were measured using computer vision (OpenCV, Python) (fig. S13). Thermal actuation was conducted by placing samples on a 150°C hot plate. Photoactuation was conducted by illuminating samples with 370-nm light for 15 min (PR160L-370 Gen 2, Kessil Lighting). Thermal actuation samples were cooled at room temperature before cycling. Photoactuation samples were heated on a 150°C hot plate to induce cis-trans isomerization before cooling at room temperature and cycling. The actuation process was repeated five and three times for thermal and photoactuation, respectively. Each print parameter combination was measured in triplicate.

Experimental validation

Thermal actuation was performed well above TNI (fig. S3). Samples were placed on a well-oiled plastic film and translated between a hot plate set at 150°C and cooling at room temperature. Samples were heated for 6 min to allow for sufficient heating and cooled for 12 min. Samples were cycled five times through heating and cooling. All data were collected via video recording directly overhead of the sample.

For photoactuation, samples were actuated on a well-oiled Teflon block. Samples were photographed with a digital camera placed directly overhead before and after actuation for analysis. Photoactuation occurred via overhead illumination with a 370-nm lamp for 15 min (PR160L-370; Kessil Lighting; separating distance of ~7.5 cm). Samples were allowed to cool to below 35°C to remove the effect of photothermal actuation before data were collected. Samples were then illuminated with a 456-nm lamp for 15 min to drive cis-trans isomerization and return the material system to its original state. Samples were cycled three times to assess repeatability.

Nodal error calculations

R2 values and RMSD were calculated using the computationally predicted nodal positions as reference points and experimentally realized nodes as observations. To calculate normalized RMSD, an appropriate characteristic length, selected from the computational predictions, was required. Given the large degree of actuation and nonuniform node distribution after optimization, we defined characteristic lengths related to the global shape of each design in both its actuated and unactuated states. For unactuated states, we chose the longer side length as the characteristic length, 50 mm and 75 mm for thermoresponsive and photo- or multiresponsive systems, respectively. For actuated states, we selected the global fit parameter presented in table S3 (the longer of the two if multiple existed), specifically diameter, long diagonal, major axis, major axis, and horizontal length for the square-to-circle, shearing, circle-to-ellipse, circle-to-ellipse control, and photo- and multistimuli systems, respectively. These values were then used to normalize the RMSD used in nodal error calculations.
