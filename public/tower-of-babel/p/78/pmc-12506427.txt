Oral frailty status and influencing factors in a community-based elderly population: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Lijuan Lu, Xiaofen Liu, Hui Wang, Qian Liu, Huan Liu, Wenjing Xiang, Wenjie Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the current state and influencing factors of oral frailty in a community-based elderly population systematically.MethodsThis study retrieved eligible articles from online databases such as Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, SinoMed, CNKI and Wanfang published through July 29, 2024. Two researchers reviewed the literature, extracted data, and assessed quality. Stata18.0 was used for the meta-analysis.ResultsThis study included 13 retrieved articles containing 7,352 total patients, covering 9 influencing factors. A meta-analysis of the 13 studies revealed a 42% incidence of oral frailty (95% CI: 32–51%) in community-dwelling elderly individuals. The meta-analysis revealed 10 statistically significant influencing factors for oral frailty in the elderly population: advanced age (95% CI: 1.02–2.22), female sex (95% CI: 0.30–5.87), few remaining natural teeth and denture use (95% CI: 0.88–22.79), chronic disease history (95% CI: 1.31–2.93), physical frailty (95% CI: 1.97–4.14), poor motor function (95% CI: 0.80–2.46), living alone and eating alone (95% CI: 0.54–16.60), depression (95% CI: 1.11–2.29), smoking (95% CI: 1.98–5.28), high education level (95% CI: 0.17–0.37), high oral health score (95% CI: 0.42–1.60), and sleep quality (95% CI: 0.26–2.29).ConclusionElderly individuals have a relatively high incidence of oral frailty. Factors influencing oral frailty in elderly individuals include advanced age, female sex, low oral health score, few remaining natural teeth and denture use, chronic disease history, physical frailty, poor motor function, living alone and eating alone, depression, smoking and poor sleep quality. Early screening and intervention should be carried out to reduce the impact of adverse outcomes on patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06914-2.Keywords: Elderly, Debilitation, Oral frailty, Influencing factors, Meta-analysis

Population ageing has been recognized as a major challenge and a key medical issue [1] worldwide. Frailty is a frequent condition among elderly people and is recognized as a significant public health issue [2] because of its close link with hazards such as osteoporotic fractures, hospitalization, and death. As a unique phenotype of frailty, oral frailty refers to age-related alterations in oral conditions (e.g., tooth loss, reduced oral function, and poor oral hygiene), often accompanied by declining interest in oral health, reduced physical/mental resilience, and eating disorders, leading to overall deterioration of physical and mental function [3, 4]. A four-year longitudinal study [3] reported that oral frailty predicts frailty, long-term care demands, sarcopenia, and increased mortality. Oral frailty, which affects 17.8–55.7% of elderly individuals, has been associated with negative consequences such as cognitive impairment, malnutrition, and sarcopenia [4]. Research [5, 6] further confirms the strong predictive role of oral frailty for physical frailty, disability, mortality, and reduced quality of life. Maintaining oral function may contribute to healthier ageing and longevity [7]. The rapid growth of the ageing population imposes significant economic and social burdens on health care systems and elderly individuals. Thus, understanding the current state of oral frailty and the variables that drive it is critical for directing preventative efforts, treatments, and improvements in the oral health and quality of life of older individuals.

Methods

This study adopted the WHO standard of defining individuals aged 60 years and older as elderly individuals [1]. This systematic review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA2020) guidelines, and the protocol is registered at the international prospective register of systematic reviews PROSPERO (CRD420251056078).

Retrieval strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across seven databases (CNKI, Wanfang, PubMed, SinoMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science) from their inception through July 2024. The retrieval was carried out in both Chinese and English by combining subject and free terms. The Chinese search terms used were “elderly”, “frail”, “oral frailty”, “influencing factors”, “risk factors”, “related factors”, etc. The English search terms used were “elderly”, “aged”, “older adults”, “oral frailty”, “oral health decline”, “oral dysfunction”, “risk factors”, “influencing factors”, and “associated factors” (Table 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a cohort, case‒control, or cross-sectional study published in either Chinese or English; (2) studies of adult subjects aged ≥ 60 years; and (3) studies with the outcome indicators of factors influencing oral frailty and reliable original data reported in the literature. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies that could not provide valid data; (2) studies without detailed data information and without access to the full published text; (3) literature reviews; and (4) duplicative publications of the literature.

Literature selection and data extraction

Two researchers were responsible for screening eligible literature independently using Endnote. The extracted articles were subjected to cross-checking by two researchers. A third researcher was invited to discuss and decide in case of any disagreement in the process of literature extraction. The content of the literature extraction included the first author, publication year, sample size, study type, region, and influencing factors. Attempts were made to contact the original author in cases where the extracted information appeared incomplete. Studies were excluded if the original author could not be contacted.

Literature quality evaluation

Two investigators independently evaluated the quality of the included studies. Specifically, cohort and case‒control studies were assessed with three aspects of the NOS criteria, with a total score of 9 (low: 0–3; medium: 4–6; high: 7–9). Cross-sectional studies were assessed using the AHRQ checklist (11 items), where each “yes” response was scored as 1 point (total score: 0–11; low: 0–3, medium: 4–7, high: 8–11) [8].

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed with Stata 17.0, with the odds ratio (OR) as the statistical effect size. On the basis of the heterogeneity of the included studies, a fixed effects model was adopted if no heterogeneity was found (P > 0.1 and I2 ≤ 50%); otherwise, a random effects model was used. Furthermore, the literature with statistical heterogeneity was analysed by the source of heterogeneity, and relevant studies were excluded one by one to compare the changes in the results. For topics with ≥ 10 studies that included a certain influencing factor, a funnel plot was drawn to evaluate publication bias, combined with Egger’s test.

Results

Literature screening

During the preliminary Literature search, our initial screening identified 1157 publications, involving 253 Chinese studies and 904 English papers. After 534 duplicates were discarded and titles/abstracts were screened, 29 articles remained. The full-text review consisted of 13 papers (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow chart for the study search, selection, and identification

The prevalence of oral frailty among community-based elderly people

A meta-analysis of 13 papers was used to determine the prevalence of oral frailty. Given the heterogeneity (I2 = 98.8, p < 0.001), the random-effects model was utilized to pool effect sizes. The results revealed that the incidence of oral frailty was 42% (95% CI: 32–51%)) in the community-based elderly population. Owing to high heterogeneity (I² = 98.8%, P < 0.001), a random effects model was used (Fig. 2).

Forest map of meta-analysis on incidence of oral frailty in the community-based elderly population

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis by sex revealed an oral frailty incidence of 25% (95% CI: 20–30%) in males and 30% (95% CI: 20–30%) in females. Age-based subgroup analysis indicated that the incidence of oral frailty was 45% (95% CI: 17–85%) in individuals aged ≥ 85 years and 39% (95% CI: 23–61%) in individuals aged 74–84 years. In addition, the incidence of oral frailty was 35% (95% CI: 19–52%)) in individuals aged 65–74 years.

Sensitivity analysis

As evidenced by the sensitivity analysis, the highest incidence of oral frailty among the community-based elderly population was 43% (95% CI: 32–53%) (heterogeneity: I2 = 98.9%, p < 0.001). Moreover, the lowest incidence was 31% (95% CI: 30–32%) (heterogeneity: I2 = 98.7, p < 0.001). These results are similar to the pooled initial effect size of 42% (95% CI: 32–51%), indicating stable results in the present meta-analysis (Table 2).

Publication bias

Funnel plot analysis was performed on the 13 included articles, revealing an asymmetrical distribution of the research results. Egger’s test was applied to check the publication bias of ≥ 10 included articles, with a certain degree of publication bias in this study (t = 2.54, p = 0.028). The trim-and-fill method was used for modification, and 4 articles were added. The results revealed that the incidence of oral frailty was 30% (95% CI: 19–40%) among the community-based elderly population, which was not significantly different from the effect value of 42% (95% CI: 32–51%) reported before the trim-and-fill method; this finding supports the stable results of this meta-analysis.

Discussion

Incidence

With the rapid progression of global ageing, oral health problems are becoming notable in elderly individuals. The study revealed a significant prevalence of oral frailty (42%, 95% CI: 32–51%) among community-dwelling elderly individuals.

Factors that influence oral frailty in the community-based elderly population

Demographic factors

Advanced age and female sex have been established as significant predictors of oral frailty in community-dwelling older persons, and a high education level is a protective factor for oral frailty. Older individuals within this demographic exhibit an elevated risk of developing this oral frailty, findings that align with prior studies [22, 23]. With increasing age, elderly individuals may have weakened functions of the oral and maxillary systems (chewing, swallowing, breathing and vocalization) due to body dysfunction. There have been reports concerning an increased risk of oral frailty in the older population [24, 25]. Moreover, the physiologic quality and strength of oral muscles decrease with increasing age. Tongue pressure, lip and tongue movement functions, and chewing and swallowing functions decrease, all of which lead to oral frailty [26] (Table 3). Furthermore, this study revealed that elderly women in the community were more likely to suffer from oral frailty than elderly men were. This may be attributed to the greater proportion of women in the elderly population, the disturbance of bone metabolism and calcium loss affected by postmenopausal decreases of oestrogen levels, and the gingivae as a target organ of oestrogen. A decrease in oestrogen may induce alveolar bone osteoporosis and decrease salivary secretion, velocity, and muscle strength of the oral and maxillary systems. However, some scholars have proposed no correlation [22, 24] between sex and oral frailty in the community-based elderly population. The association between oral frailty and sex in the older community-based population is still disputed on the basis of the available data. People with higher education levels, which is usually accompanied by higher income levels, are more likely to have oral health knowledge, and may have significant differences in the accessibility of medical resources and good oral health behaviours. Considering that these analyses are based on demographic factors, special attention should be given to the elderly and female populations to develop corresponding clinical screening, prevention and intervention measures (Fig. 3).

Factors influencing oral health status

In the community-based senior population, having fewer natural teeth and increased denture use were all contributing factors to oral frailty. A high oral health score is a protective factor for oral frailty. People with low oral health scores usually have viscous saliva, perceived dry mouth, fewer natural teeth, inadequate oral cleaning, tooth pain and poor oral health behaviours. These conditions may further weaken oral motor, chewing, and swallowing functions and trigger other adverse oral conditions, thereby aggravating the occurrence of oral frailty. In a systematic review of a longitudinal study, oral health and oral frailty exhibited a significant longitudinal association, and its underlying mechanism may be attributed to low oral health scores, which may be related to nutrition, dietary choices, and chewing [27]. Moreover, the few remaining natural teeth and more dentures in the community-based elderly population can be explained by the absorption of bone tissue in the alveolar bone after denture wearing, leading to a mucosal gap between the surface of the base tissue and the bearer area and fungal colonization within the oral cavity. Denture-related diseases may develop owing to changes in the oral environment, reduced saliva volume, improper denture care, food impaction, etc [28]., eventually increasing the incidence of oral frailty. Therefore, comprehensive measures should be formulated and applied for the early detection and intervention of oral problems in the community-based elderly population. Moreover, there is a need to improve the compliance of patients wearing dentures to return to the healthcare provider on time, promote health education related to personalized denture care, and establish good denture use habits. Good oral health and good dental function are especially important for preventing oral frailty.

Physiological factors

The oral frailty of the community-based older population was influenced by a chronic medical history, a weak physique, and poor motor function. This study confirmed that concurrent diseases were a potential cause of oral frailty in the community-based elderly population. Chronic disease is common in elderly individuals and may induce oral frailty, which in turn aggravates systemic diseases. Oral frailty and systemic diseases interact and have a causal relationship, leading to adverse clinical outcomes. In a cross-sectional study including patients with a history of chronic disease and taking multiple medications, patients with diabetes mellitus and osteoporosis were prone to oral frailty, which was consistent with the results of our study [29]. Dry mouth is an adverse drug reaction and a cause of dental caries, periodontal disease, difficulty in chewing and swallowing, oral infection and other oral diseases. Patients with severe periodontal disease are more likely to suffer from oral frailty than are those with healthy gums. Dental plaque, a pathogenic factor of periodontitis, may stimulate the release of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α), activate osteoclasts, and induce alveolar bone resorption. Furthermore, the loss of bone mass may result in the loss of support for teeth, leading to tooth loosening and, consequently, tooth loss. The significantly reduced masticatory function may also cause malnutrition and further increase the occurrence of oral frailty [30, 31]. Therefore, elderly individuals with oral frailty complicated by chronic diseases should be of particular concern and provided with strategic therapeutics to manage existing diseases in an effort to delay the process of oral frailty. Oral frailty was Linked to physical frailty in a cross-sectional study of 682 people, according to pathway analysis. Consistently, elderly people with poor oral function usually avoid meat, nuts, vegetables and other nutrient-rich foods, and the mechanism of physical and oral frailty may be related to the mediating role of nutritional pathways [32]. According to this study, managing oral frailty should also involve evaluating and managing physical frailty, and developing preventative treatment strategies for oral frailty should consider physical frailty risk factors. There is a two-way relationship between poor motor function and oral frailty in elderly individuals. Elderly individuals with poor motor function often experience aggravated oral diseases owing to insufficient activity, reduced saliva secretion, weakened oral self-cleaning effects, and increased risk of bacterial breeding. Moreover, poor motor function in elderly individuals may cause increased psychological stress, leading to grinding or clenching of teeth and other bad oral habits, thereby damaging teeth and periodontal health. In the clinical setting, appropriate exercise should be employed to improve the blood circulation of the whole body and enhance oral and maxillofacial muscle function to maintain oral health and prevent and delay the occurrence of oral frailty in elderly individuals.

Lifestyle and psychological influencing factors

Oral frailty in the elderly population was found to be influenced by living alone and eating alone, melancholy, smoking, and sleep quality. In our meta-analysis, social participation factors, including living alone and eating alone, were potential contributors to the oral frailty of the studied population. Elderly individuals can establish social opportunities with others through living and eating together; however, in contrast, living alone and eating alone eliminates an important way for elderly individuals to connect with society, often accompanied by a lack of dialogue and chewing actions, less oral muscle movement and low oral function. In a prior randomized controlled trial [33], oral muscle building combined with exercise to increase the chewing load during lunch improved oral function in elderly individuals. Living alone or eating alone may also be associated with depression. A large cross-sectional study in Taiwan showed [34] that depression had a combined effect on physical weakness and oral frailty (OR = 36.81) and that depression was closely related [35–37] to smoking-related oral diseases [38, 39]. Free radicals contained in nicotine products weaken the body’s defence mechanisms and cause malignant transformation of cells, leading to smoking-related oral cancer; moreover, a decrease in proinflammatory cytokines may trigger an immunosuppressive effect, resulting in deterioration of periodontal status and an increased prevalence of caries. Raising awareness of the detrimental effects of smoking on the oral cavity and encouraging people who smoke to stop smoking are important steps in preventing oral frailty. This study also revealed favourable associations between oral health-related quality of life and sleep quality/sleep deprivation and oral health habits. Poor sleep quality may increase adverse oral behaviours, and is another explanation for poor sleep quality and physical weakness leading to oral frailty in the elderly population [40, 41]. Owing to the lack of further exploration, the relationship between sleep quality and oral function-related measures remains inconclusive.

Limitations of this study

This study included mainly cross-sectional studies carried out in China and Japan, which might affect the results in terms of regional differences such as health care systems, dietary culture, and oral health behaviours. Egger’s test suggested that this study had publication bias to some extent. The impact of publication bias on the results of this meta-analysis was evaluated using the trimming and imputation methods. Following repeated pooling analysis after trimming 4 articles, the pooled effect size after trimming and imputation did not change significantly from the initial merged effect size, indicating that publication bias had little effect on the results of this meta-analysis. Second, the included studies were too restricted to fully reflect the prevalence of oral frailty in the elderly population living in the community, as they covered only a few nations. Third, owing to the limited data included in many studies and the lack of representativeness, an insufficient number of studies were included in some groups after grouping, which might weaken the reliability of the research results. Most of the studies that included influencing factors were cross-sectional studies without prospective research or sufficient evidence strength, challenging the determination of the temporal relationship between oral frailty and influencing factors and making it difficult to infer causal relationships. Therefore, multicentre, large sample, high-quality prospective studies are needed to investigate and discuss the incidence and influencing factors of oral frailty among the community-based elderly population.

Conclusion

In summary, many factors can affect oral frailty in the community-based elderly population. When performing early screening of high-risk populations, the key screening targets are elderly people, women, elderly people living alone or eating alone, and elderly people with a history of chronic illness or depression, all of whom should be considered in routine screening for oral frailty by community medical institutions. The risk should be evaluated on the basis of oral health scores, remaining teeth, and denture usage. Meanwhile, regular oral care (at least one professional cleaning and denture adjustment per year) should be provided for individuals with few natural teeth or dentures while promoting the “20 natural teeth” target education, community brushing guidance courses, and emphasis on training in gum massage and denture cleaning methods. We propose developing and issuing a “paired dining” program for elderly individuals living alone or with depression to reduce loneliness and monitor their nutritional intake. Through lifestyle adjustments, smoking cessation support, and joint efforts with public health institutions, specialized smoking cessation services should be provided for elderly people who smoke, emphasizing the association between smoking and oral mucosal atrophy. Patients with sleep apnoea should undergo oral screening to improve the quality of life of elderly individuals with this condition. Additionally, systematic and comprehensive measures should be taken to reduce the risk of oral frailty in elderly individuals, improve their quality of life, and reduce subsequent health burdens.
