Improving outcomes in myasthenia gravis: uni-portal video-assisted thoracoscopic (UVATS) thymectomy in a single-centre retrospective cohort
Authors: A Rahim, I Samsudin, M Menon, J X Hoo, T Jayaselan, J Y Ooi, Z S Nicolas Ong, C D Sudarshan, S Krishnasamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThymectomy is a well-established treatment for myasthenia gravis (MG), with minimally invasive approaches increasingly preferred due to reduced morbidity. This study evaluates the safety and clinical outcomes of uni-portal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (UVATS) thymectomy in MG patients at a Malaysian tertiary centre.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 30 patients who underwent UVATS thymectomy for MG at Universiti Malaya Medical Centre between August 2016 and July 2025. Of these, 24 patients with complete perioperative and follow-up data were included. Outcomes assessed included postoperative complications, length of stay, disease severity by Modified Osserman classification, medication requirements, and symptomatic improvement.ResultsThe cohort (58% female, mean age 42.5 ± 18.2 years) had 50% thymoma. Postoperatively, 88% demonstrated clinical improvement. Osserman Class I proportion increased from 8% preoperatively to 75% postoperatively (p < 0.001). Corticosteroid dependency decreased from 75% to 33% (p = 0.003), pyridostigmine from 67% to 29%. Mean hospital stay was 3.4 ± 1.2 days. Complication rate was 8% (all minor), with no perioperative mortality.Conclusions“UVATS thymectomy is safe and demonstrates favorable short-term clinical outcomes in appropriately selected MG patients as the result has demonstrated significant clinical improvement, reduced medication requirements, and low morbidity. These findings support UVATS as the preferred minimally invasive approach in suitable candidates.Keywords: Myasthenia gravis, Thymectomy, VATS, Uniportal, Minimally invasive surgery

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder affecting the neuromuscular junction, characterized by fluctuating muscle weakness that worsens with activity and improves with rest [1]. Autoantibodies against nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (n-AChRs), muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), or lipoprotein-related protein 4 (LRP4) impair neuromuscular transmission, leading to the clinical manifestations of the disease [2]. The thymus gland plays a central pathogenic role, with 10–20% of MG patients having an associated thymoma [3]. Histopathological examination commonly reveals thymic hyperplasia with germinal centres in n-AChR-positive patients [4].

MG has a reported prevalence of approximately 20 per 100,000 in the United States [5], with a bimodal age distribution: females under 40 and males over 50 [6]. In Asian populations, a distinct pattern of childhood-onset MG has been described, with up to 50% of patients presenting below age 15, typically with predominant ocular symptoms [7].

We employed the Modified Osserman Classification for disease severity assessment, as this system was consistently documented in our retrospective records. While acknowledging that MGFA classification represents the current standard [reference], the Osserman system remains widely used in Asian centres and provides clinically meaningful stratification of disease severity [8].

First-line medical therapy includes acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (pyridostigmine) and immunosuppressive agents (corticosteroids). However, thymectomy has emerged as a disease-modifying intervention, indicated for patients with thymoma, non-thymomatous n-AChR-positive MG, and selected seronegative cases [1]. The landmark MGTX trial demonstrated superior outcomes with thymectomy combined with prednisone versus prednisone alone in non-thymomatous MG [9].

Several surgical approaches exist: transcervical, transsternal (median sternotomy), video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), and robotic-assisted thymectomy. Median sternotomy, historically the gold standard, provides excellent exposure but carries significant morbidity [10]. VATS has gained acceptance due to reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, faster recovery, and superior cosmetic outcomes [11, 12]. Uniportal VATS (UVATS) represents further refinement, using a single intercostal incision to minimize trauma further.

This study evaluates the clinical outcomes and safety of UVATS thymectomy in MG patients at a single Malaysian tertiary centre, contributing to the growing evidence supporting minimally invasive approaches in this challenging patient population.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective single-centre cohort study was conducted at Universiti Malaya Medical Centre (UMMC), Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, a tertiary referral hospital. The study period spanned August 2016 to July 2025. Ethics approval was obtained from the UMMC Medical Research Ethics Committee (MREC ID No: 2023125-11234). The study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cohort studies [13].

In patients with thymoma, en bloc resection was performed with careful attention to capsular integrity. The cervical extensions were routinely resected in all cases. No conversions to open surgery occurred. Of the 24 UVATS cases, 19 (79.2%) were performed via the right-sided approach and 5 (20.8%) via the left-sided approach, based on surgeon preference and tumour location.”

We did not systematically collect pain scores, analgesic requirements, or validated quality-of-life measures (e.g., MG-QoL15), limiting our ability to directly demonstrate UVATS-specific advantages over other approaches. These should be incorporated into future prospective studies.

During the study period, UVATS was the preferred surgical approach for MG patients meeting the following criteria: (1) thymoma ≤ 5 cm without radiological evidence of capsular invasion, (2) non-thymomatous MG with generalized symptoms (Osserman Class II-III), (3) BMI ≤ 30 kg/m², and (4) no prior thoracic surgery. Patients underwent median sternotomy if they had large/invasive thymomas (> 5 cm or Masaoka stage ≥ III), significant prior thoracic adhesions, or contraindications to single-lung ventilation. The UVATS cohort represents consecutive eligible patients during the study period.

Participants

30 patients who underwent UVATS thymectomy for MG at Universiti Malaya Medical Centre between August 2016 and July 2025. Of these, 24 patients with complete perioperative and follow-up data were included. Inclusion criteria were: confirmed MG diagnosis, UVATS thymectomy, and complete perioperative and follow-up data. Exclusion criteria were: incomplete medical records (> 70% missing variables), loss to follow-up, concomitant connective tissue disease, or inability to complete telephone follow-up. Median follow-up duration was 2weeks to 1 months. Neurological outcomes were assessed at the most recent follow-up visit.

Surgical technique

UVATS thymectomy

Patients were positioned in right lateral decubitus position with the left chest elevated. A single 2 cm incision was made in the 4th intercostal space at the anterior axillary line. Thoracoscopic visualization used a 10 mm 30° camera. Thymectomy was performed using standard thoracoscopic instruments, with dissection proceeding from the Right phrenic nerve to the Left Phrenic, Nerve resecting the thymus and adjacent mediastinal fat. Complete resection included the cervical extensions With removal of both the thymic horns.

Right-sided UVATS was similarly performed with the patient in left lateral decubitus position, with incision in the 4th intercostal space on the right side. Selection between left- and right-sided approaches was based on surgeon preference and anatomical considerations.

Median sternotomy

For comparison purposes, patients undergoing median sternotomy (n = 46) underwent standard midline sternal splitting with complete thymectomy and mediastinal fat resection.

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records. Missing data were obtained via structured telephone interviews conducted by trained personnel. All participants provided verbal consent for telephone follow-up; written consent was waived due to the retrospective design with anonymized data.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used: frequencies and percentages for categorical variables; means and standard deviations for continuous variables. Pre- and postoperative comparisons were analysed using McNemar’s test for paired categorical variables and paired t-tests for continuous variables. Fisher’s exact test was used for comparing complication rates between groups due to small sample sizes. All statistical tests were two-sided with α = 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Sample size

No formal sample size calculation was performed; the study included all eligible patients meeting inclusion criteria during the study period.

Patient demographics

Among 70 thymectomy patients, 30 underwent UVATS; 24 met inclusion criteria (Table 1). The cohort was predominantly female (58%, 14/24), with mean age 42.5 ± 18.2 years (range 15–83). Histopathology revealed thymoma in 50% (12/24), thymic hyperplasia in 29% (7/24), thymic cyst in 8% (2/24), and normal/atrophic thymus in 13% (3/24).

Surgical outcomes

Mean operative time was 75 min (range 55–120). There were no conversions to open surgery. Mean length of CICU stay was 1.2 ± 0.4 days, with total hospital stay 3.4 ± 1.2 days (range 2–7).

Postoperative complications

Complications occurred in 2 patients (8.3%), both minor: one pleural effusion requiring observation only, and one transient phrenic nerve irritation resolving spontaneously. There were no episodes of major haemorrhage, myasthenic crisis, prolonged ventilation, chest reopening, pneumonia, or perioperative mortality. No patients required readmission within 30 days.

Discussion

This study reports outcomes of UVATS thymectomy in a Southeast Asian MG cohort. To our knowledge, this is the first published UVATS thymectomy series from this region, where MG has distinct epidemiological features including a higher prevalence of childhood-onset disease and different antibody profiles compared to Western populations [7]. Our study contributes incremental knowledge by: (1) reporting UVATS outcomes in a multi-ethnic Asian population with both thymomatous and non-thymomatous MG, (2) providing a direct comparison with concurrent sternotomy patients treated at the same institution over the same period, and (3) quantifying medication reduction outcomes that support structured postoperative tapering protocols. The 88% clinical improvement rate and substantial medication reduction in our cohort align with and extend findings from prior UVATS series [14, 15].

Clinical improvement and disease modification

The 88% clinical improvement rate and dramatic shift from Class III (42% to 8%) to Class I (8% to 75%) confirm the therapeutic efficacy of thymectomy in MG. These results are comparable to those reported by Lashkarizadeh et al. [14], who found 80% Class I/II conversion post-VATS, and Al-Bulushi et al. [15], who reported 76% clinical improvement. The MGTX trial [9] established thymectomy’s long-term benefit in non-thymomatous MG, and our data support extending these benefits to a mixed cohort including thymoma patients.

The reduced medication dependency is clinically significant. The 56% reduction in corticosteroid use (75% to 33%) and 57% reduction in pyridostigmine (67% to 29%) translate to substantial improvements in quality of life and reduced steroid-related morbidity (osteoporosis, diabetes, infections). This finding supports incorporating post-thymectomy medication tapering protocols into MG management algorithms.

Although we did not formally quantify pain or quality-of-life outcomes, our informal patient feedback (22/24 reporting rapid return to activities) and short hospital stay (3.4 days) are consistent with the established benefits of minimally invasive surgery [11, 16]. Future studies should incorporate validated instruments such as MG-QoL15 and PROMIS-29 to better capture these patient-reported outcomes.

Surgical safety and recovery

The 8.3% complication rate and zero mortality are comparable to published VATS series (5–15% complication rate, < 1% mortality) [17, 18]. Importantly, all complications were minor and self-limiting. The short CICU stay (1.2 days) and hospital stay (3.4 days) reflect the benefits of UVATS: reduced tissue trauma, less postoperative pain, earlier mobilization, and faster recovery [11, 16, 19].

Compared to median sternotomy, UVATS demonstrated superior recovery metrics and lower complication rates, consistent with multiple systematic reviews [11, 20]. These advantages, including better cosmetic outcomes, make UVATS particularly attractive for younger MG patients with long life expectancies.

Impact of thymoma on outcomes

Although not reaching statistical significance (likely due to sample size), thymoma patients had somewhat less favorable outcomes than non-thymomatous patients: 67% vs. 92% achieving Class I postoperatively, and more residual medication requirements. This is consistent with literature showing that patients with thymic hyperplasia or early-onset MG have better remission rates [15, 21, 22]. Thymoma-related autoantibody persistence may explain slower improvement, but our data confirm UVATS is safe and effective even in thymoma patients when tumour size is appropriate (< 5 cm).

Comparison with robotic-assisted thymectomy

While robotic-assisted thymectomy offers excellent visualization and precision, our UVATS results (operative time 75 min, hospital stay 3.4 days, complication 8.3%) compare favourably to robotic series (operative time 100–150 min, hospital stay 2–4 days, complications 5–12%) [23, 24]. UVATS offers comparable outcomes with lower cost and wider accessibility, particularly important in resource-limited settings [25].

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the small sample size (n = 24) limits statistical power and subgroup analyses. Second, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias; patients selected for UVATS likely had less complex disease (smaller thymomas, better performance status) than sternotomy patients. Third, the lack of standardized outcome instruments (e.g., MG-QoL15, QMG score) limits comparability with future studies. Fourth, follow-up duration was heterogeneous (range 6 months to 9 years), and long-term remission rates cannot be accurately determined. Fifth, single-centre design may limit generalizability to other practice settings.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes real-world evidence supporting UVATS thymectomy in a Southeast Asian population, where such data are scarce.

Clinical implications

UVATS thymectomy should be considered the preferred surgical approach for MG patients meeting criteria for thymectomy when technically feasible.

Future directions

Multicentre prospective studies with standardized outcome measures, including quality of life instruments (MG-QoL15) and quantitative MG scores, are needed. Long-term follow-up (> 5 years) is essential to assess sustained remission and recurrence rates. Comparative effectiveness research between UVATS, robotic-assisted, and sternotomy in well-matched cohorts would further define optimal surgical strategies for different MG subgroups [26].

Conclusion

UVATS thymectomy is safe and demonstrates favorable short-term clinical outcomes in appropriately selected MG patients, with significant improvement in disease severity, substantial reduction in medication requirements, and low perioperative morbidity. These findings support UVATS as a viable minimally invasive surgical option for MG patients meeting thymectomy criteria.

While our results are encouraging, the retrospective, non-comparative design precludes definitive conclusions about efficacy. Prospective comparative studies with standardized outcome measures (MGFA-PIS, QMG scores, MG-QoL15) and longer follow-up are needed to establish UVATS as the preferred approach over other minimally invasive techniques. For now, sternotomy remains indicated for complex cases including large or invasive thymomas.
