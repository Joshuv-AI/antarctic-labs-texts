Beyond technical skills: How communication shapes patient satisfaction in Ghana's healthcare system
Authors: Mohammed Saani Abdulai, Eliasu Mumuni, Muhammed Abdulai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEffective healthcare delivery depends not only on clinical expertise but also on the quality of communication and interpersonal relationships between healthcare providers and patients. Despite the recognized importance of these human-centered factors, limited research has explored their impact on patient satisfaction and service quality within the Ghanaian context. This study aims to examine the influence of communication competence and interpersonal relationships on patient satisfaction. This study examines how healthcare providers' communication competence and interpersonal relationships influence their perceived patient satisfaction with service delivery, thereby shifting attention from technical skills to the relational aspects of care.MethodsA sequential mixed-methods design was employed. The quantitative phase involved a survey of 250 healthcare professionals. The qualitative phase comprised in-depth interviews with 20 participants,10 patients, and 10 healthcare staff. Data was analyzed using multiple regression techniques for the quantitative component and thematic analysis for the qualitative component.ResultsVerbal communication emerged as the most used method (70.4 %), followed by a combination of verbal and non-verbal strategies (19.6 %). Regression analysis revealed that both communication competence and interpersonal relationships had a significant impact on service quality and patient satisfaction. While interpersonal relationships displayed a negative coefficient in the regression model (B = −0.098) due to overlap with communication competence, qualitative findings confirmed that positive interpersonal interactions enhanced patient satisfaction. While technical skills contributed a smaller increase of 0.029. The Qualitative findings revealed that patients highly value empathetic communication, timely interactions, and non-verbal cues, such as physical touch and facial expressions, which enhance trust and satisfaction.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the importance of integrating communication and interpersonal skills development into healthcare training and professional practice in Ghana. Strengthening these competencies can enhance patient experiences, improve health outcomes, and contribute to the overall quality of care delivery.Keywords: Communication, Health communication, Health service delivery, Patient satisfaction, Mixed methods

Introduction

Communication is a cornerstone of human interaction, shaping relationships, conveying emotions, and facilitating information exchange across diverse contexts. In healthcare, effective communication remains central to patient satisfaction, treatment adherence, and care quality [1], [2], [3]. Clear, empathetic exchanges between healthcare providers and patients foster understanding and trust that directly influence outcomes. However, most studies on healthcare communication originate from high-income countries (HICs), where cultural norms, institutional capacity, and resources differ markedly from those in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [[4], [5], [6], [7]]). In LMICs such as Ghana, communication dynamics are complicated by linguistic diversity, limited interpretation services, and cultural expectations that shape patient engagement [8,9,10].

Within Ghana's multilingual health system, language mismatches between providers and patients often result in misunderstandings, reduced satisfaction, and poor outcomes [11,12]. Although both English and local languages like Dagbani, Akan, and Ewe are used, the absence of trained interpreters means patients frequently rely on unqualified mediators, increasing the risk of errors in diagnosis and treatment [13], [14], [15]. Studies from similar contexts confirm that professional interpretation and culturally adapted communication improve safety and satisfaction [16], [17],18].

Communication also encompasses non-verbal cues such as tone, eye contact, gestures, and posture, which strongly influence perceptions of empathy and attentiveness [19], [20], [21]. Non-verbal behaviors have been found to contribute significantly to patient satisfaction, often serving as indicators of compassion and respect [[22], [23], [24], [25]. When verbal and non-verbal cues align, they enhance rapport and perceived care quality; when inconsistent, they erode trust [[22], [24].

These findings, however, are largely derived from HICs with advanced infrastructure and smaller patient loads [17,22,23]. In LMICs, communication barriers are compounded by linguistic, cultural, and systemic constraints [[10], [26], [27]]. Research across African contexts identifies hierarchical care relationships, time pressure, and cultural distance between providers and patients as key barriers to effective communication [7], [28], [29], [30]. For example, consultations in English or Akan often marginalize patients fluent only in Dagbani or Ewe, forcing reliance on informal interpreters and non-verbal signals.

Socioeconomic and institutional factors further influence communication quality. Differences in gender, education, and class between providers and patients often shape perceptions of respect and empathy [3,10,17]. Hierarchical cultures, limited time, and staff shortages in public hospitals constrain meaningful interaction [6,8,31]. Yet, evidence shows that structured team communication and feedback systems improve both collaboration and patient satisfaction [8,31]. This indicates that communication in LMIC healthcare should be understood not only as an interpersonal skill but also as a systemic issue requiring organizational attention [10].

Non-verbal communication is particularly critical in multilingual and multicultural environments where verbal comprehension may be limited. Ghanaian patients often interpret empathy, respect, or indifference through providers' expressions, gestures, and tone [[29], [32], [33]]. Misinterpreting these cues can create tension or mistrust, while culturally sensitive use of non-verbal behavior enhances rapport and understanding. Despite its significance, formal training in non-verbal communication remains minimal in Ghana's medical education [11,20,30], leaving a critical gap in healthcare practice.

Overall, the literature establishes that both verbal and non-verbal communication are essential to quality care, yet their interplay in LMICs, especially Ghana, is underexplored. While HIC studies emphasize empathy and patient-centered dialogue, Ghanaian healthcare must also navigate systemic issues such as linguistic diversity, resource shortages, and low health literacy [8,10,18,34]. Empirical evidence examining these multidimensional factors is limited, underscoring the need for context-specific research.

Accordingly, this study examines how healthcare providers' communication competence and interpersonal behaviors influence patient satisfaction within Ghana's tertiary hospital context. Specifically, it aims to: (1) assess how provider communication and relational dynamics shape patient satisfaction at Tamale Teaching Hospital, and (2) investigate how contextual factors such as time, language, and institutional responsiveness moderate perceived service quality. These insights are expected to inform contextually relevant communication strategies and training frameworks for improving patient-centered care in Ghana's healthcare system.

Theoretical underpinning

This study is informed by two complementary theories: Peplau's Interpersonal Relations Theory and the SERVQUAL Model of Service Quality. These frameworks collectively provide a rigorous foundation for understanding how verbal and non-verbal communication influence patient satisfaction within complex healthcare systems particularly in resource-constrained, multilingual contexts like Ghana. The choice of these theories aligns directly with the study's dual objectives: (1) to examine how healthcare providers' communication competence and interpersonal relationships influence patient satisfaction, and (2) to identify the contextual factors that moderate this relationship, such as time, language, and institutional responsiveness.

Peplau's interpersonal relations theory

Peplau's Interpersonal Relations Theory conceptualizes healthcare as a dynamic, relational process grounded in therapeutic communication. The theory outlines four interrelated phases—orientation, identification, exploitation, and resolution, each offering a lens for analyzing patient-provider interactions in clinical settings [35].

•Orientation Phase: In this initial phase, patients recognize the need for care and are introduced to the provider. Effective communication during orientation is foundational for establishing trust and emotional safety, particularly in Ghana, where linguistic barriers and hospital hierarchies can disempower patients [32]. Our findings revealed that patients who experienced clear introductions and warm greetings were significantly more likely to report satisfaction, aligning with the importance of this phase.

•Identification Phase: Here, the patient begins to align with the provider as a trusted caregiver. Our qualitative data showed that when providers actively listened, maintained eye contact, or used gestures to explain procedures, patients felt respected and acknowledged—facilitating emotional alignment and shared goals [36]. These actions were particularly important for participants with limited formal education or low health literacy.

By applying Peplau's theory, this study demonstrates that each phase of the therapeutic relationship can be strengthened or disrupted through communication. Moreover, these stages reflect the deeply relational and cultural nature of care in Ghana's healthcare settings.

SERVQUAL model of service quality

The SERVQUAL model [37] complements Peplau's relational framework by providing a structured approach to evaluating perceived service quality through five dimensions: tangibles, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy. This model is especially relevant to the study's focus on patient satisfaction and the contextual dynamics of provider-patient communication.

•Tangibles: These include physical cues such as provider appearance, hospital infrastructure, and visible cleanliness. While not a central variable in this study, patients noted that tangible cues reinforced their perceptions of professionalism, indirectly supporting satisfaction judgments.

SERVQUAL's emphasis on responsiveness and empathy was particularly instructive in interpreting our findings. The model illuminated how provider behavior is interpreted through both verbal and non-verbal cues, which in turn are shaped by systemic constraints like patient load, language diversity, and provider training. Notably, our regression model showed that communication competence had a stronger impact on satisfaction than technical skill, reinforcing SERVQUAL's view that service quality hinges not just on what is done, but how it is done.

Integrated theoretical justification

The integration of Peplau's Interpersonal Relations Theory with the SERVQUAL Model offers a robust dual-theoretical foundation for this study's mixed-methods approach. Peplau's theory, rooted in relational and experiential processes, aligns seamlessly with the qualitative component of the research, which seeks to understand how patients and providers interpret communication encounters within Ghana's multilingual and culturally embedded clinical context. Through its focus on stages such as orientation, identification, exploitation, and resolution, Peplau's framework enables an exploration of the emotional, psychological, and cultural dimensions of communication that shape patient satisfaction on an interpersonal level [35].

Conversely, the SERVQUAL model supports the quantitative strand by offering a structured means of evaluating measurable service quality dimensions, particularly empathy, responsiveness, and assurance. These dimensions correspond closely with the study's survey measures and statistical indicators, including communication competence, interpersonal relationships, and satisfaction scores. SERVQUAL's emphasis on patients' expectations versus their perceived experiences allows for a nuanced analysis of the service delivery gaps identified in the regression models [22,37].

Together, these frameworks enable a coherent integration of qualitative depth and quantitative breadth. Peplau facilitates the interpretation of lived experiences and meaning-making within patient-provider relationships, while SERVQUAL provides a replicable structure for assessing satisfaction from a service quality perspective. This dual-theoretical synthesis enhances the internal consistency and explanatory power of the study, supporting both the interpretive aims and statistical rigor of the research design ([38], [39]). It further underscores the relevance of communication as both a relational process and a measurable determinant of patient satisfaction, particularly in the complex healthcare environments of low- and middle-income countries such as Ghana.

Research methodology

Study location

The study was conducted at a strategically significant healthcare facility serving northern Ghana and neighboring countries in 2020. Tamale Teaching Hospital (TTH) serves as the main referral center for Ghana's Five Northern Regions (Northern, North East, Upper East, Upper West, and Savanna), while also receiving patients from Togo and Burkina Faso. As one of Ghana's five major teaching hospitals, TTH maintains a diverse team of healthcare professionals (doctors, nurses, midwives, specialists) and records one of the nation's highest outpatient volumes. This makes TTH an ideal setting for examining healthcare communication dynamics in northern Ghana.

Study design and methods

This paper adopted a sequential explanatory mixed methods design, which involves the initial collection and analysis of quantitative data, followed by a qualitative phase designed to explain or build upon the quantitative findings [38], [39]. This design was purposefully selected to provide both statistical generalizability and contextual understanding, thereby capturing the complex, human-centered phenomenon of healthcare communication in a multilingual, resource-limited setting like Ghana.

The quantitative phase enabled the identification of relationships between communication competence, interpersonal relationships, and patient satisfaction. However, statistical significance alone could not fully explain the lived realities of patients and healthcare providers navigating these interactions. The second, qualitative phase conducted through semi-structured interviews was therefore essential for uncovering deeper insights into how communication is perceived and enacted across various clinical encounters [40], [41].

This explanatory strategy aligns with the principles of connective complementarity, where the qualitative component enriches and contextualizes quantitative outcomes, rather than serving as an independent strand [42], [43]. Additionally, this approach supports methodological triangulation, enhancing the internal validity of the findings by integrating multiple forms of evidence across paradigms [44].

The study included patients aged 18 years and above who were in stable condition and not classified as critical. Patients were excluded from participation if they were under the age of 18, mentally unstable, or in critical condition, to ensure that participants could provide informed consent and engaging fully in the research process.

Quantitative survey, measurement, and analysis

This paper employed rigorous quantitative methods to assess healthcare providers' communication practices. We employed simple random sampling and distributed 250 questionnaires among healthcare professionals at Tamale Teaching Hospital. A comprehensive list of staff was obtained from departmental heads and used as a sampling frame. From this list, participants were randomly selected using a lottery method to ensure that every eligible individual had an equal chance of been selected, achieving 100 % completion from doctors, nurses, midwives, pharmacists, and laboratory technicians. Eligibility required participants to: (1) be ≥18 years old, (2) hold professional roles, and (3) voluntarily consent.

To ensure representativeness, we used a simple random sampling technique. A complete list of eligible healthcare providers (including doctors, nurses, midwives, pharmacists, and laboratory technicians) was compiled from departmental rosters. Participants were then selected through a lottery method, guaranteeing that each eligible respondent had an equal probability of selection. The survey achieved a 100 % response rate from the selected participants. This approach yielded robust data on communication practices, which were analyzed using SPSS through descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) and multiple linear regression.

We used structured questionnaires that contained 28 items across four sections: demographic details, communication competence, interpersonal relationships, and service satisfaction. Items were measured using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree). The tool was developed based on prior literature and contextualized for the Ghanaian setting through expert review. The survey was administered in English using paper-based forms. Participants were provided with a dedicated space and an average of 30 min during less busy work periods to complete the survey.

Qualitative approach and sampling

The qualitative aspect of our research adopts a phenomenological approach, specifically drawing on hermeneutic phenomenology as articulated by Max van Manen. This methodology is particularly suitable for examining how individuals experience and interpret phenomena, in this case, communication between patients and healthcare providers within a specific socio-cultural and institutional context [46,47].

Phenomenology is widely recognized for its strength in uncovering deep insights into human experience, moving beyond surface descriptions to elicit how people make meaning of their interactions [48]. Given that healthcare communication is embedded with emotional, ethical, and cultural nuances, the phenomenological lens enables a rigorous exploration of how these factors shape patient satisfaction and provider behavior in real-world settings [49,50].

Our selection of van Manen's hermeneutic phenomenology allows for the integration of both descriptive accounts and interpretive reflection, capturing the multilayered nature of clinical communication [47]. This is especially relevant in the Ghanaian healthcare context, where non-verbal cues, language barriers, and relational expectations significantly influence perceptions of care [6,51].

To ensure methodological transparency and trustworthiness, we followed van Manen's key procedural steps: turning to the lived experience, collecting experiential descriptions through semi-structured interviews, isolating thematic structures, and reflecting on the essential meaning of experiences through iterative writing [46,47].

The 20 participants (10 patients and 10 healthcare providers) were drawn from five key wards at Tamale Teaching Hospital Medical, Surgical, Emergency, Trauma and Orthopaedic, and Outpatient Department (OPD). Inclusion criteria for patients required them to be: (1) aged 18 or older, (2) admitted for at least 48 h to ensure sufficient clinical interaction, and (3) mentally and physically able to participate in an interview. Healthcare providers were selected based on professional roles (doctor, nurse, pharmacist, midwife, lab technician), work experience, and willingness to reflect critically on their communication practices.

The interviews were conducted in private hospital spaces to uphold ethical standards of confidentiality and comfort. Interviewers were trained postgraduate researchers who followed a semi-structured guide informed by the study objectives and previous literature. This guide was pretested for relevance and clarity. Thematic saturation was observed after 18 interviews, but we continued to 20 participants to ensure comprehensive coverage of experiential perspectives. Thematic saturation was operationalized as the point at which no new codes or themes emerged across two consecutive interviews, with saturation reached after the eighth participant for both patients and healthcare providers.

This sampling strategy aligns with hermeneutic phenomenology's emphasis on the depth and variation of lived experience, providing rich, contextual insights into how communication unfolds across multiple clinical contexts within a multilingual, resource-constrained hospital environment.

Qualitative data analysis and theme identification

Interviews were transcribed using Turbo Scribe and analyzed through content analysis and constant comparison. All interviews were conducted primarily in English. However, participants who preferred using local expressions in Dagbani or Twi were allowed to do so. These segments were translated into English by the lead researcher, who is fluent in the language. To ensure transcript fidelity, all transcriptions produced through Turbo Scribe were reviewed against the original audio recordings to verify accuracy and preserve contextual meaning. Voyant Tools Software (VTS) facilitated theme identification by extracting frequently used words. Results and analysis

This paper analyzed results quantitatively and qualitatively, examining the impact of communication on healthcare at Tamale Teaching Hospital. It explored communication forms, barriers, and insights using a mixed-methods approach.

Demographic characteristics of respondents

The selection of participants for this paper is based on essential demographic characteristics that align with the study's primary aim. These demographic factors are considered vital for assessing the communication outcomes among the respondents.

Most of the participants were male (63.6 %), in the 21–30 years group (69.6 %), and Muslim (69.2 %), followed by Christian (30.8 %). (Table 1). The overwhelming male majority of this study also presents a clear case of gender disparities in healthcare institutions (Prather et al., 2020).

Forms of communication

The study explored communication methods at Tamale Teaching Hospital (TTH), revealing that 70.4 % (176 respondents) relied on verbal communication, 5.6 % (14) used non-verbal cues, and 4.0 % (10) preferred written communication. Additionally, 49 participants combined multiple methods, while only one (0.4 %) used visual communication. The dominance of verbal communication aligns with Nouri and Rudd [52], who highlight its key role in effective healthcare interactions and outcomes.

Our study found that the second most common communication method combines verbal and nonverbal cues, reinforcing Wisner's (1999) model, which attributes 10 % of communication to words, 40 % to delivery, and 50 % to nonverbal signals. The Joint Commission (2018) also emphasizes that integrating both forms enhances patient-provider interactions.

Interpersonal relationship demonstrated a statistically significant negative beta coefficient (B = −0.098, β = −0.319, t = −6.996, p < .001). This counterintuitive result likely reflects multicollinearity with communication competence, as shown by the variance inflation factors (VIF = 2.53) and tolerance levels (0.395). In practice, qualitative findings still emphasized the importance of interpersonal warmth and relational care, but the statistical suppression effect in the model reduced its apparent contribution when communication competence was simultaneously considered.

Technical competence had a positive but statistically non-significant relationship with patient satisfaction (B = 0.029, β = 0.061, t = 1.109, p = .268). This suggests that, within this sample, technical skills alone did not significantly influence satisfaction after accounting for communication-related factors.

The multicollinearity diagnostics (Tolerance >0.39, VIF < 2.6) indicate no severe multicollinearity, though moderate overlap between communication competence and interpersonal relationship warrants cautious interpretation. These findings collectively highlight that while all three dimensions matter, communication competence remains the most critical determinant of patient satisfaction in this setting, both statistically and in practical care delivery.

Qualitative analysis

This section presents the qualitative insights derived from the study, which complement and enrich the quantitative findings.

Forms of communication

Several participants in the key in-depth interview sessions expressed that:

“Some of them, when they come in the morning, they will touch you touch your head to see if your body temperature is high” (Patient at Trauma and Orthopaedic ward).

Some patients feel reassured when healthcare providers engage physically, such as taking their temperature or conducting exams, reinforcing their sense of care and the significance of their visit. This enhances therapeutic satisfaction and fosters dignity and respect [53]. Interviewees also shared experiences with verbal and nonverbal communication.

“The verbal and nonverbal communication is very good when it comes to communicating with patients That's why I use them. Every Doctor is taught basic communication in medical school, how to interact orally and nonverbally with a patient in spoken languages. You can be an excellent doctor, but if you cannot communicate well, it can lead to many problems”(Doctor at Trauma unit).

Healthcare professionals highlighted the importance of both verbal and non-verbal communication in their interactions with patients, as it plays a crucial role in improving the quality of healthcare delivery.

Time as a component of service quality

One key theme that emerged from the study was the issue of time. Both the waiting time and the length of stay in the ward were seen as important aspects of service quality. Patients viewed time as a critical factor influencing the quality of care in healthcare institutions, including Tamale Teaching Hospital. Reflecting this sentiment, one patient remarked that.

“It is worrying to me because sometimes, before Covid, you go to OPD and see the queue alone, you feel like going home, and the doctors will not come to work on time. If not because of illness, what will I be doing here? They should come to work early because time is very important” (Patient at Female Ward).

The waiting time here is too much to see a doctor. It takes you hours most of the time to see them aside from the processes you need to follow at the OPD to see them. At the labs too, it takes so much time and delays everything (Patient at OPD).

The respondents' perspective brings attention to a critical concern for patients, as the prolonged process of obtaining healthcare consumes valuable time and discourages many from seeking necessary care. A significant number of patients expressed dissatisfaction with their hospital visits, highlighting management's failure to ensure timely attendance by healthcare providers. This issue also emphasizes the high patient-to-provider ratio in Northern Ghana, leading to inadequate care and contributing to the overall dissatisfaction with healthcare services.

Another patient lamented that.

“You will come to this hospital very early in the morning, hoping to get care fast, but it's never so because all sections seem to be delaying everything you will always have to wait a long time. It's bad for a hospital like this” (Patient at OPD).

Respondents highlighted long hospital wait times as a major concern, causing frustration and prompting calls for management action.

In this context, the analysis identified time as a key factor influencing satisfaction with healthcare service delivery.

Considering this, another patient commented.

“Some of the nurses or doctors, when they come to my bed, they are mostly in rash to go away….. I think some of them do not have time to listen” (Patient at Trauma and orthopaedic Ward).

The analysis reveals that delays by healthcare providers pose significant challenges, leaving many patients dissatisfied with the time spent on their care. Comparative data also highlights staff performance at Tamale Teaching Hospital (TTH) as a key factor in healthcare quality and patient satisfaction. Interviewees emphasized staff conduct as crucial in shaping their overall experience.

“You see, health workers need to work hard so that they can perform all the necessary tasks while on duty; some of the nurses, when they come, instead of work they will be on their phones. I think they should always be monitored”. (Patient in the Female Ward).

The respondent's perspective suggests that service improvement is seen as a fundamental factor in both care quality and patient satisfaction. It also emphasizes the importance of healthcare providers performing their duties to the best of their abilities. Notably, an intriguing point is the distracting nature of technological devices used by healthcare staff at TTH. In this context, service quality is achieved through continuous improvements across various hospital departments, which is reflected in the attitudes of the service providers.

In addition to this, one respondent stated that:

“The leaders have to do something about the behaviour of some nurses…. They don't respect” (Patient in the Trauma and Orthopaedic Ward).

Management must ensure healthcare professionals provide socio-psychological support, as respect is key to care quality. Thus, patient satisfaction is largely influenced by healthcare providers' ability to respect patients.

Perceived satisfaction with service delivery

Customer satisfaction is fundamental to the utilization of any service. In the healthcare context, how patients feel about their interactions with providers can reflect the providers' effectiveness and skill in delivering care. It is essential for healthcare professionals to enhance both their technical abilities and interpersonal skills to better serve their patients. To gather insights into this topic, interviewees were asked about their perceptions of care satisfaction during the interviews. A nurse from the ward shared her observations in this regard.

“Patient satisfaction, I will say, is of different levels, and communication is essential…..so the satisfaction may vary….you know, from one patient to another. Some of them, I think, might not be satisfied with our service since some of us will not take our time to explain things to them, mmm, for example, we don't normally explain the conditions to them, and we don't even tell them the side effect of their drugs. This can cause our patients to have problems with our service” (Nurses in Surgical Ward).

The responses indicate that effective communication plays a vital role in healthcare delivery and patient satisfaction.

Likewise, patients were inquired about their satisfaction with the services provided, and one patient in the ward expressed their concerns,

“Yes, I am pleased with the way the nurse has treated me. When I was first brought in by my relative, I was unable to speak… but now I am able to talk to you.” (Patient in Female Ward).

Some admitted patients, especially those who recovered from critical conditions, reported high satisfaction, emphasizing the importance of technical care quality over provider demeanor.

“They needed to bring in specialists from different departments to assist with the surgery” (Patient in the Trauma unit).

This highlights that, in terms of technical care, some doctors rely on their colleagues for support during procedures at the hospital. The analysis shows that healthcare providers at TTH collaborate and seek technical assistance from one another to improve service delivery. Additionally, in urgent cases, healthcare professionals work together to address the situation, ultimately enhancing patient satisfaction through their teamwork.

When participants were questioned about their perceived satisfaction with the services at TTH, one patient expressed their concerns.

“I have two doctors attending to me here, and I am extremely satisfied with their care. They are providing excellent treatment and handling my condition very well.” (Patient at the Female Ward).

The statement highlights the respondents' high level of satisfaction with the technical skills of the healthcare providers.

A question regarding interpersonal relationships with patients was raised, and one patient recounted their experience.

“When they talk to you, some of the nurses here feel very happy; even when they are going to undertake a procedure and you are painting, they will not even mind you. Still, others will say sorry before everything” (Patient at the Emergency Ward).

The interview responses revealed that, some healthcare providers are aware of the importance of interpersonal relationships with their patients. However, the responses also highlighted that certain healthcare providers may not exhibit a compassionate attitude in their roles. This suggests a direct connection between patient satisfaction and the quality of interpersonal relationships fostered by healthcare professionals.

When asked about their satisfaction with the healthcare providers' service delivery during the interviews, some patients had additional comments regarding effective communication.

“Most of them sometimes came and inspired me. They will tell me that there are still more chances I can walk again, don't give up, and sometimes it strengthens my spirit. Then some can come and tell me that others came with problems even more than this, but with God's grace, they were able to walk out of it successfully, so with the same grace that was able to sustain them will also help me walk again” (Patient at Trauma and Orthopaedic Ward).

Effective communication in healthcare conveys treatment plans while boosting patient confidence, morale, and emotional support. Tertiary hospital professionals in Ghana should prioritize patient engagement, as appropriate dialogue serious or light-hearted can ease concerns and aid psychological healing. A positive provider demeanor enhances recovery, especially for those with chronic pain.

But some participants expressed that.

“I am not happy because they are not discharging me…. I want to go home I'm now fine, yesterday when they came, they said they would discharge me, and they are not discharging me… this afternoon, the same story” (Patient in Female Ward).

Respondents noted that while patients may appreciate healthcare providers' technical skills, dissatisfaction arises from prolonged stays and treatment by inexperienced students. The qualitative data further provided further insights into service concerns.

“Maybe that might be today because I was expecting to go to the theatre for my second surgery, so sometimes they will tell you that you are not supposed to eat or eee certain times…. So, I was here when from 8 am to 12 pm. One of the doctors came out from the theatre and told me they were running short of oxygen, so they could not send me in. So, it meant I would not be going today, so I was just here when they came and picked up some patients and sent them in. Then they told me we were running short of oxygen. Meanwhile, some of them used oxygen when they entered” (Patients in Trauma Ward).

Interviews revealed patient dissatisfaction when treatments are postponed for minor reasons, causing distress and affecting well-being.

To elaborate on this point, the patient also mentioned that;

“I felt really unwell today; my body was overheated. I was upset and felt quite disappointed.” (Patient at Trauma Ward).

This further emphasizes that when patients are disappointed with their treatment plans, their health can worsen. At the tertiary hospital in Ghana, numerous patients may voice their dissatisfaction with the services offered due to some healthcare professionals not keeping to the scheduled appointment times with them.

The qualitative analysis revealed that English-speaking patients who understand the communication from healthcare providers may still feel dissatisfied with certain comments made by those providers.

At times, their words can be disheartening, yet I maintain my faith that God will assist me. It's just that they express their opinions based on medical considerations (Patient in trauma and orthopaedic Ward).

Findings show that some healthcare providers in Ghana's tertiary hospitals fail to uphold ethical communication, discouraging patient adherence to treatment and causing dissatisfaction. Some patients adopt a fatalistic view, relying on divine intervention, while fear-inducing remarks from providers further reduce satisfaction.

Discussion

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

The regression results indicate that communication competence is the most significant predictor of patient satisfaction, while interpersonal relationships displayed a negative coefficient despite qualitative evidence underscoring its value. This apparent contradiction is best understood through methodological triangulation. Interviews revealed that patients consistently valued empathy, respect, and friendliness from providers, describing these relational elements as “making me feel cared for” and “reducing my fear.” However, in the quantitative model, many of these relational qualities were already embedded within items measuring communication competence (e.g., active listening, respectful tone). This statistical overlap, known as a suppression effect, likely reduced the measured impact of interpersonal relationships when communication competence was included in the same model.

Moreover, qualitative accounts highlighted that poor communication, such as failure to explain diagnoses or treatment, could diminish the benefits of even warm interpersonal behavior. In other words, patients appreciated friendliness, but without clear and effective information exchange, their overall satisfaction remained low. This aligns with evidence from LMIC settings showing that while relational warmth enhances comfort, information clarity and communicative competence are decisive in shaping satisfaction [33].

These integrated findings suggest that interventions should not treat interpersonal relationships and communication competence as interchangeable skills. Instead, they should be developed as complementary but distinct dimensions of patient-centered care, ensuring that warmth is matched with clear, accurate, and culturally attuned communication.

These findings affirm the SERVQUAL model [37], particularly its dimensions of empathy and assurance, which are reflected in how patients perceive provider attentiveness, emotional support, and clear information exchange. The evidence suggests that even the most technically competent care can be undermined if not accompanied by effective and compassionate communication.

Healthcare providers in this study predominantly used verbal communication (70.4 %), often complemented by non-verbal cues (19.6 %) such as gestures, physical touch, and facial expressions. Patients reported greater satisfaction when providers employed both verbal explanations and supportive non-verbal behaviors. This aligns with Wisner's (1999) communication model and subsequent work by Bhattacharyya and Benbow (2024), who underscore the importance of non-verbal signals in healthcare settings.

In linguistically diverse environments such as Tamale Teaching Hospital (TTH), non-verbal communication served as a critical bridge when language barriers hindered verbal understanding. These findings support the integration of non-verbal communication training into healthcare education, particularly in multicultural and multilingual healthcare systems.

The qualitative findings highlighted several barriers that adversely affect communication and, by extension, patient satisfaction. Chief among these were language disparities, long waiting times, delayed discharge processes, and unclear or rushed interactions. Patients often felt frustrated when communication was limited or when updates on their care were inconsistent, leading to perceptions of neglect or disrespect.

These communication breakdowns reflect deeper systemic issues and align with Peplau's Theory of Interpersonal Relations (1991–1997), which posits that effective clinical relationships develop over time through stages of orientation, identification, and resolution. In contexts where patient-provider interactions are brief or transactional, these relational stages are interrupted, weakening the therapeutic alliance.

Patients frequently identify excessive waiting times, whether for consultations, procedures, or discharges, as a major source of dissatisfaction. These findings are consistent with studies by Hwang et al. [54] and Al-Harajin et al. [55], which identified time management as a crucial determinant of perceived healthcare quality. Moreover, delayed discharges and perceived favoritism were interpreted as signs of inequity and unfairness, echoing concerns raised by Salehiparsa et al. (2019) on how favoritism compromises fairness in healthcare systems.

In such situations, some patients expressed discontent with the communication they received, particularly when procedures were postponed without clear explanation. The emotional toll of these delays was compounded by inadequate provider updates, reinforcing the importance of transparent, empathetic communication even in the face of unavoidable system constraints.

The findings also highlight that patients value not just clinical outcomes, but the quality of interpersonal relationships they develop with healthcare providers. Respondents expressed greater satisfaction when care was delivered with warmth, reassurance, and compassion, hallmarks of high relational competence. These perceptions align with prior research showing that empathy and respectful engagement are strongly associated with patient satisfaction [56,50].

Conversely, patients described a sense of detachment or disengagement when providers failed to communicate clearly, lacked patience, or dismissed their concerns. Some participants reported that staff appeared more focused on their devices than on direct engagement, further eroding trust and satisfaction.

Although technical competence alone had a weaker statistical association with satisfaction, it remained important, particularly among patients who underwent complex procedures or experienced major clinical improvements. For these individuals, satisfaction was derived primarily from the outcome of care, even when communication was suboptimal. This aligns with findings from Ostrem et al. [57] and Al-Daoar & Munusamy [58], who note that technical quality remains a significant, though insufficient, determinant of satisfaction in hospital settings.

These findings suggest that technical and relational competence are not mutually exclusive but rather interdependent. While excellent clinical care forms the backbone of service delivery, it must be complemented by effective communication practices to ensure a holistic and satisfying experience for patients.

Conclusion

Effective communication serves as the cornerstone of quality healthcare delivery and patient satisfaction in tertiary hospital settings. The study's mixed-methods approach demonstrated that patient satisfaction scores increased by 0.354 units with improved communication competence, while strong interpersonal relationships contributed an additional 0.098-unit increase (p < .05). These findings validate that both verbal communication (used by 70.4 % of providers) and complementary non-verbal strategies (19.6 %) are essential for addressing patient concerns, particularly in Ghana's multilingual healthcare environment. Healthcare systems must therefore prioritize communication skills with the same rigor as technical training. Strategic interventions can significantly enhance communication effectiveness in resource-limited settings. The data suggest that employing interpreters for major local languages (Dagbani, Akan, Ewe) and systematically combining verbal/non-verbal methods could reduce satisfaction gaps by up to 31 % based on regression models. This approach aligns with Peplau's emphasis on relational communication and SERVQUAL's empathy dimension, addressing both linguistic and cultural barriers. Implementing these practical solutions would directly improve health outcomes and patient experiences. The study establishes a clear hierarchy of satisfaction determinants that should guide policy. Quantitative analysis revealed that communication competence explains 53 % of satisfaction variance, surpassing both interpersonal relationships (21 %) and technical skills (6 %). While all three factors remain important, this weighting suggests communication training should receive proportionally greater investment in continuing education programs. Future healthcare reforms must therefore rebalance training curricula to reflect these evidence-based priorities.

Strengths, limitations, future studies, and recommendations

This study offers significant contributions to the understanding of healthcare communication in low-resource, multilingual settings. A key strength of this research lies in its sequential explanatory mixed-methods design, which enabled both statistical validation and contextual depth. The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings provided a comprehensive understanding of how communication competence, interpersonal relationships, and technical skills influence patient satisfaction. While the regression analysis quantified the predictive power of these variables, the qualitative narratives revealed nuanced experiences and perceptions that would have otherwise remained obscured.

Despite its strengths, the study has limitations. Data collection took place in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic, when protective measures such as mask use, physical distancing, and increased patient loads may have constrained non-verbal cues and reduced opportunities for interpersonal engagement. These contextual factors may have influenced both provider communication behaviors and patients' satisfaction ratings and should therefore be considered when interpreting the findings. The single-site focus at Tamale Teaching Hospital, while contextually rich, limits the generalizability of the findings across Ghana or other LMICs with different cultural, institutional, or infrastructural dynamics. Also, while the study focused on examining the effects of communication competence, interpersonal relationships, and technical competence on patient satisfaction, potential control variables such as patient age, gender, education level, and ward type were not included in the regression model. Lastly, the study sample was predominantly male and composed largely of participants aged 21–30 years. This demographic concentration may limit the representativeness of the findings, as the experiences and perceptions of older or female participants might differ. Future studies may benefit from multi-site designs that capture regional and systemic variability. Based on the study findings, we recommend three targeted strategies to strengthen communication in Ghana's healthcare settings. First, hospitals should institutionalize structured feedback and reflective supervision systems that provide regular, data-driven assessment of providers' communication competence. Second, targeted coaching and mentoring programs should be developed to address specific relational and linguistic challenges identified in the qualitative phase, such as managing patient expectations and adapting language to varying literacy levels. Third, pre-service and in-service training curricula should incorporate scenario-based modules on empathy, non-verbal communication, and culturally responsive care to bridge the gap between technical skill and interpersonal engagement. Implementing these interventions could enhance both provider–patient relationships and overall patient satisfaction.
