A Charge Transfer Mediated Sequential Triplet Energy Transfer Model Beyond the Dexter Limit in Organic Photocatalysis
Authors: Lin Liu, Yibo Shi, Jialong Jie, Xiao Liu, Qian Wang, Yu Qiao, Kai Feng, Weihai Fang, Hongmei Su, Xuebo Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Classical distance-dependent Dexter energy transfer models fail to account for the complex, geometry-sensitive kinetics observed in flexible and dynamically disordered organic photocatalytic systems. To resolve these persistent inconsistencies, particularly in photosensitized [2 + 2] cycloaddition reactions, we investigate how charge-transfer (CT) intermediates mediate triplet–triplet energy transfer (TTEnT) beyond the conventional Dexter framework. Through a combined approach integrating high-level multistate CASPT2 calculations, ultrafast transient absorption spectroscopy, and electrochemical analysis, we uncover two distinct and previously unrecognized TTEnT pathways: a CT trap mechanism and a CT-mediated sequential TTEnT mechanism in which electron and hole transfer steps bridge the donor and acceptor triplet states. Both pathways are governed by nonadiabatic electron transfer processes through CT intermediates and are highly sensitive to the redox potential and steric environment. Using alkenylboronates and N-sulfonylimines as representative acceptors, we demonstrate that favorable redox matching and geometrically confined donor–acceptor interfaces promote long-range TTEnT via CT bridges, effectively bypassing the spatial limitations of Dexter exchange. Compared to the classical model, this CT-bridged sequential TTEnT mechanism exhibits reduced dependence on orbital overlap and stronger correlation with redox thermodynamics, rendering it particularly suitable for sterically hindered or conformationally flexible systems. Based on these findings, we propose a unified TTEnT framework that integrates Dexter-type exchange with dynamic CT-mediated pathways, offering a new conceptual foundation and design principles for next-generation photosensitizers and photocatalytic systems.

Introduction

Triplet–triplet energy transfer (TTEnT) plays a crucial role in various fields, including energy science, optoelectronics, − medical imaging, and sustainable synthesis. − Compared to direct excitation, TTEnT provides efficient and controllable indirect sensitization, reducing the reliance on high-energy light sources. By enabling directional transfer of excitation energy and spin angular momentum, TTEnT modulates the electronic and spin state distributions of both donor and acceptor, generating highly reactive triplet excitons even in substrates with poor visible-light absorption or low intersystem crossing (ISC) efficiency, thereby expanding the applicability of reactions.

Since Dexter proposed the classic energy transfer mechanism in 1953, it has remained the theoretical foundation for describing TTEnT. The Dexter energy transfer (DET) model defines TTEnT as a short-range two-electron exchange between donor and acceptor orbitals (Figure A) with transfer efficiency decaying exponentially with increasing distance. This strong distance dependence limits its application in systems with a large spatial hindrance or spatial separation. Closs and co-workers − experimentally verified that, in bridged systems, the electronic coupling between electron transfer (ET) and hole transfer (HT) decays more slowly than the two-electron transfer, highlighting the advantage of single electron transfer (SET) in TTEnT. In 2010, Wasielewski and colleagues first captured transient signals of the bridge-occupied state in donor-bridge-acceptor (D-B-A) systems, demonstrating that increasing the length of the bridge unit could tune the energy transfer (EnT) mode from a single-step tunneling process to a multistep hopping process, thus overcoming the distance dependence of the Dexter model. This discovery revealed the important role of bridging coupling mechanisms in mitigating the spatial limitations of the Dexter model. In recent years, Zhang and colleagues , have reported ultrafast singlet exciton energy transfer mechanisms mediated by interfacial or intramolecular charge-transfer (CT) states in organic photovoltaic systems using ultrafast spectroscopy. Similarly, in biological systems, Zhong and his team , discovered that photolyases exhibit both a two-step hopping mechanism and a direct tunneling electron transfer mechanism during DNA repair. However, the studies described above mainly focus on singlet exciton energy transfer or purely single-electron transfer processes. Currently, the electron or hole transfer mechanisms of triplet excitons have been studied in core–shell quantum dots, − organic doped thin-film phosphorescent systems, and iron-anthracene dyad, but they remain extremely rare in solution-phase organic photocatalytic systems.

However, a growing body of experimental evidence suggests that the classical Dexter model is insufficient to fully account for the complex kinetics observed in organic photocatalytic reactions. In particular, [2 + 2] cycloaddition reactions involving alkenylboronates and N-sulfonylimines reveal anomalous behaviors that deviate significantly from Dexter-based predictions: , ideal triplet energy matching between donor and acceptor does not necessarily lead to higher reactivity; photocatalysts with greater steric hindrance or weaker orbital overlap can exhibit superior catalytic performance; and isoenergetic photocatalysts often display opposite reactivity trends depending on subtle variations in redox potential or coordination geometry. These discrepancies indicate that Dexter-type orbital overlap is not the sole determinant of TTEnT efficiency and that transient CT intermediates, redox potential, and conformational flexibility may play critical, yet underappreciated mechanistic roles. Systematically elucidating TTEnT pathways beyond the Dexter regime remains challenging, especially in flexible, solution-phase systems, where high conformational entropy and weak intermolecular interactions hinder the stabilization of CT intermediates and thus suppress efficient triplet exciton transfer.

To address these mechanistic inconsistencies and explore the underlying driving forces of TTEnT in photocatalysis, we selected the archetypal visible-light-induced [2 + 2] photocycloaddition reaction as a model system , (Figure B). Using two representative acceptors, (E)-4,4,5,5,5-tetramethyl-2-styryl-1,3,2-dioxaborolane (Aal) and 3-phenylbenzo­[d]­isothiazole-1,1-dioxide (Aim), that feature distinct redox potentials, electronic structure, and steric profiles, we systematically investigated the excited-state dynamics through a combination of Multistate Second-Order Complete Active Space Perturbation Theory (MS-CASPT2) calculations within the Marcus electron transfer framework and ultrafast transient absorption spectroscopy. These substrates are broadly representative of visible-light-active acceptors in organic photocatalysis and allow us to probe how steric, electronic, and energetic factors modulate the TTEnT efficiency. Our study reveals a CT-mediated sequential TTEnT mechanism that coexists and competes with the classical Dexter pathway and exhibits switchable behavior depending on redox alignment and molecular geometry. By explicitly incorporating the formation, evolution, and energetic modulation of CT intermediates into the TTEnT framework, we propose a CT-bridged Sequential TTEnT Model that transcends the limitations of Dexter theory and provides a unified description of triplet energy transfer in both rigid and flexible systems. Unlike the classical Dexter model, which treats TTEnT exclusively as a short-range two-electron exchange, our Extended TTEnT framework integrates triplet-state energy and redox dependence, thereby extending its applicability to flexible, sterically hindered, and redox-active photocatalytic systems. This expanded framework not only rationalizes anomalous reactivity patterns observed in visible-light-induced cycloaddition reactions but also establishes mechanistically grounded design principles for next-generation photosensitizers and photocatalytic platforms.

Methods

Time-Resolved Spectroscopy

Time-resolved transient absorption spectroscopy measurements were performed by using an Edinburgh Instruments LP980 spectrometer with a dual-channel detection system. A Continuum Surelite II-10 Q-switched Nd:YAG laser (10 Hz repetition rate) generated third-harmonic excitation pulses at 430 nm, which were directed into a Horizon I Midband optical parametric oscillator (OPO) to produce tunable pump pulses spanning 200–2300 nm. The sample, subjected to multiple freeze–pump–thaw cycles under vacuum for oxygen removal, was photoexcited by 430 nm laser pulses (1 Hz, 16.5 mJ/pulse·cm–2, fwhm ≈ 7 ns) in a 1 cm path length quartz cuvette. A 150 W pulsed xenon lamp provided probe illumination across the 300–700 nm spectral range with dispersed light analyzed by a 300 mm focal-length monochromator coupled to a photomultiplier tube (PMT) detector. Transient signals were temporally resolved through synchronized triggering and recorded with a Tektronix TDS 3012C oscilloscope (100 MHz bandwidth, 1.25 GS/s sampling rate) before computational analysis. Real-time data acquisition, differential optical density (ΔOD) calculations, and multiexponential decay fitting were performed using the LP980 software suite.

DFT Methods

Unless otherwise specified, all density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out using the Gaussian16 program package. Geometry optimizations of all stationary points were performed with the PBE0 functional incorporating Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction with Becke-Johnson damping (PBE0-D3BJ). , Solvent effects were modeled using the polarizable continuum model (PCM) − with dichloromethane (DCM) as the solvent. A mixed basis set (BS-1) was employed for geometry optimization, where the Iridium atom was described by the Stuttgart effective core potential (ECP60MWB) − and its associated basis set [ECP60MWB (8s7p6d2f1g)/[6s5p3d2f1g]], while the light elements (C, H, F, B, O, S, and N) were treated with the all-electron 6-31G­(d) basis set. Parameters for the pseudopotential and basis set used for Ir can be found at http://www.tc.uni-koeln.de/PP/clickpse.en.html. Open-shell and closed-shell species were optimized by using unrestricted and restricted formalisms, respectively. Frequency calculations were conducted at the same level of theory to verify the nature of the stationary points; minima were confirmed by the absence of imaginary frequencies. To refine the relative Gibbs free energies, single-point energy calculations were performed using the PBE0-D3BJ functional in conjunction with a larger mixed basis set (BS-2). In BS-2, the Ir atom was again treated with the ECP60MWB pseudopotential and associated basis set, while a more extended 6-311+G­(d,p) , basis set was used for the nonmetal elements (C, H, F, B, O, S, and N).

MS-CASPT2 Methods

For the isolated organic substrates Aal and Aim and the photosensitizers Ir­(ppy)3 and Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6), as well as the encounter complexes formed between photocatalysts and substrates, we employed a complete active space self-consistent field (CASSCF) − approach with state-averaged optimization based on structures optimized at single-reference levels in order to accurately capture static correlation energy effects. The size and composition of the active space were determined using a configuration selection and orbital localization strategy developed by our group. − Through extensive benchmark calculations and careful evaluation (see the Supporting Information for details on the active space testing and selection process), we identified an appropriate active space that achieves a balance between computational efficiency and accuracy. This ensured that the selected orbitals are directly relevant to the TTEnT process. To eliminate the errors in the potential energy surface caused by dynamic electron correlation, MS-CASPT2 was employed. For conical intersections between states with the same spin multiplicity, the Constraints keyword in OpenMolcas was used in combination with state-averaged CASSCF to accurately locate the crossing structures with zero energy gap by simultaneously optimizing both target states. For intersections between states of different spin multiplicities, the minimum-energy crossing region was first located using the sobMECP , methods, followed by further refinement with the state-averaged CASSCF approach. Based on the optimized stationary points (minimal and crossing points) obtained from the potential energy surface, intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) , calculations were carried out to connect the initial, intermediate, and final states, thereby constructing the minimum energy pathway (MEP). The x-axis of the IRC represents the mass-weighted distance between the current and initial conformations with units of amu1/2·bohr. To accelerate the computations, the Cholesky − decomposition technique with dynamically generated unbiased auxiliary basis sets was adopted. An imaginary level shift (imag = 0.2 au) was introduced to avoid intruder state problems. In addition, the ionization potential/electron affinity (IPEA) parameter was set to 0. All MS-CASPT2 calculations were based on multistate-averaged CASSCF wave functions and were performed using OpenMolcas v24.02. − The incorporation of Cholesky decomposition combined with on-the-fly auxiliary basis set generation significantly enhanced the computational efficiency and enabled analytical gradient calculations. The spin–orbit coupling (SOC) leads to the mixing of pure triplet and singlet wave functions. We calculated the SOC values between singlet and triplet states using the CASSI method along with the single-electron relativistic approximation. Results and Discussion

Model Systems for Studying TTEnT Processes

To investigate TTEnT mechanisms beyond Dexter, four representative model systems were constructed. Ir­(ppy)3 and Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6) were selected as energy donors and Aal and Aim, as energy acceptors. These model systems possess the following distinct features, making them suitable for systematic investigation of their EnT behavior under visible-light-driven conditions. The selected Ir-based donors exhibit characteristic CT bands around 300–500 nm (for Ir­(ppy)3, Figure A) and 350–450 nm (for Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6), Figure B), while both Aal and Aim show negligible absorption above 350 nm (Figure C,D). This ensures that only the donor is excited under visible-light irradiation, thus eliminating direct excitation and ISC from the substrate. Such a design guarantees that any triplet population observed on the acceptor originates from a well-defined TTEnT process. Combining high-precision excited-state calculations and photoluminescence spectra analysis indicates that the T1 states of Aal and Aim correspond to localized ππ* excitations on their alkene or cyclic imine (Figures S2–S6, Tables S1–S6). Importantly, their triplet energies lie just below the 3MLCT states of the two Ir-based donors, satisfying the energetic prerequisites for Dexter-type energy transfer. Further spectral analysis confirms negligible overlap between the phosphorescence emission of the photocatalysts and the ground-state absorption of the substrates (Figure S23). This excludes the possibility of Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) or any radiative-based sensitization mechanism, thus providing a clean backdrop for isolating short-range Dexter and/or CT-mediated transfer , processes. Despite similar photophysical properties, the energy transfer reactions involving Aal and Aim as acceptors exhibit markedly different reactivities under identical reaction conditions. This reactivity divergence cannot be rationalized solely by traditional Dexter theory , or Stern–Volmer quenching kinetics, suggesting that other factors, such as CT intermediate stabilization, redox potential, or electronic coupling, play critical roles (Figures S18–S25, Tables S15–S17). As such, these model systems offer a mechanistic probe into how subtle changes in substrate and photosensitizer electronics or sterics can modulate the TTEnT efficiency.

Classical DET Model

To elucidate the TTEnT mechanism in these model systems, we first explored the excited-state potential energy surfaces of Ir­(ppy)3-Aal and Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6)-Aal (Figure A,B, Figures S30–S31). Both systems were found to follow analogous TTEnT pathways initiated by visible-light absorption yet differ in their reactivity and kinetics. Upon photoexcitation, each Ir­(III) photocatalyst undergoes a singlet metal-to-ligand charge transfer (1MLCT) excitation, followed by rapid ISC to generate a long-lived triplet MLCT (3MLCT) state. This state subsequently engages with the ππ* triplet-local excited state of Aal (3Aal_LE) through a conical intersection (CI), enabling concerted, bidirectional electron flow between the donor and acceptor. At this CI point, π-electron donation from Aal to the Ir center is complemented by back-donation from the Ir ligands (ppy or dtbbpy) to the π* orbital of Aal, constituting a classic simultaneous two-electron Dexter exchange (Figure S38).

Importantly, along the entire relaxation pathway, the intermolecular CT state remains energetically inaccessible on the potential energy surface. This disfavors a stepwise ET mechanism and supports TTEnT proceeds through a concerted Dexter process without functional involvement of a CT intermediate. Despite their mechanistic similarity, the two systems exhibit stark differences in TTEnT efficiency. In the Ir­(ppy)3-Aal complex, the 3MLCT → 3Aal_LE crossing is energetically favorable with a low barrier of 0.11 eV, facilitating efficient energy transfer. In contrast, the Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6)-Aal system encounters a significantly higher CI barrier of 0.30 eV, attributed to (i) the electron-withdrawing fluorine substituents, which reduce the electron-donating capability of the metal center, and (ii) the steric bulk of the dtbbpy ligand, which increases inner-sphere reorganization energy and hinders conformational relaxation.

Electrochemical analysis further supports the exclusion of ET-based pathways. Due to its high oxidation potential and low reduction potential, Aal is thermodynamically incompatible with either oxidative or reductive SET from the photocatalysts. The calculated redox mismatches (ΔG ox > 0.83 V, ΔG red > 0.47 V) provide a negligible thermodynamic driving force for charge separation, confirming that the observed energy transfer does not proceed via CT formation. These trends are reinforced by Marcus kinetic evaluations. While both systems exhibit similar thermodynamic driving forces for TTEnT (ΔG ≈ −0.35 eV), their energy transfer rates differ by 4 orders of magnitude: k(Ir­(ppy)3-Aal) = 8.4 × 109 s–1; k(Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6)-Aal) = 3.4 × 105 s–1 (Figure C,D, Table S18). This stark contrast arises from distinct activation barriers and electronic coupling characteristics. The Ir­(ppy)3 system benefits from higher structural rigidity and strong donor–acceptor orbital overlap (H = 43.94 cm–1), while the fluorinated ones suffer from increased activation barriers and reduced coupling (11.36 cm–1), resulting in suppressed TTEnT kinetics. These findings confirm that TTEnT between Ir-based photocatalysts and Aal proceeds via a direct, concerted Dexter-type mechanism, which involves initial MLCT excitation, synchronized bidirectional CT, and subsequent substrate activation. The process is governed by synchronized orbital interactions at a conical intersection, indicating that systems with alkenyl boronic esters as substrates conform to the classical DET model.

CT Trap Pathway that Bypasses TTEnT

Unlike the Aal system, the Ir­(ppy)3-Aim complex follows a distinct SET coupling exciton recombination mechanism, driven by the intrinsic electronic properties of Aim. Specifically, the strongly polarized CN bond in Aim (Δq = 1.06 e) contrasts with the nonpolar CC bond in Aal (Δq = 0.26 e), rendering Aim a more electrophilic acceptor. This enhanced polarity facilitates a thermodynamically favorable oxidative SET from photoexcited Ir­(ppy)3 to oxidized Aim. Electrochemical measurements confirm that the excited-state reduction potential of Ir­(ppy)3* (−1.73 V) is more negative than that of Aim (−1.24 V), providing a sizable driving force (ΔG = −0.49 V) for forward ET and promoting the formation of a stabilized ion pair (Ir­(ppy)3 +···Aim •–).

Excited-state PES analysis (Figure A) reveals that, after 386 nm photoexcitation and subsequent ISC, the system relaxes to the 3MLCT state of Ir­(ppy)3, accompanied by moderate geometric distortions at the metal center. Crucially, a moderate energy difference (ΔE = 0.54 eV) separates the 3MLCT and intermolecular CT states, opening a stepwise SET pathway. Despite the initially large donor–acceptor distance (Ir–N = 6.18 Å), antiparallel dipole alignment between Ir­(ppy)3 and Aim facilitates CT. Structural evolution including CN bond elongation (1.29 → 1.36 Å) and Ir···Aim distance contraction (6.18 → 5.22 Å) lowers the CT state energy and reduces the 3MLCT → 3CT activation barrier to 0.19 eV. The resulting 3CT state is stabilized by both Coulombic attraction and geometric reorganization, forming a deep potential well (ΔG = −1.05 eV) with a high dipole moment (19.10 D), indicative of a strongly bound Ir­(ppy)3 +···Aim •– ion pair (Figure S32).

Transient absorption spectroscopy provides experimental support. The 3MLCT state of Ir­(ppy)3 alone exhibits a characteristic positive ΔOD signal at 300–380 nm and ground-state bleaching at 380–430 nm with a phosphorescence signal centered at 522 nm (Figure B, Figure S45). A long-lived excited-state absorption (ESA) band of Ir­(ppy)3 is detected, extending into the microsecond time regime. Upon Aim addition, a new, broad absorption band emerges at nearly 600 nm (Figure C, Figure S45). This band matches the reported , signatures of the Ir­(ppy)3 + species (Figure S44), providing experimental evidence for the formation of the Ir­(ppy)3 +···Aim •– ion pair. This observation strongly supports the presence of an oxidative SET mechanism. Following ion-pair formation, a high barrier for reverse ET (1.22 eV) thermodynamically inhibits the reductive SET process and 3Aim_LE state generation. The kinetic data demonstrate that, upon Aim addition, the decay at 400 and 520 nm becomes markedly accelerated (Figure S46). Such a rapidly decaying charge-transfer intermediate is unfavorable for subsequent hole transfer. Instead, the system is funneled into the vibration relaxation channel. Orbital analyses reveal a sequential electron flow: metal d → bipyridine π* → Aim π*­(NC) → back to metal, reflecting a unidirectional SET process (Figure S39).

Competing excited-state pathways were further quantified through kinetic modeling (Figure D, Table S19). Among the three candidate pathways, which include classical DET, sequential EnT and CT-trap, the initial oxidative SET step is ultrafast (k ox‑SET = 9.54 × 109 s–1), far outpacing the direct Dexter channel (k DET = 6.29 × 10–2 s–1). However, reductive SET is nearly blocked (k re‑SET = 1.86 × 10–8 s–1), forming a self-trapped CT state. This trap dominates excited relaxation dynamics, with charge recombination (k CR = 1.40 × 108 s–1) outpacing effective energy transfer. The results indicate that the system preferred transitions from a two-electron process into a dissipation-prone energy trap when the CT intermediate is sufficiently stabilized. The imbalance between fast oxidation and slow reduction locks the system into a nonproduct pathway, thereby impeding efficient triplet energy transfer and significantly diminishing photocatalytic performance.

Sequential TTEnT Pathway via Dynamic CT Intermediates

To further clarify the functional landscape of CT intermediates in TTEnT, we next examined the Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6)-Aim system, which shares the same acceptor as Ir­(ppy)3-Aim but features a more electron-deficient and sterically hindered donor. These structural and electronic modifications give rise to a modulated sequential energy transfer mechanism that leverages the benefits of transient CT states while avoiding exciton trapping.

Upon photoexcitation at 361 nm, Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6) reaches its 1MLCT state and rapidly undergoes ISC and vibrational relaxation, stabilizing at the 3MLCT minimum (Figure A). The energy difference between the 3MLCT and 3CT states (ΔE = 0.45 eV) is sufficient to enable forward oxidative SET at the Franck–Condon geometry. Although the activation barrier for CI crossing (0.32 eV) is higher than that in the Ir­(ppy)3-Aim system (0.19 eV), the donor–acceptor pair undergoes Coulomb-induced contraction (Ir···N = 5.06 → 4.86 Å), leading to CT formation. The process avoids formation of a deep exciton trap because the CT state’s energy is tuned upward by two synergistic features: (i) a less negative excited-state reduction potential of Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6)* (−0.93 V vs −1.73 V for Ir­(ppy)3), and (ii) substantial steric hindrance from the dtbbpy ligand. As a result, the reverse ET barrier is lowered to 0.28 eV, allowing the CT state to act as a dynamic bridge rather than a trap. The geometry of the CT minimum features a significantly elongated CN bond (1.45 Å) and a distorted dihedral angle (142.29°), characteristic of a dual-radical Aim species. Population analysis shows electron movement from the metal d-orbital to the bipyridine π* orbital, then to Aim π*­(NC) and Aim π (NC), and back to the metal center (Figure S40).

Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6) also exhibits a long-lived ESA consistent under an N2 atmosphere. The spectra display ESA in the 360–450 nm region and a phosphorescence signal at 522 nm (Figure B, Figure S45). Upon Aim addition, the negative phosphorescence signal at 450–700 nm is suppressed and partially converted into a weak positive band, while a more pronounced ESA feature emerges around 480 nm, concomitant with the formation of Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6)+ (Figure C, Figures S41–S45). Kinetic traces at 420 and 480 nm show that (Figure S47), in the presence of Aim, the decay lifetime at 420 nm becomes longer, while the transient at 480 nm evolves from a negative to a positive signal. A pronounced loss of the ESA band accompanied by its prolongation indicates that the catalyst is being consumed, while a new chemical species is formed. Together with computational evidence, this supports the generation of a moderately stabilized Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6)+···Aim •– ion pair, consistent with an oxidative quenching pathway as described in prior studies by McCusker and co-workers. , Kinetic modeling in the Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6)-Aim system (Figure D, Table S19) reveals clear dominance of the stepwise pathway. The oxidative SET step (k ox‑SET = 4.51 × 107 s–1, ΔG‡ = 0.32 eV) serves as the rate-determining step in this mechanism. Subsequently, the faster reduction process (k re‑SET = 3.30 × 108 s–1) is induced by the moderately stabilized CT state. In contrast, the concerted EnT pathway is significantly slower (k DET = 1.14 × 104 s–1) due to unfavorable activation energy and weak coupling.

These results reveal that the combined steric and electronic effects introduced by Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6) modulate the CT energy level to facilitate efficient energy relay along the sequential EnT pathway. Unlike the concerted route, which is sensitive to donor–acceptor coupling and energy match, the sequential mechanism enhances single-electron coupling (H ox‑SET = 201 cm–1 and H re‑SET = 177 cm–1) to compensate for steric hindrance-induced orbital overlap reduction and raises the transient CT energy level through greater steric bulk and a less reducing excited-state potential, thereby facilitating radical formation on the acceptor while avoiding deep exciton traps. This CT intermediate facilitates efficient radical generation and suppresses energy dissipation, thereby offering a mechanistic basis for the enhanced triplet energy transfer efficiency and superior photocatalytic performance observed in systems utilizing bulky ligands.

Proposal, Validation, and Application of CT-Mediated Sequential TTEnT Model

Our investigation across representative TTEnT systems uncovered two mechanistically distinct CT-mediated pathways that extend beyond the scope of the classical Dexter model. In the Ir­(ppy)3-Aal system, energy transfer proceeds via a conventional Dexter-type orbital exchange. In contrast, the Ir­(ppy)3-Aim system exhibits a deep CT trap mechanism, wherein a low-lying charge-transfer intermediate acts as a kinetic sink and entirely bypasses TTEnT. Meanwhile, the Ir­[dFppy]2(dtbbpy)­(PF6)-Aim system reveals a sequential TTEnT pathway in which redox-coupled electron and hole transfer via transient CT intermediates bridge the donor and acceptor triplet states. This mechanistic diversity (Figure A), spanning Dexter-type tunneling, CT trapping, and CT-bridged multistep transfer, underscores the inadequacy of classical models that rely solely on triplet energy resonance and spatial orbital overlap.

Such systems involve multiple interlinked photophysical events, ISC, donor–acceptor encounters, charge separation and recombination, electron exchange, and excited-state relaxation, whose rate-determining steps are governed by both reorganization energy (λ) and driving force (ΔG). Their nonlinear coupling, dictated by the system-specific electronic structure (Figure B), necessitates high-level excited-state potential energy surface calculations. However, such simulations are computationally demanding, and classical Dexter theory cannot capture these CT-mediated processes.

To address this limitation, we developed an extended CT-mediated sequential TTEnT model, which unifies concerted Dexter-type exchange and stepwise SET mechanisms within a Marcus-theory-based framework. A core innovation of the model is the redefinition of the effective donor energy boundary: instead of assuming the donor’s T 1 energy [T 1(D)] as the energetic ceiling, we introduce ΔG D+A‑, the thermodynamic energy of the donor–acceptor CT intermediate. When ΔG D+A‑ < T 1(D), the sequential pathway becomes thermodynamically and kinetically preferred, allowing delocalized electron flow and recombination to populate the acceptor triplet state.

To operationalize this model, we propose three redox-based thermodynamic descriptors derived from the Rehm–Weller framework, , all compatible with experimental solvent environments (Figure C,D): (1) ΔG CS, the free energy of photoinduced charge separation; (2) ΔG CR*, the driving force for recombination of the CT state into the acceptor triplet; (3) ΔG D+A‑, the energy of the D–A CT intermediate, which we identify as the decisive descriptor that governs the feasibility of CT-mediated TTEnT. ΔG D+A‑ effectively replaces T 1(D) in a revised extended EnT-factor, a semiquantitative Marcus-type expression that continuously integrates Dexter and sequential ET contributions.

We validated this model across a series of photosensitizer–substrate pairs using MS-CASPT2 calculations and electrochemical data (Tables S20–S24). In the representative Ir­(ppy)3-Aim system, the CT intermediate lies substantially below the T 1 of the donor (1.96 eV vs 2.42 eV), rationalizing its CT-trapped energy dissipation behavior. Substituting ΔG D+A‑ for T 1(D) in the EnT-factor calculation significantly improved the model’s correlation with experimental kinetics (corrected factor = 7.62 vs 13.77 from the classical Dexter model). Across all systems tested, the corrected EnT-factors show strong positive correlation with observed TTEnT rates and photocatalytic yields (Figure E,F), confirming the model’s enhanced predictive accuracy and mechanistic sensitivity. Unlike traditional models that treat Dexter exchange and SET-mediated TTEnT as mutually exclusive, the extended TTEnT model provides a unified, semiquantitative framework that captures their coexistence and dynamic competition. It enables more accurate prediction of TTEnT outcomes in structurally flexible, sterically hindered, and redox-diverse photocatalytic systems.

Conclusion

The photosensitized cycloaddition reaction observed in this work results from the competition between concerted and sequential TTEnT, involving a dynamic energy bridge mediated by a CT state. This mechanism differs from the well-established classical Dexter model, which describes a single-step process involving the simultaneous transfer of an electron and a hole and is highly dependent on the orbital overlap between donor and acceptor molecules. The study reveals that the energy transfer behaviors of alkenylboronates and N-sulfonylimines differ due to variations in their redox potential. Because N-sulfonylimines and the photosensitizer possess a suitable redox potential range, the exciton can rapidly reach the CT state via an SET step, initiating a sequential dual-electron transfer that proceeds much faster than the traditional Dexter process. Bulky substituents separate the donor and acceptor, which slows the traditional Dexter pathway while accelerating the second SET step of sequential energy transfer. It can be inferred that the photophysical processes triggered by photosensitizer excitation involve a linear combination of electron-transfer and energy-transfer wave functions. These two pathways can potentially be modulated and designed through redox potentials and structural factors, such as steric hindrance or molecular flexibility.

Hence, the accurate assessment of energy transfer models is crucial for the screening of photosensitizers. Based on these findings, we unified the classical DET and CT-mediated sequential TTEnT models, providing a more comprehensive framework for evaluating photosensitizer–substrate compatibility. By revisiting the traditional Dexter model, we incorporate the CT-state energy (ΔG D+A‑) alongside conventional parameters such as T 1(D), charge separation and recombination free energies (ΔG CS and ΔG CR*), and reorganization energy (λ). By comparing the CT state with the donor excitation level to determine the initial state of the final nonradiative process, this work demonstrates that additional considerations significantly improve the predictive accuracy for photosensitizer–substrate pairs. In solution-phase organic photocatalysis, the energy transfer between the photosensitizer and substrate is strongly influenced by the redox potential. Thus, both sequential energy transfer and the traditional DET pathway should be considered when excited-state dynamics is regulated to optimize reaction conditions.
