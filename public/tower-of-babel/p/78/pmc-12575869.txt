Time to incorporate positive experiences and outcomes in psychosis research: genetic differential susceptibility to childhood experiences works for “better and for worse”
Authors: Neus Barrantes-Vidal, Pilar Torrecilla, Valeria Lavín, Patricia Mas-Bermejo, Sergi Papiol, Marian J. Bakermans-Kranenburg, Marinus H. van Ijzendoorn, Thomas R. Kwapil, Araceli Rosa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Research on gene-environment interactions in psychosis has been guided by diathesis-stress models, which emphasize the role of negative experiences. In contrast, the Differential Susceptibility (DS) model indicates that individuals differ in sensitivity to both adverse and supportive environments. This study replicates and expands an initial proof-of-concept DS study on psychosis by examining whether genetic sensitivity to the environment, indexed by polygenic scores of environmental sensitivity (PGS-ES) and psychotic-like experiences (PGS-PLE), moderated the impact of early-life positive and negative experiences on trans-syndromic outcomes, including subclinical psychotic features and positive mental health, in a DS manner. PGS and measures of early environmental, psychopathology, positive outcomes, and functioning were collected from 941 nonclinical young adults. A comprehensive factorial analysis of positive and negative environmental measures resulted in a general exposome score and four specific factors. A competitive-confirmatory approach examined DS patterns using LEGIT R package. Individuals with higher PGS-ES reported greater psychopathology, poorer functioning, and lower positive mental health outcomes when exposed to fewer positive experiences or poorer paternal parenting; but also exhibited less symptoms and more positive outcomes when exposed to supportive environments compared to individuals with lower PGS-ES. PGS-PLE only predicted psychopathology in a diathesis-stress manner. Findings support the trans-syndromic validity of the DS model, indicating that genetic susceptibility to the environment operated “for better and for worse” by impacting both ill and positive mental health. This highlights the importance of positive experiences and underscores the need to integrate risk and resilience models in psychosis.

Introduction

Research on gene-environment interactions has traditionally been guided by diathesis-stress models that posit that individuals carrying genetic-risk variants are more vulnerable to the effects of adversity and more prone to develop psychopathology [1]. At its core, diathesis-stress is a disease model with a focus on dysfunction. Evolutionary theory, however, suggests that some individuals may differ in their susceptibility (or sensitivity) to the full spectrum of environmental factors, that is, both adverse and supportive [2–4]. The model of Differential Susceptibility (DS) to the environment posits that individuals traditionally considered to carry greater vulnerability may be better conceptualized as having a greater susceptibility to the environment (i.e., being more plastic or malleable) [5]. It is hypothesized that some of the same genetic variants or temperament traits involved in increasing the negative effects of adversity could also enhance the likelihood of benefiting from positive experiences (“for better and for worse”) [2]. A related model of gene-environment interaction (GxE) is vantage sensitivity [6], which poses that certain genetic variants may enhance the likelihood of benefiting from positive experiences without implying an increase in the susceptibility to negative experiences. This model is the mirror image of diathesis-stress, and while DS serves as an overarching framework that incorporates the conceptual elements of both diathesis-stress and vantage sensitivity, it remains theoretically distinct. DS is grounded in evolutionary theory and proposes that certain traits confer sensitivity to both positive and negative environmental factors [7].

A critical difference of DS with disease models such as diathesis-stress is that individuals are not considered as either vulnerable or resilient, but rather as more or less responsive to their environment. DS considers that experience-driven plasticity (i.e., mechanisms that enable learning in response to environmental experiences to allow adaptation), often neglected in psychopathology [8], are related to both adverse and positive experiences, allowing the integration of causal models that include both risk and resilience. Thus, DS provides conceptual grounding for interventions aimed at enhancing environmental conditions to bolster resilience of highly susceptible individuals, rather than merely perceiving them as vulnerable and focusing on risk reduction.

Despite emerging recognition that many transdiagnostic developmental pathways can lead to psychosis [9], developmental psychopathology models in psychosis have been historically framed within the diathesis-stress paradigm and focused on the interaction of genetic risk with environmental insults (e.g., obstetric complications) to trigger abnormal neurodevelopmental trajectories leading to risk and symptoms [10]. Research would benefit from broader developmental perspectives that consider the interaction of disorder-specific and unspecific risk factors as well as development-enhancing environmental experiences as proposed by DS, such as: (i) increasing recognition of phenotypic dimensionality that is consistent with evidence that psychosis genetic risk is broadly distributed in the population [11] and associated with multiple disorders [12], (ii) adverse experiences are strong determinants of psychosis [13], whereas positive experiences can be protective factors (e.g., Butler et al. [14]), and (iii) population studies indicate that genetic and environmental risk for psychosis is associated with positive outcomes, such as exceptional social achievement [15] and creativity[16], converging with robust findings connecting creativity with schizotypy and soft bipolarity [17–19].

The notion that the diagnosis of schizophrenia may be associated with any positive features may seem counterintuitive. However, psychosis is expressed across a dynamic continuum in which schizophrenia represents the most extreme form of a broadly distributed psychosis liability including schizotypy traits and psychotic-like experiences (PLE) [20–23]. PLE are also expressed in non-psychotic manifestations such as anxiety or depression [24], consistent with trans-syndromic models such as the Research Domain of Criteria (RDoC) [25] and the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) [26]. Integrated hierarchical models of psychopathology consider a general psychopathology factor (e.g., p factor) [27] that confers broad risk for symptoms across multiple domains, thus enabling the study of shared risk and protective factors across diagnostic spectra. Nonetheless, the idea that a single dimension can fully capture variation in psychopathology has been challenged [28]. For instance, schizotypy explains much less variance when considering it as a homogeneous unidimensional construct than when examining its dimensions (i.e., positive, negative and disorganized), that have shared yet distinct etiological factors [23, 29].

General models of psychopathology focus on symptom dimensions, but do not integrate key aspects of mental health such as well-being and functioning. The dual-continua model of mental health [30] recognizes psychopathology and well-being as distinct yet interconnected spectra in which mental illness and well-being can coexist. Thus, the absence of psychopathology does not guarantee positive mental health, and individuals with psychological disorders can experience well-being [31]. Similarly, research supports that psychopathology and functioning are different constructs to be investigated separately (e.g., Yang et al. [32]).

Childhood adversity is a key factor for mental illness [33], including psychosis [34] and subclinical psychosis [35]. Recent research suggests that the exposome, the lifetime interconnected network of environmental exposures encountered by an individual [36, 37], is predictive of a wide range of psychopathological manifestations, including psychosis [38, 39]. The exposome framework takes into consideration correlations among exposures, and it has been shown that an aggregate weighted score (exposome score for schizophrenia) predicts not only psychosis-spectrum disorders, but also general mental health problems and functioning [40]. However, limited studies have examined positive exposures and their potential for resilience in at-risk or affected individuals [41].

Barrantes-Vidal et al. [42, 43] recently conducted a proof-of-concept study on the suitability of DS to the extended psychosis phenotype with nonclinical young adults that supported DS predictions. Participants with higher scores on genetic sensitivity to the environment showed increased subclinical psychosis, depression, and anxiety if they experienced more childhood adversity, but fewer symptoms if they reported less adversity, compared to those with lower genetic sensitivity. Genetic sensitivity was indexed by a Polygenic Score of Environmental Sensitivity (PGS-ES) developed by Keers et al. [44] in a genome-wide study using a twin design. By associating intra-pair differences in emotional problems to genetic variants in genetically identical twins in their study, PGS captures emotional response DS to twins’ nonshared environments. Similarly, a PGS specific to psychotic-like experiences (PGS-PLE) [45] moderated the effect of adversity on PLE, positive schizotypy, and depression following the DS model, but only PGS-ES interactions survived correction for multiple testing [42]. Overall, our proof-of-concept supported the hypothesis that sensitivity to the environment and psychosocial experiences are relevant for multiple outcomes, including the extended psychosis phenotype, in a DS manner.

The present study

This study replicates our initial examination of the adequacy of the DS model to the psychosis extended phenotype [42] and extends it by examining a larger sample and specifically assessing positive environmental experiences and positive mental health outcomes, rather than relying on low adversity and psychopathology scores as proxy indicators of protective environments and positive mental health outcomes, respectively. We used competitive and confirmatory model-testing approaches [46, 47] to assess the fit of GxE interactions between PGS-ES and childhood environment to DS, diathesis-stress, and vantage models.

Our main goal was to examine whether individual differences in genetic sensitivity to the environment moderated the impact of early-life adverse and positive experiences in a DS manner on three major outcome domains: (i) subclinical psychopathology, as indexed by a general p-factor encompassing psychosis-spectrum, depressive, and anxiety features; (ii) a positive mental health index; and (iii) general and social functioning. If significant GxE models emerged, we conducted exploratory examinations of the GxE interactions for the individual psychopathology and positive mental health measures. This allowed us to identify which components of the major outcome domains yielded specific GxE patterns. Study hypotheses, methods, and data analytic plan were pre-registered at the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/ysqa5).

Consistent with DS, we hypothesized that individuals with high genetic susceptibility to the environment would report (i) increased psychopathology and impairment when exposed to adversity and, at the same time, (ii) better functioning and positive mental health when exposed to positive environmental factors and/or low adversity compared to those genetically less sensitive to the environment. Secondarily, we explored whether a PGS specifically related to PLE in nonclinical samples (PGS-PLE) likewise moderated the impact of the full spectrum of environmental experiences on our outcomes in a DS manner. Considering that sensitivity to the environment is probably a nonspecific causative factor of mental health and evidence that PRS-Schizophrenia confers transdiagnostic risk for mental suffering [48], we hypothesized that part of the variance captured by PGS-PLE relates to a heightened sensitivity to the environment and would thus fit a DS pattern. Lastly, we hypothesized that the positive, but not the negative, dimension of schizotypy, would show DS for both PGS-ES and PGS-PLE, given that the positive dimension of psychosis is related to childhood adversity across subclinical and clinical expressions [49].

Methods

Participants

The sample consisted of 941 nonclinical participants (Mage = 23, SD = 5.9, age range 18–62 years, 96% between 18–35 years, 76% self-reported as female) recruited at two universities in Barcelona recruited by advertisements to students and staff. Exclusion criteria were a) <18 years-old, and b) having grandparents of non-Spanish origin (required for the ancestry homogeneity of genetic analyses). Initially, 1220 participants completed our measures, with 39 excluded due to invalid protocols (score > 3 on the Infrequency Scale) [50]. From 1181 with valid self-report data, 1014 provided saliva for DNA extraction. After genetic quality control procedures (see supplementary material), the final sample included 941 participants.

All participants provided written informed consent. The study was performed in accordance with the Ethics Committee of the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona (Comissió d’Ètica en l’Experimentació Animal i Humana, ref. 5426).

Materials and procedure

Polygenic scores (PGS)

DNA was extracted from saliva and genotyped using the Illumina Infinium Global Screening Array v3.0 + MD Chip. GenomeStudio v2.0.4 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Details on the genotyping, quality control, and imputation procedures are reported in supplementary material.

The PGS-ES was calculated based on Keers and colleagues [44]. For the secondary goal, PGS-PLE was computed based on the Legge et al.’s [45] GWAS for PLE (see results with PGS-PLE in supplementary material). The classical Clumping + Thresholding method with PLINK v1.9 was applied. Independent variants were selected by clumping (r2 < 0.1 within a 1000 kb window for PGS-ES and r2 < 0.02 within a 1000 kb window for PGS-PLE) using the 1000 Genomes Project phase 3 [51] as a European linkage disequilibrium (LD) reference panel. Consistent with previous evidence using PGS-ES showing most consistent results with higher thresholds of PGS-ES [42, 43, 52], we employed scores with p-value thresholds of 0.05 and 0.1. For the sake of consistency, the same thresholds were employed for the secondary exploratory analyses with PGS-PLE.

Exposome of early-life experiences

Participants completed the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Brief (CTQ-B) [53], the Childhood Experiences of Care and Abuse Questionnaire-3 Brief (CECA.Q-3) [54], the Parental Bonding Instrument (PBI) [55] the Positive Childhood Experiences Scale (PCE) [56], the Benevolent Childhood Experiences Scale (BCE) [57], and 4 items assessing family-level protective factors from Crush et al. [58].

Given content overlap across scales, we conducted (i) successive Exploratory Factor Analyses (EFAs) to eliminate irrelevant or redundant items using Iterative Target Rotation (ITR) [59, 60], and (ii) a Bifactor Confirmatory Factor Analysis (BCFA) using the WLSMV estimator to identify a general factor. A total of 145 items were included. Details about the data reduction procedure can be found in Lavín et al. [61] Finally, five factor scores were derived from the BCFA (Fig. 1), based on the 94 items retained after the data optimization process. Factor 1 included extra-familiar positive experiences and peer support (“Positive Experiences”); Factor 2 included negative experiences or lack of care related to the paternal figure (“Paternal adversity”); Factor 3 included the same experiences as Factor 2, but for the maternal figure (“Maternal adversity”); and Factor 4 included rarely endorsed experiences such as role reversal and emotional or physical abuse (“Other Adverse Experiences”). Finally, a general factor included loadings from all variables of the model (“General Exposome Factor”). The final bifactor structure showed excellent fit (e.g., comparative fit index) and good model-based reliability (e.g., H index and omega-hierarchical) (see Lavín et al. Phenotypic measures

Psychopathology factor (P-factor)

The Multidimensional Schizotypy Scale-Brief (MSS-B) [62] assessed positive (Cronbach’s α = 0.77), negative (α = 0.70) and disorganized (α = 0.88) schizotypy dimensions. The Community Assessment of Psychic Experiences (CAPE) [63] assessed positive PLE (α = 0.83). Due to a technical issue in the data collection software, 4% of participants had missing data for items from the disorganized MSS subscale and the CAPE positive PLE, which were addressed using multiple imputation [64]. The “Bar Procedure“ [65] was then applied to compress the multiply imputed SPSS files into a single file by pooling the values for each missing data point. The Ideas of Reference (α = 0.75) and Suspiciousness (α = 0.77) subscales of the Schizotypal Personality Questionnaire (SPQ) [66] assessed trait paranoia. Depressive symptoms were measured using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI; α = 0.91) [67] and anxiety symptoms were assessed with the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI; α = 0.90) [68].

An EFA using ITR was performed using the measures described above. It revealed that items could be grouped into four distinct factors with some expected cross-loadings [63]. This four-factor configuration was introduced in the subsequent BCFA. The Model’s fit was good (CFI = 0.947, TLI = 0.945, RMSEA = 0.047, SRMR = 0.066). Four specific factors capturing Affective Dysregulation, Social/Cognitive Malfunctioning, Positive Schizotypy, and Negative Schizotypy, as well as a general factor, accounting for the shared variance among all variables (“p-factor”) were derived.

Positive mental health

Three well-established measures were used: the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES; α = 0.88) [69], the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC; α = 0.84) [70], and the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale (WEMWBS; α = 0.91) [71].

A composite measure of positive mental health was obtained performing an EFA with oblimin rotation. Items clustered by measure, yielding a three-factor solution devoid of cross-loadings. To obtain scores for the positive mental health factor this three-factor structure was entered as the basis of the subsequent BCFA. The model exhibited excellent fit indices (CFI: 0.973; TLI: 0.970; SRMR: 0.044; RMSEA: 0.087).

Functioning

The Social Functioning Questionnaire [72] covers aspects of both social and general functioning (α = 0.62).

Analytic plan

First, descriptive and correlational data between study variables were examined to disregard potential gene-environment correlations that could interfere with the interpretation of GxE effects. GxE analyses were conducted using version 3.6.3 of the LEGIT package in R [73], which was designed to examine the fit of GxE interactions. Following Belsky & Widaman [47], we first used a standard regression to test the significance of the interaction term. We examined whether the interaction yielded an F > 1, which indicates sufficient variation in the interaction term [47, 74] to proceed to the main analytic step, the competitive-confirmatory model testing [48, 75]. This approach works by re-parameterizing the crossover point of the interaction to envision weak and strong versions of GxE models: DS, diathesis-stress, and vantage-sensitivity, and it determines which provides the best fit to the data. Moreover, LEGIT incorporates a recent improvement as compared to the original re-parametrization approach [47], by including re-parameterization of models that do not include the GxE term (intercept only, gene only, environment only, gene and environment only) to examine the fit of these non-GxE models along with the GxE models. The best model fit is evaluated based on the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) [76] fit index. An interaction is classified as “DS” if this model has a) the lowest AIC, and b) the 95% interval of its estimated crossover point is within observable bounds of the environmental score. However, if any of the four models without the interaction term shows the lowest AIC, the interaction will be considered as showing no evidence of GxE. Of note, as a conservative approach to avoid overinterpreting small differences—and although there is no universally accepted rule—models differing by less than 1 point in AIC will be considered statistically indistinguishable in terms of fit, and both should be considered plausible [77]. Including non-GxE models in the comparison process of re-parametrized models is critical to minimize the rate of false positive findings to 5% or lower. Even if the F ratio strategy from the initial regression model already prevents fitting Type I error more than traditional p values would [48], fit indices such as AIC provide a more holistic evaluation of the model by considering both the goodness-of-fit and the simplicity of the model, thus leading to a more robust model selection, especially when multiple models are examined [76, 77].

All analyses included the first two ancestry-informative principal components as covariates in the regression model (see supplementary material) and were trimmed from the subsequent competitive-confirmatory test phase if they were nonsignificant. Finally, resampling procedures including k-fold cross-validation and bootstrapping were conducted to assess model stability and generalizability; details are provided in the supplementary material. The code used for data analysis is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations are in supplementary material (Table S1 and S2). No significant correlations were found between susceptibility factors and environmental or outcome measures, except for small effect size associations between PGS-ES (p < 0.10) and resilience (r = 0.07), and PGS-PLE with PLE (r = 0.06, for threshold p < 0.05), suspiciousness (r = 0.07–08, for thresholds p < 0.05 and p < 0.10 respectively) and ideas of reference (r = 0.06, for threshold p < 0.10).

Discussion

Overall, this study replicates our proof-of-concept examination of the adequacy of the DS model for the psychosis extended phenotype [42, 43] and extends it by using a larger sample and including the full spectrum of positive and negative early-life environmental experiences and both negative and positive outcomes. Several findings supported the DS model, as individuals with higher genetic susceptibility to the environment reporting lower levels of positive experiences in childhood or poor paternal parenting showed increased levels of subclinical psychotic and affective symptoms, decreased functioning and lower positive mental health outcomes; at the same time, they displayed fewer symptoms and increased positive outcomes when exposed to supportive childhood environments compared to participants with lower genetic sensitivity. Thus, as hypothesized, individual differences in genetic sensitivity to the environment operated “for better and for worse”, impacting both positive and negative outcomes. Thus, the almost exclusive focus on negative environmental exposures constrains our understanding of environmental contributions to risk and resilience, and might account for the heterogeneity of results across studies. Notably, the DS relevance of positive experiences was not restricted to affective symptoms, but was trans-syndromic, applying to positive, paranoid and disorganized psychosis dimensions. This is consistent with the critical role of psychosocial factors in psychosis and shows, for the first time, that genetic susceptibility to the environment moderates both adverse and positive experiences. Although the findings require replication, overall, they support the hypothesis that the genetic bases of mental disorders may reflect in part genetic variability in sensitivity to the environment.

The finding that positive environmental factors not only contributed to individuals’ positive mental health and thriving but also showed a protective effect for both affective and psychotic-like symptoms is particularly relevant to psychosis research, traditionally focused on negative exposures. Although recent work has demonstrated protective effects of good family environments [14, 78] and social support [79], including close friendships [80] and neighborhood cohesion [58], in relation to PLE, research is scant regarding early positive environmental experiences. Interestingly, epigenetic research points to potential neurobiological mechanisms that may account for the impact of positive environmental experiences on the genome. For example, the oxytocin system, which has been suggested to be central to social behaviors, appears to adjust to environmental cues among other mechanisms through oxytocin receptor (OXTR) gene methylation. Early parental care has been hypothesized to reduce OXTR methylation, which may enhance gene expression and facilitate positive social responses [81]. But the complicated interplay between genes, epigenetics, hormones and human behavior still is largely uncharted territory [82].

Our nonclinical sample presented greater variance in positive than negative environmental measures, which may have facilitated the detection of DS effects at ‘both sides’ of the continuum of positive experiences. Of note, the Positive Experiences factor was mostly characterized by peer support during school years [61], which is critical during late childhood and adolescence. The fact that our sample is mostly in their 20’s might have facilitated the recall and appraisal of the salience of experiences with peers.

Paternal Adversity also exhibited trans-syndromic effects and impacted individuals’ functioning in a DS manner—in fact, it was the only environmental factor predicting functioning. Thus, for participants with higher genetic susceptibility, paternal experiences had a negative impact when higher levels of adversity existed, but better functioning and less psychopathology when lower adversity and higher care were present. These findings align with recent longitudinal evidence showing that paternal, rather than maternal, care reduced psychiatric symptoms, particularly among individuals with higher environmental sensitivity [83]. Also meta-analytic work has underscored the distinct role of parental behavior in child emotional development, with stronger effects for fathers than mothers [84]. Fathers have an important role in children’s social development (e.g., providing social representations of the external world) [85], which may partially explain why social functioning was only affected by paternal behavior. Of note, our sample is 77% female. Research shows differential gender-based parental dynamics in the context of traditional gender roles (e.g., fathers being more authoritarian or strict than mothers [86], particularly among father-daughter dyads [87]), and gender-specific sensitivity to paternal bonding experiences (e.g., women being more sensitive to paternal caregiving) [88]. Maladaptive paternal control tends to have a more detrimental impact on daughters’ adjustment than similar maternal behaviors [89]. However, increased father involvement in caregiving is associated with fostering resilience in children’s development [85]. These differential effects between maternal and paternal parenting align with the unexpected lack of significant effects of maternal adversity in the present study. One may speculate that optimal maternal behavior is often perceived as a normative aspect of caregiving, and that higher levels of variability may be needed to impact children’s psychological distress, compared to paternal behavior. Furthermore, protective effects of positive paternal behavior may serve as a proxy for overall positive family environment. Father engagement in parenting has been associated with decreased maternal burden and stress and, thus, a healthier family dynamic [90]. Although the Paternal Adversity factor includes some positive experiences (with negative loadings on the factor), both Paternal and Maternal Adversity factors assess adversity due to currently available measures, which may limit detection of effects for positive mental health.

As hypothesized, evidence of DS was found for most dimensions of the extended psychosis phenotype except for negative schizotypy, which was not expected to be associated with increased sensitivity as it is characterized by low openness to experience [91], replicating our initial proof-of-concept study [41]. PGS-ES impacted positive PLE in a DS manner but, unexpectedly, not positive schizotypy. Of note, MSS positive schizotypy had a narrower distribution than CAPE PLE. Despite their overlap, the MSS includes experiences of odd somatic ideation and physical perceptions, not tapped by CAPE, that were less endorsed in our nonclinical sample. The effects of both Positive Experiences and Paternal Adversity were consistently found for the disorganized dimension in a DS manner, supporting the notion that these psychotic-like manifestations are proxy indicators of severity across a mental health severity continuum [48, 92] in which a greater sensitivity to the environment, in interaction with environmental insults, would have more impact on these phenotypes. Suspiciousness showed DS specifically to the effects of Paternal Adversity for those with elevated PGS-ES. Excessive parental overprotection has been associated with paranoid symptoms as a form of self-protection against perceived threats [93], which may be particularly salient in our predominantly female sample [86]. This association may also underlie evidence of intergenerational transmission of paranoia, as parents with higher levels of paranoia may communicate perceived threats to their children and exhibit overprotective parenting behaviors, thus conveying a sense of danger and prompting development of suspicious ideation [94].

No significant interactions emerged for the Exposome or Other Adverse Experiences factors—the latter not being surprising as it comprised a mix of experiences, including those with the lowest endorsement (e.g., parental violence, emotional abuse and physical abuse). The general exposome factor is adversity-driven and reflects methodological constraints in combining valenced experiences. Positive and negative exposures likely represent distinct dimensions rather than bipolar opposites, suggesting that aggregated measures may obscure specific effects. The lack of significant interactions between the Exposome and genetic susceptibility may indicate that a cumulative approach to environmental exposures, as opposed to a specific adversity-types approach, may be less effective to capture DS effects. Different effects may operate for the different types of adversities and protective factors [95]. Likewise, the exposome factor encompassed items from factors that yielded significant GxE effects (i.e., Positive Experiences and Paternal Adversity) and items from factors that did not (i.e., Maternal Adversity and Other Adverse Experiences). Our results suggest that a ‘net’ effect is elusive, that is, a supportive group of friends or positive school environment does not counteract the impact of certain adverse family experiences on the same continuum. Consequently, the detrimental impact of the latter may obscure the potential benefits of the former, and vice versa. Finally, the lack of findings may also be influenced by the nature of the sample, as many college students in this study had low rates of severe childhood adversity. Nonetheless, the exposome approach offers the opportunity to integrate multilevel, multimodal and multidimensional environmental data to adopt a holistic view of environmental experiences [37]. To our knowledge, this is the first study examining DS employing both cumulative and specific approaches, so future studies employing this complementary approach should provide further insights into the potential commonalities and specificities of differential susceptibility to the environment.

The PGS-PLE showed a significant interaction with the Exposome on the p-factor, consistent with the diathesis-stress model, indicating that higher adversity led to more symptoms in individuals with higher PGS-PLE. Zhang & Belsky [96] argue that GWAS-derived scores based on the direct association between genetic variants and the same phenotype to be predicted may not function in a DS fashion, but PGS-PLE was chosen because it was developed from a large UK biobank population sample, unlike clinically derived scores like the schizophrenia polygenic score [45]. The diathesis-stress pattern of PGS-PLE suggests higher vulnerability to risk. The previous proof-of-concept study [44] found some DS effects with PGS-PLE, although not statistically robust, possibly due to differences in sample selection as individuals were oversampled for high schizotypy, whereas the present study used an unselected population.

Several strengths characterize the present study, such as the use of polygenic approaches that show larger cumulative effect sizes and have greater predictive power than candidate-gene studies [96] in DS research. The LEGIT package, used to test DS employing the competitive-confirmatory approach, has demonstrated good accuracy of correctly assigned patterns of interactions and greater predictive ability as compared to other methods (e.g., Regions of Significance) [73]. Essential for DS testing, positive environmental and outcome measures were used. A novel factor-derived approach characterizing the Early Exposome was employed, which allowed testing the impact of an overarching general factor as well as specific factor domains that capture unique aspects of early experiences. Regarding limitations, future replication studies with gender-balanced general population samples as well as clinical samples with longitudinal designs are needed to test generalizability of the present exploratory study’s findings. Employing a nonclinical sample enables the investigation of shared mechanisms across the psychosis continuum without the many confounds associated to clinical status such as medication, biographical disruption, self-stigma, comorbidity, severe impairment, etc. Particularly relevant to the research question, the study of nonclinical participants likely allows for a more accurate assessment of protective psychosocial experiences and positive outcomes. Furthermore, studying early nonclinical manifestations can inform risk and protective mechanisms that might ground preventive strategies. Another limitation of this study is its reliance on retrospective self-reports to assess early experiences. However, the reliability and validity of such reports are supported by evidence showing that subjective self-reports of adversity have greater predictive validity for psychopathology than objective or interview-based measures [97]. Most measures ranged from excellent to good reliability, but some presented acceptable and one of them poor reliability (Social Functioning Questionnaire), which might be problematic in terms of replication. Critically, validation of the cross-over interactions requires further observational, ecological momentary assessments, or multi-informant measures [98] randomized experiments [7].

Conclusion and implications

The results overall support the trans-syndromic validity of the DS model, which is particularly relevant to psychosis given the historic pessimism about prevention and recovery. Recognizing individual differences (e.g., genetic susceptibility to the environment) in response to adversity and support among affected and at-risk individuals can empower and motivate youth, enhancing therapeutic engagement [99]. The protective role of positive experiences supports incorporating resilience-based prevention programs targeting modifiable factors such as childhood environmental conditions to reduce psychosis. Findings underscore the role of interventions aimed at supportive social networks, which may be particularly beneficial for young people with psychosis [100]. Further genome-exposome research from a DS perspective may facilitate the study of emerging neural mechanistic models [101] on neurodevelopmentally sensitive periods [102] involved in increasing sensitivity to the full environmental spectrum, as well as inform precision medicine [103] to tailor interventions to individuals that differ in their susceptibility to psychopathology or responsiveness to specific therapies.
