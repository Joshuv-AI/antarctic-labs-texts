Regulatory Roles of PURPLE FRUIT 1 and OsPa in Pigmentation of Rice Grain
Authors: Yunzhang Xu, Yingqing Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurple rice has long been treasured for health benefits, but the molecular mechanism of its grain pigmentation remains to be fully understood. Previous evidence shows that a bHLH gene, OsB2, is over-expressed in purple-rice’s caryopsis to cause its increased pigmentation and a MYB gene, OsMYB3, decreases the pigmentation when knocked out in purple rice. Nonetheless, these transcription factors are inadequate to explain why hull color of purple rice can vary independently from that of caryopsis. We show here that more than two regulatory (MYB/bHLH/WDR (MBW)) complexes are involved in grain pigmentation of purple rice. A previously unsuspected MYB, encoded by PURPLE FRUIT 1 (PF1), and OsPa (bHLH), also participate in the grain pigmentation. Along with OsB2 and OsMYB3, they form redundant yet differential MBW complexes in purple-rice grain to regulate not only structural genes of the anthocyanin pathway but also OsLAR of the proanthocyanidin pathway. In the developing caryopses, co-expression patterns of OsPF1 and OsB2 can better predict accumulation process of anthocyanins than those of OsMYB3 and OsB2. Disrupting OsPF1 or OsPa via CRISPR/Cas9 in purple rice leads to reduced pigmentations of caryopsis and hulls, with OsPa’s knockout-effect on hull color particularly pronouncing. When paired with OsPa and OsTTG1 to form the MBW complex, OsPF1 can initiate a stronger activation of OsCHI but weaker one of OsLAR than OsMYB3 under the same condition. Compared to significantly enhanced expression levels of OsB2 and OsPa in grains of purple rice, transcriptions of OsPF1 and OsMYB3 remain non-differentiated between straw-white and purple grains at the same developmental stage. The new advances, along with the previously known, have enabled a mechanistic illustration of pigmentations of caryopsis and hulls in purple rice, which may guide future breeding of purple rice in creating varieties of desired grain anthocyanins to improve human health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-026-00921-9.Keywords: Oryza sativa, PURPLE FRUIT 1, Anthocyanin pathway, OsLAR, Differential regulation, Grain color, Hull color, Pathway-regulation, Regulatory alleles

Introduction

Asian rice (Oryza sativa L.) over its long cultivation history (Gross and Zhao 2014; Zuo et al. 2017) has generated over hundred thousands of varieties/landraces (Nakagahra et al. 1997; Gao 2003; Mao et al. 2009). Among them, purple rice has gained increasing attention due to its high nutrition and life-promoting benefits (Sedeek et al. 2023; Tiozon et al. 2023) documented long ago in ancient China (Deng et al. 2013) and India (Valarmathi et al. 2015). Varieties of purple rice are either Indica or Japonica type (Lei 1988) and are collectively referred to as purple/black rice (purple rice thereafter) here.

The purple color, caused by accumulation of anthocyanins, can occur in caryopsis (Yoshimura et al. 2012; Oikawa et al. 2015), hulls (Zhang et al. 2022), stigma, leaf, or leaf sheath (Takahashi 1982; Chin et al. 2016; Hu et al. 2020; Khan et al. 2020; Meng et al. 2021). It is frequently seen at the tip (apiculus) of an otherwise straw-white hull (Tsago et al. 2019; Meng et al. 2021). Traditionally, purple rice refers to only varieties having the purple grain (including both hulls and caryopsis) or purple caryopsis, regardless of its vegetative color. Biochemically, the purple color is brought mostly by one type of anthocyanins, which are generated by the branch of F3’H (flavonoid 3’ hydroxylase) of the anthocyanin pathway, namely, cynidin-3-glucoside, and followed by its methylated form, peonidin-3- glucoside (Abdel-Aal et al. 2006; Sompong et al. 2011; Frank et al. 2012; Bhuvaneswari et al. 2020). Most loci of the structural genes of the pathway have been reported, including OsCHS, OsCHI, OsF3H, OsF3’H, OsDFR, and OsANS (Reddy et al. 1996; Furukawa et al. 2006; Shih et al. 2008; Hong et al. 2012); however, the modifying genes such as Os3GT still need more evidence.

The early recognized transcription factors (TFs) were two bHLHs, encoded by Ra on the fourth chromosome and Rb on the first chromosome (Hu et al. 1996). Ra was later identified as OsB1, next to OsB2, another bHLH gene. A 5’ rearrangement of OsB2 in purple rice is considered responsible for the emergence of the purple caryopsis (Oikawa et al. 2015). Both OsB1 and OsB2 can induce anthocyanin production in aleurones of a straw-white cultivar when introduced along with C1 (Cone et al. 1986), a MYB gene responsible for anthocyanin production in maize grain (Sakamoto et al. 2001), in experimental trials. However, pericarp, instead of aleurone, is the primary location for anthocyanin storage in the grain of Asian rice (Yoshimura et al. 2012), which is just above the seed coat and the aleurone layer of caryopsis. Consistent with demonstrated up-regulation of OsB2 in purple caryopsis (Oikawa et al. 2015), OsB2 transcribes in pericarps of several purple-rice varieties, where little expression of OsC1, OsB1, or OsRb can be seen (Zheng et al. 2019). Under the name S1, OsB2 was once taken as the main factor accounting for pigmented hulls (Sun et al. 2018), along with OsC1 (Reddy et al. 1998) and OsDFR (historically A1 or Rd). OsC1 was subsequently shown to regulate anthocyanin productions in leaves (along with OsRb, Zheng et al. 2019), leaf sheath (Chin et al. 2016; Jiang et al. 2024), and stigma (along with OsB1, Meng et al. 2021). Nonetheless, its role in grain pigmentation appears small, given the low presence in grain transcriptome.

From historical perspective, the progress on the anthocyanin pathway of rice has largely built upon the pioneering work conducted in other plant systems. Genetic research on the biosynthesis of anthocyanin compounds started to take shape in the early 1960 (Grisebach and Hahlbrock 1974), and regulation of anthocyanin production was first systematically investigated in maize (Cone et al. 1986; Chandler et al. 1989; Goff et al. 1992; Marrs et al. 1995) and understood further in petunia (Quattrocchio et al. 1993; deVetten et al. 1997; Mol et al. 1998), and soon gained a broad understanding in other species in the past decades (Shirley et al. 1995; Elomaa et al. 2003; Park et al. 2007; Zhu et al. 2015). Substantial evidence indicates that anthocyanin pathways are primarily regulated by protein complexes consisting of MYB, bHLH and WDR (WD-repeating protein), often referred to collectively as MBW complexes (Hichri et al. 2011).

The operation of MBW complex in regulating tissue/organ-specific pigmentation process has also been cited in rice, as shown by the leaf-specific complex OsC1/OsPb/OsTTG1 (Zheng et al. 2019). For straw-white grains, two similarly named bHLH genes appear to influence the color of apiculus, OsPA (Os01g39580), which may increase pigmentation possibly via interacting with OsC1 (Tsago et al. 2019), and OsPa (Os04g47040), disruption of which can demolish the color of apiculus (Meng et al. 2021). For purple grains, OsMYB3, also known as OsKala3 (Maeda et al. 2014), can interact with OsB2 and its over-expression enhances the color of caryopsis (Zheng et al. 2021). The gene’s promoter region contains a specific insertion, which is proposed to be related to its increased expression (Kim et al. 2021). Meanwhile, disruption of the WDR protein-encoding OsTTG1, also named as OsPAC1 (Zheng et al. 2019), causes diminished coloration at the whole plant level (Yang et al. 2021). Thus far, one MBW complex, consisting of OsMYB3, OsB2, and OsTTG1, has been shown to participate in regulation of purple caryopsis (Zheng et al. 2021). The complex alone, however, is insufficient for understanding grains of purple caryopsis with colored or non-colored hulls, a phenomenon long exhibited in some traditional varieties (Lim et al. 2023; Subramanian et al. 2024). The reason is simple. If the complex of OsMYB3/OsB2/OsTTG1 also regulates hull coloration, it would be difficult to interpret the absence of hull color in some varieties of purple rice without implicating further factor(s). If the complex is restricted to caryopsis, then what is the complex involved in the coloration of hulls? The key issue here concerns the genetic basis of grain coloration: are there unidentified regulators participating in the coloration processes from hull to caryopsis in purple rice?

To address the issue, we focused on several MYB and bHLH genes early in our study. With accumulating evidence, two candidate genes attracted particular attention for their possible roles in grain pigmentation. One was OsPa, the function of which had not been reported in purple rice but its role in color of apiculus of otherwise straw-white varieties was reported (Meng et al. 2021). Another one is a MYB gene (Os01g50110), which was little known in rice besides its expression in developing seeds of a japonica cultivar with straw-white grain and some similarity to maize C1 in amino-acid sequence (Suzuki et al. 1997). Our preliminary survey found its significant expression in purple grains. Available data also show that similar to OsB2, OsPa hardly expressed in panicle transcriptomes of Nipponbare (Davidson et al. 2012) and 9311 (Gao et al. 2013), two cultivars of straw-white grains. Nevertheless, transcripts of its orthologs are present in transcriptomes (GSE111438) of developing panicles of O. nivara and O. rufipogon (Swetha et al. 2018), two progenitor species of Asian rice (Lu et al. 2022), which bare somewhat pigmented caryopses.

Here, through a comprehensive analysis of genotype, phenotype, gene expression, gene silencing, and various trials of crossing and functional interactions, we bring ample evidence for the involvements of the two candidate genes in the pigmentation process of purple-rice’s grain, including (but not limited to) their regulatory capacities on not only structural genes of the anthocyanin pathway but also OsLAR, a gene encoding a protein similar to leucoanthocyanidin reductase (LAR) in legume (Tanner et al. 2003). LAR of the proanthocyanidin pathway can convert leucoanthocyanidins to flavan-3-ol, a precursor to condensed tannin (reviewed by Dixon et al. 2005). We therefore named the MYB gene as PURPLE FRUIT 1 (PF1) here for its critical role in grain pigmentation.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Sampling

Two purple rice varieties, Jixuenuo (a landrace of Indica type from Yunnan, China) and Heidao (a landrace of Japonica type from Heilongjiang, China), and their crossed progeny were sampled in RIL populations D3 (F7 generation) and D5 (F8 generation) in the paddy field of IBCAS. Plants (344) of population D5 showed uniformed purple-grain phenotype, while plants (357) of D3 segregated into ones (258) of purple grains (pigmented hull and caryopsis) and ones (99) of straw-white grains. The D3 population provided samples for comparisons of the two types of grains, which were taken on neighboring plants to control for environmental heterogeneity within the paddy field (~ 6 × 10 m2).

To enable a sensible collection of samples in the field, we monitored the pigmenting process of caryopsis on multiple plants over two growth seasons. To reduce biases possibly associated with rhythmic expressions of genes, the sampling of caryopses was conducted every other day between 4 pm and 5 pm in population D5, which had uniformed purple grains. The initial 13-day period of pigmentation was subsequently captured by samples at six sequential stages (from 3 DAP to 13 DAP). Each of the stages had four random samples (≥ 5 caryopses per sample) collected among eight random plants. The four samples of each stage were then split in half at random; one half was subjected to the pigment analysis and the other to transcript estimation.

A white-rice accession, B16-44, from a cross of two straw-white rice materials (Yunjing20 and Bosanger) offered protoplasts for dual luciferase analysis, as reported (Li et al. 2024). Throughout quantitative analysis, sampling of plants was controlled for time, environment, and developmental stage across trials to enable comparisons sought in this study.

DNA/RNA Extraction and Gene Cloning

Genomic DNAs, which were extracted using the CTAB method from young leaves of purple rice, were used to amplify targeted regions (Table S1) with specific primers (Table S2). Grain RNAs were collected from part of the accessions in protocols previously shown (Wang et al. 2012). PCR products were sequenced via Sanger’s method, with the chromatograms carefully examined for accuracy. Verified sequences were compared to the orthologs of known genomes (Table S3) for genotyping. An allelic sequence identical to those of the Japonica genomes (Nipponbare, Kitaake) is referred to as _a allele, one the same as the ortholog of Indica genomes (9311, Minghui63, Shuhui498) as _b allele, and another as _c allele here. The allele specific to purple rice is designated as _p allele.

Dual Luciferase Assays

The coding regions of OsPF1, OsB1, OsB2, and OsTTG1 were obtained with a high fidelity polymerase (KOD FX Neo, TOYOBO, Japan) from grain cDNAs of Jixuenuo, and those of OsMYB3, OsC1, and OsPa were similarly gathered from Heidao. The sequences were incorporated in frame into the vector pJIT163 (Guerineau et al. 1992) to build effecter vectors. About 1Kb-long 5’ regions of the structural genes (OsCHS1, OsF3H, OsF3’H, OsDFR, and OsANS, Os3GT, and OsLAR) were amplified from gDNAs of Heidao using specific primers (Table S2) and that of OsCHI from Malugu, a landrace that has a stronger promoter than others tested here (Fig. S1). Construction of the reporter and effector vectors followed the previous protocols (Zhu et al. 2015) with enzyme cutting sites shown in Table S2.

Fresh protoplasts were gathered from about 10-d old rice leaves that grew under the condition reported (Li et al. 2024). The protocol of Yoo et al. (2007) was mostly followed, with modifications below. Leaf strips cut in 0.5 mm wide were digested at 28 °C for 7 h in the dark with gentle shaking (55 rpm). Effecter and reporter vectors were mixed in 1:1 ratio of 2 µg each before introducing into the protoplasts, along with 0.2 µg of the internal control vector (pRUC) for each reaction. The transformed protoplasts were incubated at 28 °C overnight (~ 16 h) before evaluation of expression levels of the reporter protein, as shown by Li et al. (2024).

Protein Expression and Purification

The coding sequence for the DNA-binding domain of OsPF1 or OsMYB3 was ligated in frame into pCold-TF vector (Takara, Japan) and expressed in E. coli strain Transetta (DE3) before purified by a 3 cm column of Ni Sepharose (GE Healthcare, USA). The coding sequence for the DNA-binding domain of OsB2 or OsPa was inserted in frame into the pMAL-c2G vector (New England Biolabs, USA) and expressed protein was purified using a maltose packed column, as described (Wang et al. 2013). The purified protein was examined in 12% SDS-PAGE gel to evaluate purity and concentration prior to the DNA-binding tests below.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)

High-quality oligonucleotides were prepared (Sangon, China), based on candidate cis elements at the promoter regions of the structural genes (OsCHS, OsCHI, OsF3H, OsF3’H, OsDFR, and OsANS, and Os3GT). Probes were made through natural annealing and prepared in 10 pmol. Purified protein was incubated with the probe in the same molar concentration at the room temperature for 25 min. The reaction solution was separated on 8% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel following the instructions of Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA) Kit (Invitrogen, USA). The gel was first stained with SYBR® Green to determine if a binding occurred between the probes and protein. When the binding occurred, the same gel was cleaned and further stained with SYPRO® Ruby to see whether bound protein co-migrated to the same position at the DNA-binding. Details see Wang et al. (2013).

Yeast Two Hybrids (Y2H)

The entire-coding sequence of OsPF1 or OsMYB3 was inserted in frame into vector pAD-GAL4 2.1 and that of OsB1, OsB2, OsPa, or OsTTG1 into vector pBD-GAL4 Cam using HybriZAP-2.1 yeast two-hybrid system (Stratagene, USA), as shown previously (Li et al. 2024). A possible protein interaction was evaluated at both the promoter of HIS3 on the three-deficient medium (SD/-Leu-His-Trp) added with and without 3-Amino-1,2,4-Triazole (3-AT), pending on the degree of background expression of HIS3, and the promoter of LacZ by enzyme activity of β-galactosidase, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Yeast One Hybrid (Y1H)

The 5’ sequence of a targeted gene was inserted into the pHIS2-based reporter to replace the original 5’ region (p53). TF vectors are from those of Y2H tests above, introduced in 1:1 with the reporter vectors into yeast strain Y187. Interactions of TF proteins and promoter regions were evaluated, following Li et al. (2024).

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC) Assays

The coding regions without the termination codon of OsMYB3, OsPF1, OSB2, OSPA, or OsTTG1 were inserted in frame into pSPYCE and/or pSPYNE vectors (HonorGene, China). The constructs were transferred in various combinations into Agrobacterium strain EHA105 when the bacterial concentration reached OD600 of 0.5, following Walter et al. (2004). The transformed solution was injected into the leaf epidermis of Nicotiana benthamiana that had grown for about one month in a growth chamber under the condition of 22 °C light of 16 h and 20 °C darkness of 8 h. After incubating under 22 °C of 16 h light and 20 °C of 8 h darkness for about 36 h, the YFP fluorescence was photographed at 60Х under a two-photon fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). The background fluorescence was monitored with empty vectors without TF sequences to exclude possibly signals from false positive.

Knock-Out Tests Via CRISPR/CAS9

Fresh caryopses of Heidao were prepared to make healthy calli through tissue culture. Gene-specific guides (sgDNAs) targeting N- and/or C-terminals were designed for OsPF1 and OsPa based on bioinformatic analysis and specificity. The synthesized guide DNAs were inserted into vector pCBSG032, which carried the promoter of rice U6 for sgDNA, the CAS9 gene, and the NOS terminator (Biogle, China). Each construct was introduced into Agrobacteria tumefaciens (DH5α) for infection of rice calli, following Hiei et al. (1994), to test for its effectiveness. Only healthy looking seedlings carrying desired mutations at the target site (T0) were grown to collect T1 grains. T1 generation was subject to further sequencing for confirmation of altered sites and phenotypic analysis, along with the wild type as control growing under the same growth condition.

Transcript Sampling and Quantifications

Since transcription of flavonoids genes are sensitive to environmental perturbations (e.g., those of light, temperature, metabolites, and hormones), biological replicates of the same accession were collected at comparable time of day and location to control for these factors. RNAs of Jixuenuo grown in the paddy field of IBCAS were sampled between 4 and 5 pm at the same location (~ 2 × 2 m2). In RIL population D3, plants carrying straw-white or purple grains were sampled at 6 DAP (~ 5 pm), where 5–6 caryopses per plant were collected in replicates at neighboring plants (one plant showed white-grain phenotype and the other purple-grain) of two localities in the same field. In RIL population D5, five plants growing within 1.5 × 1.5 m2 provided caryopses at each developmental stage across the pigmentation process. Quantities of newly synthesized cDNAs were measured by the protocol of Lu et al. (2012) on StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) and kept under mineral oil at -20˚C if not used immediately. Standard sequences for the target genes were prepared at a concentration of at least 108 copies, and co-amplified along with unknown samples, using allele-specific primers (Table S2) and costumed 2x qPCR solutions (Premix Ex Taq, TaKaRa, Japan). All conditions of amplification were optimized for specific alleles prior to the formal quantifications of transcript number. Technical replicates were at least 2 for each data point. The absolute number of specific transcripts was evaluated, as described (Lu et al. 2012).

Anthocyanin Quantification

Developing caryopses (> 3 per biological sample) were weighed to 0.1 mg and frozen in liquid nitrogen to be ground with magnetic beads at 55 Hz for 10 ‒ 30 s. A volume of 200–500 µl of methanol with 1% HCl (m/v) was added to each sample and the solution was slowly rotated for 24 h at 4˚C. The supernatant was cleaned by centrifugation at 14 000 rpm and the water phase was measured for absorbance at 530 nm and 657 nm under a spectrophotometer (Evolution 300, ThermoFisher, USA). For mature grains, hulls and caryopses were separated and each was extracted in 500 µl methanol with 1% HCl as above. The anthocyanin content was estimated as A530-A657 per fresh weight of each sample.

Statistical Analysis

Cladograms of related genes were constructed via the phylogenetic package MEGA 6.0 (Tamura et al. 2013) using the Maximum Likelihood method and nucleotide sequences and standard settings. A gene tree showing the best likelihood was reported. Student’s t-test was carried out for difference of means throughout the tests. One-way ANOVA was conducted for the variation of gene expressions across developmental stages and two-way ANOVA was performed for grain types and plants. All tests were performed in S-PLUS 6.1 (Insightful, USA).

Results

PF1 and Pa can Interact with Each Other and Promoters of Structural Genes of the Anthocyanin Pathway in Rice

To understand roles of OsPF1 and OsPa, we examined their transcriptions in comparable plants of Jixuenuo randomly sampled in the paddy field at the same time, along with those of known regulators using the absolute quantification method (Lu et al. 2012). Transcript copy numbers of OsPF1 in roots, leaves, and different stages of inflorescences and fruits across plants indicate that it is preferentially found in developing flowers/grains (Fig. 1a). The transcript level of OsPF1 in the 25 DAP endosperms outnumbers that of OsC1 (one-tailed t-test, P = 0.009, n = 4) and that of OsMYB3 (one-tailed t-test, P = 0.023, n = 4). OsPa transcribes significantly more in young fruits than elsewhere (Fig. 1b, P < 0.0001 according to the t-distribution of df = 7). In comparison, OsTTG1 shows low but comparable levels of transcription across organs (Fig. 1c). These con-organic expression patterns warrant examinations of possible protein interactions between OsPF1 (or OsMYB3) and OsPa (or OsB2) since components of a MBW complex typically interact with one another during regulation of its target genes. Using yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) system HybriZap2.1, we documented significant interactions of OsPF1 with OsPa or OsB2 using reporters of HIS3 (Fig. 1d) and lacZ (Fig. 1e). Protein interactions of OsPa with OsPF1, OsMYB3, or OsTTG1 were further visualized in leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana through BiFC analysis. The OsPF1-OsPa interaction is mainly inside the nucleus, as shown by the lightened nucleus rendered by the yellow fluorescent protein resulted from the interaction, whereas the OsMYB3-OsPa and OsTTG1-OsPa interactions are visible in both nucleus and cytoplasm (Fig. 1f).

Since the MYB and bHLH components of a MBW complex are expected to bind to promoters of targeted genes, binding capacities of OsPF1_b and OsPa_a were subsequently examined in the electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA), with OsMYB3_p and OsB2_p being the positive controls. The probes were prepared according to segments of the native promoters containing the suspected cis acting elements (Fig. S1) of the anthocyanin structural genes (OsCHS1, OsCHI, OsF3H, OsF3’H, OsDFR, OsANS1, and Os3GT), orthologs of which had been analyzed across multiple species (Wang et al. 2013; Zhu et al. 2015). The candidate cis elements were first tested with OsPF1 or OsMYB3. After a positive binding reaction did occur to a probe, the probe was then mutated at the suspected cis element to examine the consequence. The experimental results indicate that OsPF1 (Fig. 2a) or OsMYB3 (Fig. 2b) can bind directly to the promoters of the structural genes and recognize the same cis elements at most of the loci except OsF3’H, where different cis elements at the promoter region are preferred between the two MYBs (Fig. 2b).

Further EMSA analysis indicates that, as OsB2, OsPa can bind to G-box-like cis elements at promoters of the anthocyanin structural genes (Fig. 2c), and mutations at the predicted sites largely abolished the physical binding. At the DNA binding domain, OsB2 differs from OsPa by five amino acids, showing more tolerant than OsPa to mutations at the cis elements of OsDFR, OsF3’H, and OsANS1 (Fig. 2c). The two bHLH proteins consequently have overlapping yet diverse specificities of activation on these genes. In Y1H, OsPa alone can activate OsANS1pro (Fig. S2a) or in combination with OsMYB3 and OsTTG1 to initiate OsANS1pro (Fig. S2b) or Os3GTpro (Fig. S2c). Here, the promoter sequences (Fig. S1) are designated in subscript.

The experimental evidence above suggests that OsPF1 and OsPa are capable of forming a MBW complex with OsTTG1. Meanwhile, OsPF1 (or OsPa) can also constitute a complex with OsB2 (or OsMYB3), along with OsTTG1. Hence, four MBW complexes are likely present in purple grain. To know their relative capacities in gene activation, we carried out the experiments below.

Redundant but Differential Regulations of OsPF1 (OsMYB3)/OsB2(OsPa)/OsTTG1

Single and combined activations of OsPF1 and OsPa of the anthocyanin pathway genes were further examined by assays of dual luciferase expressions in freshly prepared protoplasts of a straw-white accession, which presumably had little expressions of the native OsPa and OsB2. The LUC reporters were driven by a 5’ sequence including at least partial local promoter, and effectors were based on entire CDs of OsPF1_b, OsMYB3_p, OsC1, OsPa_a, OsB2_p, or OsTTG1_b. When acting alone, OsPF1 initiates significant transcriptions from OsCHS1pro and OsDFRpro, so does OsMYB3 at OsCHS1pro and OsANS1pro, while OsC1 is capable of activating all three enzyme genes here of the anthocyanin pathway (Fig. S3). Likewise, OsPa alone can significantly activate OsCHIpro, OsF3Hpro, and OsANS1pro, whereas the candidate Os3GTpro responds little to these single effectors (Fig. S3).

Activations by MBW complex are more effective at tested 5’ regions than those of single TFs (Fig. 3). When supplied with the same quantity of TF vectors in cells, the four types of MBW complexes initiate the structural genes in varied efficiencies. Overall, OsMYB3/OsB2/OsTTG1 is a stronger activator for the anthocyanin pathway than others, particularly for OsCHS1pro (Fig. 3). However, OsPF1/OsB2/OsTTG1 outperforms OsMYB3/OsB2/OsTTG1at OsCHIpro (Fig. 3a). Meanwhile, OsPa-associated MBW complexes are more effective in activating OsANS1pro and Os3GTpro than the OsB2-associated MBW complexes (Fig. 3b). The differences in activation levels of the structural genes are highly significant between different MBW complexes when comparisons are made within and between panels (Fig. 3).

To understand whether the proanthocyanidin pathway can be regulated by the complexes above, as seen with an anthocyanin-related MYB in grape (Bogs et al. 2007) or MBW in Arabidopsis (Zhu et al. 2015), we investigated in rice possible activations of the complexes on OsLAR (AP014959.1), a gene carrying about 47% similarity in amino acid to legume LAR (Fig. S4a). In dual luciferase assays as performed above, OsLARpro (Fig. S4b) reacts positively to the TF effectors (harboring full coding regions of OsPF1, OsMYB3, OsPa, or OsB2) provided alone or in complexes (Fig. 3c) but not to the effector of OsB1 alone (Fig. S4c). OsLAR can also be activated by the complexes incorporating OsC1, though at levels lower than those involving OsPF1 or OsMYB3 under the same condition (Fig. S4d).

The results above indicate that structural gene of not only the anthocyanin pathway but also the proanthocyanidin pathway (pending more research on the latter) can be activated redundantly and at different rates by the MBW complexes. It becomes instantly interesting to know the relative abundance of the TFs in cells, as it can influence the relative importance of the TFs on these pathways. In the RIL population D3, we had a chance to compare grain TFs and their target genes in pigmented versus non-pigmented panicles at an early stage of fruiting, as shown below.

Active Transcriptions of OsPF1, OsMYB3, and OsLAR in the Early-Stage Caryopses

The RIL population (D3) was a good cohort for the comparisons above, due to its dimorphic grain color but a similar genetic background (Fig. 4a). Simultaneously collected grains (6 days after pollination (DAP)) from plants carrying non-pigmented caryopses and those having pigmented ones were analyzed for relative contributions of MYBs and bHLHs to pigmentation of caryopsis. Transcript levels of OsPF1, OsMYB3, OsB2, and OsPa were estimated from the samples’ cDNAs, along with representative genes of anthocyanin and proanthocyanidin pathways (OsCHS1, OsDFR, and OsLAR). Experimental results demonstrate that despite the low expressions of OsB2 and OsPa at this early developmental stage, both genes are more abundant in caryopses accumulating anthocyanins than ones without such accumulation (Fig. 4b). By contrast, the expressions of OsPF1 varied more between the localities than between colored and non-colored caryopses, and so did the expression of OsMYB3 (Fig. 4c).

Developmental Profiling Reveals Major Regulators in Coloration of Caryopsis

Field observations suggest that grain pigmentation typically starts at the tip of a young caryopsis at 5 DAP, extends to the dorsal ridge till 9 DAP, and then spreads to the lower/middle portion from 10 DAP. By 13 DAP, the entire caryopsis is covered by anthocyanin pigments (Fig. 5a). The developing fruit then darkens till maturation. Anthocyanin quantifications indicate that the coloration of caryopsis clearly follows accumulation of anthocyanins (Fig. 5b).

Transcript quantifications indicate that OsMYB3 expresses more at early stages but relatively less at later stages of caryopsis’ development, whereas expression of OsPF1 increases significantly at later developmental stages, displaying a pattern nearly parallel to that of the anthocyanin accumulation after 7 DAP (Fig. 5c). Noticeably, OsLAR shows a nearly bimodal pattern of expression, with the lowest level near 7–9 DAP (Fig. 5f). 7 DAP is the time when relevant TFs, OsMYB3 and OsPF1 in particular, are at low levels due to reduced transcriptions. Altered OsPF1 Reduces Pigmentation of Grains

Phenotypic impacts of disrupting OsPF1 were evaluated in Heidao using the CRISPR/Cas9 system under its specific guide DNA that targeted the C terminal end of OsPF1. Unlike wild-type Heidao (Fig. S6a), the insertion lines from two differently mutated plants (Fig. 6a) have a shortened OsPF1 (without 13 amino acids). Grains developed on T1 plants with homozygous insertion within each line show visibly less pigmentation either near the embryo side or over the entire caryopses, and hulls are less colored as well (Fig. 6b). The result was further verified by the anthocyanin contents of the whole caryopses, which were measured between the wild type and the mutant lines simultaneously sampled three times over the fruit maturation period (Fig. 6c). The mutant lines maintain colored ligules (Fig. S6b) but have lighter color of pedicels (Fig. S6c) and largely non-pigmented stigmas (Fig. 6d). These phenotypes suggest significant effects of mutated OsPF1 on grain color.

OsPa is Responsible for Hull-Specific Synthesis of Anthocyanins

Though quantity of OsPa’s transcripts is close to that of OsB2 at 5 DAP of purple rice at the whole-grain level (Fig. 1b), significantly more transcript copies of OsB2 than those of OsPa exhibit in caryopses on and after 6 DAP (Figs. 4b and 5d). To evaluate effect of OsPa on grain’s pigmentation, a gene silencing experiment via the CRISPR/Cas9 system above was carried out in Heidao using the guide DNA targeting the second exon (Fig. 6e). The T1 generation of mutated OsPa, validated by sequencing in more than three lines of deletion (#5, #6, and #7), consistently shows non-pigmented hulls and pedicels but still pigmented caryopses (Fig. 6f) and stigmas. The caryopses, nonetheless, have much reduced anthocyanin contents in comparison to the wild type growing in the same environment (Fig. 6b). Since changes introduced at the second exon of OsPa completely abolished the function of OsPa, the whole impact of the gene was seen on the anthocyanin contents of hull and caryopsis at grain maturity (Fig. 6c). Clearly, OsPa has a larger impact on hull than on caryopsis because hull color is hardly observable in the mutants of deletion lines (Fig. 6f).

Since the genetically modified lines of OsPF1 or OsPa all maintain purple ligules, influence of OsPa or OsPF1 on vegetative coloration of purple rice appears to be minimal. After putting the results above in the context of previous reports mentioned in the introduction, we conclude that while each of OsB2 and OsPa contributes to caryopsis’ color, OsPa has a major effect on hull’s pigmentation. Though only the C-terminal portion of OsPF1 was knocked out and the silencing experiment hence revealed its partial impact on anthocyanin quantity of caryopsis, the in vivo expression of OsPF1 (which is highly predictive of grain pigmentation) and its greater number of transcripts than those of OsMYB3 (Fig. 5c) support its significant role in caryopsis’s pigmentation. Collectively, a molecular mechanism involving OsPF1, OsMYB3, OsB2, OsPa, and OsTTG1emerges here: pigmentations of caryopsis and hulls involve four possible MBW complexes that are characterized by different MYBs and bHLHs. The relative impacts of the genes on grain pigmentation are mostly defined by their interactions, expression levels, and specificities of the complexes involved when everything else holds equal (Fig. 6g).

Discussion

Tissue-Specific Regulations of Pathways via Redundant but Differential Complexes

We have shown here that several regulatory complexes can form and function in purple rice, including OsPF1/OsPa (OsB2)/OsTTG1 and OsMYB3/ OsPa (OsB2)/OsTTG1, to interchangeably activate structural genes of the anthocyanin pathway. The stronger protein-protein interaction between OsPF1 and OsB2 and their coordinated transcript levels (higher than those of OsMYB3 and OsPa) in developing caryopsis make OsPF1/OsB2/OsTTG1 a major regulatory complex during the pigmentation of caryopsis. Unlike the mode of single regulator, such as zeaP (Grotewold et al. 1994), which typically results in the presence or absence of a character (e.g., having phlobaphene), complex-forming regulators can fine tune a trait (e.g., pigmented organ/tissue) when acting redundantly. For instance, over-expressing OsB2 can deepen coloration of both apiculus and hulls (Sun et al. 2018), and abolishing OsPa in a straw-white variety with pigmented apiculus causes colorless apiculus (Meng et al. 2021) given the generally local expression of OsB2 in straw-white varieties. Quantifications of transcripts further suggest that the expression level of OsPa in hulls can vary from that in apiculus (Fig. 4c of Meng et al. 2021). The tissue-specific expression may occur to OsB2 as well. We show here that disrupting OsPa in purple rice brings straw-white hulls but leaving apiculus still somewhat pigmented (Fig. 6f), presumably due to more OsB2 in apiculus than in the rest of hulls of purple rice. Tissue-specific expressions of functionally redundant regulators can therefore generate varied patterns of coloration between tissues (e.g., pigmented apiculus with straw-white hulls, purple caryopsis with straw-white hulls, etc.).

The tissue-specific regulation can be governed by multiple switches, including tissue-/development-specific gene expressions, gene-specific interactions at protein-protein and/or protein-DNA levels (e.g., affinities of TFs to varied cis elements), dosage/combined effect of redundant complexes, and environmental responses (Li et al. 2024). The machinery can greatly expand regulatory complexity. For examples, plant’s responses to light may trigger different levels of TF’s expression in vivo, bringing varied strength of regulation on the targeted pathway; specific TF-DNA and TF-TF interactions may cause expression patterns of structural genes to diverge even in the same TF environment (Fig. 5). It is therefore essential that expression patterns are validated by data collected across years (environments) or different populations. Cautions have been taken here in two-year sampling involving three populations (Figs. 1a and 4, and Fig. 5) to ensure coherent patterns.

Varied expression patterns of TFs during fruit development may lead to an early accumulation of proanthocyanidin and then elevation of anthocyanins. Though the metabolic data are pending in purple rice, at least two other species share this pattern. Grape berry (Vitis vinifera) accumulates proanthocyanidin first and then anthocyanins, if pigmentation of fruit is to occur (Bogs et al. 2005, 2007). A surge in proanthocyanidins is also observed in early-developing strawberry (Baldi et al. 2018). It remains to be seen whether this pattern holds for more species.

In O. sativa, MBW complexes strongly regulate structural genes of the anthocyanin pathway and moderately activate OsLAR, a gene presumably belonging to the proanthocyanidin pathway, showing no clear-cut division of labor between the pathways (Fig. 3). Additional research on OsLAR is clearly needed to further characterize the gene and verify the pattern with more data. In dicots, regulations between the pathways of anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins appear far more differentiated. In A. thaliana, anthocyanidin reductase (ANR or BAN) of the proanthocyanidin pathway is under the control of a specific complex TT2/TT8/TTG1 (Nesi et al. 2001) and can hardly be activated by the anthocyanin-regulating complex PAP1/GL3/TTG1 (Zhu et al. 2015). In Vitis vinifera, VvMYBPA1 can activate VvLAR1 of the proanthocyanidin pathway but not VvUFGT of the anthocyanin pathway (Bogs et al. 2007).

Genetic Basis for Historical Grain-Color Alterations in Purple Rice

From historical perspective, transcriptions of Pa, B2, PF1, MYB3, DFR, and ANS1 are readily seen in the panicle transcriptomes reported by Swetha et al. (2018) for modern populations of the progenitor species O. nivara and O. rufipogon of Asian rice, which is congruent with these species’ pigmented (but not dark) grains. Nonetheless, transcripts of Pa, B2, and ANS1 are extremely rare in modern white-rice varieties, as shown in the expression data reported on Nipponbare (Davidson et al. 2012), 9311 (Gao et al. 2013), and Pusa basmati 1 (Swetha et al. 2018). Consistently, lowered accumulations of anthocyanins and tannin in caryopses are observed among straw-white varieties of rice (Finocchiaro et al. 2010). Since the fading accumulation of anthocyanins and its associated phenotype of straw-white grain are common in a majority of varieties across all five subgroups of rice, the causal selection likely occurred early in rice domestication. Coherently, early-stage mutations were found under positive selection at the coding regions of OsPa and OsMYB3 (Lu et al. 2022).

We have shown here that opposite to the straw-white varieties, the darkened color of purple rice is largely due to elevated expressions of OsPa and OsB2 in grains, in line with the significant impact of OsB2 previously reported (Oikawa et al. 2015). Purple rice was once featured as “coal-black panicles” in a well-known literature of the 16th century of China (Li 1590), which clearly depicted its dark-colored hulls and caryopsis. As purple rice is notably absent in subgroups such as aus and aromatic, a selection later in the rice history than the above one is likely the cause for the creation of purple rice. The collective evidence indicates that genetic basis for historical grain color alterations in O. sativa has largely centered on the anthocyanin pathway but involved different patterns of gene expressions. Though exact details for the expression patterns of OsPa and OsB2 requires further investigation, the critical and direct roles of OsPa and OsB2 in pigmentation of different parts of a grain (i.e., hulls and pericarps, respectively) illustrated here (Fig. 6g) may help development of new breeding strategies.

Unlike OsPa and OsB2, OsPF1 and OsMYB3 show no significant changes in gene expression between grains of purple and straw-white rice that share the same genetic background (Fig. 4c). Their transcripts are readily visible in the reported transcriptomes of panicles of straw-white cultivars including PB-1 (Swetha et al. 2018), Nipponbare (Davidson et al. 2012), and 9311 (Gao et al. 2013), suggesting more or less consistent transcriptions of the genes across varieties. The increased expression of OsPF1 toward later developmental stage of purple caryopsis also concurs with a white-rice cultivar in this gene’s enhanced expression at 14 DAP (Suzuki et al. 1997), which implies lack of a major change in expression pattern during grain development between purple and straw-white varieties. Due to the absence of obvious historical changes in transcription patterns of these MYB genes, different strategies are needed in their applications in molecular breeding.

Implications for Molecular Breeding of Purple Rice

The historical down-then-up regulations of OsB2 and OsPa showcased how first straw-white then purple grains were created in Asian rice. Maintaining the expression levels of the two bHLH genes is therefore the top priority in molecular breeding of purple rice as the expression levels can be easily altered when a purple-rice variety is crossed with a white-straw variety. As high yield is the general goal of breeding programs (Maeda et al. 2014; Rahman et al. 2016; Sedeek et al. 2023), maintaining the quality of purple rice can be challenging in some cases. With the advances discussed here, novel designs can be developed in molecular breeding to reach these goals. For instance, as a more potent activator of OsLAR than OsB2 (Fig. 3c), OsPa is desired to keep a low profile in caryopsis to ensure less tannin thus better taste of purple rice; meanwhile, the opposite is preferred for hulls since more tannins in hulls may help prevent damage from herbivores and other stresses due to tannin’s antimicrobial and antioxidant properties (reviewed by Gupta et al. 2025), which in effect enhances yield.

For MYB genes, OsMYB3 is more similar (49%) to TT2 of Arabidopsis than to OsPF1 (36%) in amino acid sequence and a major influencing factor during the early stage of fruit development (< 7 DAP). It can be better used in modulating tannin content than OsPF1 in molecular breeding. The role of OsPF1 in coordinating the production of anthocyanins in grain should be kept, as its alteration may bring unwanted consequences. We cannot show here the entire strength of OsPF, since a full knock-out of the gene at the R2R3 region was not successful. The pleiotropic effects on pigmentation processes (e.g., those of stigma and grain) documented here may not favor a complete loss of OsPF1.

Besides TFs, structural genes of the flavonoid pathways can be selected as well to help breed purple rice with or without tannin (Finocchiaro et al. 2010). Since blocking tannin production may redirect metabolic flows to the production of anthocyanins, it remains to be seen whether or not pigmentation is subsequently changed in a desired way for grain. For yield improvement, breeding of purple-rice varieties may also couple with introduction of preferred alleles at other loci on yield, tolerance, and/or adaptation to different environments. When possible, purple-rice specific alleles should be examined at each generation of a breeding program to ensure integrity of the genetic basis.

Last but not least, selection on purple rice is not limited to anthocyanins and tannins. Compounds of health benefits richer in purple rice than in straw-white rice also include carotenoids and γ-oxyzanols (Pereira-Caro et al. 2013), minerals, amino acids, phytosterols and other anti-oxidants (reviewed by Mbanjo et al. 2020). Future breeding of purple rice is toward a functional food that balances health benefits of anthocyanins, such as anti-cancer activities (Li et al. 2017; Lin et al. 2017) or alleviating kidney damage (Tanaka et al. 2013), with merits of non-anthocyanin components (such as anti-inflammatory property of γ-oxyzanols (reviewed by Pansiri and Duangsrisai 2025). This balance can be monitored with a composite index that combines the above attributes of purple rice to aid seed selection during a breeding program.

Conclusion

The questions raised early in this study can now be addressed. Besides previously known OsB2, OsMYB3, and OsTTG1, two additional regulatory genes (OsPF1 and OsPa) are involved in grain pigmentation. OsPF1, a newly identified MYB gene for the anthocyanin pathway in rice, along with the new role of OsPa in pigmentation of grain (particularly hulls), substantially expand the regulatory complexes in developing grains from the previous one to the present four. Among the four regulatory complexes, OsPF1/OsB2/OsTTG1 is the primary type in developing caryopsis of purple rice, whereas OsPa-associated complexes play the major role in pigmentation of hulls. Built upon experimental results available so far, a molecular mechanism that features tissue/organ-specific expressions of the regulatory genes and the redundant but different capacities of the regulatory complexes can render tissue/organ- specific pigmentation in rice. This mechanism can be extended to include further components (e.g., unknown regulators of the bHLH genes) and more effectively guide future breeding of purple rice.
