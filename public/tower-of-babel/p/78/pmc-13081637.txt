Macrophage-centered immunotherapy for osteosarcoma: mechanisms, repolarization, and translational strategies
Authors: Zhide Li, Dong Lin, Liandi Wu, Wending Xu, Haibo Zhang, Zhaonong Zhuang, Long Zhang, Jianan Chen, Zhaoyuan Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite multimodal treatment strategies, survival rates for osteosarcoma have remained stagnant, and immune checkpoint blockade targeting PD-1/PD-L1 alone has demonstrated limited clinical benefit. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which are abundant in osteosarcoma and predominantly exhibit an M2-like polarization, contribute significantly to immunosuppression, angiogenesis, metastasis, and chemotherapy resistance. This review outlines the origins and polarization dynamics of TAMs, and highlights central mechanisms through which they impair T-cell function (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1, TIM-3/galectin-9), inhibit phagocytosis (CD47/SIRPα), facilitate metastatic spread (CCL18–Wnt/β-catenin, COX-2/STAT3), and promote therapy resistance (IL-1β, extracellular vesicles). Emerging therapeutic strategies include depleting TAMs or inhibiting their recruitment (via CCL2/CCR2 or CSF1R blockade), disrupting checkpoint signals (anti-CD47/SIRPα, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors), and reprogramming M2 TAMs toward an M1 phenotype using pharmacological agents, nanotherapeutics, physical modalities (e.g., R848, photothermal therapy), and metabolic signaling inhibitors (PI3Kγ, ERK5). Innovative approaches such as CAR-macrophages and combined immune–vascular targeting may further amplify antitumor responses. Key challenges, including non-standardized markers, methodological variability, and spatiotemporal heterogeneity, underscore the need for a TAM-reprogramming paradigm over mere depletion, and support the integration of multi-omic spatial profiling and biomarker-driven clinical trials focused on metastatic disease. Introduction

Osteosarcoma is one of the most common primary malignant bone tumors in children and adolescents, with a peak incidence between 10 and 20 years of age and an annual incidence of approximately 3–5 cases per million [1]. The current standard of care—neoadjuvant chemotherapy, definitive surgery, and adjuvant (consolidation) chemotherapy—yields a 5-year overall survival of ~ 60%–70% for patients with localized disease; however, the 5-year survival for metastatic or relapsed osteosarcoma remains only ~ 20%–30% [2]. Over the past four decades, overall survival has largely plateaued, with early pulmonary metastasis, treatment resistance, and poor outcomes after relapse representing the major barriers to further progress [3, 4].

With respect to immunotherapy, although PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have achieved success across multiple malignancies, their activity in osteosarcoma as monotherapy has been minimal, with objective response rates of 0%–5% in several phase II studies [5, 6]. Combination regimens (e.g., with low-dose cyclophosphamide or anti-angiogenic agents) have shown modest gains in disease control or progression-free survival, but results remain inconsistent and difficult to reproduce. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)–based approaches are still exploratory in osteosarcoma: preclinical studies targeting HER2, GD2, and B7-H3 have established proof-of-concept for activity and safety, and early clinical trials have primarily demonstrated feasibility and acceptable safety, with durable responses being rare. CAR-NK and CAR-macrophage (CAR-M) strategies are largely confined to preclinical work or very early-phase trials [7, 8]. Overall, the limited efficacy of conventional immunotherapies in osteosarcoma underscores the need to pivot toward strategies that more precisely address the osteosarcoma tumor microenvironment.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) constitute one of the most abundant immune cell populations within the osteosarcoma tumor microenvironment (TME), accounting for up to roughly half of tumor-infiltrating immune cells [9]. TAMs are highly plastic and, in osteosarcoma, are generally skewed toward an M2-like, immunosuppressive, tumor-promoting phenotype that contributes to angiogenesis, immune evasion, metastatic dissemination, and chemoresistance. Notably, pulmonary metastases often harbor an FABP4+ macrophage subset that supports lipid metabolic demands and promotes metastatic seeding/colonization [10]. These observations have positioned macrophage-centered interventions—such as limiting recruitment or depleting TAMs, deploying immune-checkpoint blockade, functionally re-educating/repolarizing macrophages, and harnessing engineered macrophages—as promising avenues to overcome the current impasse in osteosarcoma immunotherapy [11].

Immunosuppression

M2-like TAMs secrete anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β), dampening dendritic-cell (DC) IL-12 production, weakening antigen presentation, and recruiting/maintaining regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby suppressing effector immunity [20]. In osteosarcoma tissue, TAM programs are tightly coupled to checkpoint pathways: TAMs and tumor cells upregulate PD-L1, engaging PD-1 on T cells and promoting functional exhaustion; TAMs themselves can express PD-1 and adopt a phagocytosis-inhibited state [21]. Clinical specimens and model systems associate enrichment of CD163+ M2 macrophages with increased TIM-3+ PD-1+ exhausted T cells; depletion of this subset restores T-cell proliferation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production [22]. In parallel, osteosarcoma cells frequently elevate CD47, delivering a “don’t-eat-me” signal via SIRPα on macrophages to suppress phagocytosis and cross-presentation; blockade of the CD47/SIRPα axis significantly augments macrophage phagocytosis of osteosarcoma cells in vitro and in vivo and can synergize with chemotherapy. Together, these axes form a core molecular hub that couples immune evasion with a “T-cell exhaustion–macrophage suppression” circuit [23].

Osteosarcoma-relevant primary studies support multiple TAM-driven mechanisms that reinforce immune suppression. For example, tumor-derived SPON2 activates NF-κB/VEGF signaling and upregulates immunoregulatory cues (including IL10/CCL2/CSF1), thereby promoting M2-like polarization and an immune-suppressive microenvironment; genetic or functional attenuation of SPON2 reduces M2 features and suppresses malignant phenotypes in vivo [24]. In parallel, osteosarcoma cell–derived migrasomes represent an additional vesicle-mediated route to reinforce M2 bias: migrasome-associated MFGE8 delivery enhances macrophage M2 polarization and promotes progression and metastasis, whereas disruption of migrasome biogenesis mitigates these effects [25]. Beyond suppression, receptor-driven innate rewiring may also generate anti-tumor macrophage states: HTR2B activation engages a STAT1–NLRP3 inflammasome-linked program and expands OASL1⁺ macrophages, coinciding with reduced immunosuppressive cellular interactions in single-cell analyses [37]. Notably, for several canonical checkpoint axes (PD-1/PD-L1, TIM-3, LAG-3, and CD47–SIRPα), osteosarcoma-specific evidence in this manuscript is largely rationale-based or derived from correlative datasets and broader oncology experience rather than osteosarcoma-dedicated registrational trials. Standardized assays, thresholds, and site-matched validation—particularly in pulmonary metastases—remain key gaps that should be addressed prospectively.

Promotion of angiogenesis and metastasis

TAMs confer invasive–disseminative–colonization capacity through multiple routes. First, TAM-derived VEGF, FGF, and MMP-9/MMP-12 promote angiogenesis and matrix remodeling, increasing vascular permeability and facilitating tumor cell extravasation [26]. Second, in osteosarcoma, M2-TAM secretion of CCL18 has been specifically linked to activation of the UCA1/Wnt/β-catenin axis, enhancing proliferation, migration, and metastatic potential [27]. Third, TAMs promote angiogenesis and metastasis through osteosarcoma-relevant, experimentally supported pathways. In a well-characterized axis, TAMs drive tumor-cell COX-2/STAT3 activation, promoting EMT-like programs and pulmonary metastatic dissemination; pharmacologic inhibition of COX-2 or STAT3 reverses these protumor effects in vivo [28]. Fourth, tumor-derived SPON2 further couples immune modulation to dissemination by activating NF-κB/VEGF signaling, enhancing pro-angiogenic programs and metastatic phenotypes while shaping macrophage polarization states [24]. In addition, osteosarcoma cell–derived migrasomes aggravate proliferative and metastatic behaviors, highlighting vesicle subtype–specific contributions to metastatic niche formation [25]. Most mechanistic evidence is derived from preclinical models; the degree to which each pathway dominates in human primary tumors versus pulmonary metastases, and how it is reshaped by chemotherapy, requires larger, site-matched validation.

Therapeutic resistance

Chemotherapy-induced tissue damage and repair cues “educate” macrophages to emit survival signals that blunt drug sensitivity. In osteosarcoma models, cytotoxic therapy stimulates TAM release of IL-1β, activating tumor-cell survival and inflammatory pathways and reducing responsiveness; IL-1 receptor blockade restores chemosensitivity [29]. TAM-derived extracellular vesicles further activate tumor-cell signaling (e.g., AKT), fostering proliferation, invasion, and chemoresistance [29]. Broader preclinical evidence across tumor types shows that inhibiting CSF1R/CCR2 pathways decreases TAM burden and improves chemotherapy responses; depletion of macrophage-derived VEGF-A can “normalize” tumor vasculature, enhance drug delivery, and potentiate alkylating agents/cisplatin [30]. Correlative human osteosarcoma data indicate that poor responders to neoadjuvant chemotherapy harbor higher densities of CD68+ cells, supporting the clinical relevance of a “chemotherapy / TAM repair/protection” resistance loop. In addition to exosome-mediated signaling that supports survival and chemotolerance, migrasomes/MFGE8 provide a second vesicular route that reinforces M2-biased repair/clearance programs, potentially amplifying the “chemotherapy-injury/M2 protection” loop [25].

Crosstalk with other cellular compartments: TAMs as a hub regulator

TAMs serve as “key regulators” in the osteosarcoma TME. In terms of T cells, M2-like TAMs collaborate with inhibitory factors (IL-10, TGF-β) via the PD-1/PD-L1 and TIM-3/Gal-9 axes to drive CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cell exhaustion and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production; this phenomenon is particularly prominent in metastatic niches [31]. Regarding interactions with cancer stem cells (CSCs), TAMs help maintain stem-like populations and enhance tumor-initiating capacity. Correspondingly, repolarization strategies such as ATRA administration can suppress M2-mediated tumor initiation and stemness enhancement in preclinical models [32]. In the context of fibroblasts, osteosarcoma-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) containing TGF-β1 reprogram pulmonary fibroblasts [33]. Coupled with the pro-angiogenic and remodeling functions of TAMs, this facilitates the formation of a metastatic niche conducive to colonization. The axis between tumor cells and integrin α11β1-expressing fibroblasts has also been shown to promote metastasis [34]. With respect to myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), TAMs and MDSCs mutually reinforce immunosuppression and angiogenesis: MDSCs suppress T cells via Arg-1, iNOS, and ROS, secrete VEGF/MMP-9 to promote angiogenesis, and collaborate with TAMs and Tregs to establish pre-metastatic niches [35]. Meanwhile, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) secrete IL-6 to polarize macrophages toward an M2 phenotype and suppress T/B cell activity through exosomes, thereby further deepening immunosuppression [36]. Collectively, these interactions elucidate the interconnected systemic features of osteosarcoma “T cell exhaustion - M2 bias - stromal remodeling - metabolic support - immunosuppression” which reinforce one another. Single-cell immune profiling also connects innate sensing to macrophage states: activation of HTR2B constrains osteosarcoma progression via the STAT1 / NLRP3 inflammasome and expands OASL1⁺ macrophages, supporting an antitumor myeloid phenotype that can synergize with T-cell responses [37].

ER-stress/UPR–myeloid metabolism coupling as a reprogramming axis

High PERK (EIF2AK3) expression correlates with poor survival and increased M2 infiltration in osteosarcoma. Pharmacologic PERK inhibition reprograms TAMs toward M1 while increasing glycolytic flux and relieving immunosuppression; in tumor cells, PERK blockade amplifies ER stress and apoptosis, thereby enhancing macrophage-mediated cytotoxicity. In vivo, the PERK inhibitor GSK2606414 slows tumor growth and elevates M1 macrophage infiltration in osteosarcoma models [38]. These findings are currently supported primarily by preclinical model. The therapeutic index, optimal scheduling with ICIs/chemotherapy, and potential systemic toxicities of UPR pathway inhibition require careful evaluation before translation.

In summary, TAMs in osteosarcoma drive sustained tumor progression and undermine therapeutic efficacy through a quadruple mechanism: immunosuppression, metastasis promotion, tumor protection, and ecological niche remodeling. These processes are tightly coupled, forming a stable and self-sustaining pathological network that is resistant to spontaneous reversal. This explains why TAM-centered interventions—such as reversing phagocytic blockade, blocking immune checkpoints, inhibiting recruitment/repolarization, and combining with chemotherapy or anti-angiogenic agents—are consistently highlighted in multiple reviews as key strategies for improving the effectiveness of osteosarcoma therapy (Table 1).

Macrophage-targeted therapeutic strategies

In osteosarcoma, the tumor microenvironment is dominated by M2-like TAMs that drive immunosuppression, angiogenesis/metastasis, and chemoresistance. Accordingly, macrophage-oriented interventions can be organized into four categories: (i) reducing TAM burden (depletion or inhibition of recruitment), (ii) releasing “immune brakes” (macrophage-related checkpoints), (iii) functional re-education/repolarization (M2 - M1), and (iv) rational combinations with other modalities (Fig. 1).

Inhibition of recruitment: clodronate liposomes and CSF1R pathway blockade

Clodronate liposomes are phagocytosed by macrophages and induce apoptosis; in preclinical OS models, they reduce pulmonary metastasis and restrain primary tumor growth, thereby validating the concept that quantitative TAM reduction can improve the metastatic niche and disease course [39]. Macrophage recruitment is orchestrated by multiple axes, among which CCL2/CCR2 and M-CSF/CSF1R are of significant [40]. Pharmacologic inhibition of CCL2 (e.g., bindarit) lowers macrophage infiltration and suppresses tumor progression in osteosarcoma models, while CSF1R blockade downregulates M2 programs and augments antitumor immunity. A recent review further highlighted that the small-molecule CSF1R inhibitor pexidartinib (PLX3397) promotes M2 to M1 repolarization in vitro and in vivo and suppresses both primary and metastatic disease; in early clinical studies within the sarcoma field (in combination with rapamycin), pexidartinib was associated with increased clinical benefit rates and reduced intratumoral CD206, underscoring its translational potential in osteosarcoma [40, 41]. Upstream of CCR2/CSF1R, SPON2→NF-κB/VEGF drives an IL10/CCL2/CSF1 axis that simultaneously augments recruitment and M2 polarization while fostering angiogenesis. Targeting SPON2 or its downstream NF-κB/VEGF signaling could complement CCR2/CSF1R blockade by dampening both myeloid influx and pro-angiogenic programming.

Immune checkpoints

Osteosarcoma cells commonly upregulate CD47, delivering a “don’t-eat-me” signal through SIRPα on macrophages to suppress phagocytosis and cross-presentation. Blockade of the CD47/SIRPα axis significantly enhances macrophage phagocytosis of osteosarcoma cells in vitro and in vivo and can synergize with chemotherapy. Multiple anti-CD47/SIRPα agents have entered clinical testing in hematologic and select solid tumors; while early-generation antibodies were limited by hemolytic anemia, next-generation molecules with reduced red-blood-cell binding have improved safety profiles. Dedicated, osteosarcoma-specific registrational trials are not yet available, but the biological rationale is strong. PD-L1 expression can be detected in both primary and metastatic osteosarcoma lesions, and TAMs themselves may express PD-L1 or PD-1; together with PD-1 on T cells, these interactions reinforce T-cell exhaustion and macrophage phagocytic inhibition [42, 43]. Clinically, PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy yields low objective response rates in osteosarcoma (often less than 5%); however, in preclinical models, checkpoint blockade increases M1 polarization, reduces M2 abundance, and diminishes pulmonary metastasis—suggesting that coupling PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition with TAM modulation may be critical for efficacy [44, 45]. Additional checkpoints, including the TIM-3/galectin-9 axis and LAG-3, are upregulated in pulmonary metastases and closely associated with immunosuppression; TIM-3 blockade restores CD8+ T-cell effector function and restrains tumor growth in models, and high LAG-3 expression tracks with inhibitory microenvironments, positioning it as a potential partner target al.ongside PD-1/PD-L1 [46].

Repolarization strategies

Among macrophage-directed approaches, repolarization (“re-education over clearance”) is particularly aligned with osteosarcoma biology because it seeks to restore phagocytosis and antigen presentation while avoiding indiscriminate depletion of homeostatic myeloid cells [9, 10]. Importantly, several repolarization strategies have direct osteosarcoma-focused mechanistic support, including ATRA-mediated suppression of M2 polarization and metastasis [48], PERK inhibition that couples tumor-intrinsic ER stress with TAM M1 switching in vivo [38], and receptor-driven innate rewiring via HTR2B that expands OASL1⁺ macrophages through a STAT1–NLRP3 inflammasome program [47].

Pharmacologic approaches

All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) restrains M2 polarization and MMP-12 secretion in human and murine osteosarcoma models, thereby reducing invasion and metastasis; it also counteracts M2-mediated tumor initiation and stemness [48]. Clinical experience further suggests that ATRA combined with interferon-α can induce durable remissions in refractory, relapsed osteosarcoma, supporting translational potential [48]. Mifamurtide (L-MTP-PE), a macrophage activator derived from bacterial cell-wall components, enhances antitumor activity in patients (e.g., induction of TNF-α and IL-6) [49]. When added to chemotherapy, mifamurtide has been associated with improved overall survival and a trend toward better event-free survival. Mechanistically, it downregulates pAKT/STAT3, suppresses M2 programs while promoting M1 features, and reduces RANK expression—rationalizing combinations targeting the OPG/RANK/RANKL axis [50]. Metformin, by reprogramming macrophage metabolism, drives M1 polarization and suppresses tumor growth in osteosarcoma models [51]. A range of natural products also inhibit M2 polarization and/or STAT3 activation while curbing osteosarcoma progression and metastasis, including Onionin A (an allium-derived sulfur compound), epimedokoreanin B (from Epimedium), triterpenoids such as corosolic acid and oleanolic acid, and several flavonoid/coumarin/stilbene derivatives (e.g., baicalein/baicalin-related compounds, psoralen/aesculetin analogs such as xanthoangelol/4-hydroxyderricin, resveratrol and synthetic hydroxystilbenes) [52].

Nanotechnology

Nanoparticle delivery of resiquimod (R848, a TLR7/8 agonist)—for example, encapsulation in poly-L-histidine nanoparticles and co-formulation with cisplatin/hyaluronic acid—markedly decreases the M2 fraction in myeloid-biased models and achieves robust tumor inhibition (on the order of ~ 80%) with minimal organ toxicity in murine osteosarcoma [53]. Such platforms concurrently induce dendritic-cell maturation, re-educate TAMs, and generate durable immune memory. In parallel, graphene-oxide–mediated photothermal therapy (PTT) downregulates the M2 marker CD206 in vitro and reduces tumor burden and invasiveness in vivo, in association with pro-inflammatory cytokine induction—providing experimental support for “physical-immune” coupling as a repolarization modality [54].

Metabolic/signaling nodes

PI3Kγ functions as a myeloid “rheostat” that drives M2 activation and restrains CD8+ cytotoxicity; its inhibition flips anti-inflammatory programs toward pro-inflammatory states, induces TAM repolarization, and amplifies T-cell–mediated immunity [55]. Similarly, myeloid-specific loss of ERK5 (MAPK7) elevates M1 signatures and reduces M2 features (increased iNOS/IL-12β with reduced Arg-1/TGF-β/IL-10). Across multiple tumor models, ERK5 inhibition is linked to macrophage repolarization and tumor suppression, nominating a feasible signaling axis for osteosarcoma repolarization strategies [56, 57].

PERK inhibition

PERK inhibitors offer a dual-action approach: tumor cell killing via ER-stress–driven apoptosis and TAM re-education via increased glycolysis and M2-M1 switching. GSK2606414 provides in-vivo proof-of-concept in osteosarcoma, and the mechanism is well-suited for combination with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade or chemotherapy [38].

HTR2B activation

HTR2B agonism (e.g., BW-723C86) suppresses osteosarcoma progression and remodels the tumor immune microenvironment through a STAT1–NLRP3 inflammasome–linked program. Single-cell profiling of CD45⁺ cells further showed enrichment of OASL1⁺ macrophages after HTR2B activation, accompanied by an immune landscape less dominated by immunosuppressive interactions (e.g., reduced crosstalk with CD8 Tex and CD4 Treg populations) [37]. These data support HTR2B as a receptor-driven macrophage reprogramming/innate-priming approach that functionally shifts the myeloid compartment toward an immune-stimulatory, antitumor state, providing a rationale for combinations with immune checkpoint blockade.

Emerging Strategies

Macrophages naturally home to and infiltrate tumors. Engineered CAR-macrophages (CAR-M) demonstrate antigen-specific phagocytosis, significant tumor control in pulmonary metastasis models, and survival extension, while remodeling the TME toward an inflammatory state [58]. Importantly, CAR-Ms preserve cross-presentation capacity, enabling the activation of tumor-specific CD8⁺ T cells and reprogramming of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. A first-in-human phase I clinical trial of CT-0508, a HER2-directed CAR-M, has recently provided proof-of-concept in advanced HER2-overexpressing solid tumors (NCT04660929). In this study, CAR-Ms demonstrated a favorable safety profile, with no dose-limiting toxicities, severe cytokine release syndrome, or neurotoxicity observed. Correlative analyses further confirmed that infused CAR-Ms trafficked into tumors and remodeled the tumor microenvironment, leading to expansion of intratumoral CD8⁺ T cells. Although clinical activity was modest—44% of patients with HER2 3⁺ tumors achieved stable disease at 8 weeks—these results support the feasibility, tolerability, and biological activity of CAR-M therapy [59]. In osteosarcoma, where CAR-T cell efficacy is hindered by poor infiltration and a highly immunosuppressive milieu, CAR-Ms are considered a promising strategy to overcome these dual barriers.

Anti-angiogenic agents can relieve immunosuppression and “normalize” abnormal vasculature, improving both immune-cell trafficking and drug delivery. In sarcoma/ osteosarcoma-focused studies, nivolumab plus sunitinib in phase Ib/II testing showed an acceptable safety window and a signal for 6-month progression-free survival around 48% [60]. In Chinese cohorts, apatinib plus camrelizumab in chemotherapy-refractory osteosarcoma achieved an objective response rate of 20.9% and a 6-month PFS rate of 50.9% [61]; in another phase I trial that included an osteosarcoma subgroup, the osteosarcoma cohort exhibited an ORR of 33.3%, a disease-control rate of 50%, and a median PFS of 5.7 months [62]. These constraints highlight the need for biomarker-enriched enrollment, metastatic site stratification (especially lung), and integrated correlative endpoints (myeloid programs, spatial context, and on-treatment dynamics) to determine whether TAM-centered combinations truly convert immune resistance into durable benefit (Table 2).

Challenges and outlook

Macrophage-centered research and translation in osteosarcoma face several bottlenecks and future directions. First, TAM heterogeneity is substantial and a unified marker framework is lacking. Commonly used “pan-macrophage” and “M2” markers (e.g., CD68, CD14, CD163, CD206) exhibit overlapping specificity and cross-expression; no single marker accurately resolves functional states [63]. This limitation contributes to inconsistent conclusions across studies regarding associations between M1/M2 infiltration and prognosis. Methodological discrepancies between transcriptomic deconvolution (e.g., CIBERSORT) and immunohistochemistry further introduce differences in sensitivity, specificity, and interpretability [64]. Second, precisely delineating TAM subpopulations remains a methodological priority. TAM heterogeneity is not only a classification issue but also a determinant of therapeutic choice. Osteosarcoma TAM programs dominated by checkpoint signaling or T-cell exhaustion support combining macrophage modulation with PD-1/PD-L1 (and potentially TIM-3) blockade, whereas phagocytosis-inhibited states provide a rationale for CD47–SIRPα targeting. Pro-angiogenic/metastasis-associated and post-therapy repair-like macrophage programs further motivate immune–vascular combinations and reprogramming strategies (e.g., TLR7/8 agonists or PI3Kγ/ERK5/PERK modulation), highlighting the need for spatial/biomarker-guided trial design, particularly in lung metastases. Current evidence supports multi-marker, cross-scale approaches—single-cell sequencing, fate/lineage tracing, and multiplex immunofluorescence/flow cytometry—integrated with spatial localization to reconstruct the full ontogeny and functional spectrum of TAMs and to improve classification and patient stratification [65, 66]. Third, spatiotemporal dynamics are intrinsic to the osteosarcoma immune ecosystem. Primary tumors and pulmonary metastases differ systematically in macrophage composition and function (e.g., enrichment of FABP4+ alveolar-like subsets, higher checkpoint expression, and deeper exhaustion in metastases) [67]. Treatment also reshapes the landscape: after therapy—especially chemotherapy—repair cues drive TAMs toward M2/pro-survival programs, with IL-1β and exosome-mediated signaling contributing to resistance. These observations underscore the need for longitudinal, site-matched monitoring [68]. Fourth, clinical evidence remains limited and insufficiently biomarker-driven. PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy response rates are low in osteosarcoma; although combinations show preliminary activity, sample sizes are small and cohorts heterogeneous [69]. A composite, biomarker-guided framework—leveraging immune scores (e.g., combined CD68/CD163/CD8/PD-1/PD-L1) and single-cell/spatial omics—has been proposed to guide enrollment and companion diagnostics [70, 71].

Despite promising activity, macrophage-targeted approaches can incur clinically meaningful off-target toxicities. Broad TAM depletion or recruitment blockade (e.g., CSF1R or CCL2/CCR2 inhibition) may also affect homeostatic monocytes/macrophages, increasing susceptibility to infection and impairing tissue repair; mitigation may include biomarker-guided patient selection, intermittent dosing, and strategies that favor reprogramming over indiscriminate depletion [40]. Checkpoint modulation has mechanism-specific risks: PD-1/PD-L1 blockade can cause immune-related adverse events, whereas CD47–SIRPα targeting may induce on-target anemia/thrombocytopenia due to antigen expression on blood cells; approaches to improve tolerability include priming/step-up dosing [6], careful hematologic monitoring, and combining with tumor-localized or myeloid-restricted delivery formats when feasible. For repolarization modalities (e.g., TLR7/8 agonists or pathway inhibitors such as PI3Kγ/ERK5/PERK), systemic activation may provoke inflammatory toxicities; nanoparticle delivery or local/targeted administration may help concentrate activity within the tumor while limiting systemic exposure [42]. Integrating toxicity-aware trial design with spatial/biomarker profiling and rational combinations is essential to maximize therapeutic index in osteosarcoma.

Looking ahead, multiple reviews converge on M2-M1 re-education plus combinatorial immunotherapy as a central paradigm [72, 73]. Repolarization can be pursued with drugs (ATRA, mifamurtide, metformin, and selected natural products), nano/physical modalities (TLR7/8 agonist R848 nano-delivery, photothermal therapy), and metabolic/signaling interventions (PI3Kγ or ERK5 inhibition), followed by mechanism-informed combinations and sequencing with PD-1/PD-L1, CD47/SIRPα, and anti-angiogenic therapies [74]. Crucially, efficacy should be validated within metastatic niches, particularly the lung, enabling a translational roadmap that aligns biomarker–strategy in clinical setting.

Conclusions

TAMs constitute the dominant immune component of the osteosarcoma tumor microenvironment and are characteristically M2 dominanted [75]. They orchestrate immune suppression, angiogenesis and stromal remodeling, metastatic seeding and colonization, and chemoresistance, while networked crosstalk with T cells, cancer stem cells, fibroblasts, and MDSCs entrenches a self-reinforcing pathological circuit [76]. Compared with indiscriminate depletion, “re-education over clearance” is better suited to the osteosarcoma immune ecology: pharmacologic, nano/physical, and metabolic/signaling strategies can redirect M2 programs toward phagocytic, antigen-presenting M1 states, and when combined with checkpoint blockade and anti-angiogenic therapy, can mitigate the “immune-cold” phenotype and potentially enhance sensitivity to chemotherapy and adoptive cellular therapies [77]. For research and translation, we recommend: (1) establishing standardized, multi-omic, spatially resolved TAM characterization and taxonomy; (2) validating, in longitudinal primary–metastatic and pre/post-treatment frameworks, the causal chain linking repolarization to immune remodeling and clinical benefit; and (3) launching biomarker-anchored clinical trials that prioritize high-risk settings (e.g., pulmonary metastases) to test M2 to M1 re-education combined with ICIs, anti-angiogenic agents, and CSF1R/CD47-axis modulators [78, 79]. Pursued systematically, TAM-directed, integrated immunotherapy holds promise to overcome the long-standing plateau in survival for patients with osteosarcoma.
