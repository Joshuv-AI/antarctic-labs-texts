Comparative adsorption kinetics of malachite green using biochars from mulberry, Calotropis, castor, and tea leaves
Authors: Sujit Kumar Shah, Krishna Kumar Pathak, Binod Kumar Sah, Pawan Shah, Ramesh L. Gardas, Ajaya Bhattarai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Malachite green (MG), a toxic cationic dye pollutant, requires effective removal from wastewater. This study investigated biochars derived from four plant wastes, mulberry, Calotropis, castor, and tea leaves, as low-cost adsorbents for MG removal. Biochars were synthesised via slow pyrolysis at 400 °C and characterised by FTIR spectroscopy, SEM, N2 physisorption (BET/BJH), XRD, Raman spectroscopy, XPS, and bulk CHNS analysis, revealing oxygen-functionalised turbostratic carbon matrices with distinct pore structures and mineral compositions that facilitate dye adsorption. Batch adsorption experiments were conducted at an initial MG concentration of 20 mg L−1 with varying adsorbent dosages (2–100 mg) and contact times (10–70 min). A five-point calibration curve (4–20 mg L−1, λmax = 618 nm, and R2 = 0.9981) validated the analytical method. Tea biochar exhibited superior performance with the fastest kinetics, achieving 95% MG removal within 60 minutes and a maximum adsorption capacity of 90.57 mg g−1 at 5 mg dosage, followed by mulberry (89.23 mg g−1), castor (80.39 mg g−1), and Calotropis (73.47 mg g−1). Adsorption kinetics were best described by the pseudo-second-order model (R2 = 0.98–0.99), supported by favourable Elovich fits and multi-linear intraparticle diffusion behaviour, indicating that chemisorption-like surface processes coupled with pore diffusion dominate the rate-controlling steps, while pH-dependent uptake and spectroscopic changes (FTIR spectroscopy/XPS) corroborate the roles of electrostatic attraction, π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding and van der Waals pore-filling mechanisms.

Introduction

Synthetic dyes from textile, paper, and related industries are major water pollutants even at low concentrations, imparting intense color that reduces light penetration, increases biochemical oxygen demand, and inhibits aquatic photosynthesis.1 Many dyes are recalcitrant and toxic, notably malachite green (MG), a synthetic triphenylmethane cationic dye widely used in textiles, aquaculture, and leather processing due to its low cost and strong coloration. Global textile activities discharge approximately 700 000 tons of dyes annually, with MG constituting 10–15% of cationic dye effluents.2 MG exhibits acute toxicity, carcinogenicity, teratogenicity, and bioaccumulation potential through its metabolite leucomalachite green (LMG), which persists in fish tissue and demonstrates enhanced carcinogenicity.3 Although banned in food-producing systems by regulatory authorities in the U.S. (FDA), EU, UK, Canada, and China, its use persists in developing countries, including Nepal and India, due to weak enforcement.4 In South Asia, particularly India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh, the textile industry contributes significantly (17–20%) to global water pollution, discharging untreated dye effluents into major water bodies, causing groundwater contamination, oxygen depletion, and ecosystem degradation.5,6 MG is especially concerning due to its mutagenic, carcinogenic, and bioaccumulative nature, with residues detected in over 50% of fish species in Asia, leading to food safety issues and international trade restrictions.7,8

Conventional wastewater treatment methods, including coagulation–flocculation, membrane filtration, advanced oxidation processes, and biological degradation, face significant limitations. Chemical oxidation and biological methods generate hazardous byproducts, in some cases more toxic than the original dye, and require high energy consumption or large land areas.9 Among the available technologies, adsorption emerges as the most practical approach due to its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, low energy requirements, and broad applicability. Activated carbon remains the industry standard but is prohibitively expensive (USD 1.20–6.40 per kg) and energy-intensive (97 MJ kg−1 production energy), generating 6.6 kg of CO2 per kg produced.10 Biochar, a carbon-rich pyrogenic material produced from biomass pyrolysis, offers a compelling alternative: production costs are 40–60% lower (USD 0.80–1.50 per kg), require 16 times less energy (6.1 MJ kg−1), and are carbon-negative (−0.9 kg CO2 per kg), making it environmentally and economically superior to activated carbon while achieving comparable adsorption performance.11 In addition to adsorption, several recent studies have explored circular extraction platforms based on pseudoprotic ionic liquids and related solvent systems for the removal of cationic dyes from textile effluents and industrial wastewaters, demonstrating high removal efficiency at laboratory and pre-pilot scales.12,13

Biochar's effectiveness for dye removal stems from its structure and surface chemistry. Recent literature has demonstrated the promise of leaf-derived biochars for dye removal. Mulberry leaf biochar (500 °C pyrolysis) achieves a malachite green removal capacity of ∼120–150 mg g−1, comparable to commercial activated carbon, while requiring significantly lower production costs and energy input.16 Pomegranate peel biochar (KOH-activated) achieves 362.6 mg g−1 capacity for MG removal.17 Tea waste biochar and various plant-based biochars exhibit adsorption capacities ranging from 17.81 to 622.73 mg g−1, depending on feedstock, modification, and operational conditions.18–20 These leaf-derived materials offer distinct advantages: high oxygen-to-carbon (O/C) ratios enable preservation of functional groups; natural fibrous structures create favorable mesoporosity; low ash content yields high-purity carbon; and seasonal leaf fall from deciduous trees provides abundant, renewable feedstock with minimal collection costs.14

Despite these advances, critical research gaps persist. Existing studies predominantly evaluate single feedstock types in isolation, making systematic performance comparison impossible. Few investigations directly compare untreated plant powder versus its biochar derivative from the same source, limiting the understanding of whether energy-intensive pyrolysis genuinely enhances adsorption or if raw biomass suffices. The relationship between biochar surface chemistry (functional groups, CEC, surface area) and adsorption mechanisms remains incompletely understood. Furthermore, most laboratory studies employ synthetic dye solutions, which do not replicate the complex matrices of industrial wastewater containing mixed pollutants, varying pH, and elevated ionic strength.21 This study addresses these gaps by conducting a systematic comparative evaluation of four leaf-derived biochars (mulberry, Calotropis, castor, and tea leaves) prepared under identical pyrolysis conditions (400 °C) and tested under standardized experimental protocols. These feedstocks were selected because (1) all are abundantly available in South Asia as agricultural/industrial waste; (2) each exhibits distinct lignocellulosic composition and functional group profiles; (3) each represents underutilized waste streams requiring valorization; and (4) prior comparative data under identical conditions are absent from the literature.

The choice of 400 °C pyrolysis temperature is justified by the following: (1) maximum preservation of oxygen-containing functional groups essential for cationic dye adsorption; (2) optimal balance between CEC and surface area; (3) energy efficiency relative to higher temperatures; and (4) typical biochar yield of 30–40%, making the process economically viable.15,16 The MG concentration of 20 mg L−1 was selected based on typical textile wastewater composition (10–100 mg L−1) and UV-vis detection sensitivity (λmax = 615–620 nm), ensuring relevance to real-world contamination scenarios.22 This research contributes to sustainable wastewater treatment in resource-limited settings by (1) identifying the most cost-effective leaf-based biochar for MG removal; (2) elucidating structure–performance relationships; (3) reducing water treatment costs through low-cost adsorbent alternatives; and (4) enabling circular economy practices by valorizing agricultural waste into environmental remediation tools. Given the severity of MG/LMG toxicity, their persistent illegal use in aquaculture despite regulatory bans, and weak enforcement in developing nations, this work provides critical scientific evidence for effective, affordable wastewater treatment solutions to protect public health and aquatic ecosystems.

Herein, we investigate the comparative performance of four leaf-derived biochars for malachite green adsorption, with specific emphasis on elucidating the relationship between surface chemistry (characterized via FTIR spectroscopy and SEM) and adsorption behavior (determined through kinetic and dosage studies). By establishing a mechanistic understanding of how different feedstock compositions and surface functional groups influence dye removal efficiency and rate, this work provides practical guidance for biochar feedstock selection and demonstrates the potential of converting agricultural waste into sustainable, low-cost adsorbents for textile wastewater treatment in resource-constrained environments.

Materials and methods

Dye solution preparation

A 1000 mg L−1 Malachite Green (MG) stock solution was prepared by dissolving analytical-grade MG oxalate (≥99% purity, Merck, India) in double-distilled water, and this stock was subsequently diluted to obtain working solutions of the desired concentrations (4–20 mg L−1).

Biochar preparation

Mulberry, Calotropis, castor, and tea leaves were collected and washed thoroughly with double-distilled water to remove dirt, and then sun dried. Each dried biomass was then carbonized via slow pyrolysis in a muffle furnace. The biomass was heated in a covered ceramic crucible (limited oxygen supply) to approximately 400 °C and held for 4 hours to ensure carbonization. After cooling, the resulting charred material was ground and sieved to obtain a fine biochar powder (particle size less than 200 µm). The biochars were stored in air-tight containers for further use.

Characterization

FTIR spectroscopy

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the biochars were recorded on an FTIR spectrometer (Spectrum Two, PerkinElmer Inc., USA) using the KBr pellet technique in the wavenumber range 4000–500 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1.

SEM

Surface morphology and pore structure of the biochars were examined using a scanning electron microscope (Zeiss Evo 18, Carl Zeiss Microscopy, GmbH, Germany). Images were acquired with a secondary-electron detector (SE1 mode) at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, a working distance of approximately 8.6 mm, and magnification of around 5000×, with a 5 µm scale bar for reference.

BET

The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area and pore structure of each biochar were determined by N2 physisorption analysis at −195.5 °C using a Micrometrics ASAP 2460 instrument. Prior to analysis, all samples were degassed under vacuum at 200 °C for 12 hours to remove adsorbed moisture and volatile surface species. BET surface areas were calculated from the N2 adsorption data in the relative pressure range P/P0 = 0.05–0.03. Total pore volumes were determined from single-point adsorption at P/P0 ≈ 0.995. Pore size distributions were obtained by application of the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method to both the adsorption and desorption branches of the isotherm. Micropore volumes and external surface areas were estimated using the t-plot method.

XRD

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the biochars were recorded on a Bruker D8 diffractometer operating in θ/θ coupled mode with Cu Kα1 radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA, fitted with a Ni β-filter and a position-sensitive detector (PSD). Data were collected over a 2θ range of 10–80° with a step size of 0.029° and a step time of 30.8 s per step.

Raman

Raman spectra were acquired in the range of approximately 200–3000 cm−1 to characterise the structural order of the carbonaceous matrix in each biochar. The intensity ratio of the disorder-induced D band (∼1350 cm−1) to the graphitic G band (∼1580 cm−1), expressed as ID/IG, was calculated to assess the relative degree of graphitisation and the density of structural defects in the biochars.

XPS

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using a Thermo Scientific instrument with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source, a spot size of 400 µm, and a flood gun for charge compensation. Survey spectra were collected to determine the elemental surface composition, and high-resolution scans were acquired for the C 1s, O 1s, N 1s, and S 2p regions to characterise the surface chemical states and functional group environments of each biochar. Peak fitting and atomic percentage quantification were performed using the Thermo Avantage software with a Smart (Shirley-type) background and the ALTHERMO1 sensitivity factor library.

Bulk CHNS elemental analysis

Bulk CHNS elemental analysis of the biochars was performed using an Elementar UNICUBE combustion elemental analyser (Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Germany), with carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and sulphur contents calculated from the integrated detector signals after calibration with sulfanilamide standards.

Adsorption experiments

Batch adsorption tests were conducted using Malachite Green (MG) dye in aqueous solution. All the experiments were carried out at an initial MG concentration of 20 mg L−1, which has an initial absorbance of 2.178 at 618 nm (λmax for MG). Experiments were performed at ambient room temperature (≈298.15 K) under constant agitation. A five-point calibration curve was constructed using dye solution concentrations of 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 mg L−1, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Calibration curve for MG dye quantification.

A linear relationship between absorbance at λmax = 618 nm and MG concentration (4–20 mg L−1) was determined using UV-vis spectrophotometry. Five standard solutions (4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 mg L−1) were used to establish the linear regression equation. The excellent correlation coefficient R2 = 0.9981 demonstrates outstanding linearity and validates the analytical method for accurate determination of residual MG concentrations in biochar adsorption experiments. Each data point represents the mean of triplicate measurements.

For the adsorbent dose effect, different masses of each biochar (2, 5, 10, 50, and 100 mg) were added to separate 30 mL portions of MG solution (20 mg L−1) in stoppered glass tubes. The suspensions were shaken on an orbital shaker at 120 rpm for a contact time of 30 min. Preliminary tests indicated that 30 min was sufficient to reach near-equilibrium for higher dosages, although lower dosages might fully equilibrate within that time. After 30 min, the mixtures were centrifuged to separate the biochar, and the remaining MG concentration in the supernatant was analyzed.

For adsorption kinetics, a fixed adsorbent dose (5 mg of biochar in 10 mL (20 mg L−1) of solution, corresponding to 0.5 g L−1) was used for each biochar type. The suspension was continuously shaken, and at predetermined time intervals (10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, and 70 min), aliquots of the solutions were withdrawn. Each aliquot was immediately centrifuged, and the MG concentration in the supernatant was analyzed. All the kinetic runs were conducted in triplicate, and the average values are reported. MG concentrations were determined by measuring the solution absorbance at 618 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer.

In order to evaluate the influence of solution pH on MG adsorption, batch experiments were conducted at an initial MG concentration of 20 mg L−1 and a fixed biochar dose of 5 mg in 30 mL of dye solution. The initial pH of the MG solutions was adjusted to desired values (2, 5, 7, 8, and 9) using dilute HCl or NaOH, without further pH control during equilibration, and measured prior to the addition of the adsorbent. Each suspension was agitated for 30 min at ambient temperature and then centrifuged to separate the biochar, after which the residual MG concentration was determined spectrophotometrically at 618 nm. The percentage removal at each pH was calculated from the initial and equilibrium concentrations according to eqn (1), enabling direct comparison of pH-dependent adsorption performance for mulberry, Calotropis, castor, and tea leaf biochars.

Results and discussion

Biochar characterization

FTIR spectroscopy

The FTIR spectra of mulberry, Calotropis, castor, and tea samples, presented in Fig. 2(a–d), revealed characteristic functional groups that reflect their distinct phytochemical compositions. All samples exhibited broad O–H stretching around 3400 cm−1, indicative of hydrogen-bonded hydroxyl groups in polyphenols, alcohols, and polysaccharides.23 Aliphatic C–H stretching at 2920 and 2850 cm−1 confirmed the presence of lipids and alkyl chains across all biomaterials. The carbonyl peak at approximately 1740 cm−1, corresponding to ester CO stretching, was prominent in castor oil due to triglyceride and fatty acid ester linkages, with minor contributions from phenolic esters in other samples.24 Aromatic CC vibrations at around 1600 cm−1 and 1510 cm−1 were particularly pronounced in the mulberry and tea spectra, reflecting their high content of aromatic polyphenols, flavonoids, and catechins, while Calotropis showed characteristic peaks associated with cardiac glycosides. The fingerprint region (1500–1000 cm−1) displayed characteristic cellulosic CH2 bending at 1420 cm−1, C–O stretching from phenolic compounds at 1240 cm−1, and strong C–O and C–O–C stretching from polysaccharides at 1030–1100 cm−1. These spectral features collectively confirmed the lignocellulosic nature of all samples and their unique bioactive compound profiles, distinguishing mulberry25 and tea by their polyphenolic richness,26Calotropis27 by its cardiac glycoside constituents, and castor by its hydroxylated fatty acid composition.

SEM

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed distinct surface morphologies and pore structures in the biochar samples derived from mulberry, Calotropis, castor, and tea leaves, presented in Fig. 3(a–d), reflecting differences in composition and thermal degradation behavior during pyrolysis. Mulberry biochar exhibited a highly textured, wrinkled surface with well-defined pore structures characteristic of lignocellulose biomass carbonization.28,29 The retention of fibrous structures indicated that the original cellular architecture was partially preserved during pyrolysis, with visible macropores formed through volatile matter release during thermal decomposition of cellulose and hemicellulose.30 This porous morphology is consistent with the hierarchical pore network typical of leaf-derived biochars, where cellulose decomposition creates micropores through depolymerization, while lignin degradation contributes to mesopore and macropore formation.31Calotropis biochar displayed a rougher, more irregular surface with heterogeneous pore distribution and visible surface defects. The non-uniform texture likely resulted from the complex phytochemical composition, including cardiac glycosides and secondary metabolites that decompose through varied thermal pathways, creating irregular pore structures during carbonization. The presence of numerous small particles adhered to the surface suggested the incomplete volatilization or condensation of organic compounds during pyrolysis, which is common in biomass with high extractive content.28 Castor biochar showed a highly corrugated, porous surface with extensive macropore development.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of biochar of (a) mulberry, (b) Calotropis, (c) castor, and (d) tea leaves.

The prominent macropores observed in castor biochar are characteristic of lipid-rich feedstocks, where ester bond cleavage and devolatilization of fatty acid chains generate extensive porosity. This porous structure correlates well with the FTIR results, showing strong ester carbonyl peaks, which would have undergone thermal decomposition to release CO2 and volatile organic compounds, leaving behind a highly porous carbon matrix.29 Tea biochar exhibited a more organized, layered structure with smooth regions interspersed with well-defined pores, reflecting the anatomical features of leaf epidermal tissues subjected to thermal treatment. The relatively uniform pore distribution and preserved fibrous structure indicated moderate thermal degradation with retention of the original cellular framework. The smooth surface regions suggested that polyphenolic compounds in tea underwent condensation and aromatization reactions during pyrolysis, forming a more compact carbonaceous structure compared to the highly porous castor biochar.28,29

To further elucidate the evolution of functional groups during processing, FTIR spectra were also recorded for the raw leaves, the corresponding biochars, and the biochars after MG adsorption (Fig. S1). The evolution of FTIR spectra bands from raw leaves to biochar and spent biochar in Fig. S1 confirms the loss of polysaccharides, preservation of oxygenated groups, and the appearance of new or shifted bands after MG loading, supporting the proposed surface complexation mechanism.

XRD analysis

The XRD patterns of all four biochars (Fig. 4) were dominated by broad, diffuse scattering features consistent with an amorphous carbonaceous matrix formed at 400 °C. Cellulose crystallinity is known to be lost between 300 and 400 °C during carbonization, after which turbostratic crystallites begin to emerge within a largely amorphous carbonaceous phase, a stage described by Keiluweit et al.32 as “composite char”. In tea, mulberry, and castor biochars, the broad hump observed at 2θ ≈ 26.8° (d = 3.32–3.33 00C5) is consistent with the (002) reflection of turbostratic graphite-like carbon layers, with an interlayer spacing slightly expanded relative to crystalline graphite (d002 = 3.35 Å), confirming the onset of aromatic carbon ordering without long-range crystalline graphitisation.1 A secondary broad feature near 2θ ≈ 39.6° (d ≈ 2.27 Å), corresponding to the (100) in-plane reflection of the graphenic framework, was further resolved in tea and mulberry biochars.

The biochars exhibited low BET surface areas in the range 3.02–7.20 m2 g−1. These values are consistent with those reported from low-temperature biochars (300–500 °C) produced from lignocellulosic feedstocks, which typically displayed BET surface areas below 10 m2 g−1 owing to incomplete carbonization and partial blockage of incipient pores by residual volatile organic matter.33 The mean pore diameter of these biochars is in the mesoporous range (20–500 Å). Raman spectroscopic analysis

As summarized in Table 2, the ID/IG ratios ranged from 0.877 (mulberry) to 0.994 (castor), with all values less than unity and consistent with turbostratic, defect-dominated carbon produced at low-to-moderate pyrolysis temperatures. Low-temperature biochars (≤400 °C) characteristically display high ID/IG values (>0.8) as a result of incomplete graphitisation and retention of structural defects; a transition towards lower ratios and higher graphitic order occurs only above 600–700 °C, as demonstrated by Guizani et al.34 and corroborated by Raman studies across a range of pyrolysis temperatures.

Castor biochar (ID/IG = 0.994) exhibited the highest defect density, attributable to the non-aromatic carbonisation pathway of its lipid-rich feedstock, which generates a disordered carbon matrix with abundant edge defects and vacancies relative to the lignocellulose-derived biochars.34 Mulberry (0.877) and tea (0.885) showed the lowest ID/IG values, reflecting comparatively greater local graphitic ordering arising from efficient aromatisation of their cellulose/lignin-rich feedstocks.32 A weak 2D band was observed in all four spectra (2673–2734 cm−1), confirming a limited number of stacked graphene-like layers and turbostratic rather than crystalline graphitic stacking, in full agreement with the XRD data (Fig. 5).

XPS surface elemental analysis

The XPS survey confirmed the presence of C, O, K, Ca, Si, and P as the principal surface elements across all four biochars, with Al and Mg uniquely found in tea. The corresponding XPS survey spectra in Fig. S2 clearly display the C 1s and O 1s signals, together with the heteroatom peaks, providing direct spectroscopic evidence of distinct mineral and surface chemistries among the four biochars. The C 1s binding energies (284.29–284.49 eV) are consistent with sp2-hybridised graphitic carbon as the dominant carbon species, as reported by Ilic et al.8 for pyrochars produced from diverse plant feedstocks. The O 1s binding energies (531.09–531.81 eV) are attributable to CO and C–O functionalities, including carbonyl, carboxyl, hydroxyl, and ether groups, directly corroborating the FTIR spectra functional group assignments. According to Table 3, tea biochar exhibited the highest surface carbon content (58.65 at%) among all four samples, yielding the lowest O/C ratio (0.48), consistent with the well-established role of a low O/C ratio (typically 0.2–0.6) as an indicator of high aromatic carbon density and biochar stability under low-temperature pyrolysis.35 The dense, aromatic carbonaceous platform thus generated provides the most extensive framework for π–π electron donor–acceptor interactions with the aromatic rings of MG, which is directly consistent with tea biochar's highest observed adsorption capacity (90.57 mg g−1). The high surface oxygen content of mulberry biochar (44.72 at%) reflects the efficient retention of oxygen-containing functional groups derived from the thermal decomposition of hemicellulose and pectin at 400 °C, a characteristic feature of lignocellulose-rich feedstocks under low-temperature slow pyrolysis. The conspicuously elevated Si 2p signal in mulberry biochar (11.00 at% vs. 1.28–2.18 at% in the other three biochars) is attributable to amorphous opaline phytoliths (SiO2) that are a well-documented structural feature of Morus species leaves, as confirmed by light and SEM observation of opal phytoliths in mulberry foliage. Since phytolith-derived SiO2 is chemically inert toward ionic dye chemisorption, this elevated silica fraction effectively dilutes the active adsorption surface, partly explaining why mulberry biochar achieves a moderate adsorption capacity, despite its high surface oxygen content.36

Bulk CHNS elemental analysis

Bulk CHNS elemental analysis of the four biochars (Table 4) provides additional insight into their composition beyond surface-sensitive XPS. Tea and castor biochars exhibit the highest bulk carbon contents (52.92 and 47.20 wt%, respectively), whereas Calotropis and especially mulberry show lower carbon contents (40.20 and 25.98 wt%), consistent with a larger inorganic/ash fraction in the latter materials. The relatively high C/H ratios (18–23) across all samples are characteristic of low-temperature (400 °C) biochars enriched in condensed aromatic structures, in good agreement with the turbostratic carbon signatures observed by XRD and the ID/IG ratios obtained from Raman spectroscopy. The moderate N contents indicate the presence of nitrogen-containing functionalities that may contribute additional polar and basic sites, complementing the oxygenated groups identified by FTIR spectra and XPS in governing MG adsorption.

Removal efficiency analysis

The percentage removal efficiency of MG dye was calculated using eqn (1).1Here, C0 is the initial concentration of MG dye (20 mg L−1), and Ce is the equilibrium concentration after 30 min.

The removal efficiency of MG by the four types demonstrates systematic improvement with increasing adsorbent dosage, as illustrated in Fig. 6. This dose-dependent behavior reflects the fundamental principle that increasing adsorbent mass provides greater availability of active adsorption sites, thereby enhancing the probability of adsorbate–adsorbent contact and subsequent binding.17,37 The experimental data reveal distinct performance patterns for each biochar, with removal efficiencies ranging from as low as 12% at minimal dosage (2 mg) to near-complete removal approaching 100% at maximum dosage (100 mg), demonstrating the critical importance of optimizing adsorbent loading for effective wastewater treatment applications.

Percentage removal of MG by biochar.

At the lowest adsorbent dosage of 2 mg, all four biochars exhibited relatively poor removal performance, with efficiencies ranging from 12% to 20%. Castor biochar showed the poorest performance at only 12% removal, followed closely by Calotropis at 17%, while mulberry and tea biochars both achieved approximately 20% removal. This limited uptake at low dosage reflects severe adsorbent limitation, where the small number of available binding sites becomes rapidly saturated by the excess MG molecules present in the 20 mg L−1 solution.38 The particularly poor performance of castor biochar at this dosage suggests kinetic constraints related to its macroporous structure, which may require extended contact time for MG molecules to diffuse through large pore channels and access internal mesoporous and microporous adsorption sites.37,39 The 30 minute contact time employed in this study appears insufficient for complete pore penetration under such severely undersaturated conditions, consistent with literature reports indicating that macropore-dominant adsorbents often exhibit slower initial kinetics despite possessing high ultimate capacity.40

As the adsorbent dosage increased to 5 mg, substantial improvements in removal efficiency were observed for all biochars, though performance remained highly variable. Calotropis biochar showed the most dramatic enhancement, with removal efficiency increasing from 17% to 58%, a 3.4-fold improvement. Similarly, castor biochar removal increased sharply from 12% to 64%, representing a 5.3-fold enhancement and overtaking Calotropis in performance ranking. Mulberry and tea biochars both achieved approximately 72–73% removal at this dosage, demonstrating superior performance compared to Calotropis and castor. This dosage range (5 mg, equivalent to 0.25 g L−1) represents a transitional regime where the adsorbent-to-adsorbate ratio begins to approach more favorable conditions, allowing occupation of not only the highest-affinity binding sites but also intermediate-energy sites through diverse mechanisms, including electrostatic attraction, π–π interactions, and hydrogen bonding.16,17 The continued superiority of mulberry and tea biochars at this dosage can be attributed to their well-organized porous structures and abundant oxygen-containing functional groups identified through FTIR spectroscopy analysis, which facilitate rapid binding of cationic MG molecules, even when the adsorbent remains the limiting reagent.41

At 10 mg adsorbent dosage, the removal efficiency pattern shifted notably, with mulberry biochar achieving the highest performance at 89% removal, followed by Calotropis at 83%, tea at 74%, and castor at 72%. The exceptional performance of mulberry biochar at this dosage reflects an optimal balance between site availability and accessibility, wherein the combination of favorable pore size distribution, high surface functional group density, and adequate contact time allows efficient MG sequestration.16Calotropis biochar demonstrated substantial improvement from 58% to 83%, indicating that as the adsorbent concentration increases, the diffusional barriers associated with its heterogeneous pore structure become less limiting, and the abundance of binding sites compensates for slower kinetics. Interestingly, tea biochar showed minimal improvement from 5 mg (73%) to 10 mg (74%), suggesting possible mixing inhomogeneities or saturation of the most accessible high-energy sites, with further adsorption requiring slower diffusion into less accessible pore regions.41 Castor biochar also exhibited a relatively modest improvement from 64% to 72%, maintaining its position as the poorest performer despite the doubling of adsorbent mass. This behavior suggests that fundamental structural limitations related to pore accessibility continue to constrain performance even at moderate dosages.39

At the high adsorbent dosage of 50 mg (equivalent to 2.5 g L−1), removal efficiencies exceeded 90% for all biochars except tea, indicating the onset of adsorbate-limited conditions where binding sites are present in substantial excess. Calotropis biochar achieved complete removal of 100%, with residual MG concentration declining to only 0.042 mg L−1, the lowest value observed in this study and well below typical discharge standards of 1 mg L−1 for colored effluents.17 Mulberry biochar achieved near-complete removal at 99% (residual 0.177 mg L−1), demonstrating excellent polishing capability. Castor biochar reached 93% removal, representing a substantial improvement but still lagging behind Calotropis and mulberry. Most notably, tea biochar achieved only 86% removal (residual 2.610 mg L−1), substantially inferior to the other three biochars at this dosage. This unexpected limitation suggests susceptibility to particle aggregation effects, wherein at solid loadings approaching 2.5 g L−1, the layered structure of tea biochar may promote particle–particle interactions that reduce effective surface area and create mass transfer barriers, preventing MG access to internal pores.39,41

At the maximum dosage of 100 mg (5.0 g L−1), Calotropis and mulberry biochars maintained their exceptional performance at 100% and 99% removal, respectively, demonstrating consistent effectiveness even at very high solid loadings. Castor biochar showed a slight improvement to 95% removal (residual 0.884 mg L−1), achieving acceptable treatment outcomes for most regulatory frameworks. However, tea biochar remained stagnant at 86% removal (residual 2.543 mg L−1), confirming that aggregation phenomena rather than insufficient binding sites limit its high-dosage performance. This plateau behavior at 50–100 mg dosage indicates that further increases in tea biochar loading would yield diminishing returns, suggesting an optimal operational maximum around 2.0–2.5 g L−1.41 The convergence of removal efficiencies for Calotropis and mulberry biochars across 50 and 100 mg dosages indicates the achievement of near-equilibrium conditions where residual MG concentrations approach analytical detection limits, and further dosage increases provide no additional treatment benefit while substantially increasing adsorbent consumption and cost.17,37

This plateau behavior contrasts with the other three biochars, which show capacity increases between 2 and 5 mg dosage, and may reflect the heterogeneous pore structure of Calotropis biochar, creating distinct populations of rapidly accessible and slowly accessible sites.39

The equilibrium concentration (Ce) data provide a direct assessment of treatment efficacy from a regulatory compliance perspective, with lower Ce values indicating more complete pollutant removal. At 2 mg dosage, Ce values range from 14.71 to 16.15 mg L−1 across all biochars, leaving 74–81% of the initial 20 mg L−1 MG concentration untreated—clearly inadequate for discharge and necessitating either substantial dosage increase or sequential treatment stages.17 At a 5 mg dosage, Ce improves to 4.91–7.76 (25–39% of the initial value), with tea biochar achieving the lowest residual (4.905). These concentrations begin to approach acceptable levels for primary treatment, where subsequent polishing can achieve discharge compliance, though most regulatory frameworks for textile wastewater require residual dye below 1–2 mg L−1.37 At 10 mg dosage, mulberry biochar demonstrates superior performance with Ce = 1.937 mg L−1 (90% removal), approaching discharge standards for less stringent jurisdictions. However, Calotropis (3.031 mg L−1), tea (4.749 mg L−1), and castor (5.136 mg L−1) remain above typical limits, requiring further treatment or increased dosage.38

At 50 mg dosage, the treatment efficacy transforms dramatically, with Calotropis achieving an extraordinary Ce = 0.042 mg L−1 and mulberry reaching 0.177 mg L−1, both well below even the stringent discharge limits of 0.5–1.0 mg L−1 commonly applied to colored effluents.17,37 Castor biochar achieves Ce = 1.263 mg L−1 at 50 mg dosage, which is acceptable for most regulatory frameworks, though requiring a further increase to 100 mg (Ce = 0.884 mg L−1) to achieve the same confidence level as Calotropis and mulberry. Most critically, tea biochar shows Ce values of 2.610 and 2.543 mg L−1 at 50 and 100 mg dosages, respectively, remaining above typical discharge limits and confirming fundamental limitations related to particle aggregation that prevent the achievement of complete treatment objectives.39,41

Comparison of the capacity data with literature values provides important context for evaluating the performance of these materials. Khan et al. reported adsorption capacities of 120–150 mg g−1 for MG removal using untreated mulberry leaf powder, values 35–70% higher than our observed maximum of 89.23 mg g−1 for mulberry biochar.16 This apparent paradox (higher capacity for untreated powder versus pyrolyzed biochar) likely reflects differences in experimental conditions, particularly contact time, initial concentration, and temperature. The study by Khan et al. employed longer equilibration times (60–180 minutes) that allow more complete pore diffusion, potentially higher initial MG concentrations that drive greater loading, and optimized pH conditions that maximize electrostatic attraction. Our 30 minute contact time represents a practical compromise for industrial applications but may be insufficient for complete equilibration, particularly for diffusion into the smallest micropores.37,40 For tea biochar, literature reports for tea waste adsorbents show capacities around 159 mg g−1, approximately 75% higher than our maximum of 90.57 mg g−1.41 Again, this difference likely reflects extended contact times and optimized conditions in bench-scale studies versus our more operationally relevant 30 minute treatment period.

When compared to chemically activated biochars, the performance gap becomes more substantial. Mussa et al. reported a maximum capacity of 362.6 mg g−1 for KOH-activated pomegranate peel biochar, four times higher than our best performer.17 Similarly, rice husk biochar activated with H3PO4 achieved capacities exceeding 300 mg g−1.42 These substantial improvements arise from the ability of chemical activation to increase the BET surface area from typically 40–100 m2 g−1 for unactivated biochar to 800–1600 m2 g−1 for activated materials through the creation of micropores and the introduction of oxygen functional groups. However, chemical activation requires hazardous reagents (concentrated acids or bases), specialized equipment, extensive washing, and increased production costs of $1–3 per kg compared to $0.10–0.30 per kg for simple pyrolysis, limiting its economic viability for low-value wastewater treatment applications.39,43

The dosage-dependent performance data also reveal important differences in operational flexibility among the four biochars. Mulberry biochar demonstrates the most consistent and predictable dose–response relationship, with steady improvement across the entire dosage range and no anomalies or performance plateaus. This behavior makes mulberry particularly suitable for variable-composition wastewater, where the dosage may need to be adjusted frequently in response to changing influent concentrations.16Calotropis biochar shows poor low-dosage performance but exceptional high-dosage capability, suggesting suitability for applications where stringent removal is mandatory and adsorbent cost is secondary. Castor biochar exhibits the steepest dose–response curve, with dramatic improvement between 2 and 10 mg, followed by more gradual gains, indicating high sensitivity to dosage optimization and the potential for good performance at carefully controlled intermediate dosages. Tea biochar provides excellent intermediate-dosage performance but fails to achieve complete removal at any dosage, limiting its applicability to primary treatment stages rather than final polishing.39,41

Adsorption kinetics

Temporal evolution of adsorption

Kinetic experiments were carried out at a fixed dosage of 5 mg biochar in 10 mL of 20 g L−1 MG solution (0.5 g L−1), with contact times between 10 and 70 min. This dosage lies in the mid-range of the dosage study, allowing intrinsic kinetic behaviour to be compared without severe site or solute limitation.

For all four biochars, MG uptake increased rapidly during the first 20–30 min and then gradually approached a plateau, displaying the classic biphasic kinetic profile in which a fast initial stage is followed by a slower approach to equilibrium. The fast stage corresponds to external mass transfer and surface adsorption on readily accessible sites, whereas the slower stage is governed by intraparticle diffusion into smaller pores and towards less energetic or sterically hindered sites.44

At 70 min, the experimental capacities qt were 36.41, 37.07, 38.40, and 38.36 mg g−1 for mulberry, Calotropis, castor, and tea, respectively (all at the same initial concentration). Tea and castor thus attained the highest fraction of their theoretical equilibrium capacities, while Calotropis lagged behind, despite its larger qe predicted capacity by kinetic modeling.

Effect of pH on MG adsorption

The pH exerted a pronounced influence on MG adsorption by all four biochars, with removal efficiency increasing systematically from strongly acidic to alkaline conditions (Fig. 8). At pH 2, removal remained low for all adsorbents (17–31%), primarily because the high concentration of H+ ions competes directly with MG+ for the same oxygen-containing surface sites, while the extensively protonated –COOH and –OH groups identified in the FTIR spectra render the surface neutral or slightly positively charged, generating electrostatic repulsion toward cationic MG+. In this regime, adsorption is restricted to weaker π–π stacking and van der Waals forces, which are insufficient to drive significant uptake in the presence of excess protons. As the pH increased toward near-neutral values, progressive deprotonation of carboxylic and phenolic groups (−COOH → –COO−, –OH → –O−) imparted increasing negative charge to the biochar surfaces and reduced H+ competition, manifesting as a marked rise in removal to ≈40–57% at pH 5 and ≈54–75% at pH 7, where electrostatic attraction acted synergistically with π–π electron donor–acceptor interactions and hydrogen bonding between MG's amine groups and deprotonated surface oxygen functionalities.46

Effect of solution pH on the adsorption of malachite green (MG) by biochars derived from mulberry, Calotropis, castor, and tea leaves. Experimental conditions: initial MG concentration 20 mg L−1, biochar dosage 5 mg in 30 mL, contact time 30 min, and room temperature.

In mildly alkaline solution (pH 8–9), deprotonation of surface functional groups was essentially maximised within the investigated range, and the biochar surfaces attained a strongly negative charge while MG remained in its cationic form, making electrostatic attraction the dominant adsorption driving force. This resulted in the highest observed removal efficiencies: mulberry ≈79–83%, tea ≈80–84%, castor ≈75%, and Calotropis ≈70–73%, consistent with the widely reported optimum pH range of 7–10 for MG removal on biochar-type adsorbents. The superior performance of tea and mulberry biochars throughout the entire pH range can be attributed to their well-developed aromatic porous frameworks and abundant oxygen-containing functionalities evidenced by FTIR spectroscopy and SEM, which facilitate rapid site access and strong multivalent interactions (electrostatic, π–π, hydrogen bonding, and pore filling); by contrast, the more macroporous and structurally heterogeneous castor and Calotropis biochars, while benefiting from enhanced electrostatic attraction at high pH, do not attain equivalent site accessibility and hence display slightly lower maximum removals.47

To rationalize the observed pH-dependent behavior, the point of zero charge (pHPZC)—the pH at which the biochar surface bears a net zero charge—is a critical parameter. For plant-leaf biochars pyrolyzed at 400 °C, pHPZC values in the range of 6.5–8.0 have been widely reported in the literature,48 reflecting the preservation of oxygen-containing surface functional groups at moderate pyrolysis temperatures. When the solution pH exceeds pHPZC, the biochar surface becomes negatively charged and electrostatically attracts MG+; conversely, at pH below pHPZC, the protonated surface repels MG+, suppressing adsorption.

Proposed adsorption mechanism

All four leaf-derived biochars provide oxygen-functionalized aromatic carbon surfaces with heterogeneous pore networks, which together create multiple classes of adsorption sites for MG. The schematic diagram illustrating the overall mechanism is shown in Fig. 9.

Schematic showing the proposed mechanism of adsorption of MG onto biochar.

The first primary mechanism is electrostatic (coulombic) attraction between positively charged MG species and negatively charged sites on the biochar surface, notably deprotonated carboxyl (−COO−) and phenolic (Ar–O−) groups.40,42,49–51 Under the conditions used here, these sites become increasingly deprotonated with increasing pH, which explains the enhanced MG uptake at higher pH and the strong dependence of removal efficiency on solution pH observed for all four biochars. Electrostatic attraction is highly favourable thermodynamically and provides a strong driving force for MG uptake, particularly on mulberry and tea biochars, which exhibit more abundant carboxylic and phenolic functionalities and therefore higher-than-average removal efficiencies at moderate dosages (5–10 mg).9,17,39,45,50 The evolution of bands associated with these groups before and after adsorption in the SI FTIR spectra (Fig. S1) further supports their direct involvement in electrostatic binding.

The second important mechanism is electron donor–acceptor (EDA) interaction between the conjugated aromatic ring system of MG and the π-electron-rich aromatic domains of the biochars.9,42,51,52 In this process, the electron-deficient aromatic rings of MG act as π-acceptors and engage in π–π stacking with graphene-like and polycyclic aromatic structures within the carbon matrix, stabilised by van der Waals forces and electron delocalisation between the two π-systems.51,53,54 Tea biochar, derived from polyphenol-rich tea leaves, retains a particularly high density of such aromatic and polyphenolic structures, which provides an abundance of π-electron-rich sites and accounts for its superior kinetic parameters and rapid initial uptake compared with the other biochars.9,41,54,55

Hydrogen bonding constitutes a third contributory mechanism, arising mainly between the secondary amino groups of MG and oxygen-containing surface groups on the biochars, including hydroxyl (O–H), carbonyl (CO) and ether (C–O) functionalities.17,49,51 These interactions are individually weaker than electrostatic attraction or strong chemisorption (typically 5–40 kJ mol−1, compared with 10–50 kJ mol−1 for chemisorption and more than 100 kJ mol−1 for covalent bonding) but can make a non-negligible contribution when large numbers of such sites are available.50,54 Hydrogen bonding thus helps stabilise MG molecules at or near the biochar–solution interface, complementing electrostatic and π–π interactions.

A fourth mechanism involves van der Waals forces and pore-filling mechanisms, whereby MG molecules diffuse into and occupy the pore volume of the biochars (micropores < 2 nm, mesopores 2–50 nm, macropores > 50 nm) and are stabilised by weak dispersion forces arising from fluctuating dipole interactions with the pore walls.51,56 Although these interactions are individually very weak (<5 kJ mol−1), their cumulative effect becomes substantial owing to the large number of pores and the available internal surface area inferred from the combined BET and SEM analyses.15,49 Tea biochar, with its well-connected hierarchical network of mesopores and macropores, facilitates rapid MG transport and efficient pore filling, which is consistent with its high pseudo-second-order rate constant and initial sorption rate.47 In contrast, Calotropis biochar shows regions of pore collapse and blockage, leading to an initial uptake followed by a slower approach to equilibrium, while castor biochar is more strongly macropore-dominated, favouring fast initial diffusion but more limited sustained adsorption.35–37,47,49

Overall, the pore size distribution and connectivity exert dual control over process performance: the accessible micropore fraction governs the ultimate adsorption capacity at longer contact times, whereas meso- and macropore domains determine the effective kinetic rate constants by providing diffusion pathways to interior sites.40 Together with the surface functional groups and aromatic domains described above, this hierarchical pore structure gives rise to a multistep adsorption mechanism in which rapid chemisorption-driven surface binding (electrostatic and π–π interactions, supported by hydrogen bonding) is followed by slower intraparticle diffusion and pore filling, in agreement with the kinetic model fits, pH dependence, and the mechanistic scheme depicted in Fig. 9.

The bulk CHNS results (Table 4) further support the proposed adsorption mechanism by linking overall composition to performance. Tea and castor biochars, which possess higher carbon contents and elevated C/H ratios, are more enriched in condensed aromatic domains, consistent with Raman and XRD data and with their strong π–π interactions and favourable kinetics. In contrast, mulberry's lower bulk carbon content, combined with its high Si signal from XPS, indicates a larger fraction of inert mineral matter that dilutes the active carbon surface and helps to explain its only moderate adsorption capacity despite abundant oxygenated groups. Moderate nitrogen levels in all biochars suggest additional polar sites that can contribute to hydrogen bonding and acid–base interactions, complementing the oxygenated functionalities in MG uptake.

Conclusion

This study has shown that biochars prepared at 400 °C from mulberry, Calotropis, castor and tea leaves act as effective, low-cost adsorbents for malachite green removal from water. Combined FTIR spectroscopy, SEM, BET, XRD, Raman, XPS and CHNS analyses confirmed oxygen-rich surfaces, aromatic carbon frameworks and hierarchical porosity, with tea biochar exhibiting the most favourable combination of surface area and carbon content and achieving the highest capacity (90.57 mg g−1 at 5 mg) and fastest uptake (95% removal within 60 min), while the other three biochars provided intermediate to excellent performance at higher dosages. Kinetic fits to the pseudo-second-order and Elovich models, together with multi-linear intraparticle diffusion plots and strong pH dependence, indicate a multistep process in which chemisorption-like surface binding, electrostatic attraction, and π–π interactions are complemented by hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and pore-filling within the pore network. Complete or near-complete removal (99–100%) at 50–100 mg doses, combined with simple low-temperature pyrolysis and inexpensive leaf feedstocks, suggests that these materials are promising, sustainable alternatives to commercial activated carbon, and future work should address dynamic column operation, regeneration, multi-pollutant systems, and techno-economic evaluation under realistic wastewater conditions.
