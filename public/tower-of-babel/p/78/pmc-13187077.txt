Genomic and spectroscopic analysis for antimicrobial metabolite profiling of Microbacterium testaceum endophyte
Authors: Merihan S Sabra, Amr S Bishr, Lina Jamil M Abdel-Hafez, Ahmed M Elissawy, Khaled M Aboshanab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWild plants harbor distinct endophytes that produce potent antimicrobial metabolites that can be used to combat life-threatening pathogens. In our study, four bacterial endophytes (LP1, LP2, LP3, LP4) were isolated from the inner tissues of leaves of Pluchea dioscoridis (L.) DC (Barnûf). The antimicrobial activity of the respective extracts (P1, P2, P3, and P4) against standard, multidrug-resistant (MDR), and Candida albicans was determined using the cup agar diffusion method. The P4 extract showed broad-spectrum activity against all tested pathogens and promising antiviral activity. The endophytic bacterium LP4 was microscopically and genomically identified by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) as Microbacterium testaceum (M. testaceum) strain CCASU-2025-88. The extract of endophytic M. testaceum CCASU-2025-88 (LP4) exhibited broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against standard and multidrug-resistant pathogens as well as antiviral activity against Adenovirus 7, Herpes Simplex Virus 1, and Herpes Simplex Virus 2, and cytotoxic activity with a non-toxic concentration of 7.32 μg/mL, reflecting the promising bioactive properties of LP4 metabolites. Chemical profiling using AntiSMASH and LC–ESI–MS analysis suggested the presence of a compound putatively identified as microansamycin B; however, this assignment remains tentative, since the compound exhibits potent antibacterial activity, and it demonstrates the bioactive potential of M. testaceum metabolites. In conclusion, M. testaceum strain CCASU-2025-88 is a potential source of microansamycin B-like compound based on LC–ESI–MS evidence. This is the first report investigating the production of microansamycin B by a locally isolated endophytic bacterium, M. testaceum CCASU-2025-88. Introduction

A diverse array of microbes colonize plants. Some of which are the epiphytes that persist on the surface of the plant, whereas others can infiltrate deeper into plant tissues and are called endophytes (Chen et al. 2024). Endophytic microbes have been considered a promising source of new antibiotics as they play a crucial role in producing extracellular compounds called secondary metabolites, particularly peptides, which represent a substantial proportion of natural therapeutic compounds in the pharmaceutical market (Gouda et al. 2016; Singh et al. 2017a). These metabolites have broad-spectrum antiviral, antifungal, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antibacterial properties. Among these metabolites, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) produced by bacterial communities play a significant role in the discovery of pharmaceuticals (El-Sayed et al. 2020; Kashyap 2019). The integration of phenotypic evaluation with genomic and metagenomic analyses enables a comprehensive investigation of biosynthetic gene clusters associated with important secondary metabolites. To further characterize these metabolites, spectroscopic techniques have been used to identify and investigate bioactive compounds (Eltokhy et al. 2021a, 2021b, 2024; Soliman et al. 2024). Antimicrobial compounds produced by endophytes are considered promising, eco-friendly peptides that are toxic to certain harmful bacteria while maintaining low toxicity toward human cells (El-Housseiny et al. 2016; Singh et al. 2017b; Digra and Nonzom 2023). Given the global challenge of antimicrobial resistance caused by antibiotic overuse and the urgent need for new therapeutic agents, endophyte-derived metabolites represent valuable natural sources for the development of novel antimicrobial drugs.

This study aimed to isolate and identify promising endophytic bacteria from the uncultivated Pluchea dioscoridis (L.) DC plant, evaluate the antimicrobial activity of all bacterial extracts, and assess cytotoxic and antiviral activities of the most promising strain. Finally, Chemical profiling of this strain was performed using AntiSMASH and LC–ESI–MS analysis for the tentative identification of secondary metabolites.

Materials and methods

Plant collection and identification

The healthy plant was collected on 17 July 2023 using a box cutter, a sterile sampling bag, and sterile gloves. It was harvested on the 6th of October in Giza, Egypt, where it grew abundantly in an arid area characterized by sandy soil. The collection of plant material was established in accordance with national guidelines to confirm the plant identity. The plant was authenticated by Dr. Walaa Amer, Professor of plant taxonomy and flora, Cairo University Herbarium (CAI), as Pluchea dioscoridis (L.) DC. A voucher specimen no. (PHG-P-PD-563) was deposited in the Herbarium of the Department of Pharmacognosy, Faculty of Pharmacy, Ain Shams University, Cairo, Egypt.

Surface sterilization, isolation, and microscopical examination of endophytic isolates

The collected plant parts were surface sterilized before endophyte isolation to remove epiphytic microbes (Sahu et al. 2022). Plant parts, such as leaves, were washed under running water to get rid of environmental dust and residues, then cut into parts. Plant leaves were surface sterilized for 5 min in 5% sodium hypochlorite, followed by 1 min in 70% ethanol. It was then washed three times with sterile distilled water. To ensure surface sterilization, the final distilled-water rinse was inoculated onto a Muller-Hinton agar plate (Srivastava et al. 2024). Using a pestle and mortar, 2 g of tissue was macerated aseptically in 18 mL of sterile saline. One milliliter of the suspension was placed on Muller-Hinton agar plates with nystatin (30 μg/mL) to suppress the fungal growth (Hagaggi and Mohamed 2020). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h. The appearance of colonies was examined every day (Hagaggi and Mohamed 2020), and specific colonies from the isolation plates were chosen based on variations in colony size, elevation, and morphology. The selected colonies were subcultured 2–3 times on Muller-Hinton agar at 37 °C for 24–48 h to reach pure cultures or isolates. The preliminary identification of the recovered bacterial isolates was performed based on phenotypic features, including colony morphology, color, and size, and by Gram staining (Mahlangu et al. 2024). The bacterial isolates were selected for this study, preserved in 30% glycerol (v/v), and stored at − 20 °C for subsequent use.

Extraction of secondary metabolites by ethyl acetate

The bacterial isolates were subcultured on Muller-Hinton agar. As a preculture, a single colony was grown in 30 mL of tryptic soya broth (TSB) and incubated overnight at 33 °C on a shaker incubator (150 rpm). Then, 1.0 mL (1.0 × 10^8 CFU/mL) from this preculture was used to inoculate the main culture, 100 mL of tryptic soya broth (TSB), for 72 h at 37 °C and 150 rpm (Hagaggi and Mohamed 2020). Following incubation, the broth media were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant without cells, followed by filtration using sterile Whatman No.1 filter paper, followed by membrane filtration using a PTFE membrane of 0.2 μm pore size (Merck co., Darmstadt, Germany). Ethyl acetate was added at 1:1 (v/v) to the cell-free supernatant, and the mixture was shaken for 16 h in a shaking incubator (150 rpm) at 5 °C. The supernatant with ethyl acetate was placed in separating funnels and allowed to mix until the organic and aqueous phases were separated. The organic phase was recovered and evaporated using a rotary evaporator (BUCHI, Germany). Then, 3 mL of pure dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO) was added to dissolve the obtained deposit (Abdel-Nasser et al. 2023). The extract yield ranged from 50 to 70 mg per 100 mL of culture broth.

Evaluation of antimicrobial activity

Antibacterial activity was performed for crude extracts obtained from four isolates against three standard isolates, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 (S. aureus), Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 9027 (P. aeruginosa), Candida albicans ATCC 10231 (C. albicans), as well as nine clinical isolates: Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) CCASU-5, MRSA CCASU-8, multidrug-resistant (MDR) P. aeruginosa CCASU-28, MDR P. aeruginosa CCASU-45, MDR P. aeruginosa CCASU-47, MDR S. aureus CCASU-4, MDR S. aureus CCASU-9, MDR Klebsiella pneumoniae CCASU-22 (K. pneumoniae), MDR K. pneumoniae CCASU-36 as displayed in Table 1, which were obtained from Culture Collection Ain Shams University (CCASU, Cairo, Egypt using the cup plate method (Soliman et al. 2024). The four organic extracts, P1, P2, P3, and P4, were tested as previously reported (Soliman et al. 2024). Ciprofloxacin (8.0 μg/mL), gentamicin (5 mg/mL), and fluconazole (1 mg/mL) were utilized as a positive control. The extract showing promising antibacterial activity was evaluated using minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) according to CLSI guidelines (CLSI 2021). Plates were incubated at 37 °C and checked for an inhibitory zone after 24 h. Subsequently, the organic extract exhibiting broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity was selected for assessment of cytotoxicity and antiviral activity. The bacterial isolate producing this most active extract (LP4) was subsequently selected for whole-genome sequencing to enable comprehensive identification and further characterization.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic impact of crude extract with promising antimicrobial activity was assessed on Vero cells to determine safe concentration using the Methyl Thiazolyl Tetrazolium (MTT) assay (Van Meerloo et al. 2011; Bahuguna et al. 2017). The concentration of crude extract was adjusted to 15 mg/mL. Two-fold serial dilution on pre-cultured cell lines was done for the crude extract or a 24-h treatment at 37 °C after the growth medium had been decanted. Cell lines treated in the laboratory were examined under a microscope to detect morphological changes and detached cells. The dead cells were removed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.2 ± 0.2) containing 0.05% Tween. Cells were treated with 0.5% MTT stain at 25 µl per well. The plates were left to incubate for 3–4 h at 37 °C. To dissolve intracytoplasmic MTT formazan crystals, incubate 0.05 ml of DMSO on a plate shaker for 30 min. Optical densities were detected via a Biotek 800 ELISA plate reader from the USA, and cell viability was estimated. The Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (CC50) of test extracts was evaluated using the Master Plex-2010 program. In this study, we used a non-cytotoxic concentration for the antiviral assay (Keshavarz et al. 2021).

The estimated viability percentage was calculated in the following way:

Cell viability percentage = (OD of treated cells/OD of untreated cells) × 100 (Zakzouk et al. 2024).

Evaluation of antiviral activity

The crude extract with promising antimicrobial activity was evaluated for antiviral activity according to the method of Petricevich and Mendonça (2003). This activity was performed against Human Adenovirus serotype 7, Herpes Simplex Virus 1, and Herpes Simplex Virus 2, where all viruses were titrated both with and without extract present. The variation in viral titers results from the extract's antiviral activity. The extract was applied to Vero cells for 24 h prior to viral inoculation to assess its effect on early stages of viral replication. Vero cells were cultivated on 96-well cell cultivation dishes at a cell density of 105 cells/ml. Growth media were discarded upon confluence. Plates containing the maximum non-toxic concentration of the P4 extract (7.32 μg/mL), as determined by cytotoxicity assay, were used in the antiviral assay and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. For viral control titration, control plates were left intact and were not treated in the interim. The viruses were serially diluted tenfold in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (E-MEM). Each viral dilution was inoculated at 0.1 mL/well in Vero cell-grown plates, regardless of whether those plates had been subjected to extract. Growth media were disposed of. As a negative cell culture control, untreated wells were retained, and acyclovir at 10 µg was used as the positive control. Following incubation at 37 °C, the plates were inspected daily using an inverted microscope. After three days, the virus titers in test material-treated and untreated cells were assessed using the endpoint of the cytopathic effect (CPE) assay. The 50% cell culture infectious dose (CCID50) was calculated by recording the total number of wells per dilution that developed CPE. This is the endpoint of the CPE method, which indicates the virus's infectivity. According to Reed and Muench (1938), the 50% endpoint was estimated.

All titrations were done in triplicate. The antiviral activity of the P4 extract was evaluated by calculating the changes in mean virus titers between treated and untreated plates, with statistical analysis used to determine significance.

Genomic identification of the potential isolate

The chromosomal DNA sample was obtained with the QIAamp® DNA Mini Kit (catalog number 51304, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Following the supplier's protocol for Gram-positive bacteria, the generated DNA was quantified using a Qubit 4 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). The Rapid Sequencing Kit (SQK-RAD004; Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) was applied to prepare a library from approximately four hundred nanograms of recovered chromosomal DNA, which was extracted following the directions provided by the manufacturer. Sequencing was carried out utilizing MinIONTM and R9.4.1 flow cells (FLO-MIN106; Oxford Nanopore Technologies). The MinKNOW software version 23.11.5 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) was used to collect data.

Data analysis

On AWS EC2 g4dn.xlarge, MinIONTM reads of sequences (i.e., POD5 data) were transformed into fastq files utilising the Dorado basecall server version 7.3.9 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). The fastq data were categorized using Kraken2 (Wood et al. 2019). Using a tailored database comprising sequences for archaea, bacteria, fungi, and viruses (Lu et al. 2017), species abundance was predicted based on these classifications, which were then visualized in Krona (Ondov et al. 2011). Medaka (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka) was used to align fastq reads to the reference genome, call variants, and generate consensus sequences. The Consensus FASTA was used for gene annotation with Prokka (Seemann 2014) using the default parameters.

Determination of the biosynthetic clusters of genes

AntiSMASH version 7 was employed for sequence alignment and evaluation to detect probable secondary metabolite gene clusters. (https://antismash.secondarymetabolites.org/#!/start) (Blin et al. 2021).

LC–ESI–MS analysis

The solution (100 μg/mL) was produced by using MS-grade MeOH, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane disc filter, and then subsequently analyzed by LC–ESI–MS. 1 μL of the prepared sample was injected for the analysis using Waters Xevo TQD mass spectrometer (Milford, MA 01757, USA) equipped with a reverse-phase C-18 column (ACQUITY UPLC—BEH C18 1.7 µm particle size—2.1 × 50 mm column). The mobile Phase and analytical settings were adjusted as previously reported (Abd-Elsalam et al. 2021) as follows: Gradient elution was employed. The mobile phase consisted of two solvents; Eluent A was H2O acidified with 0.1% formic acid and Eluent B was acetonitrile acidified with 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was 0.2 mL/min. The elution was as follows: (10% B) from 0 to 5 min.; (30% B) from 5 to 15 min.; (70% B) from 15 to 22 min.; (90% B) from 22 to 25 min. and (100% B) from 25 to 29 min. Both positive and negative ion modes were used in the analysis to achieve comprehensive metabolite profiling as follows: source temperature, 150 °C; cone voltage, 30 eV; capillary voltage, 3 kV; cone gas flow, 50L/h; desolvation gas flow, 900 L/h; and desolvation temperature, 440 °C. The ESI Mass spectra were detected in the ESI-positive and -negative ion modes detected mass spectra ranging from 100 to 1000 m/z. The peaks and mass spectra were assessed using Masslynx 4.1 software and, where possible, by comparing retention time (Rt) and mass spectra with the provided data.

Results

Isolation and microscopical examination of endophytic bacteria

A total of four Gram-positive endophytic bacterial isolates, designated LP1, LP2, LP3, and LP4, were recovered from Pluchea dioscoridis (L.) DC. Three of them (LP1, LP2, and LP3) were cocci-shaped, whereas LP4 exhibited a short rod-shaped. Morphological examination revealed that all isolates formed small colonies. LP1 produced cream-colored colonies with entire margins, flat elevation, smooth texture, and no pigmentation. LP2 exhibited off-white colonies with undulate margins, raised elevation, matte texture, and no pigmentation. LP3 developed white colonies with entire margins, convex elevation, matte texture, and no pigmentation. LP4 formed pale yellow colonies with entire margins, convex elevation, smooth texture, and light yellow pigmentation.

Extraction and antimicrobial evaluation

Records of the inhibition zone (mm) of the four organic extracts (P1, P2, P3, and P4) against the tested bacterial isolates (n = 12) are displayed in Table 1 and Fig. S1. Results showed that the organic extract P4 exhibited antimicrobial activities against all the tested bacterial isolates. Accordingly, the endophytic bacterium producing extract P4 (designated LP4) was selected for further genomic characterization, while its organic extract was subjected to cytotoxicity and antiviral evaluation. The MIC values of the P4 extract against the tested bacterial strains are displayed in Table 2. Results showed promising antibacterial activities of the tested P4 extract, ranging from 4 to 32 µg/mL (Table 2).

The cytotoxicity of the ethyl acetate extract P4 was determined by treating normal Vero cells based on MTT results for the cytotoxicity assay. Assessment of antiviral activity

The antiviral activity of the crude extract P4 was tested against three viruses: Adeno 7, HSV-1, and HSV-2. In the case of Adeno 7, the untreated control showed a viral titre of 5.33. The P4 organic extract reduced viral titre by 1.33 log10, corresponding to a 25% reduction and a reduction factor (RF) of 101.33, indicating slight antiviral activity relative to acyclovir, which showed a log difference of 2.5. (Table 3). In the case of HSV-1, the untreated control showed a viral titer of 7. Treatment with P4 reduced the viral titre to 4.66 log10, corresponding to a log difference of 2.34, 33.4% viral reduction, and RF102.34, indicating that the extract has moderate antiviral properties compared to Acyclovir, which showed a log difference of 3.5 (Table 3). In the case of HSV-2, the untreated control showed a viral titre of 5.66. Treatment with the crude extract of P4 reduced the viral titre by 1.66 log10, corresponding to a 29.3% reduction (RF101.66). RF101.66 exhibited slight antiviral activity compared with Acyclovir, which showed a log difference of 2.37 (Table 3).

Identification of the LP4 isolate

The LP4 isolate was recognized genetically as Microbacterium testaceum. The whole genome sequence was placed in the NCBI GenBank and assigned an accession code (PRJNA1327108) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/?term=PRJNA1327108), BioSample: SAMN51265577, M. testaceum strain: MTE-2 Genome sequencing (TaxID: 2033), Locus Tag Prefixes: ACUS0D (SAMN51265577), https://submit.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/subs/genome/. The LP4 strain was maintained in the Culture Collection Ain Shams University (10.12210/ccinfo.CCASU) and was designated the accession code Microbacterium testaceum strain CCASU-2025-88.

Genomic mining of biosynthetic gene clusters

The alignment and evaluation of the whole-genome sequences of M. testaceum strain CCASU-2025-88 indicated the presence of four biosynthetic clusters of 12 promising antimicrobial metabolites (bioproject accession conde, PRJNA1327108). Among these, four core biosynthetic gene clusters were identified through genome analysis, showing high conservation and suggesting potential metabolic relevance as presented in Fig. 2. These include biosynthetic gene clusters of ε-poly-L-lysine (ε-PL) (Fig. 2a), carotenoide (terpene) (Fig. 2b), clifednamide A (polyketide, PK) (Fig. 2c), and microansamycin B (pentaketide) (Fig. 2d), which are highly conserved (94–100% aa identity) in the amino acid sequences. The M. testaceum strain CCASU-2025-88 biosynthetic gene cluster was 100% identical in the amino acid sequences of ε-PL to Epichloe festucae, as depicted in Fig. 2a. The pentaketide antibiotic, microansamycin B biosynthetic gene, is quite similar (89% aa conservation) to that found in Micromonospora sp. HK160111 (NCBI accession, ATY46587.1).

LC–ESI–MS analysis

LC–ESI–MS analysis of the crude ethyl acetate extract P4 led to the tentative identification of 15 secondary metabolites spanning different chemical classes (Fig. 3A, B and Table 4), dominated by phenylpropanoids, phenazines, anthraquinones, and diketopiperazines (cyclic dipeptides). Most of the compounds were tentatively identified and have been previously reported in closely related bacterial strains. A review of the literature on tentatively identified compounds found that many exhibited potent antimicrobial activity. The most predominant metabolites were tentatively identified as microansamycin B (Rt = 3.27 min), Β-phenyllactic acid (Rt = 26.03 min), cyclo (Val-Pro) (Rt = 2.33 min), SB 212021 (Rt = 16.20 min), 3-trehalosamine (Rt = 8.86 min), Chromophenazine D (Rt = 24.73 min), and 1,4,6-trihydroxy-8-isopentyl-9,10-anthraquinone (Rt = 21.21). Other classes of tentatively identified compounds include cyclic amines, aminocyclitol, quinolones, and benzenoids; despite their relatively low area percentages, they may have contributed to the overall antimicrobial activities reported in the literature. Fig. S2 illustrates the chemical structures of the tentatively identified metabolites. Combining the genome mining findings and LCMS results, M. testaceum strain CCASU-2025-88 is postulated to be a putative producer of microansamycin B; its proposed biosynthesis is further supported by genomic analysis, although the identification remains tentative.”

Discussion

The discovery of new drugs is becoming increasingly essential owing to the rising frequency of MDR and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) pathogens, as well as issues with the management and treatment of illnesses triggered by “ESKAPE” infections (Singh and Dubey 2018; Taati Moghadam et al. 2021). Therefore, there are promising prospects for discovering novel extracts from diverse organisms, such as endophytic bacteria from plant origin (Mahdi et al. 2022). Endophytic microbes are ubiquitous in most plants and confer significant benefits to their hosts, enabling them to overcome diverse abiotic and biotic stresses that may hinder growth (Afzal et al. 2019). In the current study, endophytic bacteria were isolated and cultured for whole-genome sequencing, yielding M. testaceum (accession PRJNA1327108) in the NCBI GenBank database. The endophytic bacterium was isolated from the wild as Pluchea dioscoridis (L.) DC is growing in Cairo, Egypt. This bacterium demonstrated a promising antibacterial activity against three standard strains, comprising the microbial S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and the fungus C. albicans, and even two MDR clinical pathogenic organisms, specifically P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae. Similar to our findings, a study documented that the rice endophytic M. testaceum D18 exhibited distinct plant probiotic characteristics and pathogen-antibiotic activity (Patel et al. 2022), supporting the ability of M. testaceum to produce bioactive compounds with antibacterial activities.

Our study assessed cytotoxicity, and the results show that the crude extract of M. testaceum CCASU-2025-88 (P4) had a CC50 value (131.98 μg/mL), which indicates its safety to normal cells and encourages further investigation. Moreover, the organic extract P4 showed potential antiviral activity against three viruses: Adeno 7, HSV-1, and HSV-2. This extract demonstrated good antiviral activity against HSV-1, with the highest activity (2.34 log reduction) according to European Standard EN 14476. A log reduction ≥ 1 signifies significant viral suppression based on accepted antiviral evaluation standards. Our results show that secondary antimicrobial metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters could be valuable for the isolation and identification of novel antiviral drugs.

AntiSMASH analysis predicted that the biosynthetic gene cluster encodes ε-poly-L-lysine (Wang et al. 2024a). ε-poly-L-lysine (ε-PL) is a natural poly (amino acid) that was discovered in 1977 (Wang et al. 2024b). It exhibits potential antibacterial efficacy and is widely employed in the nutrition and pharmaceutical sectors (Wang et al. 2021a). In the current investigation, A conserved biosynthetic gene cluster showing 100% sequence identity to a reported ε-poly-L-lysine biosynthetic cluster was detected in the genome of M. testaceum LP4, suggesting its potential role in ε-poly-L-lysine production. The presence of this gene cluster likely contributes to the observed antibacterial activity of the P4 extract against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, as previously reported for the antimicrobial activity of ε-PL (Shih et al. 2006; Yan et al. 2018). ε-PL production has been found in bacteria such as Bacillus subtilis, Lactococcus, and Corynebacterium (El‐Sersy et al. 2012; Bhattacharya et al. 2017; Samadlouie et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2021), suggesting that M. testaceum LP4 could represent a promising endophytic source of antimicrobial ε-PL.

In addition to ε-PL, carotenoide terpenes, clifednamide A, and the pentaketide, microansamycin B. Among these, microansamycin B was tentatively identified as the most active antibacterial metabolite (Wang et al. 2024b), exhibiting strong and broad-spectrum activity against S. aureus and E. coli. Consistent with the AntiSMASH prediction, microansamycin B was also tentatively identified through LC–MS analysis. The biosynthetic gene responsible for this pentaketide antibiotic shares 89% amino acid sequence conservation with that of Micromonospora sp. HK160111 (NCBI accession: ATY46587.1). To our knowledge, this is the first report of microansamycin B production by a locally isolated strain. This assignment is still uncertain, though, because the study's metabolite identification is based only on LC–ESI–MS data; other investigations, mainly isolating the compound in a pure form from the bacterial culture or comparison with a genuine standard of the compound, would be necessary for conclusive confirmation.

On the other hand, LC–ESI–MS analysis tentatively identified 15 secondary metabolites in the P4 ethyl acetate extract, consistent with the proposed antimicrobial and antiviral actions. Four of these metabolites were classified as phenazines. Phenazines are a group of nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds known for their antimicrobial and biological activities (Huang et al. 2024). Phenazine production has been widely documented in bacteria such as Pseudomonas and Streptomyces (Yan et al. 2021), supporting the role of M. testaceum as a potential source of bioactive secondary metabolites. Among them, 1,6-phenazinediol-5-oxide has been reported to possess antimicrobial activity (Gerber and Lechevalier 1965). Previous studies have shown that chromophenazine D exhibits moderate antibacterial activity, with inhibition zones of approximately 10 mm against B. subtilis, E. coli, and Mucor miehei (Zendah et al. 2012). This suggests that chromophenazine D adds to the extract's overall bioactivity and may be responsible for the antibacterial effect seen in this study. Additionally, SB 212021 has been reported to exhibit poor antibacterial activity (Gilpin et al. 1995). Furthermore, 1-methoxyphenazine N′-oxide has been shown to have cytotoxic action, with the N-oxide functional group playing an important role in its biological activity (Huang et al. 2024), validating the bioactivity of phenazine derivatives found in M. testaceum. In addition, three other metabolites were classified as anthraquinones. For instance, 1,4,6-trihydroxy-8-isopentyl-9,10-anthraquinone has been shown to exert cytotoxic activity (Wu et al. 2018), while 1,4,6-trihydroxy-8-(2′-oxopentyl)-9,10-anthraquinone and 1,4,6-trihydroxy-8-pentacarboxyl-9,10-anthraquinone have been documented to have antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and significant cytotoxic activity (Taechowisa et al. 2025).

In addition to the phenazine derivatives and previously mentioned compounds, the LP4 extract contained nine other secondary metabolites from various chemical classes, including diketopiperazines (cyclic dipeptides), which are known for their antimicrobial and biological activities (Zayed et al. 2022), confirming M. testaceum LP4's bioactive potential. Cyclic dipeptides have been widely reported to have antimicrobial and antifungal activities, including cyclo (Pro-Tyr) and cyclo (Val-Pro) (Nishanth Kumar et al. 2014; Rahman et al. 2020; Kwak et al. 2018), supporting the bioactive potential of diketopiperazines detected in M. testaceum. β-phenyllactic acid, a phenylpropanoid compound, has been shown to have antimicrobial and antiviral properties (Mu et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2018; Jiang et al. 2022; Yu et al. 2023; Dai et al. 2024). This suggests that M. testaceum metabolites may play a role in its bioactivity.

Moreover, 2-(2-nonenyl)-3-methyl-4-quinolonone, a quinolone, was tentatively identified in LC–MS analysis. Previous studies have documented that Burkholderia thailandensis OMVs contain 2-(2-nonenyl)-3-methyl-4-quinolone, which inhibits both drug-resistant and susceptible strains (Saalim et al. 2020). The other quinolone was tentatively identified as Diazaquinomycin h, which has been reported in previous studies to possess antibacterial activity (Mullowney et al. 2015). The 3-Trehalosamine was tentatively identified in LC–MS analysis, which was classified as an aminoglycoside. Furthermore, the study documented that 3-Trehalosamine exhibited antibacterial activity (Dolak et al. 1980). Another metabolite tentatively identified by LC–MS analysis was Vitroprocine H, classified as a Benzenoid. Liaw et al. documented that Vitroprocine H has anti-bacterial effects (Liaw et al. 2015). Finally, Cispentacin, another metabolite, was tentatively identified by LC–MS and classified as a cyclic amine. This metabolite has been reported to have poor in vitro activity; it exhibits strong and selective antifungal activity in vivo, supporting the bioactive potential of LP4 metabolites (Konishi et al. 1989). The presence of these metabolites in the crude extract is consistent with the inhibitory patterns seen in our antimicrobial and antiviral testing.

In conclusion, the endophytic bacteria demonstrated antibacterial activity against standard and MDR clinical isolates, antiviral activity against Adeno 7, HSV-1, and HSV-2, and antifungal activity against the standard C. albicans. The whole genome sequencing and LC–ESI–MS analysis suggested the existence of antimicrobial secondary metabolites by M. testaceum CCASU-2025-88. Future research should focus on optimizing culture conditions to improve the yield of the corresponding antimicrobial metabolites and on evaluating these metabolites against a broader spectrum of MDR-resistant bacteria for prospective therapeutic applications.
