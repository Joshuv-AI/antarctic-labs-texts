Immunometabolic and brain structural alterations mediate increased risk of brain diseases in rheumatoid arthritis patients
Authors: Siyu He, Zeyu Li, Feng Zeng, Yifei Jin, Wei Zhang, Xinyi Chen, Zequn Zhang, Wei Cheng, Linbo Wang, Wenze Xiao, Qiyong Mei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is associated with an increased risk of brain diseases, yet the underlying biological mechanisms remain poorly understood. Using data from the UK Biobank, including blood-based biomarkers, brain imaging, and diagnostic records, we investigated the roles of immunometabolic and brain structure alterations in the link between RA and brain diseases. The effects of antirheumatic treatments were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models. To ensure a clear temporal sequence, only individuals with RA diagnosed before baseline were included. The cohort comprised 2534 prevalent RA cases (mean age: 59.80 ± 6.99 years; 70.84% women), with a mean disease duration of 5.54 years (SD = 3.57). RA was significantly associated with depression (HR = 1.659 [95% CI: 1.444–1.905]), sleep disturbances (HR = 1.673 [1.379–2.029]), and dementia (HR = 1.466 [1.225–1.755]). Mediation analyses revealed that immunometabolic alterations (indirect effect = 0.0099, P < 0.001) and reduced white matter integrity (FA; indirect effect = 0.012, P = 0.002) mediated these associations. Notably, treatment with slow-acting antirheumatic drugs (SAARDs) was linked to a reduced risk of anxiety (P = 0.006). These findings indicate that immunometabolic alterations in RA may lead to a decrease in white matter integrity, thereby increasing susceptibility to brain diseases. They highlight the importance of managing systemic immunometabolic alterations to preserve brain health in RA patients.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease predominantly affecting the joints, but it is increasingly acknowledged as a systemic condition with a range of extraarticular manifestations, including rheumatoid nodules, pulmonary involvement, and vasculitis [1]. In addition to its established link to cardiovascular disease [2, 3], a growing body of evidence associates RA with a spectrum of central nervous system complications, including neuropsychiatric symptoms, cognitive decline, and an elevated risk for neurodegenerative disorders [4, 5]. Notably, shared genetic susceptibility loci have been identified between RA and several brain diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease [6] and bipolar disorder [7]. These genetic links are corroborated by neuroimaging findings: studies in RA cohorts have reported a higher burden of white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) compared to age-matched controls [8]. WMHs have an underlying pathology of demyelination and axonal loss and are recognized radiological manifestation of cerebral small vessel disease [9], suggesting that the systemic vasculopathy and endothelial dysfunction inherent to RA may extend to the cerebral microvasculature.

Key pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, which play central roles in RA pathogenesis, can either cross a compromised blood-brain barrier (BBB) or activate intracellular signaling pathways within the endothelium [10, 11]. This may trigger the recruitment and activation of resident immune cells in the brain, including microglia and astrocytes [12, 13]. At the mechanistic level, systemic inflammation, which is hallmark of RA, is likely a key mediator linking RA with brain disorders. Furthermore, BBB integrity is tightly regulated, in part, by immune function and lipid transport, dependent suppression of caveolae-mediated transcytosis [14]. Immune activation and dysregulated lipid metabolism may therefore compromise BBB function and exacerbate neuroinflammatory responses [15]. Notably, these immunometabolic alterations in RA are potentially reflected in peripheral blood biomarkers. However, systematic investigations into alterations in blood biomarkers among individuals with RA, and how these changes relate to brain structure and the risk of brain disorders, remain limited in large population-based studies.

Emerging evidence indicates that treatments for RA, such as methotrexate [16], might possess neuroprotective properties. For instance, studies have suggested that antirheumatic drugs use is associated with a reduced risk of dementia in RA patients [17–20]. However, the neuroprotective potential of other antirheumatic drug therapy remains poorly understood. Therefore, long-term, targeted studies are necessary to clarify whether treatment with antirheumatic drugs can specifically reduce the risk of different neurodegenerative diseases in the RA population.

This study aims to investigate the relationship between RA and brain disorders by examining the roles of immune, metabolic changes, and therapeutic strategies in mitigating brain disease risks among RA patients. We utilize comprehensive data from the UK Biobank (UKB) [21], integrating brain imaging, and blood hematological, biochemical and metabolomic data into a cohesive analysis. By employing Cox proportional hazards models and Mendelian randomization analyses, we assess the overall relationship of RA and brain disease risk. We further investigate the associations between brain structure (through diffusion tensor imaging) and blood biomarkers with the risks of RA. Additionally, we explore how these factors mediate the link between RA and brain disease risks and evaluate the influence of RA medications on these risks.

Materials and methods

Study population and phenotypes

We performed a comprehensive study of relationship between RA and brain diseases by using data from the UKB cohort, a large prospective study with phenotypic data on half a million UK participants, aged 37–73 years at recruitment [21]. The UKB cohort was approved by the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (REC Reference: 21/NW/0157), which provided oversight for this study. This study was conducted under project application number 19542, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

In this study, RA was defined according to the UKB Field 41270, a summary of distinct diagnosis codes based on ICD-10 across all patients’ hospital inpatient records in either the primary or secondary position. If the individual has a hospital inpatient record of “seropositive rheumatoid arthritis” (ICD-10 Code M05), “other rheumatoid arthritis” (ICD-10 Code M06), or “juvenile arthritis” (ICD-10 Code M08), they were classified as having RA. A total of 2534 prevalent RA cases were included (59.80 ± 6.99 years, 70.84% women), with an average disease duration of 5.54 years (SD = 3.57 years). Blood-based biomarkers

To further explore the mechanisms that link RA to brain diseases, we included three categories of blood markers in the analysis. These are blood biochemical (Category ID: 17518), blood hematological (Category ID: 100081), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomic markers (Category ID: 220). Blood biochemical markers included about 480,000 participant samples from the recruitment visit (2006–2010), and biomarker assay quality procedures are provided in the open-source document (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/biomarker_issues.pdf). In addition, we included 30 blood biochemistry markers and categorized them based on biological function, including “Liver function”, “Renal function”, “Endocrine”, “Immunometabolism”, and “Bone and joint” (Supplementary Table 2). Taking into account sex differences, we stratified testosterone, estradiol, and sex hormone-binding globulin by sex and then performed the analysis. Blood hematological markers were also collected from 480,000 participants, using Beckman Coulter LH750 instruments to analyze samples, which is provided in the document (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/haematology.pdf). After excluding the nucleated red cell count and percentage, we included a total of 29 cell counts, which were divided into “Red blood cell”, “White blood cell”, and “Platelet” (Supplementary Table 2).

NMR metabolomic markers were measured from EDTA plasma samples of approximate 280,000 UKB participants, using a high-throughput NMR-based metabolic biomarker profiling platform. The more details of sample processing are provided on the website (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/label.cgi?id=220). In total, we used 168 directly measured markers and categorized them as “Amino acids”, “Apolipoproteins”, “Lipoprotein particle sizes”, “Lipoprotein particle concentrations”, “Fatty acids”, “Triglycerides”, “Phospholipids”, “Cholesteryl esters”, “Free cholesterol”, “Cholesterol”, “Other lipids”, “Total lipids”, “Ketone bodies”, “Glycolysis related metabolites”, “Fluid balance”, and “Inflammation” (Supplementary Table 3).

Diffusion tensor imaging measures

A subset of participants (N = 43,694) from the UKB underwent diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), yielding imaging-derived phenotypes that were used to investigate the association between RA and brain structure. The details of imaging measurement and preprocessing are provided in the open-source document (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/ukb/ukb/docs/brain_mri.pdf). Specifically, we used the “dMRI weighted means” dataset (UKB Category ID: 135), which provides tract-averaged diffusion metrics for 27 major white matter tracts across the whole brain, derived from a probabilistic tractography atlas (AutoPTx). All 27 tracts were initially included in our analysis. We extracted nine microstructural parameters for each tract, including fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial (L1) and radial (L2, L3) diffusivities, diffusion tensor mode (MO), intra-cellular volume fraction (ICVF), isotropic or free water volume fraction (ISOVF), and orientation dispersion index (OD) (Supplementary Table 4), which allows for multifaceted assessment of white matter integrity. These measures were obtained using the UKB imaging pipeline, which applies standardized protocols. DTI data were acquired on a Siemens Skyra 3 T scanner and underwent preprocessing including gradient distortion correction, eddy current and motion correction (via the FSL Eddy tool), and tract segmentation using the AutoPtx plugin in FSL, which maps standard region-of-interest (ROI) masks onto individual subjects to generate tract-averaged metrics. The visualization tool for white matter was from a previous study [22].

Association of RA with brain diseases, blood markers and DTI measures

We used Cox proportional hazards model to estimate the risk of brain-related disease outcomes in UKB participants with RA compared with those without. To further explore the potential mechanisms, we examined whether blood markers or white matter microstructures were associated with RA, potentially mediating its relationship with brain diseases. These associations were tested using linear regression models. The Cox models were based on 2534 participants with RA before baseline and 489,911 controls, and were adjusted for age, sex, race (White or Non-White), body mass index (BMI), Townsend index, highest educational qualification, smoking status, and alcohol drinking status. To account for the potential confounding effects of comorbid conditions, we conducted sensitivity analyses by further adjusting for the presence of other brain disease included in this study, where these diagnoses occurred prior to the onset of the brain disease being modelled. For the analysis of blood markers, we used the same group of participants with prevalent RA prior to baseline. For the analysis on DTI measures, RA cases were restricted to individuals whose RA diagnosis occurred before the date of DTI acquisition. The DTI models were further adjusted for imaging scanning sites. Considering that the UKB provides multiple white matter tract atlases, we performed a supplementary analysis using FA from an extended DTI-48 template covering 48 tracts. This additional analysis was conducted using the same linear regression model and covariate adjustments, aiming to verify the robustness of the associations observed in the primary 27-tract analysis. The False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied for associations of RA with brain diseases and blood markers, and DTI measures.

Causal relationship between RA and depression by Mendelian randomization

We employed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to determine the causal relationship between RA and brain diseases that shown significant associations with RA in the Cox models. The analysis methods included MR Egger, weighted median, inverse variance weighted, simple mode, and weighted mode, which were implemented in the R package TwoSampleMR (https://mrcieu.github.io/TwoSampleMR/). When only two instrumental variables were available, only the inverse variance weighted method was applied. In cases with a single instrumental variable, the Wald ratio method was used. We treated RA and each brain disease as the exposure variables, respectively, to explore the two-direction causal relationship between them. The genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary result of RA was obtained from a previous meta-analysis study [23], and the GWAS summary result of brain diseases were obtained from “F5_ALLANXIOUS”, “F5_DEMENTIA”, “F5_DEPRESSIO”, “G6_EPLEPSY”, “SLEEP”, and “9_STR” in FinnGen cohort (https://r10.finngen.fi/) [24]. We selected instrumental variables (IVs) with the threshold of P < 5 × 10−8, then removed the relevant single nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs) (500 kb, r2 > 0.1) and retained only SNPs with the smallest P values associated with disease.

Mediation analysis of RA and brain diseases

To further investigate the potential mediating roles of immunometabolic dysregulation and brain structure in the association between RA and brain diseases, we conducted mediation analyses. Based on the above analysis results, we selected four categories significantly associated with RA in blood markers and DTI measures, including “Immunometabolism”, “White blood cell”, “FA”, and “MD”. Each category had multiple phenotypes, so we first selected the phenotypes significantly associated with RA (Supplementary Table 5), then adjusted the correlated direction according to the association result and finally normalized and averaged the values to represent the categorical score. Two types of mediation models were applied to explore the relationship between RA and brain diseases. The first model tested whether blood biomarkers or brain imaging metrics mediated the effect of RA on brain disease, following pathways such as RA → Immunometabolism (or White blood cell) / FA (or MD) → Brain disease. The second model examined a serial mediation pathway in which RA influenced brain disease through both blood markers and brain structure sequentially, represented as RA → Immunometabolism (or White blood cell) → FA (or MD) → Brain disease. In these analyses, brain diseases were combined into a single binary outcome indicating whether a participant had developed any of the included brain diseases during follow-up. This approach allowed for a more comprehensive assessment of RA-related brain health, accounted for overlapping mechanisms and frequent comorbidities across conditions.

To preserve the temporal ordering of exposure, mediator, and outcome, the definition of brain disease onset varied depending on the mediator. For models where blood markers were the only mediator, brain disease was defined as any diagnosis occurring after baseline, as blood smaples were collected at baseline. These models were adjusted for age, sex, race, BMI, Townsend index, highest educational qualification, smoking status, and alcohol drinking status. For models included DTI as a mediator, brain disease was defined as any diagnosis that occurred after the DTI scan date. These models were further adjusted for imaging scanning site. Analysis used 5000 iteration nonparametric bootstrap approach to estimate direct and indirect associations, which was performed using R package lavaan(https://lavaan.org).

Association of RA drug therapy with the risk of brain disease

To investigate the impact of RA drug therapies on the risk of brain disease, we conducted an analysis exploring whether pharmacological treatments for RA influence patients’ susceptibility to such diseases. The study included medications documented in the UKB drug treatment records (UKB Field 20003), covering slow-acting antirheumatic drugs (SAARDs) like methotrexate and leflunomide, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as celecoxib (Celebrex), etoricoxib, and diclofenac, and glucocorticoids including prednisone, methylprednisolone, prednisolone, dexamethasone, betamethasone, hydrocortisone, and cortisone. Specifically, these medications alleviate symptoms (e.g., pain), reduce inflammation, and slow disease progression.

We performed medication analyses in both RA and non-RA populations. In the RA group, only individuals with RA diagnosed before baseline were included; in the non-RA group, all participants had no RA diagnosis. Brain diseases were defined as diagnoses occurring after baseline, and controls were those with no diagnosis of any studied brain disease either before or after baseline. To assess how these drug therapies affect the risk of developing brain diseases, we employed Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. This method estimates the survival function from follow up data, providing insights into how different classes of RA medications might modulate the incidence of brain diseases over time. By comparing survival curves between treated and untreated groups or across different treatment regimens, we aimed to uncover potential protective or adverse effects associated with each class of medication.

Results

Population characteristics

We utilized data from a total of 492445 participants from UKB (RA cases, N = 2534; controls, N = 489,911), of whom 54.23% were female (RA cases, 70.84%; controls, 54.15%), with an average age of 56.48 ± 8.90 years (RA cases, 59.80 ± 6.99 years; controls, 56.47 ± 8.10 years), and 90.74% were of White ethnicity (RA cases, 92.86%; controls, 90.72%). The mean BMI was 27.41 ± 4.77. Additionally, and 10.49% of the participants were classified as current smokers, while 92.05% reported current alcohol consumption. In terms of educational attainment, 47.42% had completed higher education. Socioeconomic status, assessed by the Townsend deprivation index, showed an average score of −1.31 ± 3.09. Increased risk of brain diseases in RA patients

We employed a Cox proportional hazards regression model to examine the association between RA and the risk of developing brain diseases (Fig. 2). After adjusting for covariates such as age, sex, BMI, Townsend deprivation index, education, smoking, alcohol consumption, and race (White or non-White), we found that RA was associated with a significantly higher risk of brain diseases. Specifically, participants with a record of RA diagnosis exhibited a markedly higher risk of anxiety (HR = 1.41 [1.23–1.61], P = 4.91 × 10−7), dementia (HR = 1.47 [1.23–1.75], P = 2.97 × 10−5), depression (HR = 1.66 [1.44–1.91], P = 7.41 × 10−13), epilepsy (HR = 1.68 [1.21–2.32], P = 1.82 × 10−3), sleep disorder (HR = 1.67 [1.38–2.03], P = 1.71 × 10−7), and stroke (HR = 1.63 [1.38–1.93], P = 1.80 × 10−8). These associations remained significant after FDR correction (all FDR < 0.05). In contrast, no significant associations were observed for bipolar disorder, Parkinson’s disease, or schizophrenia (P > 0.05). Causal relationship between RA and brain diseases

To further probe the potential causal nature of these associations, we performed two-sample MR analyses. Our results showed that genetic liability for RA was nominally associated with an increased risk of depression (MR Egger: OR = 1.03 [95% CI: 1.01–1.04], P = 0.0046; Weighted mode: OR = 1.03 [95% CI: 1.01–1.04], P = 0.0026), which was the strongest signal among all tested brain diseases. However, after correcting for multiple comparisons, none of the associations, including that with depression, remained statistically significant (all FDR-adjusted P > 0.05; Supplementary Table 7). Bidirectional MR analyses did not support a causal effect of brain diseases on the risk of RA. These findings suggest that while there is a suggestive causal link from RA to depression, larger genetic studies may be needed to confirm this relationship robustly.

Correlation between blood markers, DTI indicators and RA risk

In our analysis of peripheral biomarkers, adjusted for covariates, we identified a total of 17 blood biochemical markers, and 24 blood hematological markers and 102 metabolic markers that reached statistical significance after FDR correction (Fig. 3A). Among the immunometabolic markers, C-reactive protein (CRP) demonstrated the strongest positive association (β = 0.9878, P < 0.001), whereas cholesterol was significantly negatively associated (β = –0.2050, P = 6.24 × 10−24). Among white blood cell markers, both neutrophil percentage (β = 0.4717, P = 1.61 × 10−166) and neutrophil count (β = 0.3867, P = 8.66 × 10−84) exhibited strong positive associations, while lymphocyte percentage showed the most significant negative association (β = –0.5448, P = 3.02 × 10−157). DTI revealed significant microstructural alterations in white matter tracts among incident RA patients (N = 43,694, Fig. 3B). FA values were markedly reduced in the right anterior thalamic radiation (β = –0.2314, P = 3.29 × 10⁻⁵), left superior thalamic radiation (β = –0.2199, P = 8.81 × 10⁻⁵), and left superior longitudinal fasciculus (β = –0.1981, P = 3.61 × 10⁻⁴), indicating compromised fiber integrity. In addition, increased MD was observed in several key white matter tracts, including the right anterior thalamic radiation (β = 0.2354, P = 4.21 × 10⁻⁶), left superior thalamic radiation (β = 0.2224, P = 2.51 × 10⁻⁵), right inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (β = 0.2081, P = 1.10 × 10⁻⁴), and right corticospinal tract (β = 0.1755, P = 1.89 × 10⁻³), suggesting disruption of white matter fiber structure.(Supplementary Table 9). To ensure the robustness and comprehensiveness of these findings, we conducted a further exploratory analysis encompassing all 48 white matter tracts available in the UKB dataset. This expanded analysis confirmed and extended our primary observations, revealing a widespread pattern of altered white matter microstructure in RA. Notably, significant associations, including reduced FA and increased MD, were consistently found across numerous additional tracts, such as the bilateral anterior limb of the internal capsule and the superior longitudinal fasciculus (full results in Supplementary Table 10). These results reinforce the conclusion that RA is associated with global, rather than focal, changes in white matter integrity.

We first investigated pathways involving a single mediator. Our analyses showed that systemic immune-metabolic dysregulation was a significant pathway connecting RA to brain disease risk. The composite score for the white blood cell count significantly mediated this relationship (indirect effect = 0.0108, P < 0.001). In parallel, we found that white matter microstructural damage served as another crucial, independent pathway. Lower FA, reflecting degraded fiber integrity, significantly mediated the link between RA and future brain disease risk (indirect effect = 0.012, P = 0.0018). Likewise, higher MD, an indicator of greater water diffusion and potential tissue damage, also acted as a significant mediator (indirect effect = 0.012, P = 0.012). These findings suggest that both systemic biological disturbances and direct brain structural changes are distinct and significant pathways through which RA increases brain disease risk (Supplementary Table 11).

Building on these findings, we tested a more sophisticated sequential mediation model to explore a potential causal cascade: RA → Blood Biomarker → DTI Indicator → Brain Disease. This model tests the hypothesis that systemic changes precede and contribute to brain structural damage. This analysis revealed a significant cascade of events specifically involving immunometabolism. We found a significant indirect effect for the pathway of RA → Immunometabolism → MD → Brain Disease (indirect effect = 0.0005, P = 0.033). A similar, though slightly weaker, significant sequential pathway was observed via FA: RA → Immunometabolism → FA → Brain Disease (indirect effect = 0.0004, P = 0.017). In contrast, the corresponding sequential pathways initiating with white blood cell count as the mediator did not reach statistical significance, with P values greater than 0.9 for both the FA and MD pathways (Supplementary Table 12).

Association of drug therapy with brain disease risk

We performed Kaplan-Meier survival analyses to evaluate whether common RA treatments were associated with a modified risk of incident brain diseases in RA patients (Fig. 5). Our analysis revealed a specific protective association for SAARDs. Patients treated with SAARDs showed a statistically significant reduction in the risk of developing anxiety (P = 0.006). However, we did not observe a significant association between SAARDs use and the risk of dementia, depression, epilepsy, sleep disorders, or stroke (all P > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant protective effects were found for patients treated with either NSAIDs or GCs across any of the brain diseases examined (all P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 13). As an extension, we examined the associations between NSAIDs, glucocorticoids, and neuropsychiatric diseases in a large non-RA cohort (N = 489,911). While SAARDs showed no significant associations, both NSAIDs and glucocorticoids were linked to increased risks of several brain disorders. Discussion

This study integrates blood biomarkers and neuroimaging data to investigate the association between RA and brain diseases. We identified significant links between RA and specific neurological and psychiatric conditions, particularly depression and anxiety. These associations persist after adjusting for extensive confounders and remain robust in sensitivity analyses accounting for comorbidities. Our findings also reveal that systemic immunometabolic dysregulation and widespread white matter microstructural alterations may serve as critical biological alterations linking RA to brain disease. Furthermore, antirheumatic therapies demonstrated protective effects against anxiety disorders, suggesting potential neuroprotective roles. These findings underscore the importance of considering the broader impact of RA on brain health and support early intervention with effective antirheumatic therapies to mitigate neurologic complications.

Our findings align with existing evidence linking RA to brain disorders [25–29]. For instance, a recent meta-analysis confirmed elevated risks of depression in RA patients and vice versa, with depressive symptoms reducing the likelihood of achieving clinical remission [30]. While MR analysis suggests a potential causal link between RA and depression, the lack of statistical significance after multiple testing correction underscores the need for larger genetic studies to validate this relationship.

The observed associations between RA and brain diseases are consistent with the systemic inflammatory and immunometabolic profile of RA. Our identification of 17 blood biochemical markers, 24 hematological markers, and 102 metabolic markers associated with RA further emphasizes the role of systemic immunometabolism in this interplay. This observation is consistent with a large body of literature demonstrating that the chronic inflammation can disrupt peripheral metabolic homeostasis and compromise BBB integrity, thereby creating a systemic environment detrimental to brain health [31–37].

Systemic immunometabolism abnormal in RA, may directly compromise the BBB integrity, activating neuroinflammatory cascades that impact brain structures. We found that microstructural integrity in white matter, characterized by reduced fractional anisotropy and increased mean diffusivity in RA, suppporting this hypothesis. These microstructural alterations in key white matter tracts, such as the anterior thalamic radiation and superior longitudinal fasciculus, are consistent with prior neuroimaging studies that have linked similar changes to cognitive and mood disorders [38–43].

Mediation analyses provide a mechanistic framework for understanding how RA exerts its neurologic effects. Systemic immunometabolic dysregulation appears to function as an upstream driver, with subsequent white matter damage acting as a downstream mediator. Notably, the sequential mediation model (RA → immunometabolism → MD/FA → brain disease) suggests a causal cascade wherein peripheral immunometabolic disturbances precede and contribute to brain structural changes. This finding is particularly significant given the increasing recognition of immune-metabolic crosstalk in neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders [44–46]. However, the modest effect sizes of these pathways (standardized indirect effects ~0.0002–0.0005) suggest that additional factors, such as genetic predispositions, environmental influences, may modulate this relationship.

Antirheumatic therapies, particularly SAARDs, were associated with reduced risks of anxiety, consistent with previous findings [47, 48]. This highlights the potential of targeting systemic inflammation as a strategy to reduce psychiatric comorbidities in RA patients. Notably, these therapeutic benefits may arise from dual mechanisms: the direct suppression of neuroinflammatory processes and the alleviation of RA-associated physical burdens, such as chronic pain and disability, which are well-established contributors to anxiety.

Although our study leverages large-scale, community-based multimodal datasets, several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the results.First, our reliance on ICD-10 codes to define RA may underestimate disease prevalence by excluding undiagnosed or early-stage cases and precludes analysis based on disease severity. Second, we cannot exclude the possibility of reverse causality between RA and brain disorders. Although our analysis focused on cases where RA onset preceded brain disorders, it is also plausible that early neuronal damage or immune responses occur before the clinical diagnosis of RA, with brain pathology present but not yet manifesting as overt symptoms. Third, the UK Biobank cohort is predominantly of White European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Replication in diverse, multi-ethnic populations is essential to confirm the broader applicability of the proposed mechanisms. Finally, our pharmacological analyses were restricted to conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs), NSAIDs, and glucocorticoids, with no data on treatment duration or advanced therapies such as biological or targeted synthetic DMARDs. These latter agents, which modulate specific inflammatory pathways, may offer more potent neuroprotective effects and warrant further investigation.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that RA is associated with extensive alterations in immunometabolic markers and widespread white matter microstructural abnormalities. The increased risk of brain disorders appears to be potentially mediated by immunometabolic alterations and disruptions in white matter integrity. Antirheumatic therapies, particularly SAARDs, may reduce anxiety risk, offering a translational pathway for neuroprotection in RA. These findings provide a mechanistic foundation for developing preventive strategies targeting the immune-metabolic-neurological axis, with the potential to reduce the global burden of brain disorders in this vulnerable population.
