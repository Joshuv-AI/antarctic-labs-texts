Optimal serum vitamin D3 levels are associated with lower osteoarthritis risk and better overall health in US adults
Authors: Haifan Wang, Zeyu Liu, Xiaoyang Wang, Xiaoqian Dang, Zhibin Shi, Haoyu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To recommend serum Vitamin D3(VD3) levels for osteoarthritis patients and susceptible populations by analyzing the dose-effect relationship between VD3 and the risk of osteoarthritis(OA) and between life’s essential 8 and VD3 in OA patients. This study analyzed the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2018 cross-sectional data, excluding incomplete cases. Weighted baselines were calculated, and multiple logistic regression was used to assess the VD3-OA risk relationship. Multiple linear regression explored the VD3-LE8 score association in general and OA populations. Curve associations were modeled with restricted cubic splines, followed by subgroup and sensitivity analysis, and segmented regression based on the spline outcomes. Of 18,107 participants, OA patients had higher VD3 (76.79 nmol/L) and lower LE8 (64.70) than normal participants (65.96 nmol/L, 70.02). VD3 levels (Q1-Q4) correlated with OA risks of 9.82%-19.77%. Higher VD3 increased OA risk (Estimate = 0.006, P < 0.0001 per 1nmol/L), but RCS and segmented regression showed a U-shaped association (cutoff: 41 nmol/L). In OA patients and normals, VD3 positively correlated with LE8 (Estimate = 0.035, P < 0.001 for per 1nmol/L), with a linear RCS relationship. Sensitivity analysis confirmed these results. Our findings reveal a U-shaped association between VD3 levels and the risk of osteoarthritis (OA), while a positive association between VD3 and LE8 score. Based on these observations, we recommend maintaining moderate serum VD3 levels, for both OA-susceptible individuals and patients to optimize its overall health benefits. Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic and progressive joint disease characterized by the breakdown of articular cartilage, resulting in pain, stiffness, and limited joint function1. It is a leading cause of disability and reduced quality of life among the elderly population, affecting millions of individuals worldwide2. The pathogenesis of OA is complex and multifaceted, involving genetic, biomechanical, and metabolic factors. Among these, vitamin D3, a fat-soluble vitamin crucial for bone health, has garnered significant attention in recent years due to its potential role in OA development and progression3,4.

Vitamin D3, also known as cholecalciferol, is primarily synthesized in the skin upon exposure to ultraviolet B radiation or obtained through dietary sources4. It undergoes metabolic conversion in the liver and kidneys to its active form, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3, which regulates calcium and phosphorus homeostasis, bone health, and muscle function5. Recent studies have suggested that vitamin D3 may also play a role in OA development3,6,7.

While many studies have reported a protective effect of vitamin D₃ against OA6,8,9, others have found no significant association4,10, and one NHANES-based study even reported a positive association between serum VD₃ levels and OA prevalence3. Notably, most prior research has focused on the adverse effects of vitamin D₃ deficiency, with limited attention given to the potential risks of excessive VD₃ levels. Our study contributes to this body of evidence by identifying a possible U-shaped relationship, suggesting that both low and high serum VD₃ levels may be associated with an increased risk of OA.

Life’s essential 8(LE8) score comprises four key health behaviors: regular physical activity, a balanced and healthy diet, abstaining from smoking, and maintaining sufficient sleep11. Additionally, LE8 includes four critical health factors: maintaining a healthy body mass index (BMI), managing total cholesterol levels, controlling blood glucose, and keeping blood pressure within normal ranges12,13. By monitoring and maintaining these factors, individuals can significantly reduce their risk of developing cardiovascular diseases and other chronic illnesses14,15. To evaluate the overall health effects of vitamin D3, we will utilize the LE8 score. The LE8 score will help us gain a more nuanced understanding of how vitamin D3 levels may influence not only OA risk but also overall well-being.

In this study, we aim to examine the association between vitamin D3 levels and OA risk using data from the NHANES 2007–2018. Specifically, we will investigate the relationship between serum vitamin D3 levels and the risk of OA. To evaluate the overall health effects of vitamin D3, the LE8 score will help us gain a more nuanced understanding of how vitamin D3 levels may influence not only OA risk but also overall well-being.

Methods

Data source and study design

The fundamental data for this research stems from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a publicly accessible initiative administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). NHANES’s core objective is to offer a cross-sectional perspective on the health and nutritional standing of a nationally representative cohort of non-institutionalized civilians residing in the United States. Data collection is conducted annually in various regions across the United States, either via household-based interviews where individuals voluntarily provide information or at mobile examination facilities. Once compiled, these data are analyzed and disseminated biennially. The NHANES program has secured approval from the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and all adult participants provide written consent, affirming their informed participation and safeguarding their rights and privacy. This rigorous approach to data collection renders NHANES an invaluable resource for public health research, especially in the domains of health and nutrition.

After the data collection and rigorous cleaning process, a comprehensive analysis was conducted to examine the association between serum VD3 levels and the risk of osteoarthritis (OA), as well as the correlation between serum VD3 levels and LE8 scores in both the general population and OA patients. Additionally, the curve relationship between these variables was identified to facilitate segmented analysis. The recommended concentration of serum VD3 was determined through a holistic consideration of its impact on OA risk and overall health status, including the LE8 score. Furthermore, this study adheres to the rigorous standards set forth by the STROBE initiative and the RECORD guidelines (https://www.record-statement.org/), ensuring a robust and methodical approach in the conduct of our research.

Study population

The dataset spanning 2007–2018, encompassing six two-year cycles, was analyzed to examine VD3’s dose-dependent influence on OA risk and its general health implications for OA patients. Participants with missing or unknown answers for identification were excluded, and those inherently lacking data due to survey design features, such as criteria-based exclusions or skip patterns, were also omitted.

As shown in Fig. 1, a screening process encompassed 55,051 participants initially. However, subsequent exclusions were made based on data deficiencies. Specifically, 7764 participants were excluded due to the absence of VD3 data, 4150 for incomplete PIR information, 3827 for missing education level data, and 13,689 for lacking osteoarthritis details. Additionally, 4520 participants were dropped for not having a LE8 score, while a further 1638 were excluded due to incomplete LE8 scores. Subsequently, 1356 participants were also excluded for missing drinking habits data. After these rigorous exclusions, the final cohort comprised 18,107 participants, including 2238 with osteoarthritis, all of whom had complete data.

Measurement of serum vitamin D3

Blood samples for vitamin D3 measurement were collected at Mobile Examination Centers (MECs), frozen at − 30◦C, and sent to the National Center for Environmental Health (CDC) in Atlanta for analysis using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2018.

Assessment of osteoarthritis

To qualify as an OA case, participants must have confirmed a doctor’s diagnosis of arthritis and indicated “OA” (2001–2010) or “OA/degenerative arthritis” (2011–2018) as the specific type.

Life’s essential 8

Four health behaviors (diet, physical activity, tobacco exposure, and sleep duration) and four health factors (body mass index, Non-high-density lipoprotein, blood glucose, and blood pressure) were used to calculate the total LE8 Score. The sum of the eight independent scores is the LE8 score.

Covariates

In this study, a comprehensive set of variables was included for analysis, encompassing age, gender, race, education level, poverty income ratio (PIR), height, body mass index (BMI), drinking habits, and smoking status. The classification of these confounders adhered closely to precedents set by NHANES publications. To minimize sample loss and multicollinearity, we limited adjustment to core covariates consistently available in NHANES. Variables such as physical activity, joint injury history, and occupation were not included due to high missingness.

Gender was dichotomized into male and female. Race was categorized as Mexican Americans, Other Hispanics, Non-Hispanic Whites, Non-Hispanic Blacks, and Other Races. Education levels were stratified into five groups: 9-11th grade (inclusive of 12th grade without a diploma), college graduate or higher, high school graduate/GED or equivalent, less than 9th grade, and some college or associate’s degree. Drinking habits were categorized into five distinct classes: former, heavy, mild, moderate, and never. Smoking status was similarly segmented into current, former, and never smoking.

For continuous variables such as age, PIR, height, and BMI, quartile analysis was performed to facilitate subgroup comparisons among participants. Age was segmented into quartiles ranging from 20 to 34 years (Q1), 34–48 years (Q2), 48–63 years (Q3), and 63–80 years (Q4). PIR quartiles were defined as 0–1.16.16 (Q1), 1.16–2.22 (Q2), 2.22–4.26 (Q3), and 4.26–5.26 (Q4). Height quartiles were 135.3–160.2 cm (Q1), 160.2–167.2 cm (Q2), 167.2–174.7 cm (Q3), and 174.7–204.5 cm (Q4). BMI quartiles were 13.18–24.37 (Q1), 24.37–28.03 (Q2), 28.03–32.6 (Q3), and 32.6–82.1 (Q4).

Statistical analysis data

Data cleansing and analysis were performed in R 4.3.2, utilizing the “survey” package (4.2-1.2) to ensure nationally representative estimates by accounting for sampling weights and survey design. Baseline characteristics were analyzed and stratified by OA status and VD3 quartiles. For continuous variables, mean values with standardized errors (SE) were reported, while percentages were used for categorical variables. Variable comparisons employed the t-test and one-way ANOVA for normal distributions, Kruskal–Wallis for skewed distributions, and Chi-square tests for categorical data.

To examine the independent link between serum VD3 and osteoarthritis, we employed multivariate logistic regression, adjusting for various covariate models. As BMI may act as both a confounder and a mediator, we used stepwise models (Model 0–Model 3). The VD3–OA association remained consistent across models with and without BMI, supporting the robustness of our findings. We calculated the odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI), considering VD3 concentrations as both continuous (per 1 nmol/L increment) and categorical variables.

Furthermore, we assessed the independent relationship between serum VD3 and LE8 scores among all participants and OA patients using multivariate linear regression, adjusting for different covariate models. Linear trend tests, subgroup analyses, and interaction assessments were also conducted.

For sensitivity analyses, we (i) re-examined the data subset comprising participants aged 45 years and above, given the higher prevalence of OA in older individuals; (ii) segmented VD₃ levels into ten consecutive, equal intervals and incorporated these into the analytical framework (Supplementary Tables S5–S10); (iii) compared the weighted baseline characteristics of the full NHANES sample, the sample excluding only participants with missing OA status, and the complete-case analytic sample to assess potential changes in representativeness (Supplementary Tables S11–S13); and (iv) conducted an alternative analysis in which the LE8 and alcohol consumption variables were not used as exclusion criteria—given that alcohol consumption showed no significant association with OA risk in our regression models—thereby lowering the overall exclusion rate from 67.1% to 53.4% (Supplementary Tables S14–S15).

Sensitivity analyses

The results of sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of our findings and are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Tables S5–S10). These included analyses using VD₃ levels divided into ten equal intervals and analyses restricted to participants aged ≥ 45 years.

In addition, we compared the weighted baseline characteristics of the full NHANES sample, the sample excluding only participants with missing OA status, and the complete-case sample (Tables S11–S13). Excluding participants with missing OA status increased the mean age from 37.9 to 46.6 years, mean BMI from 27.0 to 29.0 kg/m², and PIR from 2.84 to 2.99. Complete-case analysis yielded similar values (age: 47.1 years; BMI: 29.0 kg/m²; PIR: 3.11), suggesting that the primary representativeness shift occurred at the OA-missing exclusion stage, with minimal additional change from further exclusions.

Furthermore, in an alternative scenario where the LE8 and alcohol consumption variables were not used as exclusion criteria—given that alcohol consumption showed no significant association with OA risk in our regression models—the overall exclusion rate decreased from 67.1% to 53.4% (Tables S14–S15). The main findings, including the U-shaped association between VD₃ levels and OA risk, remained essentially unchanged in both magnitude and direction, further supporting the robustness of the results.

Discussion

The present study utilized data from the NHANES database(2007–2018), including complete serum vitamin D3, LE8 score, and osteoarthritis status. Serum vitamin D3 concentrations were quintile, and multiple regression was conducted to examine the association between serum vitamin D3 concentrations and the risk of OA.

Our results initially indicated a positive correlation between serum vitamin D3 levels and the general risk of OA. However, upon further analysis using smoothing curve fitting and subgroup evaluations, we found a U-shaped association between VD3 and the risk of OA. This U-shaped pattern was further validated through segmented analysis. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the overall health effects of VD3, we investigated the relationship between VD3 levels and the LE8 score in both the general participant pool and OA patients. Our findings reveal a significant positive association between VD3 levels and higher LE8 scores in both groups. These discoveries suggest that maintaining optimal levels of VD3 for individuals susceptible to OA and patients already diagnosed with the condition may be a potential approach to maximizing overall health benefits for populations with different serum VD3 levels.

Our findings may explain for the controversial results from previous studies on the association between serum 25(OH)D and osteoarthritis. By the way, it’s worth mentioning that VD3 is the largest constituent (higher than VD2 largely) of VD in most people16,17, even though some studies used total VD, is also permitted to compare the dose used in those studies.

Previous study attributes the controversial findings of available studies to confounding factors3, without noticing the U-shape association. However, if the concentration of VD3 is standard, most of the former studies are consistent with our findings. Felson et al. discovered that in participants who exhibit a mean VD level of 20 ng/ml, roughly equivalent to 52 mmol/L, higher levels of VD were associated with a slight elevation in the risk of osteoarthritis (OA)6. Timothy et al. further noted that when knee OA patients underwent VD treatment, their VD levels increased from 20 ng/ml to 36 ng/ml (52 nmol/L to 93 nmol/L), resulting in a slight intensification of knee pain and a significant decline in knee function7. Both a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials and a systematic review concurred that VD treatment did not confer any beneficial effect on preventing tibial cartilage loss4,18, but these studies primarily focused on the dosage of supplementation without considering baseline and post-treatment levels. Jin et al. reported that vitamin D supplementation failed to show any improvement in tibial cartilage volume or pain for individuals with symptomatic knee OA and low VD levels10. However, the definition of “low VD” was quite broad, ranging from 12.5 to 60 nmol/L, and no subgroup analysis was performed. Laslett et al. significantly observed that a VD3 level below 25 nmol/l predicted a worsening of knee pain over five years9.

Mechanistically, at low VD₃ levels, there is insufficient vitamin D to maintain bone health, calcium balance, and muscle strength19,20. This can lead to weaker bones and decreased bone density, which are key factors in the development of OA21. The reduced bone and muscle strength can result in increased wear and tear on joints, ultimately leading to OA progression22,23. It has been observed that Vitamin D deficiency expedites the development of age-related osteoarthritis in mice, while simultaneously exhibiting a protective effect against articular cartilage erosion in rats24,25. On the other hand, excessive VD₃ levels may also increase OA risk through several plausible mechanisms. High doses of VD₃ can enhance intestinal calcium absorption, leading to hypercalcemia26,27, which has been associated with ectopic calcification of soft tissues, including cartilage and synovium. Such calcification can reduce cartilage elasticity and contribute to joint damage. Excessive VD₃ may also affect cartilage metabolism by altering the balance between synthesis and degradation28. Studies have shown that high concentrations of 1,25(OH)₂D₃ can upregulate MMP-13 in chondrocytes via the p38 MAPK pathway, promoting degradation of type II collagen and aggrecan. These catabolic effects may accelerate cartilage matrix loss. Additionally, both low and high VD₃ levels may influence immune regulation29–31, which plays a crucial role in OA pathogenesis32–34. While physiological VD₃ is generally anti-inflammatory, high-dose supplementation has been associated with increased IL-6 and TNF-α in certain populations, potentially promoting chronic joint inflammation35. In summary, low VD₃ levels impair bone and muscle support, while excessive levels may promote inflammation, matrix degradation, and tissue calcification. Together, these findings support a U-shaped relationship between serum VD₃ and OA risk.

The overall health effect of VD has been reported extensively. Vitamin D plays a pivotal role in maintaining overall health and well-being, with numerous benefits spanning from bone health to immune function36. Beyond bone health, vitamin D also contributes to immune function31. Studies have shown that individuals with sufficient vitamin D levels tend to have stronger immune responses, making them less vulnerable to infections29. Moreover, it plays a role in regulating insulin levels and improving glucose tolerance, which can help prevent or manage diabetes37,38. It has also been associated with reduced risks of heart disease, hypertension, and stroke, likely due to its positive effects on blood vessel health and blood pressure39,40. In addition, some research suggests that it may play a role in preventing depression and improving mood41,42. From bone health to immune function, diabetes management, heart health, mental well-being, and muscle strength, vitamin D plays an essential role in maintaining overall health and preventing chronic diseases.

In detail, serum VD3 levels present a simple linear positive association with LE8 while U-shape association with risk of OA. For OA susceptible people(excessively high BMI, advanced age, joint trauma, menopausal women, and so on), maintaining a not moderate VD3 level is recommended. For diagnosed OA patients, too high of VD3 may accelerate the process of OA(sharing mutual potential mechanism with OA occurrence), which is also needed to maintain a moderate level of VD3. Considering the cutoff of the U-shape association between VD3 and risk of OA is 41nmol/L and the second quartering(Q2:41.6–59 nmol/L) of VD3 is not associated with a higher risk of OA than Q1 (3.05–41.6 nmol/L), 59 nmol/L is a safe upper limit, even it has been reported a safe VD concentration of 140 nmol/L43. Therefore, When the serum VD3 is below 59 nmol/L, higher levels are generally considered to be more beneficial.

In summary, our study found a new U-shape association between VD3 and the risk of OA, and the overall health effects of VD3 in both the general and OA populations. In the future, when perspective will be designed, our study provided a referenced limit of VD3(59nmol/L). Clinically, we provided a thinking of overall consideration of the risk of OA and the aimed levels of VD3 to obtain the most beneficial.

Despite the strengths of this study, including a large sample size and rigorous methods, several limitations should be noted. First, 67.1% of participants were excluded due to missing data (with consistent results observed when the exclusion rate was reduced to 53.4% by retaining LE8 and alcohol variables), which may limit generalizability, especially for lower-income or less-educated groups. Most missingness was structural (e.g., survey design), and all included data were complete and reliable. Comparisons of weighted baseline characteristics (Supplementary Tables S11–S13) showed that excluding participants with missing OA status shifted the sample toward older (37.9 → 46.6 years), heavier (27.0 → 29.0 kg/m²), and higher-income individuals (PIR 2.84 → 2.99), with minimal further change after other exclusions (47.1 years; 29.0 kg/m²; PIR 3.11). Thus, younger, lower-BMI, and lower-income groups may be underrepresented, with part of this shift reflecting the removal of participants with lower BMI. Given that higher BMI is an established OA risk factor, this change does not move the sample away from higher-risk groups. Moreover, because the excluded population was predominantly younger and OA is relatively uncommon at these ages, the impact on external validity is likely limited. This interpretation is supported by sensitivity analyses restricted to participants aged ≥ 40 years, which produced results consistent with the main findings. Subgroup analyses by income and broader inclusion criteria (Supplementary Tables S5–S10) likewise yielded consistent VD₃–OA associations, though generalization should still be made with caution. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and may involve residual confounding. Third, OA diagnosis was self-reported without joint-specific or imaging-based classification, which may limit clinical specificity. Lastly, although sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings, prospective studies are needed to validate the optimal VD₃ range for OA prevention and overall health.

Conclusions

Our findings reveal a U-shaped association between VD3 levels and the risk of osteoarthritis (OA), while a positive association between VD3 and LE8 score. Based on these observations, we recommend maintaining moderate serum VD3 levels, for both OA-susceptible individuals and patients to optimize its overall health benefits.
