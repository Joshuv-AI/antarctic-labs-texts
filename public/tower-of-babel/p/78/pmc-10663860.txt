Energy and exergy analyses of CCHP (combined cooling, heating and power) system based on co-firing of biogas and syngas produced from biomass
Authors: Penlap Landry Hervé, Tiendjou Tiombou Michael, Ngohe-Ekam Paul Salomon, Kenfack Joseph, Mbounguen Kenang Raphael, Nganhou Jean
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
This paper focuses on a CCHP (Combined Cooling, Heating and Power) system based on co-firing in an Internal Combustion Engine (ICE) of biogas from anaerobic digestion and syngas produced by biomass gasification. From an energy perspective, in order for the mixture to make sense, a relationship setting the threshold percentage of methane in the biogas has been established. Gasification and Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC) models developed in Aspen Plus software and thermodynamic modeling of the Internal Combustion Engine (ICE) have been validated by comparison with experimental work conducted by other authors. The results show a decrease in energy efficiency with an increase in the percentage of methane in biogas and the mass ratio of the mixture. For extraction rates of 80 % and 90 %, respectively, exergy efficiency increases with an increase in the percentage of methane in biogas and the mass ratio of the mixture. Additionally, an increase in gasification temperature improves the efficiencies, while an increase in biogas temperature reduces them. The ICE is a significant source of exergy destruction.

Introduction

Climate issues have a strong impact on energy-related issues. It is therefore envisaged to accentuate the deployment towards renewable energies, but also to improve the efficiency of energy systems [1]. In terms of renewable energies, particular emphasis is placed on biomass, and more specifically on certain biofuels such as biogas and syngas [2]. As far as energy systems are concerned, CCHP systems (combined cooling heating and power) are considered viable worldwide [3].

In this context, several studies have been conducted on CCHP systems based on biogas or syngas [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Other studies have explored CCHP systems based on the combination of these biofuels with other renewable resources [8,9] or fossil resources [10,11]. Bosheng et al. [8] conducted a study on optimizing the performance of a new CCHP system based on the synthetic utilization of biogas and solar energy. They obtained optimal engine capacity and hydraulic retention time of 1052 kW and 260.2 h, respectively. Jiangjiang et al. [9] proposed a hybrid CCHP system powered by biomass and solar energy. They found, through a complementarity analysis, that the biomass gasification subsystem had a greater contribution to the primary energy ratio and system efficiency. However, they emphasized that the solar subsystem was more conducive to reducing system emissions. Mohammad et al. [10] recently presented exergetic and thermo-economic evaluations of a CCHP system based on co-firing of biomass and natural gas. Their results showed that when the natural gas to syngas ratio was increased from 0.5 to 1, the net work output of the system increased by 90.77 %, while the modified ecological coefficient of system performance increased by 9.49 %. Xiaofeng et al. [11] reported exergy and exergoeconomic evaluations of a CCHP system based on co-firing of syngas and natural gas. They concluded that the introduction of natural gas improves the exergy efficiency of the system. Specifically, they noted that when the mass ratio of gas varies from 0 to 1, the unit exergy cost of electricity produced by gas turbine, chilled water, and hot water decreases from 11.26 $/GJ, 92.21$/GJ and 69.92 $/GJ to 3.84 $/GJ, 4.52 $/GJ and 23.73 $/GJ, respectively.

However, our investigation reveals that there is no work on CCHP systems based on the co-firing of biogas and syngas in an Internal Combustion Engine (ICE). Nevertheless, studies and experiments on the co-combustion of these biofuels can be found [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. These authors have focused more on the phenomenology (flame temperature, speed, ignition time, etc.) of the combustion of this mixture. However, Kan et al. [2] were able to demonstrate the performance of an engine fueled by a mixture of biogas and syngas. They noted that the use of syngas and biogas in mixed mode cannot maintain the energy performance of the engine observed in single-fuel mode, but they also emphasize that this mixed mode usage allows for a reduction in NOx emissions and lessening the tendency of knock onset.

The novelty of this study lies in the proposal of an innovative CCHP system based on the co-firing of biogas and syngas in an ICE integrated with an Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC). The originality of this study is first reinforced by taking into account the energy losses due to the partial extraction of biogas. The extraction rate is chosen as the parameter characterising this energy loss. Indeed, to our knowledge, in studies dealing with the mixture of biogas and syngas, neither the impact of the extraction rate nor the effects of gasification temperature and biogas temperature on the performance of a CCHP system have been studied yet. Subsequently, a new analysis approach is presented. This approach consists of exploring the combination of the influence of the extraction rate with the effects of classical parameters such as the percentage of methane in biogas and the mass ratio of the biogas and syngas mixture. The objectives of this study are to:-Develop and validate the models of key processes in the proposed CCHP system,-Perform a sensitivity analysis to study the effects of methane percentage in biogas and mass ratio of biogas and syngas mixture on system performance,-Illustrate the influence of gasification temperature and biogas temperature.-Explore the combined effects of biogas extraction rate, methane percentage in biogas, and mass ratio of biogas and syngas mixture.

System description

The designed CCHP system is presented in Fig. 1 below. Solid biomass (stream 1) is air gasified in a downdraft gasifier. This step allows the production of a high-temperature gas (700 °C). The obtained high-temperature gas (stream 2) is slightly cooled first through the heat exchanger (HX01), which allows further heating of the water from the ORC condensation circuit (stream 21). Subsequently, this gas (stream 3), still at a high temperature (520 °C), drives a double-effect LiBr–H2O absorption chiller (CHILLER). As a result, chilled water (stream 7) is produced. The cooled gas (stream 4) is then directed to syngas conditioning unit (SCU) for tar removal.Fig. 1CCHP system based on the co-firing of biogas and syngas.Fig. 1

CCHP system based on the co-firing of biogas and syngas.

The biogas from digesters or landfills is moderately warm. This biogas contains saturated water vapor. It must therefore first be cooled to low temperatures (close to 5 °C) to condense the water vapor. This process requires a chilled water system. Thus, the landfill or digester biogas (stream 11) will be cooled through the heat exchanger (HX04) using a portion of the previously produced chilled water (stream 9). After the removal of water from the biogas, it (stream 12) will be directed to biogas conditioning unit (BCU) to eliminate all other compounds except methane and carbon dioxide. At BCU, the biogas will be heated to meet the purification device requirements. This heating is done using hot water (stream 28) from the cooling of the syngas.

Finally, the two gases will be mixed (stream 5 and stream 14) at the mixer (MIXER) with a variable mass ratio to generate electrical energy through the ICE. The engine cooling water (stream 17) will constitute a second source of hot water. The exhaust gases (stream 19) will drive the ORC for a second electricity production. The condensation circuit of the ORC will allow preheating of the water (stream 26).

Biomass gasification

Aspen Plus model

The numerical modeling of gasification is performed using the Aspen Plus software. The property method selected in the software is the Redlich-Kwong-Soave equation of state with Boston Mathias modifications (RKS-BM). As we utilize gases, conventional inert solids, and unconventional solid materials as feedstocks, the Aspen Plus stream MIXCINC class was employed.

The following assumptions were made for the simulation of our model:➢The model is in steady-state; it is without kinetics; and the gasification process is isothermal with reactions that reach chemical equilibrium [[24], [25], [26]].➢Tars and heavy hydrocarbons are not taken into account and all sulfur (S) is converted by H2S [27]. The pressure in the system is considered constant and equal to 1 bar [28].

Indeed, biomass is considered an unconventional solid in ASPEN PLUS. We subdivide the gasifier into two reactors, namely the DECOMP reactor of the ‘RYield’ type according to Aspen Plus and the GASIF reactor of the 'RGibbs' type according to Aspen Plus. The DECOMP reactor is responsible for decomposing the biomass (BIOMASS flow) into conventional elements such as C, O, N, H, S, vapor (H2O), and ash. This decomposition was done using a Fortran program that we introduced into the CALCULATOR block of the software. This program specifies the yield distribution based on the biomass composition. The decomposed components exiting the “DECOMP” reactor via the “BIOMASS2″ stream are mixed with air for gasification. Subsequently, gasification and partial oxidation are modeled in the same “RGibbs” reactor. This reactor is a rigorous reactor for multiphase chemical equilibrium based on the minimization of Gibbs free energy in order to calculate the composition of the syngas. The separation unit (SEP) isolates the syngas (SYNGAS1 stream) from the ashes and water vapor that will form the (VASH) stream. This results in a dry and ash-free syngas (SYNGAS2 stream). Heat exchangers

Our work does not include an economic evaluation. Therefore, sizing of heat exchangers (heat exchange surface) is not relevant. This allows us to model each heat exchanger with two HEATER blocks in Aspen Plus. We name the hot and cold sides of the exchanges HEATER and COOLER, respectively. These two blocks are connected by a heat flow. In fact, we assume that the heat lost by the hot fluid is entirely received by the cold fluid. Only one of the outlet temperatures can be imposed. Model validation

To validate the ORC model, we use a temperature of 438 K and a pressure of 30 bar for subcritical operation, as experimentally done by Hüseyin et al. [34]. We obtained a net power of 44.06 kJ/kg against 44.04 kJ/kg for him. This represents a relative deviation of 0.05 %. This allows us to conclude a very good validation of the ORC model presented.

Note: Exergy is the maximum or minimum theoretical useful work that can be obtained when systems interact to reach equilibrium, with heat transfer occurring only with the environment [35]. The total exergy of a system is evaluated based on equation (27). This exergy can be divided into four components [35]:(27)E=EPH+ECH+EKN+EPTwhere EPH, ECH, EKN, and EPT are the physical, chemical, kinetic, and potential exergies of the system, respectively. The kinetic and potential exergies are generally considered to be zero, and the physical exergy is calculated based on equation (28) [35]:(28)EPH=(H−H0)−T0(S−S0)where H and S respectively denote the enthalpy and entropy of the system at the specified state, and H0 and S0 are the values of the same properties but rather for the environment. The Aspen Plus software allows obtaining the values of physical exergy. The chemical exergy per mole of a gas mixture is calculated according to equation (29) [35]:(29)e‾CH=∑xke‾kCH+R‾T0∑xklnxkwhere xk being the mole fraction of component k in the mixture, e‾kCH the standard chemical exergy of species k, R‾ is the universal gas constant (on a molar basis), and T0 is the temperature of the environment. The evaluation of chemical exergies is performed in an Excel calculation table that we have developed because, to our knowledge, Aspen Plus software does not have the functionality to do so.

The continuation of the study aims to provide a more comprehensive overview of the system's performance. Energy efficiency

Fig. 5(a and b) and Fig .6 (a,b) depict the impacts of methane percentage in biogas and mass ratio of the mixture on energy efficiency. The effects of gasification temperature and biogas temperature are also shown. The biomass extraction rate at the landfill is set at 90 %.Fig. 5Effect of the methane percentage on energy efficiency.Fig. 5Fig. 6Effect of mass mixture ratio on energy efficiency.Fig. 6

Effect of the methane percentage on energy efficiency.

Effect of mass mixture ratio on energy efficiency.

It is observed that the energy efficiency decreases with an increase in methane percentage in biogas and mass ratio of the mixture. According to equations (15) and (20), the mass of syngas used to create the mixture decreases with an increase in methane percentage in biogas and mass ratio of the mixture. This decrease in the mass of syngas will lead to a reduction in the production of hot and cold water. This observation can be explained by the fact that hot and cold water (mostly) are produced by cooling the syngas. On the other hand, an increase in methane percentage and mass ratio of the mixture leads to an increase in electricity production (as seen from equations (9), (10))). However, this increase is not enough to compensate for the cumulative reductions in hot and cold water production. The total energy produced, and therefore the energy efficiency, will decrease with an increase in methane percentage and mass ratio of the mixture.

Similarly, it is also observed that an increase in gasification temperature improves energy efficiency, while an increase in extracted biogas temperature reduces it. This is because the calorific value of the syngas increases with the gasification temperature [26]. However, an increase in the calorific value of the syngas leads to an increase in electricity production (as seen from equation (9)). On the other hand, an increase in biogas temperature results in a reduction in cold water production.

These figures also show the influence of the biogas extraction rate on the exergetic efficiency.Fig. 7Effect of methane percentage on exergy efficiency.Fig. 7Fig. 8Effect of mass mixture ratio on exergy efficiency.Fig. 8

Effect of methane percentage on exergy efficiency.

Effect of mass mixture ratio on exergy efficiency.

It is observed that the extraction rate must be at least 80 % to ensure an increase in exergetic efficiency with an increase in the percentage of methane in biogas. It is also noted that exergetic efficiency decreases with the mass ratio of the mixture when the extraction rate is less than 90 %.

Note that, for a fixed extraction rate, increasing the mass ratio of the mixture and the percentage of methane in biogas increases the exergy loss during extraction. However, even if a high value of the extraction rate reduces the loss, the direction of variation of the loss with the ratio and percentage of methane remains unchanged. Thus, the observation of an increase in exergetic efficiency can be justified by the increase in electricity production (exergetic) seen in the previous section.

In order to deepen our exergy study, we construct a Sankey diagram of exergy flows, the purpose of which is to precisely present exergy flows through the components of the system. For a methane percentage of 50 % in the biogas and a mass mixture ratio of 0.5, the Sankey diagram (Fig. 9) reveals a greater destruction of exergy at the level of the ICE (45 % of exergy), followed by the gasifier and the ORC with 8.4 % and 3 % of exergy destruction, respectively.Fig. 9Sankey diagram of exergy flows (kW) (ratio 0.5 and 50 % CH4).Fig. 9

Conclusion

This work models a new CCHP system that is fueled by a mixture of biogas and syngas. The following observations were made:-The percentage of methane in the biogas and the mass ratio of the biogas and syngas mixture were found to be significant for studying the system's performance.-The gasification temperature and the biogas temperature also affect the system's efficiency.-The biogas extraction rate at discharge was found to be crucial in ensuring the overall exergy efficiency of the system.-The ICE is a major source of exergy destruction.
