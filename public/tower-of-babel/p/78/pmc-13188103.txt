A female-specific role for Slit1 in prenatal stress-induced depression vulnerability
Authors: Kaixuan Xu, Tu Chen, Yang Yang, Weimin Liu, Huan Guo, Zheng Gong, Xinyi Ma, Jiajia Liu, Yushan Lu, Huiping Zhang, Xiaotang Fan, Hui Li, Zhongliang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWomen experience nearly twice the prevalence of depression compared to men and frequently present with comorbid anxiety, with this divergence emerging during adolescence. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying female-specific depression vulnerability remain poorly understood. Here, we found that prenatal stress (PS) induced depressive-like behaviors and selectively downregulated Slit Guidance Ligand 1 (Slit1) expression in the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) of female adolescent offspring rats. Furthermore, viral knockdown of Slit1 in the DG induced anxiety- and depressive-like behavioral phenotypes specifically in female rats. In females, Slit1 deficiency reduced the mean fluorescence intensity of the neural stem cell and immature neuron markers Sox2 and DCX in the DG, decreased postsynaptic Homer1, PSD95, and GluA1 expression without affecting Synaptophysin, and diminished the amplitude of sEPSCs. Significantly, the downstream Slit1 effector SLIT-ROBO Rho GTPase-activating protein 2 (Srgap2) was downregulated specifically in Slit1-deficient females. Together, these results suggest that Slit1 disruption selectively affects DG neuroplasticity in females and may contribute to their heightened vulnerability to PS-related depressive-like behaviors, although further mechanistic studies are required to confirm the causal role of Slit1 in these effects.Keywords: Prenatal stress, Depressive-like behavior, Slit1, Neurogenesis, Excitatory synaptic transmission

Introduction

Prenatal stress (PS) is a major environmental factor that can disrupt fetal brain development and predispose offspring to psychiatric disorders [1]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that PS is associated with increased risk of offspring anxiety and depression, with female offspring showing heightened vulnerability during adolescence [2,3]. Women not only experience nearly double the lifetime prevalence of depression compared to men, but also tend to present with earlier onset, more severe symptoms, and higher rates of comorbid anxiety disorders [4,5]. Despite these well-documented sex differences in terms of prevalence and symptomatology, the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving female-specific susceptibility to stress-related psychopathology remain poorly understood [6]. Understanding how PS shapes neural plasticity in a sex-dependent manner may therefore provide critical insights into the developmental origins of depression risk.

The hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) is particularly sensitive to stress. It plays a critical role in regulating both neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity, two processes strongly implicated in depressive-like behaviors in rodents [7]. Mature granule neurons are the principal cell type within the DG, and chronic stress persistently suppresses their generation while altering the structure of the DG [8]. Enhancing the excitability of DG granule neurons exerts significant antidepressant effects [9]. Chronic stress reduces the proliferation of Sox2-positive neural stem cells and the differentiation of DCX-positive immature neurons in the DG, impairing neurogenesis and contributing to depressive-like behaviors [10]. However, the mechanisms linking PS-induced anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors to sex-specific disruption of DG plasticity remain unclear.

Slit Guidance Ligand 1 (Slit1), a secreted axon guidance cue that binds Robo receptors, has emerged as a critical regulator of neuronal connectivity and synaptic function [11]. Evidence indicates a sex-specific role of Slit1 in stress susceptibility, as both female patients suffering from depression and stressed female mice exhibit marked reductions in Slit1 expression, which promotes anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors by modulating the excitability of pyramidal neurons in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) [12]. SLIT-ROBO Rho GTPase-activating protein 2 (Srgap2), a downstream effector of Slit-Robo signaling, plays a pivotal role in neuronal morphogenesis and synaptic development [13]. Through its interaction with postsynaptic scaffolds such as Homer1, Srgap2 facilitates the maturation and stabilization of glutamatergic synapses, which are fundamental to emotional regulation [14]. Therefore, whether Slit1/Srgap2 signaling in the hippocampus contributes to the female-specific vulnerability to depression during adolescence warrants further investigation.

Here, we aimed to investigate the molecular mechanisms through which PS disrupts hippocampal neural plasticity in a sex-dependent manner, ultimately leading to anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors. We propose that Slit1 plays a key role in this process. Specifically, we hypothesize that Slit1 deficiency may be associated with reduced expression of Sox2 and DCX, as well as impaired excitatory synaptic transmission of granule neurons in the hippocampal DG, potentially involving alterations in postsynaptic Srgap2 and Homer1 levels. Our work provides a molecular basis for understanding why adolescent females are more sensitive to stress-related depressive-like behaviors.

Materials and methods

Animals

Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, purchased from the Experimental Animal Centre of Xi'an Jiao Tong University, were housed at 25 °C ± 2 °C on a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 a.m., lights off at 20:00 p.m.) with free access to food and water. Three females (180–220 g) were paired overnight with one male (220–250 g), and successful mating was confirmed by the presence of sperm in vaginal smears (marking gestation day zero), after which pregnant rats were housed individually. All procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethical Committee of Northwest University and were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Center for Humane Treatment of Animals in Xi'an, China.

Prenatal stress model

Pregnant rats were randomly assigned to control (CON) or PS groups. From gestational days 14–20, PS rats were subjected to 45-min restraint sessions three times daily (08:00–11:00, 12:00–15:00, 16:00–19:00) in an adjustable plastic cylinder (6 cm diameter) with air holes [15,16]. Control rats remained undisturbed. After birth, offspring were kept with their mothers until weaning on postnatal day 21. To reduce the “litter effect” [17], animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, with no more than two pups per litter and nine rats per group used for testing.

Behavioural tests

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

The SPT was performed 35 days after birth. Anhedonia was assessed by offering rats a 1% sucrose solution and water for 24 h to acclimate. After 4 h of water deprivation, rats were given access to both bottles, with their positions swapped periodically. Sucrose and water consumption over 10 h were recorded, and the sucrose preference was calculated as: Sucrose preference = [sucrose consumption/(sucrose consumption + water consumption)] × 100%.

Open field test (OFT)

The OFT was performed 37 days after birth. Anxiety and locomotor activity were assessed in a 100 × 100 × 50 cm arena. Each rat was allowed 6 min to explore the space, during which we recorded the number of center grid crossings, time spent in the center, and total distance traveled. The arena was cleaned with 75% ethanol after each test to eliminate odor contamination. Anxiety-like behavior in rats was assessed by measuring the number of center entries and the distance traveled in the central zone, while total locomotor distance was used to exclude locomotor confounds.

Forced swimming test (FST)

The FST was performed 38 days after birth. Animals were subjected to a 15-min pre-exposure to the water-filled cylinder (60 cm height, 20 cm diameter; 40 cm water depth, 25 ± 1 °C) 24 h prior to the test. Immobility was interpreted as a passive coping strategy, whereas swimming and climbing were considered active coping behaviors. The 6-min test session was conducted under identical conditions, during which immobility time (the period of floating without active movements except to keep the head above water) was recorded. The water was changed between trials.

All behavioral tests were conducted between 09:00 and 16:00 during the light phase to minimize circadian variability.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum 17β-estradiol (E2) levels were determined using a competitive ELISA kit (ABclonal; RK00651) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, serum samples and standards were added to microplates pre-coated with E2-specific antibodies, followed by incubation with biotin-conjugated antigen. After washing, streptavidin-HRP was applied and the signal was developed using TMB substrate. The reaction was stopped with stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader with wavelength correction at 570 nm. Hormone concentrations were calculated from the standard curve.

RNA preparation and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA from the rat hippocampal DG was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596–026) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the EasyScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (TransGen Biotech, AE311-03). qRT-PCR was performed using the PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, TG-AQ601-02) on a Photocycler 96 system (Roche) with GAPDH as an internal control, as previously described [18]. The reaction was carried out under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, followed by 42 cycles of 5 s at 94 °C and 30 s at 60 °C. Primers were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool with the following parameters: melting temperature (Tm) of 60 °C and amplicon size ranging from 100 to 200 bp. Primer sequences are in Table 1. Relative RNA expression was calculated using the 2ˆ-ΔΔCt method.

Virus expression vector construction

Slit1 cDNA (NM_022953.2) was obtained from Genechem's cDNA library using the following specific primers:

Forward: 5′-GAGGATCCCCGGGTACCGGTCGCCACCATGGCGCTAACGCCCCAGCGCGGGTCC-3'

Reverse: 5′-TCCTTGTAGTCCATACCGGTCGCACACGGGGCACAGCCGCACTTGGTG-3'. The adeno-associated virus (AAV) virus titer was 4 × 108 TU/mL. AAV vectors expressing shRNA targeting Slit1 (GGATAGACCTGAGTAACAACC) and a negative control (GCTGAGTACTTCGAAATGTC) were cloned into the GV478 vector. Recombinant vectors were confirmed by DNA sequencing, with Slit1 inhibitor virus titers at 1.43 × 1013 v.g./mL. Virus titers were confirmed by FACS analysis of GFP-positive 293T cells and stored at −80 °C.

Stereotaxic injection of virus

Stereotaxic surgery was performed to deliver AAV vectors into the DG of the rat hippocampus. The rats were anesthetized using isoflurane and secured in a stereotaxic frame (Stoelting) placed on a heating pad maintained at 37 °C. Following scalp disinfection, a small craniotomy was performed to expose the target area. A Hamilton syringe (5 μL) was used to inject viral solutions (Slit1 knockdown vectors) at 0.5 μL/min. The injection coordinates were set at −3.6 mm anteroposterior, ±2.2 mm mediolateral, and −3.5 mm dorsoventral relative to the bregma. To ensure adequate diffusion of the viral particles, the syringe was held in place for an additional 5 min following the injection before being carefully retracted.

Immunofluorescence assay

Following behavioral testing, rats were anesthetized and perfused with 4% PFA. Brains were fixed overnight in 4% PFA, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose for 48 h, and sectioned into 30 μm coronal slices. Fifteen slices were taken per rat. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-Sox2, Proteintech, 11064-1-AP, 1:200; and anti-DCX, Proteintech, 13925-1-AP, 1:200), followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, RGAR004, 1:500) and an anti-fluorescence quenching agent containing DAPI (Beyotime, P0131). Images were captured using a Leica TCS SP2 AOBS confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Mannheim, Germany). Gain settings (500–800) were adjusted only during the initial setup to ensure signals fell within the dynamic range and to avoid saturation. For quantitative analysis, all images were acquired under identical imaging parameters within each experiment, and comparisons were restricted to groups imaged under the same conditions. Fluorescence intensity was quantified within a manually defined DG ROI using ImageJ, with background subtraction performed for each section. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) values were normalized to the ROI area to account for variability in section size, as previously described [19]. This immunofluorescence protocol, which is commonly used in neurobiological research, has been shown to be reproducible and effective for analyzing brain regions [[20], [21], [22]].

Western blot analysis

Protein from the DG was extracted using RIPA buffer and separated by SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, then incubated with primary antibodies for Slit1 (Santa Cruz, sc-376756, 1:200), PSD95 (Abcam, ab238135, 1:2000), Synaptophysin (Abcam, ab32127, 1:5000), Homer1 (Proteintech, 12433-1-AP, 1:2000), GluA1(Abcam, ab31232, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Proteintech, 10494-1-AP, 1:1000). After incubation with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies (Proteintech, RGAR001, 1:5000), protein bands were visualized with an ECL kit (Beyotime). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Patch-clamp electrophysiology

Hippocampal slices (300 μm) were prepared from ethyl carbamate-anesthetized rats using an ice-cold, oxygenated slicing solution (NMDG-HEPES ACSF) and then incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in oxygenated HEPES holding ACSF. After incubation, the slices were maintained at room temperature. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were made on visually identified DG granule neurons using patch pipettes (3–6 MΩ) filled with an internal solution. After establishing a giga-ohm seal (≥1 GΩ), the neurons were voltage-clamped at −70 mV. For spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic current (sEPSC) recordings, 5 mM picrotoxin was added to block GABA_A receptor currents. The slices were perfused with oxygenated recording ACSF at a rate of 2 mL/min, maintained at 35 °C. Please refer to Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5 for the components and molarity of the relevant solutions. Data were recorded for 10 min, filtered at 2 kHz, digitized at 10 kHz, and analyzed with MiniAnalysis software (Synaptosoft).

Procedure

Behavioral testing, image acquisition, and data quantification were conducted in a manner. Experimenters performing the behavioral tests were unaware of the treatment groups. Brain sections and protein samples were coded prior to analysis, and all image quantification (including immunofluorescence intensity measurements and Western blot band density analysis) was performed using anonymized files. Group allocation was disclosed only after completion of all analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. For datasets meeting the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, a two-way ANOVA was used for experiments with a 2 × 2 design. When a significant interaction was detected, Tukey's test was applied for post hoc multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Slit1 is selectively downregulated in the DG of PS female offspring

Pregnant rats were exposed to seven days of restraint stress during late gestation. Their offspring were evaluated for emotional behaviors at postnatal day 35 (Fig. 1A). In the OFT, there was no significant interaction between PS and sex for central crossings (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 32)= 0.2524, p = 0.6188), however, a main effect of PS exposure was observed (Two-way ANOVA, F PS (1, 32)= 55.54, p < 0.0001), indicating reduced central crossings in PS-exposed rats. Detailed statistical results for all analyses are provided in Table 6. For the distance traveled in the central zone, a significant PS × sex interaction was observed (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 32)= 26.78, p < 0.0001). Post hoc Tukey's test showed that PS significantly decreased center distance in both females (CON/Female vs. PS/Female, p < 0.0001) and males (CON/Male vs. PS/Male, p = 0.0480). No difference was observed between CON/Female and CON/Male (p = 0.9617), whereas PS/Female exhibited a lower center distance than PS/Male (p < 0.0001). Total distance traveled was unaffected by either sex or PS, excluding locomotor effects (Fig. 1B). In the SPT, there was no significant interaction between PS and sex for sucrose intake (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 32)= 0.2194, p = 0.6437), however, a main effect of PS exposure was observed (Two-way ANOVA, F PS (1, 32)= 48.13, p < 0.0001), indicating reduced sucrose intake following PS in both sexes (Fig. 1C). In the FST, no significant PS × sex interaction was detected for immobility time (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 32)= 1.069, p = 0.3088), whereas a main effect of PS was observed (Two-way ANOVA, F PS (1, 32)= 112.2, p < 0.0001), indicating increased immobility time following PS in both females and males (Fig. 1D). Collectively, these results indicate that PS induces anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors in adolescent offspring of both sexes, although sex-dependent differences were observed in specific behavioral parameters.

Two-way ANOVA statistical results.

To determine whether PS affects circulating estrogen levels in adolescent offspring, we measured serum 17β-estradiol (E2). Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant PS × sex interaction for circulating E2 levels (F interaction (1, 32)= 14.45, p = 0.0006). Post-hoc Tukey's test showed that PS significantly reduced serum E2 levels in females compared with the control group (p < 0.0001), whereas no significant change was observed in males (p = 0.9984). In addition, CON/Female exhibited higher E2 levels than CON/Male (p < 0.0001), whereas no significant difference was detected between PS/Female and PS/Male (p = 0.6673) (Fig. S1). These results suggest that circulating E2 levels are selectively reduced in female offspring following PS exposure.

We next examined Slit1 expression in the hippocampal DG. qPCR data revealed a significant sex × PS interaction for Slit1 mRNA levels (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 20)= 9.58, p = 0.0057). Post-hoc Tukey's test showed that Slit1 expression was significantly reduced in PS females (CON/Female vs. PS/Female, p = 0.0002), whereas, no significant change was observed in males (CON/Male vs. PS/Male, p = 0.8026). No difference was detected between CON/Female and CON/Male (p = 0.9855), whereas PS/Female showed lower Slit1 expression than PS/Male (p = 0.0007) (Fig. 1E). Western blotting analysis confirmed a sex × PS interaction for Slit1 protein expression (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 8)= 5.823, p = 0.0423). Post hoc Tukey's test showed that PS significantly reduced Slit1 protein levels in females (CON/Female vs. PS/Female, p = 0.0193), but not in males (CON/Male vs. PS/Male, p = 0.9617). No difference was observed between CON/Female and CON/Male (p = 0.9864), and PS/Female tended to show lower Slit1 levels than PS/Male (p = 0.0594) (Fig. 1F). Together, these findings demonstrate that PS selectively downregulates Slit1 expression in the DG of female offspring.

PS disturbs neurogenesis and excitatory synaptic transmission in the adolescent DG

To assess the impact of PS on hippocampal neurogenesis, we examined key cellular stages, from Sox2-positive neural stem cells to DCX-positive immature neurons [23]. Immunofluorescence analysis of MFI showed no significant sex × PS interaction for either DCX (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 20)= 0.0757, p = 0.7861) or Sox2 (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 20)= 0.4077, p = 0.5304). However, PS exerted a significant main effect on both DCX (Two-way ANOVA, F PS (1, 20)= 55.34, p < 0.0001) and Sox2 (Two-way ANOVA, F PS (1, 20)= 19.01, p = 0.0003), indicating reduced DCX and Sox2 MFI in the DG (Fig. 2A and B). These results indicate a marked reduction in neural stem cells and immature neurons in the DG of both male and female adolescent rats following PS exposure.

Knockdown of Slit1 in the DG induces anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors in females

To test whether Slit1 deficiency mimics PS phenotypes, we administered bilateral injections of AAV vectors carrying Slit1 shRNA into the DG of adolescent rats (Fig. 4A). Three weeks post-injection, GFP fluorescence confirmed efficient viral transduction (Fig. 4B; Fig. S3A and B). Two-way ANOVA revealed no significant sex × Slit1 knockdown interaction for either Slit1 mRNA (F interaction (1, 20)= 0.3022, p = 0.5886) or protein expression (F interaction (1, 8)= 0.0031, p = 0.9567). However, a main effect of Slit1 knockdown was observed, with significantly reduced Slit1 mRNA (F AAV (1, 20)= 124.0, p < 0.0001) and protein levels (F AAV (1, 8)= 60.83, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4C and D). In the OFT, there was no significant sex × Slit1 knockdown interaction for central crossings (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 32)= 1.194, p = 0.2827). However, main effects of sex (Two-way ANOVA, F sex (1, 32)= 7.893, p = 0.0084) and Slit1 knockdown (Two-way ANOVA, F AAV (1, 32)= 9.264, p = 0.0046) were observed, with females exhibiting fewer central crossings than males and Slit1 knockdown reducing central crossings overall. For center distance, a significant sex × Slit1 knockdown interaction was detected (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 32)= 25.92, p < 0.0001). Post hoc Tukey's test showed that Slit1 knockdown significantly reduced center distance in females (p < 0.0001) but not in males (p = 0.9588). No difference was observed between AAV-sh-Scr/Female and AAV-sh-Scr/Male (p = 0.4888), whereas AAV-sh-Slit1/Female showed a lower center distance than AAV-sh-Slit1/Male (p < 0.0001). Total distance traveled remained unchanged, excluding locomotor confounds (Fig. 4E). In the SPT, sucrose consumption showed a significant sex × Slit1 knockdown interaction (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 32)= 25.03, p < 0.0001). Tukey's test showed that Slit1 knockdown significantly decreased sucrose consumption in females (p < 0.0001) but not in males (p = 0.9094). No difference was observed between control females and males (p = 0.9111), whereas AAV-sh-Slit1 females exhibited lower sucrose consumption than AAV-sh-Slit1 males (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4F). In the FST, immobility time showed a significant sex × Slit1 knockdown interaction (Two-way ANOVA, F interaction (1, 32)= 27.86, p < 0.0001). Post hoc Tukey's test showed that Slit1 knockdown significantly increased immobility time in females (p < 0.0001) but not in males (p = 0.3893). No difference was observed between control females and males (p = 0.9993), whereas AAV-sh-Slit1 females displayed higher immobility than AAV-sh-Slit1 males (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4G). These results demonstrate that Slit1 knockdown induces anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors in a female-specific manner.

Discussion

Epidemiological studies show that women are nearly twice as likely as men to develop depression, with this gender difference first becoming apparent during adolescence [24]. The present study identifies a sex-specific molecular alteration associated with PS exposure and anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors during female adolescence. Our findings show that PS impairs neurogenesis and excitatory synaptic transmission in the hippocampal DG, accompanied by a female-specific downregulation of Slit1 expression. Slit1 deficiency was also associated with reduced Srgap2, Homer1, PSD95 and GluA1expression in females. These synaptic proteins may represent components of a candidate signaling network that could be involved in linking extracellular guidance cues with postsynaptic structural organization. Although our data do not establish a definitive causal cascade, the coordinated molecular changes are consistent with altered DG neuroplasticity and may be related to sex-biased behavioral vulnerability following early-life stress.

Slit1 is significantly downregulated in the vmPFC of female MDD patients, but not in males. This downregulation is associated with increased stress-behavioral sensitivity in a sex-specific manner in female mice [12]. Therefore, investigating Slit1 provides a molecular entry point to understand its specific downregulation in adolescent females exposed to PS. Our study extends these findings by identifying the hippocampal DG as a critical site for the sex-specific downregulation of Slit1 following PS. While previous work has emphasized its role in modulating the excitability of prefrontal pyramidal neurons [12], our findings suggest that a deficiency of Slit1 in the hippocampal DG may contribute to the phenotypes of anxiety and depression in female offspring exposed to PS, potentially by disturbing neurogenesis and synaptic transmission of granule neurons.

Previous studies conducted in adult rodents have demonstrated that chronic stress or elevated corticosterone exposure disturbs neurogenesis in the DG, with varying effects depending on sex. Males exhibit reductions across both dorsal and ventral DG regions, whereas females show selective vulnerability in the ventral DG [25]. In middle-aged mice, stress has been found to decrease the proliferation and number of neuroblasts in the female DG, whereas the male DG appears more resilient [26]. In contrast, adult rats exposed to stress demonstrate decreased cell proliferation and an increased stem cell quiescence in males. Females, on the other hand, maintain cell proliferation but suffer from impaired survival of immature neurons in the hippocampal DG [27]. Furthermore, suppression of neuroplasticity during adolescence, particularly when followed by stress exposure, can increase the incidence of behavioral pathologies in adulthood [28]. In pre-pubertal stress paradigms, reduced cell proliferation in the ventral DG has been observed only in males, accompanied by increased survival of newly generated cells [29]. These findings suggest sex-specific mechanisms underpinning DG plasticity, which may contribute to the heightened vulnerability of females to anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors during adolescence. Although we did not assess behavioral outcomes in adulthood in the present study, previous literature demonstrates that PS can produce long-lasting alterations in hippocampal structure, neurogenesis, HPA-axis regulation, and affective behaviors that persist into adulthood [30]. Suppression of neurogenesis in adolescent rats has been shown to increase vulnerability to mood-related phenotypes later in life [31]. Longitudinal follow-up studies will be necessary to establish the developmental trajectory and persistence of Slit1-associated changes. Although anxiety-like behavior in the OFT was primarily assessed using center entries and distance traveled in the center, additional parameters such as time spent in the outer zone, thigmotaxis index, and rearing behavior were not quantified in the present study. Inclusion of these complementary measures in future work would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of anxiety-like and exploratory behaviors.

Previous studies have demonstrated that stress or PS inhibits DG neurogenesis and dampens excitatory transmission, correlating with depressive-like behaviors [10,32,33]. Our findings are consistent with these studies but introduce a critical sex-dependent aspect by revealing that PS selectively downregulated Slit1 in the female DG, thereby disturbing neuroplasticity. In contrast to broader observations made in the mPFC [12,34,35], our study focuses specifically on the DG and highlights mechanisms driven by Slit1. The downregulation of Homer1 and excitatory postsynaptic markers such as PSD95 and GluA1 in Slit1-knockdown rats aligns with previous studies showing that disruption of Homer1 scaffold function disturbs synaptic plasticity and increases susceptibility to anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors induced by PS [36,37]. PSD95 is a core postsynaptic density protein essential for the organization of glutamate receptor complexes [38], while GluA1 serves as a critical subunit of AMPA receptors, mediating excitatory synaptic transmission [39]. Alterations in their expression levels are well-established hallmarks of compromised synaptic integrity and plasticity, which are strongly implicated in the pathophysiology of mood disorders [40,41]. Homer1 is tightly linked to postsynaptic signaling, serving as a platform that connects mGluR and AMPAR complexes. Its modulation has been implicated in stress-related behavioral outcomes [42]. In the present study, we observed that Slit1 knockdown suppressed excitatory synaptic transmission and reduced the amplitude of sEPSCs in DG granule neurons in a sex-dependent manner. We speculate that these changes in excitatory synaptic function stem from Homer1 downregulation in the DG, which reduces the formation of PSD95-GluA1 complexes at excitatory postsynaptic sites, consequently contributing to anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors in adolescent female rats.

Interestingly, the absence of a significant sex × treatment interaction for PSD95 and GluA1 protein levels contrasts with the sex-specific effects observed at the mRNA level and in electrophysiological recordings. Post-transcriptional regulatory processes, including mRNA stability, microRNA-mediated repression, and translational control, can buffer fluctuations in transcript levels and maintain relatively stable protein expression [43,44]. Moreover, PSD95 and GluA1 are highly dynamic synaptic proteins whose trafficking and turnover are activity dependent [45,46]. Thus, sex-specific differences in excitatory transmission may reflect altered synaptic localization or receptor composition rather than changes in total protein abundance. Future studies using synaptosomal fractionation or imaging approaches will be needed to determine whether sex-specific synaptic remodeling occurs at the level of protein localization or post-translational regulation.

In the present study, Slit1 knockdown in adolescent female rats markedly reduced the expression of Srgap2 in the DG, a RhoGAP involved in the Slit-Robo pathway. Srgap2 regulates synaptic maturation by interacting with Robo receptors to inhibit Rac1 and Cdc42 activity. Additionally, it promotes the development of excitatory synapses by associating with Homer1 [14]. Previous studies have implicated Srgap2 in cytoskeletal regulation. The coordinated reduction of Srgap2 and Homer1 observed here may reflect broader alterations in synaptic structural organization [47]. Thus, our findings raise the possibility that dysregulation of Slit1-associated signaling may contribute to impaired synaptic function and reduced neuroplasticity in a sex-specific manner in females.

Because Slit1 functions as an axon guidance molecule during neural development, stress-induced alterations may occur earlier than the adolescent stage examined here. Earlier reductions in Slit1 expression, particularly in males, may produce developmental effects that were not captured within the adolescent time window of this study. Thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that PS alters Slit1 expression at earlier developmental stages. Furthermore, behavioral testing was conducted at slightly different phases of adolescence in the PS model and the AAV-Slit1 knockdown cohort, and we cannot exclude the possibility that this difference in developmental timing may have influenced behavioral outcomes in males.

The mechanisms underlying the observed sex differences remain incompletely understood. In the present study, we observed that circulating E2 levels were significantly reduced in the female PS group compared with controls, whereas no significant changes were detected in males, suggesting that estrogen signaling may be differentially affected by PS in a sex-dependent manner. However, as the estrous cycle was not monitored prior to E2 measurement, these differences should be interpreted with caution, as they may partly reflect variations in natural cycling between groups. Estrogen plays important roles in regulating neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and emotional behaviors [48]. However, fluctuations in estrogen levels during puberty have been associated with increased vulnerability to depression in females [49,50]. Thus, alterations in estrogen signaling during this developmental window may contribute to sex-specific susceptibility to stress-related behavioral outcomes. In addition to hormonal influences, females appear more sensitive to stress-induced epigenetic changes, which can alter the expression of genes involved in mood regulation and neuroplasticity [51]. Furthermore, sex differences in transcriptional regulation, particularly in stress-responsive genes, could play a key role. Estrogen and androgen receptors, which are differentially expressed across brain regions, may regulate gene expression in a sex-specific manner, influencing emotional and neuroplastic outcomes following stress exposure [52,53].

Although depression is increasingly recognized as a network-level disorder involving coordinated dysfunction across multiple brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, and ventral hippocampus, our analyses were restricted to the DG. We focused on the DG because it represents a primary site of adult neurogenesis and is particularly sensitive to stress exposure. Moreover, our previous work demonstrated selective DG vulnerability in PS-exposed female offspring [54]. Nevertheless, we did not examine other hippocampal subregions, such as CA1 or CA3, or extrahippocampal structures that may also contribute to the observed behavioral phenotypes [55,56]. Future studies will be necessary to determine whether similar molecular alterations occur across broader limbic circuits.

In summary, our findings suggest that Slit1 represents a female-specific molecular alteration associated with anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors following PS exposure. We found that downregulation of Slit1 in the DG is accompanied by disturbed neurogenesis and impaired excitatory synaptic function. These results provide evidence supporting a potential molecular framework underlying sex-dependent vulnerability to early-life stress.
