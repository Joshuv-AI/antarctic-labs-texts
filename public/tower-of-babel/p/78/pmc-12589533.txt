The effect of probiotic baker’s yeast on egg quality, tibia characteristics, and hepatic lipid peroxidation in laying hens
Authors: Magdalena Mazur-Kuśnirek, Aleksandra Drażbo, Sebastian Wojciech Przemieniecki, Krzysztof Lipiński
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effects of dietary baker’s yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) supplementation on egg and eggshell quality, bone strength, and liver oxidative status in laying hens. A total of 120 Lohmann Brown hens were allocated to five groups (12 replicates, 2 birds per replicate), which received a basal diet (T1) or the same diet supplemented with 50 (T2), 100 (T3), 150 (T4), or 200 (T5) g/t of yeast. The body weight of birds and the feed conversion ratio were monitored. At the end of weeks 1, 5, and 11 of the experiment, 12 eggs from each group were randomly selected for quality assessment (weight, eggshell thickness and strength, yolk and albumen quality: Haugh units, color). Liver function was evaluated based on weight, proximate composition, and malondialdehyde content, while bone characteristics were assessed based on tibia ash content, dimensions, and mechanical strength analysis. In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the analyzed yeast product may positively affect selected indicators of eggshell quality, lipid peroxidation, and mechanical resilience. Improvements were observed at lower inclusion levels (50–100 g/t), indicating economic benefits, as higher doses did not yield additional effects. While the results suggest the potential for practical applications in poultry production, the underlying mechanisms remain somewhat speculative, since parameters such as gut microbiota composition, mineral metabolism, and digestive enzyme activity were not assessed. Therefore, further research on live baker’s yeast preparation is warranted.

Introduction

In recent decades, consumers have expressed mounting concerns regarding the quality and safety of animal products and environmental issues. Therefore, feed additives are used to enhance digestive function in animals and production efficiency by reducing the prevalence of pathogenic bacteria1. Probiotics are defined as “live microorganisms that may beneficially affect the host upon ingestion by improving the balance of the intestinal microflora” and prebiotics as “a non-digestible but fermentable food ingredient that beneficially affects the host by selectively stimulating the growth and/or activity of one or a limited number of bacteria in the colon”, and they have been proposed as alternatives to antibiotics2.

Yeasts are fungi naturally present in the intestinal microbiota. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (SC, known as baker’s or brewer’s yeast) is widely used in food and beverage fermentation and has been approved as safe for animals3. Yeasts have been fed to animals for decades, either in the form of by-products from breweries or commercial products4. As a source of biologically valuable proteins, B vitamins, essential minerals and cell wall components (glucans, glycoproteins, mannans, and chitin), SC has been recognized for its probiotic and prebiotic properties. Its bioactive compounds, such as β-glucans and mannan-oligosaccharides, can improve growth performance, and modulate metabolism5–7.

The majority of studies evaluating live yeast as a probiotic have been conducted in ruminants, horses, swine, poultry, and aquaculture. The addition of live yeast to animal diets may improve fiber digestibility, suppress pathogens, and stimulate the immune response8. In poultry, SC has been shown to improve feed intake, egg production, egg weight, and bone development, and lower serum and yolk cholesterol levels9–13.

The influence of the addition of baker’s yeast (SC) to layer diets on the ash content and breaking strength of the tibia has not been thoroughly investigated in the literature. The research hypothesis states that the inclusion of probiotic baker’s yeast in layer diets improves egg quality and tibia breaking strength. The aim of this study was to determine the effect of dietary supplementation with probiotic baker’s yeast on egg and eggshell quality, bone strength, and oxidative stress-related parameters in the liver of laying hens.

Discussion

Saccharomyces cerevisiae has long been used as a feed additive in poultry nutrition. The addition of probiotic baker’s yeast to poultry diets improves productivity and laying performance, the quality of animal-based products, and the health status of birds14. Baker’s yeast regulates the gut microbiota in poultry and inhibits the colonization of their gastrointestinal tract by pathogenic bacteria13. Probiotic baker’s yeast can promote sustainability in poultry production as an alternative to antibiotics. It may also enhance animal welfare by reducing the risk of fractures through stronger bones and potentially decrease economic losses by lowering the number of damaged eggs. However, research results are inconclusive due to various factors such as the type of yeast, yeast processing method, the amount of yeast added to poultry diets, diet composition, the purpose chickens are raised for (egg production or meat production), and poultry housing and management.

In the present study, yeast viability decreased by an average of 40% after three months of storage; however, this decrease did not affect production performance or egg quality during the 11-week experimental period. The survival of probiotic strains in feed is determined by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including ingredient composition, physicochemical properties, processing methods, handling, and storage conditions15. In order to maintain a sufficient number of viable microorganisms, the storage period should not exceed two months. Longer storage of feed could potentially reduce the efficacy of the yeast product. Developing appropriate technologies and stabilizing strategies to extend the effective shelf life of the SC probiotic would be valuable.

Probiotic baker’s yeast (SC) has been reported to improve egg production and egg weight in laying hens, with beneficial effects observed across a range of inclusion levels, from 1 to 4 g16,17 and from 0.05 to 3%18,19. Baker’s yeast is a rich source of protein, essential amino acids, oligosaccharides, enzymes, minerals such as Mg, K, P, and Zn, and B vitamins13,20. The yeast cell wall, containing peptidoglycans and oligosaccharides, can bind toxins and suppress pathogenic bacteria in the intestine, thereby reducing intestinal damage and potentially improving nutrient absorption, which in turn may contribute to enhanced egg production and feed efficiency21. Previous studies suggest that SC can enhance pancreatic function and stimulate the synthesis of digestive enzymes (chymotrypsin, α-amylase), which could influence nutrient digestibility and absorption, and thus indirectly affect performance22–24. Another possible mechanism proposed in the literature is that SC may improve intestinal health by enhancing intestinal morphology (e.g., increasing villus height to crypt depth ratio), strengthening gut barrier integrity, and positively modulating the composition of the gut microbiota. Such effects could lead to improved nutrient uptake and overall hen health25,26. While these mechanisms have been widely discussed in the literature, they were not directly assessed in the present study. Some researchers found that dietary SC supplementation had no effect on egg production, egg weight or the FCR in laying hens9,27–31. Discrepancies between studies may result from differences in dietary nutrient composition and in the type, inclusion rate, and duration of yeast supplementation14.

In this study, egg freshness (Haugh units) and yolk color intensity increased in response to dietary supplementation of 50 to 200 g/t yeast, significant differences were observed only at 25 weeks of age. As an indicator of egg freshness, Haugh units are important for both consumers and the poultry industry, and yeast supplementation may help preserve this trait during storage. These results corroborate the findings of other authors investigating baker’s yeast18,24. The effects exerted by baker’s yeast can be attributed to more efficient digestion and absorption of nutrients, including carotenoids, natural pigments that enhance egg yolk color due to the activity of SC ingredients such as peptides, amino acids, B vitamins, and oligosaccharides32,33.Yolk color may influence the commercial value of eggs and consumer preference. A typical target yolk color is above 7 on the Roche® color fan scale, and a medium-intensity color is generally considered more desirable in most countries34. The values found in the present study fall within the commercially acceptable range and represent a frequently observed yolk color, as reported in other studies18,35. Moreover, vitamin E, B vitamins, and phytase found in yeast may enhance the bioavailability of certain minerals (Ca, Zn, Cu), oxycarotenoid utilization18,36, and increase eggshell thickness27,37. In the current study, eggshell weight, equatorial thickness (only at 20 weeks of the experiment), and strength increased in response to the lower inclusion levels of yeast (50 and 100 g/t) at 20 and 25 weeks of the experiment. The higher supplementation levels (150–200 g/t) did not lead to significant improvements in eggshell quality traits. These results support the concept that higher inclusion levels of SC in laying hen diets, especially under optimal environmental conditions, may not provide additional benefits6,38. Some studies did not confirm the positive effect of SC on egg and eggshell quality23,39, which may be related to differences in supplementation levels, basal diet composition, hen’s age and breed, environmental stress, or housing conditions14.

The effect of SC on bone strength in laying hens has not been investigated in the literature. Muszyński et al.12 demonstrated that the addition of SC to quail diets had no impact on the biochemical parameters or ash content of bones. In turn, Suzer et al.40 found that the addition of 0.2% SC to broiler diets had a beneficial effect on mineral concentrations in bones as well as their biomechanical characteristics and strength. In other studies, probiotic yeast prevented a decrease in bone mineral content in broilers fed diets deficient in Ca and trace elements10,28. Similar effects on bone strength and eggshell quality have been reported with chelated trace minerals, such as zinc-methionine, which enhance mineral absorption and improve the mechanical properties of the tibia in laying hens41. Furthermore, it has been suggested that SC may stimulate the production of calcitriol receptors, thereby influencing Ca metabolism42. Calcitriol, the active form of vitamin D, increases blood Ca levels and promotes Ca absorption in the intestines by enhancing the expression of Ca transporters and reducing urinary Ca excretion43. The increase in serum Ca levels may help counterbalance bone Ca resorption induced by the activation of osteoclasts44. Although this study demonstrated improved bone width and tibia breaking strength, the underlying mechanisms remain speculative as Ca metabolism and serum mineral concentrations were not directly assessed. However, the present findings indicate that the observed improvement in bone strength was not accompanied by changes in ash content, suggesting that other factors (apart from the mechanisms associated with Ca absorption and mineral deposition) may also contribute to enhancing the mechanical properties of bones. Bone strength is determined not only by mineral content but also by collagen structure and maturation. Minerals influence nanomechanical properties45, while collagen enhances toughness, reduces brittleness, and helps prevent fractures46. The orientation and crosslinking of collagen fibers, shaped by enzymatic and non-enzymatic processes, maintain bone strength even under reduced mineral density47,48. Hormones and cytokines also regulate bone metabolism, growth, and remodeling: the growth hormone promotes cortical bone growth, whereas steroid hormones affect cancellous bone formation49. Additionally, dietary composition and environmental factors, such as fatty acid sources and thermal conditions, can influence bone growth and strength, suggesting that the effects of probiotics might be modulated by diet and rearing conditions50. Although these mechanisms are well documented in the literature, they were not directly evaluated in the present study.

Antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) are responsible for scavenging free radicals and preventing oxidative damage. Normally, the production and elimination of free radicals are balanced, but stressors can increase free radical production, leading to oxidative damage and higher MDA levels33. Malondialdehyde disrupts membrane integrity by cross-linking lipids and proteins, thereby impairing cellular metabolism, causing lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress. In order to counteract these undesirable phenomena, animals produce endogenous antioxidants, including enzymes such as GSH-Px and SOD51. Antioxidant protection involves inhibiting cell proliferation, inducing apoptosis, antioxidant and free radical scavenging activity, gene protection, preventing inflammatory processes and lipid peroxidation, including reduced MDA content in bird tissues35. Research has shown that yeast can enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes and reduce MDA levels in the body, thus improving the overall antioxidant status52. In the current study, MDA levels in hens fed 50–200 g/t yeast-supplemented diets decreased by 30% on average, compared with the control group. In the work of Liu et al.51, the addition of 40 g/kg SC to layer diets contributed to a 28% decrease in MDA levels and an increase in the activity of antioxidant enzymes, SOD and GSH-Px, in the blood serum. Similar results were reported by Nidamanuri et al.53, who observed a reduction in plasma MDA levels in broiler chickens of approximately 20% after seven days of exposure to a stressor, following supplementation with SC at 0.7–1.4 g/kg. Al-Baadani54 also found a decrease in liver MDA levels in birds receiving 0.75 g of either active or inactivated yeast. The above effects are induced by mannooligosaccharides, which support the antioxidant mechanism by eliminating free radicals and improving the structure of intestinal mucosa33,55. However, it should be noted that in the present study, the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD, GSH-Px, and catalase (CAT), were not assessed. Consequently, the antioxidant response cannot be fully characterized. Therefore, future research should include total antioxidant status and the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, alongside MDA. It should be conducted over longer experimental periods and involve older birds to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of oxidative status.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the analyzed yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) product may positively affect selected indicators of eggshell quality, lipid peroxidation, and mechanical resilience. Improvements were observed at lower inclusion levels (50–100 g/t), indication economic benefits, as higher doses did not yield additional effects. While the results suggest the potential for practical applications in poultry production, the underlying mechanisms remain somewhat speculative, since parameters such as gut microbiota composition, mineral metabolism, and digestive enzyme activity were not assessed. Therefore, further research on live baker’s yeast preparation is warranted.

Methods

In compliance with Polish law and the EU Directive (2010/63/EU)56, the experiments conducted in this study did not require approval from the Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experimentation in Olsztyn. All procedures followed the guidelines for animal experimentation and the care of animals included in the study. No procedures involving pain or suffering were carried out, and all analyses were performed on samples collected postmortem. The euthanasia of animals solely for the purpose of using their organs or tissues is not classified as an experimental procedure. The Directive outlines the requirements for the protection of animals used for scientific purposes; thus, these regulations do not apply to agricultural practices or standard animal husbandry. The experiment was conducted under commercial conditions. The study adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines, ensuring ethical standards and compliance with all relevant regulations, with detailed reporting of the methods, experimental design, sampling, and data analysis.

Birds, management and diets

The experimental materials comprised 120 Lohmann Brown laying hens aged 16 weeks, obtained from a commercial flock (Nowogard, Poland). The hens were randomly divided into five groups (12 replicates, 2 birds per replicate). Before the experiment, all birds were weighed individually and were placed in cages. The birds were kept under controlled environmental conditions, with 14 h of light and 10 h of dark. All birds had free access to feed and water. They received the same pre-layer diet up to 19 weeks of age. The experiment lasted for 11 weeks of the laying season (from 20 to 30 weeks of age).

The hens were fed standard complete diets whose nutritional value was adjusted to their requirements (Table 8). The content of apparent metabolizable energy and the concentrations of minerals and amino acids in layer diets were determined according to the Nutrient Requirements of Poultry57. The diets were produced in mash form. Cereal components (wheat, maize) were supplemented with soybean meal, and soybean oil was used as an additional source of energy. Synthetic methionine was used to achieve the desired levels of exogenous amino acids. Sample collection

The nutrient content of diets was determined by the Weende method61. The survival rate of probiotic baker’s yeast (SC) during storage (1, 2, and 3 months) was determined in yeast-supplemented diets. The samples were milled in the Retsch ZM200 electric mill (Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany). Then, 10 g of each sample was dissolved in 90 ml of sterile saline water (NaCl 0.85%) and serial dilutions of 10–1 to 10–12 were prepared. Surface dilutions were prepared from each serial dilution by transferring 100 µL samples to the appropriate microbial media. The experiment was carried out in four replications. Malt extract agar (MEA) was used to cultivate yeast colonies. Yeasts were cultured at 30 °C for 72 h, after which the emerged colonies were counted. The results were expressed as colony-forming units (CFUs). The number of yeast CFUs was also determined during the preparation of probiotic yeast.

Egg production was monitored for 11 weeks, from 20 to 30 weeks of hen’s age. The body weights of hens were determined at the beginning and at the end of the experiment. Eggs were collected three times a week and egg weight was determined at four-week intervals, for all collected eggs. Feed conversion (kg feed/kg eggs laid) was estimated based on egg weight and feed intake. At the end of weeks 1, 5, and 11 of the experiment (equivalent to 20, 25, and 30 weeks of hen’s age, respectively), 12 eggs from each group were randomly selected to determine their physicochemical properties. The eggs were weighed, and average eggshell thickness was measured using the ESTG-1 eggshell thickness gauge (Orka Food Technology). Next, the eggs were broken to assess yolk and albumen quality using an egg analyzer (Orka Food Technology, Herzliya, Israel), based on albumen height and egg weight expressed in Haugh units (HU). Haugh units were calculated using the following formula: HU = log10 (H − 1.7 W0.37 + 7.56), where H is albumen height (mm), and W is egg weight (g). Eggshell breaking strength was measured using and egg force reader (Orka Food Technology, Herzliya, Israel).

In accordance with Directive 2010/63/EU56 on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes, all hens were humanely euthanized. At the end of the experiment, a post-mortem examination was conducted on 50 laying hens with body weights close to the group mean. The birds were first electrically stunned and then slaughtered by severing the right carotid artery and jugular vein. The procedure adhered to standard slaughterhouse protocols approved by Council Regulation (EC) No. 1099/200962 and is recognized as an acceptable method of euthanasia when effective stunning is applied in advance. The livers were removed, washed, and weighed. The proximate chemical composition of liver samples was determined in the sample using the Weende method61. The malondialdehyde (MDA) content of liver samples was measured as described by Witte et al.63.

The right tibiae were collected to determine tibia ash content. The tibiae were stripped of the adhering tissue and dried at 100 °C, and fat was extracted with a mixture of ethanol and methanol (90% and 10%, respectively). Fat-extracted tibiae were dried at 100 °C for 24 h and incinerated in a muffle furnace at 600 °C overnight. Tibia width and length were also measured. The mechanical strength of bones was assessed during a breaking test performed on the TA.HDD plus Texture Analyser (Stable Micro Systems, UK). The bone was placed on two thin holders distant by 50 mm and was subjected to deflection made by a piston with a 5 mm thick shearing edge. The piston was moving with a speed of 2 mm∙s-1 until the complete breakdown of the bone. Changes in displacement and force were recorded over time during the test. Based on these data, the following parameters were determined for each bone: breaking force (the maximum force recorded during the breaking test, N), fracture strength (value of the force causing the first significant fracture – after which the force decreased by 10 N, in N), work to the first fracture (work done until the fracture point, mJ) and the total work to fracture (work done until the complete breakdown of the bone, mJ). The value of work was determined by numerical integration.

Statistical analysis

The results of the experiment were processed statistically by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s test. The arithmetic mean (x), the standard error of the mean (SEM), and the level of significance (P) were calculated. All calculations were performed using STATISTICA 12 software (Tibco Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA, https://www.statistica.com/).
