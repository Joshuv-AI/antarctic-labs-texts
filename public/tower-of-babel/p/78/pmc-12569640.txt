A Case of Pelvic Lymphatic Effusion Managed with N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate/Lipiodol Embolization through an Inguinal Lymph Node
Authors: Satoshi Oue, Ken Kageyama, Atsushi Jogo, Akira Yamamoto, Kazuki Murai, Mariko Nakano, Nobuyuki Otani, Eisaku Terayama, Masanori Ozaki, Shohei Harada, Kazuo Asano, Takuma Wada, Takeshi Fukuda, Toshiyuki Sumi, Yukio Miki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLymphatic ascites developed in a woman in her fifties after she underwent total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and lymph node dissection for endometrial carcinoma. Approximately 500-1,000 mL of opalescent fluid was drained daily. Initially, dietary fat restrictions failed to reduce ascites. Two lipiodol lymphangiographies identified leaks from the iliac lymphatic vessels but were only partially successful at occluding these leaks. Octreotide injection and clamping of the drainage tube were attempted without success. On postoperative day 68, a mixture of lipiodol and n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate was injected to embolize the leakage point, significantly reducing symptoms. The patient was discharged on day 76, and follow-up computed tomography two months later showed complete resolution of ascites. One year after surgery, the patient remained symptom-free.Keywords: NBCA lymphatic embolization, pelvic, refractory

Introduction

Lymphatic ascites is recognized as a postoperative complication among patients with gynecologic malignancies. The frequency of lymphatic ascites in such malignancies is approximately 0.2%-3% [1-3]. Standard managements for lymphatic ascites include dietary restrictions and somatostatin therapy. In cases when these treatments fail, lipiodol lymphangiography, lymphatic embolization and surgical ligation are considered as alternative options [4]. This report describes a case in which injection of n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA) with lipiodol from an inguinal lymph node successfully relieved lymphatic ascites that had been refractory to conservative therapies and lipiodol lymphangiography.

Case Report

The patient, a woman in her fifties, underwent simple total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and dissection of the pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes for endometrial carcinoma. Just after surgery, 500-1,000 mL of opalescent fluid was being drained daily from the pelvic drainage tube (Figures 1 and 2). The triglyceride level in aspirated ascitic fluid on postoperative day 3 was 1,343 mg/dL, with the presence of triglycerides confirming a diagnosis of chylous leakage in lymphatic ascites.

Lymphatic ascites.

Dietary modifications were initiated to restrict fat intake after surgery, but the amount of lymphatic ascites from the drainage tube increased. Ultrasound-guided ethiodized oil injections (Lipiodol 480 injection; Guerbet, Japan) were performed twice on postoperative days 25 and 33 through bilateral inguinal lymph nodes using a 23-G needle. x-Ray fluoroscopy confirmed successful administration of lipiodol into the external iliac lymphatic vessels from both inguinal lymph nodes. The first trans-nodal lymphangiography indicated lipiodol leakage from the bilateral external iliac lymphatic vessels into the abdominal cavity adjacent to the bilateral external iliac arteries and veins (Figure 3). The total amount of lipiodol used was 16 mL. After the first lymphangiography, lymphatic ascites remained in the abdominal cavity (Figure 4). The second trans-nodal lymphangiography revealed that the right-side leakage point had been occluded by the first lymphangiography. However, the left-side leakage point remained open as shown by rapid lipiodol flow into the abdominal cavity during the second lymphangiography (Figure 5). The total volume of lipiodol used was 8 mL. Despite lymphangiography being performed twice, lymphatic ascites continued to be drained from the pelvic tube, necessitating further intervention. On postoperative day 41, the patient was administered octreotide subcutaneously at 300 μg/day for 8 days (Figure 1). On postoperative day 47, the pelvic drainage tube was clamped in an attempt to halt the leakage. However, the patient gained approximately 3 kg of weight during the clamping period, suggesting persistent lymphatic leakage. Further therapeutic intervention was required. The drain was removed at postoperative day 63 to avoid the risk of infection due to long-term drainage. When the pelvic drainage tube was removed, 1,100 mL of ascites fluid was drained. On postoperative day 68, NBCA injection through the left inguinal lymph nodes was planned. First, the left-side leakage point was confirmed by lipiodol injection through the left inguinal lymph node. Second, 0.5 mL of NBCA-Lipiodol mixture (NBCA:Lipiodol = 1:5) was injected, followed by distilled water, leading to stagnation of the mixture at the leakage point (Figure 6, Supplementary Video 1). Temporary pain in the groin occurred during administration of the NBCA-Lipiodol mixture and resolved immediately after completion of the NBCA-Lipiodol injection. Fifteen minutes after injecting the mixture, we reconfirmed stagnation of the mixture in the lymphatic vessels, including the leakage point. This procedure embolized the leakage point, as confirmed by a postoperative reduction in the body weight of the patient and resolution of the feeling of abdominal fullness. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 76. Abdominal CT performed 2 months after lymphatic embolization showed complete disappearance of ascites (Figure 7). As of 1 year postoperatively, no relapse of symptoms had been encountered.

Discussion

This case showed postoperative lymphatic ascites after total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy with dissection of pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes for endometrial carcinoma. Postoperative lymphatic ascites is a recognized complication after abdominal or pelvic surgery. Recent studies have reported the incidence of postoperative lymphatic ascites as 0.3%-11%. Such ascites can significantly contribute to morbidity and mortality owing to malnutrition, dehydration, and immunosuppression [5]. Moreover, lymphatic ascites continues for several weeks to several months, potentially prolonging the period of post-surgical convalescence [4]. Previous reports have revealed that the incidence of postoperative lymphatic ascites is approximately 0.2%-4%, with a higher prevalence observed in cases involving the resection of para-aortic lymph nodes than of pelvic lymph nodes [1-3].

The therapeutic approach to lymphatic ascites encompasses conservative treatments in addition to lipiodol lymphangiography, NBCA-Lipiodol lymphatic embolization, and surgical ligation [4]. Traditional management through open or laparoscopic surgery for sustained lymphatic leakage presents risks such as prolonged hospitalization, delayed recovery times, and increased morbidity and mortality [6]. Rose et al. noted that 68.5% of 523 individuals with lymphatic ascites after retroperitoneal surgery experienced improvement with conservative management alone [4]. Conservative management strategies for postoperative lymphatic ascites include 1) dietary modifications such as a high-protein, low-fat diet supplemented with medium-chain triglycerides, fasting, and total parenteral nutrition; and 2) pharmacological interventions, including somatostatin analogues, vasoconstrictor pancreatic lipase inhibitors, diuretics, and traditional Chinese herbal medicine. Conservative treatments are generally considered more effective for managing chylous leakage than lymphatic leakage in cases of postoperative lymphatic ascites [7]. However, some studies have reported that conservative therapy also alleviates symptoms in patients with lymphatic leakage [8].

Chylous leakage is characterized by an elevated triglyceride concentration (>200 mg/dL) in the drained fluid, which confirms its diagnosis [7, 9]. In the present case, the lymphatic ascites was identified as chylous in nature, despite originating from lymphatic vessels at the iliac level. This phenomenon may be attributed to an anatomical variation, such as drainage of the primary intestinal lymphatic trunk into the lumbar lymphatic system rather than the chyle duct. Moreover, the potential for chyle to reflux into the lymphatic vasculature may contribute to this presentation in this case [10].

Recently, the emergence of interventional radiology techniques has shown promise in the treatment of lymphatic ascites. Lipiodol injection is expected to prove effective in the treatment of lymphatic leakage that is refractory to conservative treatments [11, 12]. In this case, we used 16 mL of lipiodol for the first lymphangiography. The incidence of pulmonary complications has been found to increase significantly when the amount of lipiodol injected exceeds 20 mL [13]. According to a recent report, the clinical success rate was 88% after performing lymphangiography using lipiodol with an average injection volume of 29 mL [14]. On the basis of these previous reports, our institution allows a maximum single use of 20 mL of lipiodol.

Recently, embolization with a mixture of NBCA-Lipiodol through inguinal lymph nodes has shown effectiveness for occluding leakage from lymphatic vessels [15-17], and the effectiveness of trans-nodular NBCA embolization for pelvic lymph leakage, similar to our case report, has already been reported [18]. NBCA-Lipiodol embolization has been implemented for lymphatic ascites after gynecologic cancer surgery with favorable outcomes. In a cohort of 18 patients who underwent NBCA lymphatic embolization from the inguinal lymph nodes, the daily volume of lymphatic ascites decreased from an average of 520-1,500 mL to <300 mL after this procedure [2]. Consequently, pelvic drainage tubes were removed within a week in 94% of these cases, underscoring the efficacy of NBCA for resolving pelvic lymphatic leaks. Although the precise NBCA-to-lipiodol ratios used were not specified, approximately 10 mL lipiodol was injected from the inguinal lymph nodes. Complications were observed in 17% of patients, predominantly in the form of groin pain necessitating analgesics. The risk of lower extremity lymphedema is a valid concern when multiple inguinal or iliac lymph nodes and vessels are occluded. Previous studies have suggested that embolization of one or two lymph nodes did not cause clinically significant lymphedema [15]; however, symptoms of lymphedema may worsen when four lymph nodes are embolized [19]. Three previous reports have shown that the embolic material ratio should be tailored to the extent of embolization required, with effective dilution ranges reported as 1:3-1:9, 1:4-1:6, and 1:2-1:9, respectively [15, 20, 21]. The dilution ratio of NBCA with lipiodol depends on the rate of extravasation and the distance between the accessed lymph node and the leakage site [20]. NBCA mixed with lipiodol should be injected until it fully occupies the lymph node itself, also filling the efferent lymphatic vessels, or until glue leakage is observed [21].

Hill et al. reported 24.2% dilution of NBCA with lipiodol, with 0.44 mL instilled during the first intervention [18]. In subsequent procedures, NBCA dilution was increased to 51.7%, with an instillation volume of 0.52 mL. Success rates for lymphangiography and lymphatic embolization in treating postoperative lymphatic leaks are 56% and 86%, respectively [22]. Embolization with NBCA, despite the association with a risk of postoperative pulmonary embolism, has been deemed beneficial for intractable lymphatic ascites unresponsive to lymphangiography [22]. The present case indicates the successful application of a 1:5 NBCA-Lipiodol mixture for lymphatic embolization in a patient for whom lipiodol alone was insufficient. Groin pain occurred only during administration of the NBCA-Lipiodol mixture in this case but resolved immediately after the injection, with no significant postoperative complications such as pulmonary embolism. We consider that pharmacological stimulation of the lymphatic vessels may have occurred.

The clinical decision is often delayed in cases of lymphatic leakage. Previous reports have indicated that the median duration of treatment is 10-11 days for patients who show improvement with conservative management of lymphatic leakage after retroperitoneal surgery or cervical cancer surgery with pelvic lymphadenectomy [4, 23]. Conversely, for those unresponsive to conservative management and requiring additional interventions, this median duration extended to 22 days [4]. Rose et al. recommended that interventional radiology should be performed in patients who show no decrease in the output of lymphatic leakage after receiving conservative treatment for at least 21 days [4]. The duration of conservative treatment typically ranges from several weeks to two months, after which surgical intervention should be considered if necessary [7]. With these considerations in mind, if lymphatic ascites does not improve after approximately three weeks of conservative management, lymphangiography should be considered. A previous study reported a recovery period of five days after the initial lymphangiogram [15]. Other reports have suggested that drainage can be discontinued after an average of 18 days after the final lipiodol injection [14], and that lymphangiography alone achieves clinical success within a median of 14 days [24]. Verhaeghe et al. noted that multiple lymphangiography sessions may be required to achieve clinical success [14]. Repeat lymphangiography may be performed within one to two weeks, depending on the clinical course. In cases of refractory ascites that fail to resolve despite lymphangiography, lymphatic embolization should be considered within one to two weeks after the procedure. On the basis of these findings, lymphatic embolization should ideally be performed within six weeks after surgery. Furthermore, drainage of more than 1,500 mL/day of lymphatic fluid is linked with prolonged drainage periods post-embolization [25]. Considering the previously mentioned points, it appears that the intervention in this case was delayed overall, particularly in terms of the prolonged tube clamping period. Earlier intervention may have shortened the hospital stay period. In this case, the patient's weight continued to increase even 3-4 weeks after the second lymphangiography, suggesting failure of occlusion, and the additional NBCA lymphangiographic embolization achieved resolution of ascites. NBCA lymphatic embolization could be beneficial as an early intervention in the management of refractory lymphatic ascites not relieved by conservative or lymphangiographic approaches.

NBCA lymphatic embolization may be beneficial in cases of lymphatic ascites refractory to conservative therapeutic approaches or lymphangiography.
