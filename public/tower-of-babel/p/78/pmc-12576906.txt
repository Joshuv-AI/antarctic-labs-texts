Is systemic inflammation a missing link between oral microbiome and oral squamous cell carcinoma? Results from multi-omics integration analyses
Authors: Lingjun Yan, Baochang He, Qingrong Deng, Yu Qiu, Lisong Lin, Bin Shi, Jing Wang, Fa Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo investigate whether systemic inflammation mediates the relationship between oral microbiome alterations and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) through multi-omics integration analyses.MethodsMetagenomic sequencing of unstimulated saliva samples from 65 OSCC patients and 65 matched controls was performed. Plasma levels of 34 inflammatory cytokines were profiled using Luminex assay. Six machine learning models identified potential diagnostic microbial markers. Mediation analysis assessed whether inflammation serves as a mechanistic link between oral microbiota and OSCC.ResultsOSCC patients exhibited reduced species richness and significant beta diversity alterations. Among 155 differential species identified, 25 were enriched in OSCC, including Capnocytophaga sputigena, Gemella haemolysans, Staphylococcus aureus, and several Streptococcus species, with higher abundance in poor oral hygiene conditions. The Boruta-CatBoost model achieved exceptional diagnostic performance (bootstrap AUC = 0.991; 5-fold cross-validation AUC = 0.947). Functional profiling revealed 22 metabolic pathways over-represented in OSCC, notably lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis. Nine circulating cytokines (IL-22, IL-6, IL-2, CCL5, GM-CSF, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-18, IFN-α) were significantly elevated in OSCC patients. Mediation analysis revealed that IL-22 partially mediated the effect of Staphylococcus aureus on OSCC risk, while CCL5 mediated associations of Gemella haemolysans and Streptococcus species with OSCC (mediation proportions: 29.9–50.1%).ConclusionOur multi-omics integration suggests that systemic inflammation, particularly through IL-22 and CCL5 upregulation, serves as a mechanistic link between specific oral bacteria and OSCC risk, which could provide new strategies for OSCC prevention and early intervention.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) represents one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting the oral cavity, constituting a significant global health burden. According to the latest data from GLOBOCAN 2022, there are approximately 389,000 new cases of oral cancer diagnosed annually worldwide, with projections indicating a concerning 70% increase by 2050 [1,2]. Despite significant advancements in diagnostic techniques and treatment modalities, the prognosis for OSCC patients remains poor, with five-year survival rates of approximately 50% [3]. This underscores the urgent need to identify novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets for early detection and intervention.

Mounting evidence suggests that dysbiosis—a disruption in the balanced composition and function of the oral microbiome—is associated with various oral diseases, including periodontal disease, dental caries, and even systemic conditions such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes [4–6]. Our recent study and previous studies utilizing 16S rRNA sequencing suggested that dysbiosis of the oral microbiome may contribute to the pathogenesis and development of OSCC [7–11]. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses also confirmed microbial dysbiosis in OSCC, highlighting consistent taxonomic shifts that may contribute to carcinogenesis [12,13]. However, while these findings provide critical insights into compositional alterations, there remains a scarcity of research employing advanced metagenomic sequencing techniques to investigate the functional changes within the oral microbiome of OSCC patients and their potential mechanistic implications in disease development.

Furthermore, the mechanisms through which oral microbiome dysbiosis may contribute to OSCC development remain inadequately characterized. Emerging evidence suggests that dysbiotic oral microbiota can trigger and sustain chronic inflammation, which plays a crucial role in carcinogenesis. Specific oral bacteria have been shown to activate pro-inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades, leading to the production of cytokines that create a tumor-promoting microenvironment [14,15]. However, the specific interplay between oral microbiome dysbiosis, systemic inflammatory responses, and OSCC development remains incompletely understood and warrants further investigation. In this study, we aim to investigate whether systemic inflammation serves as a critical missing link between oral microbiome dysbiosis and OSCC using an integrated multi-omics approach combining metagenomic analysis and inflammatory cytokine profiling, which may provide novel insights into the pathogenesis of OSCC and inform the development of targeted preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Study participants

A case-control study design was employed, with cases sourced from the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University during September 2018 and August 2021. The control group was drawn from individuals undergoing routine health examinations at community health centers during the same period. Controls were frequency-matched to cases based on age (±3 years), sex, smoking status, and body mass index (BMI) categories at a 1:1 ratio. The inclusion criteria of the case group were defined as newly diagnosed primary OSCC cases confirmed through histopathological examination (ICD-10 code: C01-C06). The tumor grading was performed according to the World Health Organization (WHO) histological classification system, while staging was determined using the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system (8th edition). Exclusion criteria for the case group included individuals diagnosed with benign lesions, secondary tumors, or those unable to provide clear responses due to critical illness, as well as individuals who had undergone radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or other anti-tumor treatments before hematological examination. The control group's inclusion criteria comprised individuals aged >18 years with no history of tumor diseases or head and neck surgeries.

Additionally, all study participants had not used antibiotics or other medications affecting the oral microbiome (e.g. antifungals, antivirals, antiparasitic drugs) or taken steroids, immunosuppressants, or drugs that could potentially affect the measurement of inflammatory factors in the three months preceding sample collection. Moreover, individuals with syphilis, human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis B virus, or hepatitis C virus infections were excluded, as were those with severe systemic or local infections. To minimize confounding effects on systemic inflammation, participants with the following systemic inflammatory diseases were excluded: rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, inflammatory bowel disease (including Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis), psoriasis, ankylosing spondylitis, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and other autoimmune conditions. Additionally, individuals with chronic inflammatory diseases such as chronic bronchitis, chronic sinusitis, or chronic gastritis were also excluded. Ultimately, a total of 65 cases and 65 controls were included for salivary metagenomic sequencing analysis. Based on diversity analyses reported in existing literature [7–9], we observed a moderate effect size of approximately 0.50–0.60. Using a conservative effect size of 0.50, with α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and desired power of 80%, the required sample size was calculated to be 55 participants per group. Our final sample of 65 participants per group achieved a power of 86.6%. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (No. 2018013), and followed STROBE reporting guidelines.

Collection and processing of biological samples

Saliva and blood samples were collected from case group subjects prior to treatment at initial admission, and from control group subjects during physical examination. Unstimulated saliva (2–2.5 mL) was collected under non-stimulating conditions. Peripheral venous blood (3–5 mL) was drawn using EDTA anticoagulant. All samples were stored at −80 °C until use.

Demographic and lifestyle information survey

We conducted face-to-face interviews using structured questionnaires to collect demographic and lifestyle data, including smoking, alcohol and tea consumption, chronic disease history, medication use, dietary patterns, and oral health indicators (daily tooth brushing frequency, missing teeth (presence and number), denture use and duration, dental visit patterns, and recurrent oral ulcers). Following the approach of Katsouyanni et al. [16], we calculated an oral hygiene score using effect-size weights from a multivariable logistic regression model. Each indicator was weighted by its excess odds ratio (OR-1), and the oral hygiene score represented the sum of individual weights (Supplementary Table 1). Participants were categorized using the control group's median score (2.43) as a threshold for oral hygiene status assessment.

Salivary microbiota detection and processing

Saliva DNA was extracted according to the BGI Genomics protocol, and then microbial DNA was enriched using the NEBnext microbiome DNA enrichment kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, US). DNA concentration was measured using the Qubit Fluorometer and Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA). Following quality control, PCR amplification, library quantification, and other procedures, qualified library samples were sequenced on the BGISEQ 500 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China), yielding raw data (FASTQ). The raw data underwent comprehensive bioinformatics analysis, including quality control, metagenome assembly, gene prediction, construction of non-redundant gene sets, species annotation, and functional analysis. The workflow was based on public code (https://github.com/YongxinLiu/EasyMetagenome) [17]. The main steps were described in the Supplementary Materials.

Inflammatory cytokines measurement

The Cytokine & Chemokine 34-Plex Human Panel 1 A assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, San Diego, USA) were utilized to analyze the plasma inflammatory cytokine profile. The assay covered a comprehensive panel of 34 cytokines, including IFN-γ, IL-12p70, IL-13, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, TNF-α, GM-CSF, IL-18, IL-10, IL-17A, IL-21, IL-22, IL-23, IL-27, IL-9, IFN-α, IL-31, IL-15, IL-1α, IL-1RA, IL-7, TNF-β, CCL11, CXCL11, IL-8, CXCL10, CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, SDF-1α, and CCL5. The measurement procedures strictly followed the manufacturer's instructions. Due to budget constraints, we randomly selected 80 representative participants (40 OSCC patients and 40 controls) from the overall population for plasma inflammatory cytokine profiling. Previous studies investigating systemic inflammatory markers in oral cancer reported effect sizes ranging from 0.62 to 8.93 [18]. Using the most conservative effect size of 0.62, with α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and desired power of 80%, the required sample size was calculated to be 37 participants per group. Our sample of 40 participants per group achieved a power of 84.2%. Representative analyses are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. If the data were normally distributed, they were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and compared using Student's t-test. If the data were not normally distributed, they were expressed as median (25th percentile, 75th percentile), and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, and compared using the χ2 test.

To assess the adequacy of the sequencing size in this study, sparse curve analysis was conducted using the ‘iNEXT’ package to analyze the number of species sequenced in the case and control groups. Additionally, community diversity and redundancy analysis (RDA) were calculated using the ‘vegan’ package to evaluate the phenotypic factors affecting the community. The α-diversity of the bacterial communities in the case and control groups was evaluated using estimate of richness and Shannon's index, while β-diversity and variation between-group were analyzed based on Principal Co-ordinates Analysis (PCoA) with Bray-Curtis distance and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) analyses.

In order to minimize the occurrence of false positives, only species with a cumulative relative abundance exceeding 0.01% in all samples and present in at least 10% of samples were considered for subsequent analysis. Differential microbiota between the OSCC and control groups were identified using ‘MaAslin2’ and ‘ANCOMBC’ package. Default parameters were employed for both methods, and P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, commonly known as the best False Discovery Rate (FDR). The significance threshold for the multivariate association with linear models 2 (MaAslin2) results was set at FDR < 0.2 [19,20], while for analysis of composition of microbiomes with bias correction (ANCOM-BC) results, it was FDR < 0.05 [21,22]. The final selection of differential microbiota was based on the intersection of results from both methods. The species-level colony co-occurrence network was constructed using ‘FastSpar’, and significant correlations between differential species were determined using R packages ‘igraph’ and ‘ggraph’ (absolute value of correlation > 0.3, P < 0.05). The identification of differentially enriched functional pathways was based on the Reporter score [23], with a threshold of Reporter score ≥ 1.96 used for detecting differential enrichment in KEGG pathways. The Reporter score calculation method is detailed in the Supplementary Materials.

To identify salivary microbial biomarkers, three feature selection methods were first employed: the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) using the optimal (minimum) λ value, Boruta, and the max-relevance and min-redundancy (MRMR) algorithm. These methods were utilized to select relevant subsets of differential microbial features. Based on these feature subsets, six machine learning models were subsequently constructed: logistic regression, random forest, adaptive boosting (Adaboost), extreme gradient boosting (XGboost), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), and categorical boosting (Catboost). Model performance was systematically assessed by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI), using both 5-fold cross-validation repeated 100 times and 500 bootstrap iterations for validation.

Inflammatory cytokine distributions were analyzed using raw concentrations with log10 transformation. Linear regression identified differential inflammatory cytokines between groups and adjusted for potential confounders. Multivariable logistic regression investigated associations between differential inflammatory cytokines and OSCC risk. Spearman's partial correlation analysis (‘ppcor’ package) assessed relationships between salivary microbiota biomarkers and differential inflammatory cytokines. Given the plausible biological connection between salivary microbiota, inflammation, and OSCC, mediation analysis using the PROCESS function (‘bruceR’ package) examined whether inflammatory cytokines mediate the microbiota-OSCC link. Reverse mediation analysis (inflammatory cytokines-oral microbiome-OSCC risk) served as sensitivity analysis. Bootstrap method (1000 replicates) assessed mediating effect significance, calculated percentage of mediating effect to total effect, and estimated various effect types with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical analyses used R (version 4.1.0) with two-sided tests at α = 0.05 significance level.

Results

Participant characteristics

The general characteristics of the 130 subjects enrolled in this study, comprising 65 OSCC patients and 65 controls, are presented in Supplementary Table 3. The staging distribution of OSCC patients showed 26 (40.0%) with early-stage disease (stages I-II) and 39 (60.0%) with advanced-stage disease (stages III-IV). Histological examination revealed 27 (41.5%) well-differentiated, 27 (41.5%) moderately differentiated, and 11 (17.0%) poorly differentiated tumors. The two groups exhibited comparability in the distribution of various characteristics (P > 0.05), except for a significant differentiation in meat intake frequency (P < 0.05). Regarding oral hygiene behaviors, the OSCC patients exhibited suboptimal practices (higher oral hygiene score, P = 0.002).

Sequencing data quality and microbial diversity

After sequencing, culling low-quality and junction sequences, and removing host contaminants, we obtained 6,871,528 valid sequences for the control group and 3,544,229 for the OSCC group. All samples exhibited high-quality scores, with assembly producing 4,690,486 contigs and identifying 3,810,459 non-redundant genes spanning a total length of 2,342,365,812 bases. Further specific data can be found in Supplementary Tables 4–6. Sparse curve analysis indicated that the sequencing size met subsequent analysis requirements (Figure 1A). In the OSCC group, α-diversity analysis indicated a decrease in richness estimation index compared to the control group (P < 0.05), while the Shannon index did not show statistically significant differences (P > 0.05, Figure 1B). β-diversity analysis revealed significant differences between the groups, with the OSCC group exhibiting greater variation in microbial community structure (R2 = 0.061, P = 0.001, Figure 1C). RDA analysis demonstrated that BMI, smoking, tea consumption, and hypertension significantly influenced community variation, with smoking having the greatest impact (R2 = 0.071, P = 0.013, Figure 1D).

Taxonomic composition and identification of differential species

At the biotaxonomic level, 37 phyla, 73 orders, 170 families, 383 genera, and 5551 species were identified. The top five dominant phyla in the OSCC group and the control group were Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Clostridium (Supplementary Figure 1A). The top 20 dominant genera in the OSCC group and the control group, displayed in Supplementary Figure 1B, included Prevotella, Neisseria, and Streptococcus. At the species level, 24 of the top 50 species showed statistically significant differences in relative abundance between the two groups (FDR-corrected P < 0.05), such as Prevotella melaninogenica, Haemophilus haemolyticus, Capnocytophaga sputigena, and Streptococcus mitis. Notably, the abundance of Porphyromonas gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum, commonly reported oral pathogens, was elevated in the OSCC group but did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure 1C).

The species-level datasets included 3997 bacterial species for analysis. Figure 2A–B show the top 5 species exhibiting the largest variance in relative abundance, identified by MaAslin2 and ANCOM_BC methods were consistent: Streptococcus mitis, Streptococcus oralis, Rothia mucilaginosa, Prevotella jejuni, and Prevotella spp. These methods revealed 155 differential species, with 25 increasing and 130 decreasing in abundance in the OSCC group. Notable species whose abundance increased in the OSCC group, including Capnocytophaga sputigena, Gemella haemolysans, Haemophilus haemolyticus, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus gwangjuense, Streptococcus pseudopneumoniae, and Streptococcus sp_116_D4, with significantly higher abundance in individuals with poor oral hygiene (Figure 2C–D), suggesting their potential importance in oral hygiene and OSCC risk.

Machine learning for microbial biomarkers identification

We further evaluated the differential species for their ability to discriminate OSCC using machine learning algorithms (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 7). A total of 12 overlapping microbial biomarkers (7 upregulated and 5 downregulated) were identified using three feature selection methods. Among models tested, Catboost demonstrated the most promising discriminative performance, exhibiting the smallest standard deviation and the highest AUC value across 100 repetitions of 5-fold cross-validation and 500 bootstrap validations. The Boruta-Catboost model outperformed the MRMR-Catboost and LASSO-Catboost models. Notably, Campylobacter geochelonis had the most significant impact on the model, in the Boruta-Catboost framework. Additionally, OSCC-enriched species such as Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pseudopneumoniae made substantial contributions to the Boruta-Catboost model. To further validate our findings, we conducted a hold-out validation by randomly splitting our original cohort into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets to simulate external validation conditions. The Boruta-Catboost model maintained robust performance on this independent test set (AUC = 0.938, 95% CI: 0.750–0.999), comparable to the internal validation results (AUC = 0.947, 95% CI: 0.870–0.999) (Supplementary Table 8).

Microbial network and functional metabolic pathway analysis

The co-occurrence network analysis revealed a decrease in network complexity and weakened inter-strain interactions at the species level in the OSCC group compared to the control group (OSCC vs. control: Edges, 2184 vs. 1043; Degree, 4368 vs. 2086, Supplementary Figure 2A–B). Further examination of co-occurrence relationships among enriched differential species in the OSCC group indicated a general weakening of these relationships compared to the control group. However, strong positive correlations were observed between Streptococcus mitis, Streptococcus oralis, Gemella haemolysans, Haemophilus haemolyticus, and Escherichia fergusonii, suggesting their potential significance in OSCC risk (Supplementary Figure 2C–D). Analysis of the functional metabolic pathways in the microbiota of the control and OSCC groups revealed 28 differential metabolic pathways (|Reporter score| > 1.96). Of these, 22 were found to be enriched in the OSCC group. Notably, the inflammation-associated pathway Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) biosynthesis pathway was among those enriched in the OSCC group (Supplementary Figure 3).

Inflammatory cytokine profiling and mediation analysis

Significant differences in the plasma levels of inflammatory cytokines were found between OSCC patients and the controls. Specifically, elevated levels of 12 inflammatory cytokines, including CCL4, CCL5, CCL11, GM-CSF, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-18, IL-22, IL-23, and TNF-α, were observed in OSCC patients (Mann–Whitney U test, P < 0.05, Figure 4A). Compared with the control group, these inflammatory cytokines showed only a slight increase in OSCC patients with higher oral hygiene scores, although the differences did not reach statistical significance (Figure 4B). Additionally, regression analyses identified 13 OSCC-related inflammatory cytokines, 11 of which overlapped with the results of the above test (Figure 4C, Model 1). Furthermore, multivariable logistic regression demonstrated that 13 inflammatory cytokines were associated with an increased risk of OSCC (Figure 4C, Model 2).

Discussion

Utilizing advanced metagenomic sequencing technology, this study provides supportive evidence of structural and functional dysregulation of oral microbiota in OSCC patients. The identification of 25 bacterial species enriched in the OSCC group sheds light on the potential role of oral microbiota in the pathogenesis of this disease. Furthermore, the enrichment of the inflammation-associated LPS biosynthetic pathway in the OSCC group highlights the potential mechanistic link between oral microbiota and OSCC risk. Additionally, the profiling of plasma inflammatory cytokines revealed elevated levels of nine pro-inflammatory cytokines in OSCC patients, with IL-22 and CCL5 identified as potential mediators of the association between oral microbiota and OSCC risk.

The oral microbiota maintains ecological balance in healthy individuals through stable species interactions [24,25]. Consistent with previous 16S rRNA studies of saliva samples, the OSCC group displayed reduced microbial richness and diversity compared to healthy controls. Dominant phyla in OSCC patients—Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Fusobacteria—aligned with prior findings [26–29]. Although Porphyromonas gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum, established carcinogens in vitro, increased in OSCC patients, neither reached statistical significance nor emerged as OSCC-related bacterial markers. Bacterial behavior in isolated cultures may differ markedly from community-based phenotypes, where complex microbial interactions can produce unexpected outcomes [30]. The intricate roles of these pathogenic bacteria and broader oral microbiota in carcinogenesis require further population-based and experimental investigation.

We identified 25 OSCC-enriched microbiotas at the species level, consistent with previous reports of elevated Streptococci (S. oralis, S. mitis, S. pneumoniae) in OSCC patients [31–33]. These Gram-positive anaerobic commensals maintain oral microecological balance but also contribute to acid production, fostering hypoxic tumor microenvironments [34], and generate hydrogen peroxide that may induce DNA damage [35–37]. OSCC samples showed enrichment of Capnocytophaga sputigena, Gemella haemolysans, Haemophilus haemolyticus, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus gwangjuense, with most species elevated in patients with poor oral hygiene. Capnocytophaga sputigena and Gemella haemolysans enrichment in OSCC has been confirmed previously, with Gemella haemolysans correlating positively with tumor infiltration depth [38]. Furthermore, it has been suggested that Haemophilus haemolyticus may play a role in promoting periodontitis through perturbing host-microbe interactions [39,40], similar to Porphyromonas gingivalis, while Staphylococcus aureus can trigger prostaglandin E2 production in oral keratinocytes, upregulating COX-2 transcription and cyclin D1 expression—a gene linked to oral cancer [41–43]. Machine learning analysis using the Boruta-Catboost model incorporating 21 OSCC-enriched and 22 OSCC-depleted species achieved optimal diagnostic accuracy. These results suggest salivary microbiome could serve as convenient, noninvasive biomarkers for OSCC diagnosis in the future.

In this study, we found a distinct enrichment of bacteria in OSCC samples, closely associated with pro-inflammatory markers such as IL-22, IL-1α, CCL5, and IL-6, highlighting a possible link between oral microbiota and inflammatory cytokines in OSCC pathogenesis. Mediation analysis implicated Staphylococcus aureus in elevating OSCC risk via increased IL-22, consistent with reports that Staphylococcus aureus delays wound healing and boosts pro-inflammatory cytokines, notably IL-22 [44,45]. We also identified several Streptococcus species (including Streptococcus mitis, Streptococcus pneumoniae) and others—that may influence OSCC by modulating CCL5. While the precise mechanisms remain to be elucidated, our findings emphasize a complex interplay between specific bacteria and inflammatory mediators in the pathogenesis of OSCC.

The strength of this study lies in its investigation into the changes in salivary microbiota and inflammatory cytokines linked to OSCC using advanced metagenomic sequencing and Luminex multiplex assays technologies. The study also delves into the potential role of inflammation levels in mediating the relationship between oral microbiome and OSCC risk through a comprehensive analysis of multi-omics data. However, it is important to note the limitations of this study. Firstly, the sample size utilized for metagenomic sequencing and detection of inflammatory cytokine profiles was relatively small. Secondly, the study employed a single-center case-control study design, and while the findings suggest a potential association between the salivary microbiota and OSCC, the underlying causal relationship remains unclear. Thirdly, our machine learning models were validated using internal cross-validation and hold-out validation strategies, but lacked external validation using independent cohorts or public databases. Future studies should prioritize external validation using independent cohorts and standardized protocols to establish the clinical utility of these microbial signatures for OSCC diagnosis. Additionally, the study only utilized a simple mediation model to analyze the mediation effects of various inflammatory cytokines, but these cytokines may be highly correlated or even causally related, indicating the need for further analysis using more complex statistical models. Future research will require large-scale prospective cohort studies and additional in vivo and in vitro experiments to enhance our understanding of the pathogenesis of OSCC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study utilizes multi-omics data analysis of the oral microbiome and the inflammatory proteome to demonstrate that OSCC patients exhibit significant alterations in the composition and functionality of salivary microbiota, as well as elevated levels of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines in the plasma. Furthermore, IL-22 and CCL5 may partially mediated the relationship between Staphylococcus aureus, Gemella haemolysans, and certain Streptococci with the risk of OSCC. These findings provide valuable new insights for further investigation into the mechanisms by which oral microbiota influence the risk of OSCC.
