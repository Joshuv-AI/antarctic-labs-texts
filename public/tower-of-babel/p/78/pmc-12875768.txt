Selection–diversification interplay in oligonucleotide chemical evolution
Authors: Jiro Kakizaki, Ryo Mizuuchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The emergence of catalytic RNAs (ribozymes) may have set the stage for an “RNA world” preceding protein evolution. The probability of ribozyme emergence and maintenance would have depended on available oligonucleotide compositions. Excessively high or low sequence diversity could hinder ribozyme formation, whereas balanced diversity is likely more favorable. Multiple steps of chemical evolution—from nucleotide supply and oligomerization to subsequent copying and assembly through nonenzymatic reactions—likely shaped oligonucleotide diversity. In this review, we discuss how oligonucleotide chemical evolution may have involved both selective enrichment and diversification of sequence compositions, with their interplay generating oligonucleotide pools of varying diversity across environments and evolutionary timescales. Current experiments on nonenzymatic RNA-based reactions remain limited to short timescales, but strategies combining DNA and protein enzymes could provide efficient models to investigate the compositional dynamics of oligonucleotides.

Significance

The emergence of catalytic nucleic acids, such as ribozymes, is considered a critical step in the origins of life. Although sequence diversity is often assumed to promote ribozyme emergence, excessive diversity within oligonucleotide pools can reduce the copy number of individual sequences, hindering ribozyme survival. Such diversity may also constrain functional optimization through local sequence sampling. This review highlights how iterative processes of sequence selection and diversification during chemical evolution could have shaped oligonucleotide pools, facilitating sequence exploration toward sustained ribozyme-based systems.

Introduction

RNA can function both as a catalyst and an information carrier. The RNA world hypothesis views this dual role of RNA as central to the emergence of life on early Earth [1,2]. The discovery of numerous catalytic RNAs (ribozymes), including ribosomal RNA, in both natural and laboratory settings supports the plausibility of an RNA-based stage preceding the evolution of proteins [3,4]. The widespread use of nucleotide-derived cofactors in modern enzymes further reinforces this scenario [5,6]. Although a comprehensive picture remains elusive, accumulating evidence has outlined potential pathways for RNA synthesis under prebiotic conditions [7,8]. Once formed, RNA oligonucleotides could explore sequence space through nonenzymatic template-directed polymerization [9–11], ligation [12–15], and recombination [16,17]. These reactions would copy, mutate, and assemble oligonucleotides, generating diverse nucleotide compositions and lengths. Over time, such processes could yield ribozymes, including those capable of self-reproduction [18] (Fig. 1A).

The likelihood of a ribozyme arising from a pool of RNA oligonucleotides depends on sequence diversity. In principle, a more diverse pool increases the probability of containing a ribozyme. However, the mere presence of a ribozyme does not ensure its catalytic activity, persistence, or selection under prebiotic conditions. Given the astronomical number of possible sequences even for relatively small ribozymes (e.g., 450=~1030 sequences of length 50 nt), a highly diverse pool may dilute any ribozyme to concentrations too low for meaningful catalytic effects. Such rare ribozymes could also be readily lost through hydrolysis or consumed in side reactions. Excessive diversity may further hinder the thorough exploration of local sequence space needed for efficient optimization of catalysis. Thus, selective enrichment of certain nucleotide compositions, driven by chemical or environmental factors, may have been crucial for ribozyme evolution [19,20]. Conversely, excessive compositional constraint could impede ribozyme emergence, as functional sequences are likely exceedingly rare in sequence space [21,22]. A lack of diversity may also restrict sequence exploration by reducing effective RNA–RNA interactions necessary for template-directed reactions. We therefore propose that successful chemical evolution of RNA required a balance between sequence selection and diversification (Fig. 1B).

In this review, we explore the interplay between selection and diversification during oligonucleotide synthesis and subsequent non-Darwinian evolution, with a particular focus on changes in nucleotide composition. We first revisit the potential significance of constrained oligonucleotide compositions, then summarize processes that drive selection and diversification during oligonucleotide formation. Finally, we discuss how selection and diversification could operate in prebiotic, short, random-sequence oligonucleotides undergoing template-directed reactions and other environmentally driven processes. While RNA is widely considered the primary candidate for early genetic material, we also consider the dynamics of DNA oligonucleotides, which may have served as a genetic molecule alongside RNA on early Earth [23,24]. Even if not, its chemical similarity to RNA makes it a convenient model for studying oligonucleotide behavior. We also highlight studies that employed protein enzymes to investigate oligonucleotide compositional dynamics more efficiently and over longer timescales.

Potential roles of constrained oligonucleotide compositions

Highly diverse oligonucleotide sequences can be both advantageous and disadvantageous for chemical evolution. As noted above, greater sequence diversity increases the likelihood of including functional nucleic acids such as ribozymes. Consistently, most in vitro selection experiments begin with fully random sequence pools and have successfully yielded functional DNA and RNA molecules [25]. Conversely, biasing oligonucleotide compositions can focus sequence exploration around particular nucleic acid structures that may be critical for function, especially when sequence length exceeds the range for complete sequence-space sampling within a finite population [25].

Oligonucleotide composition strongly influences structural folding propensity. For example, computational RNA secondary structure predictions showed that elevated G and C content in 40-mer RNAs promoted the formation of relatively complex structures [26], whereas higher G content in 35-mers favored less common structural motifs [27]. In addition, functional RNAs are generally more folded than the average of fully random sequences [28]. Motivated by such observations, several studies have performed in vitro selection experiments starting from compositionally biased sequence pools. For example, Ruff et al. used DNA containing 60-nucleotide random regions designed to promote secondary structure formation, yielding more efficient aptamers than those from unbiased pools [29]. Similarly, Chizzolini et al. employed preselected, structurally enriched RNA containing 70-nucleotide random regions and observed higher frequencies of aptamers and potential ribozymes compared with fully random RNA libraries [28]. These findings suggest that preselection of compositionally biased, structurally predisposed oligonucleotide pools can improve access to functional sequences. Although such pools inevitably exclude certain regions of sequence space, potentially including functional ones, this limitation could have been mitigated if distinct compositional biases arose under diverse environmental conditions on early Earth, as discussed below.

A further potential advantage of constrained diversity is the reduction of combinatorial interaction complexity in iterative template-directed reactions. In general, unconstrained chemical reactions generate a vast array of products, leading to a combinatorial explosion in which molecules of interest become vanishingly diluted [30]. Strategies to limit the outcomes of random chemical reactions are therefore of central interest in origins-of-life research [31–35]. In this context, we previously simulated iterative template-directed ligation and recombination among RNA oligonucleotides with varying sequence diversities, focusing on the emergence of RNA reproduction [36]. We found that reproduction robustly occurred only at intermediate diversity levels, often through the formation of autocatalytic networks. By contrast, pools with too little or too much diversity permitted overly narrow or excessively broad sets of reaction pathways, respectively, hindering the propagation of specific RNA sequences.

Altogether, constrained oligonucleotide compositions may have played a central role in prebiotic nucleic acid evolution. Although direct evidence for optimal diversity remains limited, clarifying how certain nucleotide compositions could have been selectively enriched while retaining sufficient diversity is essential for reconstructing plausible scenarios of chemical evolution.

Compositional selection and diversification during oligonucleotide synthesis

It is generally thought that nucleotides were synthesized on early Earth from building blocks supplied through geochemical processes, originating from terrestrial or extraterrestrial sources. Current evidence suggests that these processes generated compositionally biased nucleotide mixtures. For example, nucleobases have been identified in meteorites, with abundances varying according to the type of base and meteorite [37–39]. Numerous prebiotic chemistry experiments have demonstrated the synthesis of nucleobases and nucleotides, again with widely varying efficiencies depending on the base identity, available chemical precursors, and reaction conditions, as extensively reviewed by Yadav et al. [40]. Although a prebiotically plausible environment supporting the synthesis of each of the four ribonucleotides has been identified [41], their simultaneous synthesis in a single setting remains challenging. Despite many uncertainties, it appears that different nucleotides were not equally available on early Earth.

Once nucleotides became available and were appropriately activated, they could have spontaneously oligomerized to form short sequences. Previous studies have shown that RNA oligomerization efficiency depends on base identity, activation chemistry, and reaction conditions. For example, Ertem and Ferris investigated RNA oligomerization catalyzed by montmorillonite clay using all four 5'-phosphorimidazole-activated ribonucleotides (ImpNs, N=A, G, C, or U) and observed predominant synthesis of dimers initiated with purine nucleotides [42]. Using similar oligomerization conditions, follow-up experiments with ImpA and ImpC (up to pentamers) or ImpA and ImpU (up to trimers) yielded oligomers that preferentially initiated with A and terminated with C or U [43,44]. Another line of research has examined spontaneous RNA oligomerization using 2',3'-cyclic phosphate-activated ribonucleotides (cNMPs), a form of activation readily generated via RNA hydrolysis or prebiotically plausible chemistry [45–47]. Dass et al. studied the oligomerization of binary mixtures of cNMPs under drying conditions and observed strong incorporation of G nucleotides in products up to 10-mers [48]. The high reactivity of cGMP has been further confirmed under different environmental conditions [49].

Importantly, a tendency for biased sequence production during oligomerization does not necessarily preclude the formation of compositionally balanced oligonucleotide pools. If oligomerization occurred from an already biased nucleotide pool—as is likely under realistic prebiotic conditions—nucleotide-specific incorporation tendencies could counteract the initial imbalance and result in more compositionally diverse products (Fig. 1B).

Several studies have also explored environmental conditions conducive to more balanced nucleotide incorporation during oligomerization (Fig. 2). Monnard et al. oligomerized ImpNs up to ~20-mers in an ice eutectic phase and found nearly equal incorporation of all four nucleotides [50]. Caimi et al. oligomerized cNMPs and achieved relatively balanced nucleotide compositions of up to pentamers at high pH (≥11) under wet–dry (hydration–dehydration) cycling [49]. Dirscherl et al. reported relatively balanced oligomerization of cCMP and cGMP up to 7-mers in a non-equilibrium environment mimicking a rock pore, with an air–water interface exposed to a thermal gradient [51].

While this review focuses on nucleotide compositions, other oligonucleotide properties, such as length and backbone chemistry, could also vary during prebiotic oligomerization. Typically, the oligomerization of activated nucleotides yields products biased toward shorter lengths, mostly under 20-mers [43,48–51]. However, certain geological environments, such as mineral surfaces, air–water interfaces, and hydrothermal pores, can accumulate longer sequences and could thereby counteract sequence-length biases [52–55]. In addition, nucleotide oligomerization often produces a mixture of canonical 3'–5' and noncanonical 2'–5' phosphodiester linkages [42,43,48,50]. This backbone heterogeneity is at least partly compatible with downstream processes relevant to RNA evolution, including template-directed polymerization [56], template-directed ligation and reproduction [14], and ribozyme catalysis [57]. Gradual backbone homogenization toward biologically relevant 3'–5' linkages may have occurred through selective hydrolysis of 2'–5' linkages and subsequent correction by 3'–5' ligation [58,59].

Overall, prebiotic chemistry experiments suggest that every step from nucleotide synthesis to subsequent oligomerization could have inherently selected for or against certain nucleotide compositions. Depending on nucleotide availability and environmental conditions, the resulting oligonucleotide pools could have been highly skewed or relatively diverse—variability that likely influenced the structural and functional potential of early oligonucleotides.

Compositional selection and diversification after oligonucleotide synthesis

Once diverse oligonucleotides were continuously supplied, they likely began to replicate and explore sequence space through nonenzymatic template-directed polymerization [9–11], ligation [12–15], and recombination [16,17] (Fig. 3A). Other mechanisms, such as loop-closing ligation, may also have contributed [60]. These processes could amplify, maintain, or balance existing nucleotide biases in oligonucleotide pools, thereby influencing the sequence space available for the emergence of ribozymes (Fig. 3B).

Nonenzymatic template-directed polymerization typically yields products with biased nucleotide compositions, owing to nucleotide-dependent polymerization rates and misincorporation frequencies [61]. Duzdevich et al. investigated template-directed polymerization using 2-aminoimidazole-activated nucleotides and random RNA oligonucleotide templates, and found that the resulting sequences were heavily biased toward G and C nucleotides, likely reflecting stronger base-pairing with the templates [62]. We and others have examined nonenzymatic ligation and recombination of random RNA oligonucleotides [14,17,63]. Among various ligation and recombination pathways, template-directed mechanisms were likely the most prevalent. These studies focused on 2',3'-cyclic phosphate-assisted ligation and on recombination plausibly involving the generation of the same cyclic phosphate group through spontaneous RNA hydrolysis. Incubation of random 16- or 20-mer RNA oligonucleotides under different conditions produced diverse ligation and recombination products with nucleotide biases near putative ligation junctions, although the specific patterns varied. Moreover, these products tended to form more stable secondary structures than randomized sequences of equal length and nucleotide composition [14,63]. In another study, Wu et al. studied untemplated, tetraloop-closing ligation of activated semi-random RNA oligonucleotides and observed the emergence of biased loop sequences, including common biological motifs such as UNCG and GNRA (R=A or G) [64].

Experimental studies of nonenzymatic template-directed reactions have so far been restricted to short-term evolution, owing to inherently slow kinetics, difficulties in reactivating hydrolyzed substrates, and challenges in separating products from templates—limitations that must be overcome to enable continuous reaction cycles [61]. Among these, template–product separation may be the least problematic, as several prebiotically accessible environments (e.g., low pH, high temperature, high viscosity, and low salt concentrations) can weaken template–product binding [20,65–67]. The other constraints could become less severe once suitable ribozymes are available. In line with this, Attwater et al. recently combined environmental fluctuations in pH, temperature, and salt concentration that promote template–product separation with an RNA polymerase ribozyme capable of utilizing trinucleotide substrates [68]. This system supported the continuous synthesis of short (≤~60-mer) RNA oligonucleotide mixtures, including sequences complementary to the ribozyme and random sequences progressively enriched in G and C nucleotides. G/C-rich sequences were selected despite their higher duplex stability than A/U-rich ones, likely because the polymerase ribozyme preferentially incorporated G/C-rich trinucleotides and because those trinucleotides bound to dissociated strands more effectively, preventing template–product reannealing.

More efficient iterative template-directed reactions have been achieved using protein enzymes. Although prebiotically implausible, such enzyme-based systems can serve as valuable models for exploring the compositional dynamics of oligonucleotide evolution. Kudella et al. employed a DNA ligase to perform cyclic template-directed ligation of random binary 12-mer DNA oligonucleotides composed solely of A and T, under thermal cycling conditions that promoted hybridization and melting [69]. Over time, longer ligation products displayed biased A:T ratios, favoring either A or T, along with specific nucleotide patterns near ligation junctions. At the population level, nucleotide composition shifted toward A-richness, likely amplified from an initial subtle bias. In a complementary computational study, Tkachenko and Maslov modeled iterative template-directed ligation and degradation of artificial polymers with ligation rates dependent on monomer identity, and similarly demonstrated the emergence of sequence-specific enrichment [70]. Likewise, a model developed by Göppel et al. suggested that kinetic stalling, slowed ligation caused by non-complementarity between substrate strands and templates at the ligation site, can be a primary driver of enrichment for particular biased monomer compositions or sequence patterns [71].

While many studies have demonstrated the amplification of compositional biases, others have revealed potential mechanisms to maintain or reduce initial biases. For example, Duzdevich et al. found that the nucleotide incorporation bias during nonenzymatic template-directed polymerization could be reduced by adding random-sequence oligonucleotides of varying lengths, co-activated with mononucleotides using 2-aminoimidazole and methyl isocyanide [72]. These oligonucleotides competed for templates, helped generate newly activated nucleotides, and directly ligated, leading to more compositionally balanced products. In another study, Derr et al. showed that nonenzymatic template-directed ligation using cyanogen bromide could reduce initial sequence biases [73]. Starting with compositionally biased, random 8-mer DNA oligonucleotides, ligation resulted in more balanced sequences, possibly because unbiased oligonucleotides served as more effective templates.

Bias homogenization through iterative template-directed reactions has also been studied in enzymatic systems. Serrao et al. examined how nucleotide biases in random binary 12-mer DNA oligonucleotides evolved during polymerization by a DNA polymerase [74]. The polymerase’s strand-displacing activity, combined with priming via partial hybridization or self-templation, enabled isothermal and successive sequence elongation. Starting with different initial biases in nucleotide composition (e.g., 60% A and 40% T), the extended sequences consistently exhibited reduced overall biases. Interestingly, this global homogenization coexisted with positional biases, including the emergence of periodic trimer motifs.

Finally, template-directed reactions may drive both compositional diversification and selection simultaneously. Ianeselli et al. used a DNA polymerase in a model primordial environment mimicking a heated rock pore with cyclic dew droplet formation, where fluctuating pH and salt concentrations enabled continuous template-directed polymerization through periodic template–product separation and diverse priming [75]. Starting from only a few defined DNA oligonucleotide templates, this process generated long, randomized sequences. Strikingly, these sequences evolved strong A/T biases, likely selected for their ability to facilitate strand separation and reannealing. At higher temperatures, which further promoted template–product separation, the A/T bias became less pronounced, shifting the population toward more compositionally balanced sequences.

Taken together, prebiotically relevant template-directed processes can amplify, maintain, or balance nucleotide compositional biases (Table 1). Notably, different replication mechanisms and environmental conditions could select for sequences with distinct biases, such as G/C-rich or A/T-rich ones [68,75], thereby giving rise to diverse sequences with varying functional potentials—including G/C-biased, highly structured species [26,27]. Collectively, template-directed reactions could have enabled prebiotic oligonucleotide pools to explore sequence space effectively, facilitating the emergence and enrichment of functional sequences such as ribozymes.

Environment-driven sequence selection

Oligonucleotides on early Earth were likely subjected to a wide range of environmental constraints [76]. For example, exposure to intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation, as well as hydrolysis induced by temperature, pH, or metal ions, could readily damage or degrade oligonucleotides. Both UV damage and hydrolysis have been shown to cause sequence-dependent decay of DNA or RNA oligonucleotides [77,78], potentially increasing compositional biases within oligonucleotide pools (Fig. 4). Interestingly, Kufner et al. examined the effects of UV irradiation on random-sequence DNA oligonucleotides and proposed that differential UV susceptibility among DNA sequences may have contributed to the evolution of early codons [79].

In overly harsh environments, the survival of oligonucleotides likely required protective mechanisms, such as compartmentalization [80]. Compartmentalization, the spatial segregation of oligonucleotides into defined locales, could impose sequence-selective pressures. One candidate for primitive compartments is liquid–liquid phase-separated (LLPS) droplets, whose membraneless nature allows facile molecular exchange with the environment [55,81]. LLPS droplets can sequester RNA oligonucleotides based on their physicochemical properties and are compatible with nonenzymatic polymerization and ligation of DNA or RNA, as well as ribozyme catalysis [82–85]. Wollny et al. investigated how random RNA sequences derived from mammalian cells partition into artificial LLPS (coacervate) droplets and observed preferential accumulation of relatively short (≤50 nt) specific RNA motifs, likely mediated by RNA–RNA hybridization [86] (Fig. 4). In another study, Bartolucci et al. used defined DNA oligonucleotides with varying degrees of complementarity and demonstrated the selection of specific sequences via spontaneous phase separation, especially when coupled with cyclic oligonucleotide exchange with the environment [87]. Computational analyses suggested that this principle could also apply to random sequences as well. Other geochemically relevant environments that accumulate oligonucleotides, such as mineral surfaces and air–water interfaces [55], may likewise contribute to sequence selection.

Conclusions and future perspectives

This review has summarized how primitive oligonucleotide compositions could have been shaped during their synthesis and subsequent sequence exploration. At each stage of chemical evolution, specific oligonucleotide sequences may have been selectively generated, retained, or scrambled, depending on synthetic pathways and geochemical locales. Consequently, the degree of sequence diversity likely varied across environments and evolutionary timescales. Appropriately constrained diversity could increase the probability of accessing catalytic nucleic acids, such as ribozymes, with the optimal degree of diversity likely depending on the target function or structure.

Under laboratory conditions, sequence exploration based on prebiotically relevant nonenzymatic reactions remains limited to short timescales. Although experiments with protein enzymes have provided valuable insights into possible pathways of oligonucleotide evolution, they represent prebiotically unrealistic scenarios. The next key step is therefore to identify prebiotically plausible environmental conditions or chemical moieties capable of sustaining iterative, nonenzymatic template-directed reactions.

In addition, the influence of environmental parameters, such as temperature, pH, and the types and concentrations of metal ions, on sequence exploration in random oligonucleotide pools remains to be investigated more systematically. While most studies have focused on fixed conditions, varying these parameters within a single experimental setup could more directly reveal how different environments steer sequence populations toward distinct compositional spaces [75].

During nonenzymatic sequence exploration, random RNA oligonucleotide mixtures may already contain simple, short catalytic RNAs [14,88,89] or assemble into larger, more complex ribozymes [90–95]. These RNAs could utilize metal cofactors that originally facilitated nonenzymatic reactions [6]. A relatively unexplored avenue is the potential cooperation between RNA catalysis and random oligonucleotides, whereby ribozymes exploit sequence mixtures to enhance their activity. A recent study by Han and Müller demonstrated RNAs that become catalytically active by recruiting random DNA oligonucleotides as cofactors [95], offering a glimpse into how ribozymes could emerge within random oligonucleotide pools. Future work should more thoroughly investigate how random oligonucleotides themselves may help activate ribozymes.

A crucial remaining question is how a randomly generated ribozyme could be selected from an oligonucleotide population—without manual intervention, as is typical in laboratory selection experiments [25]. For selection to occur, an RNA’s catalytic activity must be linked to an increased probability of its own reproduction, either directly (autocatalysis) or indirectly. Several autocatalytic RNAs have been identified, ranging from simple oligonucleotides to complex ribozymes [14,90,94,96], and evolutionary pathways for their emergence have been proposed [18,97]. Many indirect contributions to reproduction are also conceivable, particularly in highly heterogeneous mixtures, such as substrate regeneration or mismatch correction during template-directed reactions [98]. In all cases, appropriately constrained oligonucleotide compositions may facilitate specific RNA reproduction by reducing excessive off-target catalysis [36]. Such constraint could also ensure the presence of a sufficient number of identical sequences within the population—another prerequisite for reliable reproduction. Demonstrating the spontaneous emergence and selective amplification of functional RNAs from random oligonucleotides remains a key goal for future studies.
