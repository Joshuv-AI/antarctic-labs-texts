Uncomplicated robotic-assisted laparoscopic hysterectomy (RALH) in a peritoneal dialysis patient with endometrial cancer and clinical obesity - a case report
Authors: Sarah Halmer, Susanne Schubert, Martin Wiesholzer, Radovan Pilka, Birgit Pfaller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMajor abdominal surgery frequently leads to peritoneal dialysis (PD) dropout and technique failure, owing to complications that compromise the integrity of the abdominal wall or the peritoneal cavity.Case presentationWe report the first case of an uncomplicated robotic-assisted laparoscopic total hysterectomy for endometrial cancer (EC) in a 46-year-old PD patient with clinical obesity (Body Mass Index 38 kg/m2). Following a short-term planned transfer to hemodialysis in the immediate postoperative period, she was able to resume her regular PD regime, demonstrating that technique survival is feasible despite major surgery and multiple risk factors.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that robotic surgery can be safely performed in a PD patient with endometrial cancer, when supported by careful planning and close interdisciplinary collaboration.

Background

Patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD) may require abdominal surgery for various indications. Surgical interventions pose a significant risk for technique failure and the need for permanent transfer to hemodialysis (HD) for patients undergoing PD, mainly due to the risk of infectious and mechanical complications such as dialysate leakage, hernia formation, and ultrafiltration failure. Faster recovery, reduced surgical trauma and a lower risk of adhesions make laparoscopic surgery a favorable technique for PD patients requiring appendicectomy, nephrectomy or cholecystectomy [1]. Minimally invasive approaches, including laparoscopic and robotic-assisted laparoscopic hysterectomy (RALH) are widely performed for both benign and malignant gynecological conditions, including endometrial cancer. However, there are limited reports of PD patients who have successfully undergone hysterectomy and were able to continue PD postoperatively.

Case report

A 46-year-old female patient on PD underwent dilatation and curettage due to endometrial hyperplasia. Her medical history was significant for clinical obesity (Body Mass Index 38 kg/m2), arterial hypertension and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM). She was first diagnosed with chronic kidney disease of unknown cause in 2015 and has been on PD since 2021. Weight loss associated with Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1) agonist treatment led to cuff migration and a tunnel infection, for which she had previously undergone cuff shaving. Histology confirmed a well-differentiated adenocarcinoma (G1, MX), requiring surgical treatment. Pre-operative work-up included expert ultrasound examination, as well as computed tomography of the thorax and abdomen and magnetic resonance imaging of the pelvis. The patient initially expressed concern about the possibility of permanent transfer to HD. At her request, and following close consultation with the dedicated PD team, the hysterectomy was planned with an experienced robotic surgeon. The multidisciplinary team approach supported her informed decision-making. She was reassured that resumption of PD after surgery would likely be possible. The decision was made to electively plan a postoperative PD interruption with transfer to HD, due to anticipated wound healing complications secondary to T2DM, as well as a high risk of dialysate leakage from increased intra-abdominal pressure associated with clinical obesity. Robotic-assisted laparoscopic total hysterectomy (RALH) was performed under general anesthesia. Prophylactic antibiotics (cefuroxime 1.500 g and metronidazole 500 mg) were administered prior to skin incision and continued for 14 days postoperatively due to the increased risk of peritonitis and port-site infections in patients with chronic kidney disease receiving PD [1, 2]. The patient had a preserved residual urine output of approximately 2000 ml per day; therefore, a transurethral catheter was inserted for intraoperative bladder drainage. The abdominal skin including the area around the PD catheter, was disinfected, and the PD catheter was covered with a sterile dressing to ensure sterility. A Veress needle was used for the induction of a pneumoperitoneum; an 8-mm port was placed through the umbilicus, and three additional 8-mm trocars and one 12-mm AirSeal trocar were positioned in a horizontal line. Due to the position of the dialysis catheter, one trocar was placed slightly more cranially. The PD catheter kept slipping into the surgical field, so it was secured to the ventral abdominal wall during surgery (Fig. 1). The omentum was found to be adherent to the umbilicus, these adhesions were released and a small umbilical hernia was closed using non-absorbable sutures without mesh implantation to avoid introducing foreign material in a patient receiving PD. The peritoneum was opened for sentinel lymph node mapping and hysterectomy. The uterus was of normal size for a premenopausal woman and the peritoneal membrane appeared unremarkable. Dissection was complicated by a mildly increased bleeding tendency; however, no major hemorrhage occurred. A continuous V-LocTM suture was used to carefully close the peritoneum over the vaginal cuff as well as the dissection sites of the sentinel lymph nodes. At the end of the procedure, the PD catheter was repositioned into the pelvic cavity to ensure optimal dialysate drainage. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and she was discharged on day four with scheduled outpatient HD sessions, during which the PD catheter was flushed regularly by the PD nursing team to ensure patency. Peritoneal dialysis was resumed using low-volume cycler therapy (filling volume 1000 ml) with close monitoring for leakage or wound complications to minimize intra-abdominal pressure in the early postoperative phase. After confirming the absence of leakage, dialysate volumes were gradually increased and a daytime dwell reintroduced, with full-dose PD resumed six weeks postoperatively.

The final histological examination confirmed a well-differentiated endometrioid adenocarcinoma, FIGO 2023 stage IA2, pN0 (0/3), L0, V0, R0, microsatellite stable (MSS), and p53 wildtype. No adjuvant oncological treatment was indicated, and oncological follow-up was recommended.

No infectious, mechanical or wound healing complications were reported during her recovery and at present, six months post-surgery, she remains free of any adverse events.

Discussion

This case underscores the feasibility of considering robotic-assisted laparoscopic hysterectomy (RALH) in a peritoneal dialysis patient with endometrial cancer and clinical obesity [3]. To our knowledge, only three similar cases have been described in the literature, all of which were associated with postoperative complications affecting PD continuation or outcomes. Kakuda et al. described a conventional laparoscopic hysterectomy in a PD patient complicated by dialysate leakage, resulting in permanent discontinuation of PD [4]. Han et al. reported postoperative catheter occlusion following laparoscopic hysterectomy requiring prolonged HD and subsequent PD catheter replacement [5]. Lew et al. demonstrated early resumption of PD on postoperative day three after RALH; however, the course was complicated by peritonitis [6]. The main advantages of laparoscopy compared to open surgery in PD patients are the preservation of the peritoneum, minimal trauma to the abdominal wall and therefore reduced risk of dialysate leakage. Recommendations for resumption of PD are unclear and range from immediately postoperatively to 28 days after surgery [1, 7]. Detailed suturing and avoiding manipulation of the Tenckhoff catheter may reduce the risk of a leak. Robotic surgery allows improved surgical accuracy and superior three-dimensional visualization compared with conventional laparoscopy and has demonstrated favorable perioperative outcomes in obese patients with EC, supporting its role in complex surgical settings [8]. Overall, the enhanced precision, reduced blood loss and minimal tissue trauma associated with robotic surgery during retroperitoneal dissection may facilitate early resumption of PD [9]. An increasing incidence of EC also in young women highlights the need for ongoing surgical innovation and treatment planning, especially in complex cohorts like patients on PD [8, 10]. Emergency laparotomy in patients requiring PD frequently results in permanent conversion to HD due to infectious complications, dialysate leakage and catheter dysfunction. However, with the widespread adoption of minimally invasive techniques and elective surgical planning, laparoscopic approaches have increasingly enabled preservation and resumption of PD [11]. In this case, intermittent HD transfer was required for the immediate postoperative period due to individual patient risk factors including clinical obesity and T2DM. We consider surgical expertise, careful technique and interdisciplinary collaboration between the surgical team and the PD team to be key determinants of outcome.

Conclusion

Laparoscopic techniques are well documented for general abdominal surgery, data on hysterectomies in PD patients remain limited. This case highlights that interdisciplinary collaboration, and careful planning can lead to successful outcomes. Larger cohort studies are needed to support the use of RALH in this population – both to reduce unnecessary PD dropout and to strengthen evidence for avoiding intermittent HD transfer.
