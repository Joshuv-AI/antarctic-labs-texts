Relation of glucose variability on mortality from any cause and cardiovascular diseases in individuals with type 2 diabetes: mediation analysis of echocardiographic parameters
Authors: Cheng-Chieh Lin, Chia-Ing Li, Chiu-Shong Liu, Chih-Hsueh Lin, Shing-Yu Yang, Tsai-Chung Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to explore the relationship between glucose variability (GV) and echocardiographic parameters and to investigate the mediating effect of echocardiographic parameters in the association of GV with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we acquired data from the electronic health records of individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) who visited a medical center during 2001–2020 (Jan to Oct) with follow-up until December 31, 2021. Cox proportional hazards models were conducted to explore the relationship of GV and echocardiographic parameters and mortality. We employed mediation analysis to assess the role of echocardiographic parameters in linking GV to mortality.ResultsThere were 3,996 patients with T2DM. Both variability in HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) were significantly linked with mortality. FPG variability was correlated with left ventricular mass index (LVMI), left ventricular mass (LVM), e’, and tricuspid regurgitation (TR) velocity according to multivariable regression analyses. At a mean follow-up of 5.2 years, FPG variability, LVM, LVMI, s’, and TR velocity were independently linked with all-cause and expanded cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. LVM, LVMI, s’, and TR velocity mediated 3.04%, 3.75%, 2.19%, and 2.71% of the association between FPG variability and mortality from any cause, respectively; and s’ and TR velocity mediated 4.33% and 2.72% of the effect of FPG variability on expanded CVD mortality, respectively. All the tested echocardiographic parameters were not significant mediators of the relationship of HbA1c-CV with mortality from any causes and expanded CVD.ConclusionsOur study found a weak mediating effect of the echocardiographic parameters of cardiac structure and function in the association between FPG variability and mortality, but not in the association between HbA1c variability and mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05182-w.Keywords: Glucose variability, Mortality, Echocardiographic parameters, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Mediation analysis

Background

The number of people with diabetes worldwide grows continuously due to global population aging, urbanization, unhealthy diet, increased obesity prevalence, and reduced physical activity, making it a significant public health concern [1]. Diabetes mellitus (DM) is linked to an elevated risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including cardiac dysfunction and heart failure (HF), which is the major cause of all-cause and CVD mortality [2]. High blood glucose causes direct adverse alteration in cardiac morphology and performance, a phenomenon termed as diabetic cardiomyopathy, and eventually leads to severe HF [3]. DM is Linked to a higher Likelihood of HF and has been Linked to 10-fold higher mortality compared with DM without HF [4]. Therefore, targeting HF risk factors in patients with DM is critical to prevent early death.

Echocardiography could be clinically useful for the assessment of cardiac morphology and performance in the treatment and diagnosis monitoring of heart diseases, including HF. Previous studies demonstrated glucose variability (GV) has been linked to echocardiographic parameters in individuals with DM, showing GV’s ability to predict left ventricular (LV) diastolic dysfunction [5–8], LV adverse remodeling [7, 9], and left cardiac structure and function changes [10]. Echocardiographic parameters may be useful for the early recognition of preclinical abnormalities and are also important predictors of Survival. Epidemiological studies showed that echocardiographic parameters can predict mortality from any cause and CVD mortality in individuals with type 2 (T2) DM [11], HFpEF [12], and normal ankle–brachial index [13]; general population [14, 15]; outpatients and hospitalized inpatients [16, 17]; and patients with HF [18]. All these results indicated that persons with high-risk echocardiographic features have a high risk of all-cause and CVD mortality.

GV is associated with echocardiographic parameters and all-cause and CVD mortality. Recent systematic reviews demonstrated that GV is also associated with mortality from any cause and CVD in individuals with DM [19, 20]. However, whether GV adds to the increased risk of mortality from any cause and CVD mediated by echocardiographic parameters has never been evaluated. Mediation analysis in research is crucial for understanding mechanisms, making policies and developing interventions, developing precision medicine approaches, enhancing predictive models, and advancing science. It also helps in understanding the underlying mechanisms or pathways through which an independent variable (such as GV in diabetes) influences a dependent variable (such as mortality). This insight goes beyond mere correlation, providing a deep understanding of how and why certain relationships exist. By identifying the intermediary variables (mediators) explaining the relationship between the independent and dependent variables, mediation analysis can inform the development of targeted interventions or policies. For instance, if certain echocardiographic parameters mediate the relationship between GV and mortality, interventions targeting these parameters could potentially mitigate mortality risks. Mediation analysis also aids in the development of personalized or precision medicine approaches. By knowing the specific mediators that are critical in disease progression or outcomes, healthcare providers can effectively tailor treatments or management strategies to individual patients. By accounting for mediating variables, predictive models can accurately forecast outcomes and identify at-risk populations. Mediation analysis contributes to scientific advancement by refining existing theories and generating new hypotheses. However, the association between GV and mortality mediated by echocardiographic parameters was not yet explored. Hence, we carried out a retrospective cohort study to assess the potential mediation effects of echocardiographic parameters on the association of GV with all-cause and expanded CVD mortality among patients with type 2 DM (T2DM).

Methods

Study subjects

This longitudinal retrospective cohort study consisted of all individuals with T2DM who underwent serial screening echocardiography from January 2001 to October 2020 at China Medical University Hospital (CMUH), Taiwan. All the individuals were diagnosed with DM derived from the International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM diagnosis code 250). Individuals excluded from the study were individuals younger than 30 years old, those who had not undergone echocardiographic screening, those with type 1 diabetes (250.x1/x3) or gestational diabetes (648.83). The spanning period for patient enrollment was from 2001 to 2020 (Jan-Oct). Participants were followed from the index date until study withdrawal, study end (Dec. 31, 2021), or death. Index date was defined as one year prior to the date of the first assessment for echocardiographic parameters. Aiming to minimize diabetes-related complications and mortality, the Diabetes care management program (DCMP) focuses on delivering high-quality, continuous care through intensive patient monitoring. The enrollment period was selected to maximize the number of eligible study participants and ensure a minimum of one year of follow-up, thereby reducing the risk of reverse causality. The study cohort was dynamic (open), meaning that participants entered the study at varying time points and had differing lengths of follow-up. Data for the covariates measured on the date closest to the first assessment for echocardiographic parameters were extracted from the DCMP database and electronic health record as baseline. The patients who had missing data of laboratory examination, anthropometric measurements, (n = 957), and GV information (n = 1,616) were further excluded. From the available dataset, 3,996 enrolled patients with diabetes met the above inclusion criteria (Supplemental Figure S1). Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Review Board of CMUH (CMUH113-REC1-216).

Measurements

Upon enrollment in the DCMP, participants underwent medical assessments including urine and blood tests, body measurements, and an interview with a managed care nurse. The interview covered dietary habits, lifestyle, and medical history—both past and current—using a standardized computerized questionnaire. Clinical tests were conducted annually as part of routine care. The following baseline information was collected for all the included subjects: social and demographic factors (age and gender), lifestyle habits (alcohol drinking, smoking, and exercise), diabetes-associated factors (duration DM and diabetes onset age), types of anti-diabetes treatments, medication-related variables, variables for self-are, comorbidities and diabetic complications, variables for anthropometric measurements, biomarkers, GV, and echocardiographic parameters.

Sociodemographic factors included age at first echocardiography (at baseline) and gender. Lifestyle habits included status of passive smoking, smoking, physical activity, and alcohol drinking. Each variable was classified into two classes according to the study subjects’ self-reports: yes versus no. Diabetes-associated factors included early onset of T2DM, defined as diabetes onset age < 40 years, and T2DM duration as duration since DM diagnosis. Types of anti-diabetes treatments included oral treatments of seven classes: meglitinide, sulfonylurea, α-glucosidase inhibitor, biguanide, dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitor, insulin sensitizer, and other compounds. Six types of insulin shot were considered: insulin aspart, regular insulin, neutral protamine Hagedorn insulin, levemir, lantus, and glucagon-like peptide-1 analogue. Types of anti-diabetes treatments were divided into four groups based on electronic medical record: oral hypoglycemic drug, inject insulin, both (oral hypoglycemic drug plus inject insulin), and no (diet or exercise). Comorbidities consisted of short-term complications (such as severe hypoglycemia, hyperglycemic hyperosmolar nonketotic coma [HHNK], and ketoacidotic diabetic episode) and long-term complications (such as peripheral neuropathy, stroke, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, severe hypoglycemia, and nephropathy). Data were extracted from the DCMP dataset during interview. Short- and long-term comorbidities were each classified into two categories: yes versus no. Other treatment variables included blood pressure-lowering agents (e.g., calcium antagonists), lipid-lowering drugs (e.g., statins), and cardiovascular medications. Each medication was classified into two classes according to electronic health records: yes versus no. Body measurement variables included height, weight, and body mass index (BMI). Height and weight were measured using an electronic anthropometric device (super-view, HW-666) while the participants were shoeless and dressed in light attire. BMI was derived from the equation: weight (kg)/(height)2 (m2).

GV

Fasting blood samples were taken from an antecubital vein and processed within 4 h. Biological markers fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) were processed using a bio-chemical auto-analyzer (Beckman Coulter Synchron system, Lx-20, Fullerton, CA, USA). HbA1c was assessed by high-performance liquid chromatography using boronate-affinity (normal range: 4.6–6.5%). HbA1c inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) for were 2.91% for normal, 1.79% for intermediate, and 1.09% for high levels. FPG was measured in blood obtained using NAF TUBE, which contains 5 mg of sodium fluoride to inhibit glucose metabolism and 4 mg of potassium oxalate to chelate calcium and prevent coagulation. The inter- and intra-assay CVs for FPG were both 4%. The GV evaluated by HbA1c or FPG variability was calculated as CV. The CVs for HbaA1c and FPG were calculated as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean. The CV was divided by inverse of to correct for the different measurement count per person [21]. These GV variables were calculated based on at least two measurements of HbA1c and FPG obtained at 1-year intervals following the index date.

Echocardiographic examinations

Echocardiographic assessments were performed by a senior cardiologist employing the transthoracic echocardiography system (Vivid 7, GE Medical Systems, Horten, Norway) equipped with a 3.5 MHz transducer under established protocol. The parameters were evaluated in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines [22–24]. Patients were positioned in the left lateral decubitus position and breathed quietly. Standardized two-dimensional guided M-mode images were obtained. Doppler sample volume was placed at the mitral leaflet tips to record left ventricular inflow from the apical four chamber view, ensuring alignment with flow. Tissue Doppler imaging was performed at the lateral mitral annulus, with wall filter and gain adjusted to minimize noise. All echocardiographic measurements and report data, including cardiac morphology, cardiac diastolic, and cardiac systolic parameters, were collected. The cardiac morphologic parameters included left ventricular diastolic diameter (LVDd), left atrium diameter (LAD), left ventricular systolic diameter (LVSd), left ventricular mass (LVM), left ventricular mass index (LVMI), and left atrial volume index (LAVI). The cardiac systolic parameters included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and systolic myocardial velocity (s’) were qualitatively assessed. The cardiac diastolic parameters consisted of atrial diastolic velocity (A), early diastolic myocardial velocity (e’), speed of early transmitral flow (E), E/A ratio, E/e’ ratio, deceleration time, and tricuspid regurgitation (TR) velocity.

Outcome ascertainment

This study examined deaths from any causes and expanded CVD ascertained annually via record Linkage with the National Death Registry employing personal ID and birth dates from the Taiwan Ministry of Health and Welfare. Participants were followed from the index date until death, withdrawal from the DCMP, or Dec. 31, 2021 or until death or. The underlying causes of death from 2001 to 2008 and from 2009 to 2021 were coded in accordance with ICD-10-CM and ICD-9-CM, respectively. Mortality from expanded CVD was considered to be death due to diabetes (ICD-10-CM codes E10– E14, ICD-9-CM code 250), CVD (ICD-10-CM codes I00–I99, ICD-9-CM codes 390–459), and kidney diseases (ICD-10-CM N00–N29, ICD-9-CM 580–589). These codes have been validated and used in prior studies [25].

Statistical analysis

Sociodemographic factors and clinical characteristics were reported as count (proportion) for categorical factors and mean ± standard deviation for continuous factors. Bivariate analysis involved t-tests for continuous data and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data. Multiple linear regression models were used to evaluate the association between GV and echocardiographic parameters, controlling for sociodemographic variables and clinical characteristic. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression was employed to examine the associations of GV and echocardiographic parameters with all-cause and expanded CVD mortality by reporting hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The following multivariate model-building steps proposed by Hosmer and Lemeshow [26] guided our data analysis. The built multivariate analyses were sequentially controlled for sociodemographic variables, lifestyle behaviors, diabetes-associated factors, and baseline comorbidities and biomarkers.

Baron and Kenny’s (1986) steps were employed for mediation analysis [27] to determine the direct and indirect effects of GV on mortality through echocardiographic parameters. First, mortality from any causes or expanded CVD was regressed on GV to verify the latter as a significant determinant of the former. Second, echocardiographic parameters were regressed on GV to make sure the latter is a significant determinant of the former. If echocardiographic parameters are not linked to mortality from any causes or expanded CVD, in that case, they cannot serve as mediators. Third, mortality from any causes or expanded CVD was simultaneously regressed on GV and echocardiographic parameters to assess whether echocardiographic parameters are significant determinants of mortality from any causes or expanded CVD and check whether the GV coefficients in absolute values are smaller than the original GV coefficient from the first step. The findings can lead to the conclusion that echocardiographic parameters serve as mediators in the association between GV and mortality from any causes or expanded CVD. The bootstrapping method [28] was employed to assess the significance of the mediation effect—that is, whether the association between GV and mortality from any causes or expanded CVD is significantly attenuated after accounting for echocardiographic parameters. Direct and indirect effect estimates on mortality from any causes or expanded CVD were derived using the Cox proportional hazards models. Direct, indirect, and total, effects were calculated as HRs with 95% CIs and proportion mediated effect (PME) with 95% CIs. PME was defined as 100%, a measure relative to the total effect. The contribution of direct and indirect effects to GV effect was estimated on the ln (HR) scale as the effects were additive on this scale. Bootstrapping was used with 1000 bootstrap samples to obtain 95% CIs for the total effect and indirect effects. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). All p-values were calculated using two-tailed test with values less than 0.05 regarded as statistically significant.

Following an average follow-up period of 5.2 years (20736 person-years), 464 expanded CVD deaths and 963 all-cause deaths were recorded. Relative to the survivors, the patients who died were older; had higher BMI, longer diabetes duration, coronary artery disease, hypertension, stroke, peripheral neuropathy, severe hypoglycemia, HHNK, and nephropathy; took more medications for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and CVDs; and showed higher FPG levels.

The multivariable-adjusted HRs and 95% CIs of per one SD increase in HbA1c-CV, FPG-CV, and all echocardiographic parameters for mortality from any causes and expanded CVD are shown in Fig. 1. In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, FPG-CV was the strongest determinant of mortality from any causes and expanded CVD (HRs and 95% CIs per one SD: 1.49, 1.41–1.58; 1.54, 1.42–1.66, respectively), after accounting for sociodemographic variables, lifestyle habits, diabetes-associated factors, comorbidities, and medication-related variables. HbA1c-CV was linked to mortality from any causes and expanded CVD (1.19, 1.11–1.27 per-1SD; and 1.19, 1.08–1.31, respectively).

Association of glucose variability and echocardiographic parameters with mortality in persons with type 2 diabetes

The results of mediation analysis of echocardiographic parameters for FPG-CV and mortality are shown in Tables 3, and those for HbA1c-CV and mortality are displayed in Supplemental Table S1. The total effects of FPG-CV for mortality from any causes and expanded CVD were 41% (95% CI 35–48%) and 44% (36–52%), respectively (Table 3). LVM, LVMI, s’, and TR velocity significantly mediated approximately 3.04% (0.28–6.10%), 3.75% (0.98–6.96%), 2.19% (0.33–4.13%), and 2.71% (0.40–5.28%) of the total effect of FPG-CV on mortality from any causes, respectively. Meanwhile, s’ and TR velocity significantly mediated 4.33% (95% CI 0.46–8.49%) and 2.72% (0.29–6.13%) of the total effect of FPG-CV on mortality from expanded CVD, respectively. The total effects of HbA1c-CV were 18% (12–26%) and 18% (9–28%) for mortality from any causes and expanded CVD, respectively (Supplemental Table S1). Although LVSd, LVEF and TR velocity accounted for a larger proportion of the mediation effect, their point estimates for mediating the association between HbA1c-CV and both all-cause and expanded CVD mortality were not statistically significant. Among these, LVSd had the largest mediating effect on the association of HbA1c-CV with all-cause and expanded CVD mortality (7.20%, − 3.39–17.92%; 9.61%, − 10.11–29.84%, respectively), followed by LVEF (6.10% CI − 4.98–17.16%; 7%, 7.66% CI − 15.82% to 28.75, respectively) and TR velocity (5.71%, 95% CI − 0.74–13.50%; 7.90%, 95% CI − 0.84–21.25%, respectively). All the tested echocardiographic parameters were not significant mediators of the relationship of HbA1c-CV with mortality from any causes and expanded CVD.

Discussion

Our study of individuals with type 2 DM found that both HbA1c and FPG variability were significantly Linked to mortality from any causes and expanded CVD. In addition, the association between echocardiographic parameters with mortality from any causes and expanded CVD, was consistent with previous reported. The estimated HRs of mortality from any causes and expanded CVD were 1.5 per one SD increase in FPG-CV and 1.2 per one SD increase in HbA1c-CV. The total effects of FPG-CV for mortality from any causes and expanded CVD were 41% (95% CI 35–48%) and 44% (36–52%), respectively; and those of HbA1c-CV were 18% (12–26%) and 18% (9–28%) for mortality from any causes and expanded CVD, respectively. Mediation analysis demonstrated that LVM, LVMI, s’, and TR velocity mediated 3.04%, 3.75%, 2.19%, and 2.71%, respectively, of the relationship between FPG-CV and mortality from any causes. Only s’ and TR velocity mediated the relationship between FPG-CV and expanded CVD mortality (4.33% and 2.72%, respectively). These results indicated a weak but statistically significant mediation effect of echocardiographic parameters on the association between FPG-CV and all-cause mortality and expanded CVD, but not on the association between HbA1c-CV and all-cause mortality and expanded CVD.

Understanding the potential mediation pathways leading from GV to mortality from any causes and expanded CVD is important to avoid early death among patients with T2DM. This line of research can provide suggestions for future research to find potential effects and glycemic control targets. Mediation analysis has emerged as a powerful statistical tool to examine the total effect of exposure on the outcome, the direct effect of exposure on the outcome without any mediator, and the indirect effect of exposure on the outcome through a mediator [29, 30]. A thorough review of mediation literature revealed that four studies employed the mediation analysis of echocardiographic parameters on cardiovascular events or mortality [31–34]. These studies focused on the following: association between etiology with mortality with LVEF as the mediator [32]; fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF-23) with atrial arrhythmia, congestive heart failure (CHF), and mortality with left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) as mediators [33]; US patients versus Japanese patients with CVD events with LV structure and function as the mediator [34]; and primary ventricular fibrillation with 30-day mortality with LVEF as the mediator [31]. An observational cohort investigation reported that LVEF mediated 19% of the total effect of coronary artery disease on mortality in patients with CHF [32]. A prior study indicated that LVH mediated 11.2%, 7.4%, and 21.9% of the effect of FGF-23 on atrial fibrillation, mortality from any causes, and CHF in individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD), respectively. An international comparative research showed that ejection fraction, LVMI, and their combination mediated the observed CVD difference (50%, 27%, and 57%, respectively) and CHF (62%, 33%, and 70%, respectively) in patients with CKD [34]. An observational single-center study reported that LVEF mediated 62.7% of the effect of TW-Lambda pattern on mortality within 30 days among individuals experiencing ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction [31]. As far as we are aware, this study is the first to evaluate the mediatory effect of echocardiographic parameters on the association between GV and death outcomes. Our study found that cardiac morphological parameters (LVM and LVMI), cardiac systolic function (s’), and cardiac diastolic functions (TR velocity) mediated the relationship between FPG-CV and all-cause mortality in patients with T2DM. This role was not observed for LVEF.

Impaired diastolic function and increased LV mass are common in patients with diabetes [35–37]. In our T2DM cohort, LVM, LVMI, s’, and TR velocity played important mediating roles in GV–mortality relation. Hyperglycemia facilitates the built-up of harmful glycation products [38] in circulating blood and various tissues; elevation of aldosterone concentration in serum that causes myocyte growth and extracellular matrix changes; and activation of cytokines and angiotensin II that triggers endothelial damage, oxidative stress, and disease pathology, resulting in myocardial fibrosis and a subsequent rise in LV mass [39–41]. In addition, By reducing the elasticity and compliance of the ventricle, the presence of myocardial fibrosis may lead to impaired diastolic dysfunction, which may precede the development of systolic dysfunction [42].

Our findings indicated that the total effects of FPG-CV on mortality from any causes and expanded CVD are greater than those of HbA1c-CV. We also observed the mediating effect of the echocardiographic parameters of cardiac morphology and performance, such as LVMI, LVM, s’, and TR velocity, on the relationship of FPG variability with mortality from any causes and cardiovascular. However, no such mediating effect was observed on the relationship between HbA1c variability and mortality from any causes and cardiovascular. This discrepancy may be due to the fact that HbA1c indicates the mean blood glucose concentration over the previous 2–3 months, making visit-to-visit variation in HbA1c insufficiently sensitive to capture short-term glucose fluctuations. By contrast, FPG measures glucose concentration in the blood after an overnight fast, providing a snapshot of an individual’s blood sugar level at a single point in time when he/she has not eaten. Conversely, HbA1c reflects the average blood glucose levels over the previous 2–3 months. The erythrocytic hemoglobin binds to glucose; the higher the glucose concentration in the blood, the higher the HbA1c level. This parameter provides an overall indication of blood sugar control over time and is commonly used for monitoring long-term blood sugar regulation.

Although these effects are small, they are robust and consistent across models incorporating several cardiac structure and function markers of echocardiographic parameters, suggesting the presence of underlying mechanisms that warrant further investigation. In complex pathways leading to mortality, even modest mediating effects—especially those that are modifiable—can cumulatively contribute to risk reduction when addressed at the population level. While these results may not yet justify immediate clinical action, they can serve as valuable indicators of potential intervention points or early signals of risk. Nonetheless, we acknowledge the limited practice significance of these findings. Although the mediation effects are statistically significant, they should be interpreted with caution and regarded as exploratory rather than conclusive evidence of clinically meaningful pathways. Future research should aim to replicate these findings in larger and more diverse populations and to explore whether these small effects accumulate over time or interact with other risk factors in meaningful ways.

The main strength of our study was the use of repeated measures of outpatient FPG or HbA1c levels within 1 year to explore the association between GV and mortality. Other strengths included a hospital-based setting, a large sample size, accredited echocardiographic laboratories, and high-quality mortality variable from the national death registry. Several limitations of our study must also be mentioned. First, our research is a single-site retrospective study, and the participants experienced echocardiography. Hence, the findings might be affected by selection bias. However, the use of echocardiographic examination in the community setting or diverse several clinical settings can be challenging and is often not feasible. Data obtained from various clinical settings may introduce measurement bias due to the lack of standardized procedures. Second, GV was evaluated using CV representing an overall indicator of variability, instead of a complete trajectory through time. CV is a useful indicator of GV in clinical practice because of its simplicity, relative familiarity, and unambiguity [43]. It is intended to standardize and account for variability across individuals with varying mean glucose levels and to identify individuals at a high risk of hypoglycemia [44]. Third, our study’s findings could have restricted applicability to patients with T2DM but may be valid for those who had indications for echocardiographic measurements. Fourth, baseline covariates were defined based on the measurements closest to the date of the first echocardiographic assessment. As a result, these covariates may have been measured at different times across individuals—potentially even years apart—introducing the possibility of misclassification bias, particularly if factors Such as BMI or medication use changed significantly prior to the echocardiographic assessment. However, all sociodemographic, Lifestyle, comorbidity, and medication data were collected annually, which helps to minimize this potential bias. In our cohort, the covariates were measured within a one-year interval, with a mean interval of 0.87 years. Last, due to the nature of study design, a treadmill exercise test or other diagnostic evaluation for coronary artery disease (CAD) were not performed. Therefore, we could not definitively exclude individuals with suspicious for CAD. However, individuals with a self-reported diagnosis of CAD, as assessed by questionnaire, were identified and considered in the multivariate analysis.

Conclusion

This study examined the association of GV and echocardiographic parameters with mortality from any causes and expanded CVD in individuals with T2DM. Among individuals with T2DM, the increased mortality associated with visit-to-visit GV in FPG was partially explained by abnormalities in cardiac morphology and performance such as LVMI, LVM, s’, and TR velocity. All the tested echocardiographic parameters were not significant mediators of the relationship of HbA1c-CV with mortality from any causes and expanded CVD. People who have T2DM may need regular blood glucose monitoring and echocardiographic examination to check their cardiac structure and function, especially diastolic dysfunction.
