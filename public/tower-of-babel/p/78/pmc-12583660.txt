PARP1-TRPM2-PKC cascade distinctly regulates reactive astrogliosis and clasmatodendrosis through NF-κB and AKT pathways in the hippocampus of chronic epilepsy rats
Authors: Ji-Eun Kim, Su Hyeon Wang, Tae-Cheon Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe epileptic hippocampi of rodent models show reactive astrogliosis and autophagic astroglial death (clasmatodendrosis) in the CA1 region, which contributes to spontaneous seizures. Oxidative stress upregulates transient receptor potential melastatin 2 (TRPM2, a non-selective Ca2+ permeable cation channel) in astrocytes. However, the roles of TRPM2 in reactive astrogliosis and clasmatodendrosis in the epileptic hippocampus are largely unknown. In the present study, we found that TRPM2 was upregulated in astrocytes within the CA1 region of chronic epilepsy rats. N-acetylcysteine (NAC, an antioxidant) and PJ34, a poly-(ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP1) inhibitor, downregulated TRPM2 expression in reactive astrocytes and attenuated clasmatodendritic degeneration in this region, accompanied by reduced nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) p65 serine (S) 311 phosphorylation. Both NAC and PJ34 increased pleckstrin homology domain and leucine-rich repeat protein phosphatase 1 (PHLPP1, a phosphatase for AKT) expression concomitant with reduced AKT S473 phosphorylation and microtubule-associated protein 1 A/1B-light chain 3 (LC3)-II/LC-I ratio. However, TRPM2 knockdown deteriorated clasmatodendritic degeneration and elevated AKT S473 phosphorylation and LC3-II/LC-I ratio without altering PHLPP1 expression. Bisindolylmaleimide (BIM, a protein kinase C inhibitor) exacerbated clasmatodendrosis accompanied by reduced p65 S311 phosphorylation and elevated AKT S473 phosphorylation in reactive astrocytes without affecting TRPM2 expression. Therefore, our findings suggest that PARP1-TRPM2-PKC-NF-κB signaling pathway may inhibit clasmatodendrosis, but facilitate reactive astrogliosis. Introduction

Epilepsy is a neuropathic condition showing repeated unprovoked seizures that are uncontrolled by antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) in approximately 30% of patients1. After epileptogenic insults, the aberrant neuronal networks, reactive astrogliosis/microgliosis and dysfunctions/degeneration of astrocytes in epileptic foci cause ictogenesis (seizure generation)2–5. Clasmatodendrosis is one of the astroglial degeneration patterns, which is firstly observed by Alzheimer in the tissues obtained from patients of epilepsy, syphilis and Alzheimer’s dementia6,7. The epileptic hippocampi of rodent models also show clasmatodendritic astroglial death in astrocytes within the stratum radiatum of CA1 region (CA1 astrocytes), which is characterized by extensive hypertrophy, cytoplasmic vacuolization derived from lysosomes and disintegrated/beaded processes8,9. Clasmatodendritic astrocytes show beclin-1 and microtubule-associated protein 1 A/1B-light chain 3 (LC3) upregulation and lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) positive vacuoles, indicating activation of autophagy8,9. Although autophagy is an adaptive catabolic process for cell survival by clearance of impaired organelles and misfolded proteins by lysosomes, excessive and/or aberrant autophagy induces autophagic cell death10–12. Thus, clasmatodendrosis is one of the astroglial degeneration evoked by aberrant and unregulated autophagy8,9,13,14, which contributes to spontaneous seizures in chronic epilepsy rats15,16. However, the underlying mechanisms evoking clasmatodendrosis are not fully understood. Recently, we have reported that glutathione (GSH, an endogenous antioxidant) deprivation is relevant to clasmatodendrosis in the hippocampus of chronic epilepsy rats. Briefly, reduced GSH level leads to glutathione peroxidase-1 (GPx1) downregulation17,18. Since GPx1 is a selenoprotein and scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) through the reduction of H2O2 by using GSH19,20, ROS-mediated events are involved in clasmatodendrosis in the epileptic hippocampus whose underlying signaling pathways have been largely unknown.

The transient receptor potential melastatin 2 (TRPM2), also known as long transient receptor potential channel 2 (LTRPC2) and transient receptor potential channel (TRPC7), is a non-selective Ca2+ permeable cation channel that expresses in various cell types in the brain. TRPM2 is involved in the pathogenesis of several neurological conditions including juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) in human patients and pentylenetetrazole (PTZ)-induced kindling mice21,22. TRPM2 activity is regulate by ROS, poly-(ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP1), ADP-ribose, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide+ (NAD+), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and intracellular Ca2+21–28. Although TRPM2 expression is very low in astrocytes under physiological condition25,26, oxidative stress upregulates TRPM2 expression, which plays a pathological role in a wide range of neuroinflammation27,28. Furthermore, PARP1 inhibition and TNF-α neutralization attenuate clasmatodendrosis in chronic epilepsy rats8,29 and PARP1 suppresses H2O2-induced autophagy30. Therefore, it is likely that TRPM2 may contribute to clasmatodendritic degeneration in the epileptic hippocampus. However, the role of TRPM2 in autophagy has been still controversial: TRPM2 inhibits the autophagy process or facilitates autophagic cell death30–32. With respect to these reports, it is tempting to investigate whether TRPM2 is one of the upstream effectors participating clasmatodendritic degeneration, which remains to be elusive.

Here, we demonstrate that TRPM2 upregulation was detected in astrocytes more than microglia in the CA1 region of chronic epilepsy rats. N-acetylcysteine (NAC, an antioxidant and a precursor of GSH) and PJ34 (a PARP1 inhibitor) downregulated TRPM2 expression in reactive astrocytes (not clasmatodendritic astrocytes) and attenuated clasmatodendritic degeneration in this region, accompanied by reduced nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) p65 serine (S) 311 phosphorylation. Furthermore, both NAC and PJ34 increased pleckstrin homology domain and leucine-rich repeat protein phosphatase 1 (PHLPP1, a phosphatase for AKT at S473 site) expression concomitant with reduced AKT S473 phosphorylation. However, TRPM2 knockdown deteriorated clasmatodendritic degeneration and enhanced AKT S473 phosphorylation without altering PHLPP1 expression. Bisindolylmaleimide (BIM), a protein kinase C (PKC) inhibitor targeting all PKC-subtypes, exacerbated clasmatodendrosis accompanied by reduced p65 S311 phosphorylation and elevated AKT S473 phosphorylation in reactive astrocytes without affecting TRPM2 expression. Furthermore, TRPM2 knockdown further elevated LC3-II/LC-I ratio in the epileptic hippocampus, while NAC and PJ34 attenuated it. Therefore, our findings suggest that ROS-PARP1-TRPM2-PKC-NF-κB signaling pathway may inhibit clasmatodendrosis, but facilitate reactive astrogliosis. In addition, NAC and PJ34 may also attenuate clasmatodendrosis by activating PHLPP1-AKT-mediated pathway.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and chemicals

Seven-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (200–220 g), purchased from Daehan Biolink (Umseong, Chungcheongbuk-do, South Korea), were housed in standard conditions (23–25 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle) with access to water and food ad libitum. Animal experiments were designed and performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. For all the procedures of the study we followed the ARRIVE guidelines. All animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Hallym University (Hallym 2021-30, approval date: 17, May. 2021 and Hallym 2024-38, approval date: 25, Oct. 2024), and all experimental procedures were conducted in full compliance with these approved protocols. Unless otherwise stated, all chemicals and reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Generation of chronic epilepsy model

Rats received LiCl (127 mg/kg, i.p.) 1 day before pilocarpine injection (30 mg/kg, i.p.). Twenty minutes before pilocarpine administration, atropine methylbromide (5 mg/kg i.p.) was treated to inhibit the peripheral effect of pilocarpine. Two hours after status epilepticus (SE) onset, diazepam (Valium; Hoffmann-la Roche, Neuilly-sur-Seine, France; 10 mg/kg, i.p.) was given to cease convulsion and repeated as needed. Control animals received saline. To detect spontaneous recurrent seizures indicating chronic epilepsy rats, animals were video-monitored 8 h a day from 2 weeks after SE16,17,33,34.

Drug and SiRNA trials

Chronic epileptic rats were implanted with a brain infusion needle (Alzet, Cupertino, CA, USA) into the right lateral ventricle (coordinates: 1 mm posterior; 1.5 mm lateral; 3.5 mm depth) under Isoflurane anesthesia (3% induction, 1.5–2% for surgery, and 1.5% maintenance in a 65:35 mixture of N2O: O2), and connected with an Alzet 1007D osmotic pump (Alzet, Cupertino, CA, USA) containing (1) vehicle, (2) PJ34 (3 µM), (3) BIM (25 µM), (4) non-silencing control siRNA (GCAACUAACUUCGUUAGAAUCGUUAUU), (5) TRPM2 siRNA (GACGAAGCAAUAGCAGUCU = tt) and (6) PHLPP1 siRNA (GAAUGUACAAUGUCCGAAA, Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea). Some vehicle-infused animals, NAC (70 mg/kg) was administered once a day by intraperitoneal (i.p.) over 7 days17,33. The location of needle was verified when the brain was sectioned.

Tissue preparation and immunohistochemistry

Seven days after surgery, rats were perfused with normal saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4) via the ascending aorta under urethane anesthesia (1.5 g/kg, i.p.). Control, acute- (1 day after SE), latent (7 days after SE) groups were also perfused by the same method. The brains were cropped, post-fixed with in the same fixative overnight and cryoprotected with 30% sucrose overnight. Thereafter, 30 μm thick coronal sections were cut on a cryostat. Sections were incubated in blocking buffer (3% bovine serum albumin in PBS) for 30 min, and subsequently reacted with a mixed solution of primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) overnight at room temperature. All antibodies were diluted with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100. After washing, sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated with Cy2- or Cy3-fluorescent dye. Some tissues were incubated in biotinylated IgG and avidin-peroxidase complex and developed in 3,3′-diaminobenzidine in 0.1 M Tris buffer. Negative control test was performed with pre-immune serum substituted for the primary antibody. The intensity of each antibody was measured in the random-selected 5 areas/animals (300 µm2/area) in the stratum radiatum of the CA1 region (5 sections from each animal) with AxioVision Rel. 4.8 and ImageJ software. Intensities were also measured in 3 reactive or clasmatodendritic astrocytes randomly collected in each rat. Intensity was normalized by setting the mean background. For quantification of clasmatodendritic astrocytes, cell counted were performed in areas of interest (1 × 104 µm2) of 10 sections per each animal17,33.

Western blot

Seven days after surgery, animals were sacrificed by decapitation under urethane anesthesia (1.5 g/kg, i.p.). Control, acute- (1 day after SE), latent (7 days after SE) groups were also sacrificed by the same method. The hippocampus was dissected out and homogenized in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris containing 50 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (pH 7.4), ethylene glycol tetraacetic acid (pH 8.0), 0.2% Tergitol type NP-40, 10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (pH 8.0), 15 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 100 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 mM NaF, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM sodium orthovanadate, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and 1 mM dithiothreitol). Total protein content was measured by BCA protein assay kit. Western blot was performed according to standard procedures (Supplementary Table 1). The signals were scanned and analyzed by ImageQuant LAS4000 system (GE health). The values of each sample were normalized with the corresponding amount of anti-β-actin (input). The phosphoprotein/total protein ratio was represented as the phosphorylation density13,25.

Data analysis

To analyze statistical significance of data, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Mann–Whitney test and Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison were applied. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to be significant.

Results

TRPM2 expression reduces in CA1 neurons, but increases in CA1 astrocytes following SE

Status epilepticus (SE) is one of the epileptogenic factors, which leads to a chronic epileptic state (> 3 weeks after SE) through acute (24–48 h after SE) and latent (silent) periods (48 h−3 weeks)35,36. Thus, we investigated the chronological changes in TRPM2 expression in the hippocampus following SE.

Consistent with previous studies37,38, TRPM2 expression was mainly detected in CA1-3 pyramidal cells and dentate granule cells (DGC) under physiological condition (in control rats, Fig. 1a). TRPM2 expression was very weakly detected in glial cells (Fig. 1a). Following SE, TRPM2 expression was gradually decreased in CA1 pyramidal cells, but not in DGC (χ2(3) = 16.068, p = 0.001, n = 5, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 1a,b). Double immunofluorescent study revealed that TRPM2 expression was decreased in CA1 neurons of 1-day post SE (acute period) group, although neuronal death was not obviously observed (Fig. 2a,b). TRPM2 expression was gradually reduced in CA1 neurons of 7-day post SE (latent period) and 4-week post-SE (epileptic period) groups (χ2(3) = 65.21, p < 0.001, n = 21 cells in 7 rats, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 2a,b), due to massive CA1 neuronal death (χ2(3) = 62.39, p < 0.001, n = 21 cells in 7 rats, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 2a,b).

Western blot data revealed that TRPM2 level was reduced in the whole hippocampus from 1 day to 7 days after SE (χ2(3) = 17.176, p = 0.001, n = 5, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 1c,d and Supplementary Fig. 1), but rebound at 4 weeks after SE (p = 0.011 vs. 7 days after SE, Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 1c,d and Supplementary Fig. 1).

In contrast CA1 neurons, glial TRPM2 expression in the CA1 region was enhanced at 7 days- (latent period) and 4 weeks (epileptic period) after SE, while it was rarely observed at 1 day after SE (acute period; Fig. 1a). TRPM2 expression was increased in astrocytes more than microglia of chronic epilepsy rats (z = 3.62, p < 0.001, n = 21 cell in 7 rats, respectively, Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Fig. 1e,f). TRPM2 was upregulated in CA1 astrocyte of 7-day post SE (latent period) and 4-week post-SE (epileptic period) groups (χ2(3) = 59.48, p < 0.001, n = 21 cells in 7 rats, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 2a,c). These findings indicate that TRPM2 upregulation may be involved in astroglial responses in the epileptic hippocampus.

NAC downregulates TRPM2 expression in reactive astrocytes but not clasmatodendritic astrocytes

Oxidative stress-induced GSH consumption (an endogenous antioxidant) leads to inflammatory responses in astrocytes, which is linked to Ca2+ influx through TRPM227. Indeed, GSH deprivation increases TRPM2 expression and its activity27,39 and propels clasmatodendrosis17,18,33. Since NF-κB p65 S311 phosphorylation is a useful marker for CA1 reactive astrogliosis40,41, we validated the effect of NAC (a sulfhydryl donor antioxidant and GSH precursor) on TRPM2 expression and p65 S311 phosphorylation to investigate the role of TRPM2 upregulation in reactive astrocytes. In the present study, TRPM2 expression in reactive CA1 astrocytes was higher than that in clasmatodendritic (vacuolized) CA1 astrocytes. NAC decreased TRPM2 expression in reactive astrocytes, but not clasmatodendritic astrocytes (χ2(3) = 16.068, p = 0.001, n = 21 cells in 7 rats, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 3a,b). Similar to TRPM2 expression, p65 S311 phosphorylation in reactive astrocytes was elevated than that in clasmatodendritic astrocytes. NAC mitigated p65 S311 phosphorylation in reactive astrocytes, but not clasmatodendritic astrocytes (χ2(3) = 64.924, p < 0.001, n = 21 cells in 7 rats, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 3c,d). In addition, NAC reduced the number of clasmatodendritic astrocytes (z = 3.07, p = 0.002, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test; Fig. 3a–e). Western blot studies also revealed that NAC diminished both TRPM2 and p65 S311 levels to 0.68- (z = 3.13, p = 0.002, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test) and 0.58-fold (z = 3.13, p = 0.002, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test) of vehicle-treated rat levels (Fig. 3f,g and Supplementary Fig. 2). These findings indicate that NAC may inhibit TRPM2-mediated reactive astrogliosis and ameliorated clasmatodendrosis in the epileptic hippocampus.

PARP1 Inhibition attenuates reactive astrogliosis and clasmatodendrosis concomitant with TRPM2 downregulation

Oxidative stress leads to DNA strand breaks that induce PARP1 activation, which is attenuated by NAC42,43. Interestingly, PARP1 is upregulated in reactive astrocytes, but not in clasmatodendritic astrocytes, in the CA1 region of the epileptic hippocampus29,41,44. Considering PARP1-mediated TRPM2 expression45, we evaluated the effects of PJ34 (a PARP1 inhibitor) on TRPM2 expression. Consistent with previous studies29,41,44, PARP1 expression was observed in reactive astrocytes, but not in clasmatodendritic astrocytes. NAC decreased PARP1 expression level in reactive astrocytes without affecting that in clasmatodendritic astrocytes (χ2(3) = 49.142, p < 0.001, n = 21 cells in 7 rats, respectively, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn-Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 4a,b). Furthermore, PJ34-induced PARP1 inhibition effectively decreased TRPM2 expression in reactive astrocytes, but not clasmatodendritic astrocytes (χ2(3) = 57.266, p < 0.001, n = 21 cells in 7 rats, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 4c,d). Although PARP1 expression was rarely detected in clasmatodendritic astrocytes, PJ34 also attenuated clasmatodendrosis (z = 2.692, p = 0.007, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test; Fig. 4a,c,e). Western blot data showed that PJ34 decreased both TRPM2 and p65 S311 levels to 0.65- (z = 3.006, p = 0.003, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test) and 0.61-fold (z = 3.134, p = 0.002, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test) of vehicle-treated rat levels (Fig. 4f,g and Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings indicate that PARP1 may be an upstream regulator of TRPM2 expression during reactive astrogliosis, and that PJ34 may inhibit clasmatodendrosis through another pathway, independent of PARP1.

TRPM2 knockdown deteriorates clasmatodendritic degeneration with the enhanced AKT S473 phosphorylation

Genetic TRPM2 deletion alleviates reactive astrogliosis following PTZ-22 and pilocarpine-induced acute seizures46. The present study also revealed that both NAC and PJ34 ameliorated reactive astrogliosis and clasmatodendrosis, accompanied by TRPM2 downregulation. To elucidate whether TRPM2 directly regulates reactive astrogliosis and clasmatodendrosis in the epileptic hippocampus similar to the cases of acute seizure models, we applied TRPM2 siRNA to chronic epilepsy rats. TRPM2 siRNA decreased TRPM2 expression level in both reactive astrocytes and clasmatodendritic astrocytes (χ2(3) = 58.358, p < 0.001, n = 21 cells in 7 rats, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 5a,b). Unlike NAC or PJ34, however, TRPM2 knockdown exacerbated clasmatodendritic degeneration (z = 2.305, p = 0.021, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test; Fig. 5a,c). TRPM2 knockdown reduced p65 S311 phosphorylation (z = 3.141, p = 0.002, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test), accompanied by the diminished TRPM2 expression (z = 3.134, p = 0.002, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test) in the epileptic hippocampus (Fig. 5d,e and Supplementary Fig. 4).

On the other hand, the enhanced AKT S473 phosphorylation plays a crucial role in mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)-independent astroglial autophagy47,48. Thus, we explored the effect of TRPM2 siRNA on AKT S473 phosphorylation. In control siRNA-infused animals, AKT S473 phosphorylation was enhanced in clasmatodendritic astrocytes more than reactive astrocytes. TRPM2 siRNA increased AKT S473 phosphorylation in reactive astrocytes (χ2(3) = 44.173, p < 0.001, n = 21 cells in 7 rats, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 5f,g). Western blot data revealed that TRPM2 siRNA elevated AKT S473 phosphorylation level to 1.29-fold of control siRNA-treated rat level in the epileptic hippocampus (z = 3.13, p = 0.002, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test; Fig. 5d,e and Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings indicate that TRPM2 may play a role in the inhibition of AKT-mediated autophagic process in reactive astrocytes.

TRPM2-mediated PKC regulation distinctly regulates reactive astrogliosis and clasmatodendrosis

TRPM2-mediated Ca2+ signaling activates PKC49, which regulates NF-κB S311 phosphorylation and reactive astrogliosis50,51. Interestingly, TPRM2 deletion increases AKT phosphorylation after hypoxic-ischemia in neonatal mouse brain52. TRPM2 activation increases glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β) activity (dephosphorylation)53. Furthermore, PKC inhibition increases AKT S473 phosphorylation54,55, which inactivates (phosphorylates) GSK-3β and leads to bax-interacting factor 1 (Bif-1)-mediated clasmatodendritic degeneration16,47,56. Therefore, we evaluated the role of TRPM2-mediated PKC activation in reactive astrogliosis and clasmatodendrosis by applying BIM (an PKC inhibitor targeting all PKC-subtypes)57. As compared to vehicle, BIM elevated AKT S473 phosphorylation in reactive astrocytes (χ2(3) = 43.304, p < 0.001, n = 21 cells in 7 rats, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 6a,b). BIM also aggravated clasmatodendritic degeneration (z = 2.433, p = 0.015, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test; Fig. 6a,c). Western blot study revealed that BIM diminished p65 S311 phosphorylation (z = 3.13, p = 0.002, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test), but increased AKT S473 phosphorylation (z = 3.07, p = 0.002, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test) without altering TRPM2 expression (z = 0.512, p = 0.609, n = 7, respectively, Mann–Whitney test) in the epileptic hippocampus (Fig. 6d,e and Supplementary Fig. 5). These data indicate that TRPM2-mediated PKC activation may lead to NF-κB-mediated reactive astrogliosis, but inhibit AKT-mediated clasmatodendritic degeneration in the epileptic hippocampus.

NAC and PJ34 inhibit clasmatodendrosis by PHLPP1 upregulation, independent TRPM2-mediated signaling pathway

Unexpectedly, the present data showed that NAC and PJ34 attenuated reactive astrogliosis and clasmatodendritic degeneration, although they effectively inhibited TRPM2 upregulation in the epileptic hippocampus. Thus, the remaining questions are how these compounds ameliorated clasmatodendrosis in parallel with TRPM2 downregulation. PHLPP1 is a ubiquitous phosphatase that dephosphorylates AKT at S473 site58. Thus, inhibition of PHLPP1-AKT binding facilitates clasmatodendrosis48. Interestingly, NAC increases PHLPP1 levels59. Similarly, PJ34 upregulates PHLPP1 expression and attenuates AKT S473 phosphorylation independent of DNA repair impairment60. Therefore, it is likely that NAC- and PJ34-induced PHLPP1 upregulation may attenuate clasmatodendritic degeneration. To confirm this, we investigated whether NAC and PJ34 lead to PHLPP1 upregulation. Both NAC and PJ34 increased PHLPP1 expression in the epileptic hippocampus, while TRPM2 knockdown did not affect PHLPP1 expression (χ2(5) = 25.202, p < 0.001, n = 7, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 7a,b and Supplementary Fig. 6). NAC and PJ34 diminished AKT S473 phosphorylation in the epileptic hippocampus, while TRPM2 knockdown enhanced it (χ2(5) = 28.224, p < 0.001, n = 7, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 7a,c and Supplementary Fig. 6). Since LC3-I is modified to LC3-II that is required for the autophagosome formation, an increase in LC3-II/LC3-I ratio is an indicative of activation of autophagy process13,14,61. To investigate the effects of TRPM2 siRNA, NAC and PJ34 on autophagy flux, therefore, we also evaluated LC3-II/LC-I ratio. As compared to control animals, LC3-II/LC-I ratio was increased in the epileptic hippocampus. TRPM2 knockdown further elevated LC3-II/LC-I ratio, while NAC and PJ34 attenuated it (χ2(5) = 27.571, p < 0.001, n = 7, respectively, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc comparison; Fig. 7a,d and Supplementary Fig. 6). These findings indicate that NAC and PJ34 may attenuate clasmatodendrosis by PHLPP1 upregulation, although they inhibited TRPM2 upregulation in CA1 astrocytes.

Discussion

Since TRPM2 activation induces expression of transcription factors and kinases regulating cell proliferation and survival, it plays an important role in the preservation of cell viability after oxidative stress62. In the hippocampus neurons, TRPM2 is coupled to voltage gated Ca2+ channels and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors through a rise in intracellular Ca2+ level. Thus, TRPM2 hyperactivation is one of the causes for hippocampal neuronal death in response to various stimuli37,38. In the present study, TRPM2 expression was strongly detected in CA1-3 pyramidal cells and DGC under physiological condition. Following SE, TRPM2 level was gradually decreased in CA1 pyramidal cells, but not in DGC. Considering the high vulnerability of CA1 neurons in response to various insults2,63,64, these findings indicate that reduced TRPM2 expression in CA1 neurons may be a consequence from SE-induced massive neuronal degeneration or an adaptive response to prevent excessive intracellular Ca2+ loads induced by SE.

Of note, the present study also reveals that TRPM2 was upregulated in reactive astrocytes, but not clasmatodendritic astrocytes, within the CA1 region of the epileptic hippocampus. TRPM2-medaited Ca2+ signaling cascades play an important role in the regulation of transcriptional and translational responses to oxidative stress in astrocytes65. Indeed, oxidative stress increases PARP1 expression/activation and the subsequent TRPM2-mediated neuroinflammatory response in astrocytes27,29,41,44,45. Furthermore, TRPM2-mediated Ca2+ signaling activates PKC that phosphorylates NF-κB p65 S311 site, which activates astrocytes49–51. In the present study, NAC diminished PARP1 expression in reactive astrocytes. In addition, NAC and PJ34 attenuated astroglial TRPM2 upregulation and p65 S311 phosphorylation. Oxidative stress leads to activation of NF-κB-mediated inflammatory pathway in astrocytes, which is reduced by PJ3466 or TRPM2 knockdown27. Furthermore, TRPM2 is a “chanzyme”, acting as both ion channel and enzyme, with the properties of a cation channel and an ADP-ribose hydrolase that catabolizes toxic ADP-ribose67,68. Therefore, our findings indicate that the ROS-PARP1-TRPM2 signaling pathway may increase astroglial viability against oxidative stress and promote reactive astrogliosis, which would contribute to neuroinflammation in the epileptic hippocampus. Although reduced TRPM2 expression may prevent excessive intracellular Ca2+ loads37,38, TRPM2 downregulation in CA1 neurons and CA1 astrocytes could also lead to the accumulation of toxic ADP-ribose, potentially contributing to CA1 neuronal degeneration and clasmatodendritic degeneration.

Clasmatodendrosis is relevant to excessive autophagy, although it is unknown whether aberrant autophagy directly leads to this astroglial degeneration8,9. In the present study, TRPM2 expression was lower in clasmatodendritic astrocytes, as compared to reactive astrocytes. In addition, TRPM2 knockdown deteriorated clasmatodendritic degeneration. Since oxidative stress evokes clasmatodendrosis17,18,33, it is likely that TRPM2 activation/upregulation may inhibit clasmatodendritic degeneration. Indeed, TRPM2-dependent Ca2+ influx inhibits oxidative stress-induced autophagy31,69,70. Consistent with these previous reports, the present data demonstrate that TRPM2 siRNA and BIM elevated LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and AKT S473 phosphorylation in the epileptic hippocampus, which deteriorated clasmatodendritic degeneration. The conversion from LC3-I to LC3-II is essential for autophagosome formation13,14,61. Aberrant AKT S473 phosphorylation also leads to GSK-3β-Bif-1-dependent astroglial autophagy47,48. Furthermore, TPRM2 ablation and PKC inhibition increase AKT activity52,54,55, while TRPM2 activation decreases GSK-3β function that is inactivated by AKT53,56. Therefore, it is likely that TRPM2-mediated Ca2+ influx may abrogate AKT-GSK-3β-Bif-1-medaited astroglial autophagy. However, other studies report that TRPM2 facilitates autophagy30–32. In particular, TRPM2 ablation attenuates autophagy in the hippocampus in pilocarpine-induced acute seizure model (though the specific cell types remain unexplored)46. As mentioned earlier, TRPM2 either inhibits the autophagy process or facilitates autophagic cell death30–32. Briefly, membrane TRPM2-dependent Ca2+ influx inhibits oxidative stress-induced autophagy31,69,70, while lysosomal TRPM2 activation facilitates autophagy30–32. Given that membrane TRPM2 is regulated by PARP1, while lysosomal TRPM2 is modulated by PARP230, these discrepancies may arise from differences in cell types, the properties of the hippocampus (normal hippocampus in acute seizure model vs. epileptic hippocampus), TRPM2 localization (membrane vs. lysosome) or variants in upstream regulator. In the present study, however, we found that TRPM2 expression was downregulated in clasmatodendritic astrocytes but upregulated in reactive astrocytes. Furthermore, TRPM2 knockdown worsened clasmatodendritic degeneration of CA1 astrocytes. Therefore, we conclude that TRPM2 inhibits autophagic astroglial degeneration in the epileptic hippocampus.

In a previous study, we have reported that PARP1 expression is rarely detected in the nuclei of clasmatodendritic astrocytes, while it is obviously observed in the nuclei of reactive astrocytes. Furthermore, PJ34 attenuated clasmatodendritic degeneration and reactive astrogliosis29. Paradoxically, poly-(ADP-ribose) (PAR, a product of PARP1 and a precursor of ADP-ribose) level in clasmatodendritic astrocyte is higher than that in naïve and reactive astrocytes29. Since PJ34 reduces PAR level in clasmatodendritic astrocytes29 and ghost cells exhibiting digested nuclei possess a potentially much greater capacity to synthesize PAR71, we had speculated that PARP1 activation might be involved in clasmatodendritic degeneration. Furthermore, the absence of PARP1 expression or its degradation could result from nuclear digestion or autophagic processes29. Considering TRPM2’s role as an ADP-ribose hydrolase67,68 and the requirement of the presence of the PARP1 protein (rather than its activity) for NF-κB activation72,73, it is plausible that increased PAR level in clasmatodendritic astrocytes may be a consequence from TRPM2 downregulation rather than PARP1 activation. Conversely, it is also possible that PARP2, acting as a substitute for PARP1, might also synthesize PAR in these cells. Further studies are needed to elucidate how PAR is synthesized or accumulated in clasmatodendritic astrocytes.

On the other hand, the present data show that NAC and PJ34 attenuated clasmatodendrosis in parallel with the TRPM2 downregulation in reactive astrocytes. Notably, both NAC and PJ34 increase PHLPP1 expression levels59,60. The present study also reveals that NAC and PJ34 diminished the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and AKT S473 phosphorylation in the epileptic hippocampus, accompanied by PHLPP1 upregulation. These findings suggest that NAC and PJ34 may mitigate clasmatodendritic degeneration through PHLPP1-AKT-GSK-3β-Bif-1-mediated signaling pathway. Therefore, our findings propose that NAC and PJ34 may prevent both reactive astrogliosis and clasmatodendrosis through TRPM2 inhibition and PHLPP1 upregulation, respectively.

The findings of the present study highlight the critical role of the PARP1-TRPM2-PKC pathway in the astroglial responses to epileptic seizures. Considering that attenuation of clasmatodendritic degeneration reduces seizure duration, but not its frequency or behavioral severity, in chronic epilepsy rats16,33, therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway may provide significant clinical benefits by mitigating seizure-induced neuronal damage and cognitive deficits. Beyond epilepsy, astrocytes respond to various pathological changes in the brain74. Dysregulated autophagy and neuroinflammation are key mechanisms of cell death involved in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis75–78. Autophagy influences multiple processes in astrocytes, thereby affecting neuronal function, and also plays a crucial role in regulating neuroinflammation75–78. Furthermore, autophagy contributes to the clearance of amyloid-β and ROS by astrocytes75,77,78. If the same molecular mechanism also contributes to neurodegenerative diseases, the modulation of PARP1-TRPM2-PKC pathway may represent a promising therapeutic approach to slow disease progression by reducing astroglial stress and inflammatory responses. Targeting this pathway could also mitigate secondary neuronal injury. Future studies are needed to evaluate therapeutic interventions targeting this pathway across various neurological disorders.

Conclusion

In the present study, we found that the PARP1-TRPM2 cascade in the hippocampi of chronic epilepsy rats facilitated reactive astrogliosis by activating PKC-mediated NF-κB p65 S311 phosphorylation. Additionally, it inhibited clasmatodendritic degeneration by disrupting AKT-GSK-3β-Bif-1-mediated autophagy. Furthermore, NAC and PJ34 attenuated clasmatodendrosis through a parallel PHLPP1-AKT-mediated pathway, despite inhibiting TRPM2 upregulation in CA1 astrocytes (Fig. 8e). Taken together, our findings suggest that the PARP1-TRPM2 signaling pathway may act as a double-edged sword for astroglial viability and reactive astrogliosis in the epileptic hippocampus. Inhibition of ROS generation and PARP1 activation could thus provide a promising therapeutic strategy to mitigate astrocyte-related pathogenesis in neurological diseases.
