Nonsurgical orthodontic treatment of a bilateral scissors bite by bodily tooth movement through the maxillary sinus
Authors: Yuqing Wang, Yujiang Liu, Tieliang Yang, Xiumei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBilateral scissor bite is a rare malocclusion. For adult patients with skeletal disharmonies, orthognathic surgery is usually adopted for correction. Non-surgical orthodontic treatment is more difficult and rarely reported. During orthodontic treatment, it is necessary to reposition malpositioned molars. However, in some patients, excessive pneumatization of the maxillary sinus allows the molar roots to extend into the sinus cavity. This significantly increases the difficulty of moving the molars.Case presentationThis case reports an adult patient with transverse maxillary hyperplasia, bilateral scissor bite, deep overbite, mandibular retrusion, and deviation. Additionally, due to excessive maxillary sinus pneumatization, the roots of the maxillary molars were located within the maxillary sinus. In this case, mini-implants were used in combination with palatal long-arm traction hooks of the first molars. Concurrently, the maxillary second molars were extracted, and the third molars were used as replacements. Following 45 months of treatment and a 12-month retention period, the patient displayed a normal overbite and overjet, proper molar relationships, improved facial aesthetics, and a healthy temporomandibular joint.ConclusionsThis case demonstrated that bodily movement of the tooth through the maxillary sinus can be achieved by using suitable force systems, correcting the bilateral scissor bite caused by transverse maxillary hyperplasia and avoiding surgical damage and complications.Keywords: Scissor Bite, Maxillary sinus pneumatization, Orthodontic mini-implants, Bodily tooth movement

Background

Scissor bite is a malocclusion that is clinically characterized by unbilateral or bilateral palatal cusps of the maxillary posterior teeth being in the buccal position to the buccal cusps of the mandibular antagonist posterior teeth [1, 2]. It not only adversely affects chewing and muscle functions but also interferes with the anterior and lateral excursive movements of the mandible, leading to mandibular retraction or deviation and impairing the normal growth and development of the mandible [3–5]. In addition, scissors bite also causes issues such as a deviated occlusal plane, facial asymmetry, and reduced masticatory efficiency. Furthermore, an unfavorable occlusal relationship may increase the risk of temporomandibular joint disorders [6–8].

During treatment for scissors bite, if teeth are relatively upright within the bone but displaced buccally or lingually, bodily movement is necessary. The bodily movement is typically challenging. Moreover, the roots of maxillary molars often lie adjacent to the maxillary sinus. In some patients, excessive pneumatization of the maxillary sinus occurs, with the sinus floor extending to the posterior alveolar process, causing the root to enter the sinus [9]. The maxillary sinus floor (MSF), which is composed of a layer of compact bone overlying mucosa, presents a physiological anatomical constraint during maxillary posterior tooth movement [10]. Consequently, the uncertainty associated with moving teeth through the maxillary sinus (MTTMS) significantly increases the complexity of such cases.

This study delineates the orthodontic treatment process for an adult patient presenting with bilateral scissor bite, excessive maxillary sinus pneumatization, mandibular functional position abnormality, and deep overbite. This case employed mini-implants, long-arm appliance, and straight wire appliance to achieve treatment objectives, including narrowing the maxillary dental arch to mask excessive maxillary transverse development, correcting molar crossbite, and improving the facial profile.

Case presentation

Diagnosis and etiology

The patient was a 21-year-old female who visited the hospital because of poor occlusal function. The patient’s face was asymmetrical, with a chin deviation to the right side and an upward canting of an occlusal plane on the right side. The patient presented a convex profile (Fig. 1). Intraoral examination and study of the model revealed that the patient had a Class II molar relationship. The maxillary molars were in buccal and mesial malposition accompanied by extrusion of teeth 17 and 27. The mandibular molars were lingually inclined(Figs. 1 and 2). In addition, the patient had moderate crowding in the posterior region of the maxillary arch and mild crowding in the anterior region of the mandibular arch. 18 and 28 were fully erupted, and 38 and 48 were partially erupted. The midline of the upper and lower dental arches deviates to the right relative to the midsagittal plane. There was mild dental fluorosis (Fig. 1).

Cephalometric analysis

The patient reported clicking sounds in the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) accompanied by joint locking, along with soreness in the joint area during eating. Clinical examination revealed bilateral joint clicking and a leftward deviation in the opening style. CBCT examination revealed bilateral condylar asymmetry. The anterior joint space was relatively large, wheras the superior and posterior spaces were narrower (Figs. 5C and 6A).

Treatment objectives

The treatment objectives were to (1) correct bilateral scissor bite, harmonize the forms of the maxillary and mandibular arches and restore occlusal function; (2) establish Class I canine and molar relationships and normal anterior teeth overbite and overjet; and (3) improve mandibular retrusion and facial asymmetry.

Treatment alternatives

Option 1: Orthodontic Treatment. Teeth 17, 27, 38, and 48 were extracted. Remove 17 and 27 and replace them with 18 and 28. Mini-implants were placed in the maxillary posterior region and combined with long-arm traction hooks to achieve distalization and palatal bodily movement of teeth 16 and 26. Mini-implants were placed in the mandibular posterior region to correct the lingual inclination and extrusion of the second molars, the scissor bite was rectified, and the arch width was harmonized. A straight wire appliance was used to align and level the dentition while coordinating occlusal relationships. This approach reduces the treatment complexity for the scissor bite and minimizes costs, although it requires the extraction of healthy teeth from the dentition.

Option 2: Combined orthodontic and orthognathic surgery. Multiple segmental and Le Fort I osteotomies in the maxilla are performed in conjunction with preoperative orthodontic treatment, followed by postoperative fine-tuning of the occlusion. This approach corrects skeletal developmental abnormalities, but it involves significant invasiveness and carries a high risk of complications.

The patient ultimately chose option 1.

Treatment progress

17, 27, 38, and 48 were removed. A biteplate was worn in the maxillary region to relieve occlusal interference. Mini-implants were placed at the midpalatal suture and on the bilateral mandibular external oblique area. Elastomeric chains were applied for traction to achieve movement of teeth 16 and 26 towards the palatal and gingival directions and movement of teeth 37 and 47 towards the buccal and gingival directions. Two weeks later, occlusal contacts appeared in the molar regions. However, owing to the insufficient intrusion effect, an open bite developed in the anterior region (Fig. 7). Rubber Elastomeric chains were used to apply reciprocal traction between the maxillary first and third molars, closing the extraction space. After three months, significant intrusion of the mandibular molars was achieved, and the open bite was solved (Fig. 8A). Fixed appliances were bonded to all the teeth to align the maxillary dentition. Following eight follow-up appointments, we observed the relationship of the molars transitioning from class II to class I (Fig. 8B).

Appliances for correcting the scissor bite

At the 11th follow-up visit, the maxillary molars had a lingual inclination. Positive torque was applied to the stainless-steel wire to torque movement (Fig. 8C). Four months later, CBCT revealed that substantial palatal movement of the root within the maxillary sinus was required for maxillary first molars (Fig. 9). Owing to suboptimal initial root control, the bands were placed on 16 and 26, and long-arm traction hooks were welded to their palatal surfaces. Elastomeric chains connected hooks to mini-implants placed bilaterally in the midpalatal suture, aiming to direct traction forces through the roots’ center of resistance. Concurrently, positive torque was applied to the wire (Fig. 10). After 14 months, the application of force ceased due to detachment of the mini-implant. Subsequently, the brackets on 16 and 26 were interchanged and inverted, applying positive torque to the teeth. Concurrently, positive torque was applied on the wire. This approach was continued for 7 months. Final adjustments were made to the maxillary and mandibular dentition and occlusion (Fig. 11). A Hawteys retainer was fabricated for long-term retention. Treatment involved approximately 30 follow-up appointments. However, irregular intervals resulted in a total treatment duration of approximately 45 months.

Treatment results

Posttreatment photographs revealed improved mandibular deviation and increased height in the lower third of the face. Intraoral photographs and dental models revealed that the treatment resolved the scissor bite and the deep overbite, establishing a normal overbite and overjet relationship and a Class I molar relationship (Figs. 11 and 12).

Both the maxillary and mandibular incisors have a labial tipping movement. Teeth 18 and 28 were successfully positioned to establish favorable occlusal relationships with their antagonistic and adjacent teeth.

Discussion

Scissor bite, also known as buccal crossbites, telescopic bite, and Brodie syndrome, is a malocclusion [11]. This malocclusion may result from the excessive width of the maxillary bone/arch, the narrowness of the mandibular bone/arch, or a combination of both [12]. The etiology of the patient is transverse maxillary hyperplasia combined with an overly wide maxillary dental arch, along with narrowing of the dental arch caused by the lingual inclination of the mandibular molars. Among the multiple molars involved in the scissor bite, the maxillary second molars exhibited the most severe buccal malposition and vertical extrusion. Consequently, three - dimensional bodily movement of 17 and 27 is required, presenting significant orthodontic challenges (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Meanwhile, teeth 18 and 28 have fully erupted in a distally inclined position without significant buccal malposition or extrusion. They can substitute for the extracted 17 and 27 (Fig. 5). Following extraction, not only is the scissor bite of the second molars resolved, but reciprocal traction can be applied to the maxillary first molars and third molars that are to address the challenge of distally moving the first molars. Therefore, this case required distal and palatal bodily movement of the maxillary first molars, mesial movement of the maxillary third molars, buccal tipping movement of the mandibular molars, and intrusion of the second molars to address the underlying etiology.

Next, we assessed the difficulty of moving other teeth and found that all roots of the maxillary first molars had entered the gasified maxillary sinus (Fig. 4). Current case reports indicate that closing the gaps of the extracted tooth by moving teeth through the maxillary sinus was feasible [13–15]. Kuroda et al. further elucidated the biological mechanism through an experimental study, demonstrating that applying mechanical stress to teeth induces new bone formation on the maxillary sinus surface [16, 17]. Therefore, we decided to attempt the bodily movement of the molars through the maxillary sinus. During the treatment process, initially, we implanted only one mini-implant adjacent to the midpalatal line and performed the tipping movement via the buttons on the palatal side of the 16 and 26 tooth crowns. Surprisingly, within just two weeks, occlusal contacts were established between the maxillary and mandibular molars (Fig. 7). CBCT scans during treatment showed that the maxillary molars were undergoing inclination movement at this stage (Fig. 8). The reason for that is that the blood supply to the cortical bone is relatively poor, and the remodeling rate is slower, resulting in greater resistance to tooth movement [18]. During the process of MTTMs, the root apex is in contact with the cortical bone at the floor of the maxillary sinus, while the area surrounding the root neck is cancellous bone, leading to different resistances between the two. Therefore, molars are more prone to tipping movement. In the second stage, we mainly performed palatal torque movement of the molar roots. Due to the detachment of the first-stage palatal implant, and considering that the subsequent bilateral molar torque movement requires greater anchorage support, we re-implanted two implant screws on both sides of midpalatal line. We designed long - arm traction hooks and welded them to the palatal side of the molar crowns (Fig. 10). By increasing the ratio of moment to force, we aimed to position the rotational center on the dental crown to achieve torque movement [19]. At the same time, we combined the positive torque of the archwire and the bracket to provide palatal rotational torque to the tooth roots, ultimately making the teeth stand upright in the bone (Fig. 3).

Although this article presents a successful case, it is important to contextualize the treatment method within the broader spectrum of non - surgical treatments for scissor bite. Previous studies have shown that various non - surgical methods can successfully correct scissor bite, including intermaxillary elastics [11], temporary micro - screw anchorage devices [20], bonded double - helix appliances [21], mandibular lingual arches [7], functional removable appliances [9] and so on. However, these methods have certain limitations. For example, conventional intermaxillary elastics would cause tooth extrusion, leading to undesirable effects such as clockwise rotation of the mandible, inclination of the occlusal plane, or anterior open bite [22]. Moreover, they can only cause the teeth to undergo tipping movement. In this case, the patient presented with mandibular retrusion and a high mandibular plane angle. Additionally, the first molars required extensive bodily movement through the maxillary sinus to conceal the skeletal width discrepancy. Therefore, we chose mini - implants that can provide strong anchorage and control the vertical direction, and applied a new device, namely the long - arm traction hook, to control the bodily movement of the teeth using TADs. A CBCT scan taken after treatment revealed that the maxillary molars had undergone transverse and sagittal bodily movement through the maxillary sinus (Fig. 3). The width of the maxillary arch was decreased by 6 mm, which masked the transverse maxillary hyperplasia (Table 2). This case demonstrated that, without surgery, narrowing the maxillary dental arch width through the bodily movement of teeth in the buccolingual direction yielded an effect comparable to that achieved through surgical treatment [23]. This provides clinicians with another therapeutic approach.

Furthermore, there was also a sagittal difference in the maxilla and mandible of this case. The patient revealed an ANB angle of 6.6°and a Class II molar relationship, indicating mandibular retrusion (Table 1). The patient had a normal skeletal length of the mandibular ramus and body. However, CT imaging revealed reduced posterior and superior joint spaces bilaterally, leading to the diagnosis of functional mandibular retrusion (Fig. 6A). After the release of the scissor bite, the patient achieved Class I occlusion. Pretreatment CT also revealed suspected prior resorption of the posterior slope of the right condyle, which potentially contributed to the rightward deviation of the chin point (Fig. 6A). Posttreatment, the mandibular deviation improved. To observe the direction of mandibular movement, we extracted the 3D model from the CBCT image for superimposition (Fig. 14). Observation revealed that the mandible moved anteriorly and inferiorly, with the right mandibular body moving more than the left mandibular body. Cephalometric superimpositions confirmed anterior and inferior movement of the mandible (Fig. 13). Posttreatment CT demonstrated restoration of the condylar position and alleviation of the patient’s TMD symptoms (Fig. 6B). Studies by Min - Ho Jung and Sang - Hoon Lee indicate that mandibular movement occurs in the anticipated direction after the scissor bite is resolved, whether functional mandibular retrusion or functional mandibular deviation, without the need for orthodontic appliance guidance [24, 25]. It demonstrated that in this case, the scissor bite was related to TMD and abnormal functional mandibular positioning. Removing the scissor bite as soon as possible may be more beneficial for the health of the joint.

The treatment duration for this patient was approximately 45 months. The main reasons for this are as follows: Firstly, compared with similar cases of scissor bite [3, 7, 22], the MSF of this patient extends to the area between the root bifurcations of the molars. During the tooth movement process, to maintain the integrity of the sinus floor, compensatory new bone deposition needs to form before bone resorption occurs on the pressure side [14]. However, the remodeling rate of the cortical bone in the sinus floor is relatively slow, and the surface activation frequency is lower, which is more obvious in adults [26]. Therefore, using continuous gentle force and low - speed movement can provide sufficient time for tissue remodeling. Secondly, this case is a rare bilateral scissor bite. Compared to the MTTMS - based space closure cases reported by Cha S, Oh H, and Re S [14, 26, 27], the maxillary molar in this case not only needs to overcome resistance from the sinus floor cortical bone to achieve sagittal movement but also needs to overcome the resistance from both the sinus floor and the palatal cortical bone to achieve buccal - lingual movement. Moreover, the tooth movement distance is large, and the total movement of the bilateral molars is approximately 6 mm. Additionally, although the teeth ultimately achieved bodily movement in this case, when the molars began to move, no force was applied through the center of resistance, which was one of the reasons for the long treatment course. However, if bodily movement had been achieved from the start of moving the molars, the delayed manifestation of root control effects would have prolonged the process of unlocking the scissor bite. The advantages and disadvantages of this need to be carefully considered and weighed. Finally, due to the patient’s personal reasons, she did not attend follow - up visits on time during the orthodontic treatment.

MTTMS also carry some risks of complications, mainly including root resorption, loss of pulp vitality, dehiscences in the cortical plates, and complications related to maxillary sinus perforation [26, 28]. When the root is in contact with the cortical bone, root resorption is a common complication. In some similar cases, obvious root resorption was observed [9, 25]. However, in this patient, based on radiological observation, although some apexes of the root became blunt, no obvious root resorption was found. In addition, applying long - term and excessive orthodontic force to the tooth can lead to insufficient blood supply to the pulp, degeneration, calcification, and loss of vitality [29]. The treatment duration of this case was long, and the orthodontic force was gentle. So no pulp necrosis was found during the orthodontic process. Another point to note is whether there is a dehiscence in the cortical bone and perforation of the maxillary sinus [30–32]. According to CBCT, the buccal and palatal cortical bone of the first molars’ roots is intact, and the buccal reformed cancellous bone has a significant thickness. A layer of bone imaging can be seen around the root bifurcation of 16 and 26, completely covering the palatal side of the buccal root and the buccal side of the palatal root. However, no obvious bone image was observed on the palatal side of the palatal root (Fig. 3). This is consistent with the research results of Takayoshi et al., where the alveolar bone on the buccal side remodels faster than that on the palatal side [31]. Although no obvious bone image was found on the palatal side of the palatal root, this might be due to the thinness or insufficient density of the new bone, which is not visible on CT. Even without bone coverage, the palatal root surface may directly contact the sinus floor mucosa, and the mucosa and the underlying periosteal cells will continue to complete the remodeling of bone [16]. We need to conduct long - term follow - up to observe the subsequent results.

Conclusion

In summary, this case employed tooth extraction and the straight-wire arch technique, supplemented by mini-implants and long-arm traction hooks, to correct the scissor bite. Following resolution of intermaxillary interference, the mandible underwent anteroinferior and leftward, resulting in improved facial aesthetics and alleviation of TMD symptoms. The challenge in this case lies in achieving transverse and sagittal bodily movement of the tooth though though the maxillary sinus. It narrows the maxillary dental arch and moves the molars distally, restoring the dental arch to its normal width and morphology while masking the skeletal transverse discrepancy. This treatment plan avoided surgery, expanding the scope of nonsurgical treatment for patients with skeletal developmental anomalies.
