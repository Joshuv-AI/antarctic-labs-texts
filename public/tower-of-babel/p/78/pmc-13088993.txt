Minimal Change Disease Following B-Cell Maturation Antigen–Directed Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-Cell Therapy
Authors: Kiran Shivaraj, Omar Mamlouk, Amanda Tchakarov, Krina Patel, Ala Abudayyeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a well-recognized complication of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, typically attributed to cytokine release syndrome and acute tubular injury. However, glomerular disease post CAR-T cell is rare. We report a case of minimal change disease presenting with AKI and nephrotic-range proteinuria 3 weeks after B-cell maturation antigen–directed CAR-T cell therapy, ciltacabtagene autoleucel, in a patient with relapsed refractory multiple myeloma. The kidney biopsy revealed diffuse podocyte foot process effacement and a predominance of CD4+ T cells with potential on-target, off-tumor effect of B-cell maturation antigen–directed CAR-T cell therapy, leading to podocyte injury. The patient received one dose of rituximab along with a short course of corticosteroid and had complete kidney recovery by week 4 of therapy. This report emphasizes the need for further investigation into the mechanism of kidney toxicity following CAR-T cell therapy, and potential benefits and risks of immunosuppressive therapy in this context.Index Words: CAR-T cells, multiple myeloma, glomerulonephritis, minimal change disease

Two chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies targeting B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) have been approved by the FDA for treatment of relapsed refractory multiple myeloma, idecabtagene vicleucel and ciltacabtagen autoleucel.1,2 Phase 2 and 3 clinical trials have demonstrated the benefit of these BCMA-targeting CAR-T cells by improving the overall response and progression free survival in triple-class refractory multiple myeloma patients.3

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is commonly reported following CAR-T therapy.4 The most likely etiology of AKI was thought to be acute tubular necrosis (ATN) owing to hypotension and capillary leakage secondary to cytokine release syndrome (CRS). In this setting of a T-cell-mediated inflammatory state, induction of acute interstitial nephritis and ATN secondary to cytokine injury may occur. However, activation of T cells to attack cancer cells, indiscriminate injury to organs may take place leading to immune adverse events that can include autoimmune induction, not just T-cell infiltration. Also, direct cytokine injury can occur, leading to podocytopathy and endothelial injury.5,6 Two distinctive types of glomerular injury, collapsing focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and thrombotic microangiopathy, were described in association with CAR-T cell therapy in patients with hematological malignances.7,8 In the present report, we describe a unique patient with refractory multiple myeloma in whom AKI and nephrotic-range proteinuria developed owing to minimal change disease (MCD) following BCMA-directed CAR-T therapy.

Case Report

The patient was a woman in her 60’s who was diagnosed with IgA kappa–type multiple myeloma in 2018. She underwent multiple lines of therapy for relapsed disease, including autologous stem cell transplantation in 2019. After the fourth line of treatment, she underwent CAR-T therapy with ciltacabtagene autoleucel. She tolerated the treatment well except for grade 1 CRS on day 8, which resolved the next day. She was discharged on day 13. The patient was readmitted on day 21 for having severe kidney injury associated with complaints of nausea, poor urine output, and generalized weakness. She was normotensive with normal sinus rhythm, afebrile, and appeared euvolemic on examination. Her laboratory test results were significant, with an increase in her serum creatinine level from a baseline of 0.98-1.20 mg/dL to 8.96 mg/dL, hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin of 2.5 g/dL), microscopic hematuria with pyuria (3 RBC/HPF and 20 WBC/HPF), and proteinuria with 3.8 grams of urine protein/24 hours. However, the myeloma laboratory test results demonstrated a good response with reduction of her kappa light chains as well as paraprotein level. Interestingly, a cytokine panel demonstrated elevated interleukin-2 levels. Given the severity of AKI and the suspicion for glomerulonephritis, we initiated hemodialysis for volume and metabolic clearance and obtained a kidney biopsy. The biopsy revealed severe ATN along with tubulointerstitial inflammation with few neutrophils. Routine immunofluorescence staining was negative for IgA, IgG, IgM, κ, λ, C3, and C1q. Ultrastructural examination revealed diffuse podocyte foot process effacement, consistent with MCD. Additional immunohistochemical stains showed many CD3 positive T cells (202 cells per 10 high power fields [HPF]) with a predominance of CD4 over CD8 positive T cells (137 cells/10 HPF and 106 cells/10HPF, respectively). Few CD138 positive plasma cells were present (23 cells/10 HPF) (Figure 1).

With the kidney biopsy confirming MCD, rituximab was administered (1 gram-flat dose) and prednisone started at 60 mg then tapered rapidly over 10 days. The patient’s kidney function recovered, she stopped undergoing dialysis within 1 week, and she had complete kidney recovery (serum creatinine was back at baseline of 1-1.3 mg/dL) by the end of fourth week of AKI diagnosis. Serum albumin increased to normal value of 4.3 g/dL and proteinuria to 381 g/24 hours.

Discussion

MCD is characterized by an injury to the kidney podocyte cytoskeleton. Its exact pathophysiology has not been discovered yet; however, T cells have long been thought to play a key role in injury as demonstrated by clonal proliferation of T cells in the peripheral blood and the disease response to calcineurin inhibitors. There is as well growing evidence about the potential role of B cells in MCD supported by the positive response to B-cell depletion with anti-CD20 therapy such as rituximab, and possibly restoring the balance between the regulatory and effector T cells.9, 10, 11

In studies focusing on AKI following CAR-T therapy, the cause has been attributed to CRS, owing to either a cytokine-mediated direct injury or hypotension that might develop in the context of a capillary leak. The incidence rates for AKI ranged from 5% to 33%, although the need for kidney replacement therapy in these cases was rare.4,12

Nonetheless, the development of severe proteinuria is rare and unexpected in patients with suspected ATN because it is typically suggestive of glomerular pathology, and this may explain the limited number of reported kidney biopsies after CAR-T cell therapy.

In a case series of 5 patients with lupus nephropathy who were treated with anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy, one patient developed proteinuria at month 4 of therapy with a kidney biopsy finding of MCD. He had good response to angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and a pulse of corticosteroids.13 TMA is another reported potential kidney complication and was reported by Wu et al8 in a multiple myeloma patient 3 months post CAR-T cell therapy. Postulated etiologies are direct endothelial injury from CAR-T cell, CRS, infection, or conditioning chemotherapy related injury.8 A case report by Acharya et al7 described a patient with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia in whom CRS developed after the third cycle of CD19-directed CAR-T therapy and he experienced nephrotic-range proteinuria. A kidney biopsy demonstrated collapsing focal segmental glomerulosclerosis with complete podocyte effacement, which was attributed to a direct cytokine-mediated podocyte injury or was secondary to what has been described as on-target, off-tumor toxicity of CAR-T therapy.14 Authors described this phenomenon in previous studies, including lung toxicity following ERBB2-targeted CAR-T therapy for colon cancer15 and liver toxicity following CAIX-targeted CAR-T therapy for renal cell cancer.16 Of note, nephrotic-range proteinuria and AKI developed in our patient 21 days after CAR-T therapy, which is beyond the usual time of 5-8 days for CRS following anti-BCMA therapy.17 Hence, a different immune-mediated mechanism should be considered. In our case, CAR-T therapy was directed against BCMA, which belongs to the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily found on malignant plasma cells and mature B cells. Even though B cells and plasma cells constitute a small portion of immune cells (∼1.5%) in the kidneys,18 CAR-T therapy directed against these cells may explain the on-target, off-tumor toxicity phenomenon in our case. However, there is no clear evidence of cross reactivity of BCMA-targeted CAR-T cell with kidney tubulointerstitium or glomeruli. Based on additional immunohistochemical staining of the patient kidney biopsy sample, our patient had a predominance of CD4 over CD8 T cells, with a ratio of 1.3, which may have been the infused CAR-T cells that infiltrated the kidneys. The biopsy showed limited infiltration by plasma cells with a ratio of 8.8 T cells relative to plasma cells, suggesting the BCMA-targeted CAR-T therapy may have been active against plasma cells infiltrating the kidney, leading to toxicity. Nonetheless, we did not conduct further studies to confirm the presence of the CAR-T cells or their target antigen in the kidney biopsy sample. Furthermore, in MCD the activation of CD80 on the podocytes can lead to podocytopathy. In normal conditions, CD80 expression on podocytes is minimal and regulated; however, direct binding of the podocyte by cytokines from activated T cells or in our case CAR-T cells can lead to actin rearrangements and results in proteinuria.19

Another possible mechanism of podocyte injury associated with CAR-T therapy is induction of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, also known as macrophage activation syndrome or more recently immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic lymphohistocytosis like Syndrome IEC-HS.20,21 Finally, our patient responded well to therapy and our rationale for using rituximab and a short course of prednisone was to avoid the potential for prolonged corticosteroid therapy to negatively impact CAR-T cell viability and efficacy.22 Figure 2 summarizes a proposed pathophysiology of AKI following CAR-T therapy.

Conclusion

Glomerular injury after CAR-T cell treatment is rare but potentially a serious complication that can be associated with severe kidney injury and proteinuria. Further studies are needed to better understand the mechanism of the injury but we speculate a potential on-target, off-tumor toxicity phenomenon. Treatment with immunosuppressive agents may improve this subgroup of patient’s kidney outcome; however, the potential treatment associated risk of compromising the proliferation and efficacy of CAR-T cells needs to be considered.
