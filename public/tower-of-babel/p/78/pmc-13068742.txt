Emergency laparotomies in trauma management: a six-year case series
Authors: Greta Ahrens, Michael Hoffmann, Nils Proksch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEmergency laparotomies, defined as procedures performed within 24 hours of hospital admission following trauma, are associated with considerable mortality. Notably, blunt abdominal injuries carry higher mortality rates compared to penetrating injuries. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate whether emergency trauma laparotomies performed at our Level I trauma center—serving a population with a comparatively higher proportion of penetrating injuries—achieve outcomes comparable to contemporary benchmark data.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of patients treated between 2019 and 2024 in the surgical trauma bay at Asklepios Klinik St. Georg, Hamburg, who underwent laparotomy within 24 hours of hospital admission. Mortality was defined as survival until hospital discharge. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS).ResultsA total of 64 patients were included. Of these, 39.0% sustained penetrating trauma and 61.0% underwent laparotomy following blunt trauma. Among penetrating injuries, stab wounds predominated (92.0%), while 8.0% resulted from gunshot wounds. The most frequent mechanisms of blunt trauma were traffic accidents (30.8%) and falls from height (25.6%). Overall survival was 71.9% (mortality 20.0% for penetrating trauma; 33.3% for blunt trauma). Prehospital cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) had been performed in 18.8% of patients. The majority of incidents (57.8%) occurred during daytime hours (08:00–20:00).Survivors received significantly fewer units of packed red blood cells during resuscitation (3.1 vs. 12.4; p < 0.0001) and were more frequently operated on in the central operating room under cardiopulmonary stability. The mean GOS among survivors was 4.6 ± 0.8. The overall Injury Severity Score (ISS) averaged 34.5, while survivors had a significantly lower mean ISS of 17.9 (p < 0.0001).ConclusionEmergency laparotomy remains a valuable treatment option that offers a substantial chance of survival for haemodynamically unstable patients with both blunt and penetrating trauma. A structured trauma care, rapid recognition of physiologic extremis, and timely operative intervention within an organized Level I trauma system can achieve survival outcomes comparable to contemporary benchmark data, even in a severely injured cohort.

Introduction

Emergency laparotomies, defined as laparotomies performed within 24 h of hospital admission following trauma, are associated with a high mortality rate. Outcomes differ considerably depending on the mechanism of injury, particularly between penetrating and blunt abdominal trauma.

Penetrating abdominal injuries, such as stab or gunshot wounds, account for a relatively small proportion of abdominal trauma cases in most European countries [1]. Recent studies have reported an incidence below 10%, although these injuries are associated with comparatively higher mortality rates [2–4]. In contrast, in low- and middle-income countries, the incidence may be substantially higher — up to 48% in Nigeria [5]. However, an increasing trend in penetrating trauma has been observed in Germany, underscoring the growing clinical relevance of this injury pattern in urban trauma care [6, 27].

Penetrating trauma is typically characterized by localized injury patterns, but stab and gunshot wounds often cause extensive tissue destruction and major vascular or visceral injury, necessitating immediate surgical intervention. In haemodynamically unstable patients or those with active intra-abdominal bleeding, laparotomy remains the treatment of choice to achieve rapid haemorrhage control [7].

Blunt abdominal trauma represents one of the most frequent indications for emergency laparotomy, commonly resulting from motor vehicle collisions, falls from height, or violent assaults [8]. In contrast to penetrating injuries, the entry site and extent of internal damage are often not apparent on initial assessment, and the pattern of injury can be multifocal, involving multiple organs [9]. This contributes to the diagnostic challenge, as early symptoms may be nonspecific, yet clinical deterioration can occur rapidly if not recognized and treated promptly.

The management algorithm for abdominal trauma recommends immediate laparotomy in haemodynamically unstable patients with a positive FAST (Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma) as part of damage control surgery (DCS) [10]. In contrast, haemodynamically stable patients may be managed with diagnostic laparoscopy or selective non-operative management (SNOM) under close clinical monitoring when imaging and examination findings are inconspicuous [2].

Emergency trauma laparotomy remains one of the most resource-intensive and physiologically demanding procedures in trauma care. While mortality rates have been well described, less is known about how contemporary trauma systems with high levels of structural preparedness perform in real-world settings, particularly in populations with mixed blunt and penetrating mechanisms.

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate whether emergency trauma laparotomies performed at our Level I trauma center—serving a population with a comparatively higher proportion of penetrating injuries—achieve outcomes comparable to contemporary benchmark data. Beyond confirming established associations between physiological instability, injury severity, and mortality, we sought to characterize the physiological profile, operative patterns, and short-term functional outcomes of this cohort in order to assess trauma system performance in a real-world, high-acuity setting and identify potential areas for quality improvement.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective study included all patients who underwent an emergency laparotomy as part of trauma management between 2019 and 2024. Both patient groups with blunt and/ or penetrating abdominal trauma who received a laparotomy within the first 24 h after hospital admission were included.

All patients were initially assessed and treated according to the principles of Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS®) by trauma surgeons at a Level I trauma centre. Patients of all ages were eligible for inclusion. Data were extracted from the institutional trauma registry and supplemented with information from electronic medical records.

Patient selection

Inclusion criteria comprised all adult trauma patients undergoing open laparotomy for abdominal injuries. Patients managed non-operatively or laparoscopically were not included; therefore, findings apply specifically to the laparotomy cohort and should not be extrapolated to the broader abdominal trauma population. Mechanism of injury was recorded and categorized as blunt or penetrating; despite the heterogeneity within blunt trauma (e.g., traffic collisions, falls, crush injuries), pathophysiological mechanisms (direct compression or deceleration) were considered sufficiently coherent to permit descriptive analysis of outcomes.

Definitions, parameters, and outcome measures

Emergency laparotomy was defined as any laparotomy performed within 24 h of hospital admission due to either penetrating or blunt abdominal trauma.

The primary endpoints of this study were mortality and functional outcome among survivors. Mortality was defined as survival until hospital discharge. Functional outcome was evaluated using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS).

Secondary variables included demographic data (age, sex, time of injury), Injury Severity Score (ISS), mechanism of injury, transfusion requirements within the first 24 h, and relevant laboratory parameters.

Statistical analysis and ethics approval

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For variables meeting normality assumptions, group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA with post-hoc testing. Non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with pairwise comparisons where appropriate. Analyses were conducted using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. An ethics committee approval was obtained (reference number: 2025-101630-BO-ff).

Results

A total of 64 patients who underwent an emergency laparotomy were included in this study. The overall mortality rate in this cohort was 29.5%. When differentiated by mechanism of injury, mortality was 20% in penetrating trauma and 33.3% in blunt trauma.

Demographic data

During the six-year study period, a total of 2,111 trauma patients were treated in our trauma resuscitation area. The present study exclusively includes the 64 patients who underwent emergency open laparotomy. Patients managed non-operatively or laparoscopically were not included.

During the study period, 64 emergency laparotomies were performed (Table 1). The mean age of the patients was 45.27 ± 17.06 years, with no statistically significant difference between survivors (44.33 ± 16.34 years) and non-survivors (47.67 ± 19.04 years) (p > 0.99) (Fig. 1).

A total of 79.69% of patients were male. Prehospital cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was required in 18.75% of all cases. Among non-survivors, 55.56% had already undergone prehospital CPR.

Mechanism of injury

In 39% of all cases, emergency laparotomy was performed because of a penetrating injury. The predominant cause was stab wounds (92%), followed by gunshot wounds (8%). Among patients with penetrating injuries, 20% mortality was observed; only patients who had sustained stab wounds survived. For blunt trauma, the most common mechanisms of injury were motor vehicle accidents (30.78%) and falls from great height (> 3 m, 25.64%). Overall, 71.88% of all patients survived (mortality: 20% in penetrating vs. 33.3% in blunt trauma). Most injuries occurred during the daytime (57.81%, 08:00–20:00).

Clinical and laboratory parameters

The mean systolic blood pressure on admission was 114.6 ± 48.96 mmHg. Non-survivors had significantly lower values (71.36 ± 63.67 mmHg) compared with survivors (126.5 ± 36.88 mmHg, p = 0.0287). The mean heart rate was 98.43 ± 34.09 bpm, without significant differences between survivors (104.1 ± 25.73 bpm) and non-survivors (80.62 ± 49.73 bpm, p > 0.99).

A significant difference was observed in the initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS): non-survivors presented with a lower GCS (7.64 ± 5.36) compared to survivors (12.28 ± 4.1, p = 0.0127). Similarly, oxygen saturation was markedly reduced in non-survivors (76.64 ± 28.32%) compared to survivors (94.54 ± 5.15%, p = 0.0067).

Arterial blood gas analysis (BGA) revealed significant differences between groups. Non-survivors had lower haemoglobin levels (9.36 ± 2.894 g/dl vs. 11.83 ± 3.094 g/dl; p = 0.0172), lower pH (7.052 ± 0.21 vs. 7.327 ± 0.08; p = 0.0006), and more negative base excess (BE) (− 15.14 ± 9.7 vs. −1.519 ± 3.86 mmol/l; p < 0.0001). Lactate levels were significantly higher among non-survivors (p = 0.0005).

Prehospital intubation was performed in 37.5% of patients, and 15.6% had already undergone cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) prior to hospital admission (as summarized in Table 1).

Outcome

Within the polytrauma management pathway, survivors required significantly fewer red blood cell transfusions (3.08 ± 6.4 vs. 12.44 ± 7.4 units; p < 0.0001) and were more frequently operated in the central operating theatre when hemodynamically stable (70.31%) compared to emergency department laparotomy (29.69%), where the mortality rate was 68.4%. In two cases, diagnostic laparoscopy preceded laparotomy.

Among the 64 patients, 71.88% survived the laparotomy. Two patients later died because of septic complications, so the overall survival rate was 70.5% (mortality: 20% in penetrating vs. 33.3% in blunt trauma) (Fig. 2). The most frequent cause of death was haemorrhagic shock (66.67%), followed by hypoxic brain injury (22.22%). Sepsis (11.11%) and multiple organ failure (5.56%) were less common causes.

Among solid organ injuries, the liver and spleen represented the most frequently affected organs. Liver injuries demonstrated a mean AAST grade of 2.76 ± 1.30, with injuries distributed across grades I to V, indicating a wide spectrum of injury severity within the cohort (as summarized in Table 2). Splenic injuries showed a slightly higher mean severity (AAST grade 2.93 ± 1.03), with most cases clustered in grades II–IV. These findings highlight the predominance of moderate to severe parenchymal injuries among patients requiring operative management.

Hollow viscus injuries were also frequently observed. Small bowel injuries demonstrated a mean AAST grade of 2.06 ± 0.94, with the majority of injuries falling within grades I–III. Similarly, large bowel injuries exhibited a mean AAST grade of 2.11 ± 1.10, with injuries ranging from minor serosal damage to more advanced lesions requiring operative repair. Gastric injuries were less common and generally of lower severity, with a mean AAST grade of 2 ± 1.

Mesenteric injuries occurred less frequently and were predominantly low-grade lesions (mean AAST grade 1.67 ± 1.12). In contrast, vascular injuries were identified in 13 cases and represented a clinically significant subset of patients with complex hemorrhagic pathology. Retroperitoneal bleeding was documented in six cases, while pelvic bleeding was present in eight patients, reflecting the involvement of additional bleeding sources outside the intraperitoneal cavity.

Urological injuries were identified in a small number of cases, with a mean AAST grade of 2.2 ± 1.3, indicating predominantly moderate injury severity. Pancreatic injuries were rare within the present cohort and occurred in only two cases.

A wide range of operative procedures were performed depending on injury type and intraoperative findings. Abdominal packing represented the most frequently performed intervention (n = 35), reflecting the substantial proportion of patients presenting with severe hemorrhage and physiological instability requiring damage control strategies (as summarized in Table 3).

Primary repair of bowel injuries was performed in 15 patients and represented the most common management strategy for hollow viscus injuries. Splenectomy was required in 13 cases, consistent with the frequency and severity of splenic injuries observed in the cohort.

Management of liver injuries included suture repair or the application of fibrin sealant in 10 cases. In one patient, a non-anatomical liver resection was required due to the extent of parenchymal injury. Vascular injuries necessitated repair or ligation in 11 cases, highlighting the operative complexity associated with hemorrhagic trauma.

Nephrectomy was performed in one patient due to severe renal injury not amenable to organ-preserving management.

Median laparotomy represented the predominant surgical approach and was performed in 57 patients, reflecting its role as the standard access for emergency trauma laparotomy (compare in Table 4). Alternative surgical approaches were used in selected cases, including upper midline laparotomy (n = 5), Kocher laparotomy (n = 2), and mini-laparotomy (n = 1), depending on injury localization and operative requirements

With regard to abdominal closure, primary closure was achieved in 31 patients following definitive or damage control procedures (as summarized in Table 5). Temporary abdominal closure was required in 24 patients, reflecting the need for staged surgical management in the setting of ongoing physiological instability or planned re-exploration.

In nine patients, definitive abdominal closure could not be performed because death occurred intraoperatively prior to completion of surgical management.

Discussion

In the present study, a total of 64 patients who underwent emergency laparotomy as part of polytrauma management were analysed. The overall mortality rate was 29.5%, with marked differences depending on the mechanism of injury. Patients with penetrating trauma showed a mortality rate of 20%, whereas those with blunt trauma had a mortality rate of 33.3%. Notably more than half of the patients who required prehospital cardiopulmonary resuscitation (55.6%) did not survive, while patients with stable haemodynamics and surgery performed in the central operating theatre demonstrated significantly higher survival rates. These findings highlight the high lethality of severe abdominal trauma and emphasize the importance of haemodynamic stability and timely surgical intervention for patient survival.

In the reported timeframe a total of 2111 patients were treated in our resuscitation room. Out of these 2111 patients 64 underwent an emergency laparotomy, equaling 3%.

The relatively low number of emergency laparotomies in our cohort reflects contemporary abdominal trauma management rather than limited institutional exposure. The present study intentionally included only open emergency laparotomies and excluded patients managed non-operatively or laparoscopically. In modern trauma systems, only a minority of abdominal trauma patients ultimately require open surgery: registry and single-center data indicate that approximately 25% of blunt trauma patients and 25–35% of penetrating stab injuries undergo laparotomy [11–13]. Furthermore, reported rates of negative or non-therapeutic laparotomies range from 4 to 61%, underscoring the established role of selective non-operative management, particularly in blunt trauma [14]. During the study period, penetrating trauma accounted for only a small proportion of all patients treated in the trauma resuscitation area (approximately 8% of the total trauma population), further contributing to the limited number of laparotomies observed. As the majority of abdominal injuries in this setting resulted from blunt trauma, and most blunt abdominal injuries are currently managed non-operatively—often with adjunctive interventional radiology—the proportion of patients ultimately requiring open laparotomy remained comparatively low. In addition, the increasing availability and diagnostic reliability of laparoscopic exploration in hemodynamically stable patients has further reduced the need for open laparotomy; systematic reviews report a missed-injury rate of only 1.4%, with the majority of procedures being therapeutic and a minority requiring conversion to open surgery [15]. Taken together, the observed case volume is consistent with current European trauma practice characterized by selective operative indication, low penetrating trauma rates, and integration of minimally invasive strategies.

In the subgroup of non-survivors, the time to laparotomy was significantly shorter compared to survivors. This finding primarily reflects the greater haemodynamic instability and higher injury severity (ISS) observed in this group, necessitating immediate surgical intervention. Approximately one-third of the cohort required immediate laparotomy in the resuscitation room, which was associated with a significantly increased mortality of 68.4%. Importantly, this association should not be interpreted as an intrinsic risk of expedited operative pathways, but rather as a consequence of profound physiological extremis. In our cohort, abbreviated time-to-surgery was predominantly driven by severe haemorrhagic shock and impending or manifest circulatory collapse. Patients in extremis or peri-arrest were transferred directly to the operating room or underwent trauma-bay laparotomy because of immediate life-threatening conditions. Therefore, shortened time-to-laparotomy should primarily be understood as a surrogate marker of injury severity and physiological collapse rather than as a modifiable performance indicator.

Trauma-bay laparotomies were performed exclusively in patients with profound hemodynamic instability or peri-arrest to minimize transport delay. The trauma resuscitation area is structurally equipped to function as a fully operative environment, including sterile setup and complete surgical instrumentation. These procedures were reserved for extreme physiological compromise and do not represent routine practice.

At the same time, the reduced time-to-laparotomy also reflects structural preparedness within the trauma system. The German prehospital trauma system operates under a centralized dispatch model with structured pre-notification of major trauma cases. Early communication between prehospital teams and the receiving trauma center allows advance activation of the resuscitation team and operative staff. In cases of suspected exsanguinating hemorrhage, the surgical team may already be assembled and operative equipment prepared prior to patient arrival. We therefore interpret our findings as follows: primarily as a marker of injury severity and hemodynamic instability, and secondarily as an indicator of efficient trauma system activation and operational readiness. Importantly, we do not interpret these findings as evidence of operational inefficiency or as suggesting that expedited surgery is intrinsically harmful.

The indications for emergency laparotomy are diverse and depend largely on the type and extent of injury. In patients sustaining violent trauma such as stab wounds, gunshot injuries, or major accidents, the decision to proceed with laparotomy must be made rapidly and precisely, as it is often crucial for survival and requires an individualized, context-sensitive approach [16].

Prompt identification and treatment of patients with potentially life-threatening injuries are essential to prevent complications such as sepsis, multiple organ failure, or permanent disability.

In this cohort, haemorrhagic shock represented the predominant cause of death, observed in approximately two-thirds of non-survivors, highlighting the urgent need for rapid haemostatic intervention. Notably sepsis was responsible for approximately 10% of fatalities, emphasizing the ongoing relevance of infectious complications following abdominal trauma.

Emergency laparotomy is indicated in the presence of haemodynamic instability, peritonitis, evisceration, or impalement, as these conditions represent critical scenarios requiring immediate surgical intervention to control intra-abdominal bleeding and contamination [17].

The observed mortality of 29.5% aligns with published benchmark data for trauma laparotomy. Given the high proportion of physiologically unstable patients, this rate reflects expected risk rather than excessive mortality. Our findings confirm that outcome following emergency laparotomy is predominantly driven by preoperative physiology and injury severity. Shorter time-to-surgery and trauma-bay laparotomy should therefore be interpreted as markers of injury severity rather than indicators of operational inefficiency.

Several recent studies have examined mortality rates associated with emergency laparotomy in trauma patients. A meta-analysis from Japan reported an overall mortality rate of approximately 30% [18, 19], while a Dutch study observed a comparable rate of 16.7% [20], with slightly higher mortality in cases of penetrating trauma (20%). In contrast, a study from the United States reported mortality rates as high as 46% [21]. These figures underline the high-risk nature of emergency laparotomy, with outcomes influenced by factors such as injury severity, physiological status, and timeliness of surgical management. The mortality rates observed in our study (20% in penetrating and 33% in blunt trauma) are consistent with those reported by Hietbrink et al., while overall survival was higher compared to the U.S. cohort described by Harvin et al.

While mortality following emergency laparotomy has been well documented, data on neurological outcomes (e.g., cognitive function, neurological deficits) remain scarce. A recent study assessing long-term outcomes after emergency laparotomy demonstrated that, although overall quality of life normalized in most patients within 180 days, approximately 7% continued to experience persistently reduced health-related quality of life, including impairments in the domain of mental health, which may indirectly reflect neurological sequelae [22]. The use of GOS provides a global assessment of discharge status. A mean of 4.6 suggests functional independence in most survivors. However, this measure does not capture surgery-specific morbidity or long-term recovery. Further research is warranted to better define neurological and functional outcomes following emergency laparotomy.

One factor contributing to persistently high mortality rates in abdominal trauma is the increasing preference for a selective non-operative management strategy. As a consequence, rates of non-therapeutic (“negative”) laparotomies have declined, with recent reports citing incidences of approximately 12% [23]. While this trend appropriately reduces unnecessary surgical interventions, it also increases the threshold for operative decision-making, meaning that patients ultimately undergoing laparotomy often present with more advanced physiological derangement. This development underscores the diagnostic and clinical challenges inherent in balancing rapid decision-making with avoidance of unnecessary surgery.

The study period spans six years, during which trauma care is subject to ongoing refinement in imaging strategies, resuscitation concepts, and operative decision-making. However, no fundamentally new international guidelines were introduced that substantially altered the core principles of abdominal trauma management during this interval. The overarching concepts of damage control surgery, permissive hypotension, balanced transfusion strategies, and selective non-operative management of solid organ injuries remained consistent. Institutional protocols at our center were therefore largely stable throughout the study period.

Nevertheless, gradual evolution in practice patterns may occur over time even in the absence of major guideline shifts. We did not perform a formal year-by-year subgroup analysis, as this was not the primary objective of the study; such an analysis might reveal subtle temporal trends and could be considered in future evaluations. An important external factor during the later part of the study period was the COVID-19 pandemic. During this time, overall trauma volume declined, consistent with national and international observations. In addition, institutional infection-control policies required preoperative SARS-CoV-2 testing in hemodynamically stable patients. While unstable patients proceeded immediately to surgery under full protective precautions, operative intervention in stable patients could be delayed until test results were available. This may have influenced workflow timing in selected cases, although operative indications themselves remained unchanged. The decision-making process for emergency laparotomy typically begins with a diagnostic assessment, most commonly the extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (eFAST), which allows for rapid detection of free intraperitoneal fluid — a key indicator of intra-abdominal injury [24]. Subsequent management decisions, including whether to proceed with laparotomy or consider less invasive approaches, depend on the eFAST findings, haemodynamic stability, and response to initial resuscitation.

In recent years, laparoscopy has emerged as a potential alternative to traditional open laparotomy in selected cases of blunt abdominal trauma. However, its exact role remains uncertain due to limited evidence. Laparoscopy can be safely performed in both blunt and penetrating trauma if the patient remains haemodynamically stable and the appropriate expertise is available [25–27]. It offers advantages such as reduced morbidity and avoidance of unnecessary laparotomies. Some studies report that laparoscopy can prevent laparotomy in up to 38% of cases, particularly in penetrating injuries with peritoneal violation. Nevertheless, its sensitivity for detecting gastrointestinal or hollow-organ injuries remains relatively low, with some studies reporting sensitivities as low as 18%, limiting its diagnostic reliability [23].

In the present retrospective study, only patients who underwent laparotomy were included; therefore, differences in outcome and mortality between emergency laparoscopy and laparotomy could not be assessed and should be explored in future studies.

In a retrospective analysis by Saillant et al. (2015), patients requiring emergency laparotomy for severe abdominal trauma demonstrated a mean Injury Severity Score (ISS) of 27.5, often due to multifocal injuries involving the liver, spleen, or bowel [17]. Similarly, van Gool et al. (2015) reported a mean ISS of 24.6 in trauma patients with combined abdominal and polytraumatic injuries, frequently including thoracic or cranial involvement, contributing to higher injury severity [28]. Gunshot and blast injuries were associated with particularly high ISS values, reflecting the extensive organ damage and massive haemorrhage typical of these mechanisms [29]. The patients in our study exhibited a mean ISS of 34.47 ± 27.6, comparable to previously published data, underscoring the severity of injuries necessitating emergency laparotomy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations.

First, it represents a retrospective, single-centre analysis, which may be subject to selection bias. The results therefore primarily reflect the treatment standards of one Level I trauma centre and may not be generalizable to other institutions.

Second, the sample size (n = 64) was relatively small, limiting the statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses (e.g., penetrating vs. blunt trauma; shock room vs. central operating theatre).

Importantly, the study was restricted to open laparotomy cases. Patients managed non-operatively or laparoscopically were not included, limiting extrapolation to the broader abdominal trauma population.

Finally, the functional outcome was assessed solely using the Glasgow Outcome Scale at hospital discharge. Long-term follow-up regarding quality of life, neurological recovery, and rehabilitation status was not performed; therefore, conclusions about long-term outcomes remain limited.

Future prospective, multicentre studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate these findings and further clarify the role of emergency laparotomy within modern trauma care.

Conclusion

Emergency trauma laparotomy remains a critical life-saving intervention in severely injured patients with abdominal trauma. Mortality is primarily determined by physiological instability and injury severity rather than operative timing per se. The observed unadjusted in-hospital mortality of 29.5% in our cohort aligns with previously published trauma laparotomy series.

A structured trauma care, rapid recognition of physiologic extremis, and timely operative intervention within an organized Level I trauma system can achieve survival outcomes comparable to contemporary benchmark data, even in a severely injured cohort. Conceptually, our findings reinforce that patient physiology and injury severity—rather than the performance of laparotomy itself—remain the dominant determinants of outcome. Detailed physiological and laboratory characterization may therefore assist in early risk stratification, resource allocation, and perioperative management.

Future studies should further delineate the role of emergency laparotomy within the evolving landscape of trauma management and clarify its value in relation to increasingly selective operative strategies.
