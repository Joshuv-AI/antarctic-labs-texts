Brain monitoring during anesthesia and critical care: a call for research, collaboration, and implementation
Authors: Michele Introna, M Kreuzer, A R Absalom
Published: 2026

The brain is both the target and the vulnerable organ in much of perioperative and critical care medicine. While we have seen major advances in anesthetic drugs, ventilatory support, hemodynamic monitoring, and organ-protective strategies, monitoring of the effect of sedative and anesthetic on the brain is still not part of routine care. In fact, in respect of this our practise remains on the same technical level it was decades ago. Among anesthesiologists that do incorporate processed electroencephalography (pEEG) monitoring in their practice, reliance on proprietary indices is the current standard [3], despite the fact that the indices have been questioned since their introduction, and a growing body of scientific knowledge on how the EEG can be better interpreted in ways that could help to improve patient care. This Special Collection, Brain Monitoring During Anesthesia and Critical Care, has been conceived to help address that gap.

Over the last two decades, there has been growing awareness that simple processed EEG indices do not suffice and that we have to update our algorithms, educate our clinicians [1], and move toward a broader and more nuanced concept of multimodal neuromonitoring [2]. In contemporary practice we can use EEG, cerebral oximetry, evoked responses, autonomic markers, and advanced signal analysis to not only assess individual responses to anesthetic and sedative drugs,, but also to explore brain vulnerability, perioperative recovery, and the neurological consequences of critical illness. We need to understand that the electroencephalogram must interpreted in the context of the individual patient, where pharmacology, systemic physiology, age, frailty, neurological vulnerability, surgical stress, and critical care evolution all interact [4]. We further have to extend this knowledge from the operating room to the ICU to cover all situations where a patient receives anesthetic or sedative drugs affecting brain activity.

In the operating room the initial purpose of brain monitoring was to titrate drug doses to achieve a certain “anesthetic depth” to prevent intraoperative awareness [5]. While these objectives remain important, they are no longer sufficient to capture the breadth of contemporary practice. The focus of using the devices has shifted towards investigating the relationship between intraoperative EEG patterns and postoperative outcomes, including delirium, postoperative pain, cognitive dysfunction, quality of emergence, and recovery trajectories [6–8]. There is also a consensus that brain monitoring needs to move away from a one-dimensional “depth” monitoring by incorporating important information about (i) the nociception-antinociception balanceof the brain (REF), (ii) the assessment of brain frailty, or (iii) the use of adjunct, non-GABAergic drugs. We need to shift this focus towards a more holistic approach of protecting the individual brain before, during, and after anesthesia.

As mentioned, this concept needs to be extended to the intensive care where we face additional challenges. Sedation targets in critically ill patients differ fundamentally from those in the operating room. ICU patients may require prolonged sedation, are exposed to rapidly changing physiology, frequently receive multiple interacting drugs, and often present with sepsis, respiratory failure, shock, or primary neurological injury. In this setting, interpretation of (processed) EEG information and other brain-monitoring modalities is more complex, yet potentially even more important [9]. Better monitoring could support more individualized sedation, reduce both under- and oversedation, improve comfort and safety, and perhaps contribute to lower cognitive burden after critical illness.

At the same time, several important questions remain unresolved. Which signals are robust enough for routine bedside use? How can we educate clinicians to correctly interpret EEG-derived variables across different age groups, anesthetic agents, and disease states [10]? Which monitoring strategies improve meaningful patient-centered outcomes, rather than surrogate endpoints alone? How do we move from device availability to true physiological understanding and clinically actionable interpretation [11]? How can we collaborate to create data bases that allow for high quality data mining, etc.? These questions are especially relevant to improve monitoring in vulnerable populations, including older adults, children, neurocritical care patients, and those with pre-existing cognitive impairment or neurological disease[12].

This Special Collection aims to capture the richness and complexity of this rapidly developing field. We welcome original research, narrative and systematic reviews, methodological contributions, translational studies, and clinically focused perspectives addressing brain monitoring during anesthesia and critical care. Particularly relevant topics include, but are not limited to, raw and processed EEG interpretation, multimodal neuromonitoring, cerebral oxygenation, delirium and postoperative neurocognitive outcomes, individualized anesthetic titration, ICU sedation monitoring, pharmacology-informed monitoring strategies, machine learning and artificial intelligence applications, and new approaches to integrating neuromonitoring into perioperative and critical care workflows.

We also encourage submissions that move beyond a proof-of-concept as we need studies that clarify external validity, help us understand and overcome implementation barriers, highlight training needs, and assess real-world clinical impact. Monitoring technologies will only fulfil their promise if they can be meaningfully incorporated into bedside care, interpreted by educated clinicians with confidence, and linked to interventions that improve outcomes. In this sense, the future of brain monitoring will depend as much on clinical translation and education as on engineering innovation.

The title of this Special Collection intentionally spans both anesthesia and critical care to help close a translational knowledge gap. Historically, these domains have often developed in parallel, despite sharing common pharmacological principles, overlapping monitoring challenges, and a common goal- the safe and individualized management of the injured, vulnerable, or therapeutically altered brain. We believe that closer dialogue between anesthesiologists, intensivists, neurologists, neurophysiologists, engineers, and data scientists is essential as the most important advances in this field are likely to emerge through collaboration and interdisciplinarity.

As Guest Editors, we hope this Special Collection will serve not only as a repository of high-quality scholarship, but also as a platform for shaping our discipline’s future. Brain monitoring should no longer be viewed as a niche or optional layer of care based on the interpretation of heavily processed information by personnel that are often untrained in the use the raw EEG or its spectral representation. Appropriate brain monitoring should be an essential component of a broader movement toward brain-aware, physiology-guided, and outcome-oriented perioperative and critical care medicine.

We invite investigators and clinicians from across disciplines to contribute their work and perspectives to this collection. By bringing together methodological rigor, clinical relevance, and technological innovation, we hope to advance a field whose ultimate purpose is clear: to better understand, protect, and care for the brain in some of the most challenging environments in medicine.
