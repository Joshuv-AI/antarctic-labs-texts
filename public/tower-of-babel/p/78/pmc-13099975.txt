24-week multidimensional predictors of return to play post-ACLR in high-sports demanders: a randomized trial
Authors: Feng Hao, Niu Yuhong, Yang Xuyuan, Cui Mu, Li Qiangze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Predicting successful return to play (RTP) during mid-stage rehabilitation after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) remains a critical, unresolved clinical challenge. High-sports demanders (HSDs)—non-professional athletes participating in level-1 sports (cutting, pivoting, jumping) ≥ 3 times/week with Tegner score > 5—represent a distinct subgroup who face unique rehabilitation challenges yet lack evidence-based mid-stage prediction tools. The primary objective of this study was to identify multidimensional predictors at 24 weeks post-ACLR for successful RTP at 48 weeks, and to construct a clinical scoring tool to guide individualized intervention decisions during mid-stage rehabilitation. This single-blind randomized controlled trial was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Medical University, Xi’an, China (April 2024–May 2025). Sixty-four HSDs post-primary ACLR were randomized to a functional rehabilitation model (FRM; n = 32) or traditional rehabilitation model (TRM; n = 32). At 24 weeks, isokinetic strength, hop tests, mSEBT, proprioception, and patient-reported outcomes (IKDC, Lysholm, ACL-RSI) were assessed. Forward stepwise logistic regression with bootstrap internal validation (1,000 iterations) identified independent predictors of 48-week RTP success. Among 57 completers (89.1% follow-up), the overall RTP rate was 75.4%. FRM significantly outperformed TRM (89.3% vs. 62.1%; P = 0.038; NNT = 3.7). Six independent predictors were identified: FRM vs. TRM (OR = 3.49), 60°/s extensor LSI ≥ 77.8% (OR = 2.13), single-leg hop ≥ 68.5 cm (OR = 1.87), ACL-RSI ≥ 67.5 (OR = 1.69), mSEBT anterior ≥ 63.2 cm (OR = 1.39), and 45° proprioceptive error ≤ 6.1° (OR = 0.62). Apparent AUC = 0.87 (95% CI: 0.79–0.95); bootstrap-corrected AUC = 0.81 (95% CI: 0.72–0.90). This exploratory six-factor model demonstrates promising discriminative ability (apparent AUC = 0.87; bootstrap-corrected AUC = 0.81) for mid-rehabilitation RTP prediction in HSDs. The identified predictive associations, rather than established causal relationships, may facilitate early risk identification and individualized intervention planning. External validation in independent, multicenter cohorts is required before clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43911-z.

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is one of the most common and impactful knee injuries in sports medicine, with an annual incidence of approximately 75 per 100,000 person-years in the general population and up to 4.5 per 100 athlete-years in high-risk athletic populations (e.g., female semi-professional handball)1,2. Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR), as the gold standard treatment for restoring knee stability and function, ultimately aims to enable patients to safely and effectively return to their pre-injury level of play3,4. However, despite continuous improvements in surgical techniques and evolving rehabilitation concepts, the success rate of return to play (RTP) remains far from ideal. Only about 80% of patients return to any sport after ACLR, with an even lower proportion of approximately 55% returning to their pre-injury play level5,6. More concerning is that among those who successfully return to play, the incidence of secondary ACL injury ranges from 6 to 25%, far exceeding the injury risk in healthy populations7–9. These data indicate significant deficiencies in current return-to-play assessment and decision-making systems, highlighting an urgent need for more precise and comprehensive predictive tools to guide clinical practice.

Among post-ACLR populations, HSDs constitute a distinct and important subgroup. HSDs are defined as non-professional athletes who actively participate in sports activities ≥ 3 times per week, have a Tegner activity score > 5 (a threshold that corresponds to competitive recreational sports involving cutting, pivoting, and jumping; this cut-off has been widely validated in ACL research to differentiate high-demand from low-demand athletic populations, with scores of 6–9 representing competitive recreational to elite-level activities10, and primarily engage in level 1 sports involving cutting, pivoting, and jumping movements10. This population represents a substantial proportion of ACL injury patients, with studies reporting that 70–80% of ACL injuries occur in recreational athletes participating in level 1 sports11.

The HSD population faces unique rehabilitation challenges and return-to-play demands that distinguish them from other patient groups. First, compared to professional athletes, HSDs lack continuous support from specialized medical teams, individualized rehabilitation program design, and access to high-quality rehabilitation facilities12. Second, compared to recreational sport participants, HSDs have higher expectations for athletic performance, more stringent criteria for successful return to play, and face higher-intensity sport loads and injury risks13. Third, rehabilitation adherence and motivation levels in the HSD population typically fall between those of professional athletes and recreational exercisers, and this moderate level of rehabilitation engagement may impact final functional recovery outcomes14. These unique characteristics underscore the need for tailored predictive frameworks that capture the multidimensional nature of HSD rehabilitation.

Traditional return-to-play decision-making has primarily relied on time-based criteria (e.g., 6–9 months postoperatively) or single functional tests, which have proven insufficient to accurately reflect individual rehabilitation status and return-to-play readiness15,16. In recent years, researchers have begun focusing on multidimensional assessment approaches, including strength testing, jump tests, balance assessment, and psychological readiness scales17–19. However, a critical clinical question remains: when can final return-to-play outcomes be accurately predicted? Identifying an optimal mid-stage timepoint for such prediction could transform clinical management of HSDs.

From a clinical practice perspective, 24 weeks postoperatively (approximately 6 months) represents a critical assessment timepoint. At this stage, patients have completed the basic rehabilitation phase, most physiological indicators have stabilized, and both clinicians and patients urgently need to understand the likelihood of eventual return to play to timely adjust rehabilitation strategies or psychological expectations20,21. If return-to-play success at approximately 12 months postoperatively can be accurately predicted at this timepoint, it would provide crucial guidance for individualized rehabilitation decision-making.

Existing return-to-play prediction research has primarily focused on several aspects: Grindem22 found in their cohort study that each 1% decrease in quadriceps limb symmetry index increased reinjury risk by 3%, emphasizing the importance of strength recovery. The six clinical discharge criteria proposed by Kyritsis23 significantly reduced reinjury rates, but these criteria were primarily applied immediately before return to play. Ardern24 found in their prospective study that psychological readiness was an independent predictor of successful return to play, with importance even exceeding certain objective functional indicators. Despite these advances, the evidence base for mid-stage prediction specifically in HSDs remains insufficient.

However, significant gaps remain in existing research regarding the HSD population: first, there is a lack of studies specifically predicting return to play in HSDs during mid-stage rehabilitation; second, most studies focus on final assessment before return to play rather than early prediction during mid-stage rehabilitation; third, there is an absence of comprehensive predictive models integrating multiple dimensions to guide decision-making during mid-stage rehabilitation25. While several existing prediction models have incorporated physical or psychological variables measured at end-stage rehabilitation (typically 6–9 months), no study to date has attempted to develop a multidimensional predictive model specifically at the 24-week mid-rehabilitation time point in HSDs, where intervention adjustments remain feasible. Our model is distinguished by its integration of rehabilitation model type as a predictor alongside physical, functional, balance, proprioceptive, and psychological domains.

Dynamic balance control, as an important indicator reflecting neuromuscular function integration, has gained increasing attention in post-ACLR rehabilitation assessment. The modified Star Excursion Balance Test (mSEBT) has been proven effective in evaluating lower extremity dynamic stability and is significantly associated with reinjury risk26. Proprioceptive function, as an important component of knee joint stability, shows significant deficits after ACL injury, but its value in early prediction requires further clarification27. Together, these neuromuscular indicators represent essential domains that any comprehensive mid-stage predictive model should incorporate.

The choice of rehabilitation protocol has significant impact on return-to-play outcomes, yet this factor is often overlooked in predictive models. Functional rehabilitation protocols emphasizing neuromuscular control, sport-specific training, and individualized progression criteria may have advantages in improving return-to-play rates28. Including rehabilitation protocol type in predictive models not only improves prediction accuracy but also provides evidence-based guidance for rehabilitation strategy selection.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically evaluate the predictive value of multidimensional indicators at 24 weeks post-ACLR for return-to-play success at 48 weeks in the HSD population through a rigorous prospective randomized controlled trial design, establish an early comprehensive predictive model, and provide scientific evidence for individualized decision-making and intervention strategy adjustment during mid-stage rehabilitation. Specifically, the study addresses the following research problems: (1) Main problem: Which combination of multidimensional factors assessed at 24 weeks post-ACLR best predicts successful RTP at 48 weeks in HSDs? (2) Sub-problem 1: Does rehabilitation model type (FRM vs. TRM) independently predict RTP success? (3) Sub-problem 2: Among physical, functional, balance, proprioceptive, and psychological domains, which specific indicators are independent predictors, and what are their optimal cut-off values? (4) Sub-problem 3: Can a clinically usable scoring tool derived from these predictors stratify mid-stage patients by RTP probability and guide individualized rehabilitation decisions?

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a single-blind (assessor-blinded), prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) design, comparing a functional rehabilitation model (FRM) against a traditional rehabilitation model (TRM) in high-sports demanders (HSDs) following primary anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR). The trial was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Medical University, Xi’an, Shaanxi, China, between April 2024 and May 2025, and adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines29. Sample size was estimated based on the primary between-group comparison of RTP rates using a chi-square test for two independent proportions (α = 0.05, two-sided; power = 80%; 1:1 allocation); a 30% dropout allowance was incorporated, yielding 32 participants per group. Randomization was performed by independent research personnel using computer-generated block randomization; allocations were concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof envelopes and revealed only after baseline assessment.

Study group

Sixty-four consecutive HSDs who underwent primary ACLR were enrolled during the study period. Outcome assessors conducting all functional, proprioceptive, and psychological tests were blinded to group allocation throughout the study; however, due to the nature of the rehabilitation interventions, participants and treating therapists could not be blinded, introducing potential performance bias, which is a recognized limitation of rehabilitation trials. Participants were allocated to either the functional rehabilitation model (FRM) group (n = 32) or traditional rehabilitation model (TRM) group (n = 32) following ACLR surgery. Baseline demographic characteristics, surgical information, and pre-injury function of all randomized participants are presented in Table 1.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 13–45 years; (2) undergoing primary ACLR surgery; (3) HSDs defined as actively participating in sports activities more than 3 times per week with Tegner score > 5 points; (4) pre-injury participation in level 1 sports (involving cutting and pivoting activities); (5) no cognitive impairment and able to cooperate with rehabilitation training and follow-up. Exclusion criteria included: (1) ACL revision surgery; (2) concomitant other knee ligament repair/reconstruction; (3) patients outside the age range. During the trial, 7 participants were lost to follow-up: in the FRM group, 1 participant sustained a secondary injury due to unauthorized return to play, 1 withdrew due to persistent pain at 12 weeks, and 2 relocated and could not continue treatment; in the TRM group, 3 participants relocated and could not continue treatment. Final analysis included 57 participants (Fig. 1).

Data collection tools

The following validated instruments were used. (1) Biodex Multi-Joint System 4 Pro (Biodex Medical Systems, Shirley, NY, USA): isokinetic strength testing (extensor and flexor peak torque at 60°/s and 180°/s) and passive joint position sense (PJPS) at 30°, 45°, and 60° knee flexion; demonstrated acceptable test–retest reliability for proprioceptive testing (ICC = 0.75–0.89) in prior validation studies, although not independently assessed in this cohort. (2) Functional hop tests: single-leg hop for distance, single-leg lateral hop, and single-leg box jump; limb symmetry index (LSI) calculated for each. (3) Modified Star Excursion Balance Test (mSEBT): dynamic postural control in the anterior (ANT), posteromedial (PM), and posterolateral (PL) directions; inter-rater reliability ICC = 0.82–0.96. (4) ACL-Return to Sport after Injury scale (ACL-RSI): 12-item patient-reported outcome assessing psychological readiness for RTP (0–100); Cronbach’s α = 0.92. (5) IKDC Subjective Knee Evaluation Form: validated 18-item scale assessing overall knee function. (6) Lysholm Knee Score: 8-item scale evaluating knee symptoms and daily function; ICC = 0.85–0.97.

Data collection

Data were collected at three standardized time points: baseline (pre-randomization), 24 weeks post-ACLR (predictive assessment), and 48 weeks post-ACLR (outcome assessment). At baseline, demographic characteristics, surgical details, and pre-injury functional scores were recorded. At 24 weeks, the comprehensive multidimensional assessment battery described in Sect. 2.3 was administered by blinded outcome assessors. At 48 weeks, RTP status was recorded via structured questionnaire.

Rehabilitation interventions

Both groups received 24 weeks of standardized rehabilitation intervention under the allocated model.

Traditional Rehabilitation Model (TRM): A standard rehabilitation protocol based on tissue healing time was employed, strictly following internationally recognized post-ACL reconstruction rehabilitation guidelines. The rehabilitation process was divided into 5 fixed time phases: early protection phase (0–2 weeks), functional recovery phase (3–6 weeks), strength rebuilding phase (6–12 weeks), motor control phase (12–20 weeks), and return-to-play preparation phase (> 20 weeks). Focus was placed on range of motion recovery, basic strength training, and progressive functional exercises.

Functional Rehabilitation Model (FRM): A progressive rehabilitation protocol based on functional performance was utilized, with phase transitions based on functional achievement criteria rather than fixed time (Supplementary Table S5). It comprised 5 function-oriented phases: Phase I (Weeks 1–4) focused on graft protection, swelling reduction, and quadriceps reactivation with neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES); Phase II (Weeks 4–6) introduced open kinetic chain exercises at restricted range (90°–45°), proprioception training, and bilateral balance exercises; Phase III (mid-stage) emphasized low-to-moderate load strengthening (12–20 RM) with supplementary NMES and blood flow restriction training, progressing closed kinetic chain exercises and introducing multidirectional unstable surface proprioceptive training; Phase IV progressed to moderate-to-high load strengthening (8–12 RM), functional movement re-patterning, unilateral exercises, and gait re-education; Phase V incorporated sport-specific training including cutting, deceleration, reactive movement drills, and graded return-to-sport simulation. Each phase required meeting specific advancement criteria including pain thresholds (VAS 0–2), effusion control (stroke test), range of motion milestones, and strength benchmarks (e.g., LSI > 80% for Phase IV advancement). Rehabilitation sessions were conducted 3–5 times per week, with session duration of 45–90 min. Psychological readiness was assessed and addressed through phased functional challenges and graded sport-exposure activities integrated throughout Phases III–V, whereas the TRM did not include structured psychological interventions.

To ensure consistency and quality of care, all rehabilitation sessions were conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Medical University and were delivered by licensed physiotherapists with a minimum of 5 years of experience in sports rehabilitation who had undergone standardized training on the specific study protocols.

Outcome assessments

Successful return to play was defined using strict multidimensional criteria, referencing the standards by Turk30: (1) passing ≥ 2 functional tests (quadriceps isokinetic strength LSI > 90%, modified Star Excursion Balance Test > 85%); (2) psychological readiness achieved (ACL-RSI score > 65); (3) postoperative time ≥ 8 months; (4) patient self-reported return to pre-injury sport level. This definition ensured comprehensive consideration of both objective and subjective assessments of successful return to play.

Comprehensive assessment was performed at 24 weeks postoperatively, including the following dimensions:

At 24 weeks, the following functional assessments were performed using instruments described in Sect. 2.3: (1) isokinetic strength — extensor and flexor peak torque and LSI at 60°/s and 180°/s; (2) hop test battery — single-leg hop for distance, lateral hop, and box jump; (3) dynamic balance — mSEBT reach distances in the ANT, PM, and PL directions; (4) proprioception — passive joint position sense error at 30°, 45°, and 60° knee flexion.

Patient-reported outcomes were collected at 24 weeks, including the ACL-RSI, IKDC Subjective Knee Evaluation Form, and Lysholm Knee Score.

At 48 weeks postoperatively, only return-to-play status was assessed through structured questionnaires recording return-to-play capability, participation level, and whether predetermined criteria for successful return to play were met.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 28.0. Continuous variables, confirmed for normality via Shapiro-Wilk test, were reported as mean ± standard deviation; categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons used independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Candidate predictors from 24-week assessments included: (1) rehabilitation model (FRM vs. TRM); (2) isokinetic strength (extensor/flexor LSI at 60°/s and 180°/s); (3) hop tests (single-leg hop distance, lateral hop, box jump, and LSIs); (4) dynamic balance (mSEBT distances: anterior, posteromedial, posterolateral); (5) proprioception (PJPS errors at 30°, 45°, 60° flexion); (6) patient-reported outcomes (IKDC, Lysholm, ACL-RSI).

Binary logistic regression was used to model 48-week RTP success as a function of 24-week predictors. Forward stepwise variable selection was applied (entry P < 0.05, removal P > 0.10), given the exploratory nature of this study; this approach may overestimate effect sizes compared with penalized regression methods. The events-per-variable (EPV) ratio was below the recommended threshold of 10, which was addressed through supplementary LASSO penalized regression to assess variable selection stability (Supplementary Table S2). Odds ratios for continuous predictors are reported per 1-unit increase (per 1% for LSI; per 1 cm for hop distance and mSEBT; per 1 point for ACL-RSI; per 1° for proprioceptive error). Model calibration was assessed via the Hosmer-Lemeshow test; explanatory power via Nagelkerke R²; and discrimination via ROC analysis (AUC with 95% CI). Sensitivity and specificity confidence intervals were calculated using Wilson’s method. Optimal cut-off values were determined by the Youden index. Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors (VIF > 5 indicating potential issues) and pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients. Complete-case analysis was performed on participants who completed 48-week assessment; no imputation was applied, and this is acknowledged as a potential source of attrition bias. Effect sizes for continuous outcomes were quantified using Cohen’s d. Four supplementary analyses were performed to assess model robustness: (1) bootstrap internal validation (1,000 iterations) to estimate optimism-corrected AUC, Brier score, and calibration plots; (2) LASSO penalized regression; (3) sensitivity analysis excluding ACL-RSI to evaluate potential criterion contamination; and (4) pairwise Pearson correlation matrix with VIF.

Ethical approval

This trial was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Review Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Medical University (XYYFY2024LSKY-101) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The trial was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry under registration number ChiCTR2500104413 (17/06/2025).

Results

Between April 2024 and May 2025, 71 patients were screened; 64 met eligibility criteria and were randomized (32 per group). Seven participants were lost to follow-up (4 FRM: 1 secondary injury, 1 persistent pain, 2 relocated; 3 TRM: relocated), yielding 57 completers (89.1% retention rate). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of all 64 randomized participants are presented in Table 1. Return-to-play outcomes

At 48 weeks postoperatively, the overall return-to-play success rate was 75.4% (43/57). The FRM group achieved a return-to- play success rate of 89.3% (25/28), significantly higher than the TRM group’s 62.1% (18/29) (χ²=4.32, p = 0.038). Relative risk analysis showed that FRM group patients were 1.44 times more likely to return to play than TRM group patients (95%CI: 1.05–1.97), with a number needed to treat of 3.7 (95%CI: 2.1–14.7), indicating that treating 3.7 patients with the FRM protocol would result in one additional successful return to play (Table 2).

Multivariate predictive model

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified six independent predictors comprising the final predictive model: rehabilitation approach (FRM vs. TRM): OR = 3.49 (95%CI: 1.62–7.54, p = 0.002); 60°/s extensor LSI: OR = 2.13 (95%CI: 1.62–2.80, p < 0.001); single-leg hop distance: OR = 1.87 (95%CI: 1.35–2.58, p = 0.003); ACL-RSI score: OR = 1.69 (95%CI: 1.20–2.38, p = 0.007); mSEBT-ANT distance: OR = 1.39 (95%CI: 1.05–1.84, p = 0.023); 45° flexion proprioceptive error: OR = 0.62 (95%CI: 0.45–0.85, p = 0.002). The predictive model showed good overall fit (model χ²=42.36, df = 6, p < 0.001; Hosmer-Lemeshow test χ²=6.83, p = 0.555), with Nagelkerke R²=0.58, indicating the model explained 58% of outcome variance. The remaining 42% of unexplained variance may be attributable to factors not captured in this model, such as graft type, concomitant meniscal injury, surgical technique variations, rehabilitation adherence, and individual biological healing rates. No significant multicollinearity was found among independent variables (maximum VIF = 2.7). Pairwise Pearson correlation analysis revealed that the highest inter-predictor correlation was between extensor LSI and single-leg hop distance (r = 0.42), followed by hop distance and mSEBT-ANT (r = 0.38) and ACL-RSI and extensor LSI (r = 0.35). All pairwise correlations were below 0.70, indicating acceptable independence among predictors (Supplementary Table S4) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

ROC curve analysis and diagnostic performance

ROC curve analysis demonstrated good diagnostic performance for all predictors. The comprehensive predictive model achieved an AUC of 0.87 (95%CI: 0.79–0.95), with sensitivity of 88.4% (95%CI: 74.9%–96.1%) and specificity of 78.6% (95%CI: 59.0%–91.7%), indicating excellent discriminative ability. Optimal cut-off points, diagnostic performance, and clinical interpretation for each independent predictor were as follows: 60°/s extensor LSI ≥ 77.8%, sensitivity 85.0%, specificity 76.5%, AUC = 0.87; single-leg hop distance ≥ 68.5 cm: sensitivity 87.5%, specificity 64.7%, AUC = 0.83; ACL-RSI score ≥ 67.5: sensitivity 82.5%, specificity 70.6%, AUC = 0.81; mSEBT-ANT distance ≥ 63.2 cm: sensitivity 77.5%, specificity 70.6%, AUC = 0.76; 45° flexion PJPS error ≤ 6.1°: sensitivity 80.0%, specificity 76.5%, AUC = 0.78 (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Supplementary validation analyses

To assess model robustness, four supplementary analyses were conducted. Bootstrap internal validation (1,000 iterations) yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.81 (95%CI: 0.72–0.90), with an optimism estimate of 0.06, indicating moderate shrinkage from the apparent AUC of 0.87 (Supplementary Table S1, Figure S2). The bootstrap-corrected Brier score was 0.15 (apparent: 0.13), and the Hosmer-Lemeshow calibration test was non-significant (χ²=6.83, P = 0.555), suggesting adequate calibration (Figure S1). Bootstrap-corrected sensitivity was 83.7% (95%CI: 72.1%–93.0%) and specificity was 71.4% (95%CI: 50.0%–85.7%).

LASSO penalized regression sensitivity analysis retained all six variables identified by forward stepwise selection, with consistent coefficient directions and comparable cross-validated AUC of 0.84 (Supplementary Table S2). To address potential criterion contamination between 24-week ACL-RSI scores and 48-week RTP outcome criteria incorporating ACL-RSI > 65, a sensitivity analysis excluding ACL-RSI was performed. The reduced five-factor model achieved an AUC of 0.82 (ΔAUC = 0.05), with Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.481, indicating that the model retains substantial discriminative ability independent of psychological readiness scores (Supplementary Table S3). Pairwise Pearson correlation analysis confirmed that all inter-predictor correlations were below |r|=0.42, well below the collinearity threshold of 0.70, with all variance inflation factors below 2.7 (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

This prospective randomized controlled trial successfully developed a return-to-play predictive model based on multidimensional assessment at 24 weeks postoperatively, capable of effectively predicting the successful return-to-play rate at 48 weeks in the HSDs population. This finding has important clinical value, providing a reliable quantitative tool for individualized decision making and adjustment of intervention strategies during the mid-rehabilitation phase.

The selection of 24 weeks postoperatively as the predictive time point has important clinical rationale. At this point, patients have completed the basic rehabilitation phase, most physiological indicators have stabilized, but there remains sufficient time for intervention adjustments before the final return-to-play decision31. The predictive model developed in this study can identify patients at high risk for return to play at this critical time point, providing guidance for intensified rehabilitation in the subsequent 6 months.

Traditional return-to-play assessments are often conducted after patients have completed most of the rehabilitation process, leaving limited room for adjustments. In contrast, early prediction at 24 weeks provides a valuable time window for the following clinical interventions: for patients predicted to have low success rates, rehabilitation time can be extended, weak links strengthened, or rehabilitation focus adjusted; for patients with high success rates, return-to-play preparation can be appropriately advanced to optimize resource allocation12.

This study found that extensor limb symmetry index at 60°/s is the strongest predictive factor for successful return to play, a result consistent with multiple high-quality studies. The 2-year follow-up study by Schmitt32 found that patients with LSI < 85% had significantly higher reinjury rates. The 77.8% cutoff value determined in this study is notably below the commonly accepted clinical threshold of 90% LSI for RTP clearance. This lower threshold reflects the mid-rehabilitation assessment context at 24 weeks, where achieving 90% LSI is physiologically uncommon. Clinicians should interpret this predictive cutoff as a mid-stage benchmark rather than a final clearance criterion, We emphasize that this 77.8% threshold should not be used as a criterion for sport clearance; clinicians should still require patients to achieve LSI > 90% before unrestricted sport participation, consistent with established RTP guidelines22,23. The mid-stage threshold serves exclusively as an early warning indicator to identify patients who may benefit from intensified quadriceps rehabilitation.

The quadriceps plays a core role in maintaining knee joint dynamic stability, particularly in high-risk actions such as deceleration, changing direction, and landing33. The phenomenon of arthrogenic muscle inhibition (AMI), commonly present after ACL reconstruction, continuously affects quadriceps activation and strength output. The significant advantage of the FRM group in this study may be related to its adoption of multimodal interventions such as neuromuscular electrical stimulation, progressive closed kinetic chain training, and vibration training, methods that can effectively alleviate AMI and promote neuromuscular adaptation34.

It is noteworthy that the traditional 90% LSI standard may be too strict at the 24-week postoperative time point. The electrophysiological study by Hopkins35 confirmed that even at 12 months postoperatively, 65% of patients still exhibit varying degrees of quadriceps activation deficits. Therefore, adopting a relatively lenient but more realistic cutoff standard (77.8%) may be more suitable for phased assessment and decision making in clinical practice.

Functional rehabilitation model: manifestation of systemic advantages

The finding that rehabilitation model is the second strongest predictive factor (OR = 3.49) suggests systemic advantages of the functional rehabilitation model. The FRM model may exert its effects through the following hypothesized mechanisms, although it should be noted that these explanations draw on prior empirical work and are speculative within the context of the present study:

Neuroplasticity mechanisms: The progressive functional training adopted in FRM promotes functional reorganization of corticospinal pathways. Grooms36 confirmed through functional magnetic resonance imaging studies that functional training can significantly improve corticospinal connectivity strength in post-ACLR patients, particularly in the premotor and primary motor cortex areas. This neural remodeling process requires 6–9 months, explaining the sustained improvement of FRM in long-term follow-up.

Sensorimotor integration optimization: The unstable surface training and multidirectional perturbation stimulation introduced from phase 2 in FRM systematically recalibrate the integration function of multisensory systems. This training mode promotes the establishment of compensatory neural pathways, improving postural control ability in complex sports environments37.

Sport-specific adaptation: The sport-specific training adopted in phases III-V of FRM, by simulating real competitive scenarios, promotes the learning and consolidation of movement patterns. This training method not only improves biomechanical performance but also enhances athletes’ confidence and adaptability to complex sports tasks.

Psychological factors: an underestimated important dimension

The ACL-RSI score as an independent predictive factor (OR = 1.69) emphasizes the importance of psychological factors in return to play. The 67.5-point cutoff value is basically consistent with thresholds reported in previous studies, validating the clinical value of psychological readiness assessment.While the precise mechanisms remain to be established in the ACLR population, prior research suggests that the neural basis of kinesiophobia involves dysfunction in the amygdala-prefrontal cortex circuitry, which may affect return to play through multiple hypothesized pathways: Neuroendocrine regulation: Persistent fear states may activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, potentially leading to elevated cortisol levels that inhibit muscle protein synthesis and affect motor learning processes38. This pathway, while supported by chronic pain literature, requires direct confirmation in ACLR populations.Motor control interference: Psychological stress has been shown to affect the excitability of spinal motor neurons through descending regulatory pathways in chronic pain populations, potentially leading to changes in muscle activation patterns and decreased motor coordination39. Extrapolation to the ACLR context remains speculative.Cognitive resource competition: Based on attentional control theory, excessive anxiety and fear may occupy limited cognitive resources, affecting attention allocation and decision-making, and potentially increasing the risk of errors during sport participation. This proposed mechanism warrants direct investigation in ACLR cohorts.

The FRM model, through phased functional challenges and accumulation of success experiences, adopts therapeutic principles similar to fear hierarchy exposure, helping to rebuild sports confidence and alleviate fear symptoms. Importantly, the two rehabilitation protocols differed in their psychological components: the FRM integrated structured psychological readiness assessment and graded sport-exposure activities from Phase III onward, including progressive return to sport-like scenarios, whereas the TRM did not include any formalized psychological interventions. This differential psychological support may partially explain the observed advantage of FRM in ACL-RSI scores and warrants investigation as an independent mechanism in future dismantling studies.

Functional performance: comprehensive manifestation of kinetic chain coordination

Single-leg hop distance (OR = 1.87) and mSEBT anterior distance (OR = 1.39) as predictive factors reflect the coordination function of the entire lower limb kinetic chain and dynamic balance control ability.Biomechanical significance of hop function: Successful hop actions require precise coordination of hip, knee, and ankle joints in the sagittal and frontal planes. This study adopted an absolute distance standard of 68.5 cm rather than the traditional relative LSI standard, considering potential functional decline in the uninvolved limb during rehabilitation40. This method can more accurately reflect patients’ actual functional levels.Control mechanisms of dynamic balance: The mSEBT anterior test assesses the ability to maintain postural stability under dynamic conditions, an ability dependent on the precise integration of visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive systems. The 63.2 cm cutoff value reflects the recovery level of sensorimotor integration ability. The prospective study by Plisky41 found that for every 4 cm decrease in Star Excursion Balance Test anterior reach distance, injury risk increases by 2.5 times, which is highly consistent with the findings of this study.

After ACL injury, the reduction in proprioceptive input leads to the need for enhanced compensation from other sensory systems. The unstable surface training and multidirectional perturbation training in the FRM model can systematically improve sensorimotor integration ability, retraining complex muscle coordination patterns involving precise timing activation of the tibialis anterior, peroneus longus, quadriceps, and gluteus medius42.

Proprioception: key indicator of precise control

The finding that proprioception error at 45° flexion is a negative predictive factor (OR = 0.62) has important clinical and theoretical significance. The 6.1° threshold set in this study is basically consistent with the latest research standards but relatively lenient compared with the 3° standard proposed earlier, a difference that may be related to the study population and assessment time point.Persistence of proprioceptive deficits: The systematic review by Fleming43 shows that knee joint proprioception remains impaired 6–24 months after ACL reconstruction, confirming the universality and persistence of proprioceptive deficits. The meta-analysis by Relph44 further confirms that proprioception in ACL injury patients is significantly worse than in uninjured individuals (standardized mean difference = 0.35°; P = 0.001), and the injured side is significantly worse than the uninjured side (standardized mean difference = 0.52°; P < 0.001).

Complexity of neural mechanisms: Based on current understanding, the loss of mechanoreceptors caused by ACL injury not only affects joint position sense but may also disrupt the normal function of the γ-motor neuron system. The FRM model systematically recalibrates muscle spindle sensitivity settings through closed kinetic chain exercises and unstable surface training, promoting the establishment of compensatory neural pathways45.

Quantitative risk assessment: The prospective cohort study by Zazulak46 found that for every 1° increase in core proprioceptive repositioning error, the odds ratio for knee joint injury increases by 2.9 times, and for ligament/meniscus injury by 3.3 times. This finding provides important evidence for the quantitative relationship between proprioceptive error and sports injury risk.

Clinical application value of the predictive model

The 6-factor predictive model developed in this study has the following clinical application advantages:

High predictive accuracy: The model achieves an apparent AUC of 0.87 (bootstrap-corrected: 0.81), with sensitivity of 88.4% and specificity of 78.6%, indicating good discriminative ability that is retained after correction for optimistic bias.

Operational feasibility: All indicators in the model are commonly used clinical assessment tools, requiring no additional equipment investment, and are easy to standardize across different medical institutions. Clear cutoff standards eliminate variability in subjective judgment, improving the objectivity and consistency of assessments.

Individualized guidance: The model can be used to identify patients at high risk for return to play, guiding the formulation of individualized rehabilitation protocols. For patients predicted to have low success rates, rehabilitation time can be extended, weak links strengthened, or training focus adjusted; for patients with high success rates, return-to-play assessment can be appropriately advanced to optimize resource allocation.

Cost-effectiveness optimization: Through early identification and intervention, the model helps reduce the incidence of unsuccessful return to play, lower reinjury risk, thereby decreasing medical costs and patient economic burden. Unexplained variance and model boundaries: The model’s Nagelkerke R² of 0.58 indicates that 42% of outcome variance remains unexplained. Several factors not captured in the current model may contribute to this residual variance. Graft type (hamstring tendon vs. bone-patellar tendon-bone) is known to influence quadriceps recovery trajectories and may differentially affect mid-stage predictor values. Concomitant meniscal injury, present in a substantial proportion of ACL injuries, can alter rehabilitation timelines and functional outcomes independently. Rehabilitation adherence, which was not systematically monitored in this study, likely introduces meaningful variation, as patients with higher session completion rates may achieve superior functional outcomes regardless of predictor values. Individual biological factors including graft ligamentization rates, systemic inflammatory responses, and genetic predisposition to collagen remodeling may also contribute to unexplained variance. Future models incorporating these variables may improve predictive accuracy beyond the current six-factor framework.

Clinical Scoring Tool and Decision Framework: To facilitate systematic clinical application, we developed a preliminary scoring tool derived from the six-factor predictive model (Fig. 4). Each predictor is assigned a weighted score based on its adjusted odds ratio: predictors with OR ≥ 2.0 receive 2 points, and predictors with OR < 2.0 receive 1 point. The rehabilitation model variable, as a categorical predictor (FRM vs. TRM), receives 2 points for FRM based on its OR of 3.49. The total score ranges from 0 to 8 points. This simplified scoring approach enables rapid bedside assessment without requiring statistical software, while preserving the relative contribution of each predictor to the overall model. This scoring tool is intended as an exploratory clinical aid and requires external validation before routine implementation.

Risk stratification and clinical decision framework: Based on the total score, patients are classified into three risk categories: (1) High probability of RTP success (score 6–8): The patient demonstrates strong recovery across most domains. Clinicians may proceed with progressive sport-specific training and schedule formal return-to-play clearance testing. (2) Moderate probability (score 3–5): The patient shows mixed recovery. Study limitations

Although this study has important clinical value, it still has some limitations:

Sample size limitation: Although the expected sample size requirement was met, the relatively small sample size may affect the generalizability of the results, particularly requiring further validation for applicability in different sports and populations.

Single-center design: The study was conducted at a single medical institution, potentially involving selection bias and regional specificity, affecting the applicability of results in other medical environments.

Sport-specific considerations: This study grouped all level-1 sports (e.g., soccer, basketball, handball) together without stratified analysis. Different level-1 sports impose distinct biomechanical demands: soccer emphasizes unilateral cutting and deceleration, basketball requires repetitive vertical landing and lateral shuffling, and handball involves overhead throwing combined with rapid direction changes. These sport-specific movement patterns may differentially weight the relative importance of each predictor; for instance, proprioceptive accuracy may be more critical in sports requiring rapid directional changes, while quadriceps strength symmetry may be more predictive in sports dominated by deceleration tasks. Future studies with larger, sport-stratified samples should investigate whether sport-specific prediction models improve upon the generalized model presented here.

Sex-specific considerations: Despite known sex differences in ACL injury mechanisms, rehabilitation responses, and RTP outcomes, sex-specific or interaction analyses were not performed due to the limited sample size (71.9% male). Future studies with larger and more balanced cohorts should explore sex as a potential moderator of predictive model performance.

Age heterogeneity: The broad age range (13–45 years) introduces biological, developmental, and psychosocial variability that may influence rehabilitation trajectories. Age-stratified analyses were not feasible given the sample size, and this should be addressed in future validation studies.

Internal validation: The predictive model was internally validated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and ROC analysis. Supplementary bootstrap validation (1,000 iterations) revealed an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.81 (optimism = 0.06), with adequate calibration (Brier score = 0.15; calibration plot: Figure S1; bootstrap distribution: Figure S2). While these supplementary analyses provide reassurance regarding model stability, the model has not been externally validated on an independent cohort, and the cut-off values derived from ROC analysis may still exhibit optimistic bias. External validation in multicenter, geographically diverse cohorts remains essential before the model is adopted in routine clinical practice.

Generalizability: The study population consisted exclusively of non-professional athletes at a single Chinese institution. Results may not directly generalize to elite athletes, who typically have access to dedicated sports medicine teams and may achieve higher functional benchmarks at equivalent timepoints, or to purely recreational populations, whose lower sport-specific demands and different psychological motivations may alter the relative importance of predictors such as ACL-RSI. Furthermore, settings with different healthcare systems, cultural contexts, or rehabilitation resources may influence both the achievable predictor values and the applicability of identified cut-off thresholds. Cultural and healthcare-system factors, including insurance coverage, rehabilitation access, and patient expectations, may influence rehabilitation outcomes and should be considered when applying this model in other contexts.

Potential omitted variables: Several potentially relevant variables were not included in the model, such as graft type, meniscal injury status, and rehabilitation adherence rates. Rehabilitation session adherence was not systematically monitored and may act as a confounder. Additionally, criterion contamination may arise from two sources: (1) ACL-RSI scores serve as both a 24-week predictor and as part of the 48-week RTP outcome criteria (ACL-RSI > 65), potentially inflating the observed association strength; and (2) isokinetic strength LSI at 24 weeks may be correlated with the 48-week LSI > 90% RTP criterion. To quantify this concern, a sensitivity analysis excluding ACL-RSI from the model was conducted, yielding a five-factor AUC of 0.82 (ΔAUC = 0.05; Supplementary Table S3), demonstrating that the model retains substantial predictive ability independent of potential criterion contamination. Nevertheless, future studies should consider outcome definitions that minimize overlap with predictor variables.

Methodological considerations: The use of forward stepwise logistic regression, while appropriate for exploratory model building, is subject to known limitations including instability in variable selection and potential overestimation of effect sizes. The EPV ratio of 7.2 falls below the recommended threshold of 10, suggesting that the model may be prone to overfitting. As a sensitivity analysis, LASSO regression was performed and retained all six variables with consistent coefficient directions (Supplementary Table S2), providing additional support for model stability. Bootstrap internal validation (1,000 iterations) yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.81 (optimism = 0.06), confirming moderate optimistic bias in the apparent AUC of 0.87 (Supplementary Table S1, Figure S2). Additionally, the current single-timepoint prediction design does not capture the dynamic trajectory of rehabilitation progression; future studies collecting longitudinal multi-timepoint data could employ temporal modeling approaches to better characterize recovery patterns and improve prediction accuracy.

Conclusions

This prospective randomized controlled trial developed a six-factor predictive model that demonstrates strong discriminative ability for mid-rehabilitation RTP prediction in HSDs. While the model identifies rehabilitation model type, isokinetic strength, hop performance, psychological readiness, dynamic balance, and proprioception as independent predictors of RTP success, these findings reflect predictive associations rather than established causal relationships. The inclusion of rehabilitation model type as a predictor suggests that functional rehabilitation approaches may confer advantages, but the causal mechanisms underlying this association require further investigation. This exploratory model requires external validation in independent, multicenter cohorts before implementation in routine clinical practice. If confirmed, it may serve as a useful tool for early risk identification and individualized intervention planning during mid-stage rehabilitation.
