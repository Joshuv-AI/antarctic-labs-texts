Peking University Cold Aerosol Flow Tube (PKU-CAT): Development and Characterization for Studying Wintertime Secondary Organic Aerosol Formation
Authors: Jixuan Wang, Mikinori Kuwata, Yingjun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Secondary organic aerosol (SOA) is an important contributor to atmospheric particulate matter. Temperature has been suggested as a key factor impacting SOA yields, yet laboratory data are sparse, particularly for technically challenging subzero temperature ranges. To address this issue, we developed the Peking University Cold Aerosol Flow Tube (PKU-CAT). The PKU-CAT can be operated for the temperature range of −20 to 30 °C, covering most ground surface temperatures, particularly those encountered in urban environments. The PKU-CAT features a 1.5 m long quartz tube, with an inner diameter of 19 cm and a total volume of 42.5 L. It employs light-emitting diodes (LEDs) with a peak wavelength of 367 nm to photolyze isopropyl nitrite for OH radical generation, achieving an atmospheric equivalent OH exposure time of approximately 1 day for the whole operational temperature range. Relative humidity (RH) in the PKU-CAT can be controlled up to 80%, even below 0 °C. The system is equipped with a scanning mobility particle analyzer (SMPS) that allows isothermal sampling of particles. The residence time distribution and gas- and particle-transmission efficiencies of the PKU-CAT are comparable to those of existing quasi-laminar oxidation flow reactors (OFRs) operated at room temperature. Photooxidation experiments of α-pinene were conducted for characterizing SOA formation. At 20 °C, the SOA yields obtained with the PKU-CAT ranged from 13 to 21% for organic mass concentration of 17–57 μg m–3, comparable to those reported in previous environmental chamber studies. When the temperature was reduced to −16 °C, the SOA yields increased from 21% to over 100% for the same initial α-pinene mixing ratio (50 ppb), suggesting that the impact of temperature on the SOA formation process needs to be further investigated in future studies.

Introduction

Secondary organic aerosol (SOA) constitutes a major component of PM2.5 in many places over the globe. SOA does not only degrade visibility but also exacerbates respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. In addition, SOA influences the Earth’s radiative balance by directly scattering and absorbing solar radiation, as well as by altering cloud microphysical properties. The formation of SOA involves complex atmospheric oxidation of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) emitted from anthropogenic and natural sources, as well as gas-particle partitioning of the resulting semivolatile and low-volatility products. To quantify this process, laboratory studies have measured SOA yields under controlled conditions. − These empirical yield data are used to constrain parametrizations such as the two-product model and the volatility basis set (VBS), which are then applied to atmospheric models. , However, substantial uncertainties persist, including discrepancies among parametrizations derived from differing experimental data, as well as glaring deviations from field measurements. ,

A key source of modeling uncertainty is the incomplete characterization of environmental factors governing SOA yields. SOA yields can be influenced by the precursor VOC type, oxidant exposure, organic aerosol (OA) mass, NO x level, temperature, and relative humidity (RH). Among these, the influence of temperature remains particularly understudied, despite its critical role in controlling product volatility and thus gas–particle partitioning, − as well as its potential influence on reaction pathways. − Existing temperature-dependent SOA yield data are primarily obtained within ±10 °C of room temperature. , Data for subzero conditions, which are relevant to wintertime at mid- and high-latitudes, have been reported only for some limited cases of ozonolysis of monoterpenes and β-caryophyllene. The effects of RH have been more frequently investigated, though mostly at room temperature. − Evidence suggests that temperature and humidity may interactively affect SOA composition and properties. , A recent finding of a greater wintertime discrepancy between modeled and measured SOA concentrations further underscores the need to better understand SOA formation under low temperature conditions.

The limited understanding of temperature effects on SOA formation stems primarily from technical challenges. Most environmental chambers are designed to operate in the vicinity of room temperature. To date, only three chambers have been used for SOA formation experiments below 0 °C, ,,, with a few more operated down to 5–10 °C. ,,,, A key limitation of chamber study is the continuous vapor and particle wall losses over long residence time. This issue is exacerbated at low temperatures, as vapor wall loss increases substantially as temperature decreases. To obtain meaningful SOA yields under these conditions, chambers must be large, which in turn demands substantial space, incurs high costs for temperature control, and presents operational challenges.

In recent years, oxidation flow reactors (OFRs) have emerged as compact alternatives for studying SOA formation. , By maintaining high oxidant concentrations, OFRs can simulate atmospheric oxidation equivalent to hours to days within a residence time of just a few minutes. This rapid processing, combined with features such as a quasi-laminar flow design , and sampling from the reactor center, − minimizes the reactor wall effects. These advantages with low wall effects position OFRs as promising platforms for investigating SOA formation over a broad temperature range, although it should be noted that the high OH concentrations and nonambient UV conditions in OFRs may lead to certain differences in chemical pathways compared to the real atmosphere. So far, OFRs with precise, wide-range temperature control remain scarce. Von Hessberg et al. developed a water-jacketed glass flow tube with temperature control from −10 to 30 °C, but it was designed solely for dark ozonolysis and does not support photochemical studies. More recently, a temperature-controlled OFR incorporating UV lights was reported for the operational range of 7–22 °C. To our knowledge, the OFR system capable of performing photooxidation experiments below 0 °C is still nonexistent.

Herein, we report the development and characterization of the Peking University Cold Aerosol Flow Tube (PKU-CAT), designed to investigate SOA formation from VOC photooxidation across a wide range of temperatures (−20 to 30 °C) and relative humidity (0–80%). The performance of PKU-CAT is characterized by evaluating the residence time distribution, particle/O3 transmission efficiency, OH exposure (OHexp), and stability of temperature and humidity control, at both room and subzero temperatures. Using α-pinene as a model precursor, we further assess SOA yields at room temperature in comparison with literature data, and demonstrate the reactor’s low-temperature capability by measuring the yield at −16 °C.

Reactor Design

Description of the Flow Tube

As shown in Figure a, the flow tube assembly consists of three key parts: an inlet diffuser, a quartz tube body, and an outlet section. The quartz tube had a length of 1.5 m, an inner diameter of 19 cm, and an outer diameter of 20 cm. The corresponding total volume is 42.5 L, with a surface area-to-volume ratio of 21 m–1. Quartz was chosen as the material for the flow tube because of its high UV transparency and minimal wall loss of VOCs. The quartz tube is connected to the inlet and outlet sections using stainless steel flanges (KF200), FEP gaskets, and Viton O-rings.

The inlet diffuser is a cone made of Teflon-coated stainless steel, employed to assist the development of (quasi-) laminar flow. The angle of the diffuser is 30°, while its length is 34 cm. The diffuser is equipped with a KF16 flange at the upstream region for connection with the injection system.

The outlet section is a flange equipped with a conical sampling port designed to sample only the central region of the flow. The wider end of the cone (13 cm in diameter) faces the flow tube and is welded to an annulus perforated metal sheet (18.8 cm in the outer diameter). The narrow end of the cone is welded directly to the flange plate, which directs the central flow to the analytical instruments. Four additional exhaust ports are arranged around the central cone on the flange plate, enabling the surrounding flow that passes through the perforated sheet to exit uniformly. The total exhaust flow rate was regulated using a mass flow controller (MFC, 21–1–8–1–5, Alicat Scientific, USA) connected to an air pump. This configuration maintains a controlled ratio between the sampled central flow and exhaust flow. All components of the outlet section were made of Teflon-coated stainless steel.

Photolytic Environment for OH Generation

This study utilizes a custom UV-LED system to generate OH radicals through the photolysis of isopropyl nitrite (iPrONO) at 369 nm R1 iPrONO+hv→RO•+NO R2 RO•+O2→R’O+HO2 R3 NO+HO2→NO2+OH

Due to direct formation of NO, this approach can create a high-NO x environment representative of urban conditions. Additionally, iPrONO photolysis only requires UVA radiation, avoiding nontropospheric photochemistry associated with 185 and 254 nm radiation commonly used in OFRs. That said, we acknowledge that the substantially higher UVA light intensity in PKU-CAT represents a known deviation from ambient atmospheric conditions.

The flow tube is mounted within an aluminum frame (50 × 50 × 270 cm3), enclosed by panels whose interiors are lined with mirror-finish aluminum sheet to optimize light uniformity and efficiency. Four UV-LED lamps, uniformly arranged parallel to the flow tube, provide the photolytic source. This UV system features five-level adjustable light intensity (0–100% output) control. LED lamps were specifically selected for their stable low-temperature performance and minimal heat emission, both of which are essential for maintaining precise temperature control during experiments.

The measured UV–vis spectrum of the LED lamp (Figure S1) exhibits a peak wavelength at approximately 367 nm with a full width at half-maximum of 12.4 nm. Under the standard operating condition of 20% LED power used throughout this study, the photon flux at 365 nm was measured using a UV radiometer (UV–B, Beijing Shida Photoelectric Technology). The photon flux at the center of the quartz tube was determined to be 4.34 × 1015 photons cm–2 s–1.

Temperature Control System

The temperature control system for the PKU-CAT consists of a commercial freezer and a liquid circulation system. The reactor’s aluminum enclosure is housed within the freezer (BC/BD-2288, Nixue; dimensions: 3.6 × 1.0 × 0.9 m3) for overall environmental control. The wall temperature of the freezer can be adjusted from −30 to 10 °C. To minimize temperature fluctuation induced by the freezer’s on–off cycling, the enclosure is insulated from the freezer walls by 8 cm thick extruded polystyrene (XPS) boards. For experiments conducted above 20 °C, the freezer is switched off and functions primarily as an insulation chamber.

A dedicated liquid circulation system is employed for fine temperature control and active heat removal. This active temperature control system minimizes thermal interference from the LEDs, ensuring stable temperature regulation within the flow tube. The system consists of two refrigerated circulators, heat exchangers, copper tubing, and fans (Figure b). Inside the aluminum enclosure, heat generated by the UV-LED lamps is first transferred from the LED housing to copper tubes via thermally conductive silicone pads. This heat is then carried away by the circulating coolant within the copper tubes and subsequently removed by the refrigerated circulators. This coolant is also directed to two heat exchangers (1/5HP, Bo Qiang), each equipped with a fan, to regulate the air temperature surrounding the flow tube. Additional fans are installed to promote air mixing and enhance thermal uniformity. The coolant exiting from the freezer first passes through a high-power refrigerated circulator (DLSB-50L/20, Yuhua; 1.6 kW at −20 °C), and then flows to a second, high precision circulator (ZLD-20L, Blue Power) for final temperature stabilization. In addition to cooling, the second circulator is also capable of heating, thereby enabling experiments to be conducted above room temperature.

Inflow System

The inflow system for the PKU-CAT prepared the reactant mixture before it entered the flow tube. Outside the freezer, the main air stream underwent humidification before being mixed with the VOC precursor and the OH radical precursor. The VOC precursor was supplied from a standard gas cylinder balanced by nitrogen (30 ppm α-pinene, Huayuan) through a mass flow controller, and its initial concentration in the flow tube was calculated based on dilution of the standard gas mixture. The temperature of the combined flow was preadjusted by passing it through a long section of Teflon tubing (∼7 m) housed inside the freezer. This conditioned gas stream was then mixed with a flow of seed particles immediately before entering the flow tube. The total inflow was maintained at 6 sLpm. All MFCs used in the inflow system were from Horiba Precision Instruments (Model S48–300). Zero air was produced by a zero-air generator (737–13-A-CH4–240, AADCO Instruments). The subsequent subsections provide detailed descriptions of the setups for generating water vapor, the OH precursor (iPrONO), and seed particles.

Water Vapor Generation

The humidity inside the flow tube was precisely controlled by humidifying the inflow air at a known temperature. The zero air supplied to PKU-CAT had a dew point lower than −70 °C, meaning that it was sufficiently dry for experiments at −20 °C. The humidification setup was adapted from the method of Cheng and Kuwata. In this setup, two stainless steel containers, each half filled with water, were connected in series and placed inside a thermally insulated box. The temperature of this box was controlled using an external refrigerated circulator (DC-3006, Blue Power), achieving a stability of ±0.5 °C. In contrast to the common practice of bubbling, the air stream was directed to flow gently over the water or ice surface in the containers. This surface-flow design enabled stable and gentle humidification at subzero temperatures. The humidified air was then mixed with dry air before exiting the box. The flow rates for both streams were regulated using MFCs.

iPrONO Generation

Isopropyl nitrite (iPrONO; Annaiji, >95% purity) was used in this study as the precursor for OH generation. The iPrONO was delivered by a syringe pump maintained at 4 °C to prevent degradation. The liquid flow passed through a 25 cm length of 1/16” PEEK tubing (0.1 mm inner diameter) at a rate of 0.0251 μL min–1 into a 1 sLpm zero-air carrier stream within a Teflon Tee for evaporation. The resulting vapor was then mixed with an additional 5 sLpm zero-air stream at the reactor inlet. The iPrONO mixing ratio in the inflow was calculated based on the liquid injection rate and the total airflow rate.

Particle Generation

Ammonium sulfate (AS) seed particles were generated following a procedure similar to that of Cheng and Kuwata. AS particles were produced using a nebulizer (Model 3076, TSI, USA). The nebulizer solution was prepared by dissolving (NH4)2SO4 (≥99%, Tongguang) in deionized water. The nebulizer airflow was set to 3 sLpm using an MFC.

The aerosol flow was split into excess and sample flows. The excess flow was directed through a dryer bottle, a MFC, and a vacuum pump (Figure a). The remaining sample flow was first dried by two silica gel diffusion dryers at room temperature (23–27 °C), followed by two diffusion dryers containing type 3A molecular sieves in the precooling section (Figure b). As reported by Cheng and Kuwata, this procedure reduced the dew point of the aerosol sample flow to −57 °C. When the PKU-CAT was operated at −20 °C, the corresponding RH was approximately 1%.

For residence time distribution and particle loss measurements, monodisperse AS particles were generated using a nanodifferential mobility analyzer (nano-DMA, model 3085, TSI, USA). For SOA formation experiments, polydisperse AS particles were introduced directly into the OFR without a size selection.

Measurement Setup

Particle Measurement

Aerosol concentration was measured using a Scanning Mobility Particle Sizer (SMPS) (SMPS-8001, Beijing NaKe Environmental Technologies), which consists of a neutralizer, a differential mobility analyzer (DMA), and a condensation particle counter (CPC). We modified the instrument for isothermal operation to prevent evaporation of semivolatile organic compounds from particles, a process that could otherwise occur due to large temperature gradients between the low-temperature flow tube and a room-temperature SMPS system. To achieve isothermal conditions, both the neutralizer and the DMA were housed in the same freezer as the flow tube. Some modifications to the SMPS system were made for the operation in the freezer. First, the default soft X-ray neutralizer, which cannot function at low temperature, was replaced with a 241Am radioactive neutralizer. Second, the DMA and CPC were connected with a long (70 cm) conductive tube. The SMPS program was modified to account for the increased delay time. Third, dry zero air (dew point < −70 °C) was used as the DMA’s sheath flow to avoid condensation at low temperature. The temperature of the sheath flow was preadjusted to maintain isothermal conditions. The sheath-to-sample flow ratio was controlled to 10:1 using MFCs and a vacuum pump. Both sheath and aerosol flow rates were verified with a soap film flowmeter (Gilibrator-2, Gilian, USA) prior to each SOA experiment.

To verify the performance of the isothermal SMPS system, a scanning electrical mobility system (SEMS 2100, BRECHTEL, USA) was used for comparison. The SEMS instrument was operated at room temperature. Both of the two setups provided consistent data for ammonium sulfate particles (see Figure S2). The SEMS, operating at room temperature, was also used in the low-temperature SOA formation experiments for comparison with the isothermal SMPS data.

Temperature and Humidity Measurement

The temperature within the PKU-CAT system was monitored at multiple critical locations using T-type thermocouples (RC-4HC, Jingchuang). As shown in Figure b, the thermocouples were attached to the flow tube wall (one), the LED UV lamp housing (one), and the copper tubes of the coolant circulation system (two). The thermocouple data were recorded at a rate of one measurement per minute.

The RH and temperature of the airflow exiting the flow tube was measured using a capacitance RH/temperature sensor (Hygroclip HC2A–S, Rotronic Inc., Switzerland), which was placed inside the fridge (see Figure a). The Hygroclip data were obtained every second. The RH readings of the Hygroclip were calibrated at −20 °C against a reference dew point sensor (Model S-2, Shinyei, Japan). As shown in Figure S3, the RH values measured by the Hygroclip correlated with those measured by the dew point sensor (R 2 = 0.997), providing a reliable calibration data set for low-temperature application of Hygroclip.

Comparison with Other OFR Designs

Our PKU-CAT design was partially adapted from previous OFR configurations, ,,, with several specific modifications. Table S1 compares the key features of the PKU-CAT with previous OFRs from the literature, including the Caltech Photooxidation Flow Tube (CPOT), glass and stainless steel versions of Potential Aerosol Mass reactors (PAMs), the Environment and Climate Change Canada OFR (ECCC-OFR), and the Particle Formation Accelerator OFR (PFA OFR). The similarities and differences are outlined as follows.

PKU-CAT uses materials and geometry similar to those in previous studies. The reactor is made of quartz, selected for low gas-phase wall loss and high UV transparency, similar to the CPOT and ECCC-OFR. PKU-CAT’s length-to-inner diameter ratio is 7.9, higher than the PAM, ECCC-OFR, and RAAD reactors, but lower than the CPOT and PFA OFR. Following geometric optimizations by Huang et al., PKU-CAT has a conical inlet, similar to the CPOT, ECCC-OFR, and RAAD reactors. The design of discharging near-wall annular flow as exhaust is also used in other systems, including the PAM, ECCC-OFR, PFA OFR, and RAAD.

Our design introduces several key improvements. The main upgrade is the ability to maintain stable temperature and humidity across a wide range, as detailed in Sections and 2.2.1. The OH generation method is also unique. Optimized for high-NO x conditions, UVA LED irradiation minimizes heat emission and avoids irrelevant photolysis wavelengths. Another feature is the extended mean residence time. The mean residence time of PKU-CAT is 420 s, substantially longer than the typical 60–120 s residence time of oxidation flow reactors (Table S1). While a longer residence time may increase wall loss, it allows more time for gas/particle partitioning to occur. In closed systems such as flow tubes or the real atmosphere, the modeled equilibrium time scale for semisolid/liquid SOA ranges from minutes to hours, depending on the mass accommodation coefficient and the diffusivity of organic molecules in SOA. , If a unity mass accommodation coefficient is assumed, the residence time in PKU-CAT is expected to be sufficient when mass transfer is limited by interfacial transport. However, experimentally determining the equilibrium time scale for gas–particle partitioning remains challenging, and the above analysis is based solely on modeling. Further research on SOA mass transfer is needed to clarify under which conditions PKU-CAT’s residence time is long enough to approach gas–particle equilibrium.

The residence time distribution (RTD), defined as the probability distribution of time that different fluid elements spend in a continuous flow system, was measured to characterize flow profiles in the PKU-CAT. Loss of Particle- and Gas-Phase Species

Continuous injection of 50–200 nm monodisperse AS particles and ozone was used to quantify particle and gas losses within PKU-CAT, respectively. Ozone was generated using an ozone generator (UV-M3, Tonglin). The transmission efficiency was calculated as the ratio of stable concentrations measured at the reactor’s central outlet and inlet. An SMPS measured particle concentration, while an ozone analyzer (Model 49i, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) measured ozone concentration. Measurements were conducted at room temperature (20 °C) and −18 °C. The UV lamps were active during particle tests, while they were inactive during gas-phase tests to prevent ozone photolysis.

OH Exposure Quantification and Chemical Regime Characterization

To quantify OHexp, CO was injected at initial mixing ratios of 50–60 ppm. For each experiment, the UV lamps were kept off until the outlet CO concentration stabilized. The lamps were then activated to generate OH for several hours, while the outlet CO concentration was monitored (Figure S5). The corresponding OHexp was determined based on the steady-state CO concentrations [CO]bef and [CO]af measured before and after activating the UV lamps, as follow OHexp=1kOH−CO×ln⁡[CO]bef[CO]af 3 where k OH–CO is the reaction rate constant (2.13 × 10–13 cm3 molecules–1 s–1). The procedure was performed at 20 °C and −18 °C, with two replicates at each temperature. The derived OHexp was converted to equivalent atmospheric aging days by assuming a 24-h average OH concentration of 1.5 × 106 molecules cm–3.

To validate the chemical regime within the OFR, NO and NO2 concentrations were measured under typical operating conditions (20 °C with OH generation), using a broadband cavity-enhanced absorption spectrometer (BBCEAS, made by Anhui Institute of Optics and Fine Mechanics, Chinese Academy of Sciences). Due to strong interference from iPrONO, direct measurement of NO and NO2 concentrations during OFR operation is challenging with a standard NO x analyzer. The BBCEAS instrument utilizes broadband absorption spectroscopy (440–480 nm) to retrieve NO2 concentrations by fitting the entire absorption spectrum, allowing it to better distinguish between different absorbing species and providing superior resistance to spectral interference.

SOA Yield Measurement Using α-Pinene as a Precursor

α-Pinene was used as the SOA precursor for characterizing PKU-CAT. Polydisperse AS particles were used as the seed particles. Experiments were conducted at initial α-pinene mixing ratios of 25, 38, and 50 ppb at 20 °C, and 50 ppb at −16 °C. The sensitivity of SOA yield to seed particle loadings was tested at 20 °C by varying the AS particle concentrations (0–54 μg m–3, 0–1.65 × 109 nm2 cm–3) for a fixed α-pinene mixing ratio (50 ppb). In all of these experiments, RH was kept at 11–16%.

SOA mass concentration (ΔM o) was calculated using the SMPS data after correcting for particle wall loss, assuming a particle density of 1.2 g cm–3. This approach ignores potential changes of particle density with temperature and oxidation state. SOA mass yield (Y) was calculated as the ratio of ΔM o to reacted hydrocarbon concentration (ΔHC). For the condition of the present study, ΔHC was equivalent to the injection concentration due to the high OH exposure, which was confirmed by measurement using a proton-transfer-reaction mass spectrometer (PTR-Qi-ToF, Ionicon Analytic, Austria). Representative time profiles of α-pinene in a typical experiment are shown in Figure S6.

Results and Discussion

Residence Time Distributions

The RTD measurements were conducted for (1) optimizing the flow rate of PKU-CAT, (2) testing the RTD of PKU-CAT at low temperature, and (3) investigating the influence of UV lights on RTD. The optimization of the PKU-CAT flow rate was conducted at room temperature without operating the LEDs. CO (Table ) was used as the tracer for this purpose. As shown in Figure S7, lowering total flow rates from 6 Lpm to 4 Lpm broadened the RTD, probably due to enhanced recirculation and slower flow near the wall. Increasing the central flow fraction from 50% to 60% broadened the RTD, while reducing the central flow fraction to 40% only slightly sharpened the distribution. On the basis of these results, a total flow of 6 Lpm with a 50% central flow fraction was selected for subsequent experiments. Under these conditions, the measured mean residence time is 420 s.

In both cases, the experimentally determined RTDs are closer to the one for laminar flow than to that for well-stirred flow. They are broader than the ideal laminar flow. The mode of the RTD for particles appeared at a larger value of the normalized residence time (0.78) than that for gas (0.72). The interquartile ranges of RTDs for gas and particles were 0.53 and 0.47, respectively, meaning that the RTD for particles was slightly sharper than that for CO.

Specifically, the RTDs of the PKU-CAT are slightly broader than those for the PFA OFR, probably due to its longer residence time (Table S1). The RTD of the CPOT is substantially broader than that of the PKU-CAT, while the RTDs of the PAM more closely resemble those of a well-stirred reactor. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the flow behavior of the PKU-CAT is comparable to that of other established quasi-laminar flow reactors.

To assess the uniformity of flow conditions under different operating temperatures, the effects of cooling and UV operation on RTD were evaluated using AS particles as tracers (Table ). As shown in Figure , RTD is highly sensitive to nonisothermal conditions. Specifically, cooling the PKU-CAT from 20 °C to −18 °C broadened the RTD, which can be attributed to temperature inhomogeneity and the formation of recirculation zones within the flow tube. Operating the UV LEDs further broadened the RTD and shifted the peak to an earlier elution time at both temperatures, likely due to localized heating from the lamps introducing additional thermal gradients. These findings are consistent with a previous study, which reported that a 6 °C temperature increase in the CPOT reduced its peak time by one-third. Notably, even under the most challenging condition examined (−18 °C with UV on), the RTD in the PKU-CAT remains comparable to that of the PAM reactor at room temperature with UV off.

Figure also compares the particle-transmission efficiency among OFRs at room temperature. Overall, metal reactors (RAAD and metal PAM) have higher transmission efficiency than reactors made of nonconductive materials (glass PAM, ECCC-OFR, CPOT, PFA OFR, and PKU-CAT), probably due to reduced loss of charged particles. The transmission efficiency also increases with shorter mean residence times (PAM, ECCC-OFR, PFA OFR, and RAAD). The particle transmittance is higher for reactors adopting centerline sampling (ECCC-OFR, PFA OFR, RAAD, and PKU-CAT).

The particle transmittance for the PKU-CAT (quartz) is higher than that for the glass PAM, reaching a similar level of the metal PAM, likely due to the improved flow design. Among quasi-laminar flow reactors, the particle transmittance of the PKU-CAT is higher than that of the CPOT and lower than those of the ECCC-OFR and RAAD, and it exhibits a weaker dependence on particle size than that of the PFA OFR. These differences can be attributed to the differences in the residence time and wall materials (Table S1).

The transmission efficiency of ozone was 0.91 and 0.95 at room temperature and −18 °C, respectively. These values align with the efficiency of 0.98 reported for the CPOT.

OH Exposure and Chemical Regime

Consistent settings for OH generation were maintained throughout OFR operation. Based on CO decay tests (Figure S5), the OHexp at 20 °C was approximately 1.34 × 1011 molecules·cm–3·s, equivalent to 1.03 days of atmospheric oxidation (assuming a 24-h average OH concentration of 1.5 × 106 molecules·cm–3). When operating at −18 °C, the OHexp showed a slight decrease to 1.06–1.14 × 1011 molecules·cm–3·s (equivalent to 0.81–0.88 atmospheric days). Although OHexp was not directly measured during the SOA formation experiments, repeated CO decay tests over time showed good reproducibility. In future experiments, it is optional to adjust OHexp by changing the light intensity and precursor concentration, to simulate a wider atmospheric oxidation conditions.

The OFR 369-i­(iPrONO) system inherently generates both NO and NO2 during OH production, creating high-NO x conditions. Measurements using the BBCEAS showed that, under typical operating conditions, the NO concentration inside the PKU-CAT was approximately 60 ppb, while the NO2 concentration was approximately 450 ppb. The high NO level makes reaction with NO the dominant fate of alkyl RO2 radicals. The high NO2:NO x ratio allows for the formation of peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN), which is relevant to urban environments featured of high ozone and median-to-low photolysis. Furthermore, compared with OFR254/185-iN2O systems, the longer photolysis wavelengths of the OFR 369-i­(iPrONO) system allows for minimal generation of O3 and NO3, and also helps minimize other undesirable secondary reactions, such as photolysis of carbonyls and organic nitrates, , thereby enhancing the atmospheric relevance of the resulting oxidation chemistry. Combined with moderate OHexp levels equivalent to ∼1 atmospheric day, this chemical environment makes the OFR 369-i­(iPrONO) system comparable to environmental chamber studies under high-NO x conditions.

Stability of Temperature and Humidity

The temperature and humidity stability of the PKU-CAT were evaluated as summarized in Table S2. Temperature measurements across the setup (Figure ) show stable performance at six set points. When the UV lamp was turned on, heat from the lamp caused a moderate rise in reactor temperature, after which the system reached equilibrium within 0–2 h. Minor temperature fluctuations persisted at equilibrium due to the freezer’s periodic operation. As shown in Figure , the maximum standard deviation of the wall temperature during stable periods was 0.3 °C.

Humidity stability was evaluated at up to 80% RH at both 20 °C and – 20 °C (Figure S8). At both temperatures, RH remained generally stable for hours, with a maximum standard deviation of 1.13%. At 20 °C, a slight upward trend was observed, with maximum humidity increasing by 4% over three hours under midlevel RH conditions (∼45%). At −20 °C, no clear trend with time was observed, though small (∼3%) sharp fluctuations occurred, likely due to temperature variations in the freezer housing the PKU-CAT.

From the isothermal SMPS volume concentration, the SOA yield for 50 ppb α-pinene at −16 °C was calculated to be ∼120%, representing a 5-fold increase over the yield of ∼23% at 20 °C. The mass yield exceeding 100% likely results from the high O/C ratio of the condensing products. , The observed strong temperature dependence is consistent with limited prior research. Takekawa et al. reported that the SOA yield of α-pinene OH-oxidation at 10 °C was 23%, which was 4 times higher than that at 30 °C (5.2%) under conditions of 90–100 ppb of reacted α-pinene. Saathoff et al. showed that SOA yields from α-pinene ozonolysis at −30 to −20 °C approach the theoretical upper limits of ∼120%. The underlying physicochemical mechanisms warrant further investigation in future studies.

Conclusions

In summary, this study developed and characterized the PKU-CAT, an oxidation flow reactor designed to investigate SOA formation across a wide range of tropospheric conditions (−20 to 30 °C and 0–80% RH). To our knowledge, it is the first flow reactor capable of simulating atmospheric photooxidation in the challenging subzero temperature regime.

The reactor’s performance was evaluated through laboratory tests at both room and subzero temperatures. Measurements showed that the PKU-CAT has a near-laminar flow, even with a long mean residence time (420 s) to better simulate gas/particle partitioning. High transmission efficiency was observed for both reactive gases (>90% for ozone) and particles (∼90% for 100–200 nm particles). The reactor maintained excellent temperature and RH stability across all tested conditions, with standard deviations within 0.3 °C and 1.1% RH. Using iPrONO photolysis as the OH source, the PKU-CAT achieved OH exposures equivalent to roughly 1 day of atmospheric oxidation under high NO x conditions. Experiments on α-pinene photooxidation confirmed that the PKU-CAT can reproduce established chamber SOA yields at room temperature and effectively extends the experimental range to subzero conditions. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the PKU-CAT is a versatile and reliable platform for laboratory investigations of SOA formation across a wide range of tropospheric conditions.

Looking ahead, the PKU-CAT will be used to systematically quantify the influence of temperature and humidity on SOA yield from key atmospheric VOC precursors under tropospheric conditions (e.g., with varying composition and acidity of seed particles). This effort will address a critical knowledge gap in SOA formation during wintertime, leading to improved model parametrization. Additionally, detailed chemical analyses of oxidation products will be conducted to better understand the underlying mechanisms driving the temperature and humidity effects. Furthermore, by adjusting the wavelength of the UV-LED lamps to 254 nm for H2O2 photolysis, PKU-CAT might also be configured to simulate low-NO x conditions representative of the free troposphere.
