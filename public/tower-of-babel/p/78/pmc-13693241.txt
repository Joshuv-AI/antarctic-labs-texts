Alternative life history strategies of a marine parasitic dinoflagellate
Authors: Xuqing Chen, Kimberly S Reece, Sebastian G Gornik, Jeffrey D Shields
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs adaptive organisms, parasites display remarkable diversity in their life cycles and life history strategies, reflecting evolutionary pressures to find and inhabit their hosts. The order Syndiniales (i.e., Marine Alveolates II and IV) has been detected in global waters and has gained increasing interest in metabarcoding studies as cryptic members of the plankton. However, this taxon of parasitic dinoflagellates possesses a bizarre yet unstudied life history trait, where two morphologically distinct types of dinospores are produced and released when their life cycle within the host comes to an end. In this study, we employed laboratory infection assays, flow cytometry, and comparative transcriptomics to untangle the functions of the two dinospore types in the syndinean parasite, Hematodinium perezi, released from their Atlantic blue crab host Callinectes sapidus.ResultsMicro-dinospores of H. perezi are infectious to juvenile blue crab hosts through waterborne transmission. The DNA content of both spore types is twice that of the ameboid trophont, suggesting dinospore stages represent different life history strategies rather than being haploid gametes. Comparative transcriptome reveals distinct profiles among life history stages, with upregulations of genes related to motility and host invasion in the dinospore stages. The expression of meiosis genes further supports the existence of a cryptic sexual cycle within the host.ConclusionsOur results shed light on a long-standing question in the life history of a widespread group of parasitic dinoflagellates. The production of two dispersal stages is a unique adaptation of parasitic lifestyle and warrants consideration in interpretations of environmental data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-026-02690-9.Keywords: Ploidy, Alveloates, Syndiniales, Hematodinium, Life cycle, Transcriptome, Flow cytometry

Background

Adaptive life history strategies are employed by organisms with complex life cycles under different environmental conditions. Selection for such strategies operates on both phenotypic and genotypic levels and is well established in evolutionary and ecological terms [1, 2]. For example, the sexual cycles of algae and plants with their multiple ploidy levels and alternation of generations have been extensively studied in terms of morphological adaptations and genetic mechanisms [3–5]. In the context of parasites, by necessity, their environmental responses are often linked to that of their hosts and the progression of different life history stages is a switch that is central to their survival. In particular, the life cycles of ecologically and medically important protistan parasites are among the most well studied, spanning multiple phyla, including those causing malaria (Plasmodium spp.), giardiasis (Giardia spp.), and African trypanosomiasis (Trypanosoma spp.), to name a few, and resolutions of their life cycles have been further enhanced by molecular tools [6–8]. However, for the majority of protists, life cycles are still incompletely understood, with evidence of sexual reproduction largely inferred from archetypes and seldom directly observed [9]. This is especially the case for cryptic species such as syndinean dinoflagellates that are found in diverse marine host species in the global ocean [10, 11].

The Order Syndiniales is comprised exclusively of parasites, branching basal to the core dinoflagellates [12, 13]. Most syndinean dinoflagellates are considered parasitoids, or killer parasites, because their sporogenic reproduction leads to a significant loss of host biomass and eventually host death [14, 15]. These parasites consist of both host generalists and specialists with reported hosts including protists, crustaceans, and fishes [16–18]. In several marine ecosystems they can be highly prevalent, often causing significant disease outbreaks and death in their host populations [16–18].

A common feature of syndinean dinoflagellates is the generation of two types of morphologically distinct dinospores upon completion of the life cycle within a host [19–22]. The spore types are termed macro- and micro-dinospores (also macro- and microspores) and there has been some speculation that they are anisogametes—a pair of unlike gametes. To date, sexuality in syndinean dinoflagellates, however, has been rarely reported, with Coats et al. (2012) being the only recent study that recorded photographic evidence of syngamy, with fusion of two different spore types of Eudubosquella crenulata resulting in a zygote that divided into four cells [23]. However, a recent comprehensive phylogenetic analysis placed E. crenulata well within the Marine Alveolates I group (MALV-I, now recognized as the Ichthyodinida) and therefore no longer within the Syndiniales [13]. Consequently, with E. crenulata now excluded, no syndinean dinospore types have ever been observed acting as gametes fusing to form a zygote [22, 24, 25]. As a result, the sexual cycles of syndinean dinoflagellates remain unknown, and the functional roles of the different dinospore types are still unresolved.

The genera Amoebophrya and Hematodinium are among the best studied syndinean dinoflagellates, belonging to two different phylogenetic groups in Syndiniales, known as Marine Alveolates II and IV (MALV-II and MALV-IV), respectively [12]. With a comparatively large range in crustacean hosts and host sizes (amphipods, crabs, and shrimp), and rapid development time, Hematodinium perezi provides several advantages as a model for studying the different dinospore stages of a syndinean dinoflagellate. Within the host blue crab Callinectes sapidus, the most frequently observed stage is the ameboid trophont, which circulates in the host’s hemolymph and obtains nutrients through osmotrophy. Upon completion of development in an individual host, the parasite’s cells develop synchronously into either macro- or micro-dinospores, but not both, which allows each stage to be collected independently [25]. The parasite also exhibits notable responses to temperature, allowing dinospore stages to be acquired at high densities over an incubation period of 10–30 days at 25 °C [26]. Lastly, transmission experiments in laboratory settings have confirmed that waterborne transmission occurs from free-living stages, following sporulation and death of the host crab [27, 28], showing laboratory experiments are valid options for examining transmission of dinospores in controlled settings.

The functions of the different dinospores of Hematodinium spp. have been puzzling since they were first reported by Chatton and Poisson (1930) [29] and later by Meyers et al. (1987) [20] and Eaton et al. (1991) [30]. Laboratory experiments using dinospores have repeatedly ended in failure of infection or lack of progression to other free-living stages. Nonetheless, the in vitro life cycle has documented the development of cultures arising from either dinospore type into progressive stages, negating the hypothesis for them to be anisogametes [24, 25]. These different spore types are not cryptic species as evinced by Syndinium turbo, a sister taxon to Hematodinium within MALV-IV, in which SSU rDNA sequences from three spore morphotypes were identical and ITS1–ITS2 sequences were nearly identical, thereby supporting a monospecific origin [22].

Here, in an attempt to resolve the long-standing question of what the function of these two spore morphotypes is, we employed laboratory infection assays, flow cytometry, and comparative transcriptomics on each spore type. With a focus on comparing the ameboid trophont and two dinospore stages, the objectives of this study were to (1) determine whether either spore type can establish infections in host blue crabs, (2) examine the ploidy of the two dinospore stages in comparison to the ameboid trophont to assess their potential role in the life cycle; and (3) investigate functional differences between these life history stages at the level of the transcriptome, in order to gain potential insights into their underlying functionality in relation to molecular mechanisms.

Results

Micro-dinospores are capable of direct transmission of infections to crabs

To determine whether either spore type is infectious, we exposed uninfected crabs to dinospores released from naturally infected crabs. Sporulation events were sporadic at the onset of the exposure experiment and became more frequent between days 15 and 30, echoing previously reported patterns [27]. Over the course of the experiment, a total of 54 crabs released macro-dinospores and a total of 60 crabs released micro-dinospores. Loose synchronicity in sporulation appeared on day 25, where 32 crabs released micro-dinospores and nine crabs released macro-dinospores, and marked the highest daily dosage in all aquaria (Fig. 1). Macro-dinospores were dosed into the macro-dinospore treatment aquaria over a total of 22 days, spanning from day 0 to day 43. The total number of dinospores dosed in the macro-dinospore aquaria ranged from 7.33 × 105 to 9.73 × 107 cells/crab, which converts into a range of ~ 2.63 × 104 to ~ 4.68 × 106 cells/L/day (Fig. 1). Micro-dinospores sporulated from crabs later in the experiment were dosed into the treatment aquaria over a total of 11 days between days 15 and 39. The total number of cells dosed into the micro-dinospore aquaria ranged from 5.33 × 106 to 3.42 × 108 cells/crab, which converts to a range of ~ 1.40 × 105 to ~ 7.58 × 107 cells/L/day (Fig. 1).

One 38 L aquarium per treatment was dosed with the corresponding dinospore type: macro-dinospore, micro-dinospore, and both at half of the volume of each dinospore type (not shown). Crabs were dissected in groups of five and diagnosed on days 15, 29, 36, 43, and 50. Crabs dissected on day 43 and day 50 included replacements of previous dissections. Although micro-dinospores were dosed at high density, transmission of infection was sparse. Dosage of macro-dinospores, on the other hand, did not result in transmission of infection to naïve crabs. Groups of five crabs were dissected from all of the treatment aquaria on days 15, 29, 36, 43, and 50, and those dissected on days 29 and 36 were replaced (Fig. 1). A total of 15 crabs were dissected from the control aquaria at the end of the experiment. Three crabs from the micro-dinospore aquarium were found with infections (3/10 dissected), spanning two separate dissection days. Two crabs with 21-day exposures between days 15 and 36 were each found with a mixed light infection of filamentous trophont and a medium infection of ameboid trophonts of H. perezi. Another crab that was dissected a week later on day 43 was found with a heavy infection of ameboid trophonts. Although the exact timing of transmission is unknown, the onset of infection likely started in the latter half of the experiment based on the relatively early stages of infection and evidence on the acquisition of infections from field data [31, 32].

The ameboid trophont and the dinospores differ in their DNA content

The DNA content of ameboid trophonts, micro-dinospores, macro-dinospores, blue crab hemocytes, and chicken red blood cells (CRBC) was measured using flow cytometry following 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining. At least one peak was identifiable in all biological replicates of each stage as well as technical replicates of CRBC cells (Fig. 2A, Additional file 1: Figures S1–S2). The coefficient of variation (CV) values for blue crab hemocyte and chicken red blood cells were 5–9% and 3–5%, respectively, suggesting high quality measurements for the control materials (Additional file 1: Figure S3). The CV values for micro-dinospores, macro-dinospores, and ameboid trophonts were 4–9%, 4–11%, and 29–33%, respectively. High CV values in ameboid trophonts may result from variations in the copy number of mitochondria within a cell and therefore, mitochondrial genomes [33], or differences in cell cycle stage, because the ameboid trophont is a vegetative stage capable of binary fission [25].

B Relative DNA content estimated using chicken red blood cells (CRBC) as an external standard (i.e., equal to 1.0). One ameboid trophont sample had two recognized peaks, with one being an outlier as shown (n = 5)

Most ameboid trophont samples tested in flow cytometry were taken at stages prior to the production of dinospores, corroborated by the single peak observed in most replicates (Additional file 1: Figure S1 A–E). Specifically, the micro-dinospore sample, micro14, and ameboid trophont sample, amb14, were taken from the same adult crab during different periods of infection 30 days apart (Fig. 2A, Additional file 1: Table S1). The macro-dinospore sample, macro69, was collected from a different crab, and the relative DNA content was 2.44 times higher than what was seen in sample amb14 (Fig. 2A). Given the differences in DNA content, the ameboid trophont was assigned 1C as it had the lowest fluorescence, whereas that of the dinospores was assigned as 2C, due to their higher fluorescence. The macro-dinospore samples exhibited a lesser additional peak in DNA content, ~ 1.2 times lower than that of the large peak (Fig. 2A, Additional file 1: Figure S1 F–J). All samples were tested with the same protocol at constant settings and thus measurement error due to inconsistent staining was unlikely.

The genome size of H. perezi was estimated using three replicates of chicken red blood cells as external standards (Additional file 1: Figure S3 A–C). The mean relative DNA content in comparison to CRBC (test:CRBC ratio) was 0.78 ± 0.13, 1.90 ± 0.12, and 2.28 ± 0.21 for the ameboid trophont, micro-dinospore, and macro-dinospore stages, respectively (Fig. 2B). Based on the genome size of chickens at 2.1 Gb for 2C [34], the genome size of ameboid trophont, micro-dinospore, and macro-dinospore stages can be estimated as 1.6 Gb, 4.0 Gb, and 4.8 Gb; the latter are consistent with the genome size estimated for Hematodinium-like species from the Norway lobster at 4.8 Gb [35]. The mean relative DNA content of blue crab hemocytes was 1.76 ± 0.14, with an estimated genome size of 3.7 Gb, indicating that ameboid trophont samples obtained from adult crab hemolymph were not contaminated by crab hemocytes (Fig. 2B, Additional file 1: Figure S3 D–H).

As an independent line of evidence, allele frequency analyses were performed using RNA-seq data from each life history stage mapped to the reference transcriptome. Across all stages, allele frequencies form a broad distribution centered around 0.4–0.5, which is compatible with a diploid genetic signal (Additional file 1: Figure S4).

H. perezi dinospore transcriptomes display key characteristics typical of previously described Hematodinium transcriptomes

Using reads generated from ameboid trophonts, micro-, macro-dinospores, and prespores, we assembled 175,307 transcripts (Additional file 1: Table S2). Clustering of isoforms resulted in 94,395 unique transcripts, hereafter referred to as genes, with 34,132 predicted ORFs. The completeness from BUSCO returned 85.3% compared with the eukaryota_odb12 dataset and 97.0% compared with the alveolata_odb12 dataset. This is comparable to the transcriptome of Hematodinium sp. (85.9%) [36] and the genome of Amoebophrya ceratii (89.1%) [37]. Using Trinotate, we obtained 33,147 UniProtKB (< e−5) hits, 30,066 Pfam domains and 28,629 KEGG annotations (Additional file 2: Dataset S1). In addition, we recovered 20,994 genes with GO term assignments (< e−6) using Blast2GO (Additional file 2: Dataset S1). To assess the reliability of the assembly, we searched the transcriptome using a curated set of proteins related to plastid loss. We recovered matches to 21 out of 22 proteins from tetrapyrrole, isoprenoid, and fatty acid pathways, such as HemB–E, DapL, and multiple polyketide synthases (Additional file 1: Table S3). Likewise, we did not find evidence of type II FAS enzymes or proteins related to the DOXP pathway and only SufA was found in the iron-sulfur cluster assembly (Additional file 1: Table S3). Furthermore, we found proteins corresponding to the elongase (ELO) and mitochondrial cysteine desulfurase (IscS) pathways, which have been previously found in apicomplexan parasites as controls for non-plastidial pathways [38]. These collectively indicate that our transcriptome assembly reproduces main features of the Hematodinium transcriptome.

Dinospores types displayed distinct patterns of gene expression

Given that we started with the premise that the dinospore stages were not gametes, the question became what their exact functions are and why H. perezi develops two spore phenotypes that are evolutionarily stable? Through comparative transcriptome analysis, we found that the transcriptomic profiles were very different among the ameboid trophont, the prespore, macro- and micro-dinospore stages, as indicated by clear separations between stages, and biological replicates were clustered in principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 3A). Samples of the prespore stage clustered with those of macro-dinospore on PCA and had less than 0.05% differentially expressed genes when compared to each other; thus, this stage was considered an immature macro-dinospore and, therefore, was not included in subsequent analyses. In comparison with the ameboid trophont stage, the micro-dinospore stage had a larger number of differentially expressed genes (4300 upregulated and 4887 downregulated) than the macro-dinospore stage (2407 upregulated and 2575 downregulated). Interestingly, 47.7% of upregulated genes were shared between the two dinospore stages, but 55.6% of the downregulated genes were uniquely expressed in the micro-dinospore stage (Fig. 3B).

Functional enrichment analysis on differentially expressed genes revealed that aside from expected flagellar-related gene ontology (GO) terms such as cilium and microtubule genes, both types of dinospores reflected upregulations in genes coding these processes: translation (biological process, BP), cytosolic small ribosomal subunit, ribosome, and ribonucleoprotein complex (cellular component, CC) (Fig. 4). Additionally, proteasome-mediated ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic processes (BP), proteasome core complex (CC), and protein-folding chaperone (molecular function, MF) were overrepresented in micro-dinospores. Enriched GO terms in downregulated genes did not overlap between the two dinospore types. Only macro-dinospores showed lowered metabolic and enzymatic activities in all three GO categories, which included biological processes such as transcription and translation regulation; cell division and glycolytic process; cellular components such as extracellular space, nucleoplasm and cytosol; and molecular functions such as actin filament binding and NADP binding (Fig. 4).

Genes were considered significantly expressed when |log2(Fold change)|> 1.5 and adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05. BP, CC, and MF correspond to biological process, cellular component, and molecular function, respectively

Patterns observed in functional enrichment analysis allowed us to further examine the transcriptome for genes with specific functions. To determine whether cyst formation is a possibility in the life cycle, we searched the transcriptome using a list of oocyst wall proteins and 1,3-beta-glucan synthase from T. gondii [39] and did not find any cyst-related homologs. We then searched for genes known in host-parasite interactions and identified 123 homologs from the transcriptome (Additional file 3: Dataset S2), of which 59 were significantly differentially expressed between life history stages (Fig. 5). Diverse proteases were found, including aspartic protease, serine protease, cysteine protease, and metalloprotease. Specifically, we found two homologs of subtilisin-like serine protease in P. marinus [40], and both genes increased more than sevenfold in both dinospore stages. Additional proteases that were activated in dinospore stages are aspartic protease, cathepsin L, and rhomboid-like protease. We also detected proteins containing thrombospondin type 1 (TSP1) domains, which are adhesive and known to participate in cell-to-cell interactions in apicomplexan parasites [41]. Interestingly, SUMOylation/ubiquitination pathways, known to be related to pathogenesis and cyst development in T. gondii [42], seem to be mostly activated in ameboid trophonts and macro-dinospores. Cytoskeletal proteins are involved in the gliding motility and cellular invasion of apicomplexan parasites [43]. We found that the majority of actin homologs are upregulated in micro-dinospores, while myosin homologs are upregulated in ameboid trophonts.

Meiosis genes are expressed in the dinospore stages of H. perezi

To determine whether a cryptic sexual cycle exists in H. perezi and whether meiosis follows the formation of dinospores, we searched and identified 30 homologs to genes involved in meiosis in the transcriptome (Additional file 3: Dataset S2), and four of them (SPO11, MND1, DMC1, and MSH4) are exclusively related to meiosis (Fig. 6). Only MSH4 was significantly upregulated in the dinospores, with a 16.1-fold increase in expression in the micro-dinospore stage and a 5.6-fold increase in the macro-dinospore stage compared to the ameboid trophont (Additional file 3: Dataset S2). In addition, although not significant, SPO11 expression increased 2.86-fold in micro-dinospores. The gene for the protein CDC2, known to play a role in dinoflagellates cell cycle regulation, was upregulated in both dinospore types [44]. PMS1 is a meiosis-related gene involved in mismatch repair and was significantly downregulated in the macro-dinospore [45]. We also found the presence of hapless2 (HAP2), a gamete fusogen gene required for membrane fusion during fertilization and highly conserved throughout eukaryotic lineages [46, 47].

To search for clues in meiotic and mitotic activities, we also queried the transcriptome for genes encoding dinoflagellate/viral nucleoproteins (DVNP) and histones. We found eight sequences coding for DVNPs and 22 sequences coding for histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4 (Additional file 3: Dataset S2). Three DVNP genes (DVNP.3, DVNP.7, DVNP.12) are downregulated in micro-dinospores and DVNP.4 is upregulated in macro-dinospores. The majority of the histones (six out of nine), on the other hand, are upregulated in micro-dinospores (Additional file 1: Figure S5).

Discussion

This study examined the intricate life history traits of a parasitic dinoflagellate with major ecological and economic impacts using comprehensive approaches. We first identified that exposure to the micro-dinospore stage leads to infection of H. perezi in juvenile blue crabs, supporting direct transmission of at least one dinospore type. Low prevalence levels and a lack of transmission in aquaria in the other treatments suggest that the laboratory settings we used may not be ideal for transmission of H. perezi. Dosage has been shown to play a role in infection outcome; in Portunus trituberculatus, exposure to higher concentrations of H. perezi micro-dinospores resulted in higher prevalence [28]. Although maximum allowable dosage was used to overcome constraints of laboratory conditions, infection success is likely influenced by host susceptibility and physiological state, dinospore viability and virulence, as well as limited water circulation in small aquarium systems. Difference in infectivity indicates that transmission dynamics in natural populations may be context dependent, shaped by the interaction between host life cycle and stage-specific biology of each dinospore type.

Given similar crab sizes used in sporulation studies, the number of micro-dinospores generated from a single crab was ten times higher than that for macro-dinospores (Additional file 1: Figure S4). Such a difference was expected because sporulation is associated with heavy infections in the haemal spaces of an infected crab and the size of micro-dinospores is two to three times smaller than that of macro-dinospores, allowing for a higher number of micro-dinospore cells within the limited carrying capacity of the infected host. Because a crab only produces one type of dinospore, a trade-off in life history traits is expected, where macro-dinospores may face lower grazing pressure and have higher longevity by having larger cell size at the expense of lower productivity and dispersal than micro-dinospores. Although we did not quantitatively measure behavior and viability in our experiment, previous work tracking cell movement shows that macro-dinospores have substantially lower motility than micro-dinospores [48]. In this study, macro-dinospores were always found sedentary at the bottom of the petri-dish whereas micro-dinospores were always actively swimming in the water column, suggesting that they may be occupying different niches in the natural environment or using different infection modes, such as ingestion vs. penetration, respectively. Such strategies are in parallel with life cycles of free-living dinoflagellates, where motile flagellated stages and non-motile cyst stages have different ecological functions and are formed in response to environmental changes [49]. In protistan parasites, an analogous scenario might be that of the two different oocyst/spore types produced by Cryptosporidium baileyi [50] and Paranucleospora theridion [51], where one type is responsible for autoinfection, while the other leaves the host and is responsible for infecting new hosts through fecal–oral routes. Syndinean parasites like H. perezi are different from many parasitic taxa in that dinospore development is a one-time terminal investment made by the (killer) parasite, with the synchronous release of dinospores, potentially due to an exhaustion of resources and a deteriorating host environment [52]. Our results aligned with previously observed difficulties in establishing infections using Hematodinium macro-dinospores from Helice tientsinensis [28]. In parallel, a recent study on Amoebophrya shows that one type of dinospore is infectious to hosts and the other type expresses meiotic genes [53]. With this finding, it is now supported in at least two syndinean parasites, whether intra- or inter-cellular, that spore fate is pre-determined in a host before release, and at least one spore type is infectious. Such phenotypic polymorphism is commonly seen in protists exposed to heterogeneous environments [54], but the macro-dinospores are seemingly less efficient at dispersal, which is one of the fundamental needs for parasites. Therefore, further experimentations on sporogenesis, alternative routes of infection and dormancy periods in this life history stage may offer valuable insights into the evolutionary causes of complex life cycles in protists.

Our results from flow cytometry clearly demonstrate a transition in DNA content between stages and is indicative of the presence of sexual fusion in H. perezi occurring within the crab host. That is, sexual reproduction may be obligate in dinospore formation and transmission of H. perezi, much like that observed for apicomplexan parasites [55]. In sharp contrast, in free-living dinoflagellates, sexual reproduction occurs externally via a planozygote or a sexual cyst [56]. This appears not to be the case for H. perezi and is likely not the case for other endoparasitic syndineans. Moreover, intermediate allele frequencies found across stages support our inference on the presence of diploid stages. Although the same pattern is also observed in the ameboid trophont, which is expected to be haploid, it may be a consequence of multiple infections, which have previously been documented in this parasite [57]. In this context, if diploid spore stages undergo meiosis within the host, the resulting haploid population would naturally comprise multiple genotypes. Still, we acknowledge that allele frequency analyses based on RNA-seq data have inherent limitations, including allele-specific expression, mapping bias and uneven transcript coverage [58].

Prior to this study, the only comparison of DNA content between different stages in Hematodinium was made by Eaton et al. (1991) using potassium acetate precipitation and ethidium bromide spectrofluorometry [30]. They concluded that the vegetative stage and dinospore stages had similar ploidy. However, they did not include replicated samples for testing, and it is possible that the vegetative stage was mixed with sporoblast stages due to their similar morphology. Meyers et al. (1987) suggested that the advanced stages observed in Tanner crabs were most likely sporoblast stages rather than ameboid trophont stages [20]. The discrepancies call for caution in differentiating between vegetative and reproductive stages in H. perezi and determination of a refined sexual cycle in the life cycle. Although our methods provide improved resolution, estimates of DNA content based on DAPI staining may be influenced by mitochondrial DNA contributing to the fluorescence signal. The mitochondrial genome of Hematodinium is highly fragmented, with multiple copies of each gene [33], and such variability could complicate interpretations based on total DNA measurements. In Amoebophrya, mitochondria can undergo structural remodeling and volume expansion during development [59]. In particular, the dinospore stage is associated with elevated respiration, yet there is currently no evidence that this is accompanied by increases in mitochondrial DNA content. Although mitochondria are present in all stages of Hematodinium [24], it is unlikely that mitochondrial DNA alone could inflate the DAPI signal to the scale observed here. Alternative approaches, such as fluorescence in situ hybridization targeting low-copy genes [60] or telomeric probes [61], would help refine ploidy assessment in future work. Variations in DNA content between the two spore stages may be further explained by chromosomal differences or aberrations, as they are known from dinoflagellates [62], diatoms [63], and chrysophytes [64]. Alterations in chromosome condensation or arrangement due to developmental or environmental stressors cannot be ruled out either. Transmission electron microscopy of Hematodinium sp. isolated from Norway lobster shows highly compact chromatin in micro-dinospores, with the nucleus entirely filled by condensed chromosomes, while chromatin appears less densely packed in macro-dinospores [24].

The synchronous development of H. perezi granted the opportunity for comparative transcriptomics without strenuous effort in purifying each stage. Overall, 30.1% and 16.3% of genes were differentially expressed in micro- and macro-dinospores, respectively. Functional enrichment analysis on these genes suggests that although micro-dinospores underwent morphogenesis maximized for motility and dispersal, they may be preparing for host invasion by synthesizing new proteins and recycling unneeded proteins immediately after leaving the previous host. Protein synthesis and ribosome biogenesis are critical for cell division and growth [65] and are expressed in the early asexual stages of the apicomplexans Cryptosporidium parvum [66] and T. gondii [67]. Considering ameboid trophonts are metabolically more active in the nutrient-rich host environment, reduced cell signaling and secretory activities should be expected in macro-dinospores. Notably, the minimal motility, sinking behavior, and larger cell size of macro-dinospores, with the down regulations in cellular processes may be indicative of an ensuing encystment, but encystment, or a functional dormancy period, has never been observed for H. perezi. Our failure to find cyst-related homologs suggests that the macro-dinospore has a different encystment mechanism, does not form a cyst wall, or that the downregulated metabolism is not a component of transmission.

A diverse set of genes related to host invasion was identified in the transcriptome. Similar proteases have been found in the transcriptome of Perkinsus marinus [68] and Amoebophrya sp. [69]. For example, serine proteases have been reported in host invasion processes in P. marinus [70]. Aspartic protease, cathepsin L, and rhomboid-like protease are widely present in pathogens and are responsible for pathogenicity and host cell and tissue invasion [71–73]. Each stage, however, had a unique set of genes expressed. The lack of expression in SUMOylation/ubiquitination pathways in micro-dinospores suggests that they may be using stored proteins due to their high specialization or different regulatory pathways. In apicomplexan parasites, protease gene expression can be stage-specific and linked to lifecycle regulations and catabolism [74]. Likewise, patterns of cytoskeletal protein expression reflects stage-specific functional differentiation, with micro-dinospores likely relying on actin to maintain cellular structure during swimming, whereas ameboid trophont may utilize myosin and profilin for shape changes and movement, as observed in other protists [75]. Collectively, micro-dinospores seem to be more differentiated in the expression of homologous genes related to host-parasite interactions whereas macro-dinospores still maintained several functions that are shared with ameboid trophonts.

In free-living dinoflagellates Prorocentrum shikokuense, Karenia mikimotoi, and Scrippsiella acuminata, SPO11, MND1, and DMC1 are common sex-indicating genes during blooms and MSH4 is upregulated during cyst germination [76]. The proteins MSH4 and MSH5 function in facilitating crossover events in the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae [77] and are found in early recombination during meiosis prophase I in the plant Arabidopsis [78]. Because SPO11 marks the onset of meiotic recombination through DNA double-strand breaks [79], accumulation of meiosis-specific genes suggests that dinospores may be preparing for meiosis and micro-dinospores seem to have earlier priming than macro-dinospores. This aligns with our finding that micro-dinospores can transmit infections that lead ultimately to early filamentous trophonts in the crab host. The presence of HAP2 homolog also supports the occurrence of syngamy in H. perezi; yet its relation to specific gametes is far from conclusive.

Although chromosomes are permanently condensed in Hematodinium [24, 80], variations in chromosome condensation have been observed across life history stages in Noctiluca scintillans [81] and Blastodinium sp. [82]. As DVNPs strongly bind to DNA and locate to condensed chromatin in Hematodinium [35], the downregulation of their genes in micro-dinospores may suggest a reduction in DNA condensation to facilitate recombination. Although histone expression is not variable at the transcript level during the life cycle of Lingulodinium polyedrum [83] and Alexandrium pacificum [84], it has been observed to peak at G2/M phase of the dinoflagellate Prorocentrum donghaiense [85]. Thus, higher histone expression in micro-dinospores may be consistent with increased chromatin remodeling, which has been proposed to bipartition with DVNPs [86]. However, because post-translational modifications have been found in Hematodinium DVNPs [35] and dinoflagellate histones [87], transcript abundance can be decoupled from chromatin function, underscoring the need for stage-specific proteomics to define chromatin states.

All dinoflagellate life cycles described to date are haplontic [88], with the exception of the diplontic Noctiluca [81]. Figueroa et al. (2015) proposed a diplohaplontic model for Alexandrium minutum, which includes mitosis in the diploid phase [61]. For species of Hematodinium, the independent development of ameboid trophont and dinospore stages points to a biphasic life cycle, which is supported ecologically because endoparasitic and free-living stages face entirely different environments [89]. Biphasic life cycles, although present across diverse lineages of protists, are considered ancient strategies due to the evolutionary tendency towards either haplonty or diplonty [90]. However, such systems may also represent flexible states retained under specific ecological pressures. In parasitic taxa, this flexibility may confer advantages by enabling the balance of growth, reproduction, and dispersal [54]. In a basal lineage like Hematodinium, the presence of both “free-living” and ameboid stages may reflect not only ancestral traits, but adaptive strategies associated with parasitism in aquatic environments. Although the life cycle of its closest free-living relative (eleftherids) is still unknown, the asexual haploid stages exhibit traits functionally analogous to apicomplexans, suggesting possible convergent evolution of host strategies across parasitic protist lineages.

Exactly where syngamy (fusion) and meiosis occur in the life cycle of H. perezi remains undetermined. If the micro-dinospore is a 2C transmissive stage, and a reduction division is needed to derive a 1C ameboid trophont, then the earliest stage found in a crab, the filamentous trophont, is the most likely stage where meiosis may take place. This stage is often found with four nuclei and rapidly divides to form the ameboid trophont stage. Considering the difficulty in obtaining large numbers of filamentous trophonts, and their relatively large size (up to 150 µm), flow cytometry may not be the best method to study them. The only evidence for their ploidy that we have so far is the same chromosome numbers counted in both the filamentous trophont stage from shore crabs [29] and the sporoblast stage from Norway lobsters [24], which are two very different species of Hematodinium. Although neither stage was sampled in this study, a peek into the intermediate stages, the dinospores, provides valuable clues as to where meiosis likely occurs in this parasite.

The potential involvement of life history stages in sexual processes can be further investigated through crossing experiments combined with differential live staining approaches, as described in other Alveolates [91]. In H. perezi, dinospores are uninucleate and arise from repeated divisions in the sporoblast stage [24, 25]. Our findings suggest that the arachnoid trophont, with its syncytial network, is the likely site for syngamy, and the possible germinative centers (Additional file 1: Figure S6) located in the mass of the arachnoid sporonts may represent the formation of zygotes (Fig. 7). Sporoblasts arising from the arachnoid mass would then be undergoing repeated bouts of mitosis rather than meiosis, giving rise to the different dinospore types. Because the ameboid trophonts are the only putative 1C, or haploid, stages with uninucleate forms that circulate in the hemolymph, they may transition into isogametes as infections progress in the host. Syngamy or fusion of gametes, in the arachnoid tissue phase, may explain the synchronous generation of sporoblasts and dinospores in the hemolymph, because the ameboid trophonts all “disappear” in heavy infections as they develop into the syncytial mass of the arachnoid stages. Considering the ability of sporoblasts to develop into secondary filamentous trophonts, the “schizont shunt” in the life cycle described by [25] likely represents autoinfection perhaps arising vis a vis the internal sexual cycle.

Schematic figure of life history stages potentially involved in the sexual cycle of H. perezi. The difference in DNA content among ameboid trophont (1C) and the two dinospore stages (2C) suggests fusion likely occurs in the arachnoid trophont, within the syncytium-like network among cells. Sporoblasts detach from arachnoid sporont and give rise to either micro- or macro-dinospores, which are released into the water column. Micro-dinospores directly transmitted new infections to blue crabs in this study but not macro-dinospores, thus the latter is represented in a dashed arrow. The filamentous trophont is the first stage identified in new infections, where a meiotic reduction division is likely involved in its development into ameboid trophont

Conclusions

To our knowledge, Hematodinium perezi has one of the few biphasic life cycles described in dinoflagellates, where stages with different ploidies possess functions other than meiosis, such as host invasion, revealed by their distinct transcriptome profiles. Although mechanisms in generating different dinospore types wait to be explored, their doubled DNA content in comparison to the vegetative ameboid trophont stage strongly suggests that they represent very different life history strategies instead of being gametes. We believe H. perezi is a parasite with great potential for additional cellular and evolutionary studies. It has high phenotypic diversities with respect to life history stages and complex transmission pathways that appear to respond to environmental adaptation and host interactions.

Methods

Exposure experiment

Infected juvenile blue crabs < 30 mm carapace width (CW) were collected using dipnets and small scrapes (48 × 22 × 18 cm frame with 5-mm mesh bag end) from Cobb Bay (37° 19.234′ N, 75° 47.120′ W), a hyper-endemic area for H. perezi on the Virginia portion of the Delmarva Peninsula. Crabs were transported back to the laboratory at Virginia Institute of Marine Science in seawater in coolers. Each crab was placed in an individual petri dish filled with 90 ml of artificial sea water (30 psu) and kept in an incubator at 25 °C to stimulate sporulation as in [26]. All crabs were checked daily for mortality and sporulation. When a sporulation event was observed, the petri dish was examined with an Olympus IX50 inverted microscope (Olympus Life Science) to determine the type and viability of the dinospores. Spore types were distinguished on the basis of size and activity. Macro-dinospores are 15–18 µm in length and are ovoid and poor swimmers; micro-dinospores are 4–6 µm in length, often possess a short “keel” and are active swimmers [26] (Fig. 8).

Light microscopy images of different life history stages of Hematodinium perezi at 1000 × magnification. Photographs were taken with Nomarski interference contrast for samples preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin (A–C) and with Chroma 49,000 DAPI filter set for samples preserved in DAPI solution (D–F): ameboid trophont (A and D), macro-dinospore (B and E), micro-dinospore (C and F)

Naïve crabs were collected from a non-endemic area, Mobjack Bay (37° 18′ N, 76° 24′ W), on the western side of Chesapeake Bay, VA, USA. This site has been used in previous studies to obtain uninfected, or naïve, crabs [27, 31, 32]. Naïve crabs were placed in four 38-L aquaria filled with artificial sea water at 30 psu. Each aquarium contained 15 naïve juvenile crabs, placed individually in 90-ml urinalysis cups (Fisher Scientific®) that had ~ 20 drilled holes (~ 8 mm dia) and a shallow layer of crushed coral to keep them on the bottom. When infected crabs sporulated (released spores) in their petri dishes, viable dinospores were collected and dosed into one of the above aquaria based on spore type. Two aquaria were dosed with ~ 60 ml of either macro- or micro-dinospores, one aquarium received ~ 30 ml of both types of dinospores, and the control aquarium was not dosed with dinospores. To monitor for infections, a subset of 5 crabs from each dosed aquaria were dissected and replaced periodically, starting on day 15 of the experiment. Subsequent dissections occurred on day 29, day 36, and day 43. On day 50, all remaining crabs were dissected. Infections in crabs were diagnosed with a dissection smear stained with 0.3% neutral red in saline buffer as in [32].

Dinospore collection for flow cytometry and cell density

Additional infected crabs were collected and maintained as above until they died and released spores during sporulation. Water containing live dinospores was pre-filtered into two sterile Falcon tubes through a 20 μm Nucleopore™ membrane (Whatman®) using a sterile filter assembly. A 1 ml sub-sample was fixed in 1 ml of 10% neutral buffered formalin (1:1 ratio) for later density estimates using a Neubauer hemocytometer. The remaining water samples were then centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min. The pellet in each tube was washed with filtered artificial sea water and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 3 min in a sterile 1.5-ml microfuge tube. The pellets were then resuspended in ~ 1 ml of DNA/RNA shield (Zymo Research Corp) or DAPI staining solution (NaCl 8.5 g/L; Tris base 1.2 g/L; CaCl2 0.59 g/L; MgCl2 8.95 g/L; Tween 80 0.1%; DAPI 10 mg/L; dimethyl sulfoxide 10%). All samples in the DNA/RNA shield or DAPI solutions were stored at − 80 °C.

Samples of the ameboid trophont were collected from infected adult blue crabs as in [92] after diagnosis of the infection from a hemolymph smear. Briefly, 2–3 ml of hemolymph was extracted using a 1-ml syringe with a 27-gauge needle and incubated in culture flasks with sterile lobster saline medium (NaCl 27.99 g/L; KCl 0.95 g/L; CaCl2 2.014 g/L; MgSO4 2.465 g/L; Na2SO4 0.554 g/L; NaHCO3 1.92 g/L, modified from [24]). To allow host hemocytes to adhere to the flask and enrich for the parasite cells, each sample was incubated for 10-min intervals and then serially poured into new culture flasks two additional times as in [25]. The enriched samples were then centrifuged and preserved in DNA/RNA shield or DAPI using the same procedure as above for dinospores.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was undertaken to determine the relative DNA content of different cell types. Five biological replicates of three parasite stages as well as crab hemocytes were pre-fixed in the DAPI staining solution (Fig. 8). All parasite stages except one sample of a dinospore stage were obtained from sporulation events from juvenile crabs. All ameboid trophont and hemocyte samples were obtained from infected and non-infected adult crabs, respectively. Three replicates of chicken red blood cells were also fixed in the DAPI staining solution for use as an external standard. Before each run, each sample was resuspended using a 21-gauge needle and filtered through a 40-μm mesh filter membrane. Flow cytometric analysis was performed on a CyFlow™ Space (Sysmex, USA) flow cytometer equipped with a 365-nm high-power UV LED for DAPI excitation. Relative fluorescence intensities, which represent relative DNA content, were obtained using peak analysis based on Gaussian distributions in FloMax 2.11 (Partec GmbH, Germany). The resulting fps files were then exported to R and plotted with flowCore package v2.22.0 [93].

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from samples at each life history stage using a ZymoBIOMICS™ DNA/RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research Corp) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples of ameboid trophonts consisted of three individual replicates from different crab hosts and each of the dinospore stages consisted of four individual replicates from different crab hosts. In addition, the prespore stage, described in [26] as having a more rounded morphology than macro-dinospores but also released by crabs, was also sampled with four individual replicates from different crabs. Each sample was further purified and concentrated as needed using a HighPrep™ RNA Elite Clean-up System (MagBio Genomics Inc). Total RNA was quantified on a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer using Qubit RNA HS assay (ThermoFisher Scientific). Sample quality was verified for each sample with 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEB Ultra II RNA kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer to generate 150 paired end reads.

Sequence analysis

A total of 405,994,997 raw reads were generated from sequencing. The raw reads were cleaned by (1) trimming adapters, (2) deleting those when the average quality per base dropped below 25 in a 4 bp scan, and (3) discarding reads shorter than 45 nucleotides. Cleaned reads were then aligned to the genome of the blue crab [94] using bowtie2 [95] to remove reads of host DNA. A total of 341,277,281 reads were retained for downstream transcriptome assembly. To complement the flow cytometric analysis, allele frequency distributions were examined across SNPs in each life history stage as an independent line of evidence for ploidy inference. Read mapping, SNP calling and allele frequency analyses are detailed in Additional file 1: Materials and Methods.

To build a reference transcriptome, all samples were pooled and assembled de novo using Trinity v.2.15.1 [96] with a minimum contig length of 300 bp. Only contigs with ≥ 1 transcript per million (TPM) across any sample were retained, ending with a total of 347,468 contigs. To reduce redundancy, the contigs were further clustered into 175,307 contigs using CD-HIT-EST [97] at a similarity threshold of 0.90 and a word size of 8. Expression was quantified by estimating counts of reads mapped to each contig and summarized at the Trinity gene level using RSEM [98]. To evaluate the quality of the assembled transcriptome, the completeness was assessed with Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) [99] using the eukaryota_odb12 and the alveolata_odb12 datasets.

The isoform with the highest number of annotations and expression levels was selected as the representative for each gene. Genes with counts of 10 or more in at least 3 samples were analyzed using DESeq2 [100]. After low counts were filtered out, 30,595 genes were retained. Each dinospore stage was then compared against the ameboid trophont stage for differential expression analysis based on this final subset. Genes were considered as differentially expressed when they had a false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted P < 0.05 and a log2 fold change > 1.5 after shrinkage of effect size using the “ashr” estimator [101].

Functional annotation and enrichment analysis

All genes were used as inputs for the prediction of peptide sequences for coding regions using Transdecoder and annotation using Trinotate v. 4.0.2 [102]. The best BLAST hits from searches against the Swiss-Prot (UniProtKB) were retained using an e-value cutoff of 1e−5. Protein families were annotated using Pfam [103], and pathway annotations were compared with Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [104], gene ontology [105], and eggNOG databases [106]. For gene ontology (GO) term assignment, peptide sequences were searched against the NCBI nonredundant protein sequence (Nr) database and annotated using Blast2GO [107], with an e-value cutoff of 1e−6. Significantly overrepresented GO terms were identified using a hypergeometric test to calculate p-values with all genes as background, using clusterProfiler v. 4.12.6 [108]. GO terms were considered significant with an adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 after a Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction for FDR. Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis were performed in R v4.4.0 [109].

Sequences of interest

To investigate whether certain proteins of interest would be found in the transcriptome, a curated list of protein sequences relating to plastid loss [36, 38], host interactions in apicomplexan parasites, oocyst wall formation in Toxoplasma gondii [39], Hematodinium dinoflagellate/viral nucleoproteins (DVNP) and histones [35], and meiosis [110, 111] wwere downloaded from the GenBank, UniProtKB and VEuPathDB databases and used as BLASTp queries in the H. perezi transcriptome. Positive hits with query coverage greater than or equal to 30% were retained and their identities were validated by reverse BLASTp searches in the Nr database and Pfam domains.
