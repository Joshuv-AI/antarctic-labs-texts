Adrenaline restores Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms dampened by SARS-CoV-2 infection in Cthrc1-positive pulmonary fibroblasts derived from the Nile grass rat
Authors: Kyte Suga, Hayato Koizumi, Yuki Asano, Shihori Hamasaki, Miku Igarashi, Sakura Tamogami, Suresh Awale, Hiroshi Yamada, Eri Morioka, Tomoko Yoshikawa, Takatoshi Mochizuki, Yoshitomo Morinaga, Masayuki Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disturbances in sleep and circadian rhythms are observed as sequelae of COVID-19 syndrome. However, the pathogenetic mechanisms remain largely unknown. Here, we established a fibroblast cell line (GrLBmal1-luc) from the lungs of diurnal Nile grass rats stably expressing the Bmal1-luciferase reporter gene to investigate peripheral clock regulation. First, endogenous receptor expression was screened with quantitative RT-PCR, immunohistochemistry, Ca2+ imaging, and Bmal1-luciferase reporter in GrLBmal1-luc cells. Through these, the present study discovered phase-shifting effects of adrenaline via β2-adrenoceptor. Interestingly, GrLBmal1-luc cells expressed collagen triple helix repeat containing-1 (Cthrc1) and exhibited a morphology typical of fibroblasts that accumulate in the fibrotic lungs of severe COVID-19 patients. Therefore, human angiotensin-converting enzyme-2 (ACE2) receptors were overexpressed in GrLBmal1-luc cells, and the effects of SARS-CoV-2 (Omicron, XBB.1) infection were analyzed. Following SARS-CoV-2 infection, Bmal1 transcriptions were significantly reduced, while IL-6 and Ciart transcriptions were upregulated. Rev-erbα and Per1 transcriptions were not substantially impacted. The flattened Bmal1-luciferase rhythms gradually recovered 4 days after infection, and adrenaline stimulation amplified and recovered the rhythms faster compared with unstimulated controls. These results provide insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying circadian rhythm disturbances during SARS-CoV-2 infection, and they suggest that adrenergic agents may have therapeutic potential in COVID-19 patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46578-8.

Introduction

Daily physiological activities such as sleep–wake cycles and body temperature rhythms are regulated by the circadian clock1. The core oscillatory mechanisms involve transcriptional–translational feedback loops (TTFLs) among clock genes, such as Period (Per)1/2, Bmal1, Clock and Cryptochrome (Cry) in mammals1. Although the core circadian pacemaker is located within the hypothalamic suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in mammals, clock gene TTFLs are also found in peripheral tissues, such as kidney, liver and lung2,3. Furthermore, under certain conditions, transcriptional rhythms in clock genes have been observed in fibroblasts, including the NIH3T3 and Rat-1 cell lines4,5. Interestingly, human fibroblasts exhibit circadian rhythmicity in transcriptional kinetics that correlate with the chronotype of human behaviors6–8. Moreover, fibroblasts from patients with bipolar disorders display perturbations in the transcriptional rhythms of Bmal1 and the related transcription factors Rev-erbα and Dbp, which may underlie the circadian abnormalities in these patients9,10.

Although fibroblast culture models have been used to study the molecular kinetics of the human circadian clock, comparative models for laboratory animals are still lacking. Generally, activity rhythms of peripheral tissues are coupled to whole-body activity–rest cycles, meaning that the peripheral clock oscillations are antiphase between diurnal and nocturnal species11–13. Because the NIH3T3 and Rat-1 fibroblast cell lines are derived from nocturnal mice and rats4,5, their phase regulation by temporal cues differ from those in human tissues and cells. In comparison, the Nile grass rat (Arvicanthis niloticus) is an attractive animal model because its behavioral rhythms are primarily diurnal, as in humans14,15. Therefore, in the present study, we generated a monoclonal fibroblast cell line (GrLBmal1-luc) from the lung of Nile grass rats and analyzed Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms using the Bmal1-luciferase reporter.

The molecular clock is involved in important physiological responses in the lung, including inflammatory16–18 and antioxidant responses19. SARS-CoV-2 is a highly transmissible coronavirus that is the cause of the COVID-19 pandemic. Chronic fatigue syndrome, including sleep and circadian rhythm disturbances, is a typical sequela of COVID-1920,21. Although redox imbalance may contribute to chronic fatigue syndrome in COVID-19 patients22, the mechanisms are not fully understood. Virus-infected cells and tissues may be suitable for studying the effects of COVID-19 on circadian clocks; however, dying cells and tissues cannot be used for circadian rhythm experiments. The GrLBmal1-luc cell line endogenously expresses Cthrc1 and phenotypically resembles fibroblasts that amass in the fibrotic lung of severe COVID-19 patients23–25. In the present study, we overexpressed human ACE2 receptors in GrLBmal1-luc cells to investigate the effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection on gene transcriptional oscillations.

Results

Generation and characterization of a pulmonary fibroblast cell line from the Nile grass rat

Fibroblasts were cultured from the lungs of Nile grass rats, transfected with the Bmal1-luciferase reporter gene, and subcloned using antibiotics. A monoclonal cell line stably expressing the Bmal1-luciferase reporter was established by screening for chemiluminescence intensities. The GrLBmal1-luc cell line displayed uniformed shape compared with skin fibroblasts of early passage and a sinusoidal luciferase signal across 2–3 circadian cycles following 1 µM dexamethasone (Dex) treatment (Fig. 1A, Suppl Fig. 1). In addition, GrLBmal1-luc cells displayed strong Cthrc1 expression compared with early passage fibroblasts (Fig. 1B). Endogenous gene expression of G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) was assessed using quantitative RT-PCR, which indicated that GrLBmal1-luc cells expressed various adrenaline receptor subtypes (α1a, α1b, α2a and β2) and a serotonin receptor subtype (5-HT2A) (Fig. 1C, D). Consistently, the protein expression of β2-adrenoceptor was visualized in GrLBmal1-luc cells as punctate signals following immunofluorescent staining, which was consistent with the expression patterns of peri-bronchus pulmonary cells in Nile grass rats (Fig. 1E).

Functional expression of GPCRs was also screened using Ca2+ imaging by which Ca2+ responses were caused by serotonin (61 out of 79 cells in 3 dishes) and adrenaline (76 out of 79 cells in 3 dishes) but not by dopamine, melatonin, acetylcholine or histamine even at high concentrations (100 µM, 1 min pulse) (Fig. 2A, B). Either serotonin (EC50 = 1.9 µM; Fig. 2C) or adrenaline (EC50 = 1.6 µM; Fig. 2D) evoked concentration-dependent Ca2+ transients within a physiological concentration range. Nevertheless, adrenaline, but not serotonin, increased the nuclear localization of phosphorylated cAMP response element binding protein (pCREB) in GrLBmal1-luc cells (Fig. 2E). To further characterize the adrenaline receptor subtypes involved in the Ca2+ response, we examined the effects of α- adrenoceptor antagonist (phentolamine) and β-adrenoceptor antagonist (propranolol) on adrenaline-induced Ca2+ responses. The results demonstrated that pretreatment of phentolamine (10 µM) completely blocked adrenaline (10 µM, 1 min pulse)-induced Ca2+ transients (61 out of 61 cells in 3 dishes; Fig. 2F), whereas propranolol (10 µM) failed to block the Ca2+ responses (36 out of 36 cells in 3 dishes; Fig. 2G), suggesting that the α-adrenoceptor is involved in adrenaline-induced Ca2+ transients.

Adrenaline-induced phase-shifts in Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms in GrLBmal1-luc cells

Analysis of Bmal1 expression in acutely isolated lung tissues from nocturnal C57BL6/J mice kept under a 12:12-h light dark cycle demonstrated circadian elevations in transcript levels, with higher signals from late night to early day. (Fig. 3A). The corresponding rhythmic pattern was apparently phase-advanced in Nile grass rats (Fig. 3A), although precise phase-gap estimation was difficult because of the waveforms and limited number of samplings across the circadian cycle. We estimated the peak of Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms in the lung of Nile grass rats using a curve-fitting approach, which identified a plateau at zeitgeber time 17.2.

Here, we defined circadian time (CT) 17 as the peak time of Bmal1-luciferase rhythms in GrLBmal1-luc cells, which were consistent with lung tissue rhythms. Bath application of adrenaline (1 µM) at early subjective night (CT13) delayed- (Fig. 3B) and that at late subjective night (CT23) advanced the successive peak in Bmal1-luciferase rhythms (Fig. 3C). The phase-shifting effects of adrenaline were concentration-dependent (Fig. 3D), and they could be controlled by dual kinetics at low (EC50 = 2.7 nM) and high concentrations (EC50 = 333 nM) (Suppl Fig. 2). To further characterize the adrenaline receptor subtypes involved in transcriptional regulations, we examined the effects of α- and β-adrenoceptor antagonists as above for Ca2+ imaging assay. The results demonstrated that propranolol (10 µM), but not phentolamine (10 µM), blocked adrenaline (1 µM)-induced phase-delays at CT13, suggesting that the β-adrenoceptor primarily regulates Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms (Fig. 3E, F). To address the downstream signaling cascades, the present study also analyzed effects of inhibitors for Ca2+ calmodulin-dependent protein kinases (CaMK), KN-93 (10 µM) and cAMP-dependent protein kinase (PKA), H89 (10 µM) on the adrenaline (1 µM)-induced phase-delays (Fig. 3G). The results demonstrated that partial inhibition of adrenaline-induced phase-delays by KN-93 (-37%) and the larger inhibition by H89 (-76% of control) (Fig. 3H), consistent with the primarily action of Gs-coupling β-adrenoceptors.

Despite the Ca2+ mobilization following serotonin stimulation in a majority of cells, bath application of serotonin (100 µM) failed to induce phase-shifts in Bmal1-luciferase rhythms (Suppl. Figure 3), consistent with the lack of serotonin action for nuclear pCREB (Fig. 2E).

Human ACE2 receptor overexpression and SARS-CoV-2 infection in GrLBmal1-luc cells

Infection by SARS-CoV-2 depends on the binding of viral spike proteins to ACE2 receptors on the host plasma membrane. Therefore, we compared the amino acid sequences of the SARS-CoV-2 binding domain of ACE2 receptors across various mammalian species (Fig. 4A). The sequence in Nile grass rats showed higher homology to the corresponding sequence in humans (10 mutations), compared with rats (12 mutations) or mice (11 mutations), although it had the greatest homology to the sequence in the hamsters (4 mutations), which have been used to model SARS-CoV-2 transmission. Therefore, we overexpressed human ACE2 receptors (hACE2) in GrLBmal1-luc cells. Stable hACE2 expression was verified by the fluorescent protein tag encoded in the expression vector (Suppl Fig. 1D). Immunostaining for SARS-CoV-2 confirmed infection in hACE2(+) GrLBmal1-luc cells and maximal protein synthesis approximately 2 days after infection (Fig. 4B). Viral infection was dependent on hACE2, because the parental GrLBmal1-luc cell line failed to show immunostaining for the virus (Fig. 4B). SARS-CoV-2 immunoreactivity gradually diminished by day 3 after infection; however, the virus was still detectable by PCR on the fifth day of infection (Fig. 4C). The inflammatory factor interleukin-6 (IL-6) was upregulated on the fifth day of infection (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, expression of Bmal1 was significantly reduced (-51.6% of mock control) whereas circadian-associated repressor of transcription (Ciart)26 was upregulated (+ 40.2% of mock control) on the fifth day of infection (Fig. 4C). The Cry1 tended to be reduced but the differences were not statistically significant, and Rev-erbα and Per1 were unaffected by SARS-CoV-2 infection (Fig. 4C).

Next, we analyzed the effect of SARS-CoV-2 infection on Bmal1-luciferase rhythms (Fig. 5). In this experiment, cells were exposed to SARS-CoV-2 24 h prior to Dex treatment (Fig. 5A), and Bmal1-luciferase signals were compared with uninfected controls (i.e., mock treatment controls). Interestingly, circadian oscillations in the Bmal1-luciferase signal were almost fully suppressed following SARS-CoV-2 exposure (Fig. 5A, left), but the rhythms recovered by the fourth day (i.e., later than 72 h) of infection. The amplitude of the oscillations on the third day of infection was reduced by more than 80% (Fig. 5A, right), and therefore, the peak and period estimations of this circadian cycle were not applicable. To address the effect of SARS-CoV-2 infection on adrenaline-induced phase-shifts, we first examined the effects of adrenaline (1 µM) stimulation at 7 different time points after Dex treatment in mock controls (Fig. 5B, C). Through the repeated analysis using hACE2(-) cells (42 dishes) and hACE2(+) cells (42 dishes), the phase-response-curve (PRC) was characterized in GrLBmal1-luc cells. The size and direction of adrenaline-induced phase-shifts fit a typical type-0 PRC, independent of hACE2 receptor expression (Fig. 5D). Subsequently, the effects of SARS-CoV-2 on adrenaline (1 µM)-induced phase-shifts were analyzed on the third day of infection (Fig. 5E). The circadian amplitude of the adrenaline-restored Bmal1-luciferase oscillations tended to be smaller in infected cells whereas the recovered peak phase was clearly dependent on the time of adrenaline stimulation (Fig. 5F, G). Notably, the recovered peak phase was linearly dependent on the time of adrenaline stimulation with a gap of 14 ~ 16 h (Fig. 5G).

Discussion

More than one hundred studies have used the Nile grass rat to investigate diverse topics, including mechanisms linking diurnal behavior to human diseases; however, no model cell line has previously been established from this species. In the present study, we generated a pulmonary cell line, GrLBmal1-luc, characterized the function of endogenous GPCRs, and analyzed the kinetics of clock gene transcriptional oscillations. Among the multiple adrenoceptor subtypes expressed in these cells, the Gs-coupled β2-adrenoceptor proved essential for circadian phase regulation. β2-Adrenoceptor expression is well documented in various pulmonary cell types, and β2 agonists are widely used as bronchodilators27. In addition, inhaled β2 agonists have been proposed as a prophylactic strategy to prevent the development of acute respiratory distress syndrome28, a known complication of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Although the binding properties of grass rat adrenoceptors have not yet been characterized, adrenaline shifted Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms within the nanomolar range in GrLBmal1-luc cells, consistent with observations in human HEK293 cells responding to β2 agonists such as formoterol (EC50 = 1 nM), isoprenaline (EC50 = 10 nM), and salbutamol (EC50 ≈ 200 nM)29. Adrenergic stimulation produced either phase delays or advances in Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms depending on the timing of stimulation, following a type-0 PRC similar to that observed in humans in response to light stimulations30. These properties suggest that this cell line may serve as a useful model for studying human circadian regulation. Furthermore, our results demonstrated that SARS‑CoV‑2 infection markedly dampened Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms, and that these oscillations could be restored by adrenaline treatment. These findings suggest that disruptions in clock gene transcription may contribute to the malaise experienced following SARS‑CoV‑2 infection, and that appropriately timed adrenergic stimulation may help promote recovery.

Adrenergic regulation of peripheral circadian clocks

The adrenergic system is a neuro-hormonal system that regulates various physiological functions via the catecholamines adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine). Catecholamine production is regulated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis via adrenocorticotropic hormone, which stimulates the adrenal cortex to produce glucocorticoids (i.e., cortisol in humans and corticosterone in rodents). The central circadian pacemaker, consisting of the SCN and the paraventricular nucleus, coordinate daily glucocorticoid rhythms31, and therefore, the HPA axis is strongly influenced by the central circadian clock. Adrenaline and noradrenaline are synthesized in the adrenal medulla and released into the bloodstream to exert numerous effects. Moreover, the sympathetic nervous system predominantly secretes noradrenaline at nerve terminals to exert highly localized actions. Adrenergic receptors are divided into α- and β-adrenergic receptor subtypes, with the former having a higher affinity for noradrenaline, and the latter having a higher affinity for adrenaline32,33.

Adrenergic control of peripheral clocks has been widely studied using transgenic mouse models. For example, both the α-adrenoceptor agonist phenylephrine and the β-adrenoceptor agonist isoproterenol elevate Per1 expression in a concentration-dependent manner in in vitro hepatic slices prepared from Per1-luciferase transgenic mice34. Per1 expression in mouse liver can also be induced by electrical stimulation of the sympathetic nerves in vivo, suggesting noradrenergic control of this process34. Additionally, the peripheral clock in the heart is regulated by β-adrenoceptors, as isoproterenol increases the amplitude of Per2-luciferase rhythms in murine cardiac explants35 and propranolol eliminates the day–night variation in heart rates in mice36,37. Furthermore, involvement of β2-adrenoceptors in the regulation of Per1 expression in the heart has been demonstrated in vivo using knockout mice lacking these receptors36. Recently, it has also reported that selective deletion of β2-adrenoceptors in CD8+ T cells perturbed Bmal1 and Per2 transcriptional oscillations in these cells, and suggested critical function of β2-adrenoceptors for time-dependent immune responses against virus infections38. However, the involvement of β2‑adrenoceptors in regulating clock gene transcription in pulmonary cells has not yet been investigated.

In the present study, we focused on Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms in a monoclonal cell line, and therefore, our findings might not be applicable to all pulmonary cell types. While we did not fully uncover the role of adrenergic pathways in the regulation of circadian clocks, our current findings indicate a critical function of β2-adrenoceptors in the phase-regulation of Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms. Therefore, together with the evidence from cardiac cells or CD8+ T cells, our findings suggest a key functional involvement of β2-adrenoceptors in the regulation of the peripheral circadian clock. However, it should be noted that peripheral clock oscillations in the heart, liver and white adipose tissue are preserved in mice lacking dopamine beta-hydroxylase, which does not synthesize adrenaline or noradrenaline39. Thus, peripheral clock regulation is mediated by various other pathways in addition to the adrenergic system.

Circulating catecholamine levels in humans are lower during the night and higher during the day40–42, whereas nocturnal rodents exhibit converse circadian variations43. Catecholamine variations in diurnal Nile grass rats remain to be investigated, although they should be coordinated with activity–rest cycles, and thus may oscillate in phase with other diurnal species, such as humans. Notably, the present results suggest nearly reversed-phase Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms in lung tissues between nocturnal mice and diurnal Nile grass rats. In addition, the PRC for adrenaline in GrLBmal1-luc cells obeys the type-0 pattern, which is observed for core body temperature rhythm changes induced by repeated photic stimulation in humans30. Therefore, the GrLBmal1-luc cell line could serve as a useful model for investigating how peripheral circadian oscillators are temporally coordinated by the adrenergic system in humans.

cAMP, but not Ca2+ signaling, is essential for resetting pulmonary fibroblasts

Balsalobre et al. (2000) demonstrated equivalent actions of cAMP and Ca2+ in resetting transcriptional rhythms in Rat-1 fibroblasts44. Their findings are reasonable, because CREB phosphorylation is regulated by PKA45 as well as CaMK46. Ca2+ signaling is particularly important for the regulation of the central circadian clock, as SCN neurons receive photic information via glutamatergic retino-hypothalamic tracts, and post-synaptic Ca2+ influx via ionotropic glutamate receptors is the initial intracellular trigger for resetting transcriptional rhythms during nighttime47,48. Moreover, Ca2+ elevations via GPCRs reset Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms, as we previously reported phase-shifts induced by stimulation of Gq-coupled histamine 1 receptors or muscarinic acetylcholine 3 receptors in human retinal pigment epithelial cells49,50. Therefore, it was surprising that we failed to observe serotonin-induced pCREB elevations (Fig. 2) and circadian phase-shifts in GrLBmal1-luc cells (Suppl Fig. 3). We speculate that the magnitude of intracellular Ca2+ mobilizations might be insufficient to activate downstream signaling in this cell line. In addition, we observed only negligible transcript levels of CaMK2 family members in GrLBmal1-luc cells (Suppl Fig. 4). Therefore, Ca2+-dependent CREB phosphorylation could not be efficiently induced, even though a Ca2+ signal was detected. In the present study, we observed that KN-93 partially reduced adrenaline-induced phase delays, suggesting that CaMK4 (Suppl. Figure 4) may contribute to downstream signaling following adrenoceptor stimulation. However, the finding that phentolamine failed to attenuate these phase delays, whereas H89 markedly suppressed them, indicates that the Gs–cAMP–PKA pathway is the predominant mechanism mediating adrenaline-induced circadian phase shifts in GrLBmal1-luc cells. Balsalobre et al. (2000) used forskolin and a Ca2+ ionophore, calcimycin, to study the effects of cAMP and Ca2+ in Rat-1 fibroblasts44. Because the Ca2+ ionophore causes a long-lasting increase in cytosolic Ca2+, it might have a larger impact on downstream signaling processes compared with physiological stimulation. We propose that the intracellular signaling pathways affected by stimulation may vary according to cell type, especially among non-excitable peripheral cells lacking voltage-dependent ion channels. For example, noradrenaline has been reported to control clock gene expression through β-adrenoceptors via the PKA–CREB or Src tyrosine kinase–GSK-3β pathway in rat C6 glioma cells45. Therefore, a similar mechanism may underlie the circadian phase-shifts in GrLBmal1-luc cells.

Effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection on Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms

The molecular clock regulates time-dependent immune responses in various tissues. For example, a major involvement of a core clock component, Rev-erbα, has been demonstrated in the lung16–18,51. Rev-erbα regulates the time-dependent response to endotoxin in mouse myeloid and bronchial epithelial cells17. Furthermore, Rev-erbα inhibits pulmonary fibrosis development by affecting myofibroblast differentiation52, and it regulates lung fibrotic progression via collagen stabilization in a temporally dependent manner following influenza A virus infection in humans51. The temporal regulation of inflammatory responses can be observed in numerous cell types. For example, temporal regulation of viral transmission has been demonstrated in NIH3T3 fibroblasts following herpes and influenza virus infection, where viral transmission peaks during the plateau phase of Bmal1-luciferase rhythms53. Interestingly, a recent study using the Calu-3 lung cancer cell line suggests that knockdown of Bmal1 inhibits SARS-CoV-2 infection54. Moreover, it has been shown that Ciart, which was originally termed Chrono, is a regulator of a circadian feedback loop26 that plays a critical role in SARS-CoV-2 infection55.

Despite accumulating evidence of the involvement of clock genes and related transcription factors in the regulation of inflammatory responses, our understanding of the effects of virus infection on the circadian clock is still limited. However, it has been shown that the inflammatory response feedbacks onto clock gene TTFLs, because herpes virus upregulates Bmal1-luciferase and downregulates Cry1-luciferase in NIH3T3 fibroblasts53. Like other Cthrc1-positive pulmonary fibroblasts23–25, GrLBmal1-luc cells were resistant to SARS-CoV-2 infection, and therefore, we analyzed the effect of SARS-CoV-2 infection on Bmal1-luciferase rhythms in hACE2(+) GrLBmal1-luc cells. The results demonstrate temporal cancellation of Bmal1-luciferase rhythms in these cells following viral infection. Notably, consistent with immunostaining intensities of SARS-CoV-2, this striking effect of SARS-CoV-2 gradually disappeared and intrinsic Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms recovered by the fourth day of infection. Although the underlying mechanisms need to be further clarified, the upregulation of Ciart (Fig. 4C) may perturb repressor pathways among clock gene TTFLs. In comparison, Rev-erbα was relatively stable following SARS-CoV-2 infection in hACE2(+) GrLBmal1-luc cells, and thus, specific pathways may provide feedback onto clock gene TTFLs. In the current study, we could examine Ciart transcript levels only once, on the fifth day of infection, because of facility regulations. Continuous monitoring (e.g., with multi-color luciferase reporter assays) may help elucidate the impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection on clock gene networks.

Notably, adrenaline stimulation on the third day of SARS-CoV-2 infection, when Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms were almost fully suppressed, successfully restored the circadian rhythmicity. The amplitude of the recovered Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms was larger than that of the intrinsic rhythm on the fourth day of infection. Moreover, the recovered peak emerged after an almost constant latency (ca. 14–16 h after adrenaline stimulation). Thus, modulation of the recovery phase might be possible by controlling the timing of adrenaline stimulation. For example, adrenaline stimulation in the early morning might trigger the nighttime rise in Bmal1 expression that is necessary for the synchronization of peripheral clocks in diurnal species.

Growing evidence indicates that the circadian system comprises multiple oscillators coordinated by the central SCN pacemaker56. Our findings suggest that adrenaline may serve as a synchronizing signal for pulmonary oscillators. We also observed that SARS‑CoV‑2 infection markedly dampened Bmal1‑luciferase rhythms, implying that attenuation or uncoupling of peripheral oscillators may contribute to the malaise associated with SARS‑CoV‑2 infection. These results provide insight into the molecular basis of sleep and circadian disturbances following infection and raise the possibility that β‑adrenergic agents could have therapeutic value in COVID‑19.

Methods

Animals

Nile grass rats were housed under a 12:12-h light dark cycle (200–1000 lx during the light period) at a constant temperature (22 ± 1 °C) in an animal facility at the Gofuku campus of the University of Toyama. Adult Nile grass rats were fed standard food pellets (Nosan Co., Ltd., Kanagawa, Japan) and water ad libitum. Details of the breeding procedures were as described previously57. As a control, C57BL6/J mice, housed in a separate room, were used in this experiment. All procedures involving animals were approved by the Committee of Animal Care of the University of Toyama (A2018SCI-2 and A2023OIEE-1).

Plasmid generation of the Bmal1-luciferase reporter

First, the approximate promoter region of Nile grass rat Bmal1 (3,292 bp) from − 2,656 bp to + 636 bp was amplified from genomic DNA using the KOD-FX kit (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) based on reference DNA sequences (GCF_011762505.1). Subsequently, using this as a template, 967 bp upstream of the Bmal1 promoter region (− 897 to + 70 bp) was amplified. This 967-bp fragment was inserted into the Emerald Luc (ELuc)-PEST::pGL3 Basic vector, which was kindly provided by Dr. Yoshihiro Nakajima of the National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology. The cloning was performed using In-Fusion Cloning Kit (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primer sequences used are listed in Suppl Table 1.

Isolation of fibroblasts

Fibroblasts were isolated from the skin or lung tissues of young male Nile grass rats (4–21 days old) according to a standard method58. These fibroblasts were cultured and immortalized by serial passage with Eagle’s Minimal Essential Medium supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) in a reduced oxygen (5% CO2 and 3% O2) incubator kept at constant temperature (37 °C) and saturated humidity. Resultant immortalized cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, sodium bicarbonate (1.2 g/L) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a standard CO2 incubator (5% CO2 and air).

Generation of GrLBmal1-luc clones

The GrLBmal1-luc cells were generated using lung fibroblasts derived from Nile grass rats. The Bmal1-luciferase vector was transfected into lung fibroblasts using Lipofectamine-3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Subsequently, cells were cultured in medium containing 400 µg/mL G418 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for selection. The stably transfected cells were selected in 96-well plates. Of six clones, one clone displaying constant expression of Bmal1-luciferase and clear bioluminescence rhythms was selected.

To analyze the effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection, the human ACE2 expression vector (pCMV-GFP-ACE2; Sino Biological, Beijing, China) was transfected into GrLBmal1-luc cells using Lipofectamine-3000. Subsequently, cells were subcloned in medium containing 200 µg/mL hygromycin B (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Tokyo, Japan) for selection.

Ca2+ imaging

Ca2+ imaging experiments were performed as described previously59. In short, GrLBmal1-luc cells were plated on 35 mm plastic dishes, gently rinsed with buffered salt solution (BSS) consisting of 130 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 5.5 mM D-glucose and 10 mM HEPES/NaOH (pH 7.4), and incubated in BSS supplemented with 3 µM fura-2 AM (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) for 30 min in a CO2 incubator. Prior to photometry, these cells were rinsed again with BSS, and then continuously perfused with BSS at 34 °C at a flow rate of 2 mL/min. During image acquisition, dopamine hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich), serotonin hydrochloride (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co.), adrenaline (Sigma-Aldrich), melatonin (Sigma-Aldrich), acetylcholine chloride (Sigma-Aldrich), histamine dihydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich), phentolamine mesylate (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co.) and propranolol hydrochloride (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) were applied by switching the perfusate.

Immunohistochemistry

Staining of β2-adrenoceptors

The distribution of β2-adrenoceptor was visualized in lung tissues and GrLBmal1-luc cells by immunofluorescence staining using a rabbit monoclonal antibody against β2-adrenaline receptors (Clone EPR707N, Cat# ab182136; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). For tissue staining, Nile grass rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) and transcardially perfused with PBS(-) followed by ice-cold 4% (w/v) phosphate-buffered paraformaldehyde. The fixed lung was trimmed and immersed in 30% sucrose in PBS(-) for cryoprotection. Subsequently, a series of slices (40-µm thick) was cut using a cryostat and blocked with 10% (v/v) donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA) on an orbital shaker for 2 h at room temperature. These samples were reacted with the β2-adrenaline receptor antibody (1:100) dissolved in 5% donkey serum in PBS(-) on an orbital shaker overnight at 4 °C. For staining of GrLBmal1-luc cells, cells cultured on glass coverslips were fixed in 4% phosphate-buffered paraformaldehyde for 10 min and reacted with the β2-adrenaline receptor antibody (1:200) overnight at 4 °C. After three 20-min rinses with PBS(-), tissue and cultured cell samples were reacted with Alexa488-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:400, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). After thoroughly rinsing with PBS(-), slices were embedded with Vectashield with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA). Images were acquired using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (A1RMP plus; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Staining of pCREB

GrLBmal1-luc cells cultured on glass coverslips were stimulated with 100 µM serotonin or adrenaline in culture media for 10 min and fixed in 4% phosphate-buffered paraformaldehyde for 20 min. The fixed samples were rinsed with PBS and then incubated for 2 h at room temperature in 10% donkey serum dissolved in 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS. Subsequently, samples were incubated with 1:100 affinity-purified rabbit anti-p-CREB (pSer133) (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) dissolved in 5% donkey serum in PBS for 24 h at 4 °C. After three 20-min PBS rinses, samples were incubated in 1:400 Alexa488-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, samples were rinsed with PBS and embedded in ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Images were acquired as above using a confocal laser-scanning microscope.

Staining of SARS-CoV-2

For detection of SARS-CoV-2 transmission, GrLBmal1-luc cells cultured in 96-well plates were infected with SARS-CoV-2 (multiplicity of infection (MOI) = 1) and fixed in 4% phosphate-buffered paraformaldehyde for 20 min at 24, 48 and 72 h post infection. The fixed samples were then incubated for 10 min at room temperature in 1% Triton X-100 in PBS. Subsequently, samples were incubated with 1:150 rabbit anti-SARS-CoV nucleoprotein antibody (Sino Biological) dissolved in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. After PBS rinses, samples were incubated in 1:600 Alexa555-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen) for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, samples were rinsed with PBS, and nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (Dojindo Laboratories) for 5 min. Images were acquired with a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

Bmal1-luciferase assay

The GrLBmal1-luc cells were plated on 35 mm plastic dishes 3 days before the luciferase assay. Cells grown to 90% confluency were treated for 1 h with 1 µM Dex, and the culture medium was replaced with standard culture medium containing 100 µM beetle luciferin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) prior to chemiluminescent recordings. In case of kinase inhibitor assays, 10 µM KN-93 phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich) or 10 µM H89 dihydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich) were added in the serum-free luciferin medium and maintained throughout the experiments. The recording chamber of a Kronos-Dio luminometer system (Model AB-2550, ATTO Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was set at 37 °C throughout the experimental period. The duration of photon counting was set at 1 min per sample, with sampling intervals of 10 min.

To analyze phase responses to pharmacological stimulation, chemiluminescent recordings were temporally paused, and the media were collected. Drugs were added to the collected culture media and gently returned to the culture dish. This procedure was completed under dim red light (< 5 lx). Dishes underwent a medium exchange, with no drug treatment used as control. These pharmacological stimulations were performed at the second circadian cycle of Bmal1-luciferase rhythms, because the chemiluminescent signal was strongly influenced by luciferin uptake during the first cycle. Circadian phase-shifts of Bmal1-luciferase rhythms were quantified on the second to third circadian cycle with reference to unstimulated controls using KronoAnalyzer software (ATTO Co. Ltd). To acquire a concentration-dependent curve for adrenaline, 0.03 nM-10 µM adrenaline in culture media was bath-applied at CT5 to estimate the size of phase-delays.

Real-time RT-PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from GrLBmal1-luc cells, skin and lung fibroblasts, or cerebral cortical tissues from Nile grass rats using RNAiso Plus (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was amplified from 1 µg of RNA using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Primers for Cthrc1, Adrα1a, Adrα1b, Adrα1d, Adrα2a, Adrα2b, Adrα2c, Adrβ1, Adrβ2, Adrβ3, Htr1a, Htr1b, Htr1d, Htr1f, Htr2a, Htr2b, Htr2c, Htr7, Bmal1, Per1, Cry1, SARS-CoV-2, IL-6, Rev-erbα, Ciart, CaMK2a, CaMK2b, CaMK2g, CaMK2d, CaMK4, GAPDH and Ppia were designed using Primer-BLAST based on the reference mRNA sequences (Suppl. Table 2). All primers were tested for non-specific amplification and primer-dimer formation using melting-curve analysis and gel electrophoresis. Each primer was used with the Rotor-Gene SYBR Green RT-PCR Master Mix (Qiagen) in the 72-well rotor of the real-time PCR system (Rotor-Gene Q 2plex Platform, Qiagen). The following thermocycling conditions were used: initial PCR activation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 60 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 10 s. mRNA or viral RNA levels were expressed as 2−ΔCt using GAPDH or Ppia mRNA level as internal standard.

To estimate Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms in lung tissues, tissue samples (~ 100 mg) from Nile grass rats or C57BL6/J mice (> 1 month of age) were collected at zeitgeber time 2, 8, 14 and 20. Total RNA was extracted from tissue homogenates on ice using RNAiso Plus. Reverse transcription and real-time RT-PCR were performed as above for GrLBmal1-luc cells.

SARS-CoV-2 infection

SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variant (lineage XBB.1) obtained from the National Institute of Infectious Diseases, Japan, was propagated and the titer measured in VeroE6 cells with plaque assays. hACE2(+) GrLBmal1-luc cells were used for Bmal1-luciferase assay. Before chemiluminescence recordings, SARS-CoV-2 was added into the dishes at a MOI of 1. All experiments with the virus were performed in a biosafety level 3 laboratory in the Sugitani campus of the University of Toyama. The circadian period and amplitude of Bmal1-luciferase rhythms were quantified with reference to mock infection controls using the KronoAnalyzer.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as means with standard error (SEM). Magnitudes of Ca2+ increases were quantified by peak area analysis using Origin software (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). A four-parameter Hill function was used to estimate the concentration-response curve. Pairwise comparisons were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t-test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey-Kramer test or Dunnett’s test were used for statistical comparisons across multiple means of single factor. In addition, multi-way ANOVA with the Bonferroni correction was used to compare time-dependent effects of SARS-CoV-2 infections on circadian amplitudes or phases of Bmal1-luciferase rhythms. A 95% confidence level was considered to indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using BellCurve software (Social Survey Research Information Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). To estimate the peak phase of Bmal1 transcriptional rhythms in lung tissues, mathematical sine curve fitting was conducted using CircWave version 1.4 software (R.A.Hut, Groningen, NL, USA).
