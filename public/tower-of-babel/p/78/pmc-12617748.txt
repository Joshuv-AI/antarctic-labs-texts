A case report of jejunal myeloid sarcoma presented with intestinal intussusception in a 19-year-old man: An uncommon presentation of a rare disease
Authors: Mojtaba Ahmadinejad, Alireza Shakerpour, Pegah Azarimanesh, Babak Mansourian, Yasmina Ahmadinejad, Javad Zebarjadi Bagherpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIntussusception is a rare condition in adults, with an adult-to-pediatric incidence ratio of approximately 1:20. Unlike pediatric cases, adult intussusception is almost always secondary to an underlying pathological lesion. Myeloid sarcoma is an uncommon extramedullary manifestation of acute myeloid leukemia and represents a rare cause of gastrointestinal involvement.Case presentationWe report the case of a 19-year-old male who presented to the emergency department with acute abdominal pain and signs of intestinal obstruction. Abdominal computed tomography revealed jejunal intussusception. An exploratory laparotomy was performed, during which a necrotic lesion was identified on the jejunal wall, along with three additional lesions on the intestinal wall. A frozen section biopsy suggested differential diagnoses, including gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) and intestinal lymphoma. The lesion causing the intussusception was resected with clear margins, while the remaining lesions were left in situ based on intraoperative pathology. Postoperative histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of jejunal myeloid sarcoma. The patient was subsequently re-admitted for resection and re-anastomosis of the remaining lesions and was referred to the oncology department.Clinical discussionAdult intussusception is often misdiagnosed due to its rarity and nonspecific presentation. In most cases, it is caused by an underlying lesion, which necessitates thorough intraoperative evaluation and appropriate surgical management. Myeloid sarcoma involving the gastrointestinal tract is exceptionally rare and may mimic other neoplastic conditions.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of considering rare hematologic malignancies such as myeloid sarcoma in young adults presenting with intussusception.

Introduction

The small intestine is an uncommon site for gastrointestinal (GI) tumors; only 2 to 3 % of tumors arise from the small intestine. While the small intestine is more commonly affected by carcinomas, small bowel sarcomas account for less than 10 % of all small intestine malignancies [1]. Small bowel sarcomas can arise from any of the small bowel sections, with the jejunum being the most frequently involved site [1]. Small bowel sarcoma can present with a range of nonspecific symptoms, including abdominal pain, weight loss, and gastrointestinal bleeding, which can complicate the diagnostic process [2,3]. Also, it can cause small bowel obstruction, and patients may require exploratory laparotomy in such cases [4]. In the majority of nonleukemic patients with myeloid sarcoma, the condition progressed to acute myeloid leukemia (AML) within a matter of months [5]. The median time from diagnosis to AML was 10 months, while the median survival time was 22 months [5,6]. Patients who underwent induction chemotherapy that included cytosine arabinoside appeared to have a significantly lower likelihood of developing acute leukemia and experienced longer survival rates [2,[5], [6], [7]].

Advanced imaging techniques, like computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are essential for diagnosing and staging these tumors. However, despite advancements in imaging, small bowel malignancies are more frequently diagnosed through endoscopy. Approximately 25 % of patients receive their initial diagnosis during surgery [8]. The treatment of small bowel sarcoma typically involves a multimodal approach, including surgical resection, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy. Surgical intervention remains the cornerstone of treatment, particularly for localized disease. However, due to the aggressive nature of these tumors and their tendency to metastasize, adjuvant therapies are often required to improve outcomes in some cases [1]. The present study introduces an infrequent presentation of an infrequent disease, small intestinal intussusception, due to jejunal sarcoma in a 19-year-old patient.

Case presentation

A 19-year-old male presented with a chief complaint of abdominal pain localized to the peri-umbilical and hypogastric regions. The patient reported that his last defecation was 15 h before the presentation, with no notable bleeding or melena. The patient was previously in good health, with no significant past medical history. His family history was unremarkable, with no known malignancies or congenital disorders. His vital signs were stable upon arrival. The abdomen was soft and non-tender on physical examination, with no rebound tenderness. Transrectal examination revealed an empty rectum. Laboratory tests indicated leukocytosis and metabolic alkalosis. Given the clinical presentation and laboratory findings, a contrast-enhanced abdominal and pelvic computed tomography (CT) scan was performed, which demonstrated the presence of the “target sign,” indicative of intussusception, in the jejunal loops located in the left lower quadrant (LLQ) (Fig. 1.A). The imaging further revealed a high-grade small bowel obstruction with notable gastric distention and dilation of small bowel loops proximal to the intussusception (Fig. 1.B).Fig. 1Pre-operative computed tomography. During the patient's hospitalization, his vital signs deteriorated, prompting the decision to perform an emergent exploratory laparotomy. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia by a senior surgical resident under the direct supervision of an attending general surgeon. Perioperative prophylactic antibiotics included intravenous cefazolin (2 g) and metronidazole (500 mg), administered prior to skin incision. The peritoneal cavity was clear intraoperatively without evidence of purulent discharge or peritonitis. Upon further inspection, evidence of jejunal intussusception was confirmed, and a gentle manual reduction was successfully performed. Post-reduction, a thorough examination of the bowel was conducted. Approximately 30 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz, a 50 × 30 mm mass with surrounding necrotization was identified on the jejunal wall. Although there was no evidence of perforation, three additional masses were found: 40 cm from the first lesion, 50 cm from the second, and 20 cm from the third. These lesions were smaller and did not result in obstruction or perforation, remaining intraluminal (Fig. 2). A frozen section biopsy of the largest mass was obtained and sent for pathological examination, which suggested lymphoproliferative disorder. In light of the pathological findings, a decision was made to perform a limited resection of the first mass followed by anastomosis. So, about 15 cm of jejunum was resected and re-anastomosed. The remaining lesions were left intact for further evaluation. Postoperatively, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close monitoring. Management included routine observation, early mobilization, and gradual reintroduction of oral intake. No immediate postoperative complications were observed.Fig. 2Exploratory laparotomy. Post-operative histopathological and immunohistochemistry (IHC) examination diagnosed myeloid sarcoma, which was positive for BCL-2, CD45, and CD86. Also, the specimen was scattered positive for CD 15. Furthermore, the Ki67 proliferative activity index was reported to be up to 80 % (Fig. 3). EBV PCR was negative. The surgical margins were free of tumor, and 1 out of 10 lymph nodes showed tumor involvement. According to the post-operative results of the IHC examination, the patient was re-admitted and planned for resection of the remaining 3 lesions. Under general anesthesia and via laparotomy incision, the remaining lesions were located, and the resection and re-anastomosis were performed. The second surgical procedure was also performed by the same surgical team that conducted the initial operation. The patient's postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged with stable vital signs, peroral tolerance, and normal defecation. All three lesions demonstrated similar histopathological features, confirming the diagnosis of myeloid sarcoma. Lymph node involvement was identified in all three specimens, with a total of 4 out of 25 lymph nodes affected. So, he was referred to the oncology department for possible adjuvant chemotherapy. The patient underwent adjuvant chemotherapy, and after 6 months of follow-up, there was no evidence of progression to AML. Discussion

Intussusception is a condition where a segment of the intestine telescopes into an adjacent segment, leading to obstruction and potential ischemia. While it is more commonly seen in pediatric populations, it can occur in adults, often associated with underlying pathology such as tumors [10,11]. In contrast with pediatrics, intussusception in adult populations usually occurs due to the existence of a lesion. Also, about 30 % of these lesions have a malignant nature, necessitating accurate investigations [12]. This case illustrates a 19-year-old male presenting with abdominal pain and subsequently diagnosed with jejunal intussusception secondary to myeloid sarcoma.

Myeloid sarcoma can present as an obstruction in the gastrointestinal tract, mimicking other more common causes of intussusception [2]. However, patients can present with other symptoms, including non-specific abdominal pain and bleeding [2,3]. This condition can be associated with initial presentation or relapse of AML, blast crisis within a myeloproliferative disorder, or a leukemic transformation in myelodysplastic syndrome. Less commonly, it can appear as an isolated mass in nonleukemic patients, and the majority of untreated patients progress to AML within less than one year [13]. The mean age for small bowel myeloid sarcoma diagnosis is about 60 years, and it has a slight male predominance [1]. There are some controversies in the literature about the most common site of small bowel sarcoma. Some studies suggest the ilium, and another suggests the jejunum as the most commonly involved site in the small intestine [1,2]. The overall 5-year disease-specific survival is about 40 %, with a median time to death or follow-up of 22.5 months. Age over 60 years old, male gender, tumor size, and nodal involvement can significantly affect a patient's prognosis and survival; however, the tumor site, including dedendum, ileum, and jejunum, does not correlate with patients' survival [1].

Mizumoto et al. reported a rare case of isolated myeloid sarcoma of the small bowel in a 54-year-old man who presented with recurrent symptoms of intestinal obstruction [14]. Similar to our case, the diagnosis was challenging due to the nonspecific clinical presentation and the rarity of gastrointestinal involvement by myeloid sarcoma. Their patient underwent laparoscopy-assisted jejunal resection, and histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis. While their case presented with obstruction, our patient uniquely manifested with intussusception, further expanding the spectrum of clinical presentations associated with this rare entity.

Nair et al. reported a case of jejunal sarcomatoid carcinoma presenting with jejuno-jejunal intussusception and small bowel obstruction in a patient with a prior history of tongue carcinoma [15]. Pleural and bony metastases complicated their case, and the patient unfortunately died five weeks postoperatively. While the underlying pathology differed from our case, both reports emphasize the importance of considering rare malignancies as lead points in adult intussusception and the critical role of histopathology in guiding diagnosis and management.

Wong et al. reported a case of metastatic spindle cell sarcoma presenting with jejunal intussusception and small bowel obstruction in a patient with prior scalp sarcoma [16]. Their patient underwent successful resection of two intussuscepted segments and was later diagnosed with high-grade recurrent metastatic disease. While the underlying malignancy differed from our case, both reports illustrate how rare sarcomas can present as lead points for intussusception and reinforce the importance of thorough oncologic evaluation and surgical management in such scenarios.

This case highlights the diagnostic challenge posed by rare hematologic malignancies in young patients presenting with atypical gastrointestinal symptoms such as intussusception. Myeloid sarcoma, particularly in the absence of overt leukemia, can closely mimic other intra-abdominal pathologies, making intraoperative decision-making complex. Timely surgical intervention, supported by thorough histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis, is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate oncologic referral. Multidisciplinary coordination and vigilant long-term follow-up are crucial to detect potential progression to acute myeloid leukemia and to guide future therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion and future directions

This study confirms the need for clinical awareness when evaluating adult intussusception, especially in younger patients without prior hematologic disease. The identification of jejunal myeloid sarcoma in this context emphasizes the value of integrating surgical, pathological, and oncologic expertise. Future research should aim to better characterize the clinical spectrum of gastrointestinal myeloid sarcoma and establish evidence-based protocols for early detection, risk stratification, and long-term surveillance.
