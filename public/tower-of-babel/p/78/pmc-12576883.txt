RNA Granules at the Crossroads of Synaptic Dysfunction and Neurodegeneration
Authors: Rita Nóbrega‐Martins, Beatriz Barros‐Santos, Georgia Papadimitriou, Ioannis Sotiropoulos, Benjamin Wolozin, Joana Margarida Silva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRNA granules are dynamic, membraneless organelles essential for the spatial and temporal regulation of mRNA metabolism, particularly in neurons, where local protein synthesis supports synaptic plasticity and function. This review explores the diverse types of RNA granules (e.g., transport granules, stress granules, and processing bodies), their formation mechanisms, molecular composition, and relevance to synaptic physiology. We focus on the central role of RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs) in orchestrating granule dynamics and their fine‐tuning of synaptic responses under both physiological and stress conditions. Mounting evidence implicates the dysfunction of RNA granules in neurodegenerative diseases. Altered phase separation, RBP aggregation, and persistent stress granules contribute to the formation of pathological RNA granules that interfere with local translation and synaptic maintenance. Key RBPs, including TDP‐43, FUS, and TIA‐1, are frequently misregulated in disease contexts. Furthermore, Tau is a multifunctional protein traditionally associated with microtubule stabilization but is increasingly recognized for its role in the translational stress response, which includes RBP mislocalization and RNA granule disruption. We examine how chronic stress can exacerbate these mechanisms, acting as an environmental trigger of synaptic vulnerability associated with neurodegeneration. In summary, we explore a conceptual framework connecting RNA granule dysregulation, Tau pathology, and local translation disruption, three processes that converge on synaptic impairment, a central feature of many neurodegenerative diseases characterized by abnormal Tau. Investigating this triad presents a promising avenue for understanding disease mechanisms and identifying novel therapeutic targets that aim to restore RNA metabolism, prevent toxic Tau interactions, and preserve synaptic health.

Introduction

RNA granules are dynamic, membraneless condensates composed of messenger RNAs (mRNAs), a variety of RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs), RBP scaffolding proteins, components of translation machinery, and other factors crucial for RNA metabolism. These structures are conserved across species and play crucial roles in RNA homeostasis through the regulation of key processes such as RNA processing, transport, translation, and degradation (Hirose et al. 2023; Putnam et al. 2023). RNA granules allow cells to adapt protein synthesis in response to both internal and external signals and challenges, such as synaptic activity and energy availability (Holt and Bullock 2009; Kiebler and Bassell 2006; Schieweck et al. 2021; Thomas et al. 2011).

RNA granules have emerged as important regulators of brain function. In neurons, these structures are involved in critical processes such as maturation, activity‐dependent remodeling, synaptic plasticity, and localized protein synthesis (Bauer et al. 2023; Mofatteh 2020; Schieweck et al. 2021). Notably, local translation at synapses depends on RNA transport granules to keep mRNAs in a translationally repressed state during transport to distal synaptic compartments. Once in distal neuronal processes, RNA is kept sequestered in transport granules near synaptic boutons and actively transported “on demand” to the synapse, where translation is then selectively activated in response to synaptic activity. This spatial and temporal control of gene expression is essential for local synaptic function, facilitating the regulation of synapse formation and allowing for critical neurophysiological adaptations, such as long‐term potentiation (LTP) of other stable changes that are the biological underpinnings of engrams (Dargan et al. 2024; Fernandopulle et al. 2021; Nagano and Araki 2021).

The biology of RBPs differs greatly between neurons of the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral non‐neuronal cells. RBP location and function are overwhelmingly nuclear in most non‐neuronal peripheral cells, and the function is primarily linked to RNA splicing and transcription (Cassola et al. 2010). In contrast, many of these classically nuclear RBPs are abundant in the soma and processes of neurons, where axonal and dendritic transport are vital for neuronal function (the somatic localization of RBPs is particularly apparent in the hippocampus) (Hoang and Manahan‐Vaughan 2023).

Many types of RNA granules have been described over the past decades and given specific names, including processing bodies (P‐bodies), transport granules, activity‐dependent granules, and stress granules (SGs), among others. These granules exhibit substantial heterogeneity in their protein composition, but rather than being classified by their RNA content, they have been classified based on their protein constituents or function. However, the use of broad names, such as SGs, often masks the heterogeneity of regulation and function within each category. For example, SGs in CNS neurons that contain T‐cell intracellular antigen 1 (TIA‐1), microtubule‐associated protein (Tau), and G3BP2 are distinct from SGs in CNS neurons that contain TDP‐43 and G3BP1. However, the same comparisons examined in peripheral cell lines, such as U2OS, show more cross‐over among constituents (Aulas and Vande Velde 2015; Parker et al. 2025; Vanderweyde et al. 2012). Different types of RNA granules, such as SGs and P‐bodies, frequently act in concert, localize adjacent to each other, exchange components, and share core factors. SGs and P‐bodies are commonly observed in contact (Souquere et al. 2009). TAR DNA‐binding protein of 43 kD (TDP‐43) is known for its nuclear localization, functioning to prevent cryptic splicing under homeostatic conditions (Humphrey et al. 2017; Polymenidou et al. 2011). However, in stress, TDP‐43 partially or fully translocates to the cytoplasm, where it contributes as a secondary component of SG (Liu‐Yesucevitz et al. 2010; Neumann et al. 2006). TDP‐43 is also known to be highly mobile along axons and dendrites, playing a role in mRNA transportation within transport granules along neurons (Alami et al. 2014). TIA‐1 is another good example of an RNA granule‐associated protein that acts in the nucleus mainly as a transcription factor, in the cytoplasm as a core nucleating protein for SGs, and also contributes to activity‐dependent granules and transport granules (Anderson and Kedersha 2008; Ash et al. 2021; Cruz et al. 2019; Maziuk et al. 2017; Vanderweyde et al. 2016; Waris et al. 2014). The intracellular localization of such granules differs greatly, with activity‐dependent granules being present in synaptic terminals or dendrites, and SGs known to form in the periphery of the nucleus (Figure 1).

The mechanisms underlying granule formation begin with liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) of core nucleating RBPs (e.g., G3BP1), mRNA, and structural partners (e.g., Caprin‐1), which serve as recruitment sites for additional RBPs and corresponding mRNAs. The impetus for granule formation can be post‐translational modification (PTM) of RBPs (serine/threonine phosphorylation or typically arginine methylation), changes in heat shock protein (HSP) availability, or stalling of ribosomes. These core elements serve as recruiters of additional interaction partners, driving the formation of specific interaction networks and transcripts, leading to the formation of distinct granule types (Formicola et al. 2019; Roden and Gladfelter 2021; Sanders et al. 2020).

In recent years, the concept of phase separation has become central to understanding RNA granule formation. This mechanism explains how granules nucleate and grow, as well as how they may transition into more solid‐like aggregates, a feature observed in various neurodegenerative diseases. Multiple studies in yeasts and in vitro cell lines have demonstrated that RNA granules behave as dynamic, liquid‐like condensates (Liu et al. 2023), whose assembly is driven by fundamental physical principles, including maximizing entropy and minimizing Gibbs free energy, capable of assembling and disassembling in response to both internal and external stimuli (Amzallag and Hornstein 2022; Glauninger et al. 2022; Hofmann et al. 2021; Wheeler et al. 2016). In summary, RNA granules are highly regulated, adaptable structures whose assembly and disassembly involve a balance of molecular interactions, signaling events, and cellular conditions. Their dynamic nature and proper regulation are crucial for maintaining RNA homeostasis. By controlling all stages of mRNA processing, including biogenesis, translation, and degradation, RNA granules emerge as essential organelles for proper cellular function and survival.

In this review, we explore how RNA Granules, like activity‐dependent granules, P‐bodies, SGs, and Transport granules, contribute to neuronal and synaptic function, while their dysregulation is central to neurodegenerative processes. We explore how these granules regulate RNA processing, transport, and local translation, mechanisms that can be key for synaptic function, while their dysregulation can cause major neurodegeneration through synaptic dysfunction and damage in the context of neurodegeneration.

RNA Granules

RNA Granule Formation

The formation of RNA granules is mediated by biophysical and biochemical interactions. Unlike membrane‐bound organelles, RNA granules lack a lipid bilayer and instead form through LLPS, a process in which specific RBPs and RNAs reversibly condense into loose, droplet‐like structures that are largely separate environments from the surrounding cytoplasm or nucleoplasm (Banani et al. 2017; Milicevic et al. 2022; Putnam et al. 2023). LLPS is mediated by multivalent intermolecular interactions between RBPs, as well as between RNAs and proteins. RBPs that drive granule formation (including FUS, hnRNPA1, TDP‐43, and FMRP) contain intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). These IDRs have multiple features that promote dynamic transient interactions, resulting in phase separation. The process of LLPS is driven by weak interactions of multiple short peptide sequences in the IDR domains; these weak, transient interactions occur via Pi and/or hydrogen bonding (Posey et al. 2018). Because each IDR domain contains multiple short peptides, each individual peptide binds only transiently, but the group of peptide sequences binds enough to keep the proteins together as a stable complex that nevertheless remains fluid and dynamic. The composition of Pi and hydrogen bonding (driven by the amino acid composition) combines with the distance between the peptides and post‐translational modifications to create the “grammar” of LLPS that allows different RBPs to form different types of granules. mRNA also actively regulates the formation and stabilization of granules, acting as a scaffold and engaging in sequence‐ or structure‐specific interactions that promote clustering (Bauer et al. 2023; Spinazzi et al. 2023). Importantly, these IDRs lack sequences that bind strongly and drive stable three‐dimensional structures, such as α‐helices or β‐sheets (Putnam et al. 2023).

The resulting granules exhibit liquid‐like properties, such as the ability to merge, change shape, and exchange components with the surrounding environment, which is essential for their dynamic regulation and functionality (Banani et al. 2017; Putnam et al. 2023). The sharing of components among them also suggests that these structures are dynamic structures that can interchange their composition and, so, regulate the sorting of mRNAs along cell structure, their translation, and degradation. The propagation of RBP interactions throughout the membraneless organelle (MO), termed percolation, plays a critical role in determining the resulting functionality of the MO (Mittag and Pappu 2022). The optimal percolation occurs when MOs adopt a gel‐like state that allows the conformation and binding of RBPs to impact neighboring molecules, thus transmitting information; too much fluidity prevents the transfer of structural information, while too much rigidity prevents neighboring RBPs from changing their structures (Mittag and Pappu 2022). The concept of phase separation coupled to percolation (PSCP) by Mittag and Pappu brings a new perspective on the interactions and networking of RBPs that work together with the concept of density‐based phase separation. Unlike pure phase separation, the process of PSCP gives rise to sequence‐, chemistry‐, and structure‐specific distributions of clusters that can form at concentrations that are below the threshold concentration for phase separation.

Assembly and disassembly of RNA granules are tightly controlled by the interplay of thermodynamic conditions (e.g., concentration, solubility), kinetic factors (e.g., nucleation and clearance rates), molecular properties (e.g., valency, charge), and cellular processes (e.g., signaling, chaperone activity, ATP availability). Additionally, the regulation of granule dynamics is further modulated by PTMs of granule‐associated proteins. PTMs, such as phosphorylation, methylation, and SUMOylation, can alter a protein's charge, solubility, or binding affinity, either promoting granule dissociation or stabilization (Su et al. 2021). For example, methylation of arginine residues in RBPs like FUS (Fused in Sarcoma) has been shown to reduce phase separation and strengthen interactions with molecular chaperones (Wang and Li 2024). mRNA modifications (e.g., N 6‐methyladenosine (m6A)) can also regulate RNA granule dynamics by modulating how proteins bind to RNA and therefore impact the formation and function of RNA granules. m6A regulates mRNA partitioning between the polysome and the P‐body, where a pool of non‐translating mRNAs resides (Ries et al. 2023), while it is known that m6A‐mRNA is enriched in SGs (Li et al. 2024; Ries et al. 2023).

Additionally, ATP‐dependent remodeling enzymes, including RNA helicases (e.g., DDX6, DDX3X) and molecular chaperones (e.g., Hsp70, Hsp90), actively regulate granule turnover, preventing abnormal aggregation and facilitating the recycling or release of granule components due to their dynamic and reversible nature (Bauer et al. 2022; Wolozin and Ivanov 2019). Another relevant factor for the regulation of RNA granule assembly is RNA itself. Both the presence and concentration of freely available RNAs, as well as its molecular structure (e.g., secondary and tertiary), have a high influence on the formation of RNA granules. For example, DDX6 granule assembly upon synaptic inhibition is controlled by the levels of free mRNA in neuronal cultures and in vivo, supporting the importance of RNA availability for both granule assembly and activity‐dependent synaptic plasticity (Bauer et al. 2022). RNA structure has emerged as an important player for RNA‐protein interaction for RNA granule assembly, since it dictates the accessibility for protein binding, regulating the recruitment of specific transcripts into the granules or providing structural scaffolds for assembly (Sanchez de Groot et al. 2019). mRNA secondary structure and modifications like m6A influence its recruitment into condensates and the formation of distinct RNA granules by regulating RNA–RNA interactions (Langdon et al. 2018). Studies in yeasts show that they can instruct the morphology of granules by their ability to engage in intermolecular RNA–RNA interactions (Fernández‐Moya et al. 2021; Van Treeck et al. 2018).

RNA granules' dynamics are also highly controlled by cellular signaling pathways, which coordinate their behavior with the physiological state of the cell. For example, SG formation is controlled by mTOR signaling activation through S6K and consequent activation of the integrated stress response (IRS) by eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) phosphorylation in C. elegans (Sfakianos et al. 2018). In neurons, for instance, synaptic activation triggers calcium influx and activation of downstream signaling cascades (e.g., mGluR‐mediated pathways), which in turn rapidly induce granule disassembly for translation initiation. This process releases previously repressed mRNAs, making them available for local translation at synapses (Bauer et al. 2023; Fernandez‐Moya et al. 2014). On the other hand, under cellular stress conditions, cells promote granule assembly to repress non‐essential protein synthesis while storing crucial transcripts; this is also observed in in vivo and in vitro models of chronic stress and high glucocorticoids (GCs).

While RNA granule formation is typically reversible and beneficial under physiological conditions, disruption of the regulatory and catabolic systems that control their dynamics can lead to pathological outcomes. Studies have shown that prolonged stress exposure, mutations in RBPs, or age‐related dysfunction in clearing mechanisms can cause granules to become persistent, shifting into more solid‐like, less dynamic states (Rhine et al. 2022). Indeed, aging itself can lead to oxidation of membraneless organelles, reducing their fluidity and disrupting function (Dall'Agnese et al. 2025). Such abnormal granules can act as nucleating factors for toxic protein aggregation, contributing to the development of several neurodegenerative diseases (Ghosh et al. 2020; Kedersha and Anderson 2002; Nussbacher et al. 2019; Thomas et al. 2011; Wolozin and Ivanov 2019).

Types of RNA Granules: Composition, Localization, and Function

Distinct classes of RNA granules have been identified in both the cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments of cells. Despite often sharing core components and constantly exchanging transcripts and proteins, each type is characterized by a distinct set of constituents, subcellular localization, and molecular functions (Hirose et al. 2023; Kiebler and Bassell 2006; Putnam et al. 2023) (Figure 1). Here, we explore RNA granules linked with cytoplasmic mRNA metabolism, and we leave out nuclear bodies, reviewed elsewhere (Shan et al. 2024).

Transport granules are primarily found in neuronal dendrites and axons, where they are enriched near synaptic sites. Their key components include RBPs such as Staufen1 (Stau1), Purα, Fragile X Mental Retardation Protein (FMRP), and Z‐DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1), as well as translation factors (e.g., eIF4E, eIF2α) and motor proteins like Kinesin‐related protein 5 (KIF5), which facilitate their movement along the cytoskeleton (Putnam et al. 2023; Wolozin and Ivanov 2019). Transport granules participate in neuronal processes, such as synapse formation and LTP, through the storage and transport of specific transcripts (e.g., those encoding synaptic proteins) to distal neuronal regions. During transport, the mRNAs are translationally repressed (through the action of RBPs like FMRP and ZBP1), ensuring that protein synthesis is spatially restricted (Mitsumori et al. 2017; Tsang et al. 2019).

Upon reaching synaptic compartments and in response to neuronal activity, transport granules can undergo remodeling to form activity‐dependent granules. This subset of RNA granules functions as a temporary RNA repository, storing mRNAs in an inactive state and releasing them for translation in response to neuronal stimulation. These granules are enriched in synaptic mRNAs and contain activity‐regulated RBPs such as Staufen2 (Stau2) and DEAD‐box helicase 6 (DDX6), as well as motor proteins (Bauer et al. 2022; Sharangdhar et al. 2017). The assembly of this type of RNA granule is dependent on local concentration and availability of mRNA and on the presence of scaffold proteins like Stau2 (Bauer et al. 2022). Interestingly, the size and composition of these granules change during development. For instance, Stau1‐containing granules decrease in mature neurons, reflecting a developmental tuning of granule dynamics (Mitsumori et al. 2017). These granules shrink during intense activity, indicating their sensitivity to synaptic signaling and their role in rapid local translational response (Mitsumori et al. 2017).

Processing bodies (P‐bodies) are a well‐characterized class of RNA granules that are primarily involved in mRNA degradation and translational repression (Parker and Sheth 2007; Ripin et al. 2024; Vessey et al. 2006). These granules are enriched in mRNA decay machinery, including decapping enzymes (e.g., DCP1A), exonucleases, translational repressors (e.g., DDX6), and short interfering RNAs (siRNAs) / microRNAs (miRNAs) factors. Importantly, P‐bodies do not contain ribosomal subunits. In neurons and glial cells, P‐bodies are found mainly in the soma and dendrites, where they mediate post‐transcriptional silencing mechanisms like miRNA‐directed gene silencing, AU‐rich element (ARE)‐mediated decay, and nonsense‐mediated decay (NMD) (Chan and Slack 2006; Decker and Parker 2012). These processes occur functionally in cells lacking detectable P‐bodies, making them non‐essential for silencing. On the other hand, they seem to form as a consequence of silencing, since blocking microRNA and iRNA pathways prevents their formation, while polysome‐free mRNA needs to enter silencing or decapping to nucleate into P‐bodies (Eulalio et al. 2007). These observations support the hypothesis that, beyond degradation, P‐bodies primarily act as temporary storage sites for translationally repressed mRNAs, releasing them for translation when cellular conditions are favorable. Although P‐bodies are constitutively present in cells, their dynamics are modulated by external stimuli. For instance, in neurons, their size and number decrease with synaptic stimulation and increase during periods of rest, indicating a potential role in modulating protein synthesis in tune with neuronal activity (Zeitelhofer et al. 2008). They are physically and functionally associated with SGs, sharing certain proteins and disassembling in response to the same drugs (Kedersha et al. 2005).

SGs are one of the most studied RNA granules in neurodegeneration. These granules are transiently formed in response to cellular stress, such as endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress or hypoxia. Their assembly involves RBPs like TIA‐1, G3BP1 (Ras GTPase‐activating protein‐binding protein 1), and PABP (Polyadenylate‐binding protein), as well as disease‐linked proteins like FUS and TDP‐43 (N. Kedersha and Anderson 2002; Wolozin and Ivanov 2019). The components of SGs, though, vary across cell types and conditions, indicating that SGs must be viewed as a general category rather than a uniform MO (Yeo et al. 2020). SGs are nucleated by untranslated mRNAs released from polysomes and RBPs like translation initiation factors (e.g., eIF4E, eIF3), as a consequence of global protein synthesis arrest and redirecting cellular resources toward stress response and cellular adaptation. Synthesis of select stress response proteins does occur in SGs. Stress response proteins such as ATF4, HSPs, or HIF (Hypoxia‐Inducible Factor) are selectively made driven by the binding of the EIF3 complex to m6A methylated noncanonical start sites (Tegowski et al. 2024). While SGs are typically reversible and structured into a dynamic core–shell architecture, persistent or dysregulated SGs can transition into pathological aggregates, contributing to neurodegenerative diseases (Wolozin and Ivanov 2019). The particular RBPs present in SGs likely determine which mRNA are in SG and their fate, whether it is translated into protein, stored without being translated, or shunted to neighboring P‐bodies for degradation.

The role of SGs and P‐bodies in neurons is being actively investigated, particularly regarding SG/P‐body transport from perinuclear regions into dendrites. Another research topic is whether they can assemble away from the cell body, near or within synapses. Moreover, understanding the overlap between SGs, P‐bodies, and transport granules is also an important area of ongoing research. Studies in neurons using time‐lapse microscopy revealed that P‐bodies and transport granules are not co‐localized or transported together in dendrites; however, they can, in fact, dynamically interact via docking, with neuronal activation leading to a decrease in P‐bodies, suggesting that synaptic stimulation impacts their function (Zeitelhofer et al. 2008). However, we know that all of these granules share components, forming an interconnected network of mRNA regulation and protein synthesis along the neuron.

Collectively, these diverse RNA granule types underscore the intricate and compartmentalized nature of RNA regulation in eukaryotic cells, particularly within the nervous system. Whether transporting mRNAs to synapses, degrading unwanted transcripts, or buffering translationally stalled RNAs during stress, RNA granules serve as central nodes in the post‐transcriptional regulatory network. Their specialized composition, localization, and function are highly coordinated, working together to adapt gene expression to the dynamic needs of the cell.

RBPs, RNA Granules, and Synaptic Function

Neurons are highly complex cells with extensive and elaborate structures that extend significant distances from the soma, requiring precise spatial and temporal control of protein synthesis to maintain function and adaptability (Dargan et al. 2024; Doyle and Kiebler 2011; Mofatteh 2020; Nagano and Araki 2021). To respond efficiently to external stimuli, such as synaptic activation, neurons rely on mRNA transport and local mRNA translation at synapses. A tightly regulated mechanism that supports critical processes, including neuronal maturation and regeneration, axon guidance, synapse formation, and plasticity (e.g., LTP and LTD), is ultimately highly relevant for superior brain functions like emotional control, learning, and memory (Kang and Schuman 1996; Mofatteh 2020).

Another layer of complexity in these tightly regulated processes is RNA modifications. They play an important role in the regulation of mRNA transcription and, therefore, their interaction with RBPs like FRMP, TDP‐43, and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A1 (hnRNP1) (Hsu et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2015). Among the hundreds of known RNA modifications, m6A is the most abundant mRNA modification in eukaryotes, and is highly enriched in synapses, involved in neuroplasticity, learning, and memory (Livneh et al. 2020), being highly sensitive to environmental stimuli, once more supporting its regulatory role (Engel et al. 2018). Additionally, unlike somatic protein synthesis, synaptic mRNA translation depends on the spatiotemporal control of mRNA transport and localized translation, orchestrated by the interplay of RBPs and RNA granules. These play a central role in the three main steps of local translation: (1) repression of translation through the nucleation of free mRNAs into RNA granules; (2) transport of these translationally silent mRNAs along microtubules to distal compartments via transport granules; (3) granule remodeling into activity‐dependent granules and rapid translational initiation at synapses in response to neuronal stimulation (Doyle and Kiebler 2011; Fernandez‐Moya et al. 2014; Glock et al. 2017).

Transport Granules and Fine‐Tuning of Synaptic Function

The initial phase of local translation occurs in the neuronal soma, where RBPs bind to target mRNAs, simultaneously stabilizing them and halting their translation by preventing ribosomal engagement or promoting ribosomal stalling. Through LLPS, these RBP‐mRNA complexes aggregate and condense into RNA granules, which function as mobile units for the stable transport of the transcripts, alongside small ribosomal subunits and translational machinery components. After mRNA incorporation into transport granules, the stored transcripts are shuttled from the soma to distal dendritic and axonal compartments, along the cytoskeleton, primarily microtubules, using motor proteins (e.g., KIF5) (Köhrmann et al. 1999) (Figure 2a). This mechanism poses a notable adaptation of neuronal cells for the delivery of mRNAs into distal compartments, at hundreds of microns away from the cell body.

(b) At synaptic compartments, where activity‐dependent granules mediate localized translation in response to synaptic stimuli, key RBPs (Fused in Sarcoma [FUS], TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 [TDP‐43], Fragile X Mental Retardation Protein [FMRP], SH3 and multiple ankyrin repeat domains protein [SHANK]) regulate the translation of synaptic proteins (Postsynaptic density protein 95 [PSD‐95], Brain‐Derived Neurotrophic Factor [BDNF], Cyclic AMP‐Response Element Binding protein [CREB]) in close relationship with mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) and eIF4F (eukaryotic initiation factor 4F) pathways, essential for synaptic plasticity and vesicle replenishment. Apart from mutations in RBPs that have been described to affect these functions, Tau protein may also play a role in both physiological and pathological contexts, since it is known to play a role in synaptic structure and function, through the interaction of structural proteins like Fyn. (c) Upon external or internal stimuli that trigger cellular stress responses, stress granules (SGs) are formed to allow mRNA stalling and protection into liquid‐like dynamic structures that, upon stress resolution, are resolved. When stress becomes persistent, these structures adopt a more solid‐like arrangement, leading to the formation of pathological SGs. Additionally, mutations in proteins involved in their assembly, like the ones found in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), lead to an increase in nucleation and rigidity of these structures, causing the formation of these pathological structures. Another factor that has been linked with the formation of these pathological structures is aging and neurodegenerative conditions, which not only lead to increased cellular stress but also disrupt the learning pathways of the cell. On the other hand, processing bodies (P‐bodies) are normally present in the cell, associated with mRNA decay or other mRNA silencing processes. Both RNA granules are in proximity in the cytosol and are known to interact and exchange components.

Recent studies indicate that the underlying mechanisms of an efficient transport of silent mRNAs to synapses reside in the molecular architecture of RNA granules, which encompass stalled ribosomes, signaling molecules, specific complexes of transcripts, and RBPs (Krichevsky and Kosik 2001; Mallardo et al. 2003). Some emerging work using SunTag‐based reporters also show that translation may occur during transit (Wang et al. 2016). Neuronal transport granules contain stalled ribosomes paused at specific mRNA sequences, allowing rapid translation initiation in response to local cues upon arrival at synaptic compartments (Anadolu et al. 2023; Kipper et al. 2022). In addition, imaging studies have revealed that some mRNAs travel independently to dendrites rather than in large complexes, allowing for fine‐tuned control of mRNA content within specific synapses (Batish et al. 2012). This individual transport mechanism permits precise regulation of the synaptic proteome and enables synapse‐specific adaptations without the need to induce any major cellular pathway, like RNA granule formation. This targeted transport is critical for supplying synapses with the molecular components necessary for plasticity and activity‐dependent remodeling.

Recent findings also point ribosomes to be dynamic regulators of local translation within distal neuronal compartments. Fusco and colleagues demonstrated that neuronal ribosomes undergo protein exchange, generating heterogeneous ribosome populations with distinct translational capacities, allowing for selective translation of specific subsets of mRNAs in response to activity (Fusco et al. 2021). Moreover, evidence that ribosome maturation and assembly can occur locally in dendrites and axons challenges the classical view of ribosome biogenesis as a soma‐restricted process (Fusco et al. 2025). These findings suggest that RNA granules and locally remodeled ribosomes work in tandem, coordinating transcript availability with ribosomal specialization to achieve precise spatiotemporal translation at synapses. Additionally, the precise destination of specific transcripts into specific dendrites is still puzzling. The RNA signature hypothesis, an RNA‐centric perspective, indicates that each mRNA has a unique signature of regulatory elements that determines its transport, localization, and translation (Doyle and Kiebler 2011).

The RBPs involved in transport granules are FMRP, ZBP1, Stau1, and Stau2, as well as various heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) (Bassell and Warren 2008; Ohashi and Shiina 2020; Sephton and Yu 2015). Among these proteins, FMRP is perhaps the most well‐characterized RBP involved in synaptic RNA regulation, as under basal conditions, FMRP inhibits translation by binding to its target mRNAs and causing ribosomal stalling (Sephton and Yu 2015). Recent evidence demonstrates that FMRP undergoes LLPS, forming dynamic droplets that coincide with translational inhibition, while also being shown to function as a kinesin adapter, facilitating their dendritic transport along microtubules (Antar et al. 2004; Bassell and Warren 2008; Dictenberg et al. 2008). While we know that transport granules respond to neuronal activity, with mRNAs being released to the polyribosome fraction for translation activation, the repression of translation by these granules in response to neuronal activity has also been described, indicating the relevance of these mechanisms for the fine‐tuning of synaptic function and the importance of the changes in protein composition of these granules for this fine‐tuning (e.g., ZBP1, CPEB1) (Hüttelmaier et al. 2005; Stebbins‐Boaz et al. 1999).

The final phase of local translation involves the stimulus‐dependent remodeling of RNA granules at synapses, leading to the rapid reorganization of transport granules into activity‐dependent ones. Upon synaptic stimulation, granules disassemble, releasing the mRNAs and leading to the rapid initiation of local protein synthesis. Synaptic stimulation is often triggered by membrane depolarization or activation of receptors like mGluRs, which induce intracellular signaling cascades involving Ca2+ influx, ERK, and mTOR pathways. Consequently, these pathways elicit the remodulation or disassembly of RNA granules, enabling local translation of specific mRNAs that encode proteins essential for synaptic function and structural plasticity, such as PSD‐95, CaMKII, GABA receptors, and CREB‐regulated targets (Rajgor et al. 2021). However, activity mRNA few transcripts have been underexplored, remaining important to characterize localized mRNAs in neurons to clarify which proteins are needed in localized synthesis and their importance for brain function.

Studies have shown increased recruitment of RBPs like FUS, DDX17, and several hnRNPs to synaptic sites following stimulation, reinforcing remodulation of RNA granules as a direct outcome of synaptic activity (Bauer et al. 2023; Liu‐Yesucevitz et al. 2011; Sephton and Yu 2015). In fact, recent findings suggest that neuronal activity not only induces remodeling of RNA granules but also influences their dynamics and localization. For instance, activity‐induced redistribution of P‐bodies and the release of mRNAs from these granules may regulate actin cytoskeleton remodeling in dendrites, contributing to structural synaptic plasticity (Ford et al. 2019; Oh et al. 2013). Additionally, DDX6‐positive RNA granules undergo disassembly during neuronal maturation in vitro and in vivo, and this process is reversible and dependent on synaptic activity. These findings highlight the role of free cytoplasmic mRNA levels and neuronal activity states in determining granule assembly and function.

Thus, the regulation of mRNA by RBPs and RNA granules is critical for the proper execution of local translation. The coordinated interplay between RBPs, RNA granules, and neuronal activity ensures that transcripts are transported to the correct subcellular compartments and translated at the appropriate time, thereby exerting tight control over synaptic proteome dynamics and maintaining neuronal homeostasis. Activity‐dependent local translation is particularly crucial for synaptic plasticity, as it underlies synaptic strengthening and the processes involved in learning and memory consolidation (Mofatteh 2020). Disruption of this tuned system, either through altered RBP expression, mutations, or mislocalization, can lead to pathological granule formation and translational impairments, ultimately contributing to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal vulnerability (Liu‐Yesucevitz et al. 2011; Wolozin and Ivanov 2019). This will be further explored in subsequent sections of this review.

Stress Granules, P‐Bodies, and Neuronal Response Dynamics

Unlike other RNA granule classes, SGs are not constitutively present in cells under normal conditions. Instead, they form transiently and rapidly in response to cellular stressors, such as oxidative stress, heat shock, hypoxia, viral infection, or ER stress. This granule assembly is initiated by the arrest of translation initiation, often via the phosphorylation of eIF2α, which elicits the accumulation of untranslated mRNA‐RBPs complexes in the cytoplasm. Consequently, the increase of these RNA‐protein complexes and their binding to RBPs promotes phase separation, leading to the formation of cytoplasmic SGs through LLPS. The resulting condensates typically range from 200 to 400 nm in diameter and present a core–shell architecture, with a relatively stable inner core surrounded by a more dynamic outer shell. Core SG components include RBPs (like TIA‐1, G3BP1, FMRP, or PABP), along with polyadenylated mRNAs, small ribosomal subunits, and associated translation initiation factors (Kedersha et al. 2002; Markmiller et al. 2018; Wolozin and Ivanov 2019) (Figure 2b).

Multiple studies have shown that these RBPs play an important role in the SG dynamics, including assembly, stability, and disassembly. Examples of key nucleators are TIA‐1 and G3BP1, RBPs that directly bind to mRNAs and interact with the SG‐associated translational machinery, promoting the consolidation of these components into the granules (Kedersha et al. 2016; Lastres‐Becker et al. 2016). While TIA‐1 has a prion‐like domain (PLD) that promotes aggregation and granule initiation, G3BP1 functions as a scaffold protein critical for SG condensation (Yang et al. 2020).

Notably, while precise assembly of SGs is essential for an effective response to cellular stress, their timely dismantling is equally critical for restoring normal RNA metabolism and translation once homeostasis is re‐established.

SGs function as a protective mechanism to transiently sequester non‐translating mRNAs and translation‐associated proteins and factors, allowing the cell to conserve energy and redirect gene expression toward stress response (Anderson and Kedersha 2009; Kedersha and Anderson 2002). This finely tuned response enables cells to rapidly adapt to adverse conditions while safeguarding RNA integrity and proteostasis. However, the dynamic nature of SGs is essential to their protective function. Failure to efficiently disassemble these structures can lead to persistent aggregates that interfere with normal RNA metabolism and translation.

P‐bodies are quite distinct from SGs and act as cytoplasmic silencing foci that are constitutively present and can be enhanced by stress. mRNA in P‐bodies can be targeted for degradation or may return to translation (Kedersha et al. 2000, 2005). However, P‐bodies can be distinguished from SGs by the presence of decapping enzymes like DCP2 or DCP1A, proteins involved in mRNA decay, degradation, and RNAi machinery (e.g., Argonautes). Multiple observations provide evidence of a close relationship between cytoplasmic RNA granules like P‐bodies and SGs. P‐bodies and SGs respond to the same stimuli, inducing mRNA translation inhibition (e.g., mutations, drugs, stressors) (Kedersha et al. 2005; Anderson and Kedersha 2009), and they both physically interact, often docking together during stress; however, the mechanisms of transcript transfer between these MOs remain unresolved (Figure 2b).

As previously mentioned, neuronal SGs are highly investigated RNA granules, largely because of their involvement in multiple key cellular processes, including translational repression and stress response. Emerging evidence suggests that SGs may also participate in the regulation of synaptic protein synthesis, under both basal and stress‐induced conditions (Desai et al. 2026; Martin et al. 2013; Reineke et al. 2012; Thomas et al. 2014; Wolozin 2012). The detection of SGs in dendritic compartments, in proximity to synapses, supports the intriguing hypothesis that they contribute localized translational control (Figure 2c) (Donlin‐Asp et al. 2021; Sephton and Yu 2015; Wang et al. 2010). These dendritic SGs share core components with their somatic counterparts, including TIA‐1, TIAR, G3BP1, eIF4E, FMRP, and Pumilio2 (Pimentel and Boccaccio 2014; Sahoo et al. 2018; Vessey et al. 2006), many of which are known to influence mRNA metabolism and translation. Notably, G3BP1 has emerged as a critical regulator of neuronal SG dynamics, particularly in axons, where it forms SG‐like structures that appear to modulate local mRNA translation (Sahoo et al. 2018).

Recent observations suggest a model in which SGs and P‐bodies, upon synaptic stimulation, may halt translation, storing the translationally repressed mRNAs alongside proteins and translational machinery, only to release them later in response to activity cues (Doyle and Kiebler 2011; Fernandez‐Moya et al. 2014). This dynamic behavior could provide neurons with another rapid, reversible mechanism to adapt local protein synthesis to synaptic demands.

Despite these insights, many aspects of these RNA granules' direct or indirect role in local translation are still unclear, including how specific mRNAs are selected to be stored, how the composition of granules changes with neuronal activity, and how the interaction with different RNA granule types (e.g., transport granules) (Liu et al. 2025) impacts neuronal function and synaptic response. Nonetheless, growing evidence points to SGs as central players in neuronal plasticity by directly or indirectly impacting local protein synthesis in neurons, and P‐bodies to be responsive to synaptic activity, repressing translation.

RNA Granules in Neurodegeneration

Recent advances in cellular biology and neuroscience have illuminated the critical role of RNA granules, particularly SGs, in neurodegenerative disease pathogenesis. Under physiological conditions, RNA granules are highly dynamic, reversible structures that ensure cellular homeostasis and allow cells to respond efficiently to external stimuli, including stress. However, emerging evidence indicates that when their dynamics are disrupted, either by cellular stress (Silva et al. 2019), aging (Rhine et al. 2022), or genetic mutations (Jo et al. 2020), the RNA granules can undergo pathological modifications. Among the distinct classes of RNA granules, SGs and P‐bodies stand as the most associated with neurodegeneration, as these types of granules transition from transient protective condensates into persistent, aggregation‐prone structures (Figure 2b) (Jeon and Lee 2021; Li et al. 2013; Yuan et al. 2025). Pathological granules sequester essential RBPs and mRNAs, disrupt global translation, and interfere with protein homeostasis, with SGs particularly known to function as nucleating sites for the aggregation of disease‐related proteins, such as Tau (Wolozin 2012). Indeed, aberrant dynamics of these granules have been increasingly recognized as a potential contributor to neurodegenerative pathogenesis (Jo et al. 2020), being particularly pronounced in diseases such as Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), Alzheimer's Disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and other proteinopathies (Figure 2b) (Dudman and Qi 2020; Hanson et al. 2012; Jo et al. 2020; Millar et al. 2023; Sephton and Yu 2015; Zhang et al. 2018).

Pathological RNA Granules

Pathological RNA granules differ from their physiological counterparts in several key aspects, the most important being the loss of their dynamic response. Unlike physiological granules, pathological RNA granules persist in the cytoplasm long after the initial stressor has been resolved (Rhine et al. 2022). This persistence changes the granules' biophysical properties, causing a shift from a liquid‐like state to a more solid or less dynamic configuration, a process termed “vitrification” (Millar et al. 2023; Rhine et al. 2022; Yoshizawa et al. 2020), which later progresses to fibril formation and aggregation.

The transition from a beneficial to a pathological role impacts protein quality control, stress response pathways, and RNA regulation. The persistence of these dysfunctional granules leads to the sequestration of essential cellular machinery, resulting in compromised protein synthesis, altered synaptic signaling, and eventual neuronal death. Moreover, the interaction of these granules with disease‐related proteins that are hallmarks of several neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., TDP‐43, Tau) further contributes to the disease development, highlighting the interconnected nature of pathological RNA granules dysfunction and protein aggregation in the brain.

Understanding how these normally protective cellular structures become pathological requires examining the complex interplay between RBP dysfunction, chronic stress, aging, and genetic mutations that drive the transition from physiological to pathological RNA granule states.

Mechanisms Driving Pathological RNA Granules

Chronic cellular stress has been identified as a major factor driving the transition of physiological RNA granules into pathological states. While acute stress induces the formation of dynamic, transient SGs, prolonged or repeated stress leads to persistent granules that are more solid‐like, increasingly stable, and resistant to normal disassembly mechanisms (Adachi et al. 2024; Rhine et al. 2022; Wolozin and Ivanov 2019). These persistent structures may act as precursors to pathological aggregates of disease‐related proteins (e.g., TDP‐43, FUS, Tau) observed in neurodegenerative pathologies (e.g., ALS, AD, FTD), highlighting how dysregulation of RNA granule biology contributes to neuronal dysfunction and degeneration.

The consequences of chronic stress are multifaceted, encompassing oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, proteotoxic stress, and metabolic dysfunction (Dall'Agnese et al. 2025; McEwen 2007; McEwen et al. 2015; Silva et al. 2019). Oxidative stress plays a dual role in SG dynamics, with studies reporting induction (Emara et al. 2012; Lian and Gallouzi 2009) and inhibition (Arimoto‐Matsuzaki et al. 2016) of granule formation depending on the context. Exposure to oxidative agents (e.g., hydrogen peroxide, sodium arsenate) can drive SG formation (Chen and Liu 2017) and promote their shift from transient, protective structures to persistent, pathological states. Generally, it is accepted that chronic oxidative stress directly impairs SG disassembly through oxidation of key nucleating proteins such as TIA‐1, causing vitrification, which later leads to accumulation and dysfunctional SGs. However, recently, some papers support the hypothesis that pre‐conditioning to chronic cellular stress may also inhibit normal SG assembly upon acute stress (Adachi et al. 2024). Additionally, recent work by Dall'Agnese's colleagues describes that pathological signals associated with chronic cellular stress may trigger reduced protein mobility associated with oxidation of cysteine residues, with stress oxidizing some RBPs, leading to disulfide bonds that cause aggregation (e.g., TDP‐43) (Dall'Agnese et al. 2025).

Impaired RNA granule clearance is another critical driver of pathological granule persistence. Under physiological conditions, RNA granules are eliminated not only by disassembly but also through autophagy, in a process termed “granulophagy.” This pathway involves recognition of SGs and P‐bodies by autophagy receptor p62 and the ATPase VCP (also known as p97), which mediate granule delivery to lysosomes (Figure 2b) (Buchan et al. 2013; Monahan et al. 2016). Mutations in VCP, as identified in ALS and FTD, disrupt this clearance mechanism, leading to persistent SGs that may seed pathological aggregates. Recent studies have also shown that the NS1‐binding protein regulates SG turnover by inhibiting p62 ubiquitination. In cells lacking this protein, SG clearance is decreased, due to p62 ubiquitination increases (Jeon et al. 2024). Moreover, aging neurons often exhibit a decline in RNA granule elimination, partially due to broad depletion of RBPs and spliceosome components, further exacerbating granule accumulation (Pushpalatha et al. 2022; Rhine et al. 2022).

Perhaps the most direct contributor to pathological RNA granule formation is RBP dysfunction, through either genetic mutation, mislocalization, or disruption in their cellular levels. Many RBPs, such as TDP‐43, FUS, and TIA‐1, contain PLDs or low‐complexity domains that enable phase separation under normal conditions. However, these same domains can also promote pathological aggregation under chronic stress or when mutated. Pathological aggregation may occur (Gotor et al. 2020; Nedelsky and Taylor 2022; Sprunger and Jackrel 2021). Disease‐associated mutations often enhance the aggregation propensity of RBPs (Rhine et al. 2022; Sephton and Yu 2015), frequently resulting in cytoplasmic mislocalization and subsequent incorporation into pathological RNA granules, particularly SGs. Additionally, RBPs' mislocalization may also be caused by mutations in nuclear localization signals or by disruption of the Ran GTPase gradient, impairing nuclear import and leading to their cytoplasmic accumulation (Ederle and Dormann 2017; Jo et al. 2020). Beyond direct effects on granules' dynamics, dysfunctional RBPs broadly disrupt RNA processing, contributing to neuronal vulnerability and degeneration. The SMN (Survival of Motor Neuron) complex exemplifies this connection, as it functions as a molecular chaperone essential for the biogenesis of various ribonucleoproteins involved in RNA metabolism. Studies have shown that mutations leading to SMN deficiency cause spinal muscular atrophy, a disorder characterized by selective motor neuron degeneration despite ubiquitous expression of SMN (Faravelli et al. 2023). This underscores how perturbations in global RNA pathways can result in cell‐type‐specific degeneration.

Together, all these mechanisms described converge to impair RNA granule dynamics, through both inhibition of normal RNA granule assembly (e.g., protective SGs) and induction of persistent granule accumulation. These pathological RNA granules not only evade normal elimination but also act as nucleation sites for toxic protein aggregates, thereby driving cellular dysfunction and neurodegeneration (Rhine et al. 2022; Wolozin and Ivanov 2019).

Pathological RNA Granules and Key RBPs in Neurodegenerative Diseases

The disruption of RNA granule dynamics and RBP homeostasis has emerged as a common feature across multiple neurodegenerative disorders. In these pathologies, mutations or functional impairments in RBPs drive the formation of pathological RNA granules and contribute to downstream toxic effects.

TDP‐43 is a highly conserved nuclear protein that regulates RNA processing, alternative splicing, and mRNA stability through its RNA recognition motifs and glycine‐rich C‐terminal domain (Necarsulmer et al. 2023). In neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., ALS), TDP‐43 undergoes pathological missorting from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, where it forms insoluble aggregates that co‐localize with SG markers (Jo et al. 2020; Keating et al. 2022) and P‐bodies (Ye et al. 2025). The dysfunction of TDP‐43 creates a complex pathological cascade involving both loss of normal function and gain of toxic properties (Herzog et al. 2020; Necarsulmer et al. 2023). TDP‐43 normally functions in the nucleus as a suppressor of cryptic splicing. Cytoplasmic translocation of TDP‐43 leads to loss of nuclear TDP‐43 and a corresponding increase in cryptic splicing (Ganssauge et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2024). These cryptic spliced mRNAs interfere with cell function in many ways. For some proteins, such as STMN2, the cryptic spliced transcript has a premature stop site that produces a truncated nonfunctional protein and a loss of full‐length protein. The loss of full‐length protein interferes with cytoskeletal function, leading to neurodegeneration. Other cryptic splice mechanisms lead to a direct loss of full‐length protein production without production of a cryptic peptide. Regardless of the result, the cryptic peptides are being investigated as biomarkers for ALS.

Cytoplasmic TDP‐43 normally regulates SG dynamics in motor and cortical neurons, with its loss leading to defective SG assembly and clearance (Khalfallah et al. 2018). Conversely, pathological TDP‐43 aggregates sequester and deplete free nuclear TDP‐43, creating a feedforward loop of dysfunction (Keating et al. 2022; Pérez‐Berlanga et al. 2022). The accumulation of cytoplasmic TDP‐43 aggregates has many deleterious consequences. Extranuclear TDP‐43 translocates to mitochondria, where it is thought to interfere with function (W. Wang et al. 2013). C. elegans work shows that TDP‐43 aggregates also accumulate in the endoplasmic reticulum, where they interfere with proteostasis (Vaccaro et al. 2012).

RNA‐binding‐deficient TDP‐43 forms distinct pathological structures called anisosomes that readily sequester normal TDP‐43, further exacerbating cellular dysfunction (Keating et al. 2022). Chronic stress conditions promote the recruitment of TDP‐43 into SGs, where it becomes increasingly aggregated (Ratti et al. 2020); phosphorylation of TDP‐43 helps to solubilize it and shunts the TDP‐43 for degradation in the lysosome (Gruijs da Silva et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2010). This process is particularly evident in ALS, as cytoplasmic TDP‐43 aggregates are found in approximately 97% of ALS cases (Dudman and Qi 2020). This can also be observed in patient‐derived iPSC‐derived motor neurons, where prolonged oxidative stress induces both SG formation and pathological TDP‐43 aggregates (Ratti et al. 2020). Pathological studies show that nuclear loss of TDP‐43 is far more evident than its cytoplasmic accumulations, with loss of nuclear TDP‐43 evident in more neurons than the accumulation of cytoplasmic aggregates. This points to a loss‐of‐function mechanism, with a corresponding increase in cryptic splicing, which might be the most important factor contributing to neurodegeneration in ALS and FTD.

The acetylation of TDP‐43 drives neuronal dysfunction through aberrant splicing and transcription of genes regulating synaptic plasticity and stress response signaling (Necarsulmer et al. 2023). TDP‐43 pathology was originally determined to be a hallmark pathology of ALS and FTD‐TDP (with progranulin mutations). However, TDP‐43 pathology also occurs in other neurodegenerative disorders, including AD, Parkinson's Disease (PD), or Huntington's disease (HD). Cases of AD with TDP‐43 pathology tend to show more rapid progression of cognitive loss and increased inflammation. Brain regions from cases with TDP‐43 pathology exhibit cryptic splicing similar to that observed in ALS, while cases of AD without TDP‐43 pathology do not exhibit cryptic splicing (Ma et al. 2022; Miwa et al. 2025). The importance of mixed pathologies in disease risk and progression is increasingly being appreciated. Diagnosing and treating these mixed pathologies may well become a key element of any neurological workup for neurodegenerative disease.

Another critical RBP whose dysfunction has been linked to ALS is FUS. Similarly, FUS mutations have been identified in familial ALS cases, with pathological FUS inclusions observed in affected neurons (Hanson et al. 2012; Kino et al. 2011; Sephton and Yu 2015). FUS variants can cause rare juvenile, aggressive genetic forms of ALS, with FUS protein localization shifting from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, accompanied by FUS cytoplasmic aggregation in motor neurons (Vance et al. 2009). Mislocalized FUS aggregates are also observed in the frontal cortex, hippocampus, and striatum in a subtype of FTD (Mackenzie et al. 2011) and HD (Doi et al. 2010), highlighting the role of FUS in neurodegenerative disorders. Over 50 variants have been identified, with the majority of them in the nuclear localization signal domain, causing its cytoplasmic mislocalization and loss of nuclear presence, enhancing its recruitment to SGs (Kwiatkowski et al. 2009; Naumann et al. 2018). These chronic SGs become sites of protein insolubility, with key SG markers like G3BP1 and TIA‐1 becoming increasingly insoluble alongside mutant FUS proteins (Kodavati et al. 2024). Yet, it remains unclear whether persistent SG recruitment of FUS is a protective attempt to buffer stress or an early step in driving irreversible aggregation. Moreover, the conclusions taken from aggressive familial mutations must be taken with caution when exploring sporadic ALS or other neurodegenerative diseases.

Mutations in G3BP1 are not associated with neurodegenerative diseases, but homeostasis dysregulation has also been characterized in polyglutamine (polyQ) disorders, such as HD, where SGs interface directly with toxic protein aggregation. Normally, G3BP1 interacts with the mutant huntingtin protein, facilitating its proteasomal degradation (Gutiérrez‐Garcia et al. 2023). However, under stress, this RBP is sequestered into SGs, losing its protective role and allowing toxic huntingtin aggregates to accumulate. Similar patterns are observed in other polyQ diseases, where SG components are abnormally sequestered, promoting aberrant protein aggregation (Marcelo et al. 2021). The link between SG formation and Poly‐Q disorders is well explored by Marcelo and colleagues. G3BP1 and G3BP2 appear to have complementary functions. For instance, G3BP1 associates with aggregated tau, while G3BP2 associates with monomeric tau and appears to reduce the aggregation tendency of monomeric tau (Wang et al. 2023). Whether G3BP2 functions in complementary manners with other aggregating proteins, such as TDP‐43 and HTT, remains to be determined, but could represent an important regulatory axis for neuronal resilience.

FMRP, whose dysfunction was primarily identified in Fragile X Syndrome (Marcelo et al. 2021), is an RBP that coordinates activity‐dependent local translation at synapses by repressing the translation of hundreds of synaptic mRNAs (e.g., PSD‐95, Arc). This RBP blocks both the initiation and elongation steps of translation through direct association with initiation complexes and ribosomes, respectively (Kim et al. 2006). FMRP localizes to several classes of neuronal RNA granules, including transport granules, SGs, and P‐bodies. At the synapses, it is known to be associated with polyribosome complexes and regulated by metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR) activation (Nalavadi et al. 2012; Narayanan et al. 2007). Dysregulation, mutation, or loss of FMRP elicits synaptic dysfunction (Pfeiffer and Huber 2007; Telias 2019) through excessive translation, disturbed granule dynamics (Didiot et al. 2009), aberrant dendritic spine structure, and ion channel dysregulation. The link between FMRP and APP misprocessing might bring novel insights into the disease pathogenesis of AD. Several studies have identified that APP transcript is associated with FMRP (Lee et al. 2010; Westmark et al. 2008), regulating translation in response to mGluR activation (Westmark and Malter 2007). While studies in mouse models of AD further explore these connections, FMRP genetic reduction can protect against AβO‐infusion‐induced memory deficits by restoring protein synthesis (Ribeiro et al. 2024). Still, the contribution of FMRP‐containing granules to late‐onset neurodegenerative diseases lacks clear mechanistic models connecting FMRP dysfunction with progressive neuronal loss.

While we will later explore in this review the role of Tau protein for RNA granule dynamics, particularly focusing on SGs, we should note that in AD pathology, the role of Aβ within RNA granule dysfunction is underexplored. In addition to the work of Ribeiro and colleagues (Ribeiro et al. 2024), some in vitro work using neuroblastoma cell lines also explores the connection between Aβ and SGs. It was observed that exposure to monomeric or oligomeric forms of Aβ42 led to the induction of G3BP1 positive SGs in a eIF2α dependent manner, with unchanged protein translation (Ramasamy et al. 2025). Work by many has also explored Tau protein as an effector of Aβ induced neurodegeneration (Lopes et al. 2016; Roberson et al. 2007), and this link should not be forgotten when exploring these mechanisms in the context of AD. Understanding direct and indirect effects of Aβ on SG formation or RNA granule dynamics is utterly necessary for the understanding of pathological mechanisms and synaptic degeneration found in AD. However, the impact of Aβ on SG formation has not been fully understood.

All these examples illustrate a recurring pathological axis in neurodegeneration: the convergence of RBP dysfunction, RNA granules dysregulation, and toxic protein aggregation. Whether driven by genetic mutations, chronic stress, or impaired clearance, pathological RNA granules act as scaffolds for toxic protein aggregation and disrupt essential RNA regulatory processes. Nevertheless, several questions remain to be understood, focusing on whether these granules are drivers or by‐products of disease, how their dynamics change across brain regions, and to what extent we can translate our findings from cell and animal models to human pathology driven by age.

RNA Granules and Synaptic Dysfunction

As described previously in this review, physiological RNA granules play relevant roles in RNA metabolism, from transport to translation. At distal neuronal compartments, like synapses, RNA granules enable activity‐dependent translation, which is essential for normal synaptic function, in a more cost‐effective way than transporting individual proteins. Accumulating evidence indicates that synaptic dysfunction is not only a common feature across many neurodegenerative diseases, but also arises as an early and common feature, setting the stage for and likely contributing to the progression of neurodegeneration. Yet, we do not know how pathological RNA granules can elicit pathological mechanisms that ultimately lead to impairments in synaptic plasticity and activity. While the precise mechanisms remain poorly understood, plausible and interrelated pathways have been proposed to explain how pathological RNA granules contribute to synaptic impairment.

Under normal conditions, RNA granules regulate local translation at synapses by maintaining mRNAs in a repressed state until synaptic activity triggers their release and fast translation. Pathological granules, however, fail to release sequestered mRNAs upon stimulation, thereby depleting the pool of molecules available for normal synaptic protein synthesis (Fan and Leung 2016; Li and Sun 2025). The deficiency of synaptic proteins upon synaptic stimulation may compromise how individual synapses modulate their proteome in response to external stimuli. This ultimately contributes to synaptic weakening, atrophy, and impaired neuronal communication. For instance, PSD‐95 and CaMKII mRNAs, encoding crucial postsynaptic proteins for scaffolding and signaling, are stored in RNA granules and released upon stimulation to enter polyribosomes and undergo local translation (Figure 2c) (Donlin‐Asp et al. 2021; Rook et al. 2000; Vedeler and Bramham 2021). However, pathological RNA granules compromise this process by trapping these transcripts and thus preventing the synthesis of these synaptic proteins, negatively affecting spine morphology and synaptic plasticity.

Disrupted granule dynamics can also impair the trafficking of mRNAs and ribonucleoprotein complexes to distal neuronal compartments, resulting in deficient synaptic transcript levels (Hirokawa 2006; Vedeler and Bramham 2021). A critical example of trafficking dysfunction associated with RNA granules involves the DDX6‐positive granules. This type of granule normally disassembles during neuronal maturation as synaptic activity increases. When synaptic activity is suppressed, these granules reassemble into larger structures, sequestering mRNAs and preventing their proper localization. This process is dependent on Stau2, a crucial RBP required for RNA transport, and its disruption potentially leads to widespread deficits in mRNA trafficking and thus in protein expression at synapses (Bauer et al. 2022; Fernandopulle et al. 2021). In AD, studies have reported widespread dysregulation of mRNA translation that affects synaptic function (Ghosh et al. 2020). Early changes in Aβ metabolism result in increased FMRP‐regulated protein synthesis through a signaling cascade involving MAPK‐interacting kinase 1 (Mnk1) and eukaryotic initiation factor 4E (eIF4E). This leads to the reduction of CYFIP2, an FMRP‐binding protein, which subsequently contributes to the development of Tau pathology and synaptic dysfunction (Ghosh et al. 2020). The dysregulation extends to global translational control mechanisms. Increased phosphorylation of eIF2α has been observed in postmortem AD brain tissue and experimental models. This leads to general translation suppression while paradoxically increasing translation of specific mRNAs.

In addition to affecting translation and transport, pathological RNA granules can compromise RNA surveillance mechanisms, particularly NMD (Mooney et al. 2017). UPF1 (Up‐frameshift protein 1), a core NMD component, is present in both cytoplasmic and synaptosomal compartments, where it regulates distinct populations of mRNAs. After status epilepticus, alterations in synaptic UPF1 activity led to dysregulation of key neuronal transcripts involved in plasticity and excitability, including Arc and novel targets like Inhba and Npas4 (Mooney et al. 2017). Other mechanisms, such as ribosome stalling, observed in HD, further impair mRNA regulation (Eshraghi et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2022) and may promote synaptic dysfunction and neurodegeneration. High‐resolution ribosome footprint profiling revealed widespread shifts in ribosome occupancy and unique single‐codon pauses on selected mRNA targets (Eshraghi et al. 2021). Failure to rescue stalled ribosomes can initiate stress responses, impair synaptic function, and promote neuronal degeneration.

Furthermore, the aggregation of RNA granules at the synaptic compartments can interfere with normal synaptic vesicle dynamics and recycling, possibly altering neurotransmitter release (Uytterhoeven et al. 2025). The physical presence of large aggregates can disrupt synaptic ultrastructure and interfere with the precise spatial organization required for efficient synaptic transmission. Additionally, we must always keep in mind that synaptic organization itself, into its nano and micro domains, relies on multivalent protein–protein interactions that induce phase separation, leading to the formation and coexistence of distinct molecular condensates within those tiny structures, synapses. These mechanisms allow for the clustering of neurotransmitter receptors to regulate neuronal plasticity at the postsynaptic density (PSD) and for synaptic vesicles to be stored in distinct pools in the pre‐synaptic compartment. Reviewed in detail by Zhu and colleagues (Zhu et al. 2025). The exploration of liquid–liquid phase separation as an important mechanism for synaptic organization and function is also rather interesting to refer to. Work by Zhang and colleagues shows that proteins controlling synaptic signaling (e.g., PSD‐95 and SynGAP) are organized in phase‐separated condensates and function as MOs, allowing for an enrichment of proteins in quite a small compartment (Zeng et al. 2016). Its relationship to disease, though, is not understood.

RNA regulation at synapses represents a fundamental mechanism for controlling local protein synthesis and synaptic plasticity, in close balance with cytoplasmic or transport granules. This sophisticated machinery along the neurons requires a balanced and dynamic cellular response (Figure 2). However, the vulnerability of this system is highlighted in neurological disorders, where pathological aggregation of RNA granules can severely compromise synaptic function and neuronal response. Understanding these mechanisms provides important insights into the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases and offers potential therapeutic targets for restoring normal synaptic RNA regulation. The challenge moving forward is to develop interventions that can restore the delicate balance between RNA granule assembly and disassembly while maintaining the essential functions of these structures in synaptic plasticity and memory formation.

The Role of RBPs in Tau Pathology

Tau as a Multifunctional Protein: From Cytoskeleton to RBP Disruption

Within the growing framework of RBP dysfunction and aberrant SGs as pathogenic contributors to neurodegenerative diseases, Tauopathies emerge as a paradigm where these processes converge. Remarkably, half a century after its initial characterization as a Tubulin‐Associated Unit (Weingarten et al. 1975), Tau protein has evolved from being viewed as a simple microtubule‐binding protein regulating cytoskeletal assembly and stability in the axon (Black et al. 1996; Guo et al. 2017) to a protein present throughout different cellular structures, from the nucleus to the dendrites and dendritic spines. From there, it has grown as a protein that not only regulates axonal growth but also DNA stability in the nucleus, synaptic function, and interacts with RNA (Bryan et al. 1975), classifying it as a non‐canonical RBP. Despite this early observation, the scientific community has mostly overlooked this aspect of Tau in its pathophysiology. This oversight is particularly striking given Tau's high charge density and PLDs, which predispose it to RNA‐mediated phase separation (Zhang et al. 2017). This intersection with RNA biology reframes Tauopathies not merely as disorders of protein aggregation but as conditions arising from disrupted RBP homeostasis and RNA granule‐driven mechanisms of Tau pathogenesis.

RBPs and Tau Interaction in Dysfunction

Intracellular homeostasis critically regulates all stages of Tau aggregation, from initial misfolding driven by PTMs, particularly phosphorylation at sites near and in the microtubule binding domain, to oligomerization, propagation, and ultimately to fibril maturation into neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) (Alquezar et al. 2021; Kimura et al. 2007). Critically, translational stress responses (marked by ISR‐mediated SG assembly and ribosomal stalling) are overactive in Tauopathies, creating feedforward loops that exacerbate Tau aggregation (Vanderweyde et al. 2016). Evidence from multiple studies, including in vitro, in vivo rodent animal models, human iPSC cell models, and postmortem human brain samples, demonstrates the strong association of RBPs, the ISR, and Tau pathology. Integrated analyses of Tau interactomes and localized proteomics, such as those reviewed by Kavanagh and colleagues (Drummond et al. 2020; Kavanagh et al. 2022), demonstrate that RBPs are consistently enriched within pathological Tau aggregates across Tauopathies, including AD, FTD, and corticobasal degeneration (CBD). Recent efforts have expanded beyond static interaction maps by integrating interactome data with phospho‐proteomics and functional pathway analyses (Drummond et al. 2020; Radford et al. 2023; Thierry et al. 2024), revealing novel Tau‐associated protein networks that evolve with disease progression (Webber et al. 2024).

While phosphorylation of Tau by stress‐related kinases is traditionally viewed as a pathological event, it also represents a physiological regulatory mechanism. Phosphorylation facilitates rapid Tau oligomerization, and both phenomena are observed in the brains of infants (Gonzalez‐Ortiz et al. 2025; Wolozin et al. 1988), hibernating animals (Arendt et al. 2015; Bullmann et al. 2016; de Veij Mestdagh et al. 2024; Härtig et al. 2005; León‐Espinosa et al. 2013, 2024), and cold‐shocked rodent models. Under such stress conditions, phosphorylated oligomeric Tau associates with RBPs to regulate the ISR and temporarily suppress protein synthesis, conserving energy by reducing ribosome production (Jiang et al. 2021; Silva et al. 2019). However, with aging and chronic stress, lysosomal and proteasomal clearance responses decrease, becoming unable to properly handle the burden of hyperphosphorylated/oligomeric Tau, ultimately leading to the accumulation of pathological Tau.

The dysregulation of these responses is modulated by RBP‐Tau interactions, which actively contribute to modifying disease progression through mechanisms such as RNA dysregulation, SG dynamics, and LLPS. While in some neurodegenerative conditions we find aggregates of Tau and RBPs, some do not show co‐localization (e.g., hnRNP A1, A2B1, and K), suggesting a role of RNA dysregulation and translation for disease that occurs independently of direct co‐aggregation (Kavanagh et al. 2025). But we also observe aggregation of RBPs directly involved in SGs (e.g., TIA‐1, Musashi, PABP1) that show co‐localization with Tau oligomers and fibrils in in vitro (rodent/human cells), in vivo (e.g., rTg4510, PS19) models of Tauopathy, and in postmortem AD/FTD brains (Maziuk et al. 2018; Montalbano et al. 2020). The interactions of SGs and small Tau inclusions are shown to be appearing early in the disease, with Tau undergoing LLPS in pathological conditions (Wegmann et al. 2019).

A good example of Tau‐RBP interaction is with TIA‐1, which typically co‐localizes with Tau aggregates. TIA‐1 has been shown to be stimulated by pathological Tau to form SGs, which in turn accelerates Tau aggregation, as shown by previous studies of P301S in vitro and in vivo Tauopathy models (Vanderweyde et al. 2012, 2016). TIA‐1 is proposed to directly interact with Tau, augmenting Tau oligomerization, thereby accelerating the formation of insoluble Tau aggregates and Tau pathology (Ash et al. 2021). Reversibly, reduction of TIA‐1 has been found to protect against Tau aggregation and related neurotoxicity in vivo (Apicco et al. 2018).

In contrast, G3BP1 is absent from Tau aggregates, despite being an integral SG component. The absence of G3BP1 likely arises due to associations between the Tau/TIA‐1/hnRNPA2B1 complex and m6A mRNA. G3BP1 is repelled by m6A‐labeled mRNA, in contrast to G3BP2, which is attracted to it (Edupuganti et al. 2017; Jiang et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2023). Additionally, G3BP1 binds to soluble Tau in an RNA‐independent manner, preventing its oligomerization, as demonstrated in human iPSC‐derived neuron models (Wang et al. 2023).

Tau pathogenesis extends beyond aggregation, encompassing disruptions of multiple cellular compartments. Pathological Tau‐RBP complexes impair nuclear functions, including nucleocytoplasmic transport, chromatin remodeling, and nuclear lamina integrity (Montalbano et al. 2020). Interestingly, different phospho‐epitopes of Tau accumulate in different cellular compartments. For instance, Tau phosphorylated at S202/205 or S396/404 residues tends to accumulate in dendrites, while oligomeric Tau accumulates mainly in the neuronal soma. Indeed, Tau missorting to dendritic spines disrupts synaptic homeostasis (Pooler et al. 2014), and pathological Tau significantly disrupts synaptic RNA regulation through multiple mechanisms. It has been shown that Tau disrupts mitochondrial transport to synapses, interferes with normal synaptic vesicle dynamics (Uytterhoeven et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2021), and its hyperphosphorylation at synapses, triggered by AMPA and NMDA receptor stimulation, leads to abnormal synaptic function (Park et al. 2020). Furthermore, recent evidence demonstrates that Tau and TIA‐1 interact with important synaptic proteins such as complement C1q and CaMK2b, suggesting the existence of additional complexes that may contain Tau and RBPs in synaptic compartments (Webber et al. 2024). However, the link between Tau and RBPs in neurodegeneration‐related synaptic dysfunction still requires exploration.

Chronic Stress as an Environmental Trigger

While intrinsic factors, such as age, sex, and genetic predisposition (e.g., mutations in ApoE, APP, and PSEN1/2 genes), establish baseline risk for AD and other Tauopathies (Breijyeh and Karaman 2020), environmental risk factors can actively reshape the disease trajectory. Chronic exposure to stressful stimuli and high GC levels emerge as an accelerating factor of AD progression. As reviewed in depth by Burke and colleagues (Burke et al. 2024), chronic stress and elevated GCs amplify all core pathological hallmarks of AD, including the intracellular Tau hyperphosphorylation and NFT formation, extracellular Aβ plaque deposition, and sustained neuroinflammatory responses, consistently demonstrated in pre‐clinical and clinical models of AD, with similar pathways implicated in primary Tauopathies.

Interestingly, multiple studies demonstrate the role of Tau and its malfunction as a core mechanism for chronic stress and/or high GC induced neurodegeneration. While the use of Tau‐KO mice revealed that Tau is essential for stress‐induced neuronal and synaptic damages (Lopes et al. 2016); the use of transgenic mice expressing pathological human Tau, further supports the relevance of chronic stress and GC for Tau pathology progression. In vitro and in vivo studies show that chronic stress leads to hyperphosphorylation and accumulation of Tau, its spreading and synaptic missorting, ultimately leading to downstream neuronal malfunction and memory deficits (Figure 3) (Lyons et al. 2023; Sotiropoulos et al. 2008, 2011; Sotiropoulos and Sousa 2016).

Neuronal alterations induced by Tau pathology: Impact on RNA‐Binding Protein (RBP) dynamics, cytoskeletal organization, and synaptic structure. This schematic compares a healthy neuron (left panel) to a Tau pathology neuron (right panel), emphasizing the molecular and structural consequences of Tau‐induced dysfunctions. In healthy neurons, RBPs such as T‐cell intracellular antigen 1 (TIA‐1) and Ras GTPase‐activating protein‐binding protein 1 (G3BP1) are predominantly localized in the nucleus and cytoplasm in a regulated manner, participating in mRNA metabolism and stress granule assembly only under transient stress conditions. The dendritic spines exhibit well‐organized postsynaptic densities (PSDs), with anchoring of glutamate receptors (N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate receptors [NMDARs] and α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid [AMPARs]) via scaffolding proteins (PSD‐95 [Postsynaptic density protein 95], SHANK [multiple ankyrin repeat domains protein], HOMER) and actin cytoskeleton networks (F‐actin, cortactin). Tau stabilizes microtubules in dendrites, facilitating proper cargo transport and structural integrity. Fyn kinase localizes in PSDs, modulating NMDA receptor signaling. In Tau pathology neurons, misfolded and hyperphosphorylated Tau disrupts microtubule stability, leading to axonal transport defects and dendritic spine retraction. The inset (top right) illustrates the aberrant cytoplasmic translocation of nuclear RBPs (e.g., TIA‐1, G3BP1), which nucleate persistent stress granules (SGs) due to impaired clearance mechanisms. These SGs act as seeds for pathological aggregation of Tau, further exacerbating cellular dysfunction. At the synaptic level, Tau pathology induces actin cytoskeleton destabilization, affecting the anchoring of synaptic receptors and scaffold proteins. Mislocalized Fyn kinase hyperactivates NMDAR signaling, increasing intracellular calcium influx (Ca2+), which triggers excitotoxic cascades and synaptic disassembly. The cumulative effect of Tau aggregation, RBP mislocalization, and cytoskeletal breakdown culminates in synaptic dysfunction and neurodegeneration.

The mechanisms through which chronic stress induces Tau accumulation and pathology are being explored. Ongoing investigations indicate impairments in key signaling cascades like mTOR pathway and kinase hyperactivation (e.g., GSK‐3β via AKT suppression), disrupted synaptic integrity, and damaged degradation mechanisms. Studies point for Hsp70 depletion via the ubiquitin‐proteasome system (UPS) and autophagy‐lysosome pathway alterations, in parallel with mitochondrial‐ROS toxicity (Du et al. 2023; Monteiro‐Fernandes et al. 2021; Silva et al. 2019; Vaz‐Silva et al. 2018).

The impact of chronic stress in RNA homeostasis mechanisms, in particular, RNA granule dysfunction is still being explored. Work in P301L‐hTau mouse models revealed that chronic stress and high GC levels induce cytoplasmic accumulation of RBPs and SG‐like structures (including TIA‐1, G3BP1, PABP, FUS, and DDX5), directly promoting Tau accumulation and aggregation (Silva et al. 2019). Additionally, in vitro work indicates that SG formation is essential for stress‐induced Tau accumulation.

Beyond its role in Tau pathology, these findings position RBPs and RNA granules dysregulation as critical mechanisms for chronic stress. This suggests the role of RBPs extends beyond disease pathogenesis into physiological stress adaptation pathways, as a molecular switches between physiological and disease states. Further investigations need to be done regarding how chronic stress differentially regulates specific SG‐associated RBPs and how these proteins can drive Tau dysfunction in stress conditions.

Chronic stress is now a well‐documented risk factor beyond AD. It has been linked with multiple neurodegenerative diseases by promoting the aggregation of different pathogenic proteins. One important example is PD, where chronic stress exacerbates α‐synuclein pathology by promoting phosphorylation at serine 129 (pS129) and nigrostriatal degeneration, accelerating disease progression while simultaneously inducing depression‐like behaviors through hippocampal α‐synuclein dysregulation (Xia et al. 2025). Notably, α‐synuclein, like Tau, can undergo LLPS, suggesting conserved mechanisms of RBP‐mediated proteotoxicity (Ray et al. 2020). Exploring the role of chronic stress as a major risk factor for neurodegeneration could represent a universal pathway through which environmental stressors work as predisposing factors for brain disorders by precipitating and accelerating neurodegeneration.

The Triad for Neuronal Dysfunction: Pathological RNA Granules, mRNA Dysregulation, and Tau

Throughout this review, we have highlighted recent research positioning RNA granules at the core of mRNA metabolism. These dynamic, membraneless structures are essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis and mediating responses to both internal signals and environmental stimuli. The dynamics of RNA granules are tightly regulated by cellular components, such as RBPs and RNAs themselves, and modulated by neuronal activity and synaptic stimulation. This activity‐dependent remodeling enables RNA granules to transport transcripts into distal neuronal compartments (e.g., axons, dendrites, synapses), where they mediate local translation of proteins critical for synapse remodeling, axon guidance, and adaptive neuronal responses. Dysregulation of RNA granule dynamics and the resulting disruption of mRNA metabolism have been shown to impair synaptic plasticity and neuronal function. Such detrimental effects are observed in several neurodegenerative diseases, including ALS, AD, FTD, and other Tauopathies, where disease‐associated RBPs (e.g., TDP‐43, FUS, hnRNPA1, TIA‐1) are either sequestered into pathological granules or lose their normal function.

Importantly, pathological RNA granules, particularly SGs, intersect with proteinopathies by facilitating the aggregation of misfolded or hyperphosphorylated proteins such as Tau. Tau pathology has been consistently associated with synaptic dysfunction; its hyperphosphorylated and oligomeric forms disrupt synaptic signaling and spine morphology. Under pathological conditions, SGs have been found to co‐localize with phosphorylated and oligomeric Tau, supporting recent evidence for a bidirectional relationship between Tau and SG dynamics: Tau can influence SG formation and clearance, while SGs may serve as scaffolds that promote Tau aggregation. Specifically, Tau missorting into dendritic spines and synaptic compartments contributes to synaptic impairment (Figure 3). Notably, studies in Tauopathy mouse models show that reducing TIA‐1, a key SG nucleator, is neuroprotective, resulting in decreased Tau oligomer levels, restoration of synaptic structures, and improved behavioral outcomes.

Based on these findings, we propose a unifying hypothesis in which three pathological processes, such as RNA granule dysfunction, dysregulation of local mRNA translation, and Tau‐mediated neuronal disruption, converge to drive synaptic dysfunction in neurodegenerative diseases like AD. This framework integrates the interplay between SGs, Tau pathology, and translational control to explain the synaptic impairments observed in disorders such as AD and other Tauopathies.

A compelling illustration of this pathological triad is found in chronic stress, a condition increasingly recognized as a risk factor for accelerated AD progression and disorders like depression and PD. In models of chronic stress, all three elements of the triad are affected. Prolonged stress promotes the formation of persistent SGs that trap essential mRNAs, impairing local translation and disrupting neuronal processes. This leads to reduced expression of synaptic proteins such as PSD‐95 and CaMKII, which are essential for synaptic integrity and plasticity. In parallel, chronic stress induces Tau hyperphosphorylation, missorting, and aggregation events that may be either facilitated by or further promote SG formation. Rodent models consistently reveal that these molecular alterations are accompanied by synaptic abnormalities, including reduced spine density, immature spine morphology, and deficits in synaptic transmission and plasticity. As such, chronic stress represents a potent experimental system for studying the convergence of RNA granule dysfunction, Tau pathology, and translational disruption underlying synaptic degeneration.

This triad likely operates through multiple, possibly overlapping mechanisms, including direct interaction, sequential processes, or parallel but convergent pathways. One possibility is that synaptic dysfunction arises from the direct interaction of pathological SGs and Tau aggregates with synaptic translation machinery. Their co‐localization may promote further Tau oligomerization and prolong granule persistence, exacerbating translational repression, leading to local depletion of synaptic proteins and structural disintegration of the synaptic architecture. Alternatively, these events may unfold sequentially. For instance, persistent SGs could trap key mRNAs and RBPs, halting translation and recruiting Tau, which then aggregates within granules. This Tau accumulation further disrupts RNA granule dynamics and amplifies local metabolic dysfunction, creating a self‐reinforcing cycle that promotes progressive synaptic and neuronal damage. A third possibility is that SGs and pathological Tau act in parallel, each contributing to synaptic dysfunction through independent mechanisms but producing cumulative effects. SGs may impede translation and disturb mRNA homeostasis, while Tau aggregation impairs vesicle trafficking, receptor recycling, and cytoskeletal dynamics. Together, these parallel disruptions result in compounded damage to synaptic function.

In summary, the triad hypothesis of synaptic dysfunction provides a conceptual framework connecting RNA granule dysregulation, Tau pathology, and local translation disruption, three processes that converge on synaptic impairment, a central feature of many neurodegenerative diseases. Investigating this triad offers a promising path for understanding disease mechanisms and identifying novel therapeutic targets aimed at restoring RNA metabolism, preventing toxic Tau interactions, and preserving synaptic health.
