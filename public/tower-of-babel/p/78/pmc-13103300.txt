Non-canonical muscle stem cells and other lessons from regenerative vertebrates
Authors: Vijayishwer Singh Jamwal, Wouter Masselink, Karen Crawford, Prayag Murawala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this perspective, we explore the developmental mechanisms of muscle stem cells in multiple non-mammalian vertebrate species, and we compare these phenomena to those in mammals. Particularly, we discuss two recently described populations of noncanonical muscle progenitors in regenerative vertebrates: one in zebrafish and one in axolotl. We discuss the capabilities of these two populations during muscle development and regeneration, including the implications for muscle repair in mammals.Subject terms: Cell biology, Developmental biology, Evolution, Physiology

Introduction

Muscle stem cells (MuSCs) are essential for nearly all aspects of muscle biology, orchestrating muscle growth, maintenance, and repair. PAX7 is the canonical marker of MuSCs in most vertebrates and is commonly associated with satellite cells. In this perspective, we discuss the roles of two recently identified noncanonical MuSC populations—one in zebrafish and one in axolotl—that are distinct from classical PAX7+ MuSCs. We highlight how these populations differ from their mammalian counterparts and examine the implications for muscle homeostasis and repair. We propose that these noncanonical MuSCs are capable of de novo myogenesis, at least in the contexts of zebrafish and axolotl development and regeneration. We also summarize key findings from the salamander field on muscle dedifferentiation during structural regeneration. Finally, we argue that these insights into non-canonical MuSC populations and muscle dedifferentiation are highly relevant to regenerative medicine and that further study of these systems may reveal new principles of muscle development and regeneration in mammals.

A focus of our discussion of MuSC function in vertebrates is their role in primary and secondary myogenesis. In vertebrates, most skeletal muscles are laid out during embryogenesis through a process called primary myogenesis. Postnatal growth of skeletal muscles in vertebrates takes place via secondary myogenesis, the mechanisms of which differ between most mammals and non-mammalian vertebrates. In mammals, secondary myogenesis proceeds primarily via hypertrophy—a process through which descendants of Pax7+ MuSCs fuse to existing muscle fibers—and involves very little hyperplasia, i.e., the addition of new muscle fibers. In contrast, secondary myogenesis in non-mammalian vertebrates, such as fish, takes place via substantial degrees of both hypertrophy and hyperplasia. Further, in fish, hyperplastic growth takes place both in the existing musculature (mosaic hyperplasia) and via growth of new muscle on the lateral edge of the body (stratified hyperplasia). In fish, a unique, recently described noncanonical MuSC population termed external cell layer (ECL) progenitors is involved in stratified hyperplasia1–3. In axolotl, we recently described a new MuSC population that is present in the tail but not the limbs, and that we term “asomitic stem cells” in our publication4. Hereafter, we refer to these cells as DMS-progenitors, as they have the ability to contribute to dermotomic, myotomic, and sclerotomic lineages during tail regeneration in axolotl. Both ECL and DMS-progenitors in fish and in axolotl, respectively, work in tandem with canonical PAX7+ MuSC during repair and regeneration. We propose that DMS-progenitors in axolotl are equivalent to the ECL progenitors in fish, based on the capacity of each population to contribute to de novo muscle growth during regeneration, and we present novel hypotheses on the role of these noncanonical MuSCs in muscle development.

The developmental origin of skeletal muscle tissue in vertebrates

Most vertebrate skeletal muscles develop from the somites. In this process, somitic progenitor cells differentiate to give rise to the dermomyotome and sclerotome under the influence of neighboring tissues (neural tube, notochord, and lateral plate mesoderm). The dermomyotome further differentiates into the dermatome and myotome, which are the sources of the dermic (fibroblastic) and myogenic lineages, respectively, in the body. In certain vertebrate species, additional subdivisions of somites give rise to the endotome (precursors of endothelial cells)5, external cell layers (precursors of muscle tissue)6, and the syndetome (precursors of tenocytes)7. In all vertebrates, the sclerotome further differentiates and gives rise to the vertebrae.

Most stages of muscle development are conserved across vertebrates, with development beginning with expression of PAX3 or PAX7, e.g., axolotl lacks Pax38, or both. Myogenic differentiation proceeds via sequential expression of four paralogous basic helix-loop-helix transcription factors (collectively termed myogenic regulatory factors): myogenic differentiation 1 (MYOD), myogenic factors 5 and 6 (MYF5, MYF6), and myogenin (MYOG)9. Comprehensive reviews of myogenic differentiation and its epigenetic regulation, covering diverse model systems, provide species-specific insights10–12. During development in both mammalian and non-mammalian vertebrates, embryonic muscle precursors give rise to PAX7+ muscle progenitors, which in mammals are also referred to as satellite cells, as they are nestled between the cell membrane surrounding the muscle fiber (sarcolemma) and the basal lamina. PAX7+ satellite cells are widely accepted as the primary MuSCs during homeostasis in mammals13. However, recent studies suggest that embryonic muscle precursors in fish and axolotl also generate a second, distinct population of muscle progenitors4,6. In fish, these cells can be Pax7− or Pax7+, but all are Meox1+6, 14, whereas in axolotl, these cells are Pax7− and Lfng/Scx/Meox1+4. In fish, these cells are termed “external cell layer (ECL) cells,” as they are located on the lateral surface of the myotome and are responsible for stratified hyperplastic growth of musculature14; in axolotl, we refer to these cells as DMS-progenitors, as noted above.

Evolutionarily early myogenic program, including the evolution of satellite cells

Among non-mammalian models, PAX7+ MuSCs have been well-studied only in zebrafish and amphibians. In zebrafish, satellite-like cells are marked by c-met, Pax3, and PAX7, and are lineage-restricted injury-responsive stem cells that undergo asymmetric division during regeneration15,16. PAX7+ MuSCs in axolotl and frog, like those in mammals and teleosts, are responsible for muscle repair and regeneration in limbs17,18 (Fig. 1). The PAX7 protein is crucial to muscle regeneration in both frog larval tail muscles and mammalian skeletal muscle tissues19,20. Interestingly, axolotls, in contrast to zebrafish and mouse, lack a Pax3 gene8, and consequently exhibit increased involvement of Pax7 in muscle formation and regeneration8,21. Concordantly, the phenotype of Pax7−/− axolotls21 resembles that of Pax7−/−;Pax3−/− double mutant mice, which show arrested muscle development and the formation of only embryonic myotome muscle22.

Axial musculature

External cell layer (ECL) progenitors in zebrafish are

Meox1+ and are fated for secondary myogenesis. Specifically, they are responsible for the stratified addition of new muscles on the outer edge of the body during primary body-axis development6. Clonal analysis of these cells suggests that in juvenile fish, a handful of the cells act as dominant stem cells responsible for muscle growth14. While many questions remain unanswered regarding the role of the Meox1 gene in stem-cell maintenance in general, we do know that the absence of this gene in zebrafish abrogates the clonal drift that is observed normally during development14. Another study showed that Col1a2+ cells from the ECL act as MuSC progenitors in zebrafish23. It is likely that the cell populations examined in these two latter studies14,23 represent the same cell population marked by two different marker genes.

DMS-progenitors in axolotl

While ECL progenitors in zebrafish contribute only to the muscle lineage, DMS-progenitors in axolotl can contribute to all three somitic mesoderm lineages during axolotl tail regeneration (dermatome, myotome, and sclerotome)4. The transcriptional profile of DMS-progenitors is distinct from that of the somitic progenitors that arise during axolotl embryonic development. In adulthood, DMS-progenitors carry a dual identity in that they can be identified both by the somitic progenitor markers Lfng and Meox1 and by tenocyte markers such as Scx, Tnmd, and Mkx. Using lineage-tracing studies during tail regeneration in axolotl, we conclusively showed that both Col1a2 and Lfng successfully label DMS-progenitors and that these progenitors turn on the PAX7 program before giving rise to muscle fibers4 (Fig. 1). Moreover, we demonstrated that these cells are localized within the intermyotomal space. Altogether, the location and behavior of DMS-progenitors strongly suggest that these cells are equivalent to the ECL cells described in zebrafish14,23. Further studies of the role of DMS-progenitors and their influence on PAX7+ progenitors are required to understand the mechanisms of de novo muscle-fiber formation in axolotl.

Pax7− progenitors in birds and mammals

Although researchers generally agree that, in mammalian models, Pax7+ somitic progenitors undergo terminal differentiation and are subsequently lost, recent studies suggest that birds and mammals may harbor an additional, equivalent population, either in early juvenile phases or in specific parts of the body, or both. For example, a study using cross-species grafting between chicken and quail suggests that lateral plate mesoderm-derived cells may contain progenitors for the axial muscle lineage24. However, because the grafts were performed at sites of lateral plate mesoderm, the samples may have included nearby or native somitic mesoderm progenitors. In subsequent experiments in this study, lineage tracing in mouse models using Scx:Cre showed that lateral plate mesoderm-derived cells contribute to hindlimb muscles. While one can question whether Scx+ cells originate in the somitic mesoderm or the lateral plate mesoderm, or why inducible Cre was not used in these experiments in mice, it is a common consensus that PAX7+ satellite cells are not marked with Scx in mouse. Hence, these results may reflect the contribution of a PAX7− lineage to hindlimb muscles in mouse models. Two other studies validate this notion. First, a study using flow sorting and grafting showed that Pw1+/PAX7− interstitial cells (PICs) first acquire a PAX7 identity and subsequently contribute to a muscle-fiber lineage in mouse hindlimbs25. Second, researchers showed that Twist2+/Pax7− cells may contribute to adult type IIb/x myofibers in mouse hindlimb muscles. The latter study used a tamoxifen-inducible mouse model, Twist2:Cre-ERT; mice without tamoxifen did not show a contribution of the Twist2 lineage to hindlimb muscles, convincingly demonstrating that the Twist2+/Pax7− population can contribute to the muscle lineage. The latter study used a tamoxifen-inducible Twist2:Cre-ERT mouse model; in the absence of tamoxifen, no Twist2-lineage contribution to hindlimb muscle was detected, supporting the conclusion that Twist2+/Pax7− cells can contribute to the myogenic lineage. Furthermore, ablation of the Twist2 population, even in the presence of PAX7+ cells, was sufficient to cause muscle atrophy in the hindlimbs of mice. It is unclear if PICs and the Twist2+ population mark the same cells in mouse hindlimbs. In any case, together these results in mice24–26 suggest the interesting possibility that some mammals may harbor a PAX7− population capable of contributing to muscle formation.

Appendicular musculature in vertebrates

The appendicular musculature of tetrapods evolved prior to the water-to-land transition and can be traced back to the common ancestor of all vertebrates27. The myoblast precursor for the vertebrate appendage originates from the ventrolateral region, i.e., the hypaxial region, of limb-level somites and can be identified by expression of transcription-factor genes such as Lbx1 or Mox2 in fish, amphibians, birds, and mammals28–31. These results suggest conserved evolution of appendicular musculature across these groups. Reflecting this conservation, Lbx1+ cells were shown to eventually populate appendicular musculature (fin/limb) in zebrafish, Xenopus, and mouse, and to establish two cell populations: muscle-fiber cells and PAX7+ MuSCs28,30,31. Interestingly, we and others have found no evidence of DMS-progenitors or their equivalent cells in the appendicular musculature of fish or amphibians. This is in stark contrast to the primary body axis of fish and amphibians, where ECL and DMS-progenitors, respectively, persist throughout life4,14,23.

Roles of PAX7+ MuSCs and DMS-progenitors in appendicular and axial muscle growth

Here we propose an interesting hypothesis regarding muscle development that is based largely on the above observations of differences between the organism that harbors only PAX7+ MuSCs (i.e., mouse), and organisms and body parts that harbor both PAX7+ MuSCs and either ECL or DMS-progenitors (i.e., fish and the axolotl tail, respectively). In the mouse, muscle growth depends primarily on hypertrophy, with a minor contribution through mosaic hyperplasia. For this hypertrophic growth, descendants of PAX7+ MuSCs fuse with an existing muscle fiber, leading to hypertrophic muscle growth in both axial and appendicular muscles (Fig. 2A). In contrast, muscle growth in the fish trunk (the location of axial muscles) takes place via major contributions from both hypertrophy and hyperplasia. Here, while PAX7+ MuSCs promote hypertrophic growth of existing muscle fibers in a manner similar to that in fish and axolotl, the ECL progenitors are responsible for secondary myogenesis, which adds new muscle fibers in the existing musculature (mosaic hyperplasia) and on the lateral edge of the body (stratified hyperplasia) (Fig. 2B)14.

The axolotl is a unique model organism in that body regions differ with respect to the progenitors that contribute to myogenesis. While DMS-progenitors contribute to tail regeneration in axolotl, we have found no evidence of DMS-progenitors in appendicular musculature during either regeneration or normal development in axolotl, and previous work17,32,33 suggests that during axolotl limb regeneration, appendicular musculature depends exclusively on PAX7+ MuSCs.

Based on the above previous results, we propose three hypotheses. First, stratified hyperplasia reflects the capacity of ECL progenitors to perform de novo myogenesis; second, if DMS-progenitors in axolotl are equivalent to ECL-progenitors, they should—as is the case with ECL progenitors in teleosts—contribute to stratified hyperplasia during axolotl primary body-axis growth during normal development; and third, because DMS-progenitors are absent in the appendicular musculature of axolotls, we will likely not observe stratified hyperplastic muscle growth in axolotl limbs. Altogether, we propose that axolotl represents a unique model organism in which normal development and regeneration of axial (e.g., tail) musculature may mirror the process in fish, whereas normal development and regeneration of appendicular musculature may mirror the process in mouse. Our preliminary data indeed point in this direction. For example, our results in axolotls under homeostatic growth conditions suggest that axial musculature demonstrates stratified hyperplastic growth similar to the process in fish, particularly when axolotls are 2–3 months old (early larval development) and are less than 2.5 cm in length (VSJ and PM, unpublished work). Contributions from DMS-progenitors gradually decline as axolotls reach 3.5 cm in length (~4–5 months old), and we have seen no evidence that DMS-progenitors participate in or contribute to new muscle growth in axolotls beyond this stage during homeostasis, suggesting that they enter quiescence.

The curious case of muscle-fiber dedifferentiation in salamanders

In mammals, muscle repair and regeneration are governed mainly by PAX7+ MuSCs, also known as satellite cells34. Similarly, in zebrafish, PAX7+ satellite-like cells have been reported to contribute to Pax7-dependent skeletal muscle repair16. Notably, there is no evidence of muscle-fiber dedifferentiation in either mouse or zebrafish, both of which are well-studied18. Similarly, there is no evidence of muscle-fiber dedifferentiation in Xenopus18,35.

In contrast, numerous studies show that muscle-fiber dedifferentiation contributes to muscle repair and regeneration in salamanders36–47, with myotube dedifferentiation proposed as early as 195937,47 and most recently in 202241. An examination of the presence of muscle-fiber dedifferentiation in different salamanders reveal substantial differences among species and between body regions of a given species. For example, during regeneration of the axolotl limb, muscle formation is governed exclusively by PAX7+ MuSCs17, whereas regeneration of the axolotl tail may involve muscle-fiber dedifferentiation36. In addition, Cynops use only PAX7+ MuSCs for muscle regeneration in neoteny, whereas upon metamorphosis these newts switch to muscle-fiber dedifferentiation for limb muscle regeneration41. Interestingly, Notophthalmus use muscle-fiber dedifferentiation for muscle regeneration in their metamorphic form as well as during neoteny, as they metamorphose early during development32. Furthermore, the same work indicates that axolotl relies exclusively on PAX7+ MuSCs for muscle regeneration during neoteny and metamorphosis32.

The possibility that muscle-fiber dedifferentiation could be induced in mammalian muscle has been fueled by in vitro studies, particularly studies in which serum factors or salamander-derived factors were applied to mammalian myotube culture46,48–50. However, further work is needed to determine whether muscle-fiber dedifferentiation can indeed be a means of muscle repair in humans.

Applying insights from regenerative vertebrates to expand the potential for muscle repair in mammals

Insights from regenerative non-mammalian vertebrates establish a conceptual framework for expanding strategies to enhance muscle regeneration in mammals beyond the satellite-cell paradigm. First, the identification of non-canonical progenitor populations such as zebrafish external cell layer (ECL) cells and axolotl DMS-progenitors demonstrates that de novo myogenesis can arise from progenitors positioned hierarchically upstream of PAX7⁺ muscle stem cells, suggesting that mammalian tissues may harbor latent or developmentally restricted progenitor states that could be reactivated therapeutically. Second, the coexistence of hypertrophic and hyperplastic growth programs in regenerative vertebrates suggests mechanisms capable of restoring muscle mass through new fiber formation as well as through repair of existing fibers, rather than solely via the latter mechanism. This insight offers potential solutions for conditions where satellite-cell capacity becomes limited, including volumetric muscle loss and degenerative myopathies. Third, salamander muscle dedifferentiation shows that differentiated muscle fibers retain plasticity under specific molecular environments, raising the possibility that transient reprogramming strategies could enhance regenerative competence in mammalian muscle without permanent lineage conversion. Together, these findings shift the translational focus from simply enhancing satellite-cell activity toward recreating permissive developmental and injury-responsive niches that enable progenitor plasticity, lineage reactivation, and scalable tissue regeneration.

Summary/discussion

Non-mammalian vertebrate model organisms, including axolotl and zebrafish have revealed new information important for the entire muscle-research field. First, by studying these diverse organisms, we have gained a better understanding of the evolution of muscle cells, including their evolutionary origin. Second, while most mammalian MuSC research has centered around PAX7+ satellite cells and their role as MuSCs, research in axolotl has shown that DMS-progenitors that are PAX7− and hierarchically above PAX7+ cells can contribute to muscle-tissue formation in these organisms. With the ongoing advances in sequencing methodologies and reductions in sequencing costs, genome assemblies of increasing numbers of organisms are becoming available for study. Further, the growth of single-cell technologies and CRISPR-Cas9-based genome editing is fueling the use of new model organisms previously inaccessible to investigators. Together, these methodological advances will provide deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms involved in MuSC maintenance and activation. The knowledge we gain from further, more comprehensive analysis of non-mammalian models can then serve as a foundation for tomorrow’s cures for muscle injuries in mammals.
