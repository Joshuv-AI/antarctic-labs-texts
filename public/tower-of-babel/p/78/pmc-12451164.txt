Protocol for constructing an accessible exposure chamber for in vitro and in vivo modeling of airway environmental exposures
Authors: Abdo Durra, Caroline Cherry, Coline Luo, Emily Huo, Isabella Passamano, Kristen Castillo, Andrew J. Lund, Woosuk Choi, Chandani Sen, Rachana Chandran, Tammy M. Rickabaugh, Brigitte N. Gomperts
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryExposure systems to study the effects of environmental exposures can be costly to purchase and difficult to use. Here, we present an accessible and cost-effective approach to building an exposure chamber in the lab. We describe steps for constructing the exposure system and writing the code to run it and simple instructions for experiments using the system. The exposure chamber can be adapted to expose airway cells or lab mice to combustible cigarette smoke, electronic cigarette vapors, or woodfire smoke.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Durra et al.1

Before you begin

With every breath, the lungs face a constant barrage of environmental particles, toxicants, and pathogens. The airway epithelium, which lines the cartilaginous airways, plays a critical role in defending against these constant insults. This vital barrier creates both a chemical and physical shield, limiting the passage of inhaled foreign matter. Mucociliary clearance (MCC), a critical process for removing inhaled particles and pathogens from the lungs, continuously clears the airway as this essential first line of defense.2,3 Given the constant challenge posed by environmental exposures and the potential for damage to this protective barrier, it is imperative that scientists study the effects of these exposures on the airway epithelium. Our accessible exposure system has the potential to significantly advance our understanding of the health impacts of environmental exposures.

To facilitate our critical research on the health impacts of diverse environmental exposures, the exposure chamber described here provides researchers with an accessible and affordable tool to study these exposures in vitro and in vivo. For in vitro studies, airway basal stem cells need to be grown in air-liquid interface cultures using appropriate growth and differentiation medium prior to placing them in the exposure chamber. When using the exposure chamber for in vivo studies, mice need to be acclimated to the exposure chamber for a few days before starting the exposures to reduce stress. This versatile chamber can be used to investigate the effects of various environmental exposures, including combustible cigarette smoke, a major contributor to debilitating respiratory diseases such as Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), asthma, and Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF).4,5,6 It can also be used to study e-cigarettes, which have been shown to induce lung inflammation comparable to asthma or bronchitis.7 Woodfire smoke (WFS), whose components, such as particulate matter, can exacerbate respiratory diseases like asthma.8,9

All research involving human tissues or animals requires prior approval. Researchers must secure all necessary approvals and permissions from their respective Institutional Review Board (IRB) for human subjects research and/or Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) for animal research. The procedures described herein were conducted under approvals from the IRB and Animal Research Committee (ARC) specific to UCLA. Readers intending to replicate these methods are therefore obligated to obtain their own institutional approvals before commencing any experiments.

Innovation

The high cost of commercially available environmental exposure systems can be a significant financial barrier to research. This protocol overcomes this barrier by providing an inexpensive alternative exposure system that can be built in the lab for under $300 which can be used with affordable consumables. The protocol includes accessible building instructions designed for researchers without an engineering background featuring simple wiring diagrams and a ready-to-use Arduino code. The exposure chamber is highly flexible and can be adapted to use with combustible cigarettes, electronic cigarettes, or woodfire cones. Moreover, the Arduino code can be modified allowing for easy adjustment of parameters such as puff duration and frequency to simulate diverse exposures. The combination of low-cost, simplicity and adaptability in our exposure system is an important improvement that broadens participation in critical environmental exposure research.

const int relayPin = 7;

// Or whichever pin you connected

// Experimental user-configurable parameters:

// How many puffs before it stops

const int numRepeats = 3;

// Duration of each puff in milliseconds

const int puffDurationMs = 3000;

// Wait time between puffs in milliseconds

const int waitTimeMs = 6000;

void setup() {

// Initialize the relay pin as an output.

pinMode(relayPin, OUTPUT);

// Initially turn the pump OFF (relay deactivated).

// HIGH turns the pump ON and LOW turns it OFF (assuming a normally-open relay configuration).

digitalWrite(relayPin, LOW);

// Run the puff sequence.

for (int counter = 0; counter < numRepeats; ++counter) {

// Turn the pump ON for the puff.

digitalWrite(relayPin, HIGH);

// Wait for the duration of the puff.

delay(puffDurationMs);

// Turn the pump OFF.

digitalWrite(relayPin, LOW);

// Wait for the specified time before the next puff.

delay(waitTimeMs);

}

}

// The loop() function is intentionally left empty because

// the puff sequence only needs to run once in setup().

}

// Experimental user-configurable parameters:

// How many puffs before it purges

const int numRepeats = 3;

// Duration of each puff in milliseconds

const int puffDurationMs = 3000;

// Wait time between puffs in milliseconds

const int waitTimeMs = 17000;

// Duration of air purge in milliseconds

const int purgeDurationMs = 20000;

void setup() {

// Initialize the relay pins as outputs.

pinMode(exposureRelayPin, OUTPUT);

pinMode(airRelayPin, OUTPUT);

// Initially turn the pump OFF (relay deactivated).

digitalWrite(exposureRelayPin, LOW);

digitalWrite(airRelayPin, LOW);

}

void loop() {

// Run the puff sequence.

for (int counter = 0; counter < numRepeats; ++counter) {

// Turn the exposure pump ON for the puff.

digitalWrite(exposureRelayPin, HIGH);

// Wait for the duration of the puff.

delay(puffDurationMs);

// Turn the exposure pump OFF.

digitalWrite(exposureRelayPin, LOW);

// Wait for the specified time before the next puff.

delay(waitTimeMs);

}

// Run the purge sequence.

// Turn the air pump ON for purging.

digitalWrite(airRelayPin, HIGH);

// Wait for the duration of the purge.

delay(purgeDurationMs);

// Turn the air pump OFF.

digitalWrite(airRelayPin, LOW);

}

Expected outcomes

For the in vitro studies, we isolated human airway basal stem cells (ABSCs) from bronchial tissue from three healthy donors. These ABSCs were cultured under submerged conditions in proliferation media for 5 days, before air liquid interface (ALI) conditions were initiated using differentiation media, as previously described.10,11,12 We compared the effects of combustible cigarettes smoke (CS), unflavored e-cigs (vegetable glycerin propylene glycol 30:70 ratio with 5% nicotine) and mint flavored electronic cigarettes (e-cigs) vapors to room air control and demonstrated their effects on keratin 5 (KRT5) expressing ABSCs. ABSCs are the major driver of injury repair in the airway, and their activation reflects that the repair process has been initiated, and the cells are actively responding to the damage. Aerosol treatments started on day 14 post initiation of ALI and continued to day 24 at which point the cells were fixed and immunostained for KRT5. While ABSCs grown in ALI culture reach a fully differentiated state at day 21 post ALI initiation, we initiated the exposure at day 14 to evaluate the effects of the exposure on the epithelium as it is actively going through the differentiation process. We optimized our aerosol exposure time and duration to obtain a dose that allowed us to examine the effects of e-cig aerosol exposure on ABSCs and mucociliary cells with minimal cell death (data not shown). Our optimized aerosol exposures consisted of three e-cig puffs of 3 s each, with a 5-min exposure time, twice a day for 10 days. We also examined the effects of WFS exposure on KRT5 expressing ABSCs. ABSCs were cultured under submerged conditions in proliferation media for 5 days then were fully differentiated at ALI for 21 days. Initiating the exposure at this time point allowed us to see the effect of WFS on a fully differentiated surface epithelium. ALI cultures were exposed to pine bark combustion (WFS) 3 min per day for 5 days for a total WFS exposure of 15 min. On the final day, the cells were fixed and immunostained for KRT5. ALI cultures showed an increase in ABSCs after WFS exposure compared to room air controls (Figure 5B).

The exposure time points chosen in our experiments on the ALI cultures were carefully decided after multiple optimization pilot experiments were conducted. We wanted to ensure maximum effect on the cells without inducing major cell death. We suggest that researchers optimize their protocols based on the cell types and conditions they.

For the in vivo studies, 6–10-week-old male and female TCF/Lef:H2B/GFP mice were acclimated to the exposure chamber for a 5-day period by gradually increasing their time in the chamber up to the 45 min used for the actual exposure time. Mice were exposed 5 days a week to combustible cigarettes smoke (CS), unflavored e-cigs (vegetable glycerin, propylene glycol 30:70 ratio with 5% nicotine), or room air as control. The CS exposure regimen consisted of two sessions of 7 cigarettes each with at least 30 min break in between. The unflavored e-cigs exposure regimen consisted of two 45-min sessions with at least 30 min break in between. The experiment lasted for 6-weeks total at which point the mice were euthanized and tracheas were removed, fixed and processed for H&E staining.

While humans have submucosal glands throughout the trachea and bronchi, mice have a more restricted pattern. They are primarily concentrated in the proximal trachea. Following the weeks of exposure, the H&E staining shows expansion and enlargement of the SMGs with CS exposure compared to room air control, and to a lesser extent unflavored e-cig exposure (Figure 5C).

Limitations

One of the limitations of the exposure chamber is the absence of real-time air quality monitoring. While the system effectively delivers controlled volumes of smoke or vapor, it lacks the ability to directly measure key air quality parameters within the chamber during exposure. Specifically, the setup does not incorporate sensors for temperature, humidity, gas concentration, and particulate matter quantifications. These measurements are supplied with more sophisticated but expensive commercially available exposure systems that include integrated sensors. The cost and complexity of such systems can be prohibitive. The cost of buying components to build our exposure system comes under $300 USD, which is considerably cheaper than the thousands of dollars cost of more sophisticated systems (Table 1). The exposure chamber is adaptable and can be used in conjunction with sensors and instruments depending on the experimental needs. A particle counter instrument can be connected in-line with the pump where the air is passed through the instrument before it enters the chamber, or the instrument can be connected to the chamber where it can sample air from the inside at set intervals for analysis. Temperature and gas sensors can be incorporated into the chamber through drilled holes in the lid. This will allow constant measurement of those parameters inside the chamber. Moreover, the nicotine content of the air inside the chamber can be measured by protocols utilizing a gas chromatograph.13

In our studies, when studying e-cig products, we utilized a Juul device. However, other e-cig products can be used as long as they do not require the user to press a button for turning them on during the puff. As long as the e-cig device is designed to activate by sensing the suction through the mouthpiece, the device will activate when connected to the pump in the exposure system. It is important to find a well-fitted adapter to connect the device to the pump, and we have found that plastic pipette tips can be cut and utilized for this use case. In addition, other nicotine products such as heated tobacco products (HTP) can also be used with our exposure chamber.

A key limitation of the described exposure system, particularly in its application to in vivo studies with mice, is the use of a whole-body exposure regimen. Whole-body exposure includes exposure though inhalation, skin contact, and potentially ingestion (through animal grooming). Additionally, it is often less expensive and allows for higher throughput (more animals can be exposed simultaneously) compared to nose-only exposure.

While some research questions might call for specifically a nose-only exposure regimen, it requires specialized equipment and can be more stressful for the animals due to restraint. The current whole-body exposure setup can be modified for nose-only exposure by incorporating commercially available or custom-made nose-only restraints within the exposure chamber. Researchers must carefully weigh the scientific benefits from this form of exposure against the potential stress to the animals and must obtain all necessary institutional approvals before conducting any animal experiments.
