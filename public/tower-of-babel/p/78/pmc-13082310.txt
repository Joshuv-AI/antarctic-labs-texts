Assessing the role of foliar habit on nutrient losses in a sub‐Antarctic forest
Authors: Frida I Piper, Alex Fajardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiogeochemical cycles drive global biodiversity and plant productivity. In terrestrial ecosystems, nutrient losses through plants' litter are a critical component of these biogeochemical cycles. In forests, nutrient losses are thought to be higher in deciduous angiosperm species than in evergreen conifers. However, this premise of nutrient loss differentiation between contrasting leaf habits has not been evaluated in co‐occurring deciduous and evergreen broadleaved tree species. We conducted a high‐frequency monitoring study of nutrient losses in congeneric and sympatric broadleaved tree species with contrasting leaf habits. We collected litter periodically for three years from 979 co‐occurring trees of the deciduous Nothofagus pumilio and Nothofagus antarctica and the evergreen Nothofagus dombeyi in a sub‐Antarctic forest of southern Chile. We estimated and compared foliar habits' litterfall biomass (senescent and green leaves and fruits), nutrient return through litterfall's nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) contents, and synchronization between litterfall mass and nutrient concentrations for each species. Annual litter biomass was higher in the two deciduous species (on average 2.01 t ha−1) than in the evergreen species (1.63 t ha−1), with senescent leaves representing >90% of litter in the three species. Litter N and P concentrations were significantly lower in the evergreen species. Thus, deciduous species had significantly higher N (15.82 kg ha−1) and P (1.55 kg ha−1) annual returns than the evergreen species (N: 8.20 kg ha−1, P: 0.83 kg ha−1) through litter of senescent leaves. A synchronization between maximum litterfall production and minimum litter concentrations for N but not for P was found in both leaf habits, although more consistently for deciduous than for the evergreen. Nutrient losses through litter were higher in deciduous than in evergreen Nothofagus species, demonstrating a nutrient loss differentiation between contrasting leaf habits of co‐occurring broadleaved tree species. This study deepens our understanding of plant nutrient strategies associated with leaf habit and underscores the importance of diverse mechanisms of nutrient loss when evaluating forest nutrient dynamics.Keywords: biogeography, leaf habit, litter, Nothofagus, nutrient resorption proficiency, nutrient return rate, Patagonia, temperate forest

INTRODUCTION

Biogeochemical cycles are major determinants of global biodiversity and plant productivity (Vitousek et al., 1997). In terrestrial ecosystems, the nutrient inputs from vegetation into soils through litter constitute a key step of these biogeochemical cycles, showing nonetheless a high geographical variability (Chabot & Hicks, 1982). Leaf habit has been long recognized as a main driver of this variability (Chabot & Hicks, 1982; Givnish, 2002; McGlone et al., 2004; Wright et al., 2004). Deciduousness is considered an output associated with a freezing and drought avoidance mechanism that reduces respiratory losses when the potential for carbon (C) gain is low (Givnish, 2002; McGlone et al., 2004). To compensate for a shorter leaf lifespan, deciduous species produce leaves with higher instantaneous photosynthetic rates per unit mass than evergreens (Chabot & Hicks, 1982; Givnish, 2002; Wright et al., 2004), for which they require ca. 20%–60% higher concentrations of nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) (Aerts, 1996; Chabot & Hicks, 1982; Fajardo & Siefert, 2016; Piper et al., 2019; Piper & Fajardo, 2014; Silla & Escudero, 2003; Wright et al., 2004). With flimsy, highly productive leaves, deciduous species can achieve higher growth rates, but at the cost of high nutrient requirements because resorption efficiency does not differ between leaf habits as much as the nutrient concentration of mature leaves does (Aerts, 1996; Killingbeck, 1996; Tian, Du, Tang, & Peñuelas, 2025; Tian, Du, Tang, & Xia, 2025; Vergutz et al., 2012). As such, deciduous species are predicted to undergo faster nutrient turnover and, consequently, to be less dominant at nutrient‐poor soil areas (Aerts, 1995, 1996; Givnish, 2002; Goldberg, 1982). Evergreens, in contrast, reduce the amortized costs of leaf construction, nutrient acquisition, and lower instantaneous photosynthetic rates via a longer photosynthetic period associated with their longer leaf longevity and a longer nutrient residence time (i.e., lower nutrient turnover) (Castorena et al., 2022; Chabot & Hicks, 1982; Kikuzawa, 1991; Ye et al., 2022). The more conservative C and nutrient economy of evergreens is thought to come at the expense of a lower growth rate, which puts them at a competitive disadvantage against deciduous species in productive sites (Aerts, 1995). Accordingly, a recent study reports that climate and soil characteristics are the main predictors of the global distinctive distribution of deciduous and evergreen species (Ma et al., 2023). Although the previous arguments are consistent with the geographical segregation of the two leaf habits, deciduous and evergreen broadleaved species are often sympatric (Chen et al., 2023; Löiez & Piper, 2021; Piper & Fajardo, 2014; Tomlinson et al., 2014; Ye et al., 2022). Whether co‐occurring deciduous and evergreen broadleaved species differ in nutrient turnover as in allopatric deciduous and evergreen species remains unclear.

Comparisons between allopatric deciduous broadleaved and evergreen conifer tree species indicate slower nutrient turnover in the latter (Chabot & Hicks, 1982; Givnish, 2002; Neumann et al., 2018). These studies also reported similar annual litterfall biomass production between leaf habits, or even higher in deciduous species, along with higher litter nutrient concentrations in deciduous than in evergreen species. However, the allopatric distribution of deciduous broadleaved and evergreen conifers could affect the nutrient turnover comparison, as leaf nutrient concentrations vary with the site‐specific nutrient availability (Fajardo & Piper, 2015). Additionally, evergreen conifer forests have slower decomposition rates and lower soil nutrient availability than evergreen broadleaved forests (Satti et al., 2003; Zhang et al., 2008), raising doubts on whether patterns driven by phylogenetically distant species can also be expected in more closely related species. Therefore, our current understanding of nutrient dynamics in different leaf habits appears to be impaired by the potentially confounding influence of site and phylogeny. Comparisons of litterfall biomass production and nutrient turnover between species of contrasting leaf habits but closely related phylogenetically and growing at the same site could help to elucidate the role of leaf habit alone on nutrient turnover.

Biomass allocation to foliage is generally higher in evergreen species than in deciduous species (Neumann et al., 2018; Piper & Fajardo, 2014; Poorter et al., 2012), and this single characteristic could also increase nutrient demands in evergreens (Lusk & Contreras, 1999). Given that foliage is subjected to more rapid turnover than woody tissues, leaves represent an important fraction of annual biomass allocation (King, 1991). Leaves also represent the most significant sink of annual nutrient allocation in plants, since they concentrate a higher fraction of mineral nutrients, especially N and P, relative to other vegetative organs (Palacio et al., 2018; Piper, 2020; Tian, Du, Tang, & Xia, 2025). As foliage turnover represents the main nutrient loss for woody perennials (Berendse et al., 1987; Chabot & Hicks, 1982; Miller et al., 1976), it follows that the development and maintenance of foliage biomass in evergreens could also mean that they require as much, or perhaps more, nutrients than deciduous species. This may be exacerbated in evergreen broadleaved compared to evergreen conifers, given the higher nutrient concentrations of the former. Although the longer leaf lifespan may cause nutrients to turnover more slowly in evergreens than in deciduous species, few evergreen broadleaved species appear to have leaves older than 4–5 years (Reich et al., 1995). This leaf lifespan could not be sufficient to compensate for the higher nutrient demands associated with a higher foliage biomass, and therefore evergreen broadleaved may end up having similar nutrient requirements as deciduous species.

Litter production in evergreen species can take several months and follow smooth dynamics (Li et al., 2005; Staelens et al., 2011). During this period, maximum litterfall may not concur with minimal litter nutrient concentration (Niinemets & Tamm, 2005; Wang et al., 2022). An earlier litterfall has been found to be positively related to nutrient concentrations in litter (Niinemets & Tamm, 2005). Thus, a longer period of litterfall in evergreens may mean that a significant fraction of their annual litterfall is produced before the minimal litter concentration is achieved. As nutrient resorption and leaf shedding are sensitive to environmental cues like drought and low temperature (Dallstream & Piper, 2021; Marchin et al., 2010), determining whether deciduous or evergreen species vary in the synchronization of litterfall production and nutrient concentration requires considering the potentially great interannual variation in litter nutrient concentrations and litterfall. Studies accounting for such variability are scarce.

The general objective of this study was to quantify and compare the litter quantity (mass) and quality (nutrient concentrations) between tree species of contrasting leaf habits and similar leaf form. To accomplish this general objective, we used sympatric deciduous‐evergreen congeneric Nothofagus tree species, for which we monitored every 1–2 months the biomass losses of senescent and green leaves and fruits during three consecutive years using litter traps. We first quantified the annual rates of litterfall biomass and nutrient return into soil through litterfall. Second, we determined the synchronization between litterfall and nutrient concentrations in both leaf habits. When do deciduous and evergreen congeneric Nothofagus tree species shed their leaves the most? Does this happen when litter nutrient concentrations are at their minimum, as expected?

The fact that we worked with sympatric congeneric Nothofagus tree species of contrasting leaf habit—Nothofagus species of contrasting leaf habit commonly grow together in the southern Andes of South America (Diehl et al., 2008; Fajardo et al., 2013; Piper et al., 2019; Salas‐Eljatib, 2021)—growing in the same site represents a natural common garden experiment. This natural condition allowed us to show that any difference in litterfall biomass and nutrient content is solely due to foliar habit and not to site productivity (environment) or phylogeny (leaf form).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Research site and species description

The study was carried out within the Coyhaique National Reserve (45°59′ S and 71°52′ W, 650 m above sea level [asl]), Coyhaique Province, Patagonia (Chile). The mean annual and growing season (October–March) precipitation are 921 mm and 347 mm, respectively, and the mean annual and growing season temperatures are 8.0 and 11.2°C, respectively (Coyhaique National Reserve weather station, Dirección General de Aguas, 2002–2015, 400 m asl). The soil is derived from aeolian volcanic‐ash deposits, with 0.2% total N, 5% organic matter content, and 12.21 mg k−1 Olsen‐P (Piper et al., 2013). In this National Reserve, a large‐scale human‐induced fire occurred in 1950, which burned ca. 600 ha of old‐growth Nothofagus forests (Corporación Nacional Forestal CONAF, personal communication). A dense even‐aged, second‐growth forest of Nothofagus dombeyi (evergreen) and Nothofagus pumilio (deciduous) was established afterward, while Nothofagus antarctica (deciduous) was established at the downslope edge. This forest is ca. 70 years old (Fajardo, 2016; Fajardo & McIntire, 2010).

Given that leaf longevity in N. dombeyi can be slightly greater than two years (Lusk et al., 2011), we decided to conduct this study for 3 years to make a fair comparison with the deciduous Nothofagus in terms of leaf longevity. We also decided to include Nothofagus antarctica in this study. This is a deciduous treelet species that grows sparsely at the edge of the second‐growth forest (Fajardo et al., 2023). Similar edaphic and climatic conditions, along with the same age structure, make this forest ideal for a proper comparison of litter quantity and quality depending only on leaf habit, devoid of other spurious factors.

Experimental setup and leaf processing

Using maps of the Coyhaique National Reserve, we selected stands located in areas with a south aspect and a slope of 10%–20%. Once selected, within the mixed forest of N. dombeyi and N. pumilio (Figure 1), we set six parallel 75‐m‐long transects separated 30 m from one another, and within each transect we located six sampling points separated by 15 m. Two 60‐m long transects were set within the N. antarctica patch, where 11 sampling points were located. Thus, we ended up having 36 sampling points in the mixed forest and 11 sampling points in the N. antarctica stand. At each sampling point we installed litter traps, which consisted of a plastic box with a surface of 0.235 m2 (595 × 395 mm and 150 mm of depth) (Figure 1). We nailed to the ground four 35‐cm metal sticks to each of the four corners of the litter traps to avoid any displacement. Traps were completely covered inside with a plastic white 0.5‐mm mesh to avoid losses of fruits or small pieces of leaves through the holes of the plastic box (Figure 1). To trap litter at exactly the box surface, when needed we leveled the trap boxes to reach slope 0% using wood pieces and a level.

Litter traps were installed in January 2017, and successive litter collections were performed from April 2017 to April 2020. The frequency of litter collection was adjusted to the litter production and the inaccessibility to the forest due to snow. Thus, collections were more frequent in autumn and spring and less frequent in summer and winter. In total, we conducted 8, 9, 8, and 2 collections in 2017, 2018, 2019, and 2020, respectively. This rather high frequency of litter collection let us rely on the use of plastic boxes directly placed on the forest floor—instead of the most traditional way of using traps several cm above ground—which secured us to collect fresh litter devoid of decomposition. Similarly, the local mean low temperature precludes rapid decomposition of plant tissues (Zanne et al., 2022). At each collection time, litter was carefully collected from each box and placed in paper‐labeled bags and immediately brought to the laboratory, where it was separated into senescent leaves and green leaves and fruits, when necessary, per species. We incorporated in our study the green litter and fruits that arrived at the litter traps, as they are also part of the trees' nutrient losses. As litter was always in a fresh condition, the morphology of leaves and fruits conforming the litter could be visually identified at the species level based on distinctive morphological attributes of the three Nothofagus species (Rodríguez & Quezada, 2003). Species‐specific litter bags were dried at 70°C for three days in a force‐air oven (Memmert, Schwabach, Germany), and afterward, the plant material was weighted in a scale to 0.0001 g. From this, we obtained a value of dry mass litter (in grams) for each fraction (senescent and green leaves and fruits) and species and for each date of collection and per 0.235 m2. Dry litter of senescent and green leaves was ground in a ball mill (Restch MM200, Haan, Germany) for further chemical analyses.

Around each of the 46 litter traps, we set 100‐m2 circular plots (5.64 m of radius) where we tallied all the trees per species and measured the diamater at breast height (1.35 m) using a diameter tape. We assumed that all trees within plots contributed similarly with litterfall to the box. We measured the height of the five tallest trees of the plot using a clinometer (Laser Leica). With this, we computed basal area (BA, in square meters) per species and per plot, which was then scaled up to the hectare. With this inventory, we realized that 11, 4, and 11 litter traps belonged to pure N. dombeyi, N. pumilio, and N. antarctica plots, respectively; 19 litter traps belonged to mixed N. dombeyi–N. pumilio plots. In the one‐species plots, we found on average 2136 trees ha−1 and 68.045 m2 ha−1 of BA for N. dombeyi, 1800 trees ha−1 and 84.441 m2 ha−1 for N. pumilio, and 2660 trees ha−1 and 37.881 m2 ha−1 for N. antarctica. Mean height (SE) was 18 (0.3), 17 (0.3), and 8 (0.2) m for N. dombeyi, N. pumilio, and N. antarctica plots, respectively. For the one‐species plots, the litter contribution of each litter fraction of species was the total content of litter of a given fraction per box that was later scaled up to square meters (Equation 1).

Chemical analyses

Concentrations of N were determined with the Dumas method (Etheridge et al., 1998) using a combustion analyzer (LECO TruSpec Micro CHN, Centro de Investigación de Ecosistemas de la Patagonia, Coyhaique, Chile). Total P concentrations were determined according to the procedure of Murphy and Riley (1962). All concentrations were expressed per unit of dry litter mass (in milligrams per gram).

Data analysis

We conducted all data analyses using the statistical program R v.4.2 (R Development Core Team, 2024). First, we computed an annual litter contribution of each fraction per box and per species by summing the litter dry masses of all withdrawal dates per year (we consider April year 1 to March year 2 as a 1‐year period), and then we estimated a mean (with SE) annual litter dry mass per square meter per species, separately for senescent and green leaves and fruits. We computed N and P contents in the litter by multiplying the total content of litter per box (in grams per square meter) times the concentration of the element (in milligrams per gram). Like the annual litter dry mass per square meter per species, we estimated total annual N or P content in the litter per square meter per species. To evaluate the effects of the different species on annual litter production (and nutrient contents), we fitted a linear mixed model (LMM) using the R/NLME package (Pinheiro et al., 2021). To reveal the potential influences of outliers and extreme values, the fit of the model was evaluated with a graphical examination of the residuals and the fitted values.

Second, to determine the degree of synchrony between litterfall and litter nutrient concentration, we evaluated the relationship between litterfall at the different collection dates and the litter nutrient concentration (N and P) corresponding to each date (Piper & Fajardo, 2026b). For this, we used LMMs for each species and year, considering the litterfall as the response variable, the litter concentration of N (or P) as the fixed factor, and the transect as the random factor (with a similar model structure as above).

We finally computed the relative size of the effects of variables (i.e., effect size) using the R/EFFECTSIZE package (Ben Shachar et al., 2020) as a means of good practice to accomplish ecological inference (Kimmel et al., 2023). We computed the eta‐squared (η2) value (with CIs), which is a measure of the proportion of variation in the response variable that can be attributed to the independent variable. Eta‐squared (η2) values including 0 mean that the variable has no effect on the response variable, with η2 < 0.01 having a negligible effect, 0.01 ≤ η2 < 0.06 having a small effect, 0.06 ≤ η2 < 0.14 having an intermediate effect, and η2 ≥ 0.14 having a large effect on the response variable. We used 95% CIs to compare species means as a post hoc test, with nonoverlapping intervals indicating significant differences. We used this procedure instead of, for example, Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) because the CIs criterion works better with effect sizes.

RESULTS

Contribution of litterfall and nutrient concentrations to nutrient losses

Senescent leaves represented the main litterfall fraction across the three species, followed by green leaves and lastly fruits (Figure 2a–c). Mean annual litter production was higher in the two deciduous species (on average 2.01 ± 0.10 t ha−1) than in the evergreen species (1.63 ± 0.07 t ha−1), with senescent leaves accounting for more than 90% of total litter (93% ± 1.17%, 94% ± 1.24%, and 91% ± 1.90% of the total litter in N. antarctica, N. pumilio, and N. dombeyi, respectively, averaged for the three annual periods ± SEs) (Appendix S1: Figure S1). The litter of senescent leaves was significantly different among species (Table 1), particularly higher in the two deciduous species (1.89 ± 0.11 t ha−1 year−1) than in the evergreen Nothofagus (1.48 ± 0.73 t ha−1 year−1) (Appendix S1: Table S1). Although we found that the litter of senescent leaves varied significantly through years, this variation did not interact with the ranking of the species (Table 1).

The litter of green leaves was similar among species and it varied across years (Figure 2b, Table 1; Appendix S1: Table S1). Litterfall biomass of green leaves represented 7% of total litter (averaged for species and three years, Appendix S1: Figure S1), ranging from 8.3% to 5.2% for years 1 and 3, respectively. Approximately 50% of the litterfall biomass of green leaves in N. dombeyi (evergreen) was produced in wintertime (Appendix S1: Figure S2a–c). The fruit biomass production was significantly different among species (Table 1), being higher in the deciduous N. antarctica and similar between the other two species across years (Figure 2c; Appendix S1: Table S1 and Figure S2d–f). Overall, it represented 1.3% of the total litter in N. antarctica and less than 0.4% in the other two species (Appendix S1: Figure S1).

Mean annual litter N concentrations of senescent leaves varied among species but not across years (Table 1). Concentrations were similar between the two deciduous Nothofagus tree species and ca. twofold significantly higher than in the evergreen species (Appendix S1: Table S1; Figure 3a). Mean annual litter P concentration of senescent leaves differed significantly among the three species (Table 1), with N. pumilio > N. antarctica > N. dombeyi (Appendix S1: Table S1), which proved to be a very consistent species ranking across years (Table 1; Figure 3b). As a result of both a greater litterfall and higher litter nutrient concentrations of senescent leaves, cumulative annual litter N and P contents were higher in the deciduous than in the evergreen species (Table 1; Figure 3c,d; Appendix S1: Table S1 and Figure S3). On average, litter content in deciduous species was 15.82 kg ha−1 and 1.55 kg ha−1 for N and P, respectively, and 8.20 kg ha−1 and 0.83 kg ha−1 for the evergreen. Green leaf N concentrations were 18.6 ± 0.7 mg g−1 in N. antarctica, 11.3 ± 0.3 mg g−1 in N. dombeyi, and 18.9 ± 0.4 mg g−1 in N. pumilio, whereas green leaf P concentrations were 1.51 ± 0.09 mg g−1 in N. antarctica, 1.04 ± 0.05 mg g−1 in N. dombeyi, and 1.66 ± 0.04 mg g−1 in N. pumilio.

Synchronization between litterfall mass production of senescent leaves and nutrient concentrations

Litterfall mass of senescent leaves, the main fraction of litterfall, was negatively and significantly related to litter N concentrations in the two deciduous Nothofagus species for the three years under evaluation (Table 2; Figure 4a–c; Appendix S1: Table S2). In contrast, in the evergreen Nothofagus species, the relationship between litterfall mass and its litter N concentration was negative for years 1 and 2 but nonexistent for year 3 (Table 2; Figure 4a–c; Appendix S1: Table S2). Litterfall mass was only negatively and significantly related to litter P concentration in the two deciduous species for year 1 (Table 2; Figure 4d; Appendix S1: Table S2). In N. antarctica, the relationship was also negatively significant in year 2, while in N. pumilio, the synchronization switched from negative to positive for years 2 and 3 (Figure 4d,f; Appendix S1: Table S2). The evergreen N. dombeyi exhibited no relationship between litterfall mass and litter P concentration (Table 2; Figure 4d–f; Appendix S1: Table S2).

DISCUSSION

Litterfall mass and nutrient return through litter in deciduous and evergreen Nothofagus

Our study demonstrates that deciduous species still undergo higher nutrient losses through litter of senescent leaves than evergreens when both leaf habits co‐occur. In our comparison of sympatric congeneric species, we found significantly higher nutrient return rates through litter of senescent leaves—the main litter's fraction—in the two deciduous Nothofagus tree species than in the evergreen N. dombeyi. Overall, this pattern is confirmatory of previous findings when allopatric deciduous broadleaved and evergreen conifers were compared (Chabot & Hicks, 1982). N and P annual returns through the litter of senescent leaves were, respectively, 45% and 49% higher in the two deciduous species than in the evergreen Nothofagus species. These figures were driven by both higher litter nutrient concentrations and, to a lesser extent, higher annual production of litterfall of senescent leaves, which is in agreement with previous evidence (Aerts & Chapin, 2000; Chabot & Hicks, 1982). Although some studies have predicted a change in leaf habit preponderance in response to current and future soil nutrient availability (Ma et al., 2023), our results show that these predictions are unlikely to happen in the broadleaf forests of the southern Andes. By assessing coexisting Nothofagus species with contrasting leaf habits, our study controls for potential influences of phylogeny and site on the nutrient return rates to soil through litter. Yet, our comparison is restricted to a single genus and site, and generalizations to other species and study areas, particularly of different site productivity, are uncertain.

We found that green leaves represented up to 8.3% of the total litterfall across species. In the evergreen species, these losses largely occurred when deciduous species were leafless, indicating that there is a critical risk of high nutrient losses caused by winter hazards like winds and snow in wintertime by being evergreen. Although the avoidance of winter and its damaging effects on leaves has been largely recognized as one of the advantages of being deciduous, it is surprising the lack of documentation on the effects of winter's disturbance on evergreens' leaves. Assuming that the green leaves lost in wintertime by the evergreen species had a similar N concentration as green mature leaves collected in summer (Hevia et al., 1999; Tian, Du, Tang, & Peñuelas, 2025; Tian, Du, Tang, & Xia, 2025), which in our assessment corresponded to 11.3 mg g−1 (see Results ), winter losses of green leaves experienced by N. dombeyi only in year 1 (mean: 19.6 g m−2, Figure 2b) would sum up to 0.22 g m−2 of N, which is 34% of the annual N losses by senescent leaves (Figure 3c). By contrast, N lost through litter of green leaves in summer in the deciduous N. antarctica would represent only 12.7% of the annual N losses by senescent leaves. To lose a third of the N lost through senescent leaves by common disturbances like winter windstorms in evergreens constitutes a vastly underestimated and unrecognized waste of N. If this loss occurs frequently, as seems to be the case from our 3‐year assessment, it is likely to increase the nutrient requirements of evergreens. However, deciduous Nothofagus may be periodically subjected to losses that we did not quantify in this study, such as massive insect defoliations during the growing season (e.g., Piper & Fajardo, 2014). Besides, summer climatic hazards caused higher defoliation in deciduous than in evergreen Nothofagus (e.g., year 2, Appendix S1: Figure S2). Lastly, fruit loss was identified as a significant source of litterfall in the deciduous species N. antarctica, although there is no information on the nutrient content of fruits in N. antarctica. Since the basal area per ha was lower in N. antarctica than in the other two species, our estimation of litterfall for N. antarctica could have been underestimated. Overall, fractions of litter other than senescent leaves seem to significantly contribute to nutrient turnover.

Are maximum litterfall production and minimum nutrient litter concentration synchronized?

Our assessment of nutrient returns based on a high temporal resolution of litter collection allowed us to quantify the nutrient losses across the entire year, which resulted in great relevance for both leaf habits, but especially for the evergreen species. First, we found partial support for our expectation that maximum litterfall production of senescent leaves synchronized with the lowest nutrient concentrations. We found that the maximum litterfall production of senescent leaves synchronized with the lowest N concentrations more strongly and consistently in the deciduous species than in the evergreen species. However, we found no synchronization when P was considered. This result suggests that the evergreen species has a lower optimization of its N economy than the deciduous species, such that it wastes more N than the minimum corresponding to the highest N resorption. The lack of a relationship between litterfall of senescent leaves and P concentrations suggests that these species (both deciduous and evergreen) are generally N‐limited, but not P‐limited, in agreement with a previous study (e.g., Fajardo & Piper, 2015). A weaker synchronization in the evergreen species leads necessarily to increases in nutrient losses, and higher nutrient losses can also relate to higher nutrient demands. This may reduce, in turn, the difference in nutrient requirements between the two leaf habits, which otherwise could have been even greater. Second, this pattern of weaker synchronization in the evergreen Nothofagus could be a consequence of increasing self‐shedding or environmental hazards. For example, winter storms can cause leaf shedding before maximum leaf nutrient resorption occurs.

How do deciduous and evergreen Nothofagus tree species co‐occur despite their contrasting annual nutrient losses through senescent leaves? One potential explanation is that the assumed higher C gain by deciduous species, which, in theory, is associated with a faster nutrient turnover and a higher annual nutrient loss, may not necessarily translate into faster growth. This means that although deciduous species have higher nutrient demands and C gains than evergreens, the C surplus may not be allocated to growth. Deciduous broadleaved species allocate a larger fraction of their C gain to storage in comparison to evergreen broadleaved species (Piper, 2020; Piper & Fajardo, 2014; Tomlinson et al., 2013), and only a small fraction of the C stored is remobilized for seasonal growth in deciduous species (Hoch et al., 2003; Löiez & Piper, 2021; Palacio et al., 2018; Piper, 2020). What is more, several studies have shown that deciduous broadleaved species grow even slower than co‐occurring broadleaved evergreens (Fajardo, 2016; Tomlinson et al., 2014). Greater C storage at the expense of growth in deciduous than in evergreen Nothofagus species may confer advantages to tolerate severe defoliations caused by insect outbreaks (Fuenzalida et al., 2019; Piper & Fajardo, 2014), provided that deciduous Nothofagus are more susceptible than evergreen Nothofagus to this type of disturbance (Piper et al., 2019).

A second explanation has to do with the slow litter decomposition of the evergreen species and with how this condition may indirectly depress the potential growth of deciduous species (Berendse, 1994), thus acting as a mechanism promoting species coexistence. The structural and biochemical characteristics that are necessary to prolong the life of evergreen leaves by increasing physical resistance and reducing palatability (high leaf mass per area—LMA—and low N content) to herbivores may also influence the decomposition rate and nutrient cycling (Cornwell et al., 2008) not only of evergreens but also of co‐occurring deciduous species. This may have consequences for competitive interactions with other species (Aerts & Chapin, 2000). Litter decomposition rates in general are positively correlated with N content and negatively with LMA (Cornwell et al., 2008). Accordingly, deciduous species in general have higher litter decomposability than evergreen species (Aerts & Chapin, 2000; Piper et al., 2024). Where N. dombeyi co‐occurs with faster growing but more nutrient‐demanding species, like N. antarctica or N. pumilio, the low decomposability of N. dombeyi leaf litter could reduce soil nutrient availability through immobilization, possibly depressing the vigor of its competitors and increasing the probability of long‐term site retention by N. dombeyi (Aerts & Chapin, 2000; Berendse, 1994). Indeed, although the litter of deciduous Nothofagus species decomposes faster than N. dombeyi's, the litter decomposition of deciduous Nothofagus species is slower in microsites dominated by evergreen Nothofagus (Vivanco & Austin, 2008), or other evergreen angiosperms (Piper et al., 2024). By contrast, the litter of the evergreen Nothofagus decomposed faster in microsites of mixed deciduous‐evergreen Nothofagus than in microsites of N. dombeyi (Vivanco & Austin, 2008). Thus, our results could underscore the importance of species composition and leaf habit in shaping litter decomposition processes.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study brings novel results regarding litterfall and nutrient return rates from a remote forest area of the Southern Hemisphere. First, our study system is unique, as deciduous and evergreen congeneric and even‐aged tree species co‐occur. Thus, our conclusions are devoid of spurious phylogenetic comparisons, which makes it difficult to discern litterfall dynamics from phylogenetically conserved traits. Second, we offer confirmatory and robust evidence that nutrient return rates are higher in deciduous than in evergreens, which has far‐reaching consequences for ecosystem‐level N and P cycles. These differences in nutrient returns between leaf habits persisted, even though the evergreen species experienced similar nutrient losses as the deciduous species through rarely reported mechanisms, such as the loss of green leaves. We were able to identify and quantify these mechanisms thanks to our highly frequent and long‐term litter collection. Lastly, we show that differential nutrient return rates between deciduous and evergreen do not necessarily occur when these leaf habits are geographically segregated.
