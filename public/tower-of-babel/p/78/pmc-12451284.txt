Management of significant pleural effusion at ovarian cancer diagnosis: Outcomes of triage to neoadjuvant chemotherapy without thoracic assessment
Authors: Stuart A. Ostby, Chiara Ainio, Conway Xu, Kelly H. Bruce, Matthew S. Block, William A. Cliby, Amanika Kumar, Carrie L. Langstraat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Clinical Stage IVA ovarian cancer is associated with poor performance status.•Most patients have additional factors to support neoadjuvant chemotherapy use.•Complete gross resection in the abdomen was associated with > 5-year overall survival.•Any residual disease was high-risk and should prompt consideration of maintenance therapy.

Background

Management of ovarian cancer presenting with moderate to large pleural effusion is a challenge and has been correlated with the presence of macroscopic thoracic disease in approximately 70 % of patients (Diaz et al., 2010, Di Guilmi et al., 2016). The optimal management strategy for clinical Stage IVA patients is debated. While abdominal cytoreduction has consistently demonstrated improved survival outcomes in advanced ovarian cancer, it is less clear if that is sufficient when thoracic disease is suspected (Winter et al., 2008, Aletti et al., 2006, Kahn et al., 2023). The decision to pursue primary debulking surgery (PDS) versus neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) can be clarified with video-assisted thorascopic surgery (VATS), particularly by excluding macroscopic thoracic disease or by resection of limited disease (Chi et al., 2004).

The prognosis for clinical Stage IVA disease is generally informed by outcomes across all advanced stages (Narasimhulu et al., 2021, Gaillard et al., 2025, Vergote et al., 2018). Merits for performing VATS are described as prognostic, useful in sequencing treatment, and possibly therapeutic. First, by beginning with VATS, staging is more accurately determined (Kahn et al., 2023). Second, when there is thoracic disease at initial staging, thoracic disease at recurrence is more frequently observed (Larish et al., 2020, Mert et al., 2018, Lopes et al., 2019). Third, when only limited thoracic disease is encountered, resection may be achieved. Fourth, this approach has been associated with a median OS of 81.3 months (Kahn et al., 2023). However, these described benefits are based upon a limited number of patients with moderate to large pleural effusions at diagnosis. The evidence that VATS extends a therapeutic benefit to clinical Stage IVA patients is unknown. If considering a related clinical challenge, cardiophrenic lymph node metastases, the data are mixed and inconsistent for benefit (Lee et al., 2018, Laasik et al., 2019, Jamieson et al., 2017). Therefore, the best initial strategy when patients present with clinical Stage IVA disease is unknown.

At our institution, patients with moderate to large effusions are recommended triage to NACT or diagnostic evaluation by VATS. Prior to NACT or debulking surgery, this decision is standardized and based upon patient risk factors and the ability to resect abdominal disease. Patient risk factors favoring NACT administration supersedes diagnostic VATS performance, however VATS may be useful to evaluate the timing of IDS. The goal of this study was to evaluate the recurrence and survival outcomes of clinical Stage IVA disease in consecutive cases.

Methods

Identification of the target population

Cohort development and evaluation followed institutional review board approval and included only those consenting to participation in research. Patients who underwent surgery between 2016 and 2021 were included if they had a moderate to large pleural effusion(s) without evidence of a clear Stage IVB metastasis per radiologist reports. The definition used for clinical Stage IVA disease is less precise than surgical assessment and did not exclude patients whose imaging had possibly involved cardiophrenic lymph nodes or pleural disease as most patients with moderate-large pleural effusions have been described as having macroscopic disease. The cohort was developed by a nurse clinical abstractor with two physicians verifying subsequent accuracy of radiologic Stage IVA disease (SAO, CA). Clinical characteristics, the total count of factors leading to NACT recommendation (Narasimhulu et al., 2020), surgical outcomes, and oncologic treatments were abstracted. Chemotherapy response scores (CRS) were retrospectively determined by expert pathologists using pre-existing, stored specimens from IDS (Böhm et al., 2015). Homologous recombination testing was based upon next generation sequencing test results, recorded albumin levels were from the time of deciding NACT versus a primary surgical approach, and ECOG performance status used in statistical calculations was based upon the initial gynecologic oncologist assessment before treatment.

Descriptive statistics were used to describe patient characteristics. Due to small group sizes, non-parametric summaries with median, first and third quartiles, and Fisher’s exact test were used. Survival was descriptive, performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and presented as a Swimmer’s plot for transparency and to avoid statistical testing across heterogeneous groups. Univariate and bivariate analysis was performed to analyze factors initially present at first gynecologic oncology assessment to explore factors associated with OS. Statistical software use included Stata 18.0 (StataCorp. 2023. College Station, TX) and R for all analyses (Valero-Mora, 2010).

Results

Patient factors at first consultation

During the study interval, 31 patients presented with moderate to large pleural effusions without radiologic evidence of Stage IVB disease (i.e., Stage IVA), and all were initially treated with NACT. The median age was 68.0 years (IQR 63.1, 74.8) and BMI 26.3 kg/m2 (IQR 25.1, 29.3). Moderate to large pleural effusion was recorded as the only reason for triage to NACT in 3/31 (9.7 %), whereas most patients had additional institutional factors favoring NACT and were as follows: age ≥80 years in 3 (9.7 %), ECOG performance status ≥2 in 8 (25.7 %), newly diagnosed thromboembolism in 11 (35.5 %), albumin <3.5 g/dL in 14 (45.2 %), or a combination of age and anticipated high surgical complexity (Table 1) (Aletti et al., 2007). In addition to moderate-large pleural effusion, 14 (45.2 %) patients had ≥2 additional factors favoring NACT. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy course and whole cohort outcomes

Genetic testing identified a BRCA mutation in 3/27 (11.1 %). There were 8 (25.8 %) patients who did not receive IDS, 4 of whom died prior to cycle 3 of NACT, 3 who were deemed unfit for surgery, and 1 with a BRCA mutation who desired chemotherapy only. After IDS in the abdomen, 14/23 (60.9 %) had CGR whereas 7/23 (30.4 %) had 0–5 mm residual disease (RD), 2/23 (8.7 %) had 6–10 mm RD, and 1/23 (4.3 %) ≥1 cm RD. The median number of adjuvant chemotherapy cycles was 3 (range 2–6). Homologous recombination testing was performed in 16/23 (69.6 %) patients who underwent surgery and identified 8/16 (50.0 %) as deficient. Maintenance chemotherapy was used in 5/31 (16.1 %): bevacizumab for the patient with suboptimal resection and PARP inhibitor in 4 (12.9 %). Median follow-up of 49.1 months, 30/31 (96.8 %) experienced disease recurrence, and 22/31 (71.0 %) were deceased at the time of final analysis. No patients were lost to follow-up. Median OS for the whole cohort was 30.6 months (Fig. 1). Patients received neoadjuvant platinum-taxane chemotherapy for a median of 4 cycles (range 1–9). Pleural effusions resolved in 23/31 (74.2 %) after NACT alone. Of the 8 with pleural effusions after NACT, 4 underwent IDS in the abdomen. One patient had CGR in the abdomen (OS of 91.4 months) versus 3 who had RD (OS range 12.6 – 48.5 months). When pleural effusions did not resolve and patients had performance status ≥2, OS was shorter (range 1.6 to 13.3 months). There were 7 (22.6 %) who underwent an additional procedure after NACT: 6 had diagnostic VATS, 1 thoracentesis, and pleurX catheter placement occurred in 3.

Only one patient had evidence of progressive disease in the thorax without abdominal disease as the first site of disease recurrence. At first consultation for ovarian cancer treatment, the disease was predominantly identified in the upper abdomen, minimal in the pelvis, and determined by imaging to be unresectable. The patient received three cycles of NACT but did not have resolution of the pleural effusion. Next, VATS was used to exclude macroscopic thoracic disease and all pleural biopsies were negative. Next, the patient had IDS in the abdomen and achieved CGR. Following an uncomplicated post-operative course, the patient completed three additional cycles of carboplatin and paclitaxel. Re-staging demonstrated new pulmonary nodules, a new small effusion and an increase in CA-125. The decision was to provide two additional cycles and then evaluate the durability of treatment. Three months later, new neurologic symptoms led to identification of leptomeningeal disease with new, suspicious pulmonary nodules and the patient was deceased three weeks later.

Characteristics of those with ≥5-year overall survival

Median follow-up time was 49.1 months with 22/31 (71.0 %) patients being deceased at the time of survival analysis. Survival >5 years for clinical Stage IVA ovarian cancer was observed in 7 patients and restricted to those who had CGR in the abdomen at IDS. None of these patients required diaphragm stripping, splenectomy, or rectosigmoid resection at IDS; 6/7 (85.7 %) had low complexity cytoreductions. CRS scores did not correlate with ≥5-year OS (p = 0.43). Only 1 of these patients had a BRCA mutation, and this patient was the only individual to receive maintenance chemotherapy. The median PFS in this subgroup was 38.3 months (IQR 20.3, 45.1 months).

After restricting analysis to those who had surgery, univariate analysis of factors relevant to OS in ovarian cancer did not identify any statistically significant factors. The point estimates for a reduced risk of death identified ECOG performance status 0–1 versus 2 (HR 0.53, 95 % CI 0.07–4.17), normal versus low albumin level (HR 0.50, 95 % CI 0.14–1.80), and RD following cytoreduction in the abdomen as the most impactful factors based upon point estimates (HR 0.57, 95 % CI 0.18–1.84; Fig. 3). Using bivariate analysis adjusted for ECOG performance status, no factors were statistically significant, and no point estimates changed directions after adjustment to suggest confounding (Fig. 4). Conclusions

Our findings indicate that outcomes of clinical Stage IVA ovarian cancer are heterogeneous, and future studies are needed to understand the optimal treatment approach. Similar to nearly all prior studies of earlier stage ovarian cancer, long-term survival is heavily dependent upon performance status and CGR of abdominal disease at IDS. Conversely, any RD after IDS remains a strong negative prognostic factor as it is for those with Stage IIIC disease. Moderate to large pleural effusions resolved with NACT in 75 % of patients, and thoracentesis, VATS and/or pleurX catheters were helpful to determine surgical timing or manage those with persistent or symptomatic effusions before surgery. Despite a strategy focused on cytoreduction in the abdomen alone, there was only one case who developed recurrent disease in the thorax. Despite a suspected poor prognosis, relatively few patients received maintenance therapy and this was influenced by performance status and the ability to tolerate treatment.

The present cohort was considered high-risk due to older age, malnutrition and impaired performance status, with 22.5 % unable to undergo IDS (Bruce et al., 2024). The strategy beginning with VATS to promote PDS as presented by Kahn et al. differed in being generally younger, fit for surgery, and with lesser prevalence of malnutrition (Kahn et al., 2023). Together, these observations emphasize the importance of patient selection. For high-risk patients, based upon our data we recommend the approach using NACT where VATS is reserved for effusions that do not resolve during NACT (Narasimhulu et al., 2020). For the fit patient with limited intrathoracic disease, we propose that there are two equally valuable approaches: starting with NACT or VATS. When pleural effusions did not resolve or CGR was not achieved in the abdomen, these patients were at very high risk. In these patients, assuming fitness for maintenance chemotherapy, we suggest using bevacizumab or PARP inhibitor as guided by BRCA and homologous recombination status.

The present series is limited by the low incidence of clinical Stage IVA ovarian cancer, retrospective design, and the number of clinically important factors to consider. The determination of clinical Stage IVA disease without VATS is imprecise and will include patients with disease of the pleura and/or cardiophrenic lymph nodes. However, this approach has practical merit and focuses upon the relevant clinical characteristic of a moderate to large pleural effusion available at the time of consultation. Subgroup description of >5 year survival should be interpreted with caution and is influenced by confounding from tumor biology, treatment response, and the presence of high-risk pre-operative factors. Multivariable analysis was limited to two variables due to small group sizes which result in imprecise estimates. Following restriction to those who received surgery, adjustment by ECOG performance status did not reveal confounding but may suggest a ceiling effect. Strengths of the study include adherence to a standardized protocol for NACT across a large group of gynecologic oncologists as well as CGR rates consistent with other NACT trials. When RD was present, it was usually <5 mm in size. We were unable to describe the impact of maintenance chemotherapy due to low utilization and this may have affected the point estimate for homologous recombination deficiency being at a higher risk of death which is opposite of expectations. A future evaluation should review the role of maintenance therapy in this high-risk population.

While the best treatment approach for clinical Stage IVA ovarian cancer is unknown, we found that 90 % of patients had at least one high-risk factor in addition to moderate to large pleural effusion that supported triage to NACT. In these patients, outcomes were heterogeneous and most dependent upon performance status. When pleural effusions resolved after NACT and CGR in the abdomen occurred, survival was good. The negative prognostic significance of any RD at IDS, which has been repeatedly demonstrated in NACT trials, again highlights the need for decision making and preparedness to avoid futile cytoreductions at IDS. The study of Stage IVA ovarian cancer is limited by low incidence, imprecision in the methods used to detect thoracic disease, and expectations are generally influenced by outcomes across Stage III-IVB disease. Although subject to these limitations, our observations support NACT as an acceptable upfront strategy for patients with moderate to large pleural effusions.
