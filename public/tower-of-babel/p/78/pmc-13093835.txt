Low‐Volume Plasma Exchange for Fulminant Hepatic Failure Secondary to Hepatitis A in a Young Adult: A Case Report
Authors: Arham Amir Khawaja, Sameer Sattar, Hassan Faraz Chaudhry, Haseeb Mehmood Qadri, Ahmad Furqan Anjum, Hermann Yokolo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute liver failure accounts for a very fatal and severe complication. It is defined as acute liver injury with hepatic encephalopathy (HE) and an elevated international normalized ratio (INR above 1.5). There are multiple factors that can lead to acute liver failure. These different etiologies can vary depending on certain geographical areas where they are more prevalent than other factors in the area. Hepatitis caused by the Hepatitis A virus (HAV) is one of the leading causes of acute liver failure worldwide, especially in developing countries. Artificial Liver Support System (ALSS), on the other hand, consists of certain techniques by which the function of the liver is maintained artificially outside the body. These techniques try to filter out harmful substances and detoxify the blood. We report a 20‐year‐old male, with no known comorbidities, who presented to the medical outdoor with a history of high‐grade fever for 5 days. On examination, the patient had mucocutaneous jaundice, moderate epigastric tenderness. His initial laboratory investigation showed Hb = 13.5, TLC = 6.8, PLT = 236, Pt = 22 (13 control), INR 1.7, Bilirubin total = 17.9, Bilirubin direct = 11.6, Bilirubin indirect = 6.3, AST = 5868, ALT = 5368, ALP = 160. His HbsAg and HCV by ELISA were negative, hepatitis E virus IgM was negative, and Hepatitis A IgM was positive. He was preliminarily diagnosed as a case of acute viral hepatitis A. He was shifted to the medical HDU for optimization. Over the next 12 h, the patient gradually deteriorated. He developed high‐grade fever, 102°F, total bilirubin worsened, and hepatic encephalopathy grade II developed, with flapping tremors appreciated. The patient was shifted to the Medical Intensive Care Unit for monitoring and probable need for ventilatory support. Over the next 24 h, due to worsening clinical and biochemical parameters, it was decided to proceed with plasma exchange. Three sessions of plasma exchange were carried out, using eight units of fresh frozen plasma (approximately 1.6 L) as the replacement solution, over a period of 1.5 h for each session. The patient showed a marvelous improvement in clinical and biochemical parameters. He was admitted to the medical ICU for 7 days. He was stepped down to medical HDU for a further 2 days before being discharged. Low‐volume plasma exchange therapy is a relatively safe option for young adult patients with fulminant hepatic failure secondary to hepatitis A infection. The therapy can be used early in the disease course when neurological signs/haptic encephalopathy start to develop. This can lead to a better prognosis and survival in selected patients.Keywords: acute, case reports, hepatitis A, liver failure, liver support systems, plasma exchange

Introduction

The liver plays a very important role in maintaining a constant internal environment, known as homeostasis. Any factor that leads to its failure is detrimental to our health. Acute liver failure (ALF), even though it's a rare phenomenon, accounts for a very fatal and severe complication [1]. It is defined as acute liver injury with hepatic encephalopathy (HE) and an elevated international normalized ratio (INR above 1.5) [2]. Most cases of acute liver failure in the United States of America (USA), the United Kingdom (UK), and Australia are due to drugs, for example, acetaminophen [1]. The UK and USA report that 57% and 46% of total cases of ALF are due to acetaminophen overdose [1]. On the contrary, in developing countries like India, acute liver failure is mainly caused by viral hepatitis. Published reports from India mention that 90% of cases of ALF in India are due to viral hepatitis, out of which non‐A, non‐E hepatitis accounts for 38% and Hepatitis E (HEV) is responsible for 31% of cases [1]. Another regional difference is in Japan, where ALF mainly occurs because of the Hepatitis B virus (42% cases) [1]. In Pakistan, on the other hand, the major cause of acute liver disease is Hepatitis E virus [3]. Other minor causes of acute liver failure include Autoimmune Hepatitis, Wilson's disease, and ALF during pregnancy.

Acute liver injury can be in two forms: namely, acute and fulminant hepatitis. The main difference between the two is that in acute hepatitis, encephalopathy occurs within 10 days of jaundice, whereas in fulminant hepatitis, encephalopathy occurs between 10 and 30 days of the onset of jaundice [1]. Hepatitis caused by Hepatitis A virus (HAV) is one of the leading causes of acute liver failure worldwide, especially in developing countries, and the second leading cause in Western countries [4]. As far as the fatality rate is concerned, it is 0.1% to 0.3% among children and adults under the age of 40, whereas it is more than 1.8% for those patients who are more than 49 years old [3].

To treat acute liver failure, two routes can be taken: one is liver transplant (which provides a definitive and permanent cure of the disease), and the other is the Artificial Liver Support System (ALSS). The rising demand for liver transplants is the leading factor behind these long waiting times. This tends to worsen the patient's condition. It was reported in 2018 that the waiting list mortality was 13.2 per 100 waitlist‐years, with rates higher for candidates listed with ALF [5]. ALSS, on the other hand, consists of certain techniques by which the function of the liver is maintained artificially outside the body. These techniques try to filter out harmful substances and detoxify the blood. Examples of such techniques include Molecular Adsorbent Recirculating System (MARS), Single Pass Albumin Dialysis (SPAD), and Prometheus [5]. In plasma exchange therapy, a large volume of plasma is removed from the patient, and it is replaced by fresh frozen plasma or albumin [6]. The main advantage of this is that it removes toxic metabolites like bilirubin, ammonia, and other toxins that accumulate in severe liver failure, leading to complications [6]. This allows the liver to heal and, thus, as mentioned above, act as a bridge to the preparation of a liver transplant.

Seeing that Hepatitis A is a very benign condition, it is concerning to see that such a disease could cause acute liver failure. Since not many cases have been reported on this as well, this case report tends to establish why such a benign condition causes ALF. The use of PLEX as a possible treatment for Hepatitis A‐induced acute liver failure or as a bridge to liver transplant shall also be explored thoroughly during this review.

Case History/Examination

A 20‐year‐old male, with no known comorbidities, presented to the medical outdoor with a history of high‐grade fever (102 F) for 5 days. This was associated with a sore throat. There was no history of cough, sputum, loose stools, constipation, headache, dizziness, dyspnea, or chest pain. He was prescribed treatment and discharged. After 5 days, the patient developed a sudden onset of nausea, vomiting, and melena that persisted for 2 days. In a medical emergency, on examination, the patient had mucocutaneous jaundice and moderate epigastric tenderness.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

His initial laboratory investigation showed Hb = 13.5, TLC = 6.8, PLT = 236, Pt = 22 (13 control), INR 1.7, Bilirubin total = 17.9, Bilirubin direct = 11.6, Bilirubin indirect = 6.3, AST = 5868, ALT = 5368, ALP = 160. His receiving vitals were blood pressure = 110/80, HR = 84/min, SpO2 = 96% at room air, apyrexial, Glasgow coma score E4V5M6. His HbsAg, HCV by ELISA, was negative, hepatitis E virus IgM was negative, and Hepatitis A IgM was positive. His USG showed liver with normal echotexture, no focal lesion identified. The gallbladder shows diffuse wall edema measuring 8 mm. The spleen was 14 cm in size and bulky. His Upper GI endoscopy showed a small hiatal hernia with a Cameron ulcer (Forrest Class II). He was preliminarily diagnosed as a case of acute viral hepatitis A. He was shifted to the medical HDU for optimization. The Liver Transplant Unit and Gastroenterology Unit were taken on board. Conservative management was started. However, over the next 12 h, the patient gradually deteriorated. He developed high‐grade fever 102°F, total bilirubin worsened, and hepatic encephalopathy grade II developed, with flapping tremors appreciated. The patient was shifted to the Medical Intensive Care Unit for monitoring and probable need for ventilatory support. Patient did not satisfy the hospital's clinic and King's criteria for liver transplant. Therefore, medical management was opted. Over the next 24 h, due to worsening clinical and biochemical parameters, it was decided to proceed with plasma exchange. Three sessions of plasma exchange were carried out with eight fresh frozen plasma (about 1.6 L) as replacement solution over 1.5 h for each session.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient showed a marvelous improvement in clinical and biochemical parameters. He was admitted to the medical ICU for 7 days. He was stepped down to the medical HDU for a further 2 days before being discharged. He is healthy, alive. He was followed weekly for 4 weeks. Discussion

The objective of this case report was to discuss two things: hepatitis A viral infection leading to fulminant hepatic failure in an adult Asian male in Pakistan, and plasma exchange as the standard, single artificial support system until the diseased liver recovers. Hepatitis A induced ALF occurs in less than 1% of the cases and is responsible for around 2 to 4% of all ALF cases reported in Western countries, and for 4% and 6% of those reported in Australia and Japan, respectively [4]. Hepatitis A‐induced ALF has a spontaneous survival rate of 69%; the remaining 31% require emergency liver transplant or die [4].

There is no gender preponderance, and there is no clear reasoning as to why hepatitis A progresses to fulminant hepatitis in a few patients. Similar to other viral hepatitis, there is an accumulation of toxins, pathological antibodies, immune complexes, and cytokines that eventually cause systemic inflammatory response leading to organ dysfunction. This is more common in immunocompromised patients with cancer, children [2, 3]. Our case was of a post‐pubescent young male, with no apparent co‐morbidities, no past medical history, indicating that this is a very usual case of Hepatitis A‐induced fulminant hepatic failure.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the second reported case of fulminant hepatic failure caused by isolated acute viral hepatitis A infection treated successfully by three sessions of plasma exchange. Malik et al. reported a case of a 38‐year‐old adult male with hepatitis A, E co‐infection [3]. Despite all supportive efforts and the unavailability of a liver transplant, the patient could not survive [3]. Talat et al. conducted a single‐center retrospective study on the children population who presented with fulminant hepatic failure with multiple causes. She isolated that acute viral hepatitis A was the major cause (29% of patients), and 33% of patients died due to hepatitis A infection [7]. Overall, 14/15 patients with different causes of acute liver failure survived with supportive care, while one patient underwent liver transplant [7]. Another study was conducted in China in which the efficacy of an artificial liver support system (ALSS) was tested with PALS and a simplified PALS scoring system. The study tested factors of liver cirrhosis, total bilirubin, Prothrombin time, infection, and hepatic encephalopathy. The study came to the conclusion that patients presenting with acute on chronic liver failure with a low PALS score (0–2) and some with a PALS score [3, 4, 5] can benefit from ALSS, while higher score should opt for liver transplant [8]. Although our patient did not satisfy the King's College and Clichy criteria for liver transplant, it was in the best interest of the patient to resort to a less invasive approach with a trial plasma exchange session. This was used as the first approach in our patient due to worsening neurological status (grade II encephalopathy) to remove not only ammonia but also bilirubin, inflammatory toxins. A similar concept of High‐volume plasma exchange was used in a study for the treatment of acute viral hepatitis B liver failure [2].

The number, duration of sessions, and type of ALSS used to manage liver failure and toxin build‐up are debated. Braganca et al. used three sessions of High‐volume plasma exchange (HVPE), continuous renal replacement, and continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration [2]. In HVPE, 8–12 L of plasma volume are removed (more than two liters), which corresponds to 15% of the ideal body weight [2]. In another study, acute liver failure secondary to hepatitis E, complicated by thrombocytopenia, 35 mL/kg/day for 12 days, plasma exchange was used [9]. There is also controversy over the type of replacement solution. Some prefer the use of Fresh frozen plasma (FFP), human albumin, or mixed solution with varying concentrations. Braganca et al. used 5% albumin and FFP in a ratio of 1:1 to reduce the risk of bleeding and infection [2]. Caballol et al. used standard 1.5 L plasma exchange as FFP replacement solution. There was an optimum response in hepatitis A‐induced liver failure with takotsubo cardiomyopathy [4]. This is the only case reported about hepatitis A fulminant hepatic failure. In most studies, the duration of plasma exchange was limited to three sessions, with most using FFP as a replacement solution at an interval of 24 h. Each replacement solution has its risks and benefits. FFP has the advantage of being a readily available source; however, there is a risk of viral infection, citrate toxicity leading to bleeding [4]. On the other hand, human albumin has no infectious risk but is expensive in Pakistan and leads to a decline in plasma components and immunoglobulins [4]. In our patient, due to affordability issues, we opted out of human albumin and used FFP as a replacement solution. We carried out three low‐volume plasma exchange (8 FFP; corresponding to about 1.6 L) for 1.5 h as the patient tolerated every 24 h with fairly good results.

It is inevitable to highlight the decision on when to start ALSS via plasma exchange. There is no clear consensus or a definitive criterion to start plasma exchange head‐on. The evidence we can gather from existing literature is a clinically deteriorating patient (hepatic encephalopathy/neurological signs) and hematological, biochemical worsening (> 1.5 INR, thrombocytopenia) and multiorgan dysfunction [3, 4, 8]. Development or worsening of hepatic encephalopathy was the major indication to start plasma exchange. This is comparable in our patient. We opted to use plasma exchange early as the patient's clinical condition progressed to Grade II encephalopathy. This was also to prevent cerebral edema and a sign of intracranial hypertension, as consistent with a previous study [2]. Another indication can be as a bridge therapy to be used before liver transplantation, as liver transplantation is not a readily available treatment modality [6, 8]. To summarize, it is desirable to start plasma exchange therapy early on in the disease course when there is the presence of red flag signs (neurological, multiorgan dysfunction).

Conclusion

Low‐volume plasma exchange therapy is a relatively safe option for young adult patients with fulminant hepatic failure secondary to hepatitis A infection. The therapy can be used early in the disease course when neurological signs/hepatic encephalopathy start to develop. This can lead to a better prognosis and survival in selected patients. However, due to practically no significant data on the subject, a definitive conclusion cannot be drawn until further evidence is there.
