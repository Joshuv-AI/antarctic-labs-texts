Genomic data-driven framework for drug target discovery in atrial fibrillation
Authors: Yuhang Tao, Qi Liu, Yuxing Wang, Ruhong Jiang, Kai Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common type of arrhythmia and is linked to an increased risk of death, stroke, and other complications. Although studies have explored the pathophysiology of AF, its genetic mechanisms remain poorly understood, limiting the development of effective treatments.MethodsWe conducted a large-scale meta-analysis of genome-wide association study (GWAS) data from 1,347,178 participants in the FinnGen and Nielsen studies to identify novel genetic variants of AF. The novel genes were validated by expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) using a transcriptome-wide association study. The cross-phenotype genetic correlation analyses were conducted for our new GWAS. Afterward, the GWAS results were integrated with protein quantitative trait loci data from the UK Biobank, and Mendelian randomization with postselection inference and colocalization analyses were used to infer causal relationships between proteins and AF. We also conducted downstream analysis for the identified genes, including querying the Mouse Genome Informatics database, gene enrichment analysis, gene expression analysis, and druggability assessment.ResultsOur meta-analysis revealed 244 genome-wide significant AF risk variants, 77 of which were novel. In total, 372 genes were identified by a transcriptome-wide association study. Additionally, 155 proteins were significantly associated with AF, with 28 showing strong colocalization with AF genetic signals. Gene expression and enrichment analyses revealed that the identified genes were predominantly expressed in the heart and blood vessels, with related biological pathways such as cardiac development, myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing results revealed that these genes are expressed mainly in cardiomyocytes and macrophages. The quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction results revealed significant changes in the expression of most genes in the AF group compared with the control group. Through druggability analysis, we identified potential AF drug targets, some of which are linked to approved or clinical-stage drugs.ConclusionThis study provides new insights into the genetic and proteomic basis of AF. We hope our findings will inspire further research and preclinical work to advance drug development programs for AF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07217-4.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent type of arrhythmia in clinical practice and is linked to increased risks of death, stroke, and various other complications [1]. Epidemiological data indicate that AF is highly prevalent [2]. Although numerous studies have explored the pathophysiology of AF and improved treatment methods have been developed, the mechanisms of AF are still poorly understood, and determining the potential pathogenic mechanisms in individual patients remains difficult; this in turn has affected the development of effective treatments [3]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for new research methods to explore new specific drug targets for AF.

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) can be used to help elucidate disease mechanisms and provide insights into potential new drug targets and disease-causing proteins [4]. Multiple genes are associated with AF, with several links identified in classic linkage studies [5], and most of these links were established through functional analyses or cross-ancestry GWASs [6–9]. Recent GWASs have identified more than 100 AF-associated loci, including rare variants in ion channel, structural, and transcription factor genes [8]. However, translating GWAS loci into biological insights remains challenging, as the most prominent variant at a given locus is often noncausal and resides in noncoding regions. Therefore, identifying and prioritizing effector genes within AF-related GWAS loci is both a challenging and essential step in uncovering the underlying mechanisms and developing new treatments for AF.

Proteins secreted or leaked into the circulation are involved in a wide range of complex biological processes, and their expression or activity is central to disease development [10]. Currently, human proteins represent most approved drug targets [11], and research has confirmed that circulating proteins are involved in the occurrence and development of AF [12]. By utilizing data from AF and protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs), insights can be provided into the causal pathways involved in AF and offer information for new drug targets [13].

In this study, we conducted a meta-analysis of AF GWASs from FinnGen [14] and Nielsen [15] and applied Mendelian randomization with the postselection inference method (MR-SPI) and genetic colocalization analysis using the meta-analyzed AF GWASs and human blood proteins from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) [16]. We subsequently conducted extensive downstream assessments to explore the biological credibility of our genetic findings.

Methods

Study design

A schematic overview of the research framework is presented in Fig. 1. Follow-up analytical procedures were conducted to identify key genetic variants and elucidate potential drug targets for AF, utilizing GWAS datasets. All the study protocols were approved by the ethics committee, and written informed consent was provided by all the enrolled subjects.

Genome-wide association study for AF

We performed a GWAS meta-analysis of 2 large studies: the Nielsen (60,620 cases and 970,216 controls) study and the FinnGen R12 (63,532 cases and 252,810 controls). Quality control was conducted at the marker and sample levels for each included study (Supplementary methods). In short, each dataset underwent initial quality checks, imputation, postimputation validation, and association testing, adjusting for at least age (birth year), sex, and principal components included as covariates. AF phenotypes were defined across all cohorts using ICD codes (e.g., ICD-10 I48) from electronic health records and, in some cases, ECG data to confirm diagnoses. Controls were defined as individuals without any clinical diagnosis of AF on the basis of the same ICD code criteria and other available clinical information. We conducted a fixed-effect GWAS meta-analysis using METAL software [17]. The genomic inflation factor (λGC) was computed for the GWAS meta-analysis. To evaluate any remaining confounding caused by population stratification, we computed the linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) intercept using single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) LD scores derived from the HapMap3 CEU population [18]. We removed those with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.1%, and we employed FUMA [19] to annotate our findings using the default parameters. Independent significant genomic risk loci were characterized as follows: (a) P < 5 × 10− 8, (b) window 250 Kb, (c) linkage disequilibrium (LD) r12 = 0.6 and r22 = 0.1. Novel indexed variants are defined as those that were >500 Kb from reported variants of the previous two studies or prior AF signals found in the GWAS catalog.

Gene prioritization

At each independent significant genomic risk locus, we implemented a two-tiered gene prioritization strategy by considering both spatial relationships and functional evidence. This included (a) mapping the nearest protein-coding gene to the indexed SNP per locus and (b) identifying the top gene per locus with polygenic priority score (PoPS) analysis within a 500-kilobase cis-regulatory window.

PoPS [20] is a novel approach for gene prioritization that integrates GWAS summary statistics with diverse data sources, including gene expression, molecular and biological pathways, and protein‒protein interaction networks. First, MAGMA [21] is used to calculate gene association statistics and gene‒gene relationships on the basis of GWAS data and LD information. Subsequently, for PoPS, marginal feature selection is conducted by performing enrichment analysis for each gene feature individually, keeping only those that meet the significance threshold (P < 0.05). A generalized least squares (GLS) regression model is then applied to compute the joint enrichment of the selected features, incorporating gene-level covariates such as gene length. Using a leave one chromosome out (LOCO) framework, a polygenic priority score for each gene is calculated, which is separate from the GWAS data on the chromosome containing the gene. This score aids in prioritizing genes within the region of interest for each chromosome. In our study, the gene with the highest score at the locus was selected as the top gene.

Transcriptome-wide association study

We used FUSION to identify genes whose cis-regulated expression is associated with AF through a transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) (http://gusevlab.org/projects/fusion/) [22]. First, a prediction model for specific gene expression levels was constructed on the basis of expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data. This model was subsequently used to impute gene expression levels in large-scale genotyped individuals. An association test was subsequently performed to evaluate the statistical association between the imputed expression levels and a given trait of disease risk. In our analysis, we included precomputed gene expression weights generated in tissues specific to AF (GTEx v.8 data on heart left ventricle, heart atrial appendage, aorta artery, coronary artery, tibial artery, and whole blood), excluding the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region. The results were corrected for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction method.

Cross-phenotype genetic correlation of AF

The genetic associations between AF and various phenotypes were explored through cross-phenotype evaluations of GWASs utilizing the integrative cross-phenotype analysis (iCPAG) database [23]. This platform employs ancestry-specific LD reference datasets to perform enhanced multitrait correlation assessments. Our analysis incorporated genome-wide association metadata from 3,793 distinct clinical characteristics cataloged in the NHGRI-EBI repository.

Proteome-wide studies of AF

We employed MR-SPI [24] to investigate putative causal plasma protein biomarkers correlated with the risk of AF. MR-SPI is a novel statistical method designed to improve causal inference in genetic studies by identifying valid instrumental variables (IVs) for specific protein‒outcome relationships. Unlike traditional MR approaches, which often assume that all selected genetic variants are valid IVs, in MR-SPI, a data-driven voting procedure is employed to distinguish valid IVs from invalid ones. This method is grounded in the principle that valid IVs consistently estimate similar causal effects, whereas invalid IVs exhibit divergent estimates—a concept inspired by the idea that valid instruments “agree” in their signal, whereas invalid ones vary unpredictably. It does not rely on restrictive assumptions such as balanced pleiotropy or large numbers of IVs, making it suitable for proteomics data where IV counts are often limited.

Our study analyzed pQTLs from 54,219 individuals in the UKB-PPP [16], identifying 2,941 biomarkers representing 2,923 distinct proteins. To ensure robustness, stringent quality filters were applied as described in the original study: genome-wide significance thresholds (P < 1.70 × 10⁻¹¹) adjusted via Bonferroni correction as outlined in established protocols. Finally, 2,163 distinct proteins were included in the analysis. The significance threshold for multiple testing corrections was determined by the Bonferroni corrected P value (2.31 × 10− 5). For every targeted protein that had a missense pQTL variant in the cis-region, AlphaFold3 [25] was employed to generate structural predictions and analyze the three-dimensional structures of the wild-type protein and missense pQTL IV-induced mutated protein. These molecular differences were subsequently visualized through PyMOL’s rendering tools (https://pymol.org).

In addition, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis [26] for proteins showing significant associations in MR-SPI analysis. This approach systematically evaluates five mutually exclusive hypotheses regarding genetic associations: (H0) null association with both traits, (H1) correlation to trait 1 only, (H2) correlation to trait 2 only, (H3) distinct causal variants affecting two traits, and (H4) shared causal variants affecting both traits.

Default priors were applied using the coloc R package (the prior probability of a SNP being linked to trait 1 is P1 = 1 × 10− 4, with trait 2: P2 = 1 × 10− 4, and with both traits P12 = 1 × 10− 5), and we specifically investigated whether protein‒AF associations could be explained by shared genetic architecture. In the analysis, posterior probability thresholds of PPH4 >0.90 were employed to identify loci with strong evidence of colocalization. LocusCompareR was used for visualization of GWAS-pQTL colocalization events [27].

Tissue type identification and enrichment analyses

For all the identified genes, including those in the GWASs, TWAS and MR-SPI, we performed tissue-specificity analysis using the FUMA-integrated GENE2FUNC tool and evaluated gene expression patterns across 30 major human tissues from GTEx v8 [28]. Additionally, we conducted enrichment analyses on these genes using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.0.3) [29]. Specifically, Gene Ontology (GO) biological process and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway analyses were conducted, with pathways showing significant enrichment at corrected P values (Benjamini–Hochberg) < 0.05 selected for further investigation.

Querying the mouse genome informatics database

To investigate novel GWAS-identified candidate genes and MR-SPI/colocalization-predicted protein-coding genes, we performed systematic queries using the Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI) platform [30]. The MGI database integrates standardized genetic, phenotypic, and disease model data using structured biological ontologies to support cross-species biomedical research. Our investigation specifically prioritized cardiac-related pathological features.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing analysis

We defined novel candidate genes as the intersection of GWAS- and TWAS-derived candidate gene sets, supplemented with MR-SPI/colocalization-predicted protein-coding genes. The cell type-specific expression of novel candidate genes was evaluated by performing single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) on left atrial (LA) tissue samples from patients with AF and controls from the single-cell portal (SCP) [31]. The snRNA-seq data of LA tissue samples included 36,600 genes in 179,697 nuclei. To determine whether the identified genes were highly expressed in a particular cell type in LA tissue samples, differential expression analysis based on the Wilcoxon rank sum test was performed to compare gene expression levels between a cell type and the other cell types. Genes whose average log2 fold change (log2FC) was greater than 0.5 and whose false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P value was less than 0.05 were identified as enriched genes in a cell type.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Human atrial appendage tissue samples were derived from individuals receiving aortocoronary bypass, valvular surgery, or prosthetic valve implantation. Human atrial tissue acquisition received approval from the Ethics Committee at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University, following documented patient consent. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed with primers (Supplementary methods) to detect the mRNA expression of the novel candidate genes. In brief, total RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent, followed by reverse transcription into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT system (Takara, Japan) [32]. qPCR detection was performed with SYBR (Vazyme) in accordance with supplier-recommended primer-specific protocols. All the qPCR primers used were commercially synthesized (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai) [33, 34]. GAPDH served as the endogenous control for normalization, with relative gene expression quantified via the 2^−ΔΔCt approach.

Druggability

Novel candidate genes were evaluated for druggability potential using OpenTargets [35]. This system originally categorized drug targets into nine distinct groups (“buckets”) according to drug type (e.g., antibodies, small molecules) and development stage. For clarity, these categories were simplified into four groups: approved drugs (bucket 1, covering approved antibodies, small molecules, or other modalities), clinical development-stage drugs (buckets 2 and 3, including antibodies, small molecules, or other modalities), preclinical and predicted therapeutic drugs (buckets 4 and 5 for antibodies and small molecules plus buckets 6–8 for small molecules), and undruggable targets (all compounds not in the above groups). For approved drugs, we utilized our MR-SPI/colocalization results to deduce whether pharmacological action (activating or blocking effects) was required to prohibit AF and compared this with the action of current known drugs.

Results

Genome-wide association analysis

The current meta-analysis of GWASs on AF from the FinnGen and Nielsen studies included 1,347,178 participants. Manhattan plots of the meta-analysis are presented in Fig. 2, and the quantile‒quantile (Q‒Q) plot is shown in Figure S1. Following quality control, association results were obtained for 24 million genetic variants associated with AF (MAF > 1 × 10− 3). The genomic inflation factor (lambda) was 1.56 (Figure S1), and the LDSC intercept was 1.1 (standard error = 0.03), indicating that the majority of the inflation arises from AF being a polygenic trait. We observed 244 genomic risk loci with genome-wide significant signals associated with AF (Table S1). The gene nearest to the indexed SNP was identified, along with the gene exhibiting the highest score from PoPS within a 500 kb window of the indexed SNP (Table S1). Two indexed SNPs (rs8033720 and rs596878) were excluded because they had no genes selected by PoPS. Eighty-one indexed SNPs were > 500 Kb from reported variants. Four indexed SNPs (rs3822259, rs6122011, rs62482495, and rs10077410) were found to be associated with AF in the GWAS catalog. Finally, 77 SNPs were novel (Table 1). Among them, 20 effector genes were identified by both PoPS and nearest-gene annotation.

Novel loci of AF GWAS

Transcriptome-wide association study

Using data from six GTEx tissues closely linked to AF, we analyzed 42,016 genes across these tissues. After Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (P < 1.19 × 10− 6), 744 candidate genes were initially identified. For genes detected in multiple tissues, we retained the result with the smallest P value per gene. After the overlapping entries were removed, a final set of 372 unique candidate genes was obtained (Table S2).

Cross-phenotype genetic correlation of atrial fibrillation

We conducted cross-phenotype genetic association analyses on all significant SNPs with the iCPAG tool. Following Bonferroni correction, cross-phenotype analysis revealed 148 traits significantly linked to AF (Fig. 3 and Table S3). The top 5 traits showing the greatest enrichment were the PR interval (P = 1.02 × 10− 82), systolic blood pressure (P = 4.68 × 10− 78), pulse pressure measurement (P = 7.25 × 10− 71), diastolic blood pressure (P = 1.50 × 10− 68) and body height (P = 1.00 × 10− 63).

Proteome-wide MR-SPI studies of atrial fibrillation

We conducted MR-SPI to identify significant proteins that may be drug targets in AF. As presented in Fig. 4 and 155 proteins were significantly associated with AF after Bonferroni correction (Fig. 4A and Table S4). Strong evidence of colocalization between AF and pQTL signals was also indicated by 28 proteins (PPH4: one common causal variant > 0.9) for one or more of the tools (Fig. 4B and Table S5). Visualization of GWAS-pQTL colocalization events is shown in Figure S2. The 3D structural modifications of APOH resulting from the missense genetic variation of pQTL rs1801689, as predicted by AlphaFold3, are shown in Fig. 4C. The pQTL rs1801689 is a cis-SNP on chromosome 17 and was chosen as a valid IV by MR-SPI. This led to the replacement of the 325th amino acid in APOH, changing cysteine (green) to glycine (yellow), thereby inducing a local structural change in APOH (C325G). In Fig. 4D, the 3D structural modifications of PCSK9 due to the missense genetic variation of pQTL rs11591147 are presented, as predicted by AlphaFold3. The pQTL rs11591147 is a cis-SNP found on chromosome 1 and was chosen as a valid IV by MR-SPI. It led to the replacement of the 46th amino acid in PCSK9, transforming arginine (green) into leucine (yellow), which in turn caused a localized structural alteration in PCSK9 (R46L). In Fig. 4E, we display the 3D structural changes in SCARF1 caused by the missense genetic variation of pQTL rs2272011, as predicted by AlphaFold3. The pQTL rs2272011 is a cis-SNP found on chromosome 17 and was chosen as a valid IV by MR-SPI. This led to the replacement of the 425th amino acid in SCARF1, which transformed alanine (green) into valine (yellow), thereby inducing a localized structural modification in SCARF1 (A425V). For other MR-SPI-identified AF-associated proteins whose cis-pQTLs have missense genetic variation, we also used AlphaFold3 to estimate their 3D structures. The resulting structural predictions are systematically illustrated in Figure S3. Information on the missense pQTL IVs is shown in Table S6.

Tissue specificity gene expression and pathway enrichment analyses

We identified differences in the expression of all the genes in human tissues and regions. The results revealed that the genes were mainly differentially expressed in blood vessels and the heart (Fig. 5). We also conducted gene pathway enrichment analysis. In the gene ontology enrichment analysis, several biological processes were significantly enriched (Fig. 6A, Table S7). The emphasized gene sets suggest biological processes associated with heart development and morphology, as well as structural remodeling of the myocardium. On the basis of the results of the KEGG enrichment analysis for AF, several pathways and diseases were significantly enriched (Fig. 6B, Table S8). One of the most enriched pathways is the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway.

Mouse knockout models for new genes discovered through GWAS meta-analysis and MR-SPI

We searched the MGI resource for knockout (KO) mouse models to find cases in which altering the target leads to a phenotype associated with AF (Table S9). Among 51 genes (40 GWAS-identified candidate genes and 11 MR-SPI/colocalization-predicted protein-coding genes), we found evidence of a knockout linked to cardiovascular disorders. KO models of ADAM17, SCARB2, POPDC1, SMARCC2, THRA, NOS1, ERBB2, and SNCA showed phenotypes such as heart rate, abnormal sinus arrhythmia, prolonged RR interval, and abnormal QT variability, highlighting an intrinsic role in heart rhythm regulation, conduction stability, and susceptibility to arrhythmias. Other genes were associated with various phenotypes, including cardiac and vascular morphology abnormalities, functional abnormalities in the cardiovascular system and developmental abnormalities.

Single-nucleus RNA and qPCR analysis

To explore whether the novel candidate genes had any cell type-specific enrichment in LA samples, we performed single-cell expression analysis using snRNA-seq data. The cells were clustered into 15 clusters and classified into 14 cell types (Figure S4A). All the novel genes had expression data in LA tissue; the expression of these 32 novel genes in every cluster is shown in Figure S4B and S4C. Among them, 23 genes had cell type-specific enrichment in LA tissue at an average log2FC > 0.5 and an FDR < 0.05 (Fig. S4D). ANO5, COL15A1, FABP3, IRAK1BP1, NOS1, TIMP4 and TPM3 were enriched mainly in cardiac muscle cells; CTHRC1, GRN, SNCA and TNFSF12 were enriched in macrophages; NFATC1, PFKFB2 and YOD1 were enriched in megakaryocytes; DPP6, LRP11 and ZBTB38 were enriched in mesothelial cells; ADGRG1 and TIE1 were enriched in cardiac endothelial cells; KLF12 was enriched in adipocytes; CRLF1 was enriched in cardiac atrium fibroblasts; ERBB2 was enriched in T cells; and SCARF1 was enriched in vascular-associated smooth muscle cells. qPCR analysis of clinical atrial tissue samples from AF patients was performed to investigate these 32 novel genes associated with AF. The results revealed significant expression changes in 24 genes in the AF group compared with those in the control group, whereas the remaining 8 genes were not significantly altered (Figure S5).

Druggability

ANO, IRAK1BP1, KLF12, and ZBTB38 are shared findings among the PoPS, nearest-gene annotation, and TWAS analyses. ANO5 was predicted to be a drug target for AF treatment (Table S10). MR-SPI/colocalization-predicted protein-coding genes (ERBB2, PCSK9, COL15A1) were grouped as approved drugs (Table S10). There are known drugs available for repurposing that target all the genes mentioned above. NOS1, SNCA, and TNFSF12 were grouped as clinical development-stage drugs (Table S10). For approved drugs of MR-SPI/colocalization-predicted protein-coding genes, two protein-encoding genes (PCSK9 and COL15A1) demonstrated consistency between MR-SPI predictions and known drug mechanisms, with inhibitor effects indicated. Drug information for known drugs of approved drugs and clinical development-stage drugs is listed in Table S11.

Discussion

AF has the fastest-growing prevalence among all arrhythmic diseases, posing a significant challenge to health care systems and society. Currently, the aim of most pharmacological treatment strategies is to control the ventricular rate, and while these treatments alleviate symptoms, they do not significantly improve the progression of AF [3]. Therefore, there is an urgent demand for specific drug targets that can effectively treat AF. In recent years, the latest advancements in drug development have been significantly driven by genetic research. Drugs supported by genetic evidence are more likely to be approved, and many new therapies have entered clinical trials, offering hope for more effective treatments [36]. In this study, we conducted a meta-analysis of the GWAS data of AF from the FinnGen and Nielsen studies and identified 239 genes potentially associated with AF, 77 of which are newly discovered. Through a literature review, we discovered that multiple genes, including MYOG, ZFHX3, and CAV1, play a role in the function and stability of cardiac and skeletal muscles [37–39]. PITX2, TBX5, and NKX2.5 are involved in the development of cardiac processes [40–42]. ZFHX3, KCNN3, and CAV1 have been implicated in cardiac intracellular calcium handling [43–45], and SCN5A, KCNH2, and KCNJ16 contribute to the regulation of cardiac ion channel function [46–48]. Notably, among the newly discovered genes, MAPRE2 is associated with cardiac electrophysiology [49]; MYH11, FGFR2, and TRIM32 are related to cardiac hypertrophy [50–52]; IRAK1BP1 and KLF12 participate in cardiac fibrosis [53, 54]; ADAM17 promotes myocardial inflammation [55]; and MAPRE2 is related to myocardial cell proliferation [56]. Furthermore, some newly discovered genes are associated with AF risk factors, such as MED13L and RUNX1, which are related to heart failure [57, 58]; TNNT3 and ANO5, which are related to cardiomyopathy [59, 60]; PRKD1, which is associated with congenital heart disease [61]; DRD2, which is related to hypertension [62]; and FGF23, ZBTB38 and MERTK, which are related to acute myocardial infarction and myocardial ischemia [63–65]. We also conducted MR proteomics analysis to reveal potential causal effects in the protein AF, in which 28 genes passed the colocalization analysis. It has been reported that the following genes are associated with severe cardiac developmental defects: ERBB2 and POMC [66–68]; CRLF1, FABP3, HSPB6, and TIMP4 are involved in cardiac fibrosis and myocardial hypertrophy [69–72]. Additionally, NOS1 regulates intracellular calcium levels and current activity [73], and PCSK9 is related to AF risk factors such as heart failure and myocardial infarction [74].

After the above genes were initially screened, we conducted analysis to determine the biological credibility of these candidate genes. First, tissue-specific analysis indicated that the candidate genes are significantly enriched in the cardiovascular system. Second, pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the gene set is associated with biological processes involved in heart development and morphology, along with structural remodeling of the myocardium. Previous studies have shown that many genes can cause atrial structural remodeling, both during fetal heart development and when the adult heart is under stress, and that atrial structural remodeling has been confirmed to trigger AF [75]. Third, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the genes associated with the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway were significantly enriched. Studies have reported that activation of the PI3K–AKT axis promotes cardiomyocyte fibroblast proliferation and atrial fibrosis [76]. Additionally, activation of the PI3K–AKT axis may also lead to inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, and atrial fibrosis and inflammation are important causes of atrial remodeling [77]. Furthermore, genes such as GATA4 and CREBBP are enriched in the thyroid hormone signaling pathway, and the thyroid hormone axis plays a key role in the development of AF [78]. Additionally, significant enrichment was found in dilated cardiomyopathy and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, both of which may alter the electrophysiological basis of atrial structure and increase susceptibility to AF. Studies have reported that AF is the most common arrhythmia in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, with approximately 20% of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy patients having concurrent AF [79]. By querying the KO mouse model database, we revealed that these genes are associated with an AF-related phenotype; for example, SNCA is associated with a prolonged PR interval; SCARB2 and THRA are associated with a prolonged QT interval; and NOS1 and SMARCC2 are associated with an irregular heartbeat. These heart rates, PR intervals, and QT intervals are well-known intermediate phenotypes of AF [80]. We also revealed that genes such as CREBBP are associated with abnormal heart atrium morphology. These potential genes associated with AF influence cardiac structural changes and could contribute to AF progression through a mechanism known as “atrial cardiomyopathy” [75]. Furthermore, we investigated the differential expression of these novel genes in cardiac muscle cells and macrophages. Tissue sample examination revealed significant differential expression of these genes between individuals with AF and non-AF individuals. These results suggest that the genes we screened are robust candidate genes for AF.

Next, we investigated whether any of the genes associated with AF that we identified might serve as potential drug targets for existing or experimental medications by consulting the OpenTargets database, which focuses on drug‒gene interactions. Evaluating these genes can aid in the repurposing of existing drugs developed for other diseases for AF treatment. For instance, ERBB2 is involved in breast cancer pathogenesis, but research has revealed its association with pathological cardiac remodeling and fibrosis [81]. Consequently, its role in cardiovascular diseases is receiving increasing attention. Particularly within the context of cardiac fibrosis and remodeling, strategies targeting genes related to cardiac remodeling may contribute to reducing the incidence of AF. In addition, on the basis of the latest structural prediction technology released by AlphaFold3, in this study, three-dimensional models of protein complexes such as PCSK9, SCARF1 and APOH were constructed, providing insights for the development of novel AF drugs. For instance, the SNP rs11591147 (R46L) variant is recognized as a loss-of-function (LOF) mutation of the PCSK9 gene. It is associated with cardiovascular risk, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels and other factors [82]. This genetic alteration results in the substitution of arginine with leucine at the 46th amino acid position of the PCSK9 protein. The amino acid substitution is located in the prodomain region of PCSK9, a structural domain that plays a critical role in facilitating protein folding, regulating secretory processes, and ensuring functional maturation of the enzyme [83]. A meta-analysis revealed that PCSK9 monoclonal antibodies significantly reduce the incidence of AF, especially in individuals with cardiovascular diseases or equivalent risk factors [84]. Another study revealed that evolocumab can alleviate atrial inflammation, thereby reducing AF susceptibility in rats [85]. Therefore, evolocumab may be a potential preventive and therapeutic drug for reducing the risk of AF. Additionally, there are drugs in clinical development and compounds in preclinical stages. Some of these may serve as potential AF drug targets, as they are related to cardiac inflammation, atrial remodeling, cardiac ion channels, or risk factors for AF. Extensive preclinical work is needed to determine whether and how these genes can be targeted and how to translate this research into potential drug development.

Our study has several limitations. First, the genetic architecture of AF exhibits population-specific characteristics. While this study analyzed European ancestry databases, including those of FinnGen and Nielsen et al., both the effect sizes of identified loci and risk allele frequencies may differ in other populations. Furthermore, certain genetic loci associated with AF in European populations have not been consistently replicated in Asian cohorts. Future research should integrate diverse biobanks, such as BioBank Japan, China Kadoorie Biobank, and H3Africa, to validate the cross-population consistency of AF-associated loci. Trans-pQTLs typically reside distally from target protein-coding genes, exerting effects indirectly through transcription factors, signaling pathways, or epigenetic modifications. While trans-pQTLs provide clues for protein–disease relationships and causal inference, when their effects involve mediation by other molecular intermediates, future studies require experimental validation to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Third, the prioritization of candidate genes and pathway exploration were predominantly guided by bioinformatics approaches. However, these findings in silico require more validation through orthogonal methodologies. The use of PoPS and nearest-gene mapping for gene prioritization may overlook distal regulatory elements or tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms. Finally, while the study suggests potential drug targets, the identification of drugs in the preclinical stage or clinical development needs further validation through experimental studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we performed a large-scale meta-analysis of GWASs on AF to reveal new genetic determinants of AF. Through MR-SPI and colocalization analysis of human proteins, we leveraged the GWAS and pQTL results to infer a causal relationship between druggable proteins and the development of AF. We hope our findings will inspire further research and preclinical work to advance drug development programs, potentially establishing these genes as viable drug targets for AF.
