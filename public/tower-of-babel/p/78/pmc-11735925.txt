Synovial Fistula Associated With Total Knee Replacement Aseptic Loosening and Polyethylene Wear
Authors: Saul Hernandez-Rodriguez, Liannette Padilla-Martinez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynovial fistula of the knee occurs when a defect in the joint capsule creates a connection between skin, bursa, and a near tissue, manifesting as an opening with continuous draining fluid or a cyst. This is a case of an 80-year-old male who developed a synovial fistula with cystic presentation 6 years after the primary total knee arthroplasty. Management included a total knee arthroplasty revision surgery with intraoperative identification of the fistula with methylene blue, and using a gelatin-thrombin matrix for closure. This case shows a possible complication of catastrophic polyethylene insert wear and management.Keywords: Knee prosthesis synovial fistula, Synovial fistula, Polyethylene wear, Total knee revision

Introduction

Synovial fistula of the knee is a condition primarily described in case reports. A synovial fistula is an opening in the joint capsule that commonly connects with the skin. In other cases, it can connect with the bursa or any periarticular tissue, manifesting as a cyst [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Synovial fistulas of the knee have been reported in the literature with an incidence of up to 0.61% after arthroscopy [8].

The first-line treatment is cast immobilization for at least 2 weeks, and some authors suggest prophylactic antibiotic treatment [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. Not all cases respond to conservative treatment and require surgical intervention. Surgical approaches to this problem are described in case reports and vary according to the underlying etiology. Failure to treat this condition may cause a joint infection as a complication [13,14].

No documentation of a synovial fistula associated with aseptic loosening of a total knee arthroplasty (TKA) with polyethylene wear was found after a comprehensive literature review. The objective of this study is to present an atypical case of a synovial fistula with cystic mass presentation in the setting of a TKA with aseptic loosening and catastrophic polyethylene wear with the hopes of describing a management option that may decrease the risk of recurrence and complications. In addition, an alternative method to confirm the location of the fistulous tract is presented.

Case history

An 80-year-old male was referred to our clinic due to a mass located in the lateral side of his left knee that has been growing for the past 7 months. Six years ago, he underwent a primary left TKA (Optetrak Logic Primary System; Exactech, Gainesville, FL) by another surgeon due to osteoarthritis, for which he recovered uneventfully. On a physical examination, the knee range of motion was 0-115°, without skin color changes, and a tense, nonmobile translucent mass was felt on palpation. The mass had an ellipsoid configuration and measured 5.5 × 2 cm. In magnetic resonance imaging, the presence of the prosthesis artifact did not allow a complete evaluation of the characteristics of the mass. A knee radiograph taken 8 months before his initial visit showed lateral soft-tissue swelling suggestive of a smaller soft-tissue mass, with some osteolysis but maintained alignment (Fig. 1).

Initial radiograph of the patient done 6 years after the primary TKA and 8 months prior to his initial visit to our clinic showing lateral soft-tissue mass, with some osteolysis but maintained alignment. Arrow denotes location of synovial cyst.

The patient has a past medical history of renal cancer treated with a partial nephrectomy. With the possibility of a pseudotumor vs malignancy, he was taken to the operation room (OR) for soft-tissue biopsy and excision. Laboratory workup performed before the biopsy and excision were as follows: complete blood count (CBC) with white blood cell count (WBC) of 7.47 × 109/L (normal, 4.50-11.0 × 109/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) negative (normal, negative), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 29 mm/h (normal, 0-20 mm/h). An incision was performed 5 cm lateral from the anterior midline scar. Marginal resection of the cystic mass was done through a direct approach over the mass. It was located anterior to the peroneal nerve and superficial to the tibialis anterior fascia. Cultures taken in the OR were negative for bacterial growth. The patient was discharged with a negative pressure wound system, allowing for the skin incision to heal and manage fluid drainage. The pathology report from the tissue biopsy revealed fibroadipose tissue showing diffuse foam cells, acute inflammation, congestion, focal foreign material with areas of fibrin deposition, and calcification.

Preoperative workup prior to the TKA revision surgery included CBC, CRP, ESR, synovial fluid analysis, and new radiographs. In Figure 2, the radiograph shows osteolysis, loss of alignment, and a recurring soft-tissue mass. Laboratory results were as follows: CBC with WBC of 7.02 × 109/L (normal, 4.50-11.0 × 109/L), CRP <0.4 mg/dL (normal, <0.4 mg/dL), and ESR 22 mm/h (normal, 0-20 mm/h). The synovial fluid analysis reported a red cloudy appearance, culture negative, no crystals, red blood cell count of 17,325 cells/μL, WBC 1058 cells/μL with 79% polymorphonuclear cells and 21% mononuclear cells.

Radiograph obtained before knee revision surgery shows loss of alignment, osteolysis, and a lateral soft-tissue mass.

The patient was taken to the OR for TKA revision 5 months after the soft-tissue biopsy and excision. The presence and identification of the fistulous tract was confirmed with intra-articular injection of methylene blue. Methylene blue was injected proximally, where an intact capsule could be appreciated, and it exited through the fistula distally (Fig. 3). The fistula tract exited through the lateral gutter and connected to the subcutaneous tissue. After opening the joint capsule, catastrophic wear of the polyethylene insert was observed (Fig. 4). A cemented revision system TKA (Legion Revision System; Smith and Nephew, Memphis, TN) was placed following debridement and the appropriate bony preparation. After the revision prosthesis was placed, the fistula was debrided, and a gelatin-thrombin matrix (FloSeal; Baxter, Deerfield, IL) was used to fill the tract. Hemovac drainage was localized in the cystic space to avoid fluid accumulation in the dead space (Fig. 5). Drainage systems were removed 48 hours after the surgery. The patient started ambulation and range of motion exercises 24 hours after the surgery. Six months after the revision surgery, the fistula has not recurred, and the knee range of motion is 0-110°. The patient is ambulating pain-free and without any assistive device. The patient was informed that his case would be submitted for publication. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient to publish his case; a copy is available as requested.

Discussion

Synovial fistulae of the knee joint occur after a trigger event causes the synovium to herniate through the joint capsule. Initial management includes laboratory testing to evaluate the possibility of an infection. There are no management guidelines, and clinical decision-making relies on the underlying etiology.

In the absence of metal hardware, magnetic resonance imaging can be used to characterize the synovial fistula and cyst [4,6,13]. If preoperative confirmation of a fistula tract were necessary, a computed tomography arthrogram could be performed [5,6]. The computed tomography arthrogram exposes the patient to potential adverse effects and would not change clinical decision-making. No imaging modality used alone allows to rule out the possibility of an infection vs a tumor process [1]. In addition, the extent of the image interference created by the knee implant would make it difficult to trace the exact position where the fistulous tract starts.

Some authors use two simple methods for intraoperative identification of the synovial fistula: the saline load test or methylene blue dye [2,4]. Methylene blue staining was used to confirm the fistula’s location. One method has no clear advantage but allows better visualization of the capsule defect.

When reviewing records, it was found that the tibial insert used for primary TKA (Optetrak Logic Primary System; Exactech, Gainesville, FL) was on recall by the manufacturer due to a defect in packaging [15]. The oxidation of the polyethylene insert used in the primary TKA explains the advanced wear within 6 years. In addition, the effect of the oxidized insert in synovial tissue should be considered given this is the first case in the literature of a synovial fistula due to aseptic loosening and catastrophic polyethylene wear of a TKA.

After extensive debridement of the synovium and fistula, a gelatin-thrombin matrix (FloSeal; Baxter) was used to close the fistula tract. The use of this material has been reported for fistulas involving the gastrointestinal tract and in anastomotic leaks [16]. The gelatin-thrombin matrix forms a scaffold for native tissue ingrowth, allowing it to seal the fistula and reabsorb by 6-8 weeks. The main advantages are that it conforms to irregular shapes and swells 10%-20% of its volume, allowing closure [17]. Using the hemostatic matrix is easier and more cost-effective than flaps or grafts. Future studies on using this agent for synovial fistulas and its effectiveness in closure should be evaluated.

In the revision surgery, no significant soft-tissue defect was seen intraoperatively to consider the use of a flap or graft. However, in the case of a recurrence of a synovial fistula, this would be the next step in management after ruling out other causes [18,19].

Only one case report was found in which a fistula developed after arthroscopy in a patient with a unicondylar knee arthroplasty that had complete wear of the polyethylene insert and metal-induced synovitis [14]. The authors do not perform additional measures other than knee revision to address the synovial fistula. Failure to close the fistula after a revision surgery led to a prosthetic joint infection (PJI) that caused an additional revision surgery to take place [14].

The literature mentions that 2-week immobilization, although effective in most cases, can lead to recurrence, placing the patient at risk of a PJI [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. There is insufficient information to determine the risk of fistula recurrence leading to PJI if only revision surgery was performed together with postoperative immobilization. Lastly, undergoing an immobilization period is unsuitable for the patient after an arthroplasty procedure due to the risk of arthrofibrosis and adverse outcomes related to decreased range of motion.

Summary

This is the first reported case of a synovial fistula presenting as a cystic mass related to catastrophic polyethylene wear and aseptic loosening. The presence of an infection must be evaluated and excluded. In the case of a mass-like presentation, it is crucial to rule out a possible tumor. Intraoperative methylene blue could be used to identify the fistula tract and could be superior to the saline load test for visualization. After a TKA revision surgery, it is important to ensure the closure of the synovial fistula because recurrence increases the risk of PJI. A cost-effective and technically easier method was presented for synovial fistula closure with a gelatin-thrombin matrix (FloSeal; Baxter).
