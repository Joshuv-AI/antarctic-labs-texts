Artificial intelligence-driven cluster analysis for identifying clinical phenotypes in suspected sepsis patients in the emergency department
Authors: Daun Jeong, Jong Rul Park, Seung Jin Maeng, Jung Won Choi, Gun Tak Lee, Sung Yeon Hwang, Chulhong Kim, Jong Eun Park, Tae Gun Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdentifying high-risk patients in emergency departments (EDs) is crucial due to high mortality rate associated with sepsis and for the timely management. This study aimed to conduct unsupervised consensus clustering of patients with suspected sepsis from a heterogeneous ED population.MethodsThis descriptive, retrospective cohort study was conducted between November 2014 and November 2019 on patients with suspected sepsis who visited the ED. Cluster analysis was performed using variables, such as vital signs and clinical laboratory data, to classify patient characteristics. An artificial intelligence model generated a clustering plot based on the distance values of various variables, identifying five distinct clusters, each representing a unique clinical phenotype among the patient subtypes. In addition to analyzing the clinical phenotypes of each cluster, this study examined the prognosis based on antibiotic administration time through subgroup analysis to provide guidance for clinical application.ResultsA total of 14,402 patients were included in this study. Cluster analysis combined with an artificial intelligence model identified five distinct clusters among patients with suspected sepsis. The baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes of each of the five clusters are described in detail. Cluster B comprised the largest proportion of patients with septic shock and exhibited the highest percentage of critical care interventions, including vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit admission, and 28-d mortality. Cluster E had the lowest rates of these interventions. In multivariable analysis, administration of antibiotics within 3 h significantly decreased 28-d mortality in Cluster A (aOR 0.73; 95% CI, 0.54–0.98, p = 0.037) and Cluster E (aOR 0.59; 95% CI, 0.39–0.89, p = 0.013).ConclusionThe artificial intelligence model identified five distinct clusters of patients with suspected sepsis who presented to the ED and showed different outcome patterns among the phenotypes. Background

Sepsis is defined as a life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Pathophysiologically, septic shock is a subtype of sepsis characterized by circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities. Septic shock is responsible for approximately 40% of hospital mortality [1, 2] with an average 30-d sepsis mortality rate of 24.4% [3].

Given the high mortality rate associated with sepsis, significant efforts have been made to identify high-risk patients [4]. However, most existing models fail to capture the multilevel factors of sepsis that can influence treatment response and are limited by scoring systems [5, 6]. The complex clinical phenotypes of sepsis continue to challenge clinicians due to the heterogeneous circumstances encountered in the emergency department.

The identification of sepsis sub phenotypes has gained attention because of the syndrome’s heterogeneous nature [7, 8]. As sepsis is a broad condition with diverse clinical features affecting organ dysfunctions, it is necessary to classify heterogeneous patients into distinctive phenotypes or subsets [8, 9]. These diverse organ dysfunctions result in varying prognoses depending on the phenotypes [10–12]. Given the complex pathophysiology of sepsis, phenotypic classification is crucial to understand the unique features of each cluster.

Although time-critical therapy is recommended for all suspected sepsis, delayed recognition of sepsis due to atypical presentations, combined cases, hidden infection, or missed case in ED crowding contributes to serious harm and malpractice claims, ranging from approximately 8% to 21% [13–15]. Therefore, decision support tools to prioritizes early risk identification, complementing the clnical decision is required.

This study aimed to classify the clinical phenotypes of patients with suspected sepsis presented to the ED, using cluster analysis and artificial intelligence (AI). AI model learngraphical representations from the outputs of the cluster analysis, and classifies distinct phenotypes or subsets. The clinical characteristics of the identified sepsis subtypes are described follows. As an early intervention in sepsis is beneficial, we additionally analyzed prognosis based on antibiotic administration time through subgroup analysis to provide practical guidance for antibiotic administration guidance based on cluster analysis.

Methods

Study design, setting, population, and definition

This single-center, retrospective, descriptive study analyzed patients with suspected sepsis who visited the ED of Samsung Medical Center, a university-affiliated tertiary care referral hospital located in a metropolitan city with an annual visitor count of over 70,000. The study period was from November 2014 to November 2019.

The Third International Consensus Definitions Task Force (Sepsis-3) defines sepsis as a “life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.” Although this definition captures the overall concept, we adopted the operational criteria used in Sepsis-3 and the accompanying article by Seymour et al. [16]. According to Seymour et al., a ‘suspected infection’ was identified when both blood cultures were obtained and antibiotics were administered [16]. According to the Sepsis-3 definition,‘sepsis’ is characterized by an acute increase in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score (delta SOFA) of ≥ 2 points due to infection [16]. The baseline SOFA score was assumed to be zero unless the patient had preexisting organ dysfunction before the onset of infection. The maximum SOFA score (SOFA_max) was defined as the highest SOFA score within 24 h of arrival in the ED. ‘Septic shock’ was defined as sepsis accompanied by persistent hypotension, requiring vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥ 65 mmHg and a serum lactate level > 2 mmol/L, despite adequate volume resuscitation.

Patients aged 18 years and older with suspected sepsis who presented to the ED were included in this study. Suspected sepsis was defined as cases in which blood cultures were obtained, and antibiotics were administered in the ED [16]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presentation with cardiac arrest; (2) transfer to or from another hospital in the ED; (3) non-infectious conditions such as trauma or bleeding; (4) limitations to invasive care, including prior do-not-resuscitate orders or terminal malignancy; and (5) incomplete electronic medical records. All data were collected retrospectively from the electronic medical records (EMR) of patients who underwent blood culture and antibiotic administration in the ED between November 2014 and November 2019.

The following demographic and clinical data were collected from the electronic medical records: initial vital signs upon ED arrival, vital signs at the time of shock occurrence within 6 h of arrival in the ED, and demographic characteristics such as age, sex, comorbidities, suspected infectious focus, use of vasopressors, renal replacement therapy, and mechanical ventilation. Mortality outcomes, including the 28-d and in-hospital mortality rates, were tracked using the electronic clinical data of patients with suspected sepsis. Vital signs refers to systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), body temperature (BT). The time to antibiotic therapy was calculated by subtracting the ED triage time from the first broad-spectrum drug administration time. Among the suspected infection cohort, we also identified patients who met the new definition of sepsis, defined as a delta SOFA ≥ 2 points, as shown in Table 1.

Primary data analysis

We evaluated the eligibility of 17,733 adult patients who underwent blood culture and antibiotic administration in the ED between November 2014 and November 2019. After excluding patients with cardiac arrest, those transferred to or from another hospital, individuals without an infectious condition, those with limitations in invasive care, and those with inadequate data (n = 3,331), 14,402 patients were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Feature preprocessing and cluster analysis

This study employed machine learning-based cluster analysis using 14 features: vital signs and clinical laboratory data, including white blood cell count (WBC), lactate (Lac), platelet count (Plt), bilirubin (Bil), C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine (Cr), absolute neutrophil count (ANC), procalcitonin (PCT), and maximum Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA_max). The variables incorporated into the distance formulas were selected based on known prognostic factors associated with sepsis. Initial vital signs (vital signs_initial) and laboratory values were used in the data acquisition process, with ANC, Plt, and SOFA_max included as original values because of their suitability for direct use. All continuous variables were standardized using z-score normalization, while categorical variables were one-hot encoded. Missing values were imputed using median values. We then computed a scalar cluster index (CI) as a linear combination of pre-specified features. The Cluster Index was intentionally designed as a linear combination of multiple standardized clinical variables to reflect aggregate physiological severity. CI coefficients were predefined based on clinical rationale to balance contributions across major physiological domains, rather than estimated from outcome-based regression. Larger coefficients were assigned to variables representing non-redundant organ dysfunction domains to ensure meaningful representation in the composite index. Accordingly, CI stratification does not rely on any single dominant variable but emerges from the combined contribution of multiple components (Supplementary Table S3). K-means clustering was performed using the 14 z-score standardized clinical variables. The Cluster Index (CI) was computed separately and was not included as an input feature for k-means. CI was used solely for visualization and image-based encoding in the neural network refinement model. For clarity, we use CI to denote this score and reserve ‘distance’ exclusively for the Euclidean distance used by k-means. Applying artificial intelligence model by imaging data

Although k-means deterministically assigns each patient to a single cluster, assignment certainty can be low for borderline cases with similar geometric proximity to multiple centroids. These clinically ambiguous patients are of particular concern in emergency settings because they may still be at high risk of deterioration despite an initial low-risk numerical label. To support more reliable phenotype assignment in these ambiguous regions, we constructed a continuous Cluster Index (CI) that summarizes multivariate clinical variables Based on the distribution of CI values, patients were operationally categorized into five ordered phenotype groups (A–E). This design enables refinement of borderline classifications by resolving assignment ambiguity near decision boundaries and enhancing clinical decision support for early escalation. We did not train a de-novo tabular neural classifier as the primary model because the unsupervised k-means step preserves phenotype discovery without outcome labels, and the role of Neural Network is a post-hoc refinement of boundary confidence rather than label creation.

Therefore, we additionally applied an imaging-based neural network as a post hoc refinement layer. Specifically, CI-derived continuous values were encoded as grayscale image representations, and a neural network was trained to predict the CI-defined phenotype group (A–E), which served as operational phenotype labels for refinement. Because the model operates on continuous CI-derived representations rather than discrete cluster identifiers, it can learn nonlinear patterns over the continuous representation space and thereby provide more stable assignments in regions where k-means centroid separation is less distinct. Importantly, this refinement step is intended to improve assignment reliability for borderline cases rather than to redefine the underlying unsupervised phenotype structure or to claim de novo supervised label creation. We did not develop a de novo tabular supervised classifier as the primary model, because the unsupervised k-means step was retained to preserve phenotype discovery independent of outcome labels, and the role of the neural network was limited to operational refinement of phenotype assignment confidence. A stratified 90/10 split by CI-defined phenotype group (A–E) was used to create a held-out evaluation set; no patient appeared in more than one dataset, preventing data leakage between development and testing.

In this framework, k-means clustering defines the coarse phenotypic structure, while the CI-based neural network provides a nonlinear refinement layer that stabilizes phenotype assignment near decision boundaries without redefining the original unsupervised clusters. The cluster structure remains unchanged, but individual assignments may differ for boundary cases. The imaging encoder preserves subtle continuous differences in CI-related values, enabling the neural network to detect fine-grained changes that may be difficult to capture using hard centroid thresholds alone. Because the refinement model is trained to predict CI-defined phenotypes from continuous CI-derived representations, it provides an operational refinement of assignment reliability near phenotype boundaries rather than merely reproducing k-means centroid-based thresholds. This enhances discrimination by learning nonlinear mappings over continuous CI representations, particularly near regions of overlap. The AI recognized subtle differences in the encoded color corresponding to each distance value. The ability to discriminate between groups can be improved when AI is used to enhance discrimination by learning nonlinear mappings over continuous CI representations, as it enables the recognition of minimal changes in those values.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the five clusters, including their vital signs and laboratory test results. Among the 14,402 patients, 7,838 (54.4%) were male and 5,589 (44.2%) had sepsis, defined by an acute increase in SOFA score of 2 or higher (Delta SOFA score ≥ 2). Cluster B included the highest proportion of sepsis patients (71.1%), followed by Clusters A (46.9%) and Cluster C (42.9%). Cluster B exhibited the highest SOFA_max score (5.0, 95% CI: 3.0–8.0), lactate level (2.16, 95% CI: 1.41–3.46), and CRP (8.14, 95% CI: 3.05–16.92) followed by cluster A. Elevated levels of lactate and CRP, along with a higher SOFA score, suggest a more severe form of sepsis and potentially poorer outcomes, as these parameters are valuable tools in assessing the severity, predicting prognosis, and guiding treatment decisions [26]. Therefore, if a patient is assigned to Cluster B or A, the physician may anticipate severe sepsis or a potentially poor outcome.

Additionally, Cluster B had the highest proportion of patients with chronic cardiac disease or metastatic cancer. The number of missing values is detailed in Supplementary Table S1. After excluding inadequate data, including missing data, the analysis of patients with septic shock did not incorporate vasopressor administration time, duration, or missing vital signs_shock data. Since only the vital signs of patients who experienced hypotension within 6 h from the ED were additionally collected, variables labeled as vital signs_shock existed only in these subgroups.

Table 2 presents the clinical outcomes of the five clusters. The overall proportion of patients with septic shock was 1,118 (7.8%), and the vasopressor requirement was 1695 (11.8%). The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 676 (4.7%) in the entire cohort, ranging from 3.3% to 8.8% across clusters. The 28-d mortality rate of the entire cohort was 989 (6.9%), and 280 (8.4%), 121 (12.3%), 233 (6.3%), 236 (5.7%), and 119 (5.4%) across the clusters, respectively. Cluster B exhibited the poorest outcomes, including the highest rates of septic shock (302; 30.8%), critical care use (described below), in-hospital mortality (86; 8.8%), and 28-d mortality (163; 4.4%). Cluster B also had the highest vasopressor requirement (406; 41.4%), mechanical ventilator use (70; 7.1%), and intensive care unit admission (181; 18.5%), followed by Cluster A. Cluster E had the lowest rates across these metrics. Physicians may predict poor progression if a patient is allocated to Cluster B using the clustering algorithm employed in this study. It takes at least 2–3 h to obtain blood test results after ED arrival, with additional time required to calculate the SOFA score. Therefore, before the manual calculation of the SOFA score, AI-based clustering results can be used to predict sepsis severity and contribute to a more comprehensive assessment. Notably, Cluster B, which exhibited the highest severity of illness, initiated vasopressors and antibiotics the earliest and maintained their use the longest. The median time to initiate vasopressor therapy was 6.3 h (95% CI: 2.3–33.1), with Cluster B starting vasopressor the earliest time at 3.3 h after ED arrival (95% CI: 1.5–6.3), followed by cluster A at 4.4 h (95% CI: 1.9–13.2). Similarly, Cluster B initiated antibiotics the earliest at 1.5 h after ED arrival (95% CI: 0.5–3.1), followed by cluster A, while cluster B had the longest duration of antibiotics use at 7.3 h (95% CI: 0.4–16.4). Early initiation of antibiotics and vasopressors in Clusters B and A reflected the high severity of these groups, with the fastest physician recognition of severity among all clusters. However, given the guidelines recommending the administration of antibiotics and vasopressors within 1 h of septic shock recognition [27, 28], the median administration time in our study was delayed. Therefore, before the manual calculation of the SOFA score to diagnose sepsis, physicians may intervene promptly using the AI-based clustering method if the patient is allocated to cluster B or A. Thus, the AI-based clustering method may support clinical judgment before the manual calculation of the SOFA score.

Discussion

This study employed unsupervised clustering using artificial intelligence on an ED database to categorize patients with suspected infections. The clinical phenotypes of the five identified clusters were correlated with clinical outcomes. In particular, artificial intelligence-based clustering effectively identified high-severity groups characterized by higher rates of septic shock, critical care needs, in-hospital mortality, and 28-d mortality. Additionally, the relationship between antibiotic administration and 28-d mortality varied across clusters, suggesting that the effect of treatment may differ depending on the patient’s phenotype.

The heterogeneous nature of sepsis has led to an increased interest in investigating its phenotypes [7, 8, 18, 29]. Recent studies have shown sepsis phenotypes with clinical relevance are often limited to specific populations. For example, some studies have focused on ICU settings [10, 30], while others, such as those by Guilamet et al., identified four clusters within homogenous infectious diseases [31] and Lai et al. conducted clustering to identify subphenotypes in patients with dialysis-requiring sepsis-associated acute kidney injury [32]. However, few studies have explored clinical sepsis phenotypes in heterogeneous populations, which present a major challenge in grouping patients [11]. Additionally, only a limited number of studies have categorized heterogeneous groups based on hemodynamic phenotyping using vital signs [7, 33, 34]. Given that blood pressure is routinely measured in the ED, incorporating systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) into cluster analysis may offer a more precise and timely identification of phenotypes [10]. This study addressed the dynamic phenotypic changes in patients with septic shock by incorporating both initial vital signs and those recorded at the time of shock. The strengths of this descriptive study include its use of both shock time and baseline vital signs, artificial intelligence-based cluster analysis, a large dataset, and applicability to a diverse ED population, including low-risk patients.

Our result showed that cluster B had the highest rates of metastatic cancer, septic shock, critical care usage, in-hospital mortality, and 28-d mortality. Additionally, we found that early antibiotic administration within 3 h significantly reduced the 28-d mortality in Clusters A and E. Notably, the cluster with the most severe patients started vasopressors and antibiotics earlier than the other clusters, while also maintaining the longest duration of treatment. Previous studies have highlighted the heterogeneity in the presentation and timing of antibiotic initiation for suspected septic shock [35] and the relationship between the duration of the antibiotic and clinical outcomes remains contentious [31, 36]. However, several studies have demonstrated that the early initiation of antibiotics benefits sepsis patients [37]. Our findings also align with recent research indicating that cancer patients are at a significantly higher risk for sepsis and associated mortality, with hospital mortality being higher in cancer patients than in those without cancer (hazard ratio, 1.25; 95% CI, 1.24 − 1.26) [38]. The overall 28-d mortality rate in our cohort was 6.9%, with rates ranging from 5.4% to 12.3% across clusters, consistent with previous reports [39].

Many studies have established scoring systems for risk stratification in the ED [6]. However, traditional scoring systems have limitations when applied to patients who meet the Sepsis-3 criteria (SOFA score ≥ 2). Sepsis-3 diagnostic criteria narrow the sepsis population, potentially leading to false negatives and resulting in delayed or missed diagnoses [40]. In the difficult cases in recognition of sepsis including hidden infection, atypical presentations, combined cases, or missed case in ED crowding, previous studies still emphasizes the importance of sepsis alert systems to prevent the mistakes [13–15, 41].

Therefore, to prevent the mistakes or missed case, clustering analysis combined with AI could be optimized for the sepsis-3 criteria helping to uncover the hidden biological elements of sepsis. Given the complex biology of sepsis, the potential role of metabolic biomarkers, and existing knowledge gaps among healthcare workers, understanding the clinical phenotypes of sepsis through clustering and AI is essential [42, 43]. AI-driven clustering may indicate that multimodal factors are the key predictors of sepsis.

Treatment tailoring has become more prevalent in recent years as the prediction of outcomes using simple scoring systems alone is increasingly limited. The primary goal of identifying clinical phenotypes and host responses related to clinical outcomes is to provide additional guidance for healthcare providers working in diverse and complex environments. Given the high mortality rate associated with sepsis, a personalized approach to treatment is essential, as different phenotypes may respond differently to various treatment strategies [10, 12]. According to Aaron et al., identifying these phenotypes enables personalized care [11]. Understanding these phenotypes using our method would be valuable for identifying sepsis and managing patients in ways that account for individual differences in clinical practice.

Clinically, the result of cluster allocation using an AI-based clustering algorithm can be applied in many ways. The result of phenotyping can be used as the decision support tool to predict sepsis severity, augment recognition and proactive intervention. If the patient is categorized into Cluster A or B, physicians predict a higher likelihood of poor prognosis, may initiate blood cultures, administer antibiotics, and begin close monitoring even before manual calculation of the SOFA score. Since clusters enable the process evaluation including time to antibiotics subanalyses, physicians can understand the phenotypes of the patient and discuss the disposition of the patient with other department based on the results in Table 2. Also, phenotyping result provides a backup safety net for complex and hidden sepsis, and addresses missed or late identification that contributes to patient harm and medico-legal exposure [13].

The AI-based clustering model presented in this study should be regarded as a supplementary tool for clinical decision-making, rather than a definitive diagnostic guide. Importantly, we did not use supervised outcome labels to drive the initial clustering. This preserves unbiased phenotype discovery while allowing the neural network to function only as a refinement tool to improve cluster boundary reliability. The refinementep is intended to clarify k-means boundary assignments using continuous cluster index information, rather than to claim superior classification accuracy, which is not directly definable in unsupervised phenotype discovery.

This approach, which integrates multiple clinical variables, may assist in identifying complex sepsis phenotypes and supporting timely therapeutic decisions, particularly in cases with heterogeneous clinical features or when physicians face challenges interpreting the trajectory of sepsis due to disease severity. From a clinical perspective, the primary value of this framework lies not in demonstrating superior classification accuracy, but in providing an early decision-support safety net in complex emergency department environments. Missed or delayed recognition of sepsis due to atypical presentations, hidden infection, or ED crowding remains a preventable harm [13, 44, 45]. By offering phenotype classification before formal SOFA calculation, AI-based clustering may support earlier escalation, closer monitoring, and timely antibiotic administration in patients at risk of deterioration [46, 47]. In such contexts, AI-driven clustering output may serve as an additional reference to enhance physician judgment, including guidance on antibiotic administration timing. Therefore, the clustering results may provide additional guidance for physicians by characterizing the clinical phenotypes of each of the five clusters.

This study has several limitations. First, the data was obtained from a single center, meaning that the groupings may not apply to other hospitals or settings. Second, cluster analysis requires a larger volume of data to capture the underlying risk in the acquisition environment effectively. Third, the black-box nature of AI algorithms limits interpretability, warranting caution in their clinical applications. As emphasized in prior studies on explainable AI in emergency medicine [48], such models should be used in conjunction with established clinical assessments to ensure safe and effective implementation. Fourth, excluding patients with false-positive suspected infection may limit applicability to real-world clinical environments. Fifth, causal inference cannot be made due to the retrospective observational design.”Finally, after implementing our method in real clinical practice, further feedback through sepsis surveillance will be essential for refinement and validation.

Despite these limitations, this study demonstrated the value of using cluster analysis and artificial intelligence to classify clinical phenotypes among patients with suspected sepsis in the ED. When the model is expanded to a multicenter level with external validation, phenotype identification may be instrumental in risk stratification and guiding future clinical management strategies.

Conclusion

The artificial intelligence model generated five distinct clusters of patients with suspected sepsis who visited the ED. These phenotypes exhibited different clinical characteristics and outcome patterns. While these findings suggest potential value for future risk stratification, prospective external validation is needed to determine how phenotype information may assist clinical decision.
