Regional variation in temperature control after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest
Authors: Iana Meitlis, Jane Hall, Navya Gunaje, Megin Parayil, Betty Y Yang, Kyle Danielson, Catherine R Counts, Christopher Drucker, Charles Maynard, Thomas D Rea, Peter J. Kudenchuk, Michael R Sayre, Nicholas J Johnson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWe evaluated hospitals for variation in temperature control (TC) use after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in a regional emergency medical services system and assessed association of hospital-level TC utilization with survival.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of adults with non-traumatic OHCA who survived to hospital admission from 2016 to 2018 in King County, Washington. Hospitals with < 80 OHCA cases were excluded. Primary exposure was hospital-level proportion of TC. Measured outcomes were survival to hospital discharge and neurologically favorable survival (defined as Cerebral Performance Category 1 or 2). Logistic regression modeling clustered patients by treating hospital and evaluated associations between TC and outcomes with covariate adjustment.ResultsOf 1,035 eligible patients admitted to eight hospitals, 69% were male, 38% had an initial shockable rhythm, and 61% had presumed cardiac etiology for OHCA. TC was initiated in 787 patients (74%) and ranged from 57 to 87% across hospitals. Overall, 34% of patients survived neurologically intact, 74% of whom received TC. In the adjusted model, public OHCA location (OR: 1.7 [95% CI 1.3–2.3]), witnessed arrest (OR: 1.6 [1.2–2.2]), and shockable rhythm (OR: 5.5 [3.9–7.8]) were more strongly associated with survival than TC utilization (OR: 0.6 [0.4–0.8]). Similar results were seen for neurologically favorable survival and did not vary significantly by hospital.ConclusionsHospital-level TC utilization was not associated with improved survival or neurologically favorable survival after OHCA. Future studies should examine which aspects of the post-cardiac arrest care bundle most strongly influence outcomes.

Introduction

Approximately 350,000 patients in the United States suffer out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) yearly. Research and public health efforts have focused on improving the links in the chain of survival1: early arrest recognition and emergency activation, early CPR and defibrillation, advanced resuscitation, and expert post-arrest care, including recovery. Despite these efforts, the average survival rates to hospital discharge for OHCA patients across the US remains at approximately 10 %2. This average survival belies the substantial variation in regional outcomes, suggesting opportunities for improvement if we understand the underlying reasons for survival disparity3.

Guidelines recommend temperature control (TC) for all comatose OHCA patients who achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). TC has been integrated into resuscitation guidelines since 2002, after a series of trials showed that patients undergoing controlled hypothermia within the initial 4–6 h post arrest had better survival odds to hospital discharge with a favorable neurological outcome4. Since then, controversy evolved around specific parameters of TC implementation, with studies providing conflicting evidence for optimal duration and depth of cooling to achieve better outcomes5. Variation also exists in how these study findings have been interpreted. As a consequence, TC has been variably implemented, producing substantial differences in TC use at both the patient and hospital level, which may contribute to differential outcomes after cardiac arrest6, 7.

In this study, we investigate hospital-level variation in TC use within a single regional emergency medical service (EMS) system with relatively uniform training and approach to prehospital cardiac arrest care, and test whether hospital-level TC use is associated with outcomes. We hypothesize that patients treated at hospitals that provide TC more frequently have improved outcomes compared to those treated at hospitals with lower TC utilization.

Methods

Study Design, Population, and setting

The University of Washington Human Subjects Division Institutional Review Board approved this study. The investigation was a retrospective cohort study of persons ≥ 18 years of age suffering OHCA in King County, Washington, USA from 2016 to 2018 who achieved ROSC and survived to hospital admission. We excluded patients who did not receive TC for the following reasons: 1. awake or alert prior to TC initiation, 2. designated for comfort care measures only, 3. pulseless on ED arrival, 4. evidence of active bleeding or, 5. hemodynamically unstable. For hospital-level analyses, we further restricted our study to hospitals that admitted at least 80 OHCA patients during the study period to assure stable analytical models. To maintain site anonymity, individual hospital OHCA case volumes are not reported, and hospitals are randomly labeled A-H.

King County, Washington has a population of approximately 2.2 million, covers about 2300 square miles, and includes urban, suburban, rural, and wilderness areas. King County deploys a two-tiered EMS system. The first-tier response is by emergency medical technicians equipped with automated external defibrillators. The second-tier response is advanced life support (ALS) by paramedics trained via a single program based at Harborview Medical Center in rhythm identification, manual defibrillation, intravenous access, intubation, and medication administration. King County EMS maintains a robust cardiac arrest registry that is organized according to the Utstein template8, 9, 10. Trained coordinators abstract information from dispatch and defibrillator recordings, EMS reports, and entire hospital medical records to code demographic, circumstance, care, and outcome information. Data was prospectively collected comprehensively to mitigate potential selection bias.

Exposures

The primary exposure was proportion of patients who received TC at each hospital. We defined this as the proportion of patients who received active temperature management using surface or endovascular cooling to goal temperatures of 32-36° C.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was survival to hospital discharge. Secondary outcome was survival with a favorable neurologic outcome defined as cerebral performance category (CPC) 1 or 2. The CPC was measured through holistic review of hospital records and chosen as a measured outcome for its association with long-term prognosis and interrater reliability11, 12.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistical methods to present and compare characteristics. Unadjusted associations between TC and survival, and between TC and favorable neurologic status at discharge (CPC 1–2), were evaluated using a two-sample test of proportions.

A two-stage modeling approach was used to investigate adjusted associations between TC and outcomes. In Model 1, only patient-level data were examined for associations with outcomes via logistic regression, while adjusting for the following patient-level covariates selected a priori: age under 6513, sex, location of arrest, witnessed status, presumed cardiac etiology, time to ALS arrival and initial shockable rhythm. In Model 2, both patient-level data and data at the level of each treating hospital were incorporated into the model via hierarchical logistic regression, allowing for description of variance within and between hospitals. Grouping by hospital also allowed the addition of a hospital-level covariate, the rate of hospital-specific TC use, to be included in the model. In this model, hospital-specific rate of TC use was selected to serve as a proxy for an individual hospital’s experience or expertise with TC, which was hypothesized to influence effect14.

Unadjusted analyses were also performed to examine association between TC and outcomes in two subgroups 1) patient cohorts based off initial cardiac rhythm, given that patients with non-shockable rhythms are known to suffer worse outcomes and for whom there are conflicting literature data on the benefits of TC, and 2) patients with known target temperatures, to examine relationships between degree of cooling and outcomes. Due to the smaller sample sizes in these sub-analyses, covariate adjustment was not performed to prevent overfitting15.

Patients with missing covariate data needed for modeling (<1%) were excluded from analyses. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was used to establish statistical significance. All analyses were performed using Stata 15 (College Station, TX).

Results

Patient demographics and OHCA characteristics

There were 4,270 OHCA patients during the study period, of whom 1,035 were admitted to the hospital and eligible for TC (Fig. 1). Of these, mean age was 59 (SD 16.9) years and 69 % were male. A total of 38 % had initial shockable rhythm, 61 % had presumed cardiac etiology, and 61 % received bystander CPR. Average time for ALS arrival after a 911 call was 10.4 min. TC was initiated in 769 eligible patients (74 %). Most patients receiving TC were men and experienced the arrest in a residential setting, had a cardiac etiology of arrest, presented with a shockable cardiac rhythm, and received bystander CPR (Table 1). Percentage of patients with shockable initial cardiac rhythm was significantly higher among patients who received TC. Hospital characteristics

Patients were admitted to one of eight receiving hospitals. Hospitals admitted a median of 98 (IQR = 92–134) OHCA cases during the study period with a range of 87–255 cases. Among included hospitals, TC use ranged from 58 % to 87 %, survival to hospital discharge ranged from 34 % to 46 %, and neurologically favorable survival ranged from 31 % to 38 % (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Comparison of unadjusted rates of TC, survival, and neurologically favorable survival per hospital (A-H) during the study period.

Unadjusted comparisons of outcomes by Receipt of TC

Overall, 39 % of patients survived to hospital discharge and 34 % had favorable neurological status (CPC 1–2). In unadjusted comparisons, no significant difference in survival to discharge was observed between patients who received TC (40 % of patients, [95 % confidence interval (CI) 36–43 %]) versus patients who did not receive TC (38 % [95 % CI 32–44 %]). Similarly, there was no statistically significant difference in neurologically favorable survival between patients who received TC (34 % [95 % CI 30–37 %]) and those who did not (34 % [95 % CI 29–40 %]).

Adjusted associations between TC and OHCA outcomes

In Model 1, when adjusting for patient-level covariates only, several factors were associated with higher odds of survival to discharge and neurologically favorable survival: age under 65 years, public location of arrest, witnessed arrest, and shockable initial cardiac rhythm (Fig. 3). After adjustment for these covariates, TC was associated with lower odds of survival (adjusted OR = 0.6, [95 % CI 0.4–0.8]) and neurologically favorable survival (aOR = 0.5, [95 % CI 0.4–0.7]). In Model 2, when adjusting for patient-level covariates while accounting for variation across hospitals, the same associations were observed as in Model 1. Hospital-specific rate of TC use was not found to be associated with either outcome. Variation between hospitals was found to be minimal (variance < 0.02) for both outcomes. Subgroup analysis 1: Unadjusted outcomes according to initial cardiac rhythm

We then examined unadjusted associations between TC and outcomes within patient subgroups with shockable (N = 394) and non-shockable (N = 641) initial cardiac arrest rhythms, to determine if certain rhythms would benefit more from TC. In the shockable cohort, unadjusted comparison of survival between patients who received TC (63 %, 95 % CI [58–68 %]) versus those that did not (53 %, 95 % CI [37–68 %]), did not show statistically significant difference. Similarly, no statistical difference was observed in neurologically favorable survival between the TC group (57 %, 95 % CI [51–62 %]) and no TC group (50 %, 95 % CI [35–65 %]). Hierarchical modeling was not performed due to insufficient and unbalanced sample size between the TC and no TC groups within the shockable sub-cohort.

Conversely, in the non-shockable cohort, TC was associated with lower unadjusted survival rates at 20 % (95 % CI [16–24 %]) versus no TC at 35 % (95 % CI [29–42 %]), and lower rates of neurologically favorable survival at 14 % (95 % CI [11–17 %]) versus no TC at 31 %, (95 % CI [25–37 %]). In hierarchical modeling of the non-shockable subgroup, initiation of TC remained associated with lower odds of survival (aOR = 0.5, 95 % CI [0.3–0.7]), as well as neurologically favorable survival (aOR = 0.4, 95 % CI [0.2––0.6]). Hospital-level variation was minimal (variance < 1 x 10-15 in both models).

Subgroup analyses 2: Unadjusted outcomes according to goal temperature

We also conducted an exploratory, unadjusted analysis on a subset of the TC cohort (55 %) with known goal temperatures to determine whether lower target temperatures were associated with improved outcomes. Lower target temperatures of 33-34° C (N = 228) were not associated with either higher rates of survival (42 %, [95 % CI 35–48 %]) compared with the No TC cohort (38 %, 95 % CI [32–44 %]) or higher rates of neurologically favorable survival (36 %, [95 % CI 30–42 %]) versus the No TC cohort (34 %, [95 % CI 29–40 %]). Similarly, patients who were cooled to 36° C (N = 199) did not have better survival (39 %, 95 % CI [32–45 %]) or neurologically favorable survival (28 %, 95 % CI [22–34 %]) relative to the No TC group. Hierarchical modeling was not performed due to insufficient sample size for inter-hospital comparison.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we investigated the association between TC application and patient outcomes after OHCA across eight hospitals in a regional EMS system. Contrary to our hypothesis, we found that increasing hospital-level TC utilization was not associated with improved survival to hospital discharge or neurologically favorable survival. Patient demographics and cardiac arrest characteristics were more strongly associated with favorable outcomes.

Significant variation in TC utilization across different care centers in the US and internationally has been previously described3, 16. Khera et al17 examined 186 hospitals in the US and found two-fold variation in TC application among hospitals and wide variation in time of initiation, duration, and target temperature. With regional variation in patient characteristics and resuscitation efforts, studying medical interventions like TC within distinct geographical regions allows to control for differences in certain patient, community, and EMS characteristics18. In 2020, Berger et al19 found a 12-fold variation in TC use between 39 hospitals across Michigan state, with a 4-fold variation in survival rates, and 8-fold variation in survival with CPC1-2. After a multivariable analysis, their results showed that TC was inversely associated with both survival and favorable neurological outcomes. This could imply that more severely ill patients were selected for TC, but also challenges the potential effectiveness of the intervention. Conversely, when examining TC use across 36 hospitals in a large, single EMS system in Los Angeles, Hermel20 et al found that TC was significantly associated with higher odds of neurologically favorable survival, in direct contrast to Berger and our results. Discrepancy amongst studies, suggests that the role TC plays in patient outcomes may depend on multiple factors, such as quality of other resuscitation efforts, patient selection for TC, or perhaps other care practices that track with TC utilization.

We also considered baseline differences in patient characteristics between the TC and No TC groups to better understand our results. Like in other studies, TC was more likely to be implemented in male patients with initially shockable rhythm and cardiac etiology. 21, 22, 23 Interestingly, in contrast to previous studies, initiating TC in patients with more favorable prognostic characteristics was associated with significantly worse survival. When we further examined a sub-group with non-shockable initial rhythms, we found similarly worse survival outcomes associated with TC use. Initiating TC in patients with non-shockable rhythms is widely debated. Some studies showed benefit24, 25 while others showed no improvement or worse outcomes26, 27, 28, though the quality of these studies varied29. We suspect unmeasured confounders (e.g. pre-existing comorbidities) and bias of selecting more critically ill patients for TC may limit interpretation of our results.

Finally, we considered secular changes in other patient characteristics or TC implementation as possible explanations for our observations. After TTM1 study in 201330, most King County hospitals converted to a target temperature of 36° C. However, several studies since, including at our own institution, have shown that cooler target temperatures (33-34° C) were associated with higher survival odds to hospital discharge and that both TC effectiveness and implementation rates significantly decreased with protocol changes to 36° C31. In our unadjusted sub-group analysis of patients with known target temperatures, we did not find higher survival in patients cooled to 33-34° C or 36° C versus the No TC group. It is also possible that precipitating causes of OHCA have evolved over time. E.g., our region, like many in the US, has seen a marked increase in OHCA cases related to opioid overdose, which portends poor prognosis32. However, even after adjusting for arrest etiology, we did not observe improved survival in TC patients. Based on our results, we suspect that while changes in target temperatures and patient characteristics have occurred over time, these factors alone do not fully explain the observed survival trends in post-cardiac arrest care.

Our findings emerge in context of multiple previous studies, most notably the TTM1 and TTM2 trials, that found no improvement in either survival to hospital discharge or neurological function associated with different target temperature. 5, 30, 33 These studies have faced some criticism for design and implementation, e.g. patient cohorts not representing the wider US OHCA patient population given high rates of witnessed arrests, bystander CPR, and shockable initial rhythms34. Although our patient cohort did not have as high rates of favorable characteristics as in those two trials, our community has historically had high bystander CPR rates, excellent EMS care, and relatively high OHCA survival35. The similarities between our results and those of the TTM1-2 studies raise concern whether broader populations treated in real-world circumstances are more likely to benefit from TC. Overall, our results highlight the need for further research to better understand the appropriate use of TC in OHCA patients and to identify subgroups who may benefit from this intervention.

It is important for both hospitals and EMS systems to identify specific components of the post-arrest treatment bundle that result in improved outcomes. Regionalized care for cardiac arrest has been proposed36, 37, 38 as higher volume hospitals have been thought to have better outcomes. However, a recent randomized trial in the United Kingdom demonstrating no difference between immediate transport to specialty centers and prompt care at the closest hospital counters this concept39. The absence of a significant association between TC use and patient outcomes in our study, despite high rates of utilization, raises questions about the benefit of TC in OHCA care. It is possible, TC acts more generally as a proxy for high-quality care and adding TC to an already optimized system does not have a substantial effect on outcomes. It is also possible that we have yet to find the ideal target population for this therapy, or how to deliver it early enough to be effective. Experimental models and some clinical data suggest that earlier implementation of TC, perhaps even during the OHCA event, might lead to improved outcomes40, 41, 42. A definitive trial of prehospital, ultrafast hypothermia is underway43. It continues to be a consideration whether care centers should prioritize optimizing TC or focus on other resuscitation aspects, e.g. community CPR education, optimizing EMS protocols, and refining hospital practices, especially around prognostication and prevention of inappropriate withdrawal of life sustaining therapies.

Limitations

Because this study was retrospective, we can only comment on the association between TC and outcomes, not causation. Additionally, there is possibility that unmeasured confounders accounted for the selection of patients for TC and the observed association between hospital-level TC utilization and neurologic function. In particular, limited data were available on post-arrest care characteristics. This study was performed within a single EMS system that has been traditionally regarded as high performing in terms of cardiac arrest outcomes, so our findings may differ from those observed in other settings. Determination of neurological status by CPC is potentially subject to bias. Although previous studies have demonstrated high inter-rater reliability among trained abstractors, our data abstractors were not blinded to other aspects of care, which may have introduced bias14. Long-term post-discharge outcome data were not available, though previous data from our system and others indicate that discharge CPC correlates well with 1-year outcomes44, 45. Finally, our dataset did not contain granular information on temperature management for all patients, so we could not report TC protocol adherence in detail.

Conclusion

Hospital-level utilization of TC was not associated with either survival or neurologically favorable survival after OHCA. While significant hospital-level variation in TC utilization exists within our regional EMS system, OHCA characteristics remain most strongly associated with survival and neurologically favorable survival. Future research should focus on identifying patients who may benefit most from specific TC strategies based on clinical or biological phenotypes, examining earlier application of TC, and refining other aspects of the post-cardiac arrest bundle, including prognostication.
