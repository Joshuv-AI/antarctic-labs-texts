Synthesis and Evaluation of Fluorinated Peptidomimetics Enabling the Development of 18F‐Labeled Radioligands Targeting Muscarinic Acetylcholine Receptor Subtype M3
Authors: Miguel A. Herrera‐Rueda, Maxwell A. Boateng, Melinda Wuest, Frederick G. West, Frank Wuest
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The muscarinic acetylcholine receptor subtype M3 is validated as a promising target for imaging and therapy in breast cancer. Transcriptomic and proteomic profiling reveal its overexpression, particularly in triple‐negative breast cancer. To overcome challenges in developing selective M3 inhibitors, fluorinated peptidomimetic compounds are designed featuring a β‐alanine‐glycine scaffold that mimics key M3 pharmacophore elements. These constructs show strong potential as lead structures for 18F‐labeled positron emission tomography (PET) radioligands, as demonstrated with lead compound 24, exhibiting M3‐selective binding (M1/M3, M2/M3, M4/M3, and M5/M3 > 45) with an IC50 value of 0.3 μM. Compound [ 18 F]24 is prepared as the first M3‐selective PET radioligand using reductive amination with 4‐[18F]fluorobenzaldehyde, in a 35% decay‐corrected radiochemical yield. Radioligand [ 18 F]24 is tested for PET imaging of M3 receptors in preclinical breast cancer models.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer among women, accounting for 25% of all new cancer diagnoses and 14% of cancer‐related deaths worldwide.[ 1 ] While advances in early detection and treatment have improved 5‐year survival rates to 78–90%, metastatic breast cancer, particularly the triple‐negative subtype, remains incurable.[ 2 ] It is associated with earlier recurrence and poorer survival outcomes, particularly in stage IV disease. Current therapies, including immunotherapy, PARP inhibitors, and antibody‐drug conjugates, have shown limited survival benefits.[ 3 , 4 ] Given breast cancer's molecular heterogeneity, there is an urgent need to identify subtype‐specific biomarkers to enable earlier detection and the development of novel targeted therapies, especially for patients with metastatic breast cancer.

Muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (mAChRs) constitute a family of five homologous transmembrane G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs), designated M1 through M5. These receptors have been extensively explored as therapeutic targets for neurological and pulmonary disorders, as well as treating overactive bladder, primarily through M2 and M3 antagonists.[ 5 , 6 – 7 ]

Beyond their classical roles in gastrointestinal, urinary bladder, vascular smooth muscle, and airway tissue, emerging evidence highlights the aberrant expression of mAChRs, particularly the M3 subtype (M3R), in a range of epithelial and endothelial‐derived malignancies, including lung, gastric, colorectal, and skin cancers.[ 8 ] Recent studies further demonstrate the upregulation of M3R in breast cancers, especially in the triple‐negative subtype, where M3R signaling is implicated in promoting tumor cell proliferation and metastatic behavior.[ 8 , 9 ]

The development of potent and selective M3 receptor ligands, including M3‐selective radioligands for positron emission tomography (PET), remains a significant challenge despite the availability of the M3 receptor crystal structure.[ 10 ] Most PET radioligands targeting mAChRs in both preclinical and clinical studies are 11C‐ or 18F‐labeled small‐molecule inhibitors lacking subtype specificity and are primarily used for PET imaging applications in the central and peripheral nervous systems.[ 11 , 12 ]

Notable exceptions include radioligands [18F]FP‐TZTP (M2‐selective), [11C]GSK1034702 (M1‐selective), and [11C]MK‐6884 (M4‐selective) (Figure 1 ).[ 11 ] However, no PET radioligand with selectivity for the M3 subtype has been reported to date.

Subtype‐selective muscarinic acetylcholine receptor PET radioligands.

This lack of selectivity stems from the high degree of structural homology across the orthosteric binding sites of the five mAChR subtypes (M1–M5), presenting a significant obstacle in the design of highly selective M3‐targeted imaging agents and therapeutics.

Structure‐activity relationship (SAR) analyses of nonselective muscarinic antagonists and high‐affinity M3‐selective antagonists such as darifenacin have identified key pharmacophoric features necessary for M3 selectivity: a bulky nonpolar cluster, a cationic nitrogen‐containing moiety, and a hydrophobic site adjacent to the nitrogen atom (Figure 2 ).

Darifenacin‐derived orthosteric M3 pharmacophore.

To enhance spatial flexibility and subtype selectivity, Sagara et al. developed a combinatorial library by systematically modifying the spatial arrangement of the M3 pharmacophore.[ 13 ] This strategy led to the discovery of novel M3‐selective antagonists exhibiting subnanomolar K i values and high selectivity over M1, M2, M4, and M5 receptor subtypes.

Building on this structural framework for an M3 pharmacophore, the objective of the present study was to design, synthesize, and evaluate a new class of fluorine‐containing peptidomimetics targeting M3 mAChRs (M3Rs) in breast cancer, building on the darifenacin‐derived orthosteric pharmacophore (Figure 2) to expedite lead compound development. A glycine‐β‐alanine dipeptide linker was introduced between the nonpolar and cationic pharmacophore elements to optimize molecular flexibility and receptor binding. Additionally, the hydrophobic character of the M3 pharmacophore is expected to facilitate efficient 18F incorporation through established radiolabeling strategies like prosthetic group and nucleophilic aromatic substitution chemistry, positioning these compounds as promising lead structures for the development of 18F‐labeled M3R‐specific PET radioligands. Such agents have the potential to enable noninvasive imaging of M3R expression in triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC)and to support the development of future M3R‐targeted therapeutic approaches.

Results and Discussion

In addition to the design, chemical synthesis, and preliminary biological evaluation of fluorine‐containing peptidomimetics as novel M3R antagonists, we also provide additional experimental validation supporting M3Rs as an imaging biomarker and potential drug target in breast cancer.

Our transcriptomic analysis of clinical breast cancer specimens obtained from the Canadian Breast Cancer Foundation Tumor Bank revealed a significant upregulation of M3R expression in malignant tissues compared to nonmalignant controls (Figure 3a). Specifically, M3R mRNA levels were elevated twofold in breast cancer tissues relative to adjacent normal breast tissues. Other subtype genes, particularly for M2R, are also present in significant levels (Figure S1, Supporting Information). This data underscores the importance of developing M3R‐selective ligands. Stratification of tumors by molecular subtype further revealed a striking 10‐fold increase in M3R mRNA expression in TNBC samples compared to estrogen receptor‐positive (ER+) breast cancers. These findings suggest a potential subtype‐specific role for M3Rs in breast cancer, particularly in TNBC, a highly aggressive form of the disease characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2/neu expression, and associated with poor prognosis and limited treatment options.

The markedly elevated M3R mRNA expression in TNBC underscores its potential as a novel molecular imaging target and a therapeutic entry point for this clinically challenging subtype. Our results align with the growing recognition that non‐neuronal acetylcholine signaling pathways, mediated by muscarinic receptors, contribute to oncogenic processes, including proliferation, invasion, angiogenesis, and resistance to apoptosis.

The significant overexpression of M3R mRNA in TNBC provides a strong rationale for developing M3R‐targeted agents, including PET radioligands for noninvasive imaging and receptor‐directed therapeutics. We also performed western blotting and immunohistochemistry analyses to validate transcriptomic findings at the protein level (Figure 3b,c).

M3R protein was either low or undetectable in ER+ MCF‐7 cells and corresponding tumor tissues, consistent with mRNA expression patterns. In contrast, robust M3R expression was observed in the TNBC model MDA‐MB‐231 at cellular and xenograft levels. The differential expression of M3R between ER+ and TNBC models highlights its promise as a selective molecular target for imaging and therapeutic intervention. Given the urgent need for effective targeted strategies in TNBC, these findings support the continued development of M3R‐specific PET radioligands and therapeutic agents. Collectively, our data establish a strong biological foundation for targeting M3Rs in breast cancer and underpin the design and evaluation of selective M3R‐targeted fluorine‐containing peptidomimetics as leads for M3 PET radioligands.

Guided by the orthosteric M3 receptor pharmacophore model (Figure 2) and incorporating a glycine‐β‐alanine dipeptide linker to bridge the nonpolar and cationic pharmacophore elements, our initial objective centered on the structure–activity relationship (SAR) exploration of amine‐ and amide‐linked aryl‐fluoride compounds conjugated to piperidine motifs in both achiral and enantiomerically pure forms (Figure 4 ). The synthetic methodology proposed for the preparation of amine‐ and amide‐linked aryl‐fluoride compounds is adaptable to 18F radiochemistry. This approach leverages the use of readily available and well‐characterized prosthetic groups, specifically 4‐[18F]fluoro‐benzaldehyde and N‐succinimidyl‐4‐[18F]fluorobenzoate, which facilitate efficient radiolabeling under mild conditions.[ 14 ]

Reagents and conditions: a) HBTU, TEA, CH2Cl2, r.t., overnight; b) NaOH, water/MeOH (1:1), 100 °C, 30 min.; c) TFA/water (1:1) r.t., 3 h; d) TEA, CH2Cl2, r.t., overnight; and e) NaBH(OAc)3, AcOH, CH2Cl2, r.t., overnight.

The synthesis commenced with the coupling of 3,3,3‐triphenylpropanoic acid 1 and glycine methyl ester hydrochloride 2 using 2‐(1H‐benzotriazol‐1‐yl)‐1,1,3,3‐tetramethyluronium hexafluoro‐phosphate (HBTU) as a coupling agent and triethylamine (TEA) as the base, affording intermediate 3. Hydrolysis of compound 3 under basic conditions yielded the corresponding carboxylic acid 4.

Applying similar reaction conditions, compound 4 was subsequently coupled with β‐alanine ethyl ester hydrochloride 5 to produce intermediate 6, which was further hydrolyzed to furnish the dipeptide derivative 7.

To investigate the role of stereochemistry in muscarinic receptor binding, achiral 11 and enantiomerically defined R‐12 and S‐13 intermediates were prepared by coupling carboxylic acid 7 with achiral 8, and chiral R‐9 and S‐10 aminomethylpiperidines, respectively. Subsequent removal of the Boc protecting groups using trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) generated free amines 14– 16. Final derivatization of the free amines involved acylation reaction with 4‐fluoro‐benzoyl chloride 17 to afford amide derivatives 19– 21 or reductive amination with 4‐fluorobenzaldehyde 18 in the presence of NaBH(OAc)3 to yield amine derivatives 22– 24.

Building on the submicromolar M3 binding affinity and high M2/M3 selectivity of compound 24, we further explored the influence of alternative bulky nonpolar moieties on receptor binding and selectivity. Specifically, the triphenylmethyl group of compound 24 was replaced with either an adamantyl or a fluorinated biphenyl substituent to yield compounds 36A and 36B, respectively. These modifications were designed to probe steric and electronic effects at the hydrophobic binding site of the M3 receptor while maintaining favorable pharmacological profiles. Among the newly synthesized analogs, fluoronaphthyl‐containing compound 27 demonstrated the most promising activity, exhibiting submicromolar M3 binding affinity (IC50 0.9 µM) and moderate M2/M3 selectivity (selectivity ratio = 11.1), though both parameters were inferior to those of compound 24. A key structural feature of the M3 pharmacophore is the bulky nonpolar moiety, exemplified by the triphenylmethyl group in compounds 19– 28. Substitution of this motif with either an adamantyl or fluorobiphenyl group, as in compounds 36A and 36B, resulted in a complete loss of M3 binding and selectivity (Table 2), thereby reinforcing compound 24 as the lead candidate for further development as an 18F‐labeled M3‐selective radioligand.

To support the experimental data, molecular docking studies were performed with the best compound 24 using the crystal structure of the human M3 receptor (PDB ID: 4DAJ, 3.4 Å). Compound 24 exhibited a favorable docking score of −10.11 and demonstrated key binding interactions within the M3 receptor binding pocket. Specifically, glycine and beta‐alanine spacer form hydrogen bonds with Thr231 and Ala235, respectively (distance = 3.1 Å), while two terminal phenyl rings engaged in π–π interactions with Trp525 (distance = 3.4–3.8 Å) and π‐hydrogen interactions with Lys522 (distance = 3.1 Å). Additionally, the 4‐fluorobenzyl moiety in compound 24 was positioned in a deep hydrophobic pocket lined by Tyr529, Tyr533, and Tyr148, where it formed π–π and π‐hydrogen interactions, respectively (Figure 7 ).

Compound 24 docked in the M3 receptor.

These interactions underscore the high binding affinity and selectivity of compound 24 for M3. In contrast, docking analysis did not yield any energetically favorable binding pose for compound 24 within the M2 receptor (data not shown), further corroborating the experimentally observed M2/M3 selectivity profile.

In conclusion, in vitro binding assay and molecular docking data highlight compound 24 as a lead for the development of an 18F‐labeled M3‐selective radioligand for PET imaging.

The 4‐fluorobenzyl‐piperidine motif in compound 24 suggests reductive amination chemistry with readily available 4‐[18F]fluorobenzaldehyde (4‐[18F]FBA) for the preparation of radioligand [ 18 F]24. Reductive amination with [18F]FBA enables efficient labeling of amines under mild conditions. [ 15 ] [18F]FBA reacts with a primary or secondary amine to form an imine, which is then reduced, commonly by sodium cyanoborohydride, to yield the respective 18F‐labeled radioligands. The radiosynthesis of [ 18 F]24 using reductive amination chemistry with [18F]FBA is depicted in Figure 8 .

[18F]FBA was prepared in an automated synthesis unit using a published procedure developed in our lab.[ 14 ] Starting from piperidine 16, radioligand [ 18 F]24 was prepared in 35% decay‐corrected radiochemical yield within 55 min starting from [18F]FBA, including high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) purification, representing the first example of an 18F‐labeled subtype‐selective M3R‐targeted radioligand.

Conclusion

We described the muscarinic acetylcholine receptor M3 (M3R) as a promising molecular target in TNBC based on transcriptomic and proteomic evidence demonstrating significant M3R overexpression in TNBC tissues and cell lines compared to ER+ breast cancer and nonmalignant controls.

These findings support the potential of M3R as a subtype‐specific biomarker for molecular imaging and as a therapeutic drug target in this aggressive and treatment‐resistant breast cancer subtype. To exploit this opportunity, we have designed a novel class of fluorinated peptidomimetic M3R ligands guided by a rationally defined M3 pharmacophore, characterized by a bulky nonpolar moiety, a cationic nitrogen center, and an adjacent hydrophobic region. A glycine‐β‐alanine dipeptide linker was incorporated to enhance spatial orientation and receptor binding. SAR studies identified compound 24 as a potent and selective M3R ligand, exhibiting submicromolar binding affinity (IC50 0.3 µM) and exceptional M2/M3 selectivity (selectivity ratio 85). Molecular docking revealed key binding interactions consistent with the proposed pharmacophore, including π– π, π‐cation interaction and hydrogen bonding within the M3R binding site. Importantly, compound 24 was successfully radiolabeled using [18F]FBA via reductive amination, yielding [ 18 F]24 in 35% decay‐corrected yield within 55 min as a first example of an 18F‐labelled M3R‐selective radioligand. Future investigations will evaluate radioligand [ 18 F]24 for PET imaging of M3R expression in breast cancer to further validate its potential as a noninvasive imaging biomarker in TNBC.

Experimental Section

All chemical compounds were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich, Combi‐Blocks, Alfa Aesar, and ChemSpace, and used without further purification. Reactions were monitored by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) using RediSep TLC plates and visualized under UV light. Purification of intermediate and crude products was performed by flash column chromatography using a CombiFlash EZ Prep system (Teledyne ISCO) with prepacked RediSep Rf Gold silica cartridges, employing a gradient of hexane and ethyl acetate as the mobile phase. Final compound purification was carried out by reverse‐phase HPLC using a Phenomenex Luna 10 µm C18(2) 100 Å, 250 × 10 mm semipreparative column, controlled via Trilution LC 2.1 software. Elution was performed at a flow rate of 2 mLmin− 1, with a gradient from 30% to 100% acetonitrile (CH3CN) over 30 min at (TEMPERATURE °C), with UV detection at 210 and 254 nm. Analytical HPLC was used to assess compound purity using a Phenomenex Luna 10 µm C18(2) 100 Å, 250 × 4.6 mm analytical column under the following conditions: a mobile phase of 0.2% TFA in H2O/CH3CN, a gradient from 5% to 100% CH3CN over 60 min, 2 mLmin−1 flow rate at 30 °C, and UV detection at 210, 254, and 280 nm. All final compounds were determined to have a purity greater than 95%. Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) with an Agilent TOF 2 spectrometer and MassHunter Walkup System software. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Phenomenex Luna Omega 1.6 µm Polar C18 100 Å, 50 × 2.1 mm column with a mobile phase consisting of 0.2% TFA in H2O/CH3CN, using a gradient from 5% to 100% CH3CN over 10 min at a flow rate of 2 mLmin−1, monitored at 214, 254, and 280 nm. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was conducted to confirm compound structures. 1D (1H and 13C) and 2D (Cosy and heteronuclear single quantum coherence) spectra were recorded using a Bruker FT‐600 MHz spectrometer (1H: 600 MHz; 13C: 150 MHz) in CDCl3, MeOD, or DMSO‐d 6, with tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal standard. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm) and coupling constants (J) in hertz (Hz). Peak multiplicities are indicated as follows: s (singlet), d (doublet), t (triplet), q (quartet), and m (multiplet).

High‐resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR‐ESI‐MS) was carried out using an Agilent Technologies 6220 Accurate‐Mass TOF instrument. Samples were dissolved in an appropriate solvent, typically methanol, and analyzed via flow injection. Data analysis was performed using Agilent MassHunter software.

General Procedure A: Carboxylic Acid‐Amine Coupling

The corresponding carboxylic acids and amine derivatives were dissolved in 15 mL of dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) and stirred for 15 min at room temperature. HBTU and TEA were then added to the reaction mixture, which was stirred at room temperature for 12 h and monitored by TLC. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was subjected to liquid–liquid extraction with water and CH2Cl2. The organic layer was separated, dried, and evaporated under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography using a hexane/ethyl acetate gradient, affording the desired compounds as white semisolid materials.

General Procedure B: Ester Hydrolysis

The respective esters were dissolved in a 1:1 mixture of methanol and water (v/v) at room temperature. Sodium hydroxide (1.2 equivalents) was added, and the reaction mixture was heated to reflux. The progress of the reaction was monitored by TLC, and complete conversion was observed after 30 min. The mixture was then cooled to room temperature, and methanol was removed under reduced pressure. The resulting aqueous solution was placed in an ice bath and acidified with 20% aqueous HCl until the pH reached ≈5, resulting in the precipitation of a white solid. The product was collected by vacuum filtration and did not require further purification.

Radiochemistry

No‐carrier‐added [18F]fluoride was produced from [18O]H2O (18O(p, n)18F) on an ACSI TR‐19/9 cyclotron and provided as an aqueous solution. [18F]FBA was prepared in an automated synthesis unit using a published procedure developed in our lab.14 Labeling precursor 16 (6.4 mg, 2.0 mmol) and NaBH3CN (6.4 mg, 2.0 mmol) were added to the reaction vessel. Then, 300 µL of [18F]FBA in DMF (400–500 MBq) was added and the reaction vessel was placed into an oil bath at 120 °C for 30 min. The reaction completion was confirmed by radio‐TLC. After 5 min of cooling to room temperature, the reaction mixture was diluted with a 300 µl of 0.2% TFA/CH3CN (50:50)% (v/v) solution and 350–400 MBq were injected on HPLC [10 × 250 mm C18 (reverse phase) column, in a gradient way (phase A = 0.2%TFA/water; phase B = CH3CN), 2 mL/min flow rate, UV (270 nm) and γ‐ray (PIN diode) flow detectors]. The product peak eluting at 22.6 min was collected, and the solvent was evaporated under reduced pressure. Identity of collected radioligand [ 18 F]24 was confirmed by coinjection of reference compound 24. The product was resolubilized in PBS (200–300 mL), yielding 140–160 MBq of radioligand [ 18 F]24 (35% decay‐corrected radiochemical yield. The radiochemical purity exceeded 95% and was confirmed by radio‐HPLC analysis.

Competitive Binding Assays

Competitive binding assays for human mAChRs M1–M5 were performed using CHO‐K1 cell membranes expressing each receptor subtype. Crude membranes were prepared by homogenization and centrifugation (40,000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C) and stored at −80 °C until use. Binding assays were conducted in 96‐well plates containing membranes (10–20 µg protein/well), [3H]N‐methylscopolamine ([3H]NMS, 0.5–1.0 nM), and test compounds diluted in assay buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) in a final volume of 200 µL.

Nonspecific binding was defined in the presence of 10 µM atropine, which fully displaces [3H]NMS from the orthosteric binding site. Under these conditions, nonspecific binding was consistently low (typically less than 10–15% of total binding across assays) and did not significantly interfere with the determination of affinity values. This observation is critical given the relatively lipophilic nature of muscarinic ligands, which can often result in artificially elevated nonspecific binding, referring to the association of the ligand with membranes or surfaces, leading to an apparent increase in background signal that inflates measured nonspecific binding. The low nonspecific binding levels observed confirm that the assay conditions were well‐suited to reliably assess receptor binding affinity and selectivity for the tested compounds.

Plates were incubated for 90 min at room temperature and filtered through presoaked GF/B filters using a Brandel harvester. Bound radioactivity was quantified by liquid scintillation counting. IC50 values were determined by nonlinear regression analysis (GraphPad Prism, version 9). All experiments were performed in duplicates, and for replicate determinations, the maximum variability tolerated in the test was ±20% around the average of the replicates.

Computational Analyses

All ligands for molecular modeling were built using ChemBioDraw 14.0 program. M3 crystal structure (code: 4U15) was downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) webpage. Using MOE 2022.02 software, water molecules and nonpolar hydrogen atoms were removed from the ligand and protein, energy minimization, partial atomic charges, and all polar hydrogen atoms were added. Default crystallographic coordinates centered at the cocristal ligand as search space to dock the ligands were used on the MOE 2022.02 program. Molecular docking results with RMSD <2.0 Å were processed with Pymol version 4.6 and MOE 2022.02 programs. Docking Validation: The molecular docking was validated by redocking the cocrystal tiotropium in the M3 receptor. An RMS less than 2.0 Å was obtained. This root‐mean‐square deviation value means the settled parameters for docking simulations are good enough to be reproduced with the designed ligands.
