Evaluations of Penumbra Reduction Using Multi-Leaf Collimator and 3D Bolus in Scanned Proton Therapy
Authors: Yuki Tominaga, Robabeh Rahimi, Yushi Wakisaka, Mayu Nakata, Yuya Miyasaka, Akira Takagi, Akihiro Tanaka, Masaya Ichihara, Motoharu Sasaki, Takahiro Kato, Akihiro Haga, Teiji Nishio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeProton therapy offers excellent distal tissue sparing; however, precise control of the lateral penumbra is critical for protecting adjacent healthy tissues. This study systematically evaluated improvements in lateral penumbra in proton pencil beam scanning (PBS) using a surface-contacted 3-dimensional printed bolus (3DBS), compared with conventional plans employing a nozzle-mounted range shifter (RS), for beams with and without a multi-leaf collimator (MLC).MethodsA total of 460 uniform-dose PBS plans were generated for a cubic target (60 × 60 × 54 mm³) positioned at 9 different depths ranging from 0 to 280 mm in water. Plan configurations included plans with 3 different materials for 3DBSs (silicone, resin, and thermoplastic polyurethane) and were compared with plans with a polyethylene RS. Each configuration was further subdivided into plans with and without MLC. Air gaps ranging from 50 to 300 mm were evaluated. Lateral penumbra widths and mean doses to surrounding organs at risk (OARs) within a 30-mm margin were evaluated. Two-dimensional dose distributions for selected beams were experimentally verified using a 2D ionization chamber array and radiochromic films.ResultsAll plans with 3DBSs improved lateral penumbra and reduced mean OAR doses compared with plans with RS, under equivalent MLC conditions, except for a limited subset of plans at depths greater than 200 mm with minimal air gaps. Measured dose distributions demonstrated acceptable agreement with calculated plans. Comparisons among the 3 3DBS materials indicated feasibility in material selection based on required bolus geometry and clinical implementation considerations.ConclusionsThe combined use of MLC and 3DBS provides a systematic and effective strategy for lateral penumbra control in proton PBS. This approach enables improved dosimetric precision in clinically sensitive scenarios where even small reductions in dose to surrounding tissues are critical.

Introduction

Proton pencil beam scanning (PBS) delivers intensity-modulated radiation by controlling thousands of beam spots, monitor units, and energy layers.1, 2 Compared with photon therapy and passive scattering proton therapy, PBS provides superior target conformity and improved sparing of adjacent organs at risk (OARs).1, 3 However, PBS beams may exhibit a broader lateral penumbra than photon beams, particularly at shallow treatment depths where range shifters (RSs) are required.4, 5, 6, 7 Because many clinically important shallow treatment sites, including head and neck tumors, are located adjacent to critical OARs, reducing lateral dose spread remains an important objective in PBS systems.8, 9, 10 Several approaches have been proposed to improve lateral penumbra in PBS, including patient-specific apertures, dynamic collimation systems, and contour scanning techniques.11, 12, 13, 14

In recent years, 3-dimensional boluses (3DBSs), fabricated using 3D printing or related manufacturing techniques, have been introduced as an alternative to conventional nozzle-mounted RSs for treating shallow targets.15, 16 Unlike nozzle-mounted RSs, surface-contacted 3DBSs are placed directly on the patient surface, thereby reducing the air gap between the beam-modifying device and the target. By shifting modulating materials closer to the patient, 3DBSs are expected to reduce beam scattering, improve lateral dose conformity, and further decrease dose to surrounding OARs compared with deliveries using nozzle-mounted RSs.17, 18, 19, 20

Our proton therapy system is equipped with PBS and a multi-leaf collimator (MLC) mounted at the distal end of the treatment nozzle.21 Previous studies have shown that this MLC configuration improves lateral penumbra, including for targets located deeper than 150 mm. However, the treatment system uses a 60-mm RS positioned immediately downstream of the MLC, which may therefore reduce the full benefit of MLC collimation, particularly for large air gaps. Replacing RS with 3DBSs can potentially mitigate this hardware issue. Because both MLC collimation and surface-contacted 3DBSs independently improve lateral dose conformity, their combined use may provide additional complementary dosimetric benefits. To our knowledge, no previous study has systematically evaluated the MLC collimated use of surface-contacted 3DBSs in PBS. Furthermore, because MLC equipped PBS systems are not universal, understanding the dosimetric differences between RS and 3DBS under non-collimated conditions remains clinically relevant. A comprehensive and systematic evaluation of such treatment approaches would therefore provide information applicable to a broader range of common PBS proton systems.

Accordingly, the aim of this study was to quantitatively evaluate lateral penumbra and dose distributions in PBS systems for plans using nozzle-mounted RSs and surface-contacted 3DBSs, with and without MLC collimation, using both treatment planning calculations and experimental dose verification. Treatment planning calculations were performed across a wide range of target depths and air gap conditions, and selected plans were experimentally verified using 2-dimensional dosimetry and films. Specifically, we assessed whether substituting a nozzle-mounted RS with a surface-contacted 3DBS can reduce downstream beam scattering and thereby preserve the dosimetric benefits of MLC collimation.

Methods

Proton therapy system with multi-leaf collimator

At the Osaka Proton Therapy Clinic, treatments were delivered using the MELTHEA V proton therapy system (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The system is equipped with a PBS delivery nozzle and an MLC. It supports 92 discrete energy layers ranging from 70.7 to 235.0 MeV, corresponding to water-equivalent treatment depths of approximately 40 to 340 mm.21 For treatment depths shallower than 40 mm, a polyethylene RS with a minimum water-equivalent thickness (WET) of 60 mm was required. The treatment system was equipped with an MLC positioned downstream of the nozzle. The MLC consisted of 54 pairs of iron leaves, each 140 mm thick, with a nominal leaf width of 3.75 mm at the isocenter. The distance from the isocenter to the MLC varied from 250 to 560 mm.21 In air, the measured spot sizes (1σ) ranged from 3.0 to 9.5 mm.22

Because the stopping power ratios assigned to custom materials in the RayStation 2024B (RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden) treatment planning system (TPS) require experimental validation, the WETs of the investigated bolus materials were measured using the clinical proton therapy system at Osaka Proton Therapy Clinic following previously reported methodologies.26, 27 Each of the 3 bolus materials was placed inside a PLA enclosure box. A monoenergetic scanned proton beam, with an energy of 141.2 MeV, was delivered to a water phantom under 2 conditions; with and without placement of PLA box and bolus enclosed in PLA upstream of the beam. Depth–dose distributions were acquired for all configurations. The WET was determined from the displacement of the distal 80% dose fall-off position relative to the reference measurement, without material in the beam path, using an in-house Python-based analysis program.

Based on the experimentally determined WET values and using RayStation’s material assignment function, the bolus materials—silicone, resin, and TPU, also the PLA enclosure box—were modeled in the TPS as custom materials, with water specified as the base material. In plans, material contours were defined according to the physical thicknesses shown in Figure 1 and were overridden accordingly, relative to water. This approach was consistent with the density replacement method used to model beam path length changes introduced by treatment components such as tabletops or immobilization devices. Evaluation plans

Uniform dose proton PBS plans were generated for box-shaped targets. Nine target configurations were positioned at depths ranging from 0 to 280 mm in water (Figure 2a). Each irradiation field measured 60 × 60 mm², and the target thickness along the beam direction, corresponding to the spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP), was fixed at 54 mm for all depths. Only the 2 shallowest targets, 25 and 53 mm, required an absorber; therefore, plans with no absorbers were not generated. For the remaining 7 target depths, plans were generated both with and without absorbers (RS and 3DBS) (Table 1). This approach enabled direct comparisons between plans with conventional RS and plans with surface-contacted 3DBS across the full range of target depths investigated. All plans were optimized under identical conditions, using air gaps ranging from 50 to 300 mm, in 50 mm increments. Due to mechanical constraints of the treatment nozzle, with an isocenter-to-nozzle distance of 250 to 560 mm, certain air gap configurations were not feasible (Table 1). A total of 230 plans were generated without MLC (non-collimated plans), including 38 plans without any absorbers (RS or 3DBS), 48 plans with RS, 48 plans with silicone bolus, 48 plans with resin bolus, and 48 plans with TPU bolus. For all these scenarios, an additional set of 230 plans was generated with MLC implementations to those plans, therefore resulting in 460 evaluation plans in total. In a previous study, a 7-mm collimator margin—routinely used in clinical practice—was applied for all plans.22 In the present study, it was anticipated that a smaller collimator margin could better improve target dose uniformity. Accordingly, the MLC margin from the cross-line target edge was set to 0.5σ of the in-water spot size, resulting in margin sizes of 3.2 to 9.4 mm (Table 1).30 For plans with 3DBS, the MLC margin remained constant across air gaps because the bolus was positioned directly on the phantom surface, resulting in a fixed distance between the MLC and the beam-modifying device. Similarly, for plans without RS, the MLC margin was identical across all air gaps conditions. In contrast, plans with RS showed air gap-dependent MLC margins.

All plans prescribed a total dose of 10 Gy delivered in 10 fractions to 98% of the target volume. Dose calculations were performed using the commercial Monte Carlo algorithm implemented in the TPS, with both optimization and final dose calculations conducted using 10 000 ions per spot and a statistical uncertainty of 0.5%.31

Treatment planning system validations for plans with 3-dimensional printed bolus

Two-dimensional dose distributions in the isocenter plane were validated using a cross-calibrated OCTAVIUS Detector 729 (2D ionization chamber array; PTW, Freiburg, Germany) in combination with a solid water phantom (Tough Water; Kyoto Kagaku, Kyoto, Japan). The 2D array detector consisted of 729 ionization chambers arranged at 10 mm intervals in a single measurement plane. The measurement setup and the procedure followed that described in a previous study.21

For each target depth, plans with air gaps corresponding to the smallest feasible values (50-107.1 mm), intermediate value (150 mm), and the largest feasible value (193.1-300 mm) were selected for validation, as indicated in Table 1. Measurements were performed for 5 target depths and 3 representative air gap conditions (minimum, intermediate, and maximum feasible values) for each of the 3 3DBS materials (silicone, resin, and TPU), resulting in 45 measured plans. Measured and calculated dose distributions were compared using gamma index analysis implemented using an in-house software based on the PymedPhys library.32 The gamma criterion was set to 2%/2 mm with a dose threshold of 90% of the maximum dose.

Because the 10-mm detector spacing of the 2D ionization chamber array was insufficient for accurate penumbra characterization in the 3DBS plans, additional measurements were performed using Gafchromic films (EBT4; Ashland Inc, Wayne, NJ, USA) to evaluate lateral penumbra widths with higher spatial resolution. For these measurements, the dose per beam (1 Gy) was multiplied by a factor of 5 to ensure adequate signal in the penumbra region. Only plans with the minimum air gap for each target depth were evaluated, resulting in 15 film measurements in total (underlined in Table 1). Films were scanned using a flatbed scanner (EPSON DS-G20000, Seiko Epson Corp, Japan) at a resolution of 150 dots per inch. Lateral penumbra widths in cross-line and in-line directions were extracted at the isocenter plane and compared between measured and calculated dose distributions using an in-house analysis software.

Evaluations of dose-volume histograms for plans with 3-dimensional printed bolus

Dose–volume histogram metrics were analyzed for all plans to compare dose statistics between collimated and uncollimated beam configurations for different absorbers. For this purpose, dummy OARs were delineated in each plan. These OARs were positioned with a 3 mm clearance from the target to avoid overlap and were extended 30 mm outward from the target margins in all directions (Figure 2h). At each isocenter depth, the mean OAR dose (Dmean) was used as the primary metric for comparing treatment configurations; collimated and uncollimated plans. Target coverage was standardized across all plans by ensuring consistent dose delivery to D98 of the target volume. In this context, Dx denotes the dose received by x% of the structure volume.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses of OAR dose metrics and changes in penumbra widths across different air gap conditions were performed using a 2-sided Mann-Whitney U test implemented in Python with the SciPy library. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

2D array and film measurements

The mean ± standard deviation (minimum/maximum) gamma scores at the 2%/2 mm criterion for plans with 3DBS and MLC (3 air gap configurations), evaluated using the 2D array detector, were 94.7% ± 3.8% (87.1%-100.0%) for silicone, 95.4% ± 4.0% (85.7%-100.0%) for resin, and 95.7% ± 2.9% (91.3%-100.0%) for TPU. Across the 45 measured plans, only 4 yielded gamma scores below 90%, all of which corresponded to configurations with the largest feasible air gaps and the 150 mm air gap (Figure S1). For all plans, the differences between calculated and measured penumbra widths were within ±1.5 mm, except for 1 profile in the cross-line direction for 1 silicone plan (Figure S2).

For plans without MLC (Figure 3a,d), silicone 3DBS plans consistently reduced penumbra widths compared with RS plans at all depths. The difference was most pronounced for the shallowest targets. Similar trends were observed for plans with MLC (Figure 3b,d).

Notably, uncollimated silicone 3DBS plans showed penumbra widths comparable to RS plans with MLC at most depths, except for the deepest target (Figure 3c,d). These results suggested that 3DBS plans, even without MLC implementation, could achieve penumbra reductions comparable to those achieved with RS combined with MLC.

For plans without RS (Figure 3e–h), silicone 3DBS plans without MLC showed improved penumbra widths at depths shallower than 109 mm. Plans without RS but with MLC achieved the smallest penumbra widths across all depths and consistently outperformed the silicone 3DBS plans with MLC to RS plans (Figure 3f,h). However, silicone 3DBS plans with MLC consistently produced smaller penumbra widths than plans without RS and without MLC across all depths (Figure 3g,h). These findings indicated that, for plans with no RS, collimated 3DBS plans improved lateral penumbra compared with uncollimated plans. Similar trends were observed for resin and TPU 3DBSs (Figure S3 and S4).

OAR dose trends generally followed those observed for lateral penumbra widths. However, some discrepancies between penumbra reduction and OAR dose reduction were observed. This is because the optimized MLC margin reduction was applied only in the lateral direction. In the superior-inferior direction, the margin could not be reduced to 0.5σ because of the finite width of the MLC leaves. Discussions

This study systematically investigated lateral penumbra characteristics across a wide range of air gaps and target depths by combining 3 types of 3DBSs with MLC in a PBS system. Previous work demonstrated that MLC implementation alone could improve lateral penumbra even at depths exceeding 150 mm.22 The present results extended the findings by showing that substituting RS with a 3DBS yielded additional penumbra improvements across all depth regions.

Two-dimensional array verifications were performed, in prior studies, for multiple air gap conditions, with and without RS for collimated and uncollimated beams.22 Accordingly, the present experimental validation focused on plans combining 3DBS and MLC. The gamma scores observed in this study were comparable to those previously reported for the plans with MLC (97.4% ± 3.1% at 2%/2 mm).22 For film measurements, differences between calculated and measured penumbra widths were within ±1.5 mm for all profiles except one, consistent with tolerance levels reported in earlier studies and were considered clinically acceptable.11, 13, 22

All 3 investigated 3DBS materials (silicone, resin, and TPU) appeared suitable for clinical application, provided that feasibility is confirmed during treatment planning. However, a small number of plans combining shallow target depths (<100 mm) with large air gaps exhibited gamma scores below 90% at the 2%/2 mm criterion. Similar degradation in gamma performance for shallow targets has been reported previously and was attributed to insufficient modeling of secondary particles generated by the MLC configuration in the TPS dose calculations.21 The improvement in gamma agreement observed under minimal air gap conditions in the present study supports minimizing the nozzle-to-surface distance to enhance agreement between calculated and measured dose distributions.

Across nearly all evaluated scenarios, 3DBS plans demonstrated improved lateral penumbra and reduced doses to surrounding OARs, compared with the plans with RS, regardless of MLC implementations, except under certain deep-target conditions (Figure 4a–b). This improvement arises because beams passing through a surface-contacted absorber enter the water directly without traversing air, thereby reducing beam scattering.18 Penumbra reduction was particularly pronounced at depths shallower than 100 mm, highlighting the potential benefit of 3DBS in clinical cases such as head-and-neck treatments, where many critical OARs are located in shallow regions.33

As expected, the plans with only MLC in the beams achieved the smallest penumbra width overall, and collimated plans with 3DBSs could not exceed this performance, even at deeper depths (Figure 3f). Because targets shallower than approximately 40 mm cannot be irradiated without an absorber, RS combined with MLC remained the most effective configuration for shallow regions. Notably, however, collimated plans with 3DBS consistently outperformed uncollimated plans with RS (Figure 3g). As discussed in the treatment planning section, this enabled improved penumbra control even at depths where absorbers were not strictly required, while also offering potential benefits for mitigating interplay effects and reducing treatment time.28, 29

The observed depth-dependent behavior can be explained by the interplay between beamline scattering and in-water multiple Coulomb scattering. While lateral spot size in air decreases with increasing proton energy due to reduced beamline scattering, higher-energy beams undergo greater lateral broadening in water because of longer traversal distances before reaching the Bragg peak.34, 35 Consequently, the in-water lateral spot size exhibits a minimum at an intermediate energy or depth, as reported by Titt et al.34 The present results are consistent with this mechanism, with the minimum spot size observed at intermediate depths (Figure 3e). The use of a downstream absorber such as a 3DBS shifts this minimum toward shallower depths, explaining the enhanced penumbra performance of plans with 3DBS in shallow regions.34 This advantage was further amplified when 3DBS was combined with MLC (Figure 3g), whereas conventional RS did not outperform beams without MLC in these regions.

Several practical advantages of using 3DBS were identified. Unlike conventional RS devices, which are limited to a minimum thickness, for example, of 60 mm, 3DBS can be fabricated with customized thicknesses tailored to clinical requirements. In addition, favorable penumbra characteristics can be achieved without positioning the treatment nozzle close to the patient, eliminating the risk of nozzle–patient collision. As demonstrated in Figure 3c, uncollimated 3DBS plans achieved penumbra widths comparable to those of collimated plans with RS. This finding is particularly of clinical relevance for PBS systems that have no MLC in their hardware configurations.

Importantly, the plans combining RS with MLC carry an increased risk of neutron dose exposure to patients, including healthy organs and tissues.34 In contrast, secondary radiation generated by a 3DBS are better locally around the target, limiting a vast exposed volume.36

Despite these advantages, several considerations warrant caution. Because 3DBSs are placed directly on the patient’s body surface, heavy boluses may impose a physical burden on the patient.37 Moreover, since the 3DBS is effectively treated as part of the patient anatomy in the TPS, ensuring positional reproducibility can be challenging due to daily setup variations. Patient-specific fabrication also introduces additional cost compared with reusable RS devices. The workflow adjustment with regards to CT simulation and 3DBS fabrication, should be clinically evaluated and determined to be feasible. Therefore, the clinical adoption of 3DBS should be justified by clear dosimetric benefits that outweigh these logistical and financial considerations. Nevertheless, advances in manufacturing technology using 3D printers have made it possible to rapidly produce high-precision boluses at relatively low cost.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Ideally, 3DBS should be fabricated to conform precisely to the patient’s body surface geometry and validated in their final curved form. In the present work, validation was limited to rectangular prism-shaped boluses designed to match the RS geometry to ensure consistency with previously reported studies.22 Validation studies incorporating patient-specific surface geometries are to be presented elsewhere. In addition, neutron production differs between RS and 3DBS configurations, and further evaluation of secondary radiation will be required, particularly when any absorbers are used in combination with MLC.38

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the dosimetric effects of surface-contacted 3DBSs and MLC collimation to improve lateral penumbra in proton PBS systems using treatment planning calculations and experimental dose verification. Compared with conventional nozzle-mounted RS, 3DBSs consistently improved lateral penumbra widths and reduced doses to surrounding normal tissues across a wide range of target depths and air gap conditions. MLC collimation further improved dose conformity for plans with 3DBS, with the greatest overall benefit observed for the combination of MLC and 3DBS.

Among the investigated materials, silicone-based 3DBSs generally achieved the smallest lateral penumbra widths. Experimental measurements confirmed the dosimetric trends predicted by the TPS. The findings suggest that replacing conventional RSs with surface-contacted 3DBSs can improve dose conformity in proton PBS and that additional gains may be achieved through the combined use of 3DBSs and MLC collimation. The benefits of 3DBSs were observed both with and without MLC implementation; therefore, this approach may be applicable to a broad range of proton therapy facilities, regardless of hardware limitations.
