Profiles of Digital Parenting Styles: Associations With Cyberbullying Among Adolescents
Authors: Yeseul Lee, Jennifer L Doty, Xiaoqi Ma, Tracy Evian Waasdorp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCyberbullying is a growing concern in the digital age, posing serious threats to children's mental health, social relationships, and overall well‐being. Parents raising children in a highly digitalized world employ a range of digital parenting strategies, including active monitoring, content regulation, and technology control, to guide children's online experiences. However, little research has examined the presence of heterogeneous profiles of these parenting strategies and examined how they influence children's involvement in cyberbullying behaviors. Guided by neo‐ecological theory, an extension of Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory into the digital context, this study investigated profiles of digital parenting styles and their associations with cyberbullying.MethodsData were drawn from 306 parent‐child dyads in the U.S. (parents: M age = 38.52, 60.8% mothers; children: M age = 11.80, 50.7% boys). Parents completed surveys regarding digital parenting practices, while children reported their cyberbullying experiences. Latent profile analysis was conducted to identify distinct parenting profiles, and group differences in children's cyberbullying perpetration and victimization were examined.ResultsThe analysis revealed four distinct digital parenting profiles: low engagement (10%), discordant (24%), trust‐based (32%), and high engagement (34%). Children of parents with discordant and low‐engagement profiles reported higher levels of cyberbullying perpetration compared to those in high‐engagement and trust‐based profiles. Similarly, children of discordant parents showed the highest levels of cyberbullying victimization, followed by those with low‐engagement, trust‐based, and high‐engagement parents.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the importance of consistent, engaged, and trust‐oriented digital parenting in reducing children's risks of becoming involved in cyberbullying, as perpetrators or victims.Keywords: adolescence, cyberbullying, digital parenting, latent profile analysis

From an early age, reliance on digital technologies for social interactions increases adolescents' risk of being exposed to online dangers, such as cyberbullying (Zhu et al. 2021). Unlike traditional in‐person bullying, cyberbullying can reach farther, be witnessed by a wider audience, occur anonymously, and happen at any time (Hutson et al. 2018). Cyberbullying has been linked to a range of negative developmental outcomes, including depression, anxiety, low self‐esteem, absenteeism, physical health problems, and suicidal ideation (Bansal et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024), underscoring its emergence as a significant public health concern.

Marked by developmental milestones like puberty, cognitive growth, identity exploration, and increasing autonomy (Hazen et al. 2008), adolescence is a particularly vulnerable period for behavioral and relational challenges as adolescents shift their focus toward peer relationships. Parental influence remains a critical factor in shaping their behaviors and mitigating online risks (Elsaesser et al. 2017; Steinberg and Silk 2002), and cyberbullying frequently takes place at home, where parents have more direct control over adolescents' access to electronic devices (Common Sense Media 2021; Vernon et al. 2018). Despite their importance, many parents report significant challenges regarding digital parenting, including difficulties monitoring and managing their adolescents' online activities (Elsaesser et al. 2017; Helfrich et al. 2020). A more nuanced understanding of digital parenting is needed to examine and address the challenges parents may face in these domains. This study adopts a Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) approach to identify distinct digital parenting styles rather than examining digital parenting as a singular construct or dichotomy. Understanding the relationship of the profiles with cyberbullying will offer insights into tailored interventions and strategies for mitigating risk based on specific parenting styles.

Cyberbullying in Adolescence

Cyberbullying perpetration involves using information and communication technology to deliver harmful, abusive, or harassing messages (Polanin et al. 2022). Victims of cyberbullying may face deliberate exclusion from online discussions or social interactions shared on digital platforms, have their personal information or passwords stolen, or encounter harassment and threats in multiplayer gaming environments (Polanin et al. 2022). Cyberbullying can happen anytime and in multiple settings, making it more pervasive (American Psychological Association 2022). About 20% of adolescents aged 9–12 have been involved in cyberbullying (American Psychological Association 2022), emphasizing the developmental relevance of cyberbullying during adolescence.

The risk of cyberbullying involvement increases as adolescents gain more access to digital media (Zhu et al. 2021), peaking in middle adolescence (Tian et al. 2023). Adolescents who experience or engage in cyberbullying face an increased risk of both mental and physical health issues, including depression, suicidal thoughts, and behavioral challenges (Bansal et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024). Furthermore, being a victim of cyberbullying can negatively impact self‐esteem and confidence (Dennehy et al. 2020) and has been linked to higher rates of substance use and social anxiety among adolescents (Yoon et al. 2019).

As cyberbullying continues to be a significant concern, many studies have explored parenting as a preventive factor (Doty et al. 2021; Elsaesser et al. 2017; Zurcher et al. 2018). However, most research focuses on the general protective role of parenting without examining specific digital parenting styles or their distinct effects on cyberbullying. Few studies take a person‐centered approach and have separately analyzed how different digital parenting factors relate to both cyberbullying perpetration and victimization.

Digital and Traditional Parenting Practices

Parenting strategies can significantly shape adolescents' online experiences. Digital parenting encompasses active digital parenting (e.g., intentional discussions) and restrictive digital parenting (e.g., limiting and monitoring online activities; Livingstone and Helsper 2008; Wisniewski et al. 2025). These strategies, commonly used to manage children's digital engagement (Chen et al. 2023; Doty et al. 2021), shape online behavior and influence risks such as cyberbullying (Ma et al. 2026). While active digital parenting has been consistently linked to lower cyberbullying victimization by fostering open communication and responsible online behavior (Chen et al. 2023), findings on restrictive digital parenting are mixed. Some studies suggest that restrictive digital parenting can be protective, particularly when adolescents engage in risky online behaviors (Ma et al. 2026; Nikken and Schols 2015), whereas others indicate that excessive control may lead to secrecy or resistance (Zurcher et al. 2018).

In addition to digital parenting practices, indicators of strong parent‐child relationships, such as trust, disclosure, and safety, have long been recognized as central components of traditional parenting. These relational processes remain equally important in the digital context. For instance, parent–child trust facilitates open communication, thereby increasing adolescents' willingness to disclose their online experiences (Livingstone and Blum‐Ross 2020), while greater disclosure promotes transparency and reduces engagement in risky behaviors (Zurcher et al. 2018). Furthermore, family digital safety knowledge equips both parents and adolescents with the skills necessary for responsible online navigation (Wisniewski et al. 2017). Research on parenting styles indicates that children involved in cyberbullying often experience strict discipline paired with a weak emotional bond with their parents (Legate et al. 2019; Wang and Jiang 2022; Zurcher et al. 2018). Conversely, adolescents with autonomy‐supportive parents engage in less cyberbullying than those with controlling parents, emphasizing the potential of family dynamics in shaping online behavior (Legate et al. 2019). Some studies suggest that Baumrind's model (1966), which emphasizes the importance of both warmth and firm boundaries in parenting, can also be applied to digital outcomes such as cyberbullying (e.g., Chen et al. 2023).

Recent person‐centered research using LPA has provided a more refined understanding of digital parenting. For example, Antoniadou et al. (2019) and Guo et al. (2021) found that adolescents involved in cyberbullying—particularly those in bully‐victim profiles—reported lower parental support and higher family conflict, underscoring the importance of family relationship quality in shaping online behavior. These findings suggest that parenting operates through flexible and relational processes rather than a single approach. However, prior person‐centered studies have not examined specific digital parenting strategies, such as rules, monitoring, or technology‐based control. This distinction is important, as digital parenting practices may function differently from traditional parenting and uniquely influence adolescents' online experiences. Addressing this gap, the present study adopts a holistic approach grounded in neo‐ecological theory to examine how distinct combinations of digital parenting strategies are associated with adolescents' involvement in cyberbullying.

Neo‐Ecological Theory

The neo‐ecological theory extends Bronfenbrenner's bioecological framework, emphasizing the influence of technology on human development by recognizing both physical and virtual microsystems as central contexts for adolescent growth (Navarro and Tudge 2023). Adolescents engage in digital spaces where their behaviors and identity formation are shaped by interactions in virtual environments (Elmore et al. 2017). These virtual proximal processes foster learning and connection among youth but also increase exposure to cyberbullying and negative social comparison (Moreno–Ruiz et al. 2019). Parent‐child interactions in the built world provide protection, as parents guide adolescents across both digital and offline environments to help mitigate digital risks (Livingstone and Helsper 2008; Wright 2016).

The neo‐ecological theory emphasizes proximal processes that impact adolescents' daily lives across virtual and in‐person microsystems and recognizes parenting influence as a protective individual resource (Navarro and Tudge 2023). Empirical studies indicate that digital parenting strategies—such as parental monitoring, open communication, and setting technology use guidelines—reduce the risk of cyberbullying perpetration and victimization (Livingstone and Helsper 2008; Ma et al. 2026; Wright 2016). At the same time, traditional parenting characteristics, including parent trust and general emotional support, play a crucial role in shaping adolescents' well‐being and online behaviors. Neo‐ecological theory highlights the need to consider both digital and offline microsystems to fully understand how parental involvement is associated with adolescents' online behaviors (Navarro and Tudge 2023). Thus, this study integrates digital parenting dimensions into a LPA framework to examine how digital and traditional parenting strategies work together to mitigate cyberbullying risks.

The Current Study

Building on prior research, the present study aimed to identify heterogeneous profiles of digital parenting and examine how these profiles are associated with adolescents' involvement in cyberbullying. Moving beyond variable‐centered approaches, we adopted a person‐centered perspective to capture how multiple dimensions of digital parenting co‐occur within families (Nikken and Schols 2015; Symons et al. 2017).

We also examined whether profile membership varied by child and parent characteristics. We expected that younger adolescents would be more likely to belong to restrictive profiles, as restrictive practices tend to decline across adolescence (Chen and Shi 2019; Corcoran et al. 2022). We further anticipated that girls would be more likely than boys to belong to profiles characterized by higher levels of parental monitoring and restriction (Corcoran et al. 2022), and that lower socioeconomic status (SES) would be associated with less involved digital parenting (Ma et al. 2026). Associations with parent gender and age were explored.

Finally, based on prior evidence highlighting the role of parental mediation in adolescents' cyberbullying involvement (Elsaesser et al. 2017), we hypothesized that cyberbullying perpetration and victimization would differ across digital parenting profiles. By identifying these patterns, this study aims to inform more targeted strategies for promoting safer online environments.

Methods

Data and Sample

The present study utilized data from a 2019 online survey that was completed by children aged 10–14 years and their parents. The original survey was conducted as part of a larger study exploring cyberbullying prevention, media parenting, and other online risks faced by youth. This study was conducted using Qualtrics, a secure online survey platform commonly utilized in academic and corporate research. A nationwide sample of parents and their children aged 10–14 was recruited in the United States through Qualtrics panels, which connect researchers with potential participants who have opted into surveys. Both the children and their parents provided consent before participation. Parents completed the survey first and were instructed to allow their children privacy while completing the survey. Upon survey completion, participants received compensation of approximately $2.50 from Qualtrics, and quality control items (e.g., attention control question) were examined to ensure data quality. Qualtrics automated quality checks include detecting bots, removing duplicate responses, identifying respondents that finish unusually fast, and taking out respondents with invariant responses.

The present study included 306 children (M age = 11.80, SD = 1.23) and parents (M age = 38.52, SD = 5.99). There were approximately equivalent numbers of boys (n = 155, 50.5%) and girls (n = 151, 49.2%), and the majority of parents were mothers (n = 186, 60.6%). Children were mostly White or Caucasian (n = 214, 69.7%), followed by Black or African American (n = 39, 12.7%) and Hispanic or Latin American (n = 21, 6.8%). Parents were mostly White or Caucasian (n = 220, 71.7%), followed by Black or African American (n = 35, 11.4%) and Hispanic or Latin American (n = 27, 8.8%). Parents reported high levels of education, with 45.1% having a bachelor's degree and an average yearly income of $76714.40 (SD = $55837.55).

Measures

Digital and Traditional Parenting

Parenting was measured using seven indicators. Digital parenting indicators included active digital parenting, restrictive digital parenting, monitoring, and tech‐control (Livingstone et al. 2017); traditional parenting indicators included trust, safety, and parent‐child disclosure. Five items measured active digital parenting, for example, “Do you or your child's other parent/caregiver currently do any of the following activities with your child: Talk to your child about what he/she does on the Internet.” The Cronbach's alpha was 0.848. Responses were rated with 1 “not at all” to 5 “almost all the time.” Six items measured restrictive digital parenting, for example, “Please specify the extent to which you restrict your child from the following activities: give out personal information to others on the Internet, have his or her own social networking profile, and so on.” Responses were rated with 1 “never let my child” to 5 “whenever he/she wants,” and the Cronbach's alpha was 0.858. Six items measured digital monitoring, for example, “Do you check any of the following things: Which websites your child visited based on his/her Internet browsing history, Your child's profile on a social network or online community, and so on.” Responses were rated with 1 “not at all” to 5 “almost all the time,” and the Cronbach's alpha was 0.905. Five items measured tech‐control media monitoring, for example, “How often do you do the following activities: Use parental control technologies to block or filter some types of websites your child visits, use parental control technologies to keep track of the websites your child visits.” Responses were rated with 1 “not at all” to 5 “almost all the time,” and the Cronbach's alpha was 0.942.

Trust was assessed with the Parental Trust Attachment scale (Armsden and Greenberg 1987). There were 10 items, such as “I respect my child's feelings” and “I trust my child's judgment,” and responses were rated 1 “almost never or never true” to 5 “almost always or always true.” Items 3, 4, and 7 were reverse‐coded prior to scale construction. The trust scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.767). To measure safety, we used the digital safety scale (Wisniewski et al. 2017). There were seven items with questions, such as “How well do you know how to do each of the following activities online: Block someone online that you don't want to hear from” and “Change privacy settings on a social networking profile.” Responses were rated with 1 “don't know at all” to 5 “know extremely well.” The Cronbach's alpha for safety was 0.900. For parent report of child disclosure, we assessed with five items such as “How often does your child tell you about how he or she is doing in school or class?” and “How often does your child initiate a conversation with you about school or classes?” (Kerr and Stattin 2000). Responses were rated 1 “almost never” to 5 “almost always.” The Cronbach's alpha for disclosure was 0.720.

Cyberbullying

We used children's reports for cyberbullying, using the cyberbullying perpetration and victimization scales from the Problem Behavior Frequency Scale—Revised (Mehari and Farrell 2018). Participants were asked to report the frequency with which they engaged in behaviors or experienced the events in the last 30 days.

For cyberbullying perpetration, 11 items were included, such as “Used cell phone pictures to threaten to hurt someone physically”, “Called someone you know mean names online like on Facebook or Snapchat or through texting,” and “Spread rumors about someone you know online or through texting.” Responses were rated on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Never) to 6 (20 or more times). Higher values represented higher levels of cyberbullying perpetration. The Cronbach's alpha value for cyberbullying perpetration was 0.953.

For cyberbullying victimization, 11 items were included, such as “Someone used cell phone pictures to make fun of you” and “Someone spread rumors about you online or by texting.” Responses were rated on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Never) to 6 (20 or more times). The Cronbach's alpha value for cyberbullying victimization was 0.977. High values represented a higher frequency of cyberbullying engagement in the given domain.

Demographic Variables

Youths' gender, age, and race, as well as parents' gender, age, race, education, and income, were used to describe the profiles. Parents' gender was coded as “1” for male and “2” for female. Age was measured from 1 “10 years old” to 5 “14 years old” and parents' age was measured in years. Race and ethnicity options included “American Indian or Alaska Native,” “Asian,” “Black or African American,” “Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander,” “White or Caucasian,” “Hispanic or Latin American,” “Mixed Race,” and “Don't know or prefer not to answer” which were recoded race as 1 “White,” 2 “Black,” 3 “Hispanic,” and 4 “Asian, and other race/ethnicity.” Education level was measured from 1 “elementary school” to 9 “PhD degree,” and we recoded these into 1 “under Bachelor's degree” and 2 “Bachelor's degree and above.” Yearly household income was measured with a single open‐ended question (e.g., What was the income for your entire household in 2018 before taxes). Children's age was measured from 1 “10 years old” to 5 “14 years old.” Children's gender and race were measured in the same way as parents.

Analysis Procedures

The analysis was conducted in three steps. First, LPA was conducted in Mplus 8.7 to identify heterogeneous profiles of digital parenting using seven continuous indicators (Muthén and Muthén 1998–2017). LPA is a person‐centered approach that identifies unobserved subgroups based on similar response patterns across multiple indicators (Masyn 2013; Woo et al. 2018). In this study, the latent profiles represent subgroups of parents who report similar patterns of digital parenting practices and parent–child relationship factors.

The optimal number of profiles was determined by estimating models ranging from one‐ to five‐profile solutions (Weller et al. 2020). Model selection was based on both statistical fit indices and conceptual interpretability, including Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and sample‐size adjusted BIC (sBIC) (Tein et al. 2013). We also considered the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), Vuong–Lo–Mendell–Rubin (VLMR) test, and Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR), which compare the fit of k‐ versus k − 1‐profile solutions (Lo 2001). Entropy values were used to assess classification accuracy, with values closer to 1 indicating better separation between profiles (Tein et al. 2013). Parsimony and theoretical interpretability were also considered in selecting the final model.

To aid interpretation of the profiles, we examined within‐profile patterns across the seven indicators by comparing relative levels of each indicator, rather than relying solely on raw means. Second, once the best‐fitting model was identified, we examined associations between latent profile membership and demographic characteristics—including parents' gender, age, race, education, and income, as well as children's gender, age, and race—using the R3STEP approach in Mplus (Asparouhov and Muthén 2014). Third, we used the automated version of the modified BCH 3‐step method (Asparouhov and Muthén 2014; Vermunt 2010) to compare mean levels of cyberbullying (perpetration and victimization) across profiles. This approach accounts for classification uncertainty by incorporating weighting procedures that adjust standard errors and parameter estimates (Vermunt 2010). Specifically, the BCH method tests the null hypothesis that outcome means are equal across profiles using a Wald chi‐square test, rather than relying on traditional mean comparisons (e.g., ANOVA; Asparouhov and Muthén 2014). A significant Wald chi‐square statistic indicates that mean differences exist across at least some of the latent profiles.

Latent Profiles of Digital Parenting

Model fit indices and model comparison results for the 1‐ through 5‐profile solutions are presented in Table 2. As expected, AIC, BIC, and sBIC decreased monotonically with increasing numbers of profiles, indicating improved fit with greater model complexity. Entropy values were within an acceptable range across all solutions, suggesting adequate classification accuracy. The LMR and BLRT indicated that the 4‐profile solution provided a significantly better fit than the 3‐profile model. In contrast, the 5‐profile solution did not significantly improve model fit over the 4‐profile solution, as indicated by a non‐significant LMR test (p = 0.37). Following recommended guidelines (Masyn 2013), this supported retention of the more parsimonious 4‐profile model. Beyond statistical criteria, the 5‐profile solution produced smaller and conceptually overlapping classes, raising concerns about stability and interpretability given the sample size. By comparison, the 4‐profile solution yielded well‐separated and theoretically meaningful profiles with adequate class sizes. Therefore, considering statistical fit, parsimony, and interpretability, the 4‐profile solution was selected as the final model.

The first profile, labeled Low engagement digital parenting, comprised 10% of the sample. Parents in this profile endorsed relatively low levels across most digital parenting indicators. The results showed that in the low engagement group, on average, the majority fell into the lowest quartile (Q1) for active digital parenting, restrictive digital parenting, digital safety, and disclosure. This suggests that parents in this profile were the least engaged in these aspects of digital parenting, potentially granting children greater autonomy in their digital interactions. Traditional parenting indicators were lower than other profiles, but not as low as the digital parenting indicators. Their approach suggests a passive parenting style, in which children have more autonomy in their digital interactions.

The second profile, labeled Discordant digital parenting, represented 24% of the sample. Parents in this group displayed inconsistency across different dimensions of digital parenting within the same parent, rather than differences between parents. Active digital parenting was concentrated in the second‐lowest quartile (Q2), whereas restrictive digital parenting and trust were in the lowest quartile (Q1). In contrast, digital monitoring, tech‐control, and disclosure were relatively higher (Q3), indicating a pattern in which some forms of control were emphasized while relational or trust‐based practices remained low.

The third profile, labeled Trust‐based digital parenting, comprised 32% of the sample. Parents in this profile placed a strong emphasis on trust and disclosure, fostering open communication with their children. They exhibited moderate levels of active digital parenting, safety, and restrictive digital parenting, while reporting the lowest levels of digital monitoring and tech‐control. A quartile analysis of seven indicators revealed that in this profile, average trust and disclosure were in the highest quartile (Q4), indicating that these parents prioritized open dialog and mutual trust. This pattern aligns with an autonomy‐supportive parenting style, where guidance is provided through traditional parenting strategies of trust and communication rather than strict digital parenting and control.

The fourth profile, labeled High engagement digital parenting, represented 34% of the sample. These parents exhibited the highest levels across all digital and traditional parenting indicators, including active and restrictive digital parenting, digital monitoring, tech‐control, trust, safety, and disclosure, reflecting a highly involved and proactive approach. A quartile analysis of the seven indicators showed that most were predominantly concentrated in the highest quartile (Q4), indicating that, on average, parents in this group demonstrated high levels of all parenting behaviors.

Socio‐Demographics Among Digital Parenting Profiles

Parents' gender, age, race, education, and household income, along with the child's gender, age, and race, were examined to characterize the profiles of digital parenting (Table 4). Most demographic characteristics—parents’ race, education, household income, children's gender, age, and race—did not differ by profiles. However, parents' gender and age varied across profiles. Regarding parents' gender, mothers were more likely to engage in trust‐based or high‐engagement digital parenting than discordant digital parenting (respectively, OR = 3.33, p < 0.05; OR = 2.99, p < 0.05). These findings suggest that mothers, as compared to fathers, tended to adopt more structured and engaged digital parenting approaches rather than inconsistent strategies. Regarding parents' age, compared to parents in the low engagement digital parenting profile, parents in the trust‐based digital parenting profile were more likely to be younger (OR = 0.97, p < 0.05). However, compared to the discordant digital parenting profile, parents in the high engagement digital parenting profile were more likely to be older (OR = 1.03, p < 0.05).

Discussion

Guided by the neo‐ecological theory, this study aimed to explore heterogeneous profiles of digital and traditional parenting and their associations with adolescents' involvement in cyberbullying. By employing LPA, we identified four distinct profiles of digital parenting—low engagement, discordant, trust‐based, and high engagement—each representing unique patterns of parenting behaviors, including active and restrictive digital parenting, monitoring, trust, disclosure, and digital safety as indicators. The results highlight critical differences in adolescents' cyberbullying perpetration and victimization across these profiles, indicating that the likelihood of being in the discordant and low engagement digital parenting profiles is associated with more frequent cyberbullying involvement among children compared to other profiles. This approach provides valuable insights into how the virtual and family microsystems intersect in adolescents' lives and how variations in digital parenting strategies and parent‐child relationships may shape adolescents' online experiences, highlighting the need for balanced and consistent parenting practices that integrate both control and support in the digital age.

Parents in the low engagement profile reported the lowest levels of monitoring and tech control behaviors, showing that most indicators—active and restrictive digital parenting, trust, disclosure, and digital safety—were lower than in other profiles. This pattern parallels Baumrind's neglectful style, with low responsiveness and control, linked to higher adolescent risk‐taking behaviors (Baumrind 1966; Maccoby and Martin 1983). Adolescents in this profile exhibited the elevated levels of both cyberbullying perpetration and victimization. This pattern suggests that low parental engagement in the digital domain and adolescents' cyberbullying involvement tend to co‐occur. Prior research has highlighted that parental involvement—such as active guidance and oversight—can be an important correlate of reduced online risk (Elsaesser et al. 2017). Accordingly, one plausible interpretation is that limited guidance and oversight may be associated with greater exposure to risky online situations, potentially increasing vulnerability to both perpetration and victimization (Chen et al. 2023). However, the direction of effects cannot be determined in the present cross‐sectional design. An alternative explanation is that adolescents who are already experiencing cyberbullying or engaging in problematic online behaviors may elicit less consistent, less effective, or more withdrawn parental involvement over time. Thus, the observed association may reflect transactional or bidirectional processes between adolescents' online experiences and parents' digital parenting practices.

Discordant profile parents were characterized by high levels of active digital parenting and monitoring but lower levels of restrictive rules and trust. This mixed configuration with high behavioral oversight and weaker clarity on rules and relational components reflects an inconsistent balance between control and support rather than a clearly defined style. Adolescents in this profile reported highest levels of both cyberbullying perpetration and victimization. Notably, adolescents in this profile exhibited the highest mean levels of both cyberbullying perpetration and victimization among the four latent profiles. One possible interpretation is that such inconsistencies in digital parenting practices are associated with greater online risk involvement. Prior research suggests that high levels of monitoring without corresponding trust or open communication are linked to lower adolescent trust and more secretive online behaviors (Symons et al. 2017). Thus, the discordant profile may reflect transactional dynamics between adolescents' online behaviors and parents' adaptive and potentially reactive strategies.

Parents in the trust‐based profile reported the highest levels of trust and disclosure, along with the lowest levels of monitoring and tech‐control. Although their use of tech‐control was minimal, they demonstrated relatively high levels of restrictive digital parenting, suggesting that boundaries may have been communicated through household rules rather than enforced through digital surveillance tools. Overall, this configuration reflects a relationship‐centered approach characterized by openness and mutual trust. Adolescents in this profile reported lower levels of cyberbullying perpetration and victimization compared to those in the low engagement and discordant profiles. These findings indicate that high relational trust and open communication co‐occurred with lower cyberbullying involvement within this sample. Trust and disclosure may facilitate adolescents' willingness to share online experiences and seek parental guidance when encountering risks (Livingstone and Blum‐Ross 2020). At the same time, the direction of this association cannot be determined. It is also plausible that adolescents who are less involved in problematic online behaviors elicit greater parental trust and reduced monitoring, consistent with transactional models emphasized in neo‐ecological theory. Prior research suggests that parents may increase monitoring in response to adolescents' behavioral concerns (Rodríguez‐Meirinhos et al. 2020), underscoring the need for longitudinal research to clarify the directionality of these associations.

High engagement profile parents reported the highest levels across all indicators, reflecting a proactive and comprehensive digital parenting approach. This pattern balances high control with warmth and responsiveness, fostering both structure and autonomy, which have been associated with low cyberbullying (Chen and Shi 2019; Legate et al. 2019). Adolescents in this profile demonstrated the lowest levels of both cyberbullying perpetration and victimization, reinforcing prior research that links authoritative parenting to positive digital outcomes (Wisniewski et al. 2025). This approach provides adolescents with clear boundaries and guidance while fostering a secure and supportive environment that reduces impulsive or aggressive online behaviors. By integrating both preventive strategies (e.g., restrictions via house rules and monitoring) and supportive behaviors (e.g., trust and open communication) and applying them to the digital microsystem, authoritative digital parenting may serve as an effective model for mitigating online risks while promoting responsible technology use. These findings suggest that a combination of structure and relational support is associated with lower involvement in cyberbullying; however, the present design does not allow conclusions about causal influence. Rather, high engagement digital parenting and lower cyberbullying involvement appear to co‐occur within this sample.

The results showed that among various socio‐demographic variables, only parents' gender and age had significant effects on digital parenting profiles. More specifically, mothers were more likely to adopt trust‐based or high‐engagement digital parenting compared to discordant approaches. However, there were no distinct patterns of digital parenting across socioeconomic status. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as our sample consists of relatively well‐educated and economically stable participants, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Additionally, younger parents were more likely to engage in trust‐based digital parenting, while older parents were more likely to adopt high‐engagement strategies, which may reflect generational differences as younger parents were exposed to the digital microsystem at younger ages and were more familiar with digital proximal processes (Modecki et al. 2022). These results highlight the evolving nature of digital parenting across demographic groups, emphasizing that beyond broad socio‐demographic characteristics, individual parental attributes—such as age and gender—may be key determinants of how parents navigate their children's digital experiences (Savic 2022). This underscores the importance of considering parental perspectives and adaptive strategies in promoting effective digital parenting.

From a neo‐ecological perspective, the present findings underscore that parenting practices function not as isolated behaviors but as interrelated constellations embedded within adolescents' broader digital environments. By employing a person‐centered approach, this study aligns with the ecological emphasis on patterned configurations of influence rather than independent, additive effects. The identification of distinct digital parenting profiles suggests that adolescents experience qualitatively different digital ecologies depending on how parental practices co‐occur and offers a more nuanced understanding of how parenting shapes adolescents' online experiences.

Digital Parenting Programming Implications

The findings from this study have important implications for developing interventions and guidelines to support parents in digital parenting. Recognizing the heterogeneity in digital parenting strategies, interventions should be tailored to address the specific needs and challenges associated with each profile. For example, parents in the low engagement profile may benefit from foundational digital literacy training, practical guidance on monitoring tools, and support in initiating conversations about online experiences. For discordant parents, intervention efforts could focus on enhancing consistency by aligning monitoring practices with clearly communicated rules and incorporating trust‐building strategies. Parents in the trust‐based profile may benefit from additional guidance on boundary‐setting to ensure that openness is accompanied by appropriate structure. For high engagement parents, booster sessions could support the maintenance of balanced practices while adapting to adolescents' increasing autonomy. Importantly, emerging family‐based intervention research suggests that allowing parents to identify their own needs and select areas for improvement can enhance motivation and engagement (Fosco et al. 2014). Accordingly, rather than prescribing a fixed intervention pathway, profile‐informed programs may be most effective when offering flexible, modular resources that parents can choose from based on their perceived challenges and goals.

In practice, such approaches could be implemented through brief screening tools that classify parents into profiles and guide them toward relevant resources (e.g., digital literacy modules, communication‐focused workshops, or rule‐setting toolkits). Consistent with family‐centered intervention approaches such as the Family Check‐Up model (Dishion et al. 2003), maintaining parental autonomy in selecting intervention components may further strengthen program relevance and participation. These profile‐informed resources could be integrated into school‐ or community‐based parent education programs, online platforms, or individualized coaching sessions. Parents who already demonstrate balanced and adaptive practices—such as those in the high engagement profile—may opt for minimal intervention and instead access optional booster resources. Finally, the findings suggest that interventions aimed at reducing cyberbullying may benefit from prioritizing the development of parent–child trust and promoting adolescents' willingness to disclose their online experiences.

Strengths and Limitations

While this study provides valuable insight into heterogeneity in digital parenting and its associations with adolescents' cyberbullying involvement, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal or directional inferences regarding the associations between digital parenting profiles and cyberbullying outcomes. Bidirectional influences are likely, and longitudinal research is needed to examine how digital parenting practices and adolescents' online behaviors shape one another over time.

Second, the study relied on self‐reported measures, which may be subject to social desirability and subjective interpretation. This concern may be particularly relevant for digital parenting, as parents' digital literacy and familiarity with technology may affect how they perceive and report their own practices. In addition, the 30‐day recall window for cyberbullying, although consistent with prior research intended to reduce recall bias, may underestimate less frequent or episodic forms of involvement. Future studies could strengthen measurement validity by incorporating longer recall periods, multi‐informant reports, and multimethod approaches.

Third, the characteristics of the sample limit the generalizability of the findings. Participants were drawn primarily from White families with relatively high levels of parental education, reflecting the composition of the recruitment panel. The use of a Qualtrics panel may also introduce self‐selection bias and limit representativeness. In addition, the sample consisted primarily of pre‐ to early adolescents (mean age < 12), and patterns of digital autonomy, peer influence, and parenting may differ in middle and late adolescence. Accordingly, the findings should be generalized to older adolescents and more socioeconomically, racially, and culturally diverse populations with caution. Because parenting norms and expectations are embedded in broader cultural contexts, the identified profiles should not be assumed to represent universal patterns, underscoring the need for replication in more diverse and cross‐cultural samples.

Fourth, sample size may have affected the stability and interpretability of the latent profile solution. Although the sample size (N = 306) falls within the range commonly used in LPA (Masyn 2013), some profiles were relatively small, which may reduce stability and reproducibility, particularly for more complex solutions. This consideration informed our decision to retain the more parsimonious four‐profile solution rather than a five‐profile model with smaller and potentially less stable classes. In addition, dividing the sample into multiple profiles may have reduced power to detect smaller between‐profile differences. Future research using larger and independent samples, including replication and cross‐validation where feasible, is needed to further evaluate the robustness of digital parenting profile structures across developmental periods and populations.

Despite these limitations, the present study makes several important contributions. By adopting a person‐centered approach, this study moves beyond variable‐centered examinations of individual parenting practices and identifies meaningful heterogeneity in digital parenting configurations. The use of LPA provides a more differentiated understanding of how digital‐specific and traditional parenting dimensions co‐occur within adolescents' digital ecologies. By linking these distinct profiles to cyberbullying perpetration and victimization, the findings offer both conceptual and practical insight into how patterned combinations of parenting practices relate to adolescents' online experiences. Together, these contributions provide a foundation for future longitudinal, cross‐cultural, and intervention‐oriented research on digital parenting in contemporary developmental contexts.

Conclusion

The results of this study highlight the diverse and nuanced nature of digital parenting and its critical role in shaping adolescents' involvement in cyberbullying. By identifying distinct profiles of digital parenting and their associations with cyberbullying outcomes, these findings contribute to a deeper understanding of how parents can effectively support and guide their children in navigating the digital world. The distinct profiles illustrate the diverse ways families respond to digitalization. By addressing gaps in the literature and emerging challenges, these findings may inform the development of interventions that foster healthier, more balanced digital environments for families and better equip children to navigate digital challenges.
