Self‐Efficacy, Mental Health and Support Services Use Among Baccalaureate Nursing Students: A Cross‐Sectional Pilot Study
Authors: Abby Grammer Horton, Janet Brown, Christina J Ezemenaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNursing students experience high levels of academic and emotional stress, yet the relationships among self‐efficacy, mental health challenges and utilization of institutional support services remain poorly understood in nursing education. In particular, limited empirical work has examined these factors together or assessed the feasibility of studying their associations within nursing student populations.AimsTo explore the relationship between self‐efficacy, mental health challenges and utilization of student support services as potential factors influencing nursing student success and retention and to assess current utilization of student services and feasibility of continuing or adding services.DesignA cross‐sectional, exploratory pilot study.Review MethodsDescriptive and comparative analyses were conducted to examine associations between self‐efficacy scores and the use of support services among nursing students to assess current utilization of student services and feasibility of continuing or adding services.Data SourcesData were collected through a survey distributed to nursing students enrolled in a prelicensure programme at a single academic institution.ResultsTutoring emerged as the most frequently utilized student support service, followed by peer mentoring and counselling. Introduction

Baccalaureate nursing students face substantial academic and emotional demands, placing them at elevated risk for stress, anxiety and depression—factors that may compromise academic progression and retention. Identifying modifiable supports that promote persistence and well‐being remains a critical focus in nursing education, particularly as programmes strive to address workforce shortages and student attrition.

Self‐efficacy, defined as an individual's belief in their capacity to manage challenges and perform tasks successfully (Bandura 1977), has been shown to mediate the relationship between critical thinking and positive mental health among nursing students (Yurt and Hayli 2025). In nursing education, higher self‐efficacy has been linked to adaptive coping and confidence in academic and clinical performance. At the same time, nursing programmes offer a range of student support services, including tutoring, academic advising and mental health resources, intended to mitigate stress and support student success. Despite the relevance of these constructs, the literature has not clearly established how self‐efficacy, mental health challenges and utilization of support services relate to one another within nursing student populations.

Most existing studies examine these factors independently, with limited attention to their interrelationships or to the feasibility of studying them simultaneously. As a result, knowledge gaps remain regarding patterns of support service utilization, recruitment and completion challenges and appropriate analytic approaches—particularly in small or pilot samples.

This pilot study addresses these gaps by exploring associations and group differences among self‐efficacy, mental health challenges and student support service utilization among baccalaureate nursing students, with an emphasis on feasibility and descriptive findings. The results are intended to inform the design of future, adequately powered, multi‐site studies.

Findings from this work may benefit nurse educators, academic support staff and administrators by providing insight into service utilization patterns and considerations for future research design. Policymakers and educational leaders may also benefit from preliminary evidence that informs broader efforts to support nursing student success and retention. While not intended to establish impact, this study contributes foundational knowledge to advance research in nursing education.

With an ever‐increasing need for nursing graduates to meet the demands of the nursing shortage, the retention and success of nursing students are crucial. As the nursing workforce continues to retire at a rate disproportionate to the number of students graduating from colleges of nursing, the need for nursing student success and graduation continues to grow (Weston 2022). For these reasons, it is important to explore factors that may be related to student retention and academic success.

Self‐efficacy, mental health challenges, and utilization of student support services may be factors related to the retention and academic success of baccalaureate nursing students. Self‐efficacy, or an individual's confidence in their ability towards a specific task (Bandura 1977), may affect a student's potential for successful completion of a nursing degree programme. Mental health challenges may influence nursing students' academic success, particularly given the critical role of resilience, stress management and psychological well‐being in the nursing profession (Horton 2021). Student support services are one approach to fostering nursing student academic success and supporting students' mental health. The purpose of this pilot study was to examine the association between self‐efficacy, mental health challenges and student utilization of these student support services. This study was not designed or powered to test hypotheses; rather, it aimed to assess feasibility, recruitment, data completeness and preliminary patterns to inform future research.

Review of Literature

A positive relationship between self‐regulated learning and self‐efficacy has been established (Chen et al. 2019), and self‐efficacy has been shown to be a predictor for academic success (Dogan 2015). For these reasons, the self‐efficacy construct of Bandura's Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura 1977) was selected as the theoretical framework for this study. This theory states that an individual's personal (self) efficacy is the factor that determines whether an individual can manage a stressful situation. This theory posits that self‐efficacy has a key role in personal change, or that an individual's self‐efficacy predicts behaviour. However, it has not been established in current literature whether a relationship between self‐efficacy, mental health challenges and utilization of student support services exists. Research has shown self‐efficacy mediates the relationship between critical thinking and positive mental health (Yurt and Hayli 2025), that there is a high rate of depression among nursing students (Tung et al. 2018), perceived stress is high among nursing students (Aqtam et al. 2025) and interventions have been shown to have a positive influence on anxiety among nursing students (Yosep et al. 2025).

Research Questions

The specific aims of this study were to examine associations among self‐efficacy, mental health challenges (including depression, anxiety and stress) and utilization of student support services among baccalaureate nursing students. The pilot study employed a validated measure of self‐efficacy, along with survey items assessing mental health challenges, use of student support services and relevant demographic characteristics. Given the exploratory nature and limited statistical power of this pilot study, analyses were focused on identifying associations and group differences among key variables rather than evaluating predictive or causal relationships.

Specific Aim 1

RQ1: Is there an association between self‐efficacy and utilization of student support services (tutoring, peer mentoring and counselling)?

RQ2: What is the rate of utilization of each specific student support service: specifically, tutoring, peer mentoring and counselling?

Specific Aim 2

RQ1: Is self‐efficacy related to depression?

RQ2: Is self‐efficacy related to anxiety?

RQ3: Is self‐efficacy associated with stress?

RQ4: Is accessing student support services associated with depression, anxiety, or stress?

Method

After institutional review board (IRB) approval (Protocol #21‐11‐5136, 11/24/2021) all nursing students enrolled in a BSN programme at a large public university in the Southeastern United States were offered an opportunity to participate in this cross‐sectional survey. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

A total of 104 students provided consent to participate in the survey. Of these, 29 students (27.8%) completed the survey items and were included in the analytic sample (n = 29). If students provided consent, they were entered in the drawing for the gift card, but of those (n = 104), only 29 completed the survey. Participants represented all five BSN semesters: two students from Semester I (2/29, 6.9%), 11 from Semester II (11/29, 37.9%), two from Semester III (2/29, 6.9%), nine from Semester IV (9/29, 31.0%) and five from Semester V (5/29, 17.2%). All participants in the analytic sample identified as female (29/29, 100%). Most participants identified as White (25/29, 89%), reported a parental household income greater than $100,000 (20/29, 70%), were single (21/29, 70%), and identified as heterosexual (27/29, 92%). Additional characteristics included part‐time employment (4/29, 14%), first‐generation college student status (7/29, 22%) and age 21 years and under (21/29, 72.4%). Given the small analytic sample size, demographic characteristics are presented using both raw counts and percentages and should be interpreted descriptively.

Instruments

Self‐efficacy was measured using the New General Self‐Efficacy Scale (NGSE). Validation studies of the NGSE have demonstrated high internal consistency and reliability (α = 0.86 and 0.90) (Chen et al. 2001). The instrument contains eight questions related to self‐efficacy. Each question is scored using a five‐point Likert‐type scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). Higher scores are indicative of greater self‐efficacy (Chen et al. 2001). Utilization of the student support services of tutoring, peer mentoring and counselling was self‐reported by the students. Each student support service was scored yes/no as to whether the student had utilized the service. An additional question asked if they had used more than one student support service.

Mental Health Challenges were measured using the PHQ‐9 (depression), GAD‐7 (anxiety) and Perceived Stress Scale (stress). The PHQ‐9 is a nine‐item measure with a four‐point Likert Scale used to determine the presence or severity of depression within the last 2 weeks. Internal consistency and validity of the PHQ‐9 are considered high as evidenced by a study involving two different patient populations with Cronbach alphas of 0.86 and 0.89 (Kroenke et al. 2001). The GAD‐7 is a seven‐item survey used to measure the presence and severity of anxiety. The GAD‐7 has good internal validity (Cronbach α = 0.92), good test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation = 0.83) and criterion, factorial and procedural validity (Spitzer et al. 2006). The PSS is a 10‐item validated and internally consistent survey with items that are measured using a four‐point Likert Scale to indicate global stress (Cohen et al. 1983).

Scale scores were calculated only for participants who provided complete responses to all items within the scale. Internal consistency reliability was not examined within this pilot sample due to the small analytic sample size; however, the instrument has demonstrated acceptable reliability in prior studies. Procedure

A Qualtrics survey comprised of questions related to demographic information, the NGSE instrument, the PHQ‐9, GAD‐7 and Perceived Stress Scale, and the self‐report of utilization of the student support services of tutoring, peer mentoring and counselling currently offered at the college of nursing.

Study information and a Qualtrics survey link were posted on the Blackboard Learning Management System within one BSN course per semester across all five BSN semesters. Recruitment occurred during the academic term, and the survey remained open for approximately 4 weeks. All upper‐division nursing students enrolled in the College of Nursing received an email invitation containing the survey link, with reminder emails sent during the availability period. The survey was completed electronically and required approximately 20 min to complete.

Informed consent was embedded within the survey, and students indicated consent by selecting a checkbox prior to beginning the questionnaire. Upon survey submission, responses were automatically de‐identified. To encourage participation while minimizing coercion, students were offered the opportunity to enter a drawing for one of two $75 VISA gift cards through a separate Qualtrics link. The drawing survey was accessible regardless of survey participation, allowing students to answer a nursing trivia question and provide an email address to maintain voluntary participation and reduce potential response bias.

Results

Given the exploratory nature of this pilot study and the small analytic sample (n = 29), statistical analyses were selected to examine associations and group differences while balancing interpretability and robustness to assumption violations. Due to the small pilot sample, effect sizes and confidence intervals are emphasized conceptually rather than inferentially; however, precision remains limited. In accordance with STROBE guidelines, handling of missing data and justification of study size are reported transparently. Descriptive statistics were first examined to summarize participant characteristics and study variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to explore associations between continuous variables when distributions approximated normality. Independent‐samples t‐tests were applied to compare mean scale scores between groups when assumptions of approximate normality and homogeneity of variance were reasonably met. Given the limited statistical power inherent to this pilot study, analyses were conducted for exploratory purposes. Emphasis was placed on estimation, descriptive patterns and magnitude of observed differences rather than formal hypothesis testing or causal inference.

The small sample size limited statistical power and increased uncertainty around estimates; therefore, findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis‐generating.

Data was analysed using JMP Pro 15 (JMP, Version 16. SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). SAS Institute (1989). Among the 17 participants who used support services, 2 (11.8%) reported receiving counselling, 4 (23.5%) participated in peer mentoring, 11 (64.7%) received tutoring and 3 (17.6%) utilized both tutoring and peer mentoring. Mean self‐efficacy scores did not statistically significantly differ by utilization of any support service (Yes/No), with students who utilized support services having similar self‐efficacy scores (Mean = 32.0, SD = 3.8) as students who did not utilize support services (Mean = 34.2, SD = 3.5), t (27) = 1.52, 95% CI (−5.1, 0.75), p = 0.1391.

Additionally, there was not a statistically significant association between self‐efficacy scores and utilization of specific support service, with students who utilized counselling or peer mentoring services having similar self‐efficacy scores (Mean = 36.0, SD = 4.8) as students who utilized tutoring services only (Mean = 33.7, SD = 3.2), t (15) = −1.2, 95% CI (−1.8, 6.4), p = 0.2565.

Pearson's correlation was used to determine if self‐efficacy is associated with depression. No statistically significant association was detected in this small sample between self‐efficacy and depression scores, r (29) = −0.27, p = 0.1500. There were no statistically significant associations by depression severity. Pearson's correlation was used to determine if self‐efficacy is associated with anxiety. Analyses did not identify a statistically significant association between self‐efficacy and anxiety scores, r (29) = −0.008, p = 0.9569. There were no statistically significant associations by anxiety severity, results not reported. Using Pearson's correlation, self‐efficacy is not significantly associated with stress scores, r (29) = 0.11, p = 0.5540. There were no statistically significant associations by stress severity, results not reported.

Discussion

This pilot study primarily offers insight into the feasibility of examining relationships among self‐efficacy, mental health challenges and student support service utilization within a baccalaureate nursing student population. Although participation and completion rates were limited, the study demonstrates that electronic survey methods can be successfully deployed across multiple programme semesters. However, the modest analytic sample highlights challenges related to engagement and survey completion that should be addressed in future studies through enhanced recruitment strategies, simplified survey design, or alternative data collection approaches.

Patterns of support service utilization observed in this sample suggest that while services are accessed by many students, use varies across individuals and contexts. The absence of statistically significant group differences in this pilot sample should be interpreted as reflecting limited power rather than evidence of no relationship. Notably, descriptive differences in mean mental health scores across groups provide preliminary signals that warrant further investigation.

Collectively, these findings inform the design of future, adequately powered studies by clarifying feasible recruitment approaches, identifying relevant measures and highlighting the need for larger samples and refined analytic strategies. Emphasizing feasibility and descriptive patterns rather than impact, this pilot study contributes to the development of more rigorous investigations aimed at understanding factors that support nursing student success and retention.

In this pilot study, no statistically significant association was detected between self‐efficacy scores and utilization of student support services; however, descriptive patterns of service use provide useful insight. Tutoring emerged as the most frequently utilized support service, suggesting that academic support is a salient need among baccalaureate nursing students. This finding highlights the feasibility of capturing service utilization data through survey methods and points to potential areas for refinement in future studies, such as more detailed assessment of service accessibility, frequency of use and perceived usefulness. Observations from this pilot may also inform institutional considerations for monitoring tutor availability and increasing awareness of existing resources. The sample size was selected to evaluate feasibility parameters and estimate variability for future power calculations rather than to detect statistically significant effects.

The study's findings primarily inform future research design rather than conclusions about impact. Specifically, the limited analytic sample underscores the need for improved recruitment and retention strategies, streamlined instruments and larger samples to adequately examine relationships among self‐efficacy, student support service utilization, academic success and programme completion. Future studies may benefit from mixed‐methods approaches, incorporating qualitative inquiry to better capture student experiences and contextual factors influencing self‐efficacy, mental health and progression through nursing programmes. Collectively, these lessons from the pilot phase contribute to the development of more rigorous and comprehensive investigations aimed at understanding nursing student experiences and supporting success and retention in nursing education.

Limitations

Out of the 114 potential survey respondents, 104 (91.2%) consented to participate in the survey. Of those students who participated, 29 (27.8%) provided responses to survey questions. Therefore, the low response rate is a limitation of this study. A possible reason for the low participation was the use of a drawing system for the $75 VISA gift cards rather than each student receiving individual compensation. The timing of the survey participation period may not have been optimal, and although the survey was not opened during mid‐term evaluation time or finals, individual course requirements may have impacted participation. Given the exploratory pilot nature of the study, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Because reasons for non‐completion were not systematically collected, missing data may reflect competing academic or personal demands. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of no association but as inconclusive due to limited power.

Implications for Nursing Education

Exploring the relationships among self‐efficacy, student retention and utilization of support services in nursing education is important for understanding factors that contribute to academic success and professional readiness. Self‐efficacy—defined as an individual's belief in their ability to succeed in specific tasks—plays a central role in how students approach challenges, seek assistance and persist through demanding curricula such as those in nursing programmes. Prior literature suggests that higher self‐efficacy may support engagement, resilience and continued progression despite academic and emotional stressors.

Student support services, including academic advising, tutoring, mental health resources and peer mentoring, are designed to promote both academic performance and well‐being. Engagement with these services has been theorized to strengthen students' sense of competence and empowerment, which may, in turn, support self‐efficacy and retention. However, in this pilot study, no statistically significant associations among self‐efficacy, support service utilization and retention outcomes were detected, likely due to the small and underpowered analytic sample.

Although these findings should be interpreted cautiously, they highlight the complexity of factors influencing nursing student success and underscore the need for larger, adequately powered studies to more fully examine these relationships. Continued investigation may help identify effective strategies to support self‐efficacy and promote meaningful engagement with support services across diverse nursing student populations. This pilot did not have sufficient participants to achieve power; the findings are consistent with current research that highlights the relationships between self‐efficacy, resilience and mental health among nursing students, and it can be helpful to evaluate the services most helpful and most utilized by nursing students.

Nurse educators should remain attentive to students' academic, mental health and self‐efficacy needs to support positive progression and retention in nursing programmes (Clark 2018). While the current study offers preliminary insight into these interrelated factors, future research is needed to determine how targeted interventions aimed at strengthening self‐efficacy and optimizing support service utilization may improve programme completion rates and student success.

Summary

Existing literature has not clearly established the relationships among self‐efficacy, mental health challenges and utilization of student support services in nursing education, making this underpowered pilot study a novel and exploratory contribution. Although no statistically significant associations were detected, the study demonstrated the feasibility of assessing these constructs using survey methods and described patterns of support service utilization among baccalaureate nursing students. These preliminary findings should be interpreted cautiously and are intended to inform—rather than confirm—future research directions.

Importantly, this pilot highlights several considerations for subsequent study design, including the need for larger and more diverse samples, multi‐site recruitment, improved response rates and analytic approaches suited to small subgroups. Future research should build on these feasibility findings to more rigorously examine relationships among self‐efficacy, mental health, academic performance and student support utilization. Continued investigation using adequately powered, mixed‐methods and multi‐institutional designs is essential to advance understanding of nursing student experiences and outcomes.
