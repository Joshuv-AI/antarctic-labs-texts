Ag-enhanced β titanium alloys for surgical implants: microstructural regulation achieves superior strength, corrosion resistance and biocompatibility
Authors: Xiqun Ma, Yufei Tang, Sen Yu, Wenhao Zhou, Jun Cheng, Tian Bai, Lan Wang, Hanyuan Liu, Xiyuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstractβ-titanium alloys attract great interest in surgical implants due to its low elastic modulus. The addition of Ag further enhances the strength, ductility, corrosion resistance and biocompatibility of such alloys, which optimizes their compatibility with human bones. In this study, Ti-15Mo-xAg (x = 1, 5 wt.%) alloy rods were prepared by optimized melting, forging and rolling processes. The microstructural evolution, mechanical properties, corrosion behavior and biocompatibility of the alloy were systematically studied by X-ray diffraction (XRD), optical microscopy (OM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), mechanical testing, electrochemical analysis and biocompatibility assays. The results showed that the aged Ti-15Mo-5Ag alloy presented a special heterogeneous microstructure of β matrix and micro-nano scale precipitated phase (α1 + α2 + Ti2Ag), which enabled it to achieve excellent mechanical adaptability. Its tensile strength reached 1208 MPa, and the elongation was 11.5%, while the alloy maintained a low elastic modulus of 98 GPa. The self-corrosion current density of the alloy in the simulated body fluids was as low as 1.176 × 10-8 A·cm-2. In vitro biological tests indicated good biocompatibility. This alloy is expected to become a candidate material for future surgical implant applications.

Introduction

Titanium and its alloys are widely regarded as essential materials in biomedical applications due to their unique combination of lightweight properties, high strength-to-weight ratios, excellent corrosion resistance and biocompatibility [1, 2]. Among these, commercially pure titanium (Cp-Ti) and Ti-6Al-4V (ELI) alloy remain the most commonly used materials for surgical implants, particularly in orthopedics and dentistry. However, these materials exhibit notable limitations. Pure titanium lacks sufficient mechanical strength for human body load-bearing systems, whereas the widely used Ti-6Al-4V alloy releases aluminum (Al) and vanadium (V) ions during service. These ions cause cytotoxicity, neurotoxicity and adverse cellular reactions around the implants, thereby raising concerns about the long-term safety of patients and the durability of the implant [3–5]. Furthermore, the elastic modulus of Ti-6Al-4V ( ~110 GPa) significantly exceeds that of human cortical bone (10-30 GPa), thereby resulting in the stress shielding effect, that is, the phenomenon where excessive load is transferred to the implant, causing bone resorption and eventual loosening [6–10]. These shortcomings have driven the development of the next generation β-type titanium alloys, which combine a low elastic modulus with superior biocompatibility and mechanical adaptability.

Eliminating sensitizing or toxic elements (Al, V and Ni) while optimizing phase stability and biocompatibility is currently the main research direction in the field of β titanium alloys. Therefore, in the past decade, many multi-component β titanium alloys such as Ti-Nb, Ti-Mo, and Ti-Ta series have been designed and developed. Among these new types of low-modulus β titanium alloys, Ti-Mo-based alloys have become the preferred material for surgical implants due to their excellent strength, corrosion resistance and compatibility with the elastic modulus of bone. They mainly include Ti-12Mo-6Zr-2Fe (ASTM F 1813), Ti-15Mo (ASTM F 2066), Ti-15Mo-5Zr-3Al (JIS T 7401-6) and Ti-32Mo. The elastic modulus of these alloys is as low as 60–100 GPa, effectively alleviating the stress shielding effect [11–14]. Furthermore, the addition of molybdenum (Mo) stabilizes the β phase in titanium alloys, enhancing their mechanical properties and wear resistance. All of these properties are important indicators for load-bearing implants such as artificial joints and spinal fixtures devices [15–17]. However, these medical titanium alloys still face significant challenges in balancing strength, ductility and corrosion resistance. Therefore, good biomechanical adaptability remains a problem that needs to be solved for medical titanium alloys in the future. In addition, adding Ag or Cu elements to titanium alloys has emerged as a prominent area of research. Some studies have demonstrated that the titanium alloys containing these two elements may exhibit certain antibacterial properties, thereby offering potential solutions for addressing infections following implantation. Some studies have indicated that Ag-doped titanium alloys exhibit strong antibacterial effects against pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, while simultaneously improving corrosion resistance and mechanical properties [18–20]. For instance, Ti-Ag alloys produced by powder metallurgy show uniform dispersion of submicron Ag particles, which improves hardness and wear resistance without sacrificing ductility [21]. Moreover, during the corrosion process of the titanium alloys doped with Ag or Cu elements, the Ag ions released can damage the bacterial cell membranes, thereby offering dual functions as both structural and bioactive materials [18, 22].

Despite so many advantages, integrating Ag into Ti-Mo alloys presents certain technical difficulties. The large difference in melting points between Mo (2622 °C) and Ag (962 °C) leads to problems such as Ag volatilization and Mo segregation during the conventional melting processes, resulting in a compositional inhomogeneity [23, 24]. Therefore, up to now, most of the reported Ti-Mo-Ag systems have relied on powder metallurgy, additive manufacturing or more complex preparation process to achieve consistency of chemical composition and uniform microstructures [25–27], which limits the application of such metals combined into alloys and their products. Cui et al. prepared Ti-Mo-Ag alloy ingots through ten times vacuum non-consumable smelting processes and studied the macroscopic influence law of adding 10%Ag silver on the elastic modulus and hardness [28]. Xu et al. fabricated Ti-Mo-Ag devices with fine microstructures and antibacterial properties by using in-situ laser alloying technology during the additive manufacturing process [26]. These studies not only demonstrate the role of heat treatment in optimizing phase transitions (such as β → α or β → ω), but also indicate that phase transitions control various properties and microstructures of alloys. However, systematic studies on how a small amount of Ag affects the phase evolution, mechanical properties, corrosion resistance and biocompatibility of Ti-15Mo alloys have been rarely reported.

Most existing Ti-X-Ag alloys rely on complex preparation processes such as powder metallurgy and laser alloying. These processes can improve compositional uniformity but suffer from high preparation costs and difficulty in large-scale production. In this study, Ti-15Mo-xAg alloy (wt.%) rods with uniform microstructures were successfully fabricated using a combined process of vacuum induction melting (VIM) and vacuum consumable melting (VCM) combined with subsequent forging-rolling. This process can effectively inhibit Ag volatilization and Mo segregation, solving the problem of compositional inhomogeneity in traditional melting processes. Meanwhile, the microstructural evolution, mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, and biocompatibility of these alloys were systematically investigated. The investigation provides valuable insights for designing advanced biomedical titanium alloys with optimized performance characteristics.

Experimental

Materials and methods

Ti-15Mo-xAg alloys (x = 1, 5 wt.%; denoted as TMAX) were prepared via VIM followed by two VCM cycles. Raw materials included Grade 0 sponge titanium (≥99.8%), pure silver chips (≥99.9%), and molybdenum powder (Jinduicheng Molybdenum Industry Co., Ltd.). The homogenized ingots at 1000 °C for 2 h underwent six forging passes to form square billets, which were subsequently hot-rolled at 780 °C into the rods with a diameter of 10 mm. The preparation process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Cylindrical specimens (φ10 × 200 mm) were subjected to solution treatment at 750 °C for 1 h with water cooling (WC) (designated as S), followed by aging treatment at 420 °C, 520 °C and 620 °C for 8 h with air cooling (AC) (designated as SA-420, SA-520 and SA-620). Phase identification and microstructure

The samples were chemically etched in a solution of HF: HNO3: H2O = 1: 4: 3. Phase identification was performed using a D8 ADVANCE X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with the Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) operating at 40 kV and 250 mA. Diffraction patterns were obtained within the 2θ range of 30°- 90° with a scanning rate of 2°·min-1. The microstructure was characterized by a Zeiss optical microscope (OM) and a JEM-3010 transmission electron microscope (TEM) at an acceleration voltage of 120 kV. Furthermore, based on the Thermo-Cal software and the titanium alloy database, the Gibbs free energy of the phases was calculated using the phase diagram calculation method.

Mechanical properties test

The tensile specimens (with a length of 25 mm and a diameter of 5 mm) were machined in accordance with the Chinese national standard GB/T 228.1-2021 [21]. The uniaxial tensile testing was performed at ambient temperature using an Instron 5985 universal testing system with a constant crosshead displacement rate of 1.0 mm·min-1. Triplicate tests ensured the reliability of the data.

Electrochemical corrosion test

The samples with a diameter of Φ10 mm were ground to 1200 grit with SiC abrasive paper and then mirror-polished with 0.05 μm alumina suspension. Electrochemical measurements were carried out in Hank’s simulated body fluid (37 °C ± 0.5 °C), and a standard three-electrode system was used on an automatic laboratory corrosion measurement system (IM6 ZAHNER-elektrik Gmbh, Zenniom, Germany): a saturated calomel reference electrode, a platinum counter electrode, and the sample as the working electrode. According to ISO 10271: 2020 standard [29], the open-circuit potential (OCP) was recorded for 30 min to determine the open-circuit potential (Eocp). The electrochemical impedance spectrum (EIS) was measured with the range of 10-2 Hz to 105 Hz, and then potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) was carried out at a rate of 0.5 mV·s-1.

In vitro cytocompatibility evaluation

In vitro cytotoxicity tests were performed to assess the potential cytotoxicity of medical titanium alloys and predict their tissue-cell interactions in clinical applications. According to ISO 10993-5 [30], Mouse osteoblastic MC3T3-E1 cells (Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences) were cultured in α-Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM, Hyclone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS, Sloarbio) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Prior to cell seeding, TMAX alloy samples were sterilized in 75% ethanol for 30 min and UV-irradiated for 30 min.

Alloy extracts were prepared by immersing sterilized samples in complete α-MEM (10% FBS, 1% PS) for 72 h. The MC3T3-E1 cells (seeding density: 4 × 104 cells·well-1 in 24-well plates) were cultured with extracts for 1, 3 and 5 days. The hemolytic potential of TMAX alloy was evaluated in accordance with ISO 10993-4:2017 [31] using a modified quantitative hemolysis assay. The blood was then diluted with 0.9% NaCl solution in a ratio of 4:5 (blood: normal saline) for later use. Results and discussion

Phase evolution during heat treatments

Figure 2 shows the XRD patterns of TMAX alloy rods under different heat treatments conditions. In Fig. 2a, the TMA1-S alloy (solution treated and water quenched) exhibits a dual phase microstructure composed of a β-phase matrix. This observation result differs from the composition of the β+ωath phase in Ag-free Ti-15Mo alloy reported by Pavel et al. [32], indicating that the addition of Ag effectively suppresses non-diffusion displacement transformation, thereby inhibiting the formation of the ωath phase during the quenching process. The formation reaction of the ωath phase in Ag-free Ti-15Mo is described as β-Ti (Mo) → ωath -Ti (Mo). Thermodynamically, the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) for a phase transformation is defined by the fundamental relation:

where ΔH is the enthalpy change (kJ·mol⁻¹) associated with the transformation, T is the absolute temperature (K), and ΔS is the entropy change (kJ·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹) of the system. For the ωath formation in Ag-free Ti-15Mo, the calculated ΔG value is -9.7 kJ·mol⁻¹ at room temperature (≈298 K) [33, 34], indicating a thermodynamically spontaneous non-diffusional transformation. In contrast, for TMA1 and TMA5, the ΔG of ωath formation increases to +4.2 kJ·mol⁻¹ and +7.5 kJ·mol⁻¹, respectively. The positive ΔG values arise from the solid solution of Ag atoms in the β-Ti lattice, which modifies both ΔH (by increasing the lattice distortion energy) and ΔS (by reducing the configurational entropy of the β phase), thereby raising the activation energy for non-diffusional transformation and thermodynamically inhibiting ωath phase formation.

XRD patterns of different TMAX alloy rods subjected to Solution treatment (S) and different aging treatments (SA). a TMA1 b TMA5

After aging at 420 °C for 8 h (TMA1-SA420), the diffraction peaks of the hexagonal ω phase appeared alongside the pre-existing phases, indicating that the TMA1-SA420 alloy is composed of β, ω and elemental Ag phases. The formation reaction of the isothermal ω phase (ωiso) can be described as: β-Ti (Mo, Ag) → ωiso-Ti (Mo, Ag).

Based on the formation conditions of the previous studies [35–38], it was determined that the ω phase was an isothermal ω phase (ωiso). The ΔG of this ωiso formation reaction at 420 °C is -15.3 kJ·mol⁻¹ for TMA1 and -17.8 kJ·mol⁻¹ for TMA5 [39, 40]. The more negative ΔG of TMA5 explains the higher volume fraction of ωiso phase compared to TMA1. When the aging temperature rises to 520 °C, the ΔG of ωiso formation becomes positive (+8.2 kJ·mol⁻¹ for TMA1, +6.5 kJ·mol⁻¹ for TMA5), leading to the thermodynamic dissolution of ωiso phase and the transition to β → α + Ti₂Ag transformation. After aging at 520 °C for 8 h (TMA1-SA520), the α phase and Ti2Ag intermetallic compounds precipitated, while the ωiso phase was completely dissolved, and the β → α + Ti2Ag phase transformation occurred. The phase formation reaction for this transformation can be expressed as: β-Ti (Mo, Ag) → α-Ti + Ti2Ag. According to thermodynamic calculations and the reported data [41–43], the ΔG of the reaction at 520 °C is approximately -28.6 kj∙mol-1. The negative ΔG value confirms that the β → α-Ti + Ti2Ag transformation is thermodynamically spontaneous, which is consistent with the experimental observation of complete ωiso dissolution and stable precipitation of α and Ti₂Ag phases. For the TMA5 alloy with higher Ag content, the ΔG of the above reaction decreases to -32.1 kJ·mol⁻¹ (520 °C), indicating that increased Ag content enhances the thermodynamic driving force for Ti₂Ag and α phase nucleation and growth, which explains the stronger diffraction intensity of α and Ti₂Ag phases in TMA5-SA520 (Fig. 2b) compared to TMA1-SA520. This study systematically calculated and revealed the Gibbs free energy regulation mechanism of Ag element on the formation of ωath and ωiso in Ti-15Mo alloy. After Ag dissolving in the β-Ti lattice, the ΔG of ωath formation changed from negative to positive, thermodynamically inhibiting its formation. Meanwhile, it reduced the ωiso dissolution temperature by 40 °C and accelerates the β → α + Ti₂Ag phase transformation, a mechanism that has not been reported in the Ti-Mo-Ag system.

It is worth noting that the elemental Ag peaks disappeared under these conditions. Previous studies have shown that the Ti-15Mo alloy undergoes ω phase dissolution at 560 °C, which was the threshold temperature for ω → α phase transformation [44]. The results indicated that the addition of Ag significantly reduced the ωiso dissolution temperature (~520 °C) and accelerated the transformation kinetics of the alloy. With increasing the aging temperature to 620 °C (TMA1-SA620), the diffraction intensities of Ti2Ag and elemental Ag phase were significantly weakened, while the diffraction peaks of the α phase were intensified, indicating that the microstructure was mainly dominated by α and β phases.

The phase transformation trends of TMA5 alloy under the same heat treatment conditions are compared and analyzed as shown in Fig. 2b. All of the TMA5 samples exhibited similar phase characteristics, only with different relative intensities. Compared with TMA1, the Ag content affects the phase kinetics. Higher Ag can alter the nucleation and growth of the Ti2Ag and α phase and promote the formation of the two phases.

Microstructure characteristics

Figure 3 shows the optical micrographs of TMAX alloy rods after solution treatment and different aging treatments. Under the same thermal conditions, both TMA1 and TMA5 alloys exhibited nearly identical microstructures and phase distribution.

The clusters of fine black particles were distributed along the β grain boundaries, forming interconnected networks on the cross-section. There were subtle differences in particle size and density due to change in Ag concentrations. Considering the similar phase evolution in TMA5 and TMA1, Fig. 4 presents the TEM images of the TMA5 alloy after heat treatment. Figure 4a shows spherical nanoparticles (with a diameter <30 nm) uniformly dispersed within the grains, and their presence at grain boundaries can be ignored. Diffraction pattern and EDS spot scanning confirmed the main β phase and Ag nanoparticles. Figure 4b shows spherical and ellipsoidal particles (<30 nm), among which the ellipsoidal particles were analyzed by diffraction as the ωiso phase, which is consistent with the XRD results (Fig. 2b). Compared with the larger ωiso phases (50-200 nm) in other β titanium alloys aged at 380 °C–420 °C, the ωiso phase in TMA5 may be formed earlier [45, 46]. After aging at 520 °C for 8 h, two different martensite microstructures were formed in the TMA5 alloy formed (α1 and α2 in Fig. 4c). The α1 martensite appeared as coarse, elongated platelets (500 nm–1 μm in length, approximately 250 nm in width), and was interconnected in a subcrystalline arrangement. while the α2 martensite presents as finer needle-like structures (<50 nm in width) densely distributed within these regions. It was confirmed by EDS line scanning, area scanning and diffraction analysis that the Ti2Ag precipitates (<160 nm in length, <10 nm in width) was parallel to each other on the residual β phase matrix. Figure 4d shows retained α₁ martensites dimensions, whereas the α₂ martensites coarsened into short rods (approximately 160 nm in width) with unchanged distribution or length. The coarsening of α₂ martensites is thermodynamically driven by the reduction of interfacial energy. The ΔG of α₂ coarsening reaction (fine α₂ → coarse α₂) is -6.8 kJ·mol⁻¹ at 620 °C, which promotes the migration of α₂ phase boundaries and the fusion of small α₂ needles into short rods. This is consistent with the increased α phase diffraction intensity in XRD (Fig. 2b).

The TMA1-S samples exhibited excellent comprehensive mechanical properties such as high strength (874 MPa), low elastic modulus (78 GPa) and considerable elongation (17.5%). Compared with the traditional β titanium alloys such as Ti-Nb, Ti-Zr and Ti-Ta systems, these properties are superior [47, 48]. After aging treatment at 420 °C, the UTS of the TMA1-SA420 samples was significantly increased to 1607 MPa. However, this increase in strength was accompanied by a higher elastic modulus (114 GPa) and a reduced elongation (3%), which was consistent with the embrittlement effect caused by the formation of nanoscale ωiso phase in β titanium alloys [35, 37, 39, 42, 49]. When the aging temperature was raised to 520 °C, the UTS of the TMA1-SA520 decreased by 500 MPa, while retaining a high UTS of 1101 MPa, an elongation (10.5%), and a medium elastic modulus of 96 GPa. Such mechanical characteristics meet the dual requirements of high strength and ductility for the applications of surgical implants. Further aging at 620 °C aging reduced strength to the level of the solution treated state, but the ductility increased by 24% while maintaining a low modulus of 90 GPa. The mechanical evolution trend of the TMA5 alloys was similar to that of TMA1. However, under the same thermal conditions, the addition of Ag improved the overall performance of the alloy.

As shown in Table 2, the TMA5-S alloy exhibited an enhanced UTS (936 MPa) and elongation (16%) while maintaining a low modulus (80 GPa). The UTS of the TMA5-SA420 sample reached a peak value of 1680 MPa, but its ductility was extremely poor due to the precipitation of a large number of fine nanoscales ωiso phase (<30 nm) within the β matrix. In contrast, the TMA5-SA520 sample demonstrated an excellent balance of strength and ductility (UTS = 1208 MPa, elongation=11.5%), which is possibility attributed to the synergistic existence of multiple micro-nano phases, including α1 coarse precipitates (500 nm–1 μm in length, <250 nm in width), α2 fine needles (500 nm–1 μm in length, <50 nm in width) and Ti2Ag particles (<160 nm in length, 10 nm in width). This study realizes the synergistic strengthening of α1, α2 and Ti2Ag phases in Ti-15Mo-Ag alloy. The α1 provides basic strength, α2 hinders dislocation movement, and Ti₂Ag further refines grains. Their synergy enables TMA5-SA520 alloy to maintain 11.5% elongation and 98 GPa low modulus at high strength of 1208 MPa. However, the UTS of TMA5-SA620 decreased by 22% to 939 MPa comparable to TMA5-S, confirming the key strengthening role of the Ti2Ag phase. These results indicate that the mechanical properties of the TMAX alloys are controlled by the synergistic effects of the phase’s type, dimensional characteristics, spatial distribution and volume fraction. Therefore, achieving the optimal performance of surgical implants requires precise regulation of these microstructural characteristics through precisely controlled heat treatment processes.

Electrochemical corrosion behavior

Figure 7 shows the potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) curves and open-circuit potential (OCP) curves of the TMAX alloy under the different heat treatments in Hank’s solution at 37 °C. The key electrochemical parameters are presented in Table 3. The stable potential at 30 min was defined as Eocp during the OCP measurements. As shown in Fig. 7a, all TMA1 samples with different phase compositions initially exhibited a positive potential shift before stabilizing. This behavior indicated that a protective passivation film was rapidly formed on the alloy surfaces, and then the film was gradually stabilized until the electrochemical equilibrium was reached. It was worth noting that, except for the TMA1-SA420 (−0.299 V), the Eocp value of the other heat-treated TMA1 alloys exceeded those of pure titanium (Cp-Ti), indicating that improved passive film stability and corrosion resistance from optimized heat treatment process. Specifically, the TMA1-SA520 (−0.220 V) and TMA1-SA620 (−0.233 V) samples showed significantly higher Eocp values compared to TMA1-S (−0.250 V) and TMA1-SA420, highlighting the critical influence of microstructure characteristics on passivation kinetics and corrosion resistance. These observations were consistent with the previous studies by Karasoglu et al. [50] and Zhang et al. [40], in which aging treatment reduced Eocp values of Ti6Al4V and Ti-13Nb-13Zr-13Ag alloys, further confirming that electrochemical behaviors are related to the microstructure.

It can be seen from the polarization behavior in Fig. 7b that the TMA1-SA420 alloy had the most active corrosion characteristics, with the lowest corrosion potential (Ecorr = -0.466 V) and highest corrosion current density (icorr =8.611 × 10-8A·cm-2). In contrast, the Ecorr values of the TMA1-SA520, TMA1-SA620 and TMA1-S were higher, indicating that the anodic dissolution and cathodic reactions were effectively inhibited. It was worth noting that the corrosion resistance of TMA1-SA520 (−0.383 V) and TMA1-SA620 (−0.394 V) was superior to that of Cp-Ti, and their icorr values were lower (1.546 × 10⁻8 A·cm-2 and 1.724 × 10⁻8 A·cm-2, respectively). Overall, the corrosion resistance ranking of PDP and OCP was as follows: TMA1-SA520 > TMA1-SA620 > TMA1-S > Cp-Ti > TMA1-SA420.

For the TMA5 alloy (Fig. 7c, d), the same OCP/ PDP behavior trends were observed, and a similar corrosion resistance was obtained: TMA5-SA520 > TMA5-SA620 > TMA5-S > Cp-Ti > TMA5-SA420. It is notable that both the TMA5-SA520 (-0.341 V) and TMA5-SA620 (-0.359 V) demonstrated superior performance to both Cp-Ti and TMA1. They respectively showed extremely low icorr values of 1.176 × 10-8 A·cm-2 and 1.615 × 10-8 A·cm-2. This improvement is related to the optimized β + α or β + α + Ti2Ag multiphase microstructures, which is consistent with established microstructure-property relationships in titanium alloys [51]. The mechanism was further supported by Sotniczuk et al. [52], who reported that β phase microstructures promoted rapid passivation through a protective oxide layer with good electrochemical properties, while the α phase and Ti2Ag precipitates further refine the passive film structure.

To further elucidate the corrosion kinetic mechanism, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted, with Nyquist and Bode diagrams presented in Fig. 8. All samples exhibited a single capacitive arc in Nyquist plots (without Warburg impedance). This feature directly indicates that the corrosion process is dominated by the charge transfer step at the electrode–electrolyte interface, without significant ion diffusion limitations, which is consistent with the passive behavior of titanium alloys in physiological environments [53].

For both TMA1 and TMA5 alloys, Nyquist plots revealed that the semicircle diameter (an indicator of charge transfer resistance, Rct) of TMA5-SA520 was much larger than that of TMA5-S, TMA5-SA420, TMA5-SA620 and Cp-Ti. Specifically, TMA5-SA520 had an Rct approximately twice that of Cp-Ti and over three times that of TMA5-SA420, demonstrating the strongest hindrance to charge transfer and the lowest corrosion tendency. The Bode plots further confirmed this conclusion. In the low-to-medium frequency range (10⁻2-1 Hz) reflecting passive film protection, TMA5-SA520 alloy exhibited the highest impedance value (|Z | ) (≈6×105 Ω·cm2 at 10⁻2 Hz, compared to 3×105 Ω·cm2 for Cp-Ti). In the high-frequency range (103-105 Hz), its phase angle peak exceeded 85°—a value closer to 90° (ideal capacitive behavior) than all other samples. A high phase angle indicates a dense, electrolyte-impermeable passive film that effectively isolates the alloy substrate from Hank’s solution [53].

The corrosion resistance of TMAX alloys is directly linked to their microstructural evolution. For TMA5-SA520, the β + α + Ti₂Ag multiphase structure (Fig. 4c) drives passive film optimization through two main mechanisms: (1) Ti₂Ag precipitates promote Ag⁺ enrichment in the passive film, which inhibits local dissolution and enhances film stability [53]; (2) the fine, dispersed α phase disrupts continuous corrosion channels in the β matrix, preventing localized electrolyte penetration. In contrast, TMAX-SA420 alloys (with abundant nano-sized isothermal ω phases) exhibit the poorest corrosion resistance. The lattice mismatch between ω and β phases leads to discontinuous passive films, resulting in reduced Rct and increased icorr [40].

These EIS results are consistent with PDP findings, confirming that aging at 520 °C enables TMAX alloys (especially TMA5-SA520) to achieve optimal corrosion resistance by precise multiphase regulation. This microstructural optimization not only meets the long-term corrosion stability requirements for biomedical implants but also complements the alloy’s excellent mechanical adaptability and biocompatibility, further supporting its potential as a surgical implant candidate.

In vitro cytocompatibility

As a promising biomedical material, non-cytotoxicity is essential for biomedical applications. Although Ti-15Mo alloy has been certified as a safe surgical implant material by international standards since 1999, the potential biological effects of Ag-doped TMA1 and TMA5 alloys required systematic evaluation. At the same culture time, the cell proliferation rates of the TMA5 alloys were significantly higher than those of the other alloys. Hemocompatibility

Since biological materials interact with blood for extended periods, they may damage red blood cells, potentially leading to blood clotting or localized thrombosis in patients. Thus, hemocompatibility become a key criterion for evaluating biomaterials, such as dental implants, prosthetic alloys and denture bases. Although Ti-15Mo alloy has been clinically proven as a blood-compatible implant material through decades of surgical applications, hemocompatibility validation remains essential for the novel TMA5 alloy with elevated Ag content.

As shown in Fig. 10, the TMA5 specimens uniformly exhibited superior hemocompatibility characteristics. The reference materials showed hemolysis rates of 0.55% for pure titanium and 0.8% for TC4 alloy. All heat-treated TMA5 samples had hemolysis rates below 0.7%, which was much lower than the 2% threshold for nonhemolytic materials stipulated in ASTM F756-2017. The results indicate that the TMA5 alloy achieves good hemocompatibility comparable to commercial TC4 alloy, meeting the clinical requirements of blood contact implantation applications.

Conclusions

The TMA1 and TMA5 alloys rods were prepared by systematic optimization of smelting, forging and rolling processes. The solid solution and aging treatments were carried out on the samples, and the microstructural evolution, phase composition, mechanical properties, corrosion behavior and biocompatibility of them were investigated. The obtained main conclusions are as follows:

The addition of Ag effectively inhibited the formation of the ωath phase during quenching and reduced the dissolution temperature of the ωiso by 40 °C compared to conventional Ti-15Mo alloys, accelerating the phase transformation from β to α and Ti2Ag. The regulation mechanism provided a new thermodynamic theoretical basis for ω phase inhibition in Ti-Mo alloys.

Aging at 520 °C optimized the microstructural morphology. Through the synergistic effect of heterogeneous microstructures with the α phase platelets, nanoscale Ti2Ag precipitates and residual phase, the balanced mechanical properties (UTS = 1208 MPa, A% = 11.5%, E = 96 GPa) were obtained. The multiphase regulation provided a promotable technical path for performance optimization of titanium alloys for surgical implants.
