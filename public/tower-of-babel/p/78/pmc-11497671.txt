Association between pancreatic adenocarcinoma risk and concentration of organochlorine pesticides in adipose tissue and urine: A targeted‐screening analysis case‐control study (PESTIPAC)
Authors: Mathias Brugel, Sidonie Callon, Claire Carlier, Koceila Lamine Amroun, Damien Botsen, Reza Kianmanesh, Marine Perrier, Tullio Piardi, Yohann Renard, Rami Rhaiem, Souleiman El Balkhi, Olivier Bouché
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKnowledge about environmental pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PA) risk factors, including pesticide exposure, remains limited. Organochlorine (OC) accumulates in adipose tissue and can help reflect long‐term exposure.Patients and MethodsAge and body mass index (BMI) of patients with PA were matched with those undergoing a surgery for a benign disease on age and BMI (1:1). Targeted analyses screened 345 pesticides and metabolites, including 29 OC, in adipose tissue and urine samples. The primary aim was to investigate the association between organochlorine concentrations in visceral fat or urine, and PA. Adjusted conditional logistic regressions were carried out accounting for multiple testing.ResultsTrans‐nonachlor (odds ratio [OR] = 1.325, 95% confidence interval [CI] [1.108–1.586]), cis‐nonachlor (OR = 15.433, 95% CI [2.733–87.136]), Mirex (OR = 2.853, 95% CI [1.213–6.713]) and 4,4 DDE (OR = 1.019, 95% CI [1.005–1.034]) in fat and a greater number of positive samples (OR = 1.758 95% CI [1.11–2.997]) were significantly associated with higher odds of PA. In contrast, as awaited, urine samples did not yield any statistically significant associations for all tested pesticides.ConclusionSome OCs were associated with higher odds of PA. The underlying mechanisms of pancreatic aggression need to be investigated to refine these findings.Trial RegistrationClinicaltrials.gov NCT04429490.Keywords: adipose tissue, environmental exposure, organochlorine pesticides, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, pancreatic neoplasms

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PA) is the fourth cause of cancer‐related death in Western Europe, with an all‐stage 5‐year survival rate of around 10%. 1 , 2 Over the last decades, incidence rates of PA in Europe have increased dramatically, especially in France. 3 , 4 , 5 This incidence rate increase is not fully explained by known risk factors. 3 , 6 Environmental risk factors are one of the hypotheses formulated to explain the phenomenon. 7

Plant protection products, also called pesticides, are chemical substances routinely used to protect crops against pests. 8 They mainly include insecticides, herbicides, and fungicides, among others. A total of 545 substances have been authorised in Europe since 2000, and 96 have been banned since then. 9 Some of these substances have been identified as potential risk factors for human cancers, especially for haematologic malignancies. 10 Several studies indicate that contamination by pesticides is ubiquitous in the general population, waters and soils. 11 Moreover, they can persist for a long time in the environment. 12

Organochlorine compounds are a category of thousands of lipophilic, metabolism‐resistant chemical substances that can be stored all along life in adipose tissue. 13 Their residues can be measured and are designated as persistent organic pollutants (POP). Several studies have investigated the association between POP and pancreatic cancer risk. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 However, these studies have limitations such as a small number of studied chemical substances or the absence of multiple statistical testing adjustments. 19 Only one study used the concentration of organochlorine in adipose tissue as a surrogate for exposure. 15

The PESTIPAC study aimed to determine whether organochlorine levels in adipose tissue and urine were associated with the risk of PA and their impact on survival.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and participants

We conducted a single‐centre, prospective, matched, case‐control, targeted‐metabolomic study followed by a cohort study of the cases.

Cases were adult patients with histologically proven exocrine PA, regardless of disease stage or previously received treatments, who had to undergo carcinologic or non‐carcinologic surgery (by laparotomy or laparoscopy) allowing visceral fat sampling. Cases were excluded if the final histopathologic diagnosis was a neuroendocrine pancreatic tumour or an intra‐ductal papillary mucinous neoplasia‐based PA (different oncogenesis mechanism).

Controls were adult patients undergoing scheduled abdominal surgery (by laparotomy or laparoscopy) for a benign pathology, allowing visceral fat sampling, without lengthening the operating time. A recent abdominal‐pelvic imaging (less than 6 months old) read by a radiologist and/or a gastroenterologist was required to exclude a potential PA. A history of basal cell carcinoma, localised cancer of the cervix, or malignant tumour without recurrence in the last 5 years was tolerated.

Cases and controls were selected on the same operating list between 1 September 2020 and 31 January 2022 and were excluded when fat were not sampled or insufficient. Cases and controls were matched on age (±2 years) and on body mass index (BMI) (normal [<25 kg/m2], overweight [25–30 kg/m2], obese grade 1 [30–35 kg/m2], obese grade 2 [35–40 kg/m2], obese grade 3 [>40 kg/m2]). The first controls were included after a few inclusions.

Data collection

Patients underwent initial data collection on the day before surgery, including a standardised exposure questionnaire (AGRICAN study questionnaire). 20 This questionnaire is available in Figure S1 and reviewed potential situations in which the patient could have been exposed to organochlorines. Known PA risk factors and potential confounders were also collected (tobacco consumption, diabetes, current and history of obesity, waist circumference, weight loss, acute and chronic pancreatitis). Data about PA were collected for cases: weight loss compared to baseline weight and time to weight loss, localisation, size and vascular invasion of the primary tumour, disease stage and anti‐neoplastic treatment before surgery.

A fasting blood sample (albumin, HbA1c, total cholesterol, HDL, triglycerides, LDL calculation) was collected on the day before surgery. The first morning urine was sampled (5 mL minimum) on the day of the surgery. Fat was sampled in one intra‐abdominal location during the surgery without lengthening the operating time (10 g minimum). Surgeons were asked to sample visceral or omental fat. 21 All samples (fat and urine) were frozen as soon as possible after collection and were sent in one batch to the laboratory which centralised the analyses.

Chemical analysis

A total of 345 pesticides and metabolites were screened for and determined in adipose tissue (188) and urine (157) using gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (GC/MS‐MS) or liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (LC/MS‐MS). The list of the targeted substances and their respective limit of detection is available in Table S1A,B. No polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) or dioxins were measured in this study. Pesticides and organochlorine quantification was centralised and carried out at the Limoges University Hospital. Results were expressed as ng/g of tissue and ng/mL of urine.

Urinary samples were extracted using acetonitrile and QuEChERS salt before injection into the LC/MS‐MS system or the GC/MS‐MS system as detailed in Supporting Information S1: Methods. For fat tissues, 4 mL of a solution of buffered subtilisine was added to 1 g of tissue, and heated at 55°C for 1 h. One g of QuEChERS Mix 1 was then added to the digested tissue before centrifugation at 3000 g. The supernant was then treated as described for urine. All detected molecules were quantified using matrix matched calibration curves. All the GC/MS‐MS and LC/MS‐MS settings are detailed in Supporting Information S1: Methods.

Aims and objectives

The aim of the study was to investigate the association between organochlorine concentration in visceral fat or urine, and PA.

The secondary aims were (i) to investigate the association between other pesticide concentrations in fat or urine and PA and (ii) to determine whether organochlorine concentration was associated with a decreased overall survival (OS) and RFS of patients with resected PA.

Statistical analysis

The number of patients needed for the study was performed for the primary endpoint, using the NQuery software (version 4.0, Statsols, 2019), with the following assumptions: the presence of organochlorines in 50% and 90% of controls and cases, respectively. The alpha risk was set to 0.05 with a power of 0.9. For a two‐tailed test, the required number of patients was estimated to be 26 per group.

The exposure was considered using two distinct ways: the quantitative values of each substance and the detection of each substance for each patient (binary value, detected and non‐detected).

Quantitative variables were summarised using median and interquartile range and quantitative variables using frequency. Comparisons between cases and controls were performed by iterative conditional univariate and multivariate analysis logistic regression, for each substance. All the matching factors were also accounted for in the adjustment of the logistic regression, as good practice. Confounding and matching factors were chosen a priori based on literature review and expertise, including age, BMI and triglycerides. 15 , 22 For urine samples, triglycerides were not implemented in the model, and replaced by the concentration of creatinine in urine, to adjust for the renal capacity to excrete POP. To obtain robust estimates, the analysis was carried out when the substance was detected in ≥10 patients, regardless of their original group for the quantitative analysis; and more ≥10 detections and less than the total number of included patients (<52) for the qualitative exposure. A corrected p‐value using a false discovery rate was set for each group of analysis to limit type 1 error. The significance level for all these analyses was set at 0.05. Results were expressed as odds ratio (OR) and their 95% confidence interval (CI) in the text and the tables.

Survival analysis was performed only in case patients. Cases were split into two equal groups using the median of the sum of the number of detections per patient. The RFS and OS starting point is the date of the surgery, as some patients may have undergone neoadjuvant treatments. Patients who were lost to follow‐up were censored. The follow‐up ended on the first of March 2023. The RFS and OS were plotted using the Kaplan‐Meier method and compared by a log‐rank test.

Ethical approval

Both cases and controls were informed about the objectives and the modalities of the study by their oncologist or surgeon, also using the help of an information note. Participation in the study did not affect the cancer management or the surgery. Informed written consent was obtained from each enroled patient in the trial. The PESTIPAC study was approved by an independent ethics committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes, 2019, North West) and was registered in Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04429490) and eudraCT (2020‐A00339‐30). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESULTS

Population characteristics

A total of 26 cases matched with 26 controls were included in the study. As shown in Table 1, baseline characteristics were well‐balanced, including matching criteria. A history of diabetes was present in 38.5% (N = 10) and 19.2% (N = 5) of the cases and controls, respectively. Median weight change on the day of the surgery was 0 kg (IQR: −2; 1) compared to the usual weight. Among the 26 cases, 23 (88.5%) were either resectable or borderline (16 and 7, respectively), and 15 patients received chemotherapy before sampling. No difference was identified between the two groups in terms of biological results (Table S2). All subjects completed the exposure questionnaire. No pronounced difference was observed between the two groups in terms of pesticide exposure or lifestyle factors (Table S3). Fifteen subjects reported having worked in agriculture, and eight had used pesticides before inclusion.

Pesticides analysis

All the pesticides listed in Table S1A,B with a star were detected at least once in the fat or urine samples. Among them, nine were found in more than half of these samples. Every included patient had at least one pesticide detected in fat and urine. The median number of pesticides detected per patient was three (IQR: 1–52). Some of them were ubiquitous in fat, that is, detected in all samples: Dichlorodiphényldichloroéthylène, β‐Hexachlorocyclohexane, Cis‐Heptachlor‐exo‐epoxide, Hexachlorobenzene, and Trans‐nonachlor.

Risk of PA

After adjusting for age, BMI, and triglycerides, only trans‐nonachlor (OR = 1.316, 95% CI [1.103–1.571], adjusted p‐value = 0.011), cis‐nonachlor (OR = 14.409, 95% CI [2.626–79.078], adjusted p‐value = 0.011), Mirex (OR = 2.788, 95% CI [1.198–6.493], adjusted p‐value = 0.047) and 4,4 DDE (OR = 1.019, 95% CI [1.004–1.034], adjusted p‐value = 0.037) remained statistically significant in fat samples (Table 2). This association was also found to be statistically significant with a binary variable for cis‐nonachlor and Mirex (Table 3).

Survival analysis

Survival analyses were performed on 23 cases for which survival data were available and split into two groups (less or equal and over 11 substances detected) (Figure 1). The characteristics of the two groups were quite balanced, except for the stage of the disease, with more advanced disease in the over 11 pesticides groups (Table S4). Moreover, the number of individuals in this group remained low (8 cases).

Comparison of the overall (a) and progression‐free (b) survival using Kaplan Meier survival curves and log‐rank tests depending on the count of pesticides detected per individual.

The median follow‐up lasted 18.5 months (IQR: 10.4–19.4). Median RFS was 19 months from surgery. The median OS was not reached. Kaplan‐Meier curves and log‐rank tests did not identify a statistically significant difference in terms of OS or RFS for cases with a higher number of pesticides detected (>11).

DISCUSSION

In this matched case‐control study, we observed ubiquitous contamination by organochlorine substances in both cases and controls. We observed an association between the risk of PA and four organochlorine compounds detected in fat, namely trans‐nonachlor, cis‐nonachlor, 4,4 DDE, and Mirex, after adjustment for age, BMI and triglycerides and accounting for multiple statistical testing. No survival differences could be identified between the two groups.

A better understanding of the environmental risk factors of PA has been identified as an important research gap. Several environmental risk factors have been pointed out in previously published articles, such as chlorinated solvents, benzene, or heavy metals. 23 , 24 The environment surrounding the general population is complex and several factors may play a role in the incidence dynamic that has been observed or projected. 6 , 25

Persistent organochlorine pollutants are lipophilic and bioaccumulative chemicals. The general population is widely exposed to them, through food consumption, water drinking and even air breathing. 26 In France, they have been widely used as pesticides, for decades, without restriction. 9 In our study, we showed a ubiquitous presence of these POPs in our study sample, suggesting a past and maybe still ongoing exposure. We should highlight that this study was conducted in a region of France that has a lot of vineyards. This study is in line with previous findings on the topic. 11

Moreover, in our study, we measured a large panel of pesticides (more than 300) including POP using very sensitive techniques (i.e. GC‐MS/MS and LC‐MS/MS) in an appropriate biological matrix (fat) to reflect very long term exposure. We should highlight that other biological matrices such as fasting serum are appropriate for organochlorine exposure assessment. Also, the use of chemotherapy before sampling may influence organochlorine measurement. However, we did not identify a significant difference in organochlorine levels in patients receiving chemotherapy compared with those who underwent upfront surgery (Table S5). However, we did not identify the source of the exposure to these POPs using the exposure questionnaire. Specific studies need to address this question, including the level and the length of persistence of these compounds in water, soil, and food. The concentration of other pesticides and metabolites in urine was rather inconclusive due to its poor hydrophilic properties.

Previous studies have identified an increased risk of cancer associated with exposure to organochlorine compounds. 27 , 28 Porta et al. studied this association for PA in the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition cohort. 18 This study measured the plasma level of 22 compounds using GC/MS and observed a significantly increased risk of PA in the upper quartile of trans‐nonachlor levels. Although they considered the level of total lipids in plasma, we should note that this study did not account for multiple statistical tests and chose a biological matrix that does not reflect lifetime exposure. On the contrary, Hardell et al. sampled fat from cases and controls to study the occurrence of PA and the survival using unconditional logistic regression with quantitative exposure. 15 Moreover, in their study, BMI may have influenced the results as it was lower for PA cases. In our study, we identified an increased risk of PA regarding trans‐nonachlor, cis‐nonachlor, 4,4 DDE, and Mirex after matching on age and BMI, adjusting on triglycerides, and accounting for multiple testing. A trend of risk existed for Hexachlorobenzene and 2,4 DDT without reaching statistical significance. We also indirectly tested for multiple exposures by considering the total number of detected OC, observing consistent significant associations. Trans‐nonachlor was not included in this analysis as it was detected in all samples. Chlordane, including cis and trans‐nonachlor, are frequently studied as a whole, and not distinctively. The association between trans‐nonachlor and PA was already identified previously by Porta et al, in the above‐mentioned case‐cohort study. 18 However, this same study did not identify an association with 2,4 DDE. On the contrary, the association between cis‐nonachlor and Mirex (also called perchlordecone) was not previously published. Perchlordecone is a precursor of chlordecone, a well‐known substance involved in several cancers, especially in the French West Indies. 29 , 30 This substance has been banned internationally since the 2000s. Pesticide levels in urine were inconclusive, and less reliable to assess long‐term exposure, as they concerned only hydrophilic compounds. Chlordecone was analysed in urine, without being identified.

This risk increase may be explained with a direct and indirect hypothesis. First, organochlorine is known to directly damage genes. 19 Second, organochlorine compounds can be considered as potential endocrine disruptors, indirectly damaging the pancreas through diabetes. 31 It has been previously shown that they can increase blood sugar levels. 31 Diabetes is a well‐known PA risk factor. 32 In our study, 10 cases (38%) and 5 controls (19%) had a history of diabetes. Those two hypotheses could also be associated together. We cannot exclude that the association with other risk factors could be synergetic and multiply the risk. Dissecting the molecular pathways involved in PA oncogenesis through pesticides is a clear research gap.

No survival difference was observed in this study. The analyses were carried out on a limited number of patients and can be considered as underpowered compared to Hardell et al. 15 We should stress that this objective was not the main focus of this study. In their study, Hardell et al, with a similar sample size (N = 59) observed a decreased survival of patients with a high number of PCBs detected. In our study, most of the cases were diagnosed at earlier disease stages. We should keep in mind that advanced PA is associated with cachexia, loss in adipose tissue, decreased survival, and may be associated with a higher OC concentration in fat. 28 The rationale for the contribution of environmental exposure to survival differences remains weak and not well supported. Larger studies assessing survival in an adjusted model are needed to better understand the exact relationship between organochlorine compounds and the survival of patients diagnosed with PA.

This study has some limitations. First, the retrospective methodology to assess the exposure remains imperfect and could be biased as bioaccumulation is still prone to partial biodegradation. However, a longitudinal‐biological follow‐up over several decades does not seem reasonable for a large cohort of individuals. Moreover, the choice of fat as a biological matrix for measuring lifetime exposure has been previously assessed. Second, the sample size is limited. To overcome the limitation of a potentially weak association, we matched cases and controls on potential confounding factors: age (length of exposure since birth), and BMI (bioaccumulation capacity). Additionally, we adjusted our analysis on one additional biological factor, as previously pointed out: triglycerides. 22 , 33 Results remain unchanged using triglycerides, total cholesterol, or the sum of both features. We also used the false discovery rate to limit the risk of false positive discoveries. We cannot exclude that the initial power estimate calculation, based on Hardell et al.’s research, could have been misleading by different exposure profile and pollutant identification. We should point out that this study is the only one before ours measuring organochlorines in the fat of patients with PA and controls. Third, the temporality between the exposure and the outcome is not assessed in a retrospective setting. Pesticide concentration in fat was measured after the occurrence of the outcome. Therefore, we are not allowed to conclude on the causality. Nevertheless, the published corpus of evidence seems robust enough to assess the risk of PA regarding organochlorine as plausible.

This study shows the ubiquitous contamination of the general population by organochlorine compounds and the association with an increased risk of PA regarding these chemical substances, even decades after their banishment. Pesticide residues, even forbidden ones, should not be ignored in the search for new PA risk factors.
