Make it fun but keep it simple: EEG reveals the impact of easy yet engaging games for stroke rehabilitation
Authors: Romain Cardis, Claire Lugrin, Skander Mensi, Andrea Serino, Anne-Charlotte Farasse, Jean-Michel Pignat, Robert Leeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke often leads to motor disabilities, and effective rehabilitation is crucial for restoring limb function. Gamified rehabilitation programs have emerged to increase stroke survivor motivation through engaging environments and rewards. However, it is uncertain whether stroke survivors respond to video game environments and challenges in the same way as healthy individuals do, given the potential impact of stroke on mood and intrinsic motivation.MethodsEEG data were collected from stroke survivors and healthy controls under multiple conditions. Initially, a cognitively activated state was identified via an N-back task. The participants subsequently engaged with an upper limb rehabilitation game featuring an immersive 3D environment. The brain activity patterns during this engaging game were then compared to those during a version of the same task without a 3D, gamified environment. Finally, responses to a more challenging iteration of the engaging game were assessed.ResultsWhile healthy controls showed brain activity indicative of effort and attention in N-back and subsequent games, stroke survivors displayed similar brain activation patterns only when playing the engaging version of the game. Notably, increasing the game difficulty increased attentional states in controls but not in stroke survivors.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that an engagement mental state, as indexed by EEG markers, is more difficult to achieve in stroke survivors during repetitive tasks than in healthy controls. However, engaging in video game environments can significantly increase stroke survivors’ motivation and participation, provided that the difficulty level is appropriately adjusted. We believe this provides evidence that video game-based rehabilitation is a promising approach to promote stroke survivor engagement through enhanced motivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01956-1.Keywords: Stroke, EEG, Mental state, Video game, Engagement, Rehabilitation

Background

Stroke is a major contributor to motor, sensory, cognitive, and mental disabilities [12, 38], leading to diminished functioning in daily life activities; loss of health-related quality of life, and decreased life expectancy [9, 20]. Precision rehabilitation, defined as tailoring therapy to stroke survivors through frequent, intensive and personalized interventions over an extended duration, is critical for optimizing functional recovery [7, 13, 16]. Stroke survivors' motivation is required to improve their engagement in rehabilitation programs [32, 52]. Moreover, task repetition, a core component of many rehabilitative interventions [15, 42, 55], can become monotonous and tedious, potentially contributing to poor adherence, with up to 50% of stroke survivors discontinuing their exercise programs within the first year [25].

Serious games as digital neurotherapeutics have demonstrated therapeutic benefits in motor and cognitive rehabilitation [1, 28, 31]. Additionally, they improve therapy accessibility, optimize resource utilization, reduce costs, and enable personalized treatment through adaptable task settings, real-time feedback, and outcome quantification [47]. Moreover, their enriched environments are considered more enjoyable, engaging, and motivational, thereby facilitating participation and adherence to high rates of task repetition [29, 39].

The impact of serious games on stroke survivor participation aligns with the characteristics of the motivation concept. Motivation is defined as a psychological state that “moves” an individual to impel a particular behavior toward a goal [11, 26, 58]. It is shaped by internal factors, such as self-esteem, autonomy, goal-setting, meaning and pleasure, and by external factors including supportive therapists and environments that foster learning, interaction, validation, and recognition [18]. Serious games might influence both types of factors.

Motivation, being a subjective phenomenon that cannot be directly quantified outside the individual’s personal experience, is indirectly assessed through engagement, which serves as a measurable proxy for motivational states. The distinction between motivation and engagement remains debated, but there is partial consensus that engagement constitutes the externally observable process of an individual's internal motivational state [36, 48].

Engagement is a multidimensional construct encompassing cognitive (e.g., attention, working memory, and executive skills), behavioral (e.g., effort), and emotional (e.g., interest and curiosity) components, which collectively establish the framework for a flow state [23, 40]. Two other mental states are closely related to motivation and engagement. The cognitive workload refers to the amount of brain resources required for task completion and includes mental processes such as attention, working memory, and effort [6]. In this context, stroke survivors often suffer from cognitive fatigue, i.e., a subjective feeling of tiredness or exhaustion during the execution of a cognitive task that is disproportionate to recent activity levels [24]. Cognitive overload and fatigue cause task disengagement and undermine motivation. Therefore, fatigue and task difficulty should be taken into account in the design of serious games, and a novel way to measure them early in gameplay would be useful, as an excess in workload could already induce fatigue before it would be reflected in terms of gaming performance.

Both engagement and cognitive workload are potentially reflected in electrophysiological brain activity as measured by electroencephalography (EEG). EEG frequency bandwidths such as the beta (13–22 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), and theta (4–8 Hz) bands, capture different mental states and thus may be indicative of changes in engagement and cognitive workload [45]. The theta band, particularly in the frontal region, serves as a pertinent biomarker that increases with increasing cognitive workload. A decrease in alpha power and increase in beta power have also been linked to cognitive load, albeit with less consistent patterns [6]. Composite electrophysiological indices of task engagement have also been developed. One example is computed as the ratio of beta power to the sum of alpha and theta power [14, 45]. This index has been notably applied to assess engagement in video games [37]. Although electrophysiological measures of workload and engagement are assessed by the same frequency bandwidths, their outcome is not linear, as only workload increases linearly with task difficulty [2].

In this study we hypothesized that a gamified rehabilitation task, compared with a version of the same task without any gamified or engaging elements, enhances motivation in stroke survivors and that this effect might depend on task difficulty. Although investigating specific EEG markers in a stroke survivor population can be tempting, this can lead to biased results if not registered in advance [4]. Moreover, this restricts the results and potentially hides novel phenomena. Therefore, in this study we took a more explorative approach and investigated theta, alpha and beta power as EEG indices of the mental state of stroke survivors and young controls, in which motivation and engagement are supposedly easily achieved [53]. This non-age matched control group prevents meaningful direct comparison in EEG but serves as a valuable positive control of what mental state can be achieved in our conditions and gives an idea of the task difficulty compared to our post-stroke group. Specifically, we tested whether stroke survivor motivation would be reflected and measurable in the modulation of induced EEG state in different conditions and similarly to our control population of healthy participants. To this end, we divided our study into three questions.

First, can we identify a cognitively activated state that would correlate with perceived workload in our two populations (healthy and post-stroke)? For that purpose, we used a classic cognitive task, the N-back task. This task is known to induce a measurable workload in the subjects and can represent an efficient positive control for our study [44, 50]. Our results indicate that our method can indeed isolate a clear EEG state with increased theta and decreased alpha during the N-back task, with magnitudes correlated with the perceived workload, at least in the control population.

Second, can we capture the attention and motivation of individuals recovering from stroke while playing a gamified rehabilitation task? We investigated the EEG responses of the post-stroke group and our control group while using the previously identified state as a reference for interpretation. Hence, we recorded EEG data while the participants played a visually engaging rehabilitation game and, as a control condition, a less engaging, non-gamified version of the same task. Our results indicated that stroke survivors exhibited brain activation patterns, specifically increases in theta power and decreases in alpha power, indicative of attention, engagement and effort, only in the game displaying an engaging environment.

Third, how do stroke survivors respond to an increase in game difficulty? To answer this question, we increased the difficulty of the engaging game by adding obstacles. While healthy controls showed further increases in theta and decreases in alpha power, reflecting increased effort, stroke survivors did not display this change, suggesting a differential ability of their mental states to adapt to increased challenge during the task.

Methods

Subjects

We enrolled 14 participants from our convalescent stroke follow-up cohort in the study over a 1.5-month period. The inclusion criterion was that the participants had experienced a stroke a minimum of 8 months prior. The exclusion criteria were (1) inability to perform the required tasks due to cognitive or sensory‒motor impairment; (2) the presence of other neurological, brain, or psychiatric disorders; and (3) a history of screen-induced epilepsy to mitigate risk during game sessions. The mean age was 49.6 ± 10.2 years, with 57% being biological females and 43% being biological males. Among these participants, 12 were right-handed, 1 was left-handed, and 1 exhibited ambiguity or dual-handedness. Three participants were excluded during the study: two due to dropout and one because of unusable recorded EEG data in one session. Among the participants, seven experienced a stroke in the left hemisphere, and seven experienced a stroke in the right hemisphere. The mean time between inclusion and stroke incidence was 34.3 ± 29.4 months, ranging from 8 to 86 months, with a median of 16 months.

All post-stroke participants underwent multiple neuropsychological assessments during the inclusion process. Due to substantial heterogeneity among the participant’s neuropsychological symptoms, the assessment battery was not standardized across all participants but was instead individually tailored to each one. The administered tests comprised measures of verbal episodic memory, working memory and resistance to interference, attention (selective, divided, and sustained), and executive functions, complemented in some cases by clinical assessment in the absence of formal psychometric testing (details in supplementary Table 1). Although neurological presentations differed across individuals, symptoms were generally mild, ranging from no detectable impairments to mild hemiparesis, mild ataxia, or mild hypoesthesia. Cognitive profiles likewise showed heterogeneous but predominantly mild to moderate symptomatology, spanning from intact cognitive functioning to moderate attention deficits, mild memory impairments, dysexecutive features, and fatigue (Table 1). Overall, despite this interindividual variability, all participants exhibited only mild or no motor impairments and retained sufficient cognitive capacity to successfully play in the game.

Included post-stroke participants clinical information

The control group consisted of 25 healthy participants who were recruited from employees and university students via e-mail. The average age was 29.6 ± 5.7 years, 25% were female and 75% were male, and four participants were left-handed. No specific psychological examinations were made on the control group, and every participant could understand and perform the protocols.

The experiment was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. The subjects freely accepted to participate in the study and provided a written consent after being informed of the procedures. The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Canton of Vaud, Switzerland (study number PB_2017-00295).

Experimental protocol

The experiment for both groups of participants consisted of two wrist motion rehabilitation games (called Flying car game and Moving ball in this study), and an N-back protocol played at different levels of difficulty and all presented in more detail in the next sections.

The experiment was divided into two days containing several sessions of each activity (Fig. 1A). On the first day, the recordings started with a resting session with eyes open for 2 min to establish a baseline. Then, they played two sessions of the wrist motion tasks with their right hand, Flying car game (Fig. 1B) and Moving ball (Fig. 1C) at each difficulty level (level 1 and level 2) in a randomized order. Within the sessions, each game block lasted more or less 2 min with a short break in between them during which the participant filled an on-screen short version of the NASA-TLX questionnaire for perceived workload measure (see rating scale part). On the second day (Fig. 1A), the recordings started with the N-back session (Fig. 1A), followed by two shorter sessions of the two wrist motion games (see Fig. 1A). Again, each 2 min blocks were interspersed by short breaks and workload questionnaires. The order of the two experimental days was also chosen in a randomized manner and thus not the same for all participants.

The subjective perceived workload was estimated by using an on-screen adapted version of the NASA-TLX questionnaire [21] in which we asked 6 workload related questions that could be answered by placing a cursor on a visual analog scale (horizontal line) from very low to very high. The questions were as follows:

The final TLX value was then obtained by averaging the answers (after inverting the answer of question 4 on performance). To obtain a TLX value per subject and task, we averaged the answers of the blocks of the same task and level by subject.

Flying car game

The Flying car game is developed by MindMaze Therapeutics Holding SA (Geneva, Switzerland) and its affiliates as part of the MindMotion GO suite for stroke motor rehabilitation (Figs. 1B, 3A). This game was used because of its engaging context with 3D colorful graphics, clear score indications and feedback sounds. In this game, the participants control the height position of a flying car with their right hand. The wrist rests on an elevated platform, and the hand is held flat above a sensor. The controls use wrist movements upward and downward (extension and flexion), whereas the screen automatically scrolls from right to left, moving the car forward. The aim is to catch coins positioned at different heights in the air to increase the total score. At level 2, the task is complexified by the addition of cones that represent obstacles to avoid them (Fig. 5A). Hitting a cone results in the loss of 3 coins. The hand movements are detected via a Leap Motion sensor (UltraLeap, San Francisco, US). The range of achievable up-down movement is measured and used to calibrate the inputs for each user, to assure full playability even for participants with mild right-hand disability. All the movement inputs for the game are recorded together with the EEG. Each game block lasted for approximately 2 min, followed by a NASA-TLX questionnaire.

Moving ball game

The Moving ball game was designed in Python (3.4.10) using Pygame (1.9.3) and uses exactly the same calibrated hand movements inputs as for the Flying car game, detected again by the same Leap Motion sensor. In this game, the participants controlled the height of a blue dot within a vertical rectangle divided into 5 areas (Figs. 1C, 3D). The aim is to reach the green area that changes location every 5 s, between bottom, middle and top. The dynamics of the area to reach were adapted to reproduce the movements required to control the Flying car game. Therefore, the Moving ball game was a “clone” of the Flying car game in terms of gameplay, but without the engaging environment and gamified features. Indeed, in the Moving ball game, there was only a plain black background, no indication of score and no sound feedback. As for the Flying car game, all the hand inputs were recorded alongside the EEG. Each game block lasted for approximately 2 min, followed by a NASA-TLX questionnaire.

N-back

The N-back cognitive protocol is a well-known task in the literature to measure workload. The task was implemented in Python (3.4.10) using Pygame (1.9.3). The subjects were presented with a series of numbers and had to indicate if the current number was the same as the previous Nth one. If N = 1, it means that the subjects had to hit the target button (left arrow key) if the number was the same as the one before, and the non-target button (right arrow key) if the number was not the same as the one before. Each trial lasted for 2.6 s, with a number displayed in white font on a black background for 600 ms, followed by 2 s of a centered white cross during which the answer was expected. The participant performed 3 blocks at N = 1 and 3 at N = 2 (each ~ 1 min). A total of 31 stimuli were presented per block with 10 of them being targets and 20 non-targets, followed by the NASA-TLX questionnaire.

EEG acquisition

During all the tasks (games and protocol) the EEG was recorded with a TMSI Porti7 device with a 30 electrodes EEG gel head cap (Twente Medical Systems International B.V., The Netherlands) with a sampling rate of 2000 Hz. The Porti7 device implements two hardware filters, a 1st order low pass filter with -3 dB cutoff frequency set at 4.8 kHz and a low pass digital sinc3 FIR filter with cutoff frequency at 540 Hz. The electrode positions were, according to the international 10–20 system on locations: Fp1, Fpz, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, FC5, FC1, FC2, FC6, T7/T3, C3, Cz, C4, T8/T4, CP5, CP1, CP2, CP6, P7/T5, P3, Pz, P4, P8/T6, POz, O1, Oz, O2.

EEG processing

EEG data was offline processed with a custom pipeline in Python using mostly the MNE library (v1.6.0) [27]. First, the bad channels were detected and interpolated if needed (we typically had a maximum of 1–2 bad channels in only a few recordings). A bandpass filter (Butterworth order 2, 1–40 Hz, zero-phase) was applied. Eye blinks artifacts and muscular components were removed using independent component analysis (ICA). Finally, we used current source density (CSD) re-referencing to clean and refine the spatial resolution.

From start to finish of each block of activity, relative band power features were extracted using Hilbert envelope on filtered signals within specific bands (Butterworth order 2, zero-phase) at frequencies: theta (3–7 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz) and beta (14–29 Hz). Each band power was then transformed to its relative value by dividing it by the total power (3–29 Hz). The average of the relative power was computed per task block and then averaged for all of them to finally obtain one value of band power per subject, task and electrode. Of note, the EEG topographic maps of stroke survivors were not standardized to single side disability but kept in their original orientation to prevent potential masking of mental state features based on hemispheric asymmetry.

Statistics

The EEG state detected with correlation to NASA-TLX was done by using Pearson correlation at each frequency band value and each electrode with the corresponding NASA-TLX of the current task (both z-score normalized within subjects) to obtain a set of rho and p-values. The alpha threshold for p-value significance was calculated with Bonferroni correction (32 electrodes, 3 frequency bands and 2 groups for a total of 192 correlation tests). The EEG spatial power comparison statistics were done using python MNE (v1.6.0) [27]. Specifically, we used permutation cluster tests based on t-values obtained with paired t-tests for individual comparison between tasks or between task and rest [35]. Indeed, for EEG analysis specifically, we only used paired statistics and no direct comparison between groups, as parameters such as age were not matching and would prevent drawing useful conclusions. The threshold on the t-values to form clusters was set to one that would correspond to a p-value of < 0.05 for the given number of observations. Significant clusters were then determined after a permutation test as the ones reaching a p-value of < 0.05. Of note, it is important to realize that these tests can indicate a difference of EEG state based on clusters, but do not provide conclusive evidence on the exact localization and extent of these clusters [49]. For this reason, in the results we refer to significant clusters of electrodes (white dots on the plots) with a general localization and not by individual electrode significance within the clusters or the spatial extent of said clusters.

For NASA-TLX comparisons, we first verified the normality of the distributions and used two samples t-test to compare control to post-stroke groups and one-sample t-tests when comparing one task to another in the same subjects. If normality would not be assumed, we would have used respectively Wilcoxon rank sum and sign rank non-parametric tests. However, none of the distributions in this study required the use of non-parametric testing. For mixed designs we used Mixed-model ANOVAs after verifying for normality and homoscedasticity. When necessary, a Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. The p-values are given on the plots directly regardless of significance. However, to report the results as significant in the result part we used alpha threshold at < 0.05 after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparison when applicable.

Results

NASA-TLX correlations in N-back reveals specific activation pattern

The participants underwent two randomized recording days, A and B, completing multiple sessions of four different activities each day (Fig. 1A). The games were played on screen using wrist flexions as control (Fig. 1B, C, see methods).

To specifically isolate a state of active participation and cognitive engagement, we calculated the correlation between EEG relative powers in three main frequency bands, theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz) and beta (14–24 Hz) and NASA-TLX scores during an N-back protocol with N = 1 and N = 2 (Fig. 2A). The N-back task was kept short, and the participants were instructed to actively participate and do their best.

Post-stroke participants reported significantly higher NASA-TLX scores than controls for both task levels, demonstrating increased subjective workload (Fig. 2B; ANOVA effect of subject type F(1, 34) = 5.61; p = 0.02). Similarly, their performance was poorer than controls at both levels (effect of subject type F(1, 34) = 7.62; p = 0.009), indicating the task indeed posed greater difficulty for them.

In controls, a clear EEG state correlating with higher NASA-TLX was observed, characterized by increased frontal theta and decreased centro-parietal alpha and beta (Fig. 2C; controls). This state was less distinct in post-stroke participants, where we found only low Fz beta correlating with elevated NASA-TLX (Fig. 2C; post-stroke). The overall state, although not significant, resembles the one observed in controls.

Overall, the task was more challenging for post-stroke participants than controls, as reflected in both reported effort and performance. EEG data showed differing correlations with NASA-TLX between the groups, with controls exhibiting a clearer state indicative of participation and cognitive effort characterized by increased theta and decreased alpha and beta.

Rehabilitation game induced EEG states

One of the two games that we used was the Flying car game, which consists of controlling a car using hand movements in an engaging context (Fig. 3A; see methods). Looking at the result of the NASA-TLX questionnaire, the post-stroke participants reported higher difficulty than controls for this game (Fig. 3B; two-sample t-test controls vs post-stroke, t = 4.86, p = 2.6 ∙ 10–4). However, all post-stroke participants and controls managed to play the game at level 1, achieving a score close to maximum (not shown).

First, we verified that pooling the results from day A and day B would not introduce an unwanted effect in our analysis. At the population level, there was no difference in NASA-TLX reporting, nor significant cluster of EEG activity indicative of a day effect in either of our groups (one-sample t-tests for TLX difference in day A—day B in stroke survivors, t = 0.5, p = 0.6; in controls, t = 0.6, p = 0.5). Second, we verified several potential confounding factors in our heterogeneous stroke survivor population sample. The presence of moderate cognitive impairment did not induce a detectable difference in perceived difficulty in either Flying Car or Moving Ball (Mixed model ANOVA level and cognitive impairment in post-stroke, effect of cognitive impairment vs other, F(1, 9) = 0.41, p = 0.5; t-test cognitive impairment vs other in Moving ball t = 1.22, p = 0.25). Moreover, the lesion side did not induce a detectable difference in perceived difficulty (Mixed model ANOVA level and lesion side in post-stroke, effect of lesion side, F(1, 9) = 0.33, p = 0.58; two sample t-test for lesion side in Moving ball t = 0.6, p = 0.5). Finally, we could not detect a significant correlation in perceived difficulty with age in our stroke survivor population (Pearson correlation rho = 0.29, p = 0.4).

To highlight the specific brain activity associated with each task, we compared the relative power in the three extracted EEG bands to their respective level at rest (1 min resting with eyes open recorded at the beginning of day A). In the Flying car game, we highlighted a significantly different EEG state than rest in both controls and post-stroke participants. In both groups, the changes observed were the following: first, an increase in relative theta power, with the highest increase over parietal and occipital areas. Second, a decrease of relative alpha power, also carried mostly by parietal and occipital areas. Finally, there is a decrease in relative beta, with the biggest differences over frontal and central electrodes (Fig. 3C; post-stroke, control). Together these similar results suggest both groups achieved a similarly activated EEG measured state.

To measure the potential effect of an immersive environment, we developed a clone of the Flying car, implying the same motor task, but without its engaging content, that we call the Moving ball game. (Fig. 3D; see methods).

First, we assessed the subjective difficulty with the NASA-TLX result. Here again, the post-stroke participants reported higher TLX values than the controls (Fig. 3E, two-sample t-test post-stroke vs controls, t = 3.98, p = 2.6 ∙ 10–4), indicating that the game proposed a bigger challenge for them than for the controls. However, as in the Flying car game, all post-stroke participants and controls achieved an almost perfect score.

Second, we compared the EEG state induced by playing the Moving ball game to the resting state. In the control group, we found a significantly different EEG state. The state associated to Moving ball was composed of increased relative theta power, driven by central and parietal electrodes, and decreased relative alpha power, driven by occipital electrodes (Fig. 3F, controls). However, in post-stroke participants, we only found a tendency for the same state as in controls, but none of these changes were significant (Fig. 3F, post-stroke).

In summary, these results demonstrate that we could detect a statistically different brain state compared to rest in our post-stroke group only in the context of the Flying car game and not during the Moving Ball game. Moreover, the state observed in the Flying Car game in post-stroke participants is similar to the one observed in controls.

Effect of the engaging environment

In order to differentiate more precisely the differences between the two games, one with engaging environments and one without, we directly compared them in terms of EEG state.

First we wanted to verify that we could not detect a difference in terms of hand movements between the two games to make sure they were clones of each other regarding gameplay, and could be compared directly. The hand movement patterns were qualitatively similar within subjects in both games (example in Fig. 4A). Moreover, we could not detect a difference in movement frequencies neither between controls and post-stroke participants, nor between games (Fig. 4B, Mixed model ANOVA controls vs post-stroke participants F(1, 34) = 3.05, p = 0.09; Flying car vs Moving ball F(1, 34) = 0.38, p = 0.54). Regarding movement amplitude, the controls were always calibrated on the range of movements achievable by each subject, being the same for the two games.

Second, we verified that the perceived difficulty was comparable between the two games in both groups. Controls reported that Flying car was slightly easier than Moving ball (Fig. 4C; one-sample t-test in controls, t = -4.8, p = 0.0013). This is likely because coin positions in the Flying car can be anticipated thanks to the horizontal scrolling, whether green areas in the Moving ball just appear. In post-stroke participants, however, we could not detect a difference in perceived difficulty (Fig. 4C; one-sample t-test in post-stroke, t = -1.2, p = 0.23). We can therefore not conclude that the perceived difficulty between the two games were different for them.

As for the difference in EEG state between Flying car and Moving ball, we detected in controls; higher relative theta power, with strongest difference over occipital regions, lower relative alpha and beta with strongest difference over frontal and occipital regions (Fig. 4D, controls). In post-stroke participants we observe a similar result with; higher relative theta concentrated on parietal electrodes, lower alpha and beta power with strongest effect on left and right frontal regions (Fig. 4D, post-stroke participants). This suggests that both groups reached a similar EEG state while playing the Flying car game compared to the Moving ball game.

Effect of increasing gaming difficulty

As a final step in this study, we wanted to measure the effect of increasing difficulty. We focused on the Flying car game to preserve the effect of the environment. Moreover, it is the activity at the basic level 1 that showed the most similar state in both controls and post-stroke participants. Indeed, as the level 1 is comparable, we specifically test for a higher difficulty starting from a similar baseline in terms of EEG state in both groups. In the Flying car game at level 2, cones appear as obstacles shortly after the coins and the user needs to avoid them, adding difficulty and reducing the score if hit (Fig. 5A).

We first made sure that level 2 was perceived as more difficult than level 1 by comparing the NASA-TLX values. We found that all the controls indeed reported a higher workload at level 2 (Fig. 5B; one-sample t-test in controls, t = 10.2, p = 3.3 ∙ 10–10) and almost all (except for 2) post-stroke participants as well (Fig. 5B; one-sample t-test in post-stroke participants, t = 3.7, p = 0.003). Therefore level 2 indeed proposed a higher challenge on average than level 1. It is important to note that post-stroke participants already reported a higher difficulty than controls at level 1 (Fig. 2B), and that a ceiling effect might be present for some post-stroke participants.

We also verified that the participant effectively engaged in the task at level 2 by extracting movement frequency at both levels. The time between movements was effectively lower at level 2 compared to level 1 in both groups, indicating that the post-stroke participants engaged in the game at level 2 (Fig. 5C; Mixed model ANOVA controls vs post-stroke participants F(1, 34) = 1.05, p = 0.3; level F(1, 34) = 1896, p = 2∙ 10–31).

Then we measured the difference between level 2 and level 1 in terms of EEG state. In controls we highlighted a significantly different state for all three relative frequency powers. Theta was higher, mostly driven by clusters at frontal and central areas. Alpha was lower with almost all electrodes contributing. Finally, beta was lower at frontal and central regions (Fig. 5D, controls). In post-stroke participants however, only relative beta displayed a different state, being lower at left frontal and central regions. Indeed, neither theta, nor alpha was found different from level 2 to level 1 (Fig. 5D, post-stroke).

Overall, increasing the difficulty in the Flying car game clearly modified the EEG state of the controls differently than the one of the post-stroke participants. The increase of theta and decrease of alpha observed in controls was absent in post-stroke participants.

Discussion

In this study, we measured the EEG state induced by two versions of a game, differentiated by the presence or the lack of an engaging environment in post-stroke participants and healthy controls.

The classical N-back task in our study served as a benchmark task to identify a cognitively activated state. This task is known to induce an increase in frontal theta and decrease in alpha compared to rest, and by increasing the difficulty of the task [44, 50]. We could observe the characteristic power increase in our control group correlating with perceived effort. However, this was not so clear in post-stroke participants and appeared only as a tendency. It is possible that this is due to the fewer post-stroke subjects compared to the control group, which reduces statistical power, or that such a state is more difficult to achieve and maintain for this population. Moreover, our stroke survivor sample was heterogeneous in terms of lesion side, lesion type and symptoms. This heterogeneity, albeit representative within this population, can add variability and hide specific effects. Although we could not find a difference in perceived difficulty based on cognitive impairment, lesion side or age, more studies designed with these specific subgroups and enough statistical power are needed to give a more precise answer to this question.

Both games were perceived as more difficult by post-stroke participants than by controls; however, post-stroke performance showed they were still able to complete them fairly. In terms of EEG activation, post-stroke participants exhibited a brain state similar to healthy controls—marked by increased theta and reduced alpha power—only during the Flying car game which has the engaging 3D environment. This pattern qualitatively matched the cognitively activated state identified in controls during the N-back task, which served as our reference for engagement and effort. In contrast, during the Moving ball game, requiring the same movements, but without gamification cues, post-stroke participants showed only a weak tendency toward this activated state.

The specific state of increased theta and reduced alpha is described in literature and quantified using the theta / alpha ratio (TAR) as a measure of workload. It first was apparent that an increase in task complexity, or multitasking, was causing an increase of theta and consequently TAR [3, 22, 30, 46]. On the other side of the ratio, an increase in alpha rhythm is associated with idle and relaxed state and is used as an inverse measure of attention and participation [34]. An interesting point about this TAR marker is that there are at least two ways to observe an increase. To understand how, it is important to differentiate between taskload and workload. The taskload is referring to the specific work a subject is asked to perform. The workload however, is the subjective effort the user is putting into achieving the taskload [5]. On the one hand, TAR increases if the task becomes more difficult. Indeed, as the taskload increases, the subject needs to put more workload into achieving the same performance. On the other hand, with a fixed taskload, TAR increases based on the effort the subject puts willingly into the task to achieve it, or to push for higher performance.

We believe that this second scenario is the one we observe in our study, and is the most apparent when directly comparing the two games together. Indeed, the Flying car game is the task in which we observed the highest TAR-like state in controls, and the only one in which we observed it in post-stroke participants, both compared to resting state, and to the Moving ball state. As the objective and gameplay are essentially the same, and the difficulty of the Flying car game was rated similar as the Moving ball game in post-stroke participants, and even slightly easier in controls, it seems unlikely that the observed TAR increase we observe is due to task difficulty. Hence, we are left with an increased effort willingly put into the task by the participants, which, we believe, is apparent to an increase in motivation.

For the Moving ball game specifically, we did observe a tendency for post-stroke participants to achieve a state similar to the controls, and characteristic of a TAR increase. It is possible that they approached this state as well, but that we lacked statistical power to detect it. It is therefore difficult to conclude definitively that the Moving ball game was not motivating at all, but it is however certain that the Flying car game induced a higher and less variable TAR-like EEG state in the post-stroke group.

Finally, by increasing the difficulty of the Flying car game, we could observe the TAR increase characteristic of an increase in taskload in the control group. Interestingly however, the post-stroke participants maintained a state similar as when playing the level 1. Only the beta band on the left hemisphere was lower in post-stroke participants at level 2, maybe indicative of the more numerous and different movement types at this level. Overall, this lack of TAR upregulation could mean that they were not more stimulated by this increased challenge, and maintained the same workload as for level 1. This effect is likely not due to the subjects not being able to play at this difficulty, since the movement increase shows that they indeed participated efficiently. However, this shows that an increase in difficulty might not be the best way to keep post-stroke participants motivated to play and that the difficulty level should be chosen specifically to maximize this TAR-like state over the longest period possible. In our case, it would have been interesting to try to increase the difficulty level even more to see if this state becomes undetectable at some point, indicative of participant giving-up on the task. Looking forward, the objective EEG markers identified in this study, particularly the TAR-like state, could serve as personalized biomarkers for tailoring neurorehabilitation. Since the engaged brain state in stroke survivors was dependent on the intervention's specific context and difficulty, these electrophysiological indices could allow clinicians to objectively pre-select the most effective gamified environment and, critically, to fine-tune the difficulty level for each individual. This real-time feedback mechanism moves beyond simple behavioral performance and supports the vision of precision rehabilitation by maintaining the patient within their optimal zone of engagement to maximize therapeutic benefit.

One limitation of our study is that a direct comparison between groups is not feasible, nor meaningful. First, brain damage in post-stroke participants can induce massive changes in EEG response that could explain between-participant differences [19]. More details on the variations and individual brain activation maps for post-stroke participants and healthy controls across the different tasks are presented in the supplementary figures. Second, the participants in the control group were younger than those in the stroke recovery group. This choice was conscious, as we wanted a control group that would be enthusiastic about video games to identify the specific state reached with an active and motivated participation. However, it has been shown in a study that TAR was more strongly linked to performance in younger subjects rather than older ones [56]. It is unclear if older subjects simply have a lower possible range for the TAR, or if this was, as we suggest in our study, a matter of motivation. Regardless, we could still show that our post-stroke group could reach such an activated motivation state while playing the Flying car game, and that their TAR was not simply scaled down in both games compared to the younger subjects. It is, however, possible that the effect we measured here, the need for easy and engaging games for improved attention, is age-dependent and not stroke related. If so, we still think this is an interesting result as older age is often associated with this population. A healthy age-matched control group would give a more precise answer to this, but the potential usefulness of this method is already demonstrated with our current study.

This finding points to the importance of motivation in modulating performance and neural activation, especially in rehabilitation settings. Among motivation theories, the theory of flow has emerged as a suitable framework for understanding the motivational impact of gaming in rehabilitation [41]. This theory conceptualizes motivation as a dynamic system emerging from the interaction between the individual and the environment. Experiencing a state of "flow" entails engaging in achievable challenges aligned to one's skills, driven by clear, proximal goals, and supported by continuous adjustment informed by immediate feedback from progress and reinforced by intrinsic rewards. Brain-damaged stroke survivors show heterogeneous motivational profiles, which can include reduced commitment to long-term rehabilitation goals, impairments in self-awareness of deficits, and clinically relevant alterations in time perception [57]. These changes may foster a mode of functioning in which behavior is increasingly dominated by the immediate action context, with diminished reflective distance on long-term goals and on one’s own behavior. Serious games in rehabilitation can help induce a flow state in stroke survivors by: setting concrete, rule-based goals; tailoring actions to their skills; delivering clear feedback through scores or progress reports; and offering engaging information that limits distractions and facilitates concentration [51].

In our case, we used classic on-screen gaming and already saw the effect of an engaging environment on motivation and participation. It is however likely that a more immersive way of playing, such as augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) technologies would show an even higher effect. Indeed, AR and VR facilitate the creation of multisensory and motivating environments that enable dynamic interaction with users and provide real-time feedback, thereby fostering a "flow" state. Consequently, growing evidence supports the efficacy of AR and VR in gaming applications for cognitive and motor rehabilitation [8, 10, 17, 33, 43, 54]. These successes are most likely directly linked to the increased motivation caused by immersion that we could highlight in our study. Moreover, EEG proved to provide a good objective way to highlight this motivation and participation where more conventional measures, such as game score, suffer from ceiling and delayed effects at difficulty levels adapted for post-stroke participants. Indeed, by using EEG, it would be possible to measure online motivation and effort and easily adapt a specific game for a stroke survivor in order to maximize them, and therefore their therapeutic outcome.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings indicate that gamified rehabilitation programs featuring engaging environments and carefully calibrated difficulty levels can significantly enhance motivation and sustained participation in post-stroke individuals. By leveraging EEG as an objective marker of cognitive effort and engagement, we demonstrate that such enriched settings are more effective at eliciting mentally activated states compared to less immersive alternatives. This underscores the importance of designing rehabilitation tools that are not only functionally appropriate but also psychologically stimulating, ensuring that task difficulty is adapted to individual capabilities and preferences to maximize attentional engagement and, ultimately, therapeutic outcomes.
