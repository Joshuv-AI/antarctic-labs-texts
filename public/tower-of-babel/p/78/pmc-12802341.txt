Introduction

Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), an enveloped, single-stranded positive-sense ribonucleic acid (RNA) virus, belongs to the Alphavirus genus within the Togaviridae family. Transmitted primarily by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes, CHIKV infection causes chikungunya fever (CHIKF), characterized by acute fever, rash, and arthralgia. Phylogenetic analysis reveals three major CHIKV genotypes: West African (WA), East/Central/South African (ECSA), and Asian. The ECSA genotype has diversified into distinct sub-lineages, including the Indian Ocean Lineage (IOL), Central African (CA), and East/South African (ESA). While nucleotide homology across genotypes/lineages exceeds 84 %, pathogenicity exhibits notable variation among strains [[1], [2], [3]].

CHIKF was first recognized in Tanzania, Africa, in 1952 [4]. Since the early 21st century, driven by globalization, climate change, and viral evolution, CHIKV has expanded globally from its African origins [5], reaching Southeast Asia during 2004–2005. To date, 119 countries have reported CHIKV transmission, significantly impacting public health and local economies [[6], [7], [8]]. According to the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), illustrative data for the first half of 2025 documented 220,000 CHIKV cases and 80 fatalities across 14 countries/regions, primarily in the Americas, with an additional 33,000 cases reported in Asia. During July–August 2025 in China, a CHIKV outbreak in Foshan City of Guangdong Province resulted in approximately 8,000 laboratory-confirmed infections linked to an imported index case [9].

Acute CHIKV infection typically manifests as fever, rash, myalgia, and arthralgia, with low mortality rates in immunocompetent individuals. As no specific antiviral against CHIKV exist, clinical management of CHIKF relies primarily on symptomatic treatment. Transition to chronic CHIKV infection imposes substantial long-term burdens, including persistent polyarthralgia, functional disability, reduced workforce participation, and elevated healthcare expenditures [10,11]. In comparison, CHIKV infection carries significant morbidity and mortality risks in infants, elderly populations, and immunocompromised hosts. CHIKV patients with comorbidities, particularly hypertension, diabetes mellitus or cardiovascular disease, experience exacerbated baseline conditions and increased susceptibility to severe complications [12,13]. Frequent coinfections of CHIKV with other arboviruses demonstrate mutual influences that modulate replication kinetics, transmission efficiency, and pathogenicity, complicating the diagnosis, prevention and control of diseases [14,15].

To recapitulate key pathological features of CHIKV infection, diverse animal models have been established [16], including nonhuman primates (NHPs), murine systems, hamsters, tree shrews, and zebrafish. These models simulate clinical disease manifestations at varying biological scales, with each offering distinct advantages and limitations that determine their applicability to specific research objectives. Collectively, they provide indispensable platforms for investigating CHIKV pathogenesis and advancing the development of therapeutic and vaccine options. This review synthesizes current knowledge on: (1) the natural hosts and susceptibility patterns of CHIKV, (2) mechanistic insights into pathogenesis and recent development of animal models, and (3) future directions for model optimization. Further analysis under this review aims to guide the evidence-based selection, implementation, and innovation of CHIKV infection models.

Natural hosts and susceptible species of CHIKV

NHPs represent the primary natural reservoir hosts for CHIKV, with rodents and bats identified as potential reservoir species. CHIKV isolates or specific antibodies have been documented across 18 NHP species globally, including cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis), common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus), and African green monkeys (Chlorocebus sabaeus) [[17], [18], [19]]. In addition, CHIKV isolates have been recovered from Indian palm squirrels (Funambulus palmarum) and multiple bat species [20], while serological evidence indicates exposure in rodents (Oecomys spp.), water buffalo (Bubalus bubalis), and elephants (Elephas maximus) [21]. Although experimental avian infections generate antibody responses without detectable viremia [22], ectothermic vertebrates (reptiles/amphibians) develop sustained viremia following inoculation, suggesting potential roles in CHIKV maintenance cycles [23]. Laboratory-confirmed susceptible species include NHPs, mice (Mus musculus), hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus), Chinese tree shrews (Tupaia belangeri chinensis), and zebrafish (Danio rerio).

Clinical manifestations and pathogenic mechanisms of CHIKV infection

Human CHIKV infection occurs primarily through bites of infected Aedes mosquitoes, with vertical transmission documented. Approximately 75 % of infected individuals develop symptomatic disease, characterized by acute fever (88 %), debilitating polyarthralgia (90 %), and progression to chronic arthralgia in 44 % of cases. Chronic manifestations feature recurrent joint pain persisting for months to years, with an overall mortality rate of 0.3 % [24].

Following a period of 1–12 days of incubation, CHIKV acute infection typically presents symptoms of high fever, bilateral symmetric polyarthralgia (particularly affecting hands, wrists, knees, and ankles), headache, myalgia, and profound fatigue [25]. The virus demonstrates marked synoviotropism, directly infecting synovial fibroblasts and macrophages, thereby inducing inflammatory cytokine cascades that drive persistent joint inflammation [26]. The severity and duration of symptoms exhibit considerable interindividual variability. Approximately 30 % – 50 % of patients experience gastrointestinal involvement, while neurological complications may include encephalitis, optic neuropathy, acute retinal necrosis, Guillain-Barré syndrome [27], and cerebrospinal meningitis, the most frequent neuroinvasive presentation [28,29]. Emerging evidence further implicates CHIKV in cardiovascular pathology, including arrhythmias, acute myocarditis, and dilated cardiomyopathy [30].

Although most patients recover fully from acute CHIKV infection, a subset (particularly those with comorbidities) develops persistent polyarthralgia and systemic symptoms that progress to chronic disability and functional impairment [31]. The clinical and molecular presentation of CHIKV-induced arthropathy demonstrates significant overlap with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [32], creating diagnostic challenges that frequently lead to misclassification.

Research demonstrates that CHIKV enters host cells primarily via the MXRA8 receptor [33], replicating within connective tissue fibroblasts, macrophages, and osteoblasts. Localized viral replication triggers production of inflammatory chemokines that recruit and activate monocytes, neutrophils, natural killer (NK) cells, and T lymphocytes, which initiates potent innate immune responses mediated through Toll-like receptor (TLR) 3, TLR4, and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome pathways [[34], [35], [36]], alongside adaptive immunity involving activation of T cells and B cells [[37], [38], [39]]. While essential for viral clearance, these immune cascades simultaneously mediate tissue damage, particularly in articular and musculoskeletal systems. Emerging evidence further implicates the dysbiosis of gut microbiota in CHIKV pathogenesis [40]. Critically, CHIKV employs multiple strategies of immune evasion that facilitate persistent infection [41], directly contributing to chronic arthralgia and associated histopathology.

Animal models of CHIKV infection

CHIKV exhibits broad tropism across mammalian, avian, reptilian, and amphibian hosts. Current modeling efforts primarily employ five established systems: NHPs, mice, hamsters, tree shrews, and zebrafish. The following sections provide a critical summary of animal models of CHIKV infection developed across these representative organisms.

Nonhuman primate models of CHIKV infection

NHPs represent natural reservoir hosts for CHIKV, exhibiting high phylogenetic conservation with humans in genomic architecture, immune responses, and pathophysiological manifestations. These models faithfully recapitulate human viral dissemination kinetics, immunological dynamics, and chronic arthritic sequelae [42,43]. However, phenotypes of CHIKV disease in NHPs demonstrate significant modulation by host-specific variables, including species, age, gestational status, and experimental parameters such as inoculation route, viral strain, and titer (Table 1). These factors warrant paramount consideration during model design, validation, and translational application.

The intravenous infection model of cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) with the LR2006-OPY1 strain of ECSA genotype comprehensively recapitulates human CHIKF manifestations, exhibiting clinical symptoms (fever and rash) and biomarkers (viremia kinetics, hematological parameters, histopathology, viral tissue distribution, and cytokine profiles) consistent with human CHIKV infection. This model implicates persistent high-titer CHIKV infection of monocytes/macrophages as a key driver of chronic polyarthralgia. Disease severity correlates with inoculum titer but remains independent of administration route, with equivalent pathogenesis observed following intravenous and intradermal challenge [44].

Pregnant rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) infected subcutaneously with the endemic or epidemic CHIKV strains develop comparable febrile responses, joint inflammation, and leukopenia/lymphopenia. While the endemic strain induces significantly higher viremia and synovitis, the epidemic strain elicits more potent neutralizing antibodies by days 14–21 post-infection. Both strains transiently alter fetal heart rate with no evidence of vertical transmission [45].

Aged rhesus macaques exhibit enhanced susceptibility to CHIKV. Intravenous challenge with the CHIKV-39779 (WA) or LR2006-OPY1 (ECSA) strain induces fever, thoracic rash, viremia, and lymphadenopathy, with the ECSA strain demonstrating significantly greater virulence. Aged hosts display prolonged high-magnitude viremia concomitant with attenuated T-cell, B-cell, and innate immune responses, identifying immunosenescence as a critical determinant of CHIKV severity [46].

Aerosol exposure of cynomolgus macaques establish limited viremia with mild clinical signs but no significant alteration in core physiological parameters (temperature, weight, hematology, serum biochemistry and hepatic function) [47]. These findings raise questions about the epidemiological relevance of aerosol transmission, warranting further investigation into its clinical significance.

In summary, cynomolgus and rhesus macaques constitute the principal nonhuman primate models for studies of CHIKV infection. These systems have been extensively validated for evaluating diverse vaccine platforms, including virus-like particle (VLP; VIMKUNYA) [48], deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) vaccine [49], live-attenuated formulation (IXCHIQ), viral vector (MV-CHIK) [50], insect virus chimera (EILV/CHIKV) [51], whole-inactivated virus (BBV87) [52], and messenger RNA (mRNA) candidates (mCV-1/2) [53]. Of these vaccine candidates, the live-attenuated IXCHIQ and VLP-based VIMKUNYA have received regulatory approval. Notably, cynomolgus macaques exhibit heightened viral loads and increased propensity for persistent CHIKV infection, establishing them as preferred models for investigating chronic arthritic manifestations and viral reservoir dynamics. A critical knowledge gap remains due to the absence of direct comparative studies examining differential host responses between these macaque species.

Murine models of CHIKV infection

Mice (Mus musculus) represent the predominant experimental system for modeling viral pathogenesis, characterized by compact physiology, operational efficiency, low maintenance costs, genetic tractability, comprehensive analytical reagents, and established humanization protocols. CHIKV-induced disease manifestations in murine systems are modulated by host factors (age and immune status) and viral parameters (strain and inoculation route) [54]. Current models comprise three principal categories: (1) immunocompromised (including neonatal) mice, (2) immunocompetent wildtype mice, and (3) humanized murine systems.

The earliest documented murine CHIKV model, described in 1956, demonstrats experimental encephalitis following intracranial inoculation of 28-day-old female Swiss A2G mice. Infected subjects exhibited mortality by 6 days postinfection (dpi), preceded by piloerection, lethargy, and paralysis within 24–36 h. Histopathological analysis reveals perivascular lymphocytic infiltration, gliosis, and microglial proliferation, with persistent intracranial viral replication [55]. Immunocompetent adult mice exhibit minimal susceptibility to CHIKV via systemic routes, failing to develop significant viremia, pathology, or clinical disease. The pivotal establishment of pathogenesis-permissive models in 2008 utilized immunocompromised hosts (neonatal or type I interferon signaling-deficient mice), recapitulating human-relevant tissue tropism including dermal fibrosis, hepatic involvement, and musculoskeletal pathology, with severe cases demonstrating neuroinvasion [56]. Subsequent development of immunodeficient systems has enabled multilevel simulation of human infection for antiviral and pathogenesis studies, including, RAG1–/– [57,58], TCR-deficient [37,59], μMT–/– (B-cell deficient) [60], neonatal [56,[61], [62], [63], [64]], IFN signaling-deficient [56,[65], [66], [67], [68], [69], [70], [71], [72], [73]], Rsad2–/– [74], CCR2–/– [75], CXCL10–/– [76], TLR3–/– [34], MSR1–/– [77], IL17A–/– & IL17RA–/– [78] mice (Table 2). These models have elucidated critical roles of corresponding immune pathways in the control of CHIKV infection. While permitting viremia studies, immunocompromised systems cannot model infection in immunocompetent hosts. Thus, developing immunologically intact murine models remains essential for vaccine evaluation and studies of protective immunity mechanisms.

Although immunocompetent mice typically exhibit low susceptibility to CHIKV, optimal parameters of viral strain, inoculation route, and dosage enable wildtype systems to recapitulate key clinical features across age groups. These include plantar edema, viremia, hematological alterations, hepatic dysfunction, and viral dissemination to joint/muscle tissues with associated arthromyositis, establishing viable immunocompetent CHIKV models. Current wildtype C57BL/6 models encompass 6–14-day-old (neonatal) and 3–4-week-old (juvenile) [56,63,72,[79], [80], [81]], 6–8-week-old (adult) [[82], [83], [84], [85], [86], [87], [88]], and 20-week-old (aged) animals [89]. Unified by footpad inoculation of the ECSA strain, these systems differentially recapitulate clinical disease and biochemical profiles. Age-dependent susceptibility follows neonatal > juvenile > aged > adult (Table 3), with corresponding variations in disease manifestations. Adult models demonstrate transient viremia (<48 h) without persistent infection.

CHIKV infection induces both arthritic pathology and extra-articular involvement, including myocardial infection [30]. Following footpad inoculation of 1- and 3-week-old C57BL/6 mice, viral RNA localizes to cardiac tissue. In interferon signaling-deficient models, enhanced cardiac replication triggers upregulation of pro-inflammatory genes, neutrophil infiltration, vasculitis, myocarditis, and notably, myocardial fibrosis [73].

Beyond C57BL/6 systems, alternative murine strains demonstrate differential susceptibility (Table 3). Four-week-old BALB/c and Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) mice develop transient viremia without clinical manifestations [[90], [91]]. DBA/1J mice (7–8 weeks old) exhibit viral dissemination to blood, spleen, and pedal tissues [87]. CHIKV intracranial challenge in CD1 mice (2–28 days) yields age- and strain-dependent mortality, clinical indices, and central nervous system (CNS) viral loads [3]. Reporter-expressing CHIKV constructs enable real-time tracking of infection dynamics in murine models [62,[92], [93], [94]].

Murine models of CHIKV infection have been extensively leveraged for antiviral drug [85,[95], [96], [97]], therapeutic antibody [80,87,[98], [99], [100]], and vaccine development [58,65,68,70,71,86,101], alongside mechanistic studies of viral

pathogenesis [37,40,57,59,60,63,66,67,69,74,[76], [77], [78],83,84,88,[102], [103], [104], [105]]. However, significant translational limitations persist [106], with murine data frequently failing to predict clinical outcomes. Humanized murine systems, categorized as immune cells reconstituted or receptor-transgenic models, address this gap by incorporating human biological components. These chimeric platforms enhance viral susceptibility and recapitulate human-specific infection dynamics. When exposed to CHIKV via mosquito bites, immune-humanized mice develop systemic infection featuring viremia, mild-to-moderate clinical symptoms, and faithful recapitulation of human immunological responses. These include dynamic alterations in dendritic cells, monocyte/macrophage populations, T/B/NK cell compartments, and T-cell subset polarization [91] (Table 3). However, receptor-humanized models for CHIKV remain unreported to date.

Alternative animal models of CHIKV infection

Hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus) exhibit broad susceptibility to arboviral infections [107]. Subcutaneous inoculation of golden hamsters with ECSA-genotype CHIKV (105 plaque-forming unit [PFU]) induces viremia and neutralizing antibody production, with viral antigen detected in multiple tissues including the brain. Infection drives rapid dissemination to major organs and reproduces key human pathological features, notably hind limb myositis and tenosynovitis, mirroring histopathology in CHIKF patients. However, infected hamsters lack overt clinical symptoms (e.g., weight loss) and show no significant local inflammation at inoculation sites [108]. Future model optimization requires systematic evaluation of strain genetics, age-dependent responses, and inoculation parameters (route, dose and viral strain) to develop hamster systems that faithfully recapitulate human disease manifestations.

Tree shrew (Tupaia belangeri), owing to its close phylogenetic relationship with the primate [109,110], represents a promising alternative to NHPs in biomedical research. In recent years, the research community has witnessed an expanded utilization of tree shrews for modeling infectious disease. Concurrent intraperitoneal and subcutaneous inoculation of CHIKV in adult tree shrews induces systemic manifestations (significant pyrexia, weight loss, alopecia, and rash), musculoskeletal involvement (joint swelling with elevated viral loads in ankle/elbow joints and pedal muscles), hematological alterations (progressive leukopenia, lymphopenia, monocytopenia, and neutropenia), hepatic dysfunction (marked elevation of the AST/ALT transaminases) and virological dynamics (sustained viremia with viral RNA detectable in body fluids, feces, and multiple tissues). The highest viral persistence occurs in the spleen, skin, and joints of the digits. Infection elicits CHIKV-specific IgM/IgG responses as well as histopathological changes in hepatic, pulmonary, renal, and cerebral tissues [111,112]. While recapitulating key clinical features of human infection, tree shrew models face limitations including the absence of specific pathogen-free (SPF) colonies, incomplete laboratory adaptation, and scarce immunological reagents, which constrain their broader implementation.

Zebrafish (Danio rerio), possessing substantial genetic conservation with humans (orthologous to 70 % of human disease genes), offer distinct advantages for biomedical research, such as compact size, optical transparency, genetic tractability, low maintenance costs, and tolerance for prolonged anesthesia. These characteristics facilitate high-resolution in vivo imaging and dynamic cellular tracking [113,114]. Intravenous inoculation of 3-dpf larvae with GFP-expressing CHIKV-LR (ECSA OPY1 strain; 50 % tissue culture infective dose [TCID50]) induces yolk sac opacification, swim bladder distension, hemodynamic impairment (reduced blood flow, cardiac arrhythmia), edema, loss of postural control, and delayed tactile response. High viral loads in juvenile brains indicate neuroinvasion via cerebral endothelial cell infection [115]. Transcriptomic profiling, in situ hybridization, and knockout studies demonstrate CHIKV-induced neutrophil-mediated type I interferon responses critically constrain viral replication [116]. Collectively, zebrafish provide a powerful vertebrate system for real-time analysis of CHIKV replication dynamics, host-pathogen interactions, and antiviral immunity mechanisms.

Future perspectives

Chikungunya virus infection elicits multisystem pathology, causing not only debilitating musculoskeletal manifestations but also significant extra-articular tissue damage [117]. Animal models remain indispensable for deciphering CHIKV pathogenesis, host immunity, and therapeutic countermeasures. While existing models recapitulate distinct aspects of human disease, none of the current CHIKV animal models can fully recapitulate the chronic infection and typical arthritis observed in human patients. Optimal selection requires alignment with specific research objectives, including host-adaption by serial passages of the CHIKV strain in a single host. Although nonhuman primates provide high-fidelity modeling, their substantial resource requirements and technical complexity limit scalability. Strategic development of next-generation systems is therefore critical, including humanized murine models with natural mosquito-bite infection, organoid platforms capturing tissue-specific pathophysiology, and microfluidic 3D three-dimensional (3D) “infection-immunity” systems enabling high-content screening. These advanced platforms promise enhanced pathological relevance and accelerated therapeutic discovery. Furthermore, the emerging challenges of CHIKV-dengue virus (DENV)-Zika virus (ZIKV) coinfections and severe outcomes in comorbid populations demand immediate attention [13,118]. Developing sophisticated coinfection and comorbidity models represents a strategic imperative for elucidating pathogenesis mechanisms and advancing diagnostic, prophylactic, and therapeutic interventions against CHIKV-related diseases (Fig. 1).
