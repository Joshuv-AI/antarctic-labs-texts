Impact of adverse postnatal environment on emotional regulation and neuroimmune function in adolescent rats prenatally exposed to alcohol
Authors: Parker J Holman, Victoria R Vella, Samantha L Baglot, Dylan Yeates, Pushpkiran Ubi, Joanne Weinberg, Tamara S Bodnar, Charlis Raineki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndividuals with prenatal alcohol exposure (PAE) are at increased risk for experiencing early-life adversity (ELA), which may contribute to their heightened vulnerability to mental health problems. Using rat models, we examined the independent and interactive effects of PAE (liquid ethanol diet) and ELA (limited bedding) on emotional regulation, specifically anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors, and assessed neuroimmune alterations in the amygdala and hypothalamus during early and late adolescence as a potential underlying mechanism. Our results indicate that PAE induced anxiety-like behaviors across multiple tasks. PAE females exhibited anxiety-like behavior in the open field during both stages, whereas PAE males and females showed anxiety-like behavior in the light-dark test during early adolescence. In the forced swim test, depressive-like behavior was reduced in PAE females but increased in PAE males during late adolescence. ELA also produced sex- and age-dependent effects, increasing anxiety-like behavior in females during both stages and in males during early adolescence, and enhancing depressive-like behavior in both sexes during late adolescence. Importantly, ELA did not exacerbate PAE-related behavioral alterations. Neuroimmune analyses revealed age-, sex-, and region-specific cytokine changes: PAE induced a strong proinflammatory bias in the amygdala of females and altered both anti- and proinflammatory cytokines in the hypothalamus of males and females. In contrast, ELA effects were milder, with both the amygdala and hypothalamus similarly affected across sexes. Together, these findings indicate that PAE and ELA independently disrupt neuroimmune signaling and emotional regulation, supporting the hypothesis that cytokine dysregulation contributes to long-term vulnerability to emotional dysregulation.

Introduction

Early life experiences are critical in shaping later life outcomes (Levine, 2005; Raineki et al., 2014; Suchecki, 2018; Walker et al., 2017), with compounding adverse pre- and postnatal exposures increasing vulnerability to physical and mental health problems (Heindel and Vandenberg, 2015; Holman and Raineki, 2023). Among the many adverse prenatal insults, prenatal alcohol exposure (PAE) has been consistently linked to numerous negative outcomes, spanning a wide range of physical, physiological, and behavioral abnormalities (del Campo and Jones, 2017; Himmelreich et al., 2020; Lebel et al., 2011; Marquardt and Brigman, 2016; Mattson et al., 2019; Vella et al., 2025b; Wilson et al., 2024; Wozniak et al., 2017). Of great concern is the disproportionately high rate of mental health disorders in individuals exposed to PAE, with up to 90% experiencing a mental health disorder at some point during their lifetime (Pei et al., 2011). While alcohol is an undoubtedly potent teratogen, there are various environmental influences that may also affect developmental outcomes, with many postnatal phenomena acting either as protective or risk factors (Streissguth et al., 2004). Importantly, individuals with PAE are at increased risk of experiencing early life adversity (ELA), such as unstable family environments, removal from the home, and multiple foster care placements, as well as a higher risk of experiencing physical and/or sexual abuse, which may further exacerbate their already heightened risk for poor mental health outcomes (Flannigan et al., 2021; Holman and Raineki, 2023; Price et al., 2017; Tan et al., 2022). Clinical studies have begun to investigate the contributions of ELA to the effects of PAE, showing that individuals exposed to both exhibit more severe impairments across multiple domains, including cognitive function, speech, motor skills, and emotional, sleep, and stress regulation (Chandler-Mather et al., 2023; Coggins et al., 2007; Henry et al., 2007; McLachlan et al., 2016; Price et al., 2017; Rockhold et al., 2023). However, the extent to which sex and/or gender may moderate the interactive effects of PAE and ELA, as well as the underlying mechanisms by which ELA may exacerbate the effects of PAE, remain poorly understood. The controlled nature of preclinical studies provides a critical approach for disentangling the individual and interactive contributions of PAE and ELA on outcomes, including examining how males and females may respond differently to these exposures. Here, we combine a rodent model of PAE with a model of ELA to investigate potential mechanisms underlying increased vulnerability to mental health problems following PAE alone or in combination with ELA.

Cytokines, the signaling molecules of the immune system, are involved in key neurodevelopmental processes, including synaptogenesis, synaptic pruning, neurogenesis and neuronal migration (Deverman and Patterson, 2009; Meyer et al., 2009; Vella et al., 2025b). Disruptions to these critical processes, via prenatal and early postnatal insults that alter the cytokine balance, can have downstream effects on ongoing neural circuitry development and may also prime the immune system to respond inappropriately to later challenges (Bilbo and Schwarz, 2012; Lumertz et al., 2022). Moreover, increased levels of inflammatory markers within the amygdala and hypothalamus have been shown to alter neural activity, potentially through changes in neurotransmitter systems such as glutamate and GABA, leading to increased anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors (Cernackova et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2022; Zhong et al., 2022). Importantly, pharmacological treatments that reduce inflammation in the amygdala and hypothalamus can also reduce anxiety-like behaviors (Chen et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2025). Accordingly, perturbations in neuroimmune function may act as a key mechanistic link between PAE, ELA, and poor mental health outcomes, given the immune system's role in regulating neural development as well as in the pathogenesis of various mental health disorders (Danese and Lewis, 2017; Ganguly and Brenhouse, 2015; Lussier et al., 2021; Muller et al., 2019; Nusslock and Miller, 2016). In support of this hypothesis, our earlier studies have demonstrated that during early development, PAE alters cytokine levels in several brain regions, including the amygdala and hypothalamus. Specifically, we identified an early life (postnatal day [P]8) neuroinflammatory bias in both female (Bodnar et al., 2016) and male (Vella et al., 2025a) PAE rats, with the exact pattern varying by sex and brain region. Notably, the early neuroinflammatory bias seen at P8 was no longer detected by P22 in either sex (Bodnar et al., 2016; Vella et al., 2025a). Because early perturbations can prime the immune system, a critical next step is to determine whether a second hit, such as exposure to ELA, might reveal lasting effects of PAE on cytokine levels later in life. In our previous study, we found that at P12 (the day ELA ended) ELA effects on immune and behavioral responses were distinct in PAE versus control pups. Specifically, ELA reduced interferon-ɣ (IFN-ɣ), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and keratinocyte chemoattractant/growth-regulated oncogene (KC/GRO) in the serum of control but not PAE pups (Raineki et al., 2017). Additionally, while ELA increased pup vocalization, PAE pups exhibited a significantly smaller increase in vocalization compared to controls. PAE increased IL-10 and reduced KC/GRO in the amygdala, regardless of rearing condition. However, it remains unclear whether the ELA-induced changes in immune function in PAE animals are enduring and whether they influence behavioral outcomes during adolescence, a critical developmental period (Schneider, 2013; Spear, 2000) when many mental health disorders begin to emerge in humans (de Girolamo et al., 2012; Paus et al., 2008).

To address these questions, we used the same well-established rodent models of PAE and ELA to examine the lasting effects on central cytokine levels, as well as anxiety-like (elevated plus maze, light-dark test, open field) and depressive-like (forced swim test) behaviors in both male and female rats during adolescence. We utilized a naturalistic low-bedding model of ELA, in which providing insufficient bedding from P8-12 induces increased negative maternal behaviors (Raineki et al., 2010, 2012, 2015; Walker et al., 2017). Offspring were tested at both early (P30) and late (P45) adolescence to capture developmental changes across this critical period. We hypothesized that both PAE and ELA would impact central cytokine levels, potentially producing differential interactive effects in PAE versus control animals, and that these changes would be accompanied by increases in anxiety-like and depressive-like behaviors.

Methods

Animals and breeding

Male and female Sprague-Dawley rats were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (St. Constant, Quebec, Canada). Rats were housed with a same-sex cage mate and maintained at a constant temperature (21 ± 1 °C) on a 12:12 light-dark cycle (lights on at 0700h) with ad libitum access to water and standard laboratory chow (Harlan, Canada). After a 10-day acclimation period, male and female rats were paired for breeding. Vaginal smears were collected each morning, and the presence of sperm was used to indicate gestation day 1 (G1). All experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide For The Care And Use Of Laboratory Animals and the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines and were approved by the University of British Columbia Animal Care Committee.

Prenatal alcohol exposure

On G1, females were single-housed and randomly assigned to either Prenatal Alcohol Exposure (PAE) or Control conditions (Fig. 1). Dams in the PAE group were offered ad libitum liquid ethanol diet (6.37% v/v) with 36% ethanol-derived calories. The liquid ethanol diet was introduced gradually over the first 3 days with bottles containing: G1 - 66% control diet, 34% ethanol diet; G2 - 34% control diet, 66% ethanol diet; G3-21 - 100% ethanol diet. The control dams were offered ad libitum access to a pelleted form of the liquid control diet. Diets were prepared by Dyets Inc., (Bethlehem, PA; Weinberg-Keiver High Protein Experimental Diet #710324; Liquid Control Diet #710109; Weinberg/Keiver High Protein Pelleted Control Diet #102698). All diets were formulated to provide adequate nutrition to pregnant rats regardless of ethanol intake. All animals had ad libitum access to water. Experimental diets continued through G21. Tail blood samples (n = 13) were taken on G15 from a subset of dams to determinate blood alcohol levels (BALs) using Pointe Scientific Inc. Alcohol Reagent Set (Lincoln Park, MI, USA). Alcohol-consuming dams showed a mean of 176.9 ± 18.6 mg/dL.

Schematic overview of the experimental design. Prenatal alcohol exposure (PAE) occurred throughout gestation (gestational days [G]1-22). Early life adversity (ELA) was induced between postnatal days (P)8-12 using the low bedding model. Behavioral testing began in early (P30) or late (P45) adolescence. At each age, animals were divided into two cohorts: one was tested in the elevated plus maze (EPM) followed by the light-dark emergence (LD) test, while the other cohort was tested in the open field (OF) and then in a 2-day forced swim test (FST). Brains from the animals tested in the FST were collected 30 min after the end of the second day to assess cytokine levels in the amygdala and hypothalamus.

Beginning on G22, all animals were offered ad libitum access to standard laboratory chow and water, which they received throughout lactation. Pregnant dams were left undisturbed except for cage changes on G1, G7, and G14. On the day of birth (P1), litters were culled to 12 pups, with approximately equal number of males and females per litter, when possible, and transferred to a clean cage.

Early-life adversity

Half of the control and PAE dams were exposed to ELA (control-ELA and PAE-ELA groups). Within 2h of lights off on P7, dams and their pups were transferred to a clean cage with limited nesting/bedding material that consisted of 300 mL of Beta Chip® bedding (Northeastern Products Corp, Warrensburg, NY; Fig. 1). The animals remained in this limited bedding environment until the afternoon of P12 (within 2 h of lights off), at which point they were transferred to a clean cage with abundant nesting/bedding material (3000 mL). The other half of the control and PAE dams were maintained under normal conditions (control-normal and PAE-normal). Within 2h of lights off on P7, they were transferred to a clean cage with abundant nesting/bedding material (3000 mL) and remained in this environment until the afternoon of P12, when they were transferred to a clean cage with the same conditions.

Experimental design

At P22 (weaning), the dam was removed, and offspring were separated by sex, with all males housed together in one cage and all females housed together in a separate cage. At P25, once animals had time to adjust to weaning, non-siblings were pair-housed by sex and prenatal treatment/rearing condition, with no more than one male and/or one female per litter used at each age of testing. This procedure was designed to avoid the simultaneous exposure to maternal separation and immediate pairing with an unfamiliar cage mate. In the current study, we had 2 cohorts of animals that were tested in early (P30) or late (P45) adolescence to account for potential effects of gonadal hormone changes across puberty (Fig. 1). One cohort was tested in the open field at P30 or P45, followed the next day by the 2-day forced swim test. The other cohort was tested in the elevated plus maze at P30 or P45, followed the next day by the light-dark emergence test. Animals in the cohort that underwent the open field and forced swim test were terminated by decapitation 30 min after the last day of forced swim test, and brain samples were collected to measure cytokines.

Open field

All animals were tested in age-appropriate open field apparatuses (P30: 42 × 42 cm × 38 cm high; P45: 84 × 84 cm × 38 cm high) for 10 min. Open field apparatuses were made of clear Plexiglas and fitted within two frames of infrared beams, with the top frame positioned to a height that would track rearing behavior, and the bottom frame adjusted to track movement and activity. Data were recorded and automatically analyzed by MotorMonitor™ software. The duration, frequency of entries, and total distance travelled within the center of the field were measured. Testing occurred in a bright lit room between 0800 and 1200 h.

Forced swim test

All animals were tested in a clear Plexiglas cylinder filled with water (25 ± 1 °C and changed for each test) to a depth sufficient to prevented both escape and tail contact with the bottom. The forced swim test was conducted in two sessions separated by 24 h. The first session lasted 15 min and served to habituate the rats to the test situation, thereby producing a stable, high level of immobility during the 5 min test session conducted 24 h later (Porsolt et al., 2001). Test sessions were recorded and latency to and time spent immobile (passive floating without struggling, slightly hunched but upright position with minor movements to maintain head above water) were measured using Noldus Observer software. Testing occurred in a dim lit room between 0800 and 1200 h.

Elevated plus maze

All animals were tested for 5 min on the elevated plus maze apparatus that consisted of two open arms and two arms enclosed by a 40 cm wall (each arm 50 × 10 cm, center area 10 × 10 cm) elevated 40 cm above the floor. Test sessions were recorded, and latency to enter, time spent in, and frequency of entries into the open arms, as well as latency to poke into the open arm (a measure of risk assessment; Walf and Frye, 2007), were measured using Noldus Observer software. Testing occurred in a bright lit room between 1200 and 1700 h.

Light-dark emergence test

All animals were tested in age-appropriate light-dark emergence apparatuses. The light-dark emergence apparatus consisted of a white Plexiglas rectangular arena that was divided into two compartments: a small (P30: 20 × 40 cm × 38 cm high; P45: 25 × 40 cm × 38 cm high) enclosed dark box constructed of black plastic with a door (10 × 10 cm) leading to a larger (P30: 60 × 40 cm × 38 cm high; P45: 55 × 40 cm × 38 cm high) open lit box. Animals were placed in the dark compartment, and their behaviors were recorded for 10 min. Test sessions were recorded and latency to enter and time spent in the light compartment, as well as duration of nosepoking (i.e., extension of the snout into the light compartment while the head and body remain in the dark compartment) and headpoking (i.e., extension of the head, including the eyes and ears, into the light compartment while the torso remains in the dark compartment) were measured using Noldus Observer software. Testing occurred in a bright lit room between 1200 and 1700 h.

Brain collection

Thirty minutes after the end of second day of forced swim testing, animals were decapitated, and their brains were extracted, quickly frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80 °C until sectioning. Brains were sectioned coronally using a cryostat (−16 °C) with amygdala (bregma −1.08 mm to −3.48 mm) and hypothalamus (bregma −1.08 mm to −3.48 mm) punched (Paxinos and Watson, 2005) and placed into separate tubes and stored at −80 °C until tissue homogenization.

Amygdala and hypothalamus homogenization

The amygdala and hypothalamus from each animal were homogenized in tubes containing 8 zirconium oxide beads and 200 μL of cold lysis buffer using an Omni Bead Ruptor 24 (Omni International, Kennesaw, GA) in 4 cycles (speed: 3.10, time: 6 s), with 1 min on ice between cycles. Homogenates were centrifuged and separate aliquots of supernatant removed for protein and cytokine analysis and stored at −20 °C until assayed.

Multiplex cytokine immunoassays

Cytokine assays were performed using the Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) Proinflammatory Panel 1 Rat V-PLEX kit, which allows for the simultaneous measurement of IL-1β, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-10, IL-13, IFN-ɣ, KC/GRO (CXCL1), and TNF-α (MSD, Rockville, MD). Samples were diluted 1:2 for the amygdala and hypothalamus in diluent 42. Assays were performed using the standard MSD protocol. Cytokine plates were read using a Sector Imager 2400 (MSD) and data analyzed using the MSD Discovery Workbench software v. 4.0 (MSD). Total protein levels were quantified in the amygdala and hypothalamus homogenates using the Pierce Microplate BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL). Tissue homogenate samples were run in quadruplicate to determine the average protein concentration. Amygdala and hypothalamus cytokine levels were reported as pg cytokine/mg protein.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were carried out using software packages Statistica version 14.0.1.25 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, United States), IBM SPSS, version 28.0.0.0 (IBM SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, United States) and GraphPad Prism, version 10.5.0 (GraphPad Software, LLC., Boston, MA, United States). Preliminary analyses revealed pervasive sex effects for the majority of outcomes, and thus, subsequent analyses were stratified by sex to provide the clearest interpretation of PAE and ELA effects within each sex. All data were analyzed by two-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) with prenatal treatment and rearing condition as factors. Significant interactions were examined using Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference post hoc test. Distributed Aligned Rank Transform (ART) ANOVA was used for behavioral data that was not normally distributed, followed by Newman-Keuls post hoc test in the case of a significant interaction. Outliers were identified and removed using the Robust regression and Outlier removal (ROUT) method with Q = 0.05. Cytokine levels were not normally distributed and were thus Blom transformed for statistical analysis. Untransformed data are presented in graphs for clarity. All data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Based on evidence that PAE affects emotional-related behaviors (Baculis et al., 2015; Brocardo et al., 2012; Cullen et al., 2013; Hellemans et al., 2010; Raineki et al., 2016) and immune function (Baker et al., 2023; Bodnar et al., 2016; Drew et al., 2015; Vella et al., 2025a, 2025b), planned pairwise comparisons using Fisher's LSD test were conducted to evaluate the a priori hypothesis that PAE alters behavioral and immune function compared to that in controls. Similarly, given that ELA also impacts emotional-related behaviors (Raineki et al., 2012, 2015) and immune function (Ganguly and Brenhouse, 2015; Lumertz et al., 2022; Muller et al., 2019; Raineki et al., 2017), planned pairwise comparisons were used to test the a priori hypothesis that ELA differentially influences behavioral and immune outcomes in PAE and normally reared animals. In all cases, differences were considered significant when p ≤ 0.05. For clarity, only significant main effects and interactions are reported in the results section; however, full statistics are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Results

Behavioral tests

Open field test

In early adolescence, PAE-normal females exhibited greater anxiety-like behavior in the open field, spending significantly less time (p = 0.0013) and travelling shorter distances (p = 0.004) in the center of the field compared to both control-normal and PAE-ELA females (Fig. 2a and i; significant interaction between prenatal treatment and rearing condition for time in center [F1,27 = 11.12, p = 0.0025, ηp2 = 0.29] and distance travelled in center [F1,26 = 10.84, p = 0.0029, ηp2 = 0.30]). In late adolescence, PAE females continued to display increased anxiety-like behavior, entering the center of the field less frequently and travelling a shorter distance in the center compared to controls, regardless of rearing condition (significant main effect of prenatal treatment for frequency in center [F1,30 = 5.67, p = 0.024, ηp2 = 0.16] and distance travelled in center [F1,30 = 4.06, p = 0.053, ηp2 = 0.12]). No significant behavioral differences in the open field were observed in the males at either age (Fig. 2c–d, g-h, g-l).

(n = 6–10 per group).

Elevated plus maze

In females, no significant behavioral differences in the elevated plus maze were observed at either age (Fig. 3a–b, e-f). In males, neither PAE nor ELA affected the percentage of time spent in the open arms (Fig. 3c and d). However, PAE reduced anxiety-like behavior in the elevated plus maze as both early and late adolescent PAE males had shorter latencies to nosepoke into the open arm (a measure of risk assessment) compared to controls. In early adolescence, PAE-normal (p = 0.02) and PAE-ELA (p = 0.03) showed shorter latencies to nosepoke compared to control-normal males (Fig. 3g). In late adolescence, PAE males exhibited shorter latencies to nose-poke into the open arm compared to controls, regardless of rearing condition (Fig. 3h). Moreover, in early adolescence, control-ELA males showed shorter latencies to nosepoke into the open arm (p = 0.02) compared to control-normal males (Fig. 3g; significant interaction between prenatal treatment and rearing condition for latency to nosepoke open arm at P30 [F1,45 = 4.67, p = 0.036, ηp2 = 0.09]; and significant main effect of prenatal treatment for latency to nosepoke the open arm at P45 [F1,44 = 29.39, p < 0.0001, ηp2 = 0.40]).

(n = 10–14 per group).

Light-dark emergence test

In early adolescence, PAE females exhibited greater anxiety-like behavior than control animals. Specifically, PAE-normal females spent less time in the light chamber and headpoking into the light chamber compared to control-normal females (Fig. 4a and m; significant interaction between prenatal treatment and rearing condition for duration of headpoke [F1,41 = 3.97, p = 0.053, ηp2 = 0.09], no significant post hoc comparisons; a priori analysis comparing PAE-normal and control-normal animals for light chamber duration [p = 0.022] and headpoking [p = 0.036]). Moreover, early adolescent PAE females spent less time nosepoking into the light chamber regardless of rearing condition (Fig. 4i; main effect of prenatal treatment [F1,44 = 7.32, p = 0.01, ηp2 = 0.14]). In late adolescence, no significant behavioral differences were observed in the light-dark emergence test among females (Fig. 4b,f,j,n). In early adolescence and similar to females, PAE males also exhibited greater anxiety-like behavior, spending less time headpoking into the light chamber regardless of rearing condition (Fig. 4o; main effect of prenatal treatment [F1,44 = 5.03, p = 0.03, ηp2 = 0.10]). In late adolescence, no significant behavioral differences were observed in the light–dark emergence test among males (Fig. 4d,h,l,p).

Forced swim test

Although no effects of PAE nor ELA were observed in females during early adolescence, late adolescent PAE females spent less time immobile and showed an increased latency to immobility as compared to controls, regardless of rearing condition (Fig. 5a–b, e-f, significant main effect of prenatal treatment for time immobile [F1,30 = 4.78, p = 0.037, ηp2 = 0.14] and latency to immobility [F1,30 = 5.66, p = 0.024, ηp2 = 0.16]). Moreover, a priori analysis revealed that during late adolescence, control-ELA females spent more time immobile than control-normal females (p = 0.042). For males, neither PAE nor ELA affected any behavioral measures in the forced swim test in early adolescence (Fig. 5c and d). In late adolescent, though, both PAE-normal and control-ELA males had a shorter immobility latencies compared to control-normal males (Fig. 5g and h; significant interaction between prenatal treatment and rearing condition for latency to immobility [F1,28 = 5.14, p = 0.031, ηp2 = 0.16], no significant post hoc comparisons; a priori analysis comparing control-normal males to control-ELA males [p = 0.036]).

Cytokines

Amygdala

PAE females exhibited elevated levels of several cytokines in the amygdala during both early and late adolescence. In early adolescence, PAE females showed higher levels of TNF-α and IL-10 compared to controls, regardless of rearing condition (Fig. 6a and e; significant main effect of prenatal treatment for TNF-α [F1, 27 = 8.94, p = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.23] and IL-10 [F1, 27 = 5.06, p = 0.033, ηp2 = 0.16]). Moreover, PAE-normal females displayed increased levels of IFN-ɣ (p = 0.02), IL-1β (p = 0.0003), and IL-6 (p = 0.035) in the amygdala relative to control-normal females (Fig. 6b,c,d; significant interaction between prenatal treatment and rearing condition for IFN-ɣ [F1,27 = 4.327 p = 0.047, ηp2 = 0.14] and IL-1β [F1,27 = 13.97, p = 0.0009, ηp2 = 0.34], a priori analysis comparing control-normal females to PAE-normal females for IL-6 [p = 0.035]). Control-ELA females had higher IL-1β levels compared to control-normal females (p = 0.03), and that PAE-normal females had higher IL-1β levels compared to PAE-ELA females (p = 0.007; Fig. 6c). Moreover, control-ELA females had higher IL-10 levels than control-normal females (a priori analysis comparing control-normal females to control-ELA for IL-10 [p = 0.05]). Similar patterns were observed in late adolescence, as PAE females exhibited higher levels of IFN-ɣ, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the amygdala compared to controls, regardless of rearing condition (Fig. 6k,l,m; significant main effect of prenatal treatment for IFN-ɣ [F1,30 = 6.58, p = 0.016, ηp2 = 0.18], IL-1β [F1,30 = 11.36, p = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.27], and IL-6 [F1,30 = 4.65, p = 0.039, ηp2 = 0.13]). In addition, a priori analysis indicated that PAE-normal females had higher amygdalar TNF-α levels compared to control-normal females (p = 0.05; Fig. 6j).

Hypothalamus

In early adolescence, PAE-normal females exhibited higher IL-10, but lower KC/GRO levels in the hypothalamus compared to control-normal females (Fig. 8e and g; a priori analysis comparing control-normal females to PAE-normal females for IL-10 [p = 0.0141] and KC/GRO [p = 0.0344]). In late adolescence, PAE females showed higher levels of IL-4 relative to controls, regardless of rearing condition (Fig. 8q; significant main effect of prenatal treatment for IL-4 [F1,30 = 4.457, p = 0.043, ηp2 = 0.013]). Furthermore, exposure to ELA decreased IL-13 levels in the hypothalamus, regardless of prenatal treatment (Fig. 8o; significant main effect of rearing condition for IL-13 [F1,30 = 5.58, p = 0.025, ηp2 = 0.16]).

Discussion

Given that individuals with PAE are at greater risk for exposure to ELA (Flannigan et al., 2021; Holman and Raineki, 2023; Price et al., 2017; Tan et al., 2022), we used two well-established rodent models to examine the independent and interactive effects of PAE and ELA on emotional regulation (i.e., anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors) and to investigate the potential modulatory role of neuroimmune function as an underlying mechanism during the early (P30) and late (P45) adolescence. Our findings revealed that PAE induced anxiety-like behaviors; however, these effects varied in an age- and sex-specific manner. Specifically, PAE females displayed anxiety-like behavior in the open field test during both early and late adolescence, whereas both PAE males and females exhibited anxiety-like behavior in the light-dark emergence test, but these effects were limited to early adolescence. Interestingly, during late adolescence, depressive-like behavior was reduced in PAE females. ELA produced mild behavioral effects, with only ELA males exhibiting anxiety-like behavior in the elevated plus maze during early adolescence. Moreover, ELA exposure increased depressive-like behavior in both sexes during late adolescence. Notably, ELA did not exacerbate the effects of PAE on either anxiety- or depressive-like behaviors across adolescence, suggesting that the PAE and ELA models used here may not fully replicate the patterns reported in the clinical literature, which demonstrate more severe impairments across multiple domains in individuals exposed to both PAE and ELA (Chandler-Mather et al., 2023; Coggins et al., 2007; Henry et al., 2007; McLachlan et al., 2016; Price et al., 2017; Rockhold et al., 2023).

Our assessment of immune markers showed that in females, PAE led to widespread alterations in cytokine levels within the amygdala during both early and late adolescence. PAE also affected cytokine levels in the hypothalamus of females, although these effects were more limited. In contrast, in males, the effects of PAE were more pronounced in the hypothalamus, with no significant changes observed in amygdalar cytokine levels. Conversely, ELA altered cytokine levels in the amygdala of early adolescent males, while its impact on females was minimal. Immune system dysregulation, including cytokine imbalances, has been proposed to underlie the association between exposure to early life stressors, such as PAE and ELA, and later mental health problems. By disrupting cytokines during critical periods of brain maturation, both PAE and ELA can render individuals vulnerable to later poor health outcomes. Our findings are in line with this hypothesis, as we observed a number of behavioral and brain cytokine changes following PAE and/or ELA that persisted across early and late adolescence, long after either exposure.

Effects of PAE and ELA on anxiety-like and depressive-like behavior

Individually, PAE and ELA have consistently been linked to the development of anxiety and depression in humans, as well as anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors in rodents (Baculis et al., 2015; Coles et al., 2022; Cullen et al., 2013; Gershon et al., 2013; O'Connor, 2014; Raineki et al., 2012; Wong et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2022). Our findings align with the broader literature, demonstrating that both PAE and ELA impact anxiety-like behaviors in a sex- and age-specific manner. Specifically, PAE females exhibited increased anxiety-like behaviors across multiple behavioral tests during both early and late adolescence. Early adolescent PAE females spent less time in the light compartment and engaged less in nosepoking and headpoking into the light compartment of the light-dark chamber, as well as less time spent in the center of the open field, compared to controls. In late adolescence, PAE females made fewer entries into the center of the open field and travelled less within the center compared to controls.

In males, PAE-induced behavioral changes were less consistent, with signs of increased anxiety-like behavior observed only in the light-dark emergence test, where PAE males spent less time headpoking into the light compartment during early adolescence. In contrast, results from the elevated plus maze suggested reduced anxiety-like behavior, as PAE males showed a shorter latency to nosepoke into the open arm during both early and late adolescence. Both the PAE-induced increased anxiety-like behaviors observed in the light-dark emergence test and the PAE-decreased anxiety-like behaviors observed in the elevated plus maze were reflected in measures of risk assessment (i.e., nosepoke and headpoke behaviors) and may not fully capture the complexities of anxiety-related behavior, as these specific measures reflect limited aspects of approach-avoidance conflict and exploratory motivation rather than generalized anxiety per se. Moreover, differences in outcomes across behavioral tests highlight the importance of employing a comprehensive behavioral test battery to obtain a more complete assessment of emotional dysregulation following early-life insults such as PAE and ELA.

Evaluation of depressive-like behaviors using the forced swim test revealed that ELA induced depressive-like behaviors during late adolescence, with control-ELA females spending more time immobile and control-ELA males reaching immobility more quickly than control-normal males. This ELA-induced increase in depressive-like behaviors is consistent with previous studies using the same ELA model, which reported similar effects during late adolescence (P45) in rats exposed to ELA (Raineki et al., 2012, 2015). The effects of PAE on depressive-like behavior during late adolescence differed by sex. PAE males showed increased depressive-like behavior, taking less time to reach immobility compared to controls, an effect consistent with prior findings in adult rats exposed to PAE (Brocardo et al., 2012; Raineki et al., 2016). In contrast, late adolescent PAE females took longer to become immobile and spent less time immobile than controls, replicating our findings in adult PAE females (Raineki et al., 2016). While this pattern could be interpreted as reduced behavioral despair, the meaning of immobility in the forced swim test has been increasingly debated (Anyan and Amir, 2018). Specifically, greater mobility may also reflect heightened anxiety or agitation, whereas immobility could represent an adaptive energy-conservation strategy in response to an inescapable and threatening situation (Anyan and Amir, 2018). With this in mind, the PAE-induced reduction in time spent immobile in females during late adolescence may reflect the strong anxiety-like profile observed across all other behavioral tests in the present study. Additional behavioral assessments of depressive-like states, such as the sucrose preference test or the novelty-suppressed feeding test, would help clarify whether the observed differences truly reflect altered depressive-like behavior, changes in stress coping strategies, or shifts in motivational and affective processes.

While there is a large body of literature demonstrating that, PAE and ELA are individually associated with increased vulnerability to anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors (Baculis et al., 2015; Coles et al., 2022; Cullen et al., 2013; Gershon et al., 2013; Hellemans et al., 2010; O'Connor, 2014; Raineki et al., 2012; Wade et al., 2022; Wong et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2022), few studies have examined the interactive effects of these insults on behavior during the adolescent period. Findings from the present study indicate that ELA did not amplify the behavioral effects of PAE on either anxiety- or depressive-like outcomes during adolescence. This suggests that the animal models employed here may not fully capture the interactions observed in clinical populations, where co-occurring exposure to PAE and ELA is consistently associated with more pronounced and widespread impairments across multiple functional domains (Chandler-Mather et al., 2023; Coggins et al., 2007; Henry et al., 2007; McLachlan et al., 2016; Price et al., 2017; Rockhold et al., 2023). However, in our previous study examining the independent and interactive effects of PAE and ELA on social recognition memory, we found that both PAE and ELA independently impaired social recognition during early adolescence (Holman et al., 2021). In contrast, among late adolescent males, ELA alone disrupted social recognition memory on the first test day, regardless of PAE exposure, but deficits persisted on the second test day only in animals exposed to both PAE and ELA. These findings suggest additive effects of PAE and ELA on social recognition memory in males (Holman et al., 2021). Importantly, differential neural activity within the “social behavior neural network” mediated the effects of PAE and/or ELA on social recognition memory (Holman et al., 2025). Together, these findings highlight that the independent and interactive impact of PAE and ELA may vary across behavioral domains, suggesting that the currently used animal models may reveal effects that depend on the specific neural or behavioral systems being evaluated.

In this study, we evaluated the effects of PAE and ELA during both early and late adolescence to capture developmental changes across this critical period (Schneider, 2013; Spear, 2000), when many mental health disorders begin to emerge in humans (de Girolamo et al., 2012; Paus et al., 2008). Our results indicate that some effects of PAE were enduring. Specifically, PAE females exhibited increased anxiety-like behavior in the open field at both ages, whereas PAE males showed reduced anxiety-like behavior in the elevated plus maze at both ages. In contrast, other behavioral effects of PAE were age-specific: both male and female PAE animals displayed increased anxiety-like behavior in the light-dark emergence test during early adolescence, but not during late adolescence. When examining the effects of ELA, we replicated previous findings showing that ELA-related increases in depressive-like behavior emerge only during late adolescence (Raineki et al., 2012). These age-dependent behavioral effects of PAE and ELA may reflect adolescent-related changes in gonadal steroid hormones (Vetter-O’Hagen and Spear, 2012), maturation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (McCormick and Mathews, 2007; Romeo, 2018), and/or neuroimmune function (Brenhouse and Schwarz, 2016), which could influence ongoing neurodevelopmental processes within brain regions that regulate emotionality, including the amygdala and hypothalamus.

Effects of PAE and ELA on amygdala and hypothalamus cytokine levels

The current study revealed that PAE and ELA, differentially impacted cytokine levels in the amygdala and hypothalamus in an age-, sex-, and region-specific manner. These findings align with previous literature showing that both PAE and ELA can negatively affect the development and function of the immune system, thereby influencing neurobehavioral function (Baker et al., 2023; Bodnar et al., 2016, 2020; Hartwell et al., 2013; Pascual et al., 2017; Raineki et al., 2017; Topper et al., 2015; Vella et al., 2025a, 2025b). In females, PAE induced a robust and enduring proinflammatory bias in the amygdala, with higher levels of TNF-α, IFN-ɣ, IL-1β, and IL-6 during both early and late adolescence compared to controls. This proinflammatory bias observed in the amygdala of PAE females during adolescence is in line with previous work identifying a proinflammatory bias in PAE animals in early life (Bodnar et al., 2016; Drew et al., 2015; Vella et al., 2025a) as well as in adulthood (Baker et al., 2022; Cantacorps et al., 2017), and together these findings provide an ontogenetic profile of PAE-induced immune alterations across development. The pleiotropic actions of TNF-α, IFN-ɣ, IL-1β, and IL-6 are essential for ongoing neuronal development and function; however, increased levels of these cytokines have been associated with neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and various disease states (Hewett et al., 2012; Kummer et al., 2021; Monteiro et al., 2017; Tonelli and Postolache, 2005). Alterations in the developing amygdala resulting from exposure to elevated proinflammatory cytokine levels, such as those observed in the current study, could have downstream effects on emotional regulation and behavioral responses later in life (Benarroch, 2015). Thus, the PAE-induced proinflammatory bias in the amygdala may underlie the anxiety-like phenotype observed in PAE females in both open field and light-dark emergence tests. This hypothesis, that immune alterations within the amygdala contribute to anxiety-like behaviors is further supported by the findings, in males, where PAE did not alter cytokine levels in the amygdala. Correspondingly, among the anxiety-like behavior measures, the only indication of increased anxiety in PAE males was a reduced duration of headpoking in the light-dark emergence test during early adolescence. In fact, in the elevated plus maze, PAE males actually exhibited reduced anxiety-like behaviors when considering risk-assessments behaviors such as latency to nosepoke into the open arm.

In females, PAE increased IL-10 levels in both the amygdala and hypothalamus during early adolescence; notably, we previously reported elevated amygdalar and hypothalamic IL-10 levels in PAE male and female rats during infancy (Raineki et al., 2017; Vella et al., 2025a). While IL-10 is an anti-inflammatory cytokine, it often rises concurrently with proinflammatory cytokines to mitigate immune-induced damage (Ouyang et al., 2011). Thus, the observed increase in IL-10 in PAE females could reflects an adaptive mechanism aimed at counteracting the heightened proinflammatory state induced by PAE. Moreover, IL-10 plays a key role in modulating chemokine expression during inflammatory responses, including the regulation of KC/GRO (Kim et al., 1998; Shanley et al., 2000). Accordingly, the decreased KC/GRO levels observed in the hypothalamus of PAE females during early adolescence may be a downstream effect of this increase in IL-10.

In early adolescence, PAE males exhibited reduced levels of TNF-α, IL-4, and IL-10 in the hypothalamus compared to controls, suggesting that PAE may broadly suppress both pro- and anti-inflammatory signaling in this region. While PAE is typically associated with increased cytokine levels, these effects are often region-specific, with some studies reporting decreases in certain brain regions, including the hypothalamus (Bodnar et al., 2016; Raineki et al., 2017). Effective immune function depends on a balanced coordination of both pro- and anti-inflammatory signaling, ensuring that responses remain proportional (Bilbo and Schwarz, 2012). Any shift away from this homeostatic balance can disrupt ongoing immune function and related developmental processes, making even subtle changes in cytokines particularly significant. In late adolescence, PAE-normal males showed increased levels of IL-13, IL-4, and IL-5 in the hypothalamus compared to control-normal males, indicating a shift from a blunted anti-inflammatory profile in early adolescence to an enhanced anti-inflammatory phenotype in late adolescence. While enhanced anti-inflammatory signaling can be protective as it can inhibit proinflammatory signaling pathways, excessive suppression of proinflammatory activity may reduce the ability of the immune system to respond to challenges (Cicchese et al., 2018). In the long term, this enhanced anti-inflammatory profile in the hypothalamus of the PAE males could subtly alter emotional reactivity or stress capacity.

The effects of ELA on cytokine levels in the amygdala and hypothalamus were mostly restricted to early adolescence in both males and females. In the amygdala, control-ELA females exhibited increased levels of IL-1β and IL-10, whereas in males, control-ELA males showed higher levels of IFN-ɣ, IL-10, IL-5, and IL-4 compared to control-normal males. In the hypothalamus, control-ELA males exhibited increased levels of IL-1β and IL-5 compared to control-normal males. The only effect of ELA observed in late adolescence was a reduction in IL-13 levels in the hypothalamus of both sexes compared to controls. This transient effect of ELA aligns with a meta-analysis examining the impact of different ELA models on brain cytokine levels (Lumertz et al., 2022), which reported that ELA-induced cytokine overexpression, primarily proinflammatory, was temporary and disappeared in older animals. Nevertheless, even in the absence of changes in basal cytokines, ELA is known to prime the immune system, such that when ELA-exposed animals encounter a secondary challenge or stressor, they may exhibit an exaggerated immune response (Dutcher et al., 2020).

Consistent with the behavioral effects, ELA did not exacerbate the impact of PAE on the immune system, partially replicating our previous findings that PAE animals did not exhibit an exaggerated amygdala cytokine response to ELA during infancy (P12; Raineki et al., 2017). Importantly, in the present study, there were many instances where, rather than showing increased responses to ELA, PAE animals exhibited an inappropriate or altered immune response to ELA compared to controls. For example, PAE-normal females showed increased amygdalar levels of IFN-γ and IL-6 during early adolescence, and PAE-normal males showed elevated hypothalamic levels of IL-13, IL-4, and IL-5 during late adolescence; however, in none of these cases did PAE-ELA animals display the same effects. Additionally, there were instances in which ELA increased cytokine levels in control animals but decreased them in PAE animals, as observed for IL-1β in the amygdala of early adolescent females and IL-5 in the amygdala of early adolescent males. Finally, there were cases where ELA had no effect on cytokine levels in control animals but reduced them in PAE animals, such as IFN-γ in the hypothalamus of early adolescent males. Overall, these differential responses indicate that, although ELA did not exacerbate the impact of PAE on behavioral responses, PAE animals exhibit a dysregulated or maladaptive immune response to ELA, suggesting an impaired ability to appropriately regulate cytokine signaling in the brain.

Conclusions and implications

Elucidating the contribution of adverse postnatal environmental factors to the heightened vulnerability to mental health problems observed in individuals with PAE, and understanding the underlying mechanisms, are critical steps toward developing targeted interventions and treatments for affected individuals. Overall, our results indicate that PAE females exhibited a stronger anxiety-like phenotype than PAE males, as several measures indicative of anxiety in the open field and light-dark emergence tests were affected in females, whereas only a single measure of risk assessment in the light-dark emergence test was altered in males. However, PAE males did show mild signs of depressive-like behavior in the forced swim test. Exposure to ELA also had a pronounced behavioral impact on females, who displayed depressive-like behavior in the forced swim test, whereas males exposed to ELA exhibited only mild signs of depressive-like behavior in the forced swim test. Importantly, PAE affected neuroimmune function in a sex- and region-specific manner. In females, PAE induced a strong proinflammatory bias within the amygdala, whereas in the hypothalamus, PAE altered both anti- and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in males and females. In contrast, the neuroimmune responses to ELA were milder than those induced by PAE, with both the amygdala and hypothalamus of males and females being similarly affected by ELA. Importantly, our results indicate that ELA did not intensify the behavioral or neuroimmune effects of PAE during adolescence. However, the numerous instances in which PAE animals displayed differential responses to ELA suggest that PAE disrupts the normal regulation of neuroimmune signaling, leading to a maladaptive or dysregulated response to environmental challenges. Together, these findings underscore that PAE not only produces lasting neuroimmune and behavioral alterations but also disrupts the brain's ability to adapt to later environmental challenges, highlighting the need for sex-specific and developmentally informed strategies to prevent and treat mental health problems in individuals with PAE.

Note

This work is dedicated to the memory of Seymour “Gig” Levine as part of a special issue celebrating what would have been his 100th birthday. Gig's pioneering research laid the foundation for the field of developmental psychobiology and transformed our understanding of how early-life experiences shape brain and behavioral development. Through his elegant studies on early handling and maternal care, Gig revealed that subtle variations in early experience can produce lasting changes in neuroendocrine and behavioral regulation, establishing a biological basis for the link between caregiving, stress responsivity, and emotional health. One of the authors (Joanne Weinberg) had the privilege of being directly mentored by Gig, and his mentorship continues to guide the questions we ask and the frameworks we use to interpret them. By integrating models of prenatal alcohol exposure with paradigms of early-life adversity, we investigate how prenatal and postnatal environments interact to influence neurodevelopment and behavior. This integrative perspective, viewing development as a dynamic dialogue between the organism and its environment, reflects the conceptual shift initiated by Gig and sustained through the generations of scientists he trained. His curiosity and vision continue to inspire and shape the questions we ask, the models we use, and our collective understanding of how experience sculpts the developing brain.
