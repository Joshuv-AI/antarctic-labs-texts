Ruxolitinib Versus Best Available Therapy in Patients With Steroid-Refractory Acute Graft-Versus-Host Disease: Final Analysis From the Randomized Phase III REACH2 Trial
Authors: Mohamad Mohty, Gerard Socié, Jeff Szer, Dietger Niederwieser, Jason Butler, Eva Wagner-Drouet, Reuven Or, Tsila Rovenvald-Zuckerman, Sinem Civriz Bozdag, Edouard Forcade, Giovanni Grillo, Nicolaus Kröger, Friedrich Stölzel, Domenico Russo, Jaime Sanz, Rajendra Sarkar, Tommaso Stefanelli, Celine Wilke, Robert Zeiser, Nikolas von Bubnoff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractApproximately 30%-50% of patients develop acute graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD) after allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (Allo-HCT), representing a major limitation of this treatment. Although corticosteroids remain the standard first-line therapy for aGVHD, up to 50% of patients become steroid-refractory (SR). REACH2 is a phase III study of ruxolitinib versus best available therapy (BAT) in patients age 12 years and older with SR-aGVHD after Allo-HCT. We present the final efficacy and safety outcomes from REACH2 after 24 months of treatment. Cumulative median (range) duration of response was 167 (22-677) days with ruxolitinib and 106 (10-526) days with BAT. Median overall survival and event-free survival were 10.7 and 8.3 months for ruxolitinib, compared with 5.8 and 4.2 months, respectively, with BAT. Median failure-free survival was significantly longer with ruxolitinib than with BAT (4.86 v 1.02 months, P < .001). Similar numbers of nonrelapse mortality events were observed with ruxolitinib and BAT (72 v 71), and malignancy relapse/progression events remained low across both groups. Numerically higher chronic GVHD rates were noted with ruxolitinib than with BAT from 12 months; however, 95% confidence intervals overlapped. Safety observations were consistent with the primary analysis results. Ruxolitinib provided efficacy advantages over BAT in patients with SR-aGVHD over 24 months.

INTRODUCTION

Acute graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD) is an inflammatory disorder developing in 30%-50% of allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplant (Allo-HCT) recipients.1 Corticosteroids constitute the standard first-line treatment2 and are associated with significant toxicities and side effects.3 Up to 50% of patients become steroid-refractory (SR) or dependent (D).3,4 For patients with SR-aGVHD, a nonrelapse mortality (NRM) rate of 47.4% at 5 years has been observed,5 indicating the unmet need for effective second-line treatments.

JAK1/2 signaling promotes GVHD through effects mediated by T cells,6 dendritic cells,7 and neutrophils.8 These cell types contribute to aGVHD upon activation by alloantigen, pathogen-associated molecular patterns,9 and damage-associated molecular patterns.10,11 Ruxolitinib, a JAK1/2 inhibitor, provided a significantly higher overall response rate (ORR) at 28 days and a longer duration of response (DOR) than best available therapy (BAT) in the phase III REACH2 trial conducted in patients with SR aGVHD.12 Ruxolitinib is recommended by the European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation for patients with SR/D-aGVHD and chronic GVHD (cGVHD).2 Efficacy and safety of ruxolitinib in SR-aGVHD after Allo-HCT over 24 months of REACH2 and in patients who crossed over to ruxolitinib (final analysis) are presented.

METHODS

Study Design

Study design, end points, and statistical analysis for REACH2 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02913261) have been reported previously.12 Briefly, 309 patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to ruxolitinib (10 mg twice daily) or BAT. Patients who received BAT could cross over to ruxolitinib if unresponsive at day 28, had lost their response, or required additional systemic therapy. Patients who discontinued treatment entered long-term follow-up.

End Points and Assessments

DOR, overall survival (OS), event-free survival (EFS), failure-free survival (FFS), NRM, incidence of malignancy relapse/progression (MR), incidence of cGVHD, cumulative corticosteroid dosing, and safety were analyzed up to 24 months after random assignment in the full analysis set. Subgroup analysis of ORR (primary end point at day 28) and sensitivity analyses for ORR, DOR, EFS, FFS, and NRM (secondary endpoints) were performed.

Distribution of OS was estimated by the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method; hazard ratio (HR) was calculated using a stratified Cox model. OS was further investigated using a multivariate Cox regression model, a rank-preserving structural failure time model (RPSFTM), and landmark analysis to investigate the influence of prognostic factors, crossover, and response, respectively. A time-dependent Cox regression model was used to assess the impact of occurrence of cGVHD on OS. Quality of life (QoL) was assessed by the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Bone Marrow Transplant (FACT-BMT) and EQ-5D-5L instruments until end of treatment (EOT).

Statistical analyses are reported in the Data Supplement (online only). Data cutoff date (last patient, last visit) was April 23, 2021.

Efficacy

DOR was numerically longer with ruxolitinib than with BAT (cumulative median [range]: 167 [22-677] v 106 [10-526] days). The probability of disease progression or addition of systemic therapy for aGVHD was lower with ruxolitinib than with BAT at 6 months (8.4% v 37.3%) and maintained at 12 months (9.7% v 37.3%) and 18 months with ruxolitinib (11.1% v not estimable; Data Supplement, Fig S1 and Table S1).

KM-estimated median OS was 10.7 months for ruxolitinib compared with 5.8 months with BAT (P = .28; Fig 2A). Patients with grade 2 aGVHD benefitted most from ruxolitinib with HRs (95% CI) for OS of 0.61 (0.36 to 1.05) for grade 2, 0.97 (0.64 to 1.46) for grade 3, and 1.01 (0.53 to 1.94) for grade 4 (Data Supplement, Table S2). OS associations were similar in multivariable analyses and analyses adjusting for crossover (Data Supplement, Fig S2, and Tables S3 and S4).

Median EFS was 8.3 months with ruxolitinib and 4.2 months for BAT (HR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.64 to 1.13]; P = .2723; Fig 2B). Patients with grade 2 aGVHD benefitted most from ruxolitinib with HRs (95% CI) for EFS was 0.72 (0.43 to 1.19) for grade 2, 0.92 (0.61 to 1.39) for grade 3 and 0.92 (0.49 to 1.74) for grade 4 (Data Supplement, Table S5).

Median FFS was significantly longer with ruxolitinib than with BAT (4.86 months v 1.02 months; HR, 0.51 [95% CI, 0.39 to 0.66]; P < .0001; Fig 2C). There were fewer FFS events with ruxolitinib than with BAT (Data Supplement, Table S6). Individual components of FFS contributed differently to overall FFS for each arm (Data Supplement, Fig S3).

The number of NRM events was similar between arms (ruxolitinib: 72 [46.8%]; BAT: 71 [45.8%]) and the cumulative incidence curves overlapped, suggesting comparable incident rates over time (Data Supplement, Fig S4 and Table S7). The competing risk (hematologic disease relapse/progression) was low in both arms (ruxolitinib: 13.6%; BAT: 16.1%; Data Supplement, Table S7).

The probability of MR was low in both arms (ruxolitinib: 20/147 [13.6%]; BAT: 25/147 [17.0%]; Data Supplement, Table S1).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary analysis (Data Supplement Results, and Table S8 and S9).

cGVHD was evident in patients in both arms (ruxolitinib: 52 [33.8%]; BAT: 34 [21.9%]); most events were mild (ruxolitinib: 35 [22.7%]; BAT: 16 [10.3%]). Median onset time was longer with ruxolitinib (217.5 days) than with BAT (185.0 days). The estimated cumulative incidence rate of cGVHD increased with time in both arms with numerically higher rates noted for ruxolitinib compared with BAT from 12 months (Data Supplement, Table S7), which was also evident after adjustment for treatment exposure, although the 95% CIs overlapped (Data Supplement, Table S10). OS remained in favor of ruxolitinib after adjustment for cGVHD occurrence (HR, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.64 to 1.15]). Patients with cGVHD tended toward longer survival than those without cGVHD (HR, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.43 to 1.25]).

At day 56, more patients had tapered off corticosteroids with ruxolitinib (22.1%) than with BAT (14.8%), which increased to 43.5% and 31.6% (odds ratio 1.67 [95% CI, 1.05 to 2.65]), respectively, by EOT, and 77.3% and 74.2%, respectively, had achieved a ≤50% dose reduction. The overall mean reduction in corticosteroid dose was greater with ruxolitinib than with BAT (ruxolitinib: –61.6%; BAT: –50.7%; Data Supplement, Table S11).

Quality of Life

By week 24, QoL improvements were more pronounced in patients receiving ruxolitinib than those receiving BAT. Benefits in the crossover population reflected those of the randomized ruxolitinib arm (Data Supplement, Fig S5).

On-treatment deaths occurred in 43 (28.3%) and 36 (24.0%) patients on ruxolitinib and BAT, respectively. The most common cause in both arms was aGVHD. Adverse events of special interest (AESIs) were generally higher with ruxolitinib than with BAT (Data Supplement, Table S12). No new safety concerns were observed.

DISCUSSION

The long-term outcomes from REACH2 reported here confirm the primary analysis.12 Over 24 months, ruxolitinib was associated with significantly longer FFS and numerically longer EFS, especially for patients with grade II aGVHD, compared with BAT. Although this trial was not designed to detect a significant effect of ruxolitinib on OS, collectively, outcomes from additional OS analyses with multivariate Cox regression and a RPSFTM suggest a potential but limited benefit with ruxolitinib. The numerically higher incidence of cGVHD with ruxolitinib should be interpreted with caution and requires further study. The safety profile of ruxolitinib was consistent with the primary analysis12 and its known AEs14; any differences in AE rates compared with the primary analysis reflect the extended follow-up period and exposure duration. Although precautionary methods may be required to manage some AESIs, QoL was improved more with ruxolitinib than with BAT. Overall, the benefit-risk ratio for ruxolitinib remains favorable and supports its use in patients age 12 years and older with SR-aGVHD.
