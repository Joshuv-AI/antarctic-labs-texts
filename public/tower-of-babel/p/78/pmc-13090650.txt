Dynamic properties of an SARS-CoV-2 epidemic model via stochastic PINNs
Authors: Linfeng Xie, Jikai Yang, Zhiming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper introduces a novel stochastic SEIRV model for investigating the spread of the SARS-CoV-2 epidemic using stochastic physics-informed neural networks (S-PINNs). We first prove the global positivity of solutions via Lyapunov functions and Itô’s formula. Then, persistence and extinction properties are analyzed by a threshold. The S-PINNs algorithm integrates noise into the loss function for seamless data-driven and physics-based learning. Finally, the algorithm based on the SEIRV model is applied to SARS-CoV-2 data from Austria, Switzerland, and Belgium, and it outperforms PINNs, LSTMs, and Logistic models, especially on noisy data.

Introduction

Infectious diseases continue to pose serious threats to human health and socio-economic stability in the context of globalization and high population mobility. During the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, the world experienced significant excess mortality (Almeida et al., 2024; Wakefield et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022), and life expectancy declined in 29 countries in 2020 (Aburto et al., 2022), with some countries experiencing decreases not seen since World War II (Islam et al., 2021). Studies have shown that SARS-CoV-2 not only caused direct deaths but also produced indirect harm through delayed treatment of chronic diseases, healthcare resource shortages, and economic stagnation (Barbiellini Amidei et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024). In addition, long-term sequelae (“Long COVID”) have had profound impacts on individual health, labor markets, and healthcare systems (Davis et al., 2021). These results indicate that the harms of infectious diseases are long-term and systemic, going far beyond infections and mortality alone (Karlinsky & Kobak, 2022).

In mathematical epidemiology, the classical SIR model, proposed by Kermack and McKendrick, provides a foundational framework to understand epidemic thresholds and wave dynamics (Kermack & McKendrick, 1927). Subsequently, an exposed compartment (E) was introduced to form the SEIR model, which is better suited to describing diseases with incubation periods, such as SARS-CoV-2. With vaccination becoming a key measure for epidemic control, the SEIRV model was further developed (Gupta et al., 2024). Recent studies have demonstrated that SEIRV and its extensions can be applied to assess the effects of different vaccination coverages, waning immunity, reinfection mechanisms, and heterogeneous contact patterns on epidemic spread (Ali et al., 2025; Tridane et al., 2023). For example, SEIRV frameworks have been used to analyze multivaccine strategies and vaccination sequences to optimize epidemic control (Santos Junior & Gondim, 2024). Other studies have investigated the roles of waning immunity and age structure in recurrent epidemic waves (Garnier et al., 2024; Gomes et al., 2024). These studies demonstrate that SEIRV models play a critical role in bridging theoretical modeling with public health policy.

Taking into account the dissemination of information during a disease outbreak, various social media platforms utilize diverse channels to distribute details regarding symptoms, preventive strategies, and therapeutic options, while also enhancing awareness among governmental bodies and the general populace through educational initiatives. The government enacts regulatory measures proportional to the severity of the epidemic, recommending that individuals adopt appropriate protective measures. Hence, governmental policies and societal responses are pivotal in mitigating disease propagation (Saha et al., 2022).Let the sociological parameter η represent the intensity of government intervention, and D and K denote the dynamic changes in social behavior and the degree of public response, respectively. Under reasonable assumptions that 0 ≤ η < 1 and 0≤D<1, the transmission rate can be expressed as (1−η)(1−D)Kβ, which describes the process by which susceptible individuals transition to the infected state. Incorporating these factors, we establish the following deterministic SEIRV model:(1)dSt=Λ−1−η1−DKβStIt−μSSt−ϵStdt,dEt=1−η1−DKβStIt−αEEt−μEEtdt,dIt=αEEt−μIIt−γIItdt,dRt=γIIt−μRRtdt,dVt=ϵSt−μVVtdt,where the constant Λ represents the recruitment or birth rate into the susceptible, accounting for population influx. The transmission rate β quantifies the probability of disease transmission from infected to susceptible individuals upon contact, typically under the bilinear incidence assumption. The natural mortality rates for each compartment are specified as μS, μE, μI, μR, and μV, reflecting demographic losses independent of the disease. In reality, the spread of infectious diseases is subject to stochasticity, including fluctuations in contact patterns, uncertainty in transmission rates, and variability in the implementation of interventions. Consequently, researchers have incorporated stochastic perturbations into SEIR/SEIRV models to investigate extinction probabilities, stationary distributions, and threshold corrections (Jiang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2024). Environmental stochastic variations, including temperature and weather fluctuations, cause model parameters to vary unpredictably. From a biological standpoint, these uncontrollable, random environmental factors significantly alter infectious disease transmission trajectories, as exemplified by the influenza virus exhibiting enhanced transmissibility during declining temperatures, thereby modifying conventional disease propagation patterns. Consequently, infectious disease models incorporating environmental noise perturbations provide more accurate representations of disease dynamics than deterministic models. We posit that environmental noise critically affects each state in the model (2), assuming that each population group, as a cohesive unit, is collectively influenced by these environmental disturbances (Khan et al., 2019, 2021; Wang et al., 2023). Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have been widely used in SARS-CoV-2 modeling by embedding epidemiological dynamics into neural networks (Galasso et al., 2024; Millevoi et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023). Recent studies highlight their strengths in parameter estimation, state reconstruction, multi-wave forecasting, and intervention optimization, particularly under time-varying transmission, waning immunity, and complex controls. For example, Millevoi et al. (Millevoi et al., 2024) inferred time-varying SIR parameters from early Italian COVID-19 data in low-data regimes. Han et al. (Han et al., 2024) captured Germany's epidemic dynamics and trends via SIR-PINNs. Qian et al. (Qian et al., 2025) achieved superior 1–4 week forecasts for California using cases, hospitalizations, deaths, vaccination, and mobility data, outperforming data-driven models when modeling time-varying effects. Cheng et al. (Cheng et al., 2025) enhanced SEIRV temporal predictions with DINNs, showing robust performance under noise. Stochastic extensions, such as SPINODE (O'Leary et al., 2022) and physics-informed GANs (Yang et al., 2020), further enable uncertainty-aware modeling of noisy epidemic systems. These methods excel in data-scarce, non-stationary, and multi-intervention settings by tightly fusing mechanistic knowledge with observations, improving interpretability and reliability. Therefore, stochastic epidemiological models based on advanced PINNs should be considered to offer powerful tools for epidemic modeling and public health decision-making.

Based on the above analysis, we focus on the dynamic properties of the stochastic model (3) via a stochastic PINN. The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we investigate the existence and uniqueness, ergodicity, and extinction properties of the stochastic model (3), with the main theoretical results presented in Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Theorem 3. To enhance comprehension, we conduct numerical simulations in Section 3 to validate the theoretical findings presented earlier. Our main results demonstrate that model (3) exhibits excellent fitting properties and biological significance. Furthermore, in Section 4, we introduce the Stochastic PINNs (S-PINNs) algorithm and integrate the stochastic SEIRV model. In Section 5, based on SARS-CoV-2 data from Austria, we compare the S-PINNs algorithm with PINNs, LSTMs, and logistic models to demonstrate its effectiveness in handling noisy data. A brief conclusion is provided in Section 6.

Dynamic properties

Based on the stochastic SEIRV model (3), we systematically investigate the dynamical properties of the model in this section. The existence and uniqueness of positive solutions for the stochastic system ensure mathematical rigor and biological interpretability. The ergodic properties of the model focus on the existence of stationary distributions and the long-term behavior. Finally, we study the conditions for disease extinction, determining the parameter ranges for which stochastic perturbations lead to disease elimination. These theoretical analyses provide a mathematical foundation for understanding the impact of environmental noise on disease transmission, offering a basis for formulating effective control strategies.

In the process of conducting numerical simulations, we employ the control variable method, systematically modifying the parameters η, D, and K to investigate the impact of governmental interventions, public self-protective behaviors, and the level of societal awareness regarding infectious diseases on epidemic transmission. These parameters respectively represent the intensity of governmental intervention, the dynamic control capacity of public self-protective actions, and the degree of societal response to the disease. By isolating the effects of these target parameters while holding other variables constant, we were able to focus on their specific influences. Simulations demonstrate that integrated governmental interventions and public protective measures effectively interrupt epidemic transmission and reduce infections, providing a theoretical foundation for public health policy.

Stochastic physics-informed neural networks based on SEIRV model

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), introduced by Raissi et al. (Raissi et al., 2019), embed physical laws into deep learning by computing partial differential equation residuals through automatic differentiation and incorporating them into the loss function. This enables simultaneous fitting to sparse observational data and the strict satisfaction of governing equations, achieving effective data-physics integration. PINNs provide mesh-free discretization, low data requirements, and unified handling of forward and inverse problems. However, standard PINNs display clear limitations under strong noise. Mean-squared error losses are highly sensitive to non-Gaussian noise and outliers, easily causing noise overfitting. Data and physics residual terms often exhibit severe imbalance, especially in inverse problems, leading to biased parameter estimates. Automatic differentiation often amplifies noise, resulting in spurious oscillations or nonphysical solution behavior. Training stability decreases markedly, and convergence becomes difficult. Although PINNs show great promise in domains with well-defined physical priors and scarce data, substantial enhancements are still essential to ensure reliable performance in engineering applications dominated by complex, realistic noise.

Stochastic Physics-Informed Neural Networks (S-PINNs) is an extension of the PINNs algorithm that incorporates noise consideration. This algorithm innovatively integrates the powerful approximation capabilities of deep learning with stochastic process modeling, effectively capturing and quantifying the inherent stochastic dynamics and uncertainties in stochastic differential equations. Next, we integrate S-PINNs with the stochastic SEIRV model.

In the initialization phase, we employ a randomization strategy to configure the neural network's weight matrices and bias vectors, as well as the epidemic model's transmission rate β and recovery rate γ. These parameters constitute the fundamental elements of the entire learning framework and are continuously refined through optimization algorithms during subsequent iterative training procedures. Let w be the neural network weights and b be the bias. The training procedure adopts a multi-layered, nested-loop architecture. The outer loop governs the total number of training epochs, where each epoch represents a comprehensive traversal and learning phase over the complete training dataset. This design ensures that the model can fully utilize all available training information. Within each training epoch, the algorithm implements a specifically designed inner loop, whose iteration count is determined by the Monte Carlo sampling number NMC (Galasso et al., 2024). The primary function of this inner loop is to systematically capture and model various stochastic factors and uncertainty sources inherent in epidemic transmission processes by introducing different random noise perturbations and repeatedly executing numerical simulations of the stochastic epidemic model.

During each Monte Carlo iteration, the algorithm utilizes the current time point t as an input variable, processing it through the neural network's forward propagation mechanism to ultimately generate numerical predictions for the state variables in the model, including key epidemiological indicators such as S, E, I, R, and V. This can be mathematically formulated as:S,E,I,R,V=NN(t;w,b).Here, NN denotes the forward propagation function of the neural network in the black dashed section of Fig. 4. The parameters w and b are initialized using Xavier and zero initializations in TensorFlow. Subsequently, the algorithm applies Euler-Maruyama discretization to compute the subsequent values of S, E, I, R, and V based on stochastic differential equation terms and stochastic noise. The discrete update equations for each compartment are expressed as:St+1=St+Λ−(1−η)(1−D)KβS(t)I(t)−μSS(t)−ϵS(t)Δt+σ1ΔtStdBt,Et+1=Et+(1−η)(1−D)KβS(t)I(t)−αEE(t)−μEE(t)Δt+σ2ΔtEtdBt,It+1=It+αEE(t)−μII(t)−γII(t)Δt+σ3ΔtItdBt,Rt+1=Rt+(γII(t)−μRR(t))Δt+σ4ΔtRtdBt,Vt+1=Vt+ϵS(t)−μVV(t)Δt+σ5ΔtVtdBt.

The algorithm computes the discrete loss MSESDE for each temporal interval. This loss quantifies the variance among consecutive values of S, E, I, R, and V, facilitating the assessment of approximation error when discretizing continuous-time SDEs via the Euler-Maruyama scheme. The MSESDE is formulated as:MSESDE=1M∑i=1M(‖Si+1−Si‖2+‖Ei+1−Ei‖2+‖Ii+1−Ii‖2+‖Ri+1−Ri‖2+‖Vi+1−Vi‖2),where M denotes the total temporal steps. During Monte Carlo sampling, a repository maintains compartment values (S, E, I, R, and V) following each iteration. This repository documents the compartmental evolution across iterations and serves as the foundation for computing the mean solution of the stochastic framework. Upon completion of sampling, the algorithm derives the mean for each compartment from the stored values. This constitutes the stochastic model solution, offering enhanced stability in compartment value estimates by reducing stochastic fluctuations. To assess model performance, the algorithm computes a composite loss function combining data loss (MSESEIRV) and residual loss (MSEresidual). The data loss quantifies discrepancies between model predictions (S, E, I, R, V) and empirical observations (S0, E0, I0, R0, V0). This is expressed as:MSESEIRV=1N∑i=1N(‖S−Si‖2+‖E−Ei‖2+‖I−Ii‖2+‖R−Ri‖2+‖V−Vi‖2),where N indicates the observation count per compartment, corresponding to collected temporal instances. The residual loss MSEresidual represents the mean MSESDE across all Monte Carlo iterations. This loss quantifies deviations between SDE-based compartment evolution predictions and empirical observations:MSEresidual=1NMC∑i=1NMCMSESDE,where NMC represents Monte Carlo iteration count. The total loss function aggregates data loss (MSESEIRV) and residual loss (MSEresidual):Loss=MSESEIRV+MSEresidual.The algorithm uses the Adam optimizer (Torku et al., 2023), a widely adopted optimization technique, to update weights, biases, and dynamic parameters. The Adam optimizer modifies parameters based on the gradients of the loss function. Through iterative parameter updates via the optimizer, the algorithm seeks to minimize the loss function and enhance model prediction accuracy.

In our computational framework, the neural network architecture accepts a singular input parameter, designated as t. The network comprises multiple hidden layers, where inter-nodal connections are characterized by weight matrices W[i, j]. Here, i denotes the origin node index, while j represents the destination node index. The hyperbolic tangent activation function is implemented at each node within the hidden layers; that is, tanh(x) = (ex − e−x)/(ex + e−x). This activation mechanism transforms the input parameter x to a bounded interval spanning −1 to 1, thereby incorporating nonlinear characteristics into the neural architecture. By using tanh as the activation function across hidden-layer nodes, the network demonstrates the ability to capture sophisticated patterns and identify intricate data relationships. For the network's output nodes, we use the sigmoid activation function σ(x) = 1/(1 + e−x) to normalize S(t), E(t), I(t), R(t), and V(t). This activation mechanism transforms input x to values constrained between 0 and 1, thereby facilitating probability representation or normalized value ranges. The S-PINNs architecture comprises four hidden layers, each containing 64 neurons.

Overall, S-PINNs performs exceptionally across the datasets from all three countries, accurately fitting trends under low noise while maintaining low error levels in high-noise conditions, underscoring its advantages in integrating physical constraints and noise-handling mechanisms. As a foundational physics-informed neural network, PINNs excels in capturing physical constraints but remains sensitive to noise; the observed MAPE decline under high noise may stem from the model shifting toward more generalized smooth predictions, mitigating overfitting prevalent in low-noise scenarios. LSTM, as a recurrent network, is adept at modeling long-term dependencies but prone to noise-induced fluctuations; in cases like Austria, the MAPE reduction could result from high noise compelling more robust pattern learning, whereas deterioration in Switzerland reveals inherent instability. The Logistic model, as a classical parametric framework, features simplicity and smoothness, often outperforming complex models under high noise; its widespread MAPE decline can be attributed to innate resistance to noise, eschewing local fluctuations in favor of aligning with overarching S-shaped trends. According to the literature, such as in (Yin et al., 2023), MAPE as a scale-sensitive metric tends to amplify relative errors when data include small values or low noise, whereas increased noise may homogenize or enlarge actual value distributions, thereby reducing MAPE values—a common phenomenon in time-series forecasting, particularly when models like Logistic favor underestimation. Similarly, Vivas et al., (2020) discussed MAPE reductions when accounting for sources of variation, implying that noise as a variability factor can enhance relative accuracy through averaging effects.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a novel stochastic SEIRV model, based on the S-PINNs algorithm, to study the dynamics of infectious disease transmission. The research introduces stochastic perturbations to deterministic models, providing a more realistic reflection of the uncertainty and complexity of infectious disease transmission in the real world.

We constructed a stochastic SEIRV system that accounts for environmental noise and random fluctuations, dividing the population into five compartments with Brownian perturbations. By constructing appropriate Lyapunov functions and applying Itô’s formula, we rigorously proved the global positivity of solutions, ensuring the biological reasonableness. Applying ergodic theory, we analyzed the long-term behavioral characteristics of the stochastic system, proved the ergodic properties of the disease, and established criteria for disease persistence and extinction based on the threshold value R0s. Moreover, we provided an analytical expression for the stationary distribution. Numerical simulations employed the high-precision Milstein higher-order method to verify theoretical predictions through carefully designed parametric experiments. The results demonstrate that by appropriately adjusting government control intensity and public response parameters, disease transmission dynamics can be effectively controlled, resulting in significantly reduced infection peaks and a shorter epidemic duration. The numerical experiments fully illustrate the crucial role of vaccination strategies in epidemic prevention and control, providing a quantitative basis for formulating optimal vaccine allocation strategies.

To evaluate the actual application performance of the model, we proposed an innovative data-physics fusion hybrid algorithm, called S-PINNs, which represent an essential extension based on classical PINNs, with the core innovation being the explicit introduction of stochastic noise terms in the loss function, enabling the network to more reasonably learn and capture the noise characteristics and uncertainties commonly present in real epidemic data. By embedding the physical constraints of stochastic differential equations into the loss function, we achieved organic integration of data-driven learning and physical law constraints. The application to actual SARS-CoV-2 epidemic data from Austria, Switzerland, and Belgium validated the theoretical framework. By comparing the predictive performance of PINNs, LSTMs, and classical Logistic growth models across different noise levels, our model demonstrated comparable or even superior predictive accuracy, particularly excelling at predicting noisy data. This suggests that the proposed method possesses strong generalization capabilities and can be applied to other infectious diseases in complex environments.

In conclusion, the stochastic SEIRV model, combined with the S-PINNs-based hybrid method proposed in this study, provides a powerful and innovative framework for research on infectious disease dynamics. This framework not only has a rigorous mathematical foundation theoretically but also demonstrates excellent predictive performance in practical applications, offering more precise and reliable scientific support for the formulation of public health policies. Despite significant progress, several challenges remain to be addressed in future work, considering the model's assumptions and constraints. For instance, omitting an explicit R compartment could lead to a systematic overestimation of the susceptible population size, thereby underestimating the vaccination coverage threshold required for herd immunity or overestimating the sustained infection levels at the endemic equilibrium. Similarly, if infection exerts a significant adverse effect on fertility that is not accounted for in the model, it may underestimate the disease's profound impacts on population structure and long-term transmission dynamics. To assess the robustness of these assumptions and enhance the model's real-world explanatory power, future extensions could include: adopting a more comprehensive SEIRV framework; incorporating waning immunity mechanisms; explicitly modeling fertility reduction induced by infection, complemented by thorough sensitivity analyses to systematically quantify the specific effects of these factors on key predictive indicators. The emergence of viral variants may alter transmission dynamics, vaccine efficacy may fluctuate over time or with viral mutations, and the model has not fully incorporated environmental and behavioral factors. These limitations necessitate further investigation through the integration of multi-strain competition submodels or the incorporation of real-time genomic sequencing data to enhance the model's robustness and applicability.
