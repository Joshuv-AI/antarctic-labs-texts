The acute effect of squat exercises performed at different depths on muscle activation and jump performance: a randomized crossover trial
Authors: Beste Serter, Yusuf Coşar, Ahmet Akkoyun, Fatih Gür
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examined the acute effects of different squat depths (partial, parallel, full) on lower extremity muscle activation and countermovement jump (CMJ) performance.MethodsFourteen healthy sports science students participated in this randomized crossover study. Participants performed three sets of squats at their 5-repetition maximum (5RM) load across three different depths. Surface electromyography (sEMG) was recorded from the vastus lateralis (VL), rectus femoris (RF), and biceps femoris (BF) muscles during each set, while CMJ performance was assessed before and after the squat protocols.ResultsBoth mean and peak activation values of all muscles increased significantly with greater squat depth (p < 0.001). The highest activation levels in the RF and BF muscles occurred during the full squat, whereas VL activation peaked during both the full and parallel squat conditions. Regarding CMJ performance, a significant improvement between pre-test and post-test values was found only after the full squat condition (p = 0.011); however, the overall magnitude of change (ΔCMJ) did not show a significant difference among the three squat depths (p = 0.268).ConclusionIncreasing squat depth positively affects lower extremity muscle activation. While full squats provide the greatest neuromuscular stimulation and can serve as an effective stimulus for acute post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE), they should not be considered statistically superior to partial or parallel squats in terms of acute jump performance gains.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT07543237. Background

The squat exercise is a fundamental movement that strengthens the hip and knee extensors and can enhance athletic performance in athletes [1]. During the squat, both monoarticular and biarticular muscles are activated, and the level of muscular force varies depending on joint angles [2]. Performing squats at different joint angles has been shown to influence jump performance [3], power development [4] and muscle activation [5]. The execution depth of the squat is primarily determined by the degree of knee flexion. Based on movement depth, squats are generally classified into three main categories: partial squats (approximately 40° knee flexion), parallel squats (approximately 90° knee flexion), and full squats (greater than 100° knee flexion) [1].

Performing squats at different knee joint angles leads to variations in muscle activation. However, the literature presents conflicting findings regarding these activation patterns. In studies comparing muscular activation between full squats and parallel squats (approximately 90° knee flexion), inconsistent results have been reported. One study demonstrated greater activation of the hip and knee extensors during the full squat [6], whereas another study found that partial squats elicited higher activation in the hip extensors [2]. Additionally, one investigation reported higher vastus lateralis activation during the concentric phase of the full squat, while the parallel squat produced greater activation of the rectus femoris, biceps femoris, and vastus lateralis during the eccentric phase, and greater gluteus maximus activation during both phases [7]. In contrast, another study found no significant difference in the activation of hip and knee extensors between full and parallel squats [8]. In a study examining muscle activation during parallel and partial squats, parallel squats showed higher rectus femoris activation, while there was no difference in biceps femoris and gastrocnemius activation [9]. There are a limited number of studies examining muscle activation during three squat depths. At three squat depths, the full squat movement showed higher gluteus maximus activation in the concentric phase, while the partial squat showed higher vastus medialis activation [10].

Neuromuscular fatigue can impair physical performance and increase the risk of injury [11, 12]. Continuous monitoring of neuromuscular fatigue is therefore essential for regulating training load and minimizing injury risk [13]. During and following resistance training, fatigue is commonly assessed through various physiological and perceptual indicators such as blood lactate concentration, heart rate, ratings of perceived exertion (RPE) [14], jump performance [13], and electromyography (EMG) activity [15]. Previous studies investigating muscle activation at different squat depths have primarily focused on EMG activity recorded during a single set. However, to date, no studies have examined EMG responses across multiple sets of squats performed at varying depths (partial, parallel, and full) to track potential fatigue-related changes. Surface electromyography (sEMG) is a widely used, non-invasive method for monitoring and identifying muscle fatigue by detecting the electrical activity generated during muscle contractions [16].

Research on the acute effects of squat depth on jump performance has produced mixed findings. While some studies have reported no significant differences in jump performance across varying squat depths [17, 18], others have demonstrated higher jump heights with deeper squats [19, 20]. Moreover, to date, no study has investigated the use of countermovement jump (CMJ) performance to assess fatigue across three distinct squat depths. The CMJ test is considered a reference standard due to its high validity and reliability in monitoring neuromuscular fatigue [13].

To address the gaps identified in the literature, the present study evaluated muscle activity using sEMG during squat sets performed at three different depths and assessed CMJ performance before and after the sets among students enrolled in a faculty of sports sciences. The objectives of this study were: (a) to compare the effects of partial, parallel, and full squat movements on muscle activation, and (b) to compare the effects of partial, parallel, and full squat movements on CMJ performance before and after the squat sets, and (c) to assess the onset of depth-specific acute neuromuscular fatigue across multiple sets by monitoring changes in electromyography (EMG) amplitudes. It was hypothesized that: (a) deeper squat movements (parallel and full) would elicit higher muscle activation compared to the partial squat, (b) deeper squat movements (parallel and full) would result in greater CMJ performance compared to the partial squat, and (c) acute neuromuscular fatigue, appearing as a gradual decrease in EMG amplitude across sets, will be more pronounced during partial squats due to the heavier absolute loads used.

Methods

Experimental approach to the problem

A randomized crossover design was employed to evaluate differences in muscle activation and jump performance among partial, parallel, and full squat exercises performed with equivalent relative external loads. Each participant completed four laboratory sessions, separated by 48–72 h of rest to avoid carry-over fatigue.

For each participant, five-repetition maximum (5RM) testing was conducted separately for back squats at each squat depth: partial (≈ 45° knee flexion), parallel (≈ 90° knee flexion), and full (≈ 140° knee flexion). The range of motion at the knee joint was measured using a goniometer, and all participants performed the exercises at a self-selected tempo under each condition. Participants were instructed to maintain a consistent pace throughout all sets and conditions in order to minimize speed-related EMG fluctuations, and the pace maintained during all sets and conditions was monitored by the same researcher. The absolute external loads lifted during the 5RM test conducted under different conditions (mean ± SD) were measured as 99.64 ± 24.84 kg for the partial squat, 76.43 ± 20.98 kg for the parallel squat, and 50.71 ± 17.85 kg for the full squat.

sEMG signals were recorded from the vastus lateralis (VL), rectus femoris (RF), and biceps femoris (BF) muscles to assess neuromuscular activation. CMJ performance was measured to evaluate jump height before and after the exercise conditions.

Research group

Fourteen students from the Faculty of Sports Sciences at Pamukkale University voluntarily participated in the study. The mean age, height, and body mass of the participants were 22.6 ± 2.21 years, 166 ± 9.36 cm, and 65.10 ± 14.60 kg, respectively. The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1 software with a statistical power of 0.80, a significance level of α = 0.05, and a medium effect size (f = 0.25). Inclusion criteria were: being 18–35 years of age, having at least one year of resistance training experience, and being familiar with partial, parallel, and full squat techniques. Exclusion criteria included having a chronic illness, a current or recent musculoskeletal injury, or experiencing pain during any of the three squat variations. Participants were instructed to refrain from resistance training for 72 h before and throughout the testing period. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participant recruitment and data collection were conducted between April 2025 and May 2025. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Pamukkale University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee. Furthermore, to ensure methodological transparency, the trial was registered retrospectively on ClinicalTrials.gov (Identification Number: NCT07543237). This randomised controlled trial was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) extension for crossover trials. The completed CONSORT checklist is provided as a supplementary file [21]. The flow of participants through the crossover trial is illustrated in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1). All procedures were performed in the Performance Laboratory and the Indoor Sports Hall of the Faculty of Sports Sciences at Pamukkale University following ethical approval.

Procedure

During the first session, participants completed a standardized warm-up protocol consisting of both general and specific components. The general warm-up included 5 min of low-intensity treadmill running, followed by 5 min of lower-limb mobilization exercises as the specific warm-up. After the warm-up, a maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) test was performed. During the MVIC tests, participants were instructed to contract the target muscle “as fast and as hard as possible.” Five minutes after completing the MVIC tests, the 5RM loads were determined for the three squat depths. The initial test load was selected based on the participant’s training history, with the aim of selecting a weight that they could theoretically lift for exactly 5 repetitions. A failed attempt was defined as the inability to complete the 5th repetition throughout the required range of motion for a specific squat depth, a noticeable deterioration in lifting technique, or the need for physical assistance. If the true 5RM could not be achieved on the first attempt, the load was increased by 4–10 kg following a 3-minute passive rest period between attempts. This process continued until the true 5RM—defined as the maximum weight lifted for exactly 5 repetitions with flawless technique—was confirmed. To minimize neuromuscular fatigue, a maximum of three attempts was permitted for each condition, and a 10-minute rest interval was provided between testing the three squat depths. The order of the 5RM tests was randomized across participants.

In sessions 2, 3, and 4, the same standardized warm-up protocol was applied, followed by a pre-test assessment of jump performance. The random allocation sequence was generated by the same investigator using a computer-based random number generator. Participants then performed each squat depth (partial, parallel, and full) in a randomized and counterbalanced order on separate days, with 48–72 h of rest between sessions. The same investigator enrolled the participants and assigned them to their respective intervention sequences. Each condition consisted of three sets of 5RM, with a 3-minute rest interval between sets (Fig. 2). During all squat variations, participants maintained a stance slightly wider than shoulder width, with toes pointed forward or slightly outward [2]. A post-test assessment of jump performance was conducted 5 min after completing the final set. This 5-minute rest interval was selected based on the existing literature on post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE); as this duration provides an ideal timeframe for the resolution of acute neuromuscular fatigue while ensuring that the enhancing effects remain at a sufficiently high level to maximize subsequent jump performance [22]. All testing sessions were carried out at the same time of day (between 9:00 AM and 12:00 PM) by the same researcher.

Measurements

Surface electromyography measurement

A wireless sEMG system (Trigno Sensor System, Delsys Inc., Natick, MA, USA; electrode spacing = 10 mm; common mode rejection ratio = 80 dB) was used to record lower-limb muscle activity. EMG data were sampled at 2000 Hz, and both MVIC and exercise trials were processed using EMGworks® analysis software.

To ensure optimal electrode-skin contact and reliable EMG recordings, participants were instructed to shave their legs prior to testing. The skin was then cleaned with 99.9% isopropyl alcohol before electrode placement. EMG sensors were positioned on the dominant limb along the longitudinal axes of the muscle fibers according to the Surface Electromyography for the Non-Invasive Assessment of Muscles (SENIAM) protocol:

Whilst the vastus lateralis and rectus femoris muscles are the primary movers responsible for knee extension, the biceps femoris muscle has been selected because it acts as an important auxiliary muscle and joint stabilizer during the descent and ascent phases of the squat movement [24, 25].

Electromyographic signals recorded during all squat conditions (partial, parallel, and full) were normalized to the maximum EMG amplitude obtained during the MVIC performed on an isokinetic dynamometer (Cybex). Two 5-second MVIC trials were performed for each muscle of the dominant leg, with a 1-minute rest interval between contractions. The first trial was used for familiarization, and the second for data analysis. For the VL and RF MVICs, participants were seated with the torso, hands, and legs stabilized, the hip positioned at 90° flexion, and the knee between 90° and 45° flexion. When instructed, participants pushed “as fast and as hard as possible” against a static resistance for 5 s, receiving verbal encouragement from the same investigator. For the BF MVIC, participants were positioned prone with the hip in neutral (0° extension) and the knee at 45° flexion. They were instructed to flex the knee as quickly and forcefully as possible against a static resistance for 5 s to maximally activate the biceps femoris [24].

Countermovement Jump (CMJ) measurements

CMJ performance was evaluated by measuring jump height. Two CMJ trials were performed before and two after each session, and the highest value was used for analysis. A one-minute rest interval was provided between jumps to minimize fatigue effects. Jump height was measured using the My Jump 2 mobile application. The My Jump 2 app has been shown to exhibit a very high correlation (r = 0.99) and excellent reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.992–0.994) with validated jump mat systems for assessing jump height [25].

Statistical analysis

All 14 randomized participants completed the crossover trials and were included in the final statistical analyses. All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. The three squat depths for each muscle were treated as fixed factors, and differences in muscle activation were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). In addition, to assess acute neuromuscular fatigue, the between-set differences in EMG activation across three sets were analysed using a repeated measures ANOVA. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs test was applied to compare differences in jump performance. In addition, differences in the magnitude of change (ΔCMJ) across three different squat depths were assessed using a repeated measures ANOVA. When a significant main effect or interaction (F) was observed, the Bonferroni post hoc test was performed to determine pairwise differences. Effect size was calculated using partial eta squared (ηp²), and its magnitude was classified according to Cohen’s thresholds as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (0.14) [26]. Furthermore, for pairwise comparisons, the magnitude of differences was evaluated using Cohen’s d effect sizes, calculated from pooled standard deviations, and interpreted as small (0.2), medium (0.5), and large (0.8) [27]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using the SPSS statistical software package (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

This study investigated the effects of squat depth (partial, parallel, and full) on lower-limb muscle activation. The results revealed that as squat depth increased, both mean and peak activation values of the quadriceps (rectus femoris and vastus lateralis) and hamstring (biceps femoris) muscles increased significantly. For the rectus femoris, both mean activation (F(2.26) = 37.77, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.744) and peak activation (F(2.26) = 30.35, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.700) exhibited a strong increase with greater squat depth. A similar pattern was observed in the vastus lateralis, where mean activation (F(2.26) = 16.42, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.558) and peak activation (F(2, 26) = 26.10, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.668) both increased significantly. In the biceps femoris, mean activation (F(2.26) = 9.94, p = 0.001, ηp² = 0.433) and particularly peak activation (F(2.26) = 25.19, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.660) were significantly higher at greater depths (Table 1). Overall, squat depth had a substantial effect on muscle activation, with all muscles reaching their highest activation levels in the full squat condition. These findings suggest that performing squats at greater depths elicits more intense neuromuscular engagement across both quadriceps and hamstring muscles.

The results of the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test for CMJ performance at different squat depths revealed no statistically significant differences between pre-test and post-test values in the partial squat (Z = − 0.21, p = 0.833, r = 0.06) and parallel squat (Z = − 1.24, p = 0.216, r = 0.33) conditions. In contrast, a significant improvement was observed following the full squat condition (Z = − 2.55, p = 0.011, r = 0.68) (Table 2). These findings suggest that performing squats through a full range of motion may be particularly effective in enhancing lower-limb explosive strength. The large effect size further indicates a practically meaningful and clinically relevant improvement in performance.

To directly evaluate the second hypothesis and assess the comparative effects of squat depth on jump performance, the change in CMJ height (ΔCMJ = Post-test – Pre-test) was analysed across the three squat variations. Despite the full squat condition produced the greatest absolute increase (+ 2.13 cm), followed by the parallel (+ 1.11 cm) and partial (+ 0.49 cm) squats, the comparison of ΔCMJ across the three squat depths did not show any statistically significant differences (p = 0.268). Consequently, while full squats uniquely elicited significant pre-to-post improvements, the magnitude of this enhancement was not statistically greater than those observed at the other squat depths.

The Bonferroni pairwise comparison results (Table 3) revealed significant differences in muscle activation levels depending on squat depth. In the VL muscle, both peak and mean activation values differed significantly between the partial squat and the parallel and full squat conditions (p < 0.01). The highest activation was observed during the full squat, whereas no significant difference was found between the parallel and full squat. For the RF muscle, squat depth had a pronounced effect on both peak and mean activation values. Partial squats elicited significantly lower activation than both parallel and full squats (p < 0.01). Moreover, a significant difference in mean RF activation was observed between the parallel and full squat conditions (p = 0.005), indicating that RF activation progressively increased with squat depth. In the BF muscle, both peak and mean activation values were significantly higher during the full squat compared with the partial and parallel squat conditions (p < 0.01). However, the difference between partial and parallel squats was not statistically significant (peak p = 0.070; mean p = 0.332).

In the assessment of neuromuscular fatigue, pairwise comparisons between the average EMG activation per set and various squat depths were analysed (Table 4). A significant decrease in muscle activation during the set was observed only during the partial squat protocol. In the partial squat, a significant decrease was observed in the RF muscle between the 1st and 3rd sets (p < 0.00) and between the 2nd and 3rd sets (p = 0.048). Similarly, in the VL muscle during the partial squat, a significant decrease was observed between the 1st and 2nd sets (p = 0.012) and between the 1st and 3rd sets (p < 0.001). In contrast, no significant decrease was observed between sets in any muscle group under the parallel and full squat conditions (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The primary purpose of this study was to examine the effects of three squat depths on muscle activation and CMJ performance. The findings demonstrated a significant increase in both mean and peak activation of the quadriceps (rectus femoris and vastus lateralis) and hamstring (biceps femoris) muscles as squat depth increased. Moreover, a significant improvement in CMJ height was observed only in the full-squat condition, suggesting that depth-specific adaptations may enhance lower-limb explosive performance.

Consistent with previous research, the present findings indicate that greater squat depth imposes a higher neuromuscular load on the lower-limb musculature [6, 7, 9]. For the vastus lateralis muscle, the lowest mean and peak activation levels were observed during partial squats, whereas parallel and full squats produced comparable activation. Therefore, in cases where an athlete demonstrates limited mobility or when the training objective is to minimize excessive fatigue while maintaining performance, the parallel squat may serve as a suitable alternative to the full squat. In the rectus femoris muscle, the lowest mean and peak activation occurred during partial squats, whereas the highest activation levels were observed during full squats. The rectus femoris is a biarticular muscle that contributes to both hip flexion and knee extension [1]. As squat depth increases, hip flexion also increases markedly, thereby elevating tension in the rectus femoris and necessitating the recruitment of a greater number of motor units. Particularly in the full-squat condition, the combination of greater hip flexion and deeper knee angles elicits higher activation of the rectus femoris due to its dual-joint function [7, 28, 29].

On the other hand, the vastus lateralis (VL), a monoarticular muscle, primarily contributes to knee extension. The greatest torque production capacity at the knee joint occurs at approximately 90° of flexion, corresponding to the parallel squat position [30]. Because the parallel squat represents this optimal joint angle, VL activation is already substantially elevated. Although knee flexion increases further during the full-squat movement, the mechanical advantage of the quadriceps decreases, thereby limiting torque production. Consequently, VL activation during the full squat remains comparable to that observed during the parallel squat, showing no significant additional increase [10, 29, 31, 32]. The BF also demonstrated higher mean and peak activation during the full squat compared with the partial and parallel squat conditions. This finding highlights the critical role of hamstring coactivation in stabilizing both the knee and hip joints at greater knee flexion angles.

In line with our third hypothesis, the set-by-set EMG assessment highlighted a depth-specific emergence of acute neuromuscular fatigue. A notable and continuous reduction in the average activation of both the rectus femoris and vastus lateralis muscles was noted solely during the partial squat protocol. This swift onset of neural fatigue in partial squats can predominantly be linked to the extraordinarily heavy absolute weights lifted at this constrained depth [33]. Lifting supramaximal external resistance places a significant load on the central nervous system and local motor unit recruitment pools; this leads to a more rapid depletion of neural drive and, subsequently, a decrease in EMG amplitude across multiple sets [34]. Conversely, the parallel and full squats, which demand lighter absolute loads due to the expanded range of motion and mechanical disadvantage, did not produce notable set-by-set neural reductions [33, 35]. Therefore, while partial squats permit athletes to bear greater absolute weights, they also provoke a quicker onset of acute neuromuscular fatigue, an essential element that strength and conditioning specialists must take into account when designing training volume.

Our findings on jump performance are consistent with previous studies, which have reported that jump height increases as squat depth increases. In terms of jump performance and fatigue, CMJ height did not decrease following any squat depth protocol. The lack of a decline in performance calls for a deeper physiological interpretation, especially through the lens of the fitness-fatigue model. Acute explosive performance represents the net balance between potentiating mechanisms and induced neuromuscular fatigue [36]. The stability of CMJ performance across varying depths indicates that the 5RM loading regimen, coupled with the allotted recovery intervals, did not induce severe or prolonged peripheral fatigue. Although our set-by-set EMG assessment highlighted acute localized neural fatigue—particularly as a result of the supramaximal loads in the partial squat—this localized deficit seemingly did not reach a threshold sufficient to impair the multi-joint force generation necessary for the CMJ. It is quite likely that the rest intervals facilitated the effective dissipation of transient metabolic fatigue, allowing potential PAPE-related mechanisms to counterbalance any lingering neuromuscular impairments [37]. Although a significant improvement was observed between pre- and post-test conditions following the full squat condition alone, a direct comparison of the magnitude of change (ΔCMJ) across the three different depths revealed no statistically significant difference (p = 0.268). Therefore, we cannot assert that the full squat is strictly superior to the partial or parallel depths in terms of enhancing acute jump performance.

Whilst our findings are consistent with research supporting depth-specific neuromuscular advantages, it is important to acknowledge that there is no complete consensus in the literature. Some previous studies have not observed a significant difference in quadriceps or hamstring activity at different squat depths [10, 22]. These inconsistencies can largely be attributed to methodological differences, particularly the load-balancing strategies employed. For instance, when activities are conducted at the same relative intensity (e.g., maximum exertion for a particular depth), the remarkably high absolute loads lifted during partial squats might produce a neural stimulus comparable to that seen in deeper squats. Additionally, variations in the training backgrounds of participants, biomechanical technique, and the length of rest periods may clarify why the depth-specific advantage identified in our protocol was absent in certain studies. Recognizing these contradictory results underscores the intricate relationship between mechanical load, range of motion, and absolute external load.

Nonetheless, the most significant absolute growth (+ 2.13 cm) noted following the complete squat indicates it might offer a beneficial trigger for a potential PAPE-related response. The PAPE effect is generally linked to processes such as increased muscle temperature, changes in myosin phosphorylation, and enhanced motoneuron excitability [38]. While we did not directly evaluate these particular physiological processes, the notably elevated average and peak activation levels of the quadriceps and hamstring muscles during the complete squat likely provide a more robust neural stimulus [39]. From a mechanical standpoint, heightened tension within the muscle-tendon unit during a complete squat may also improve elastic energy utilization [40]. However, since these fundamental processes were not directly assessed in our research, these performance results should be regarded with caution as a functional observation rather than conclusive mechanistic proof.

In practical terms, these findings have important implications for strength and conditioning professionals. Athletes aiming to improve explosive lower-body performance may benefit more from incorporating full squats into their training programs. However, individual factors such as fatigue status, technical proficiency, and mobility level should be carefully considered when designing training cycles. Performing full squats with proper technique and under controlled conditions is essential, particularly because the mechanical load on the knee and hip joints increases substantially at greater depths. Although partial squats can be useful for developing specific strength adaptations under supramaximal loads and at limited ranges of motion, they appear to be less effective in transferring to dynamic movements that require extensive joint motion and high power output.

One limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size and the focus on acute responses; this may limit the generalisability of the results. Furthermore, although all participants were sports science students, we did not classify them by specific sports or primary training levels. Individuals with experience in explosive sports (e.g. athletics, volleyball) may exhibit different neuromuscular adaptations and PAPE results compared to those with endurance or different sporting backgrounds; this may have contributed to the inter-individual variability observed in our findings. As a result, the current findings may not be directly relevant to specialized elite athletes, sedentary individuals, or those experiencing acute or chronic musculoskeletal issues. Another methodological limitation is that reliability measures (e.g., intraclass correlation coefficient or coefficient of variation) were not presented for the maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) assessments. Despite following a strictly standardised MVIC protocol, the lack of inter-trial reliability data prevents the EMG normalisation baseline from being fully statistically validated. Future studies should overcome these limitations by involving broader and more diverse sample groups, examining long-term changes related to different squat depths, and systematically reporting reliability metrics for all neuromuscular baseline assessments.

In conclusion, the present findings demonstrate that squat depth exerts a significant influence on muscle activation, eliciting the greatest neuromuscular demands in the RF and VL muscles during the full squat. While a significant enhancement in CMJ performance was exclusively observed after the full-squat variation, the lack of significant differences in the overall magnitude of change (ΔCMJ) across the three squat depths necessitates a cautious interpretation. Therefore, even though the full squat might serve as a potent stimulus for acute performance improvement, it should not be considered statistically superior to partial or parallel squats.

Conclusions

Squat depth has a significant effect on the activation of lower extremity muscles. As squat depth increases, particularly during a full squat, it creates greater neuromuscular demands on the musculature. While a significant improvement in jump performance was exclusively noted after performing the full squat, the total magnitude of change (ΔCMJ) did not show significant differences across the three squat depths. Consequently, although the full squat may act as an effective stimulus for acute performance enhancement, it should not be labeled as categorically superior to partial or parallel squats in this context. In practice, coaches and researchers need to meticulously assess an individual’s mobility, injury history, technical proficiency, and fatigue levels when recommending specific squat depths.
