Incidence and predictors of hyperkalemia after subtotal parathyroidectomy in pediatric chronic dialysis patients with secondary hyperparathyroidism: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yanina Martinez, Martin Vilches, Silvia Gil, Marta Adragna, Laura Cristina Lopez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHyperkalemia is a life-threatening electrolyte complication reported sporadically in adults on chronic dialysis and secondary hyperparathyroidism (SHPT) after parathyroidectomy (PTX), but has never been recorded in pediatric patients. This study aimed to estimate the incidence, explore potential risk factors and early predictive markers associated with its occurrence in children.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study that included 21 pediatric patients on chronic dialysis and SHPT who underwent subtotal PTX at a single pediatric center. Patients were divided into two groups based on the development of hyperkalemia. Preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative biochemical data were analyzed. Univariate comparison and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to explore risk factors. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the predictive value of potassium levels.ResultsThe cumulative incidence of postoperative hyperkalemia was 42.8%. No preoperative risk factors were identified. However, ROC analysis showed that intraoperative potassium (cutoff: 4.83 mEq/L, AUC: 0.95) and potassium at 6 h postoperatively (cutoff: 5.46 mEq/L, AUC: 0.90) were strong predictors.ConclusionsPostoperative hyperkalemia is a frequent, yet underappreciated, complication in pediatric patients on chronic dialysis undergoing subtotal PTX. Intraoperative and early postoperative potassium levels demonstrated high predictive capacity and may help guide early risk stratification and timely intervention. Larger multicenter studies are required to validate these findings.

Background

Hyperkalemia is a common and potentially life-threatening electrolyte disorder in chronic dialysis patients. Parathyroidectomy (PTX) is effective and often required for refractory secondary hyperparathyroidism (SHPT) [2, 3]. Among the complications related to this procedure, hungry bone syndrome (HBS) and hyperkalemia have been described. The latter with an incidence of 25–80% in adult patients [3, 4] but has never been reported in pediatric patients.

Children on dialysis may present unique susceptibilities due to differences in body composition, extracellular fluid distribution, and bone metabolism during growth. However, the epidemiology and determinants of postoperative hyperkalemia in this population remain unknown.

Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the incidence of postoperative hyperkalemia and explore potential risk factors and early predictive markers associated with its occurrence in children.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Hospital de Pediatria J.P Garrahan, Buenos Aires, Argentina, between January 2010 and March 2024. A total of 25 medical records were reviewed; 21 patients under 18 years with chronic kidney disease (CKD) on hemodialysis (HD) or peritoneal dialysis (PD) and SHPT who underwent subtotal PTX were included. Four patients were excluded due to pre-existing hyperkalemia (serum potassium > 5.5 mEq/L within 24 h before anesthesia induction). Follow-up was performed 24 h before and after successful subtotal PTX.

Data collection

Serum potassium was measured once at each predefined perioperative time point: preoperative (the day before surgery), intraoperative, 6 h and 24 h after surgery. Preoperative and 24-hour postoperative samples were obtained from the central venous catheter in HD patients prior to dialysis and from a peripheral vein in PD patients. Intraoperative and 6-hour postoperative samples were obtained peripherally in both groups. Samples reported as hemolyzed by the hospital’s central laboratory were discarded and repeated.

Hyperkalemia was defined as a single serum potassium value > 5.5 mEq/L at any of the time points. Severity was classified as mild (5.5–6.5), moderate (6.5–7.0), or severe (> 7.0 mEq/L or with ECG changes/symptoms). Cases were managed according to KDIGO guidelines [1]. All patients underwent dialysis within 24 h before and after surgery. General anesthesia was induced with non-depolarizing agents. Heparin was used during routine hemodialysis sessions the day before PTX. No patient received potassium-sparing diuretics, angiotensin II receptor blockers, or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole in the perioperative period.

The following variables were recorded and explored as risk factors: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), dialysis modality (HD/PD), time on dialysis, residual diuresis, presurgical potassium and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels [2–4]. Additionally, acid–base parameters (pH, bicarbonate), interdialytic weight gain (IDWG) and the etiology of CKD were also assessed as a potential risk factor. Exposure to angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI), beta-blockers, and bicarbonate supplementation were collected from perioperative records. Dietary adherence and dialysis adequacy parameters (Kt/V, URR) were not systematically available, as most patients underwent chronic dialysis at external centers and were referred solely for surgery. Hormonal assays (aldosterone, renin activity) were also unavailable.

Intraoperative and early postoperative (6-hour) potassium levels were evaluated separately as potential predictors of hyperkalemia using ROC curve analysis. Complications related to the procedure and hyperkalemia were documented.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.0) with RStudio (2025.9.0.387).

Hyperkalemia incidence was assessed as cumulative incidence, defined as the occurrence of hyperkalemia after PTX among patients at risk of presenting this event.

Quantitative variables were reported as mean (± SD - Standard Deviation) or median [interquartile range - IQR] according to distribution (assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test); qualitative variables as absolute counts and percentages.

Patients were divided into two groups based on postoperative hyperkalemia. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Quantitative variables were compared using Student’s t-test when normally distributed and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test when non-normally distributed (two-sided). To explore potential confounding by indication, the same approach was applied to compare preoperative pH and bicarbonate levels between patients with and without bicarbonate supplementation. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Univariate comparison was first used to explore candidate risk factors. Multivariable logistic regression was then performed including a limited set of clinically relevant variables (age, dialysis modality, residual diuresis) to minimize overfitting, given the small number of events (n = 9). Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Firth’s penalized logistic regression was also applied to reduce small-sample bias.

ROC curves were generated to evaluate the predictive value of preoperative, intraoperative and early postoperative (6 h) potassium levels. The area under the curve (AUC) with 95% CI was estimated by bootstrap resampling (1,000 replicates). Optimal cut-off values were identified using the Youden index.

Results

Description of study population

Among the 21 patients included, 15 were male (71.4%). The median age was 14 years, and the median dialysis time was 4 years. HD was the predominant modality (71%, n = 15), while 29% were on PD. Residual diuresis was present in 33% (n = 7). The etiologies of CKD were congenital anomalies of the kidneys and urinary tract (CAKUT) in 38% (n = 8), glomerulopathies in 33% (n = 7), autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD) and unknown cause in 9.5% (n = 2), Alport syndrome and cystinosis in 5% (n = 1).

Incidence, complications and treatment of hyperkalemia

The cumulative incidence of hyperkalemia in patients on chronic dialysis after PTX was 42.8% (n = 9). Mild hyperkalemia was the most common (46%, n = 4), followed by severe (33%, n = 3) with a maximum potassium value of 7.61 mEq/L. No patients experienced severe clinical complications. Management included standard measures; one patient required emergency dialysis. All patients developed HBS after surgery, characterized mainly by marked hypocalcemia.

Except for bicarbonate supplementation, no significant differences were observed between groups in the variables studied, including CKD etiology (p = 0.85). Bicarbonate supplementation was more frequent in patients with hyperkalemia (p = 0.046). However, preoperative acid–base status was similar between supplemented and non-supplemented groups: mean pH 7.34 (SD ± 0.03) vs. 7.32 (SD ± 0.07) (p = 0.55), and median serum bicarbonate 22 mEq/L [IQR 20–23] vs. 22 mEq/L [IQR 20.5–22.8] (p = 0.91). These findings suggest prescription bias rather than a true association.

ROC analysis

ROC curve analysis demonstrated strong predictive capacity for intraoperative and 6 h postoperative potassium levels (Table 3; Fig. 1). Intraoperative potassium showed excellent discrimination for postoperative hyperkalemia, with an AUC of 0.95 (95% CI 0.87–1.00) and an optimal cut-off value of 4.82 mEq/L (sensitivity 89%, specificity 92%). Potassium measured at 6 h postoperatively also demonstrated good discrimination, with an AUC of 0.90 (95% CI 0.77–1.00) and an optimal cut-off of 5.46 mEq/L (sensitivity 67% and specificity 100%).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first pediatric study evaluating hyperkalemia after subtotal PTX in patients with SHPT on chronic dialysis. We found a cumulative incidence of 42.8%, comparable to that described in adults, highlighting that this complication is clinically relevant and may be underrecognized in children.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain postoperative hyperkalemia. A sudden decline in PTH after PTX causes a rapid influx of calcium into the bone. This leads to a rapid influx of sodium into the intracellular space through activation of the calcium-sodium exchanger, followed by dysregulation of the sodium-potassium ATPase pump and an increase in extracellular potassium [3]. Additionally, PTH itself also influences intracellular potassium distribution, favoring hyperkalemia, particularly in anuric patients [3]. However, in our cohort, residual diuresis did not modify risk (p = 0.64).

Regarding risk factors, none of the following variables— age, sex, BMI, residual diuresis, PTH levels, and CKD etiology —were associated with hyperkalemia, consistent with prior adult studies [2–5]. Additional variables in our cohort, including acid–base status (pH, bicarbonate), IDWG, and exposure to ACEI or beta-blockers, also showed no significant difference. The paradoxical association with bicarbonate supplementation likely reflected prescription bias, as preoperative acid–base values were comparable between supplemented and non-supplemented patients (pH p = 0.55; bicarbonate p = 0.91). This suggests that bicarbonate use was more a marker of underlying vulnerability (e.g., metabolically fragile or less adherent patients) rather than a causal risk factor.

Compared to adults reports, Yang et al. [4] found significantly higher preoperative PTH levels in hyperkalemic patients (median: 1622 pg/mL vs. 1358 pg/mL). In our study, as in others [2, 5], no such association was observed. Xi Zhu et al. [5] reported longer dialysis duration in patients who developed hyperkalemia (7.6 ± 5.2 years) compared to controls (5.7 ± 2.4 years), but similar to Yang et al. [4], we did not observe a significant difference (median: 4 years in both groups). In the same study, 43% of patients were on PD, and none developed hyperkalemia. This was not replicated in our cohort, where 22% (n = 2) of PD patients did experience the event.

The multivariate analysis by Yang et al. [4] identified presurgical potassium as an independent risk factor for postoperative hyperkalemia. Additionally, studies by Sijia Li et al. [2] and Xi Zhu et al. [5] reported significantly higher presurgical potassium levels among hyperkalemic patients, with cut-off values of 4.4 and 4.57 mEq/L, respectively. In our cohort, this association was not observed: the mean preoperative potassium was 4.42 mEq/L in both groups (p = 0.99), and ROC analysis showed poor discrimination (AUC 0.57). In contrast, intraoperative and early postoperative (6 h) potassium levels demonstrated strong predictive capacity for hyperkalemia (AUCs 0.95 and 0.90; cut-offs 4.83 and 5.46 mEq/L, respectively). These thresholds may serve as practical clinical tools to guide early monitoring, repeat measurements, timely medical therapy (insulin–glucose, resins, bicarbonate), or even anticipatory dialysis in high-risk patients.

Pediatric patients differ physiologically from adults in body composition, extracellular fluid distribution, nutritional status and bone metabolism during growth. Although these differences could increase susceptibility to potassium shifts, they were not evident in this cohort. Future studies including control groups, such as pediatric patients undergoing PTX without CKD or other surgical populations, may help distinguish PTX-specific from nonspecific perioperative potassium changes.

This study had limitations: it was retrospective, single center, and included a small sample size with a low events-per-variable ratio, which limited statistical power and rendered our analyses exploratory. Data on dialysis adequacy and dietary adherence were not available, and selection bias was possible, as many patients were referred solely for surgery. Nevertheless, the study highlights a clinically important complication and identifies intraoperative and early postoperative potassium as promising early markers in pediatric dialysis patients.

Conclusion

Postoperative hyperkalemia is a frequent, yet underappreciated, complication in pediatric patients on chronic dialysis undergoing subtotal PTX. Intraoperative and early postoperative potassium levels demonstrated strong predictive capacity and may serve as practical tools for early monitoring and timely intervention in high-risk patients. Future multicenter studies are required to validate these findings and determine their clinical applicability.
