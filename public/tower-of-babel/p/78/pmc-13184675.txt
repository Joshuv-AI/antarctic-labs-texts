Vacuolar Membrane Protein 1 and Transmembrane Protein 41B in Action: At the Right Place, With the Right Strength
Authors: Xiao-Ming Yin
Published: 2026

Phospholipid scramblases are a class of transmembrane proteins that facilitate the movement of phospholipids between the inner and outer leaflets of membrane bilayers in a bidirectional, energy-independent manner, to regulate their distribution to ensure proper membrane dynamics for the function of the cell, organelles, or the vesicles where lipid exchange or signaling occurs.1

Vacuolar membrane protein 1 (VMP1) and Transmembrane Protein 41B (TMEM41B) are phospholipid scramblases involved in multiple physiological functions.2,3 In the classic scenario where deletion of VMP1 or TMEM41B causes failure of autophagosome (AV) formation and subsequent inhibition of autophagy, the scramblase activities at the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) location are critical.4 AV maturation requires a specific lipid transferase, ATG2, to move lipids from other membrane compartments, including the ER, to the initial isolation membrane. The resulting lipid imbalance across the 2 leaflets is corrected by an autophagy-specific scramblase ATG9A at the AV membrane.5,6 ATG9A deletion causes defects in autophagosome formation. VMP1 and TMEM41B, on the other hand, may act at the ER because ATG9A cannot rescue the AV abnormality caused by the loss of TMEM41B or VMP1.4 It is possible that the transfer of lipids from ER to AV membranes also leads to lipid imbalance at the ER membrane, which may prevent a sustained lipid transfer without the scramblase activity of VMP1 or TMEM41B at the ER site. These scramblases may coordinate their actions at the ER and AV sites through protein-protein interactions. VMP1 and TMEM41B can directly bind to ATG2, whereas TMEM41B also interacts with ATG9A depending on ATG2.7

For the intriguing role of VMP1 and TMEM41B in the hepatic secretion of very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL),2,3,8 their action seems to occur at the mitochondria-associated membrane as shown by Chen et al in a paper published in this issue of Cellular and Molecular Gastroenterology and Hepatology.9 This study shows that, when VMP1 or TMEM41B is deleted in the liver, not only is VLDL secretion impaired, but VLDL maturation is also arrested in the ER, leading to significant steatohepatitis. Furthermore, these molecules are reduced in the livers of mice with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and in human patients with MASH.2,8 The mouse MASH pathology can be corrected by overexpressing TMEM41B or VAM1, further indicating a role for these molecules in MASH pathogenesis. Moreover, this study identifies these scramblases on the mitochondria-associated membranes and their roles in maintaining ER-mitochondria contact, which is critical for the synthesis of phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). Thus, the deletion of VMP1 or TMEM41B in the liver significantly reduced the level of PC and PE but elevated the level of neutral lipids. PC and PE, as well as the fatty acyl chain compositions of these lipids, are essential for the assembly and secretion of VLDL, and VMP1 and TMEM41B seem to ensure the proper supply of these phospholipids.

VMP1 and TMEM41B can form a complex and may function together.4 By comparing the phenotypes of the hepatic deficiency of the 2 molecules and the phenotypes of the mutual rescue, Chen et al have also made the novel findings regarding their functional disparity. They found that deletion of either VMP1 or TMEM41B is sufficient to impair autophagy and VLDL secretion. The double knockout (KO) does not further exacerbate the phenotype. In addition, VMP1 overexpression in Tmem41b KO mice and TMEM41B overexpression in Vmp1 KO mice partly corrected defective VLDL secretion and hepatic steatosis in these single KO mice, respectively.

These data indicate that, although one unaffected molecule (VMP1 or TMEM41B) cannot compensate for the loss of the other, enhanced expression of the unaffected molecule can compensate for the loss of the other molecule. This suggests that what is important may be the total expression level of the VMP1 and TMEM41B in context of overlapping function. The experimental evidence also suggests that VMP1 has a higher scramblase activity and plays a more dominant role. For example, VMP1 deletion causes more profound autophagic defects than does TMEM41B deletion. In addition, TMEM41B exhibits dose-dependent genetic complementation in Vmp1 KO mice, whereas VMP1 complements defects in Tmem41b KO mice only at a low gene dosage. Thus, there is an optimal and delicate balance of overall expression level of these scramblases. We speculate that this may help to ensure optimal phospholipid redistribution across organellar leaflets. At this moment, it is not clear how the threshold is set for this delicate balance.

In summary, the study by Chen et al provides novel concepts that VMP1 and TMEM41B work collectively in a threshold-dependent mechanism to delicately control the total output of the scramblase activity. Perhaps more strikingly, the data further suggest that the mitochondria-associated membrane is the site where these scramblases impact lipid metabolism, in stark contrast to their role in AV formation at the ER site.
