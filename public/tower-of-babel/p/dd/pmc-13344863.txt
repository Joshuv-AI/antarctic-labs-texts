Lead replacement due to non-infectious reasons in patients with cardiac resynchronization therapy in long-term follow-up
Authors: Ewa Jędrzejczyk-Patej, Michał Mazurek, Adam Sokal, Agnieszka Kotalczyk, Jakub Gumprecht, Jacek Kowalczyk, Patrycja Pruszkowska, Oskar Kowalski, Radosław Lenarczyk, Zbigniew Kalarus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) is an effective treatment for patients with heart failure.ObjectiveThe study aimed to determine the long-term durability of CRT leads, factors predisposing to lead replacement, and the outcomes of lead replacement in CRT patients.MethodsThe study population comprised 1059 consecutive patients who received conventional CRT implants at a university hospital between 2002 and 2019.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 1661 days (interquartile range: 815–2792), a total of 324 leads were replaced in 251 patients (23.7%) for non-infectious reasons. The overall crude incidence of lead replacement was 19.4 events per 1000 lead-years. Of those who required lead replacement, 126 subjects (50.2%) underwent the procedure within the first year after CRT implantation. Among patients followed for more than 10 years (n = 143; 13.5%), 67 subjects (46.8%) had one or more lead replacements. Duration of the index CRT procedure and fluoroscopy time were independent predictors of lead replacement in a multivariable analysis [hazard ratio (HR) 1.03, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.01–1.06, P = .02 and HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02–1.15, P = .01, respectively]. The leads that required replacement were left ventricular (11.2%), right ventricular (10.8%), and right atrial (4%), corresponding to approximate rates of 21.4, 20.5, and 7.6 replacements per 1000 lead-years, respectively. Compared with no need for re-intervention, lead-related re-do procedures were not associated with overall long-term mortality (55.3% vs 51%, P = .3) or the incidence of device-related infective endocarditis (5.6% vs 4.5%, P = .82).ConclusionsAlmost 25% of patients with CRT require lead replacement during long-term follow-up for non-infectious reasons, and half of those require replacement within the first year. Over 10 years, nearly every second patient requires a lead-related redo procedure. Lead replacement was not associated with long-term all-cause mortality. Systematic proficiency-based training should be introduced as a routine strategy in the education of novice implanters.

Introduction

Traditional cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) is an effective treatment for patients with symptomatic heart failure (HF) with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and a widened QRS complex.1–3 This therapy was first introduced for clinical use in 1996. Many studies have shown that CRT improves quality of life, left ventricular remodelling, functional capacity, and survival. Traditional CRT has advanced technologically over time, with the development of improved left ventricular (LV) leads and quadripolar electrodes.

Despite the introduction of new technologies and pacing techniques, such as physiological pacing in cardiac resynchronization,4 the number of implanted classic CRTs remains high.5,6 Because CRTs are complex devices with three leads, implantation carries an increased risk of electrode damage, dislocation, dysfunction, or device- or lead-related infection. Published data have assessed lead failure rates and types, but they focus on dysfunction in a specific lead type and do not provide data on the entire population of patients with different lead types observed over many years.

In addition, the predicted lifespan of HF patients (including CRT recipients) has increased significantly due to recent advances in pharmacotherapy,7 further increasing the likelihood of lead damage and the need for redo procedures and lead replacement over time. Because little is known about the incidence of lead replacement in CRT devices during very long-term follow-up, we aimed to determine the durability of CRT leads, factors predisposing to their replacement, and long-term outcomes among CRT recipients.

Methods

Study population

All consecutive patients who underwent traditional CRT implantation between 2002 and 2019 at a high-volume, tertiary care university hospital in a densely populated urban region of Poland were included in the study. Subjects were qualified for CRT implantation in accordance with the appropriate ESC guidelines, including LVEF ≤35%, HF symptoms in New York Heart Association class II, III, or ambulatory class IV (despite optimal medical treatment), and broad QRS complexes. Each patient was informed about the procedure and potential complications and signed an informed written consent. The study complied with international standards, i.e. the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Silesia (the Institutional Review Board number assigned: BNW/NWN/0052/KB/30/24).

The conventional CRT implantation procedure was performed in accordance with current standards. LV lead placement was based on venous branch anatomy, with the lateral or posterolateral tributary of the coronary sinus preferred. Before the procedure, all subjects received an intravenous prophylactic antibiotic (cefazolin, single-dose IV, or clindamycin, single-dose IV, in case of cephalosporin allergy). Procedures were performed by five experienced operators.

Follow-up

All patients were assessed during scheduled visits (1 week, 1 month, and every 6 months thereafter) and unscheduled visits throughout the observation period. Hospital records, outpatient notes, telephone calls, insurers’ records, and death certificates from patients and relatives were also used for data collection. Patients were followed from CRT implantation until March 2021.

Definition

Non-infectious indications for lead replacement were defined as all lead-related interventions performed because of mechanical or functional lead failure, including, but not limited to, lead fracture, insulation failure, loss of capture, sensing abnormalities, lead dislodgment, phrenic nerve stimulation, or unacceptable pacing or defibrillation thresholds. Procedures were included only when the primary indication was lead-related. Upgrades from CRT-P to CRT-D were not included unless they were directly related to lead dysfunction.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages, whereas numerical parameters were reported as the median with interquartile range (IQR), based on their distribution. Groups were compared using the chi-square, Yates-corrected chi-square, Student's t, or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Survival was analysed using the Kaplan–Meier estimator. Multivariable Cox regression was used to identify independent risk factors for mortality. The multivariable regression model was constructed to assess predictors of mortality and included baseline confounders that differentiated alive and deceased patients in the studied population, with a P value of <.05, except for redundant variables. Results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI).

A P value less than .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistica software package (version 10.0, StatSoft Inc., Tulsa, OK, USA).

Results

Study population

The study population consisted of 1059 consecutive patients who underwent traditional CRT implantation between 2002 and 2019 at a tertiary care university hospital in a densely populated urban region of Poland [949 subjects (89.6%) with CRT-D and 110 patients (10.4%) with CRT-P]. The median LVEF in the whole population was 25% (IQR: 20–29%), with a median age of 65 years (58–72), 78.6% male (n = 832), and a median QRS duration of 163 msec (150–181).

During a median follow-up of 1661 days (IQR: 815–2792) for non-infectious reasons (dislocation, dysfunction, fracture, etc.), 324 leads in 251 patients (23.7%) were replaced. Fifty-two patients (4.9%) underwent 2 lead replacement procedures, 12 subjects (1.1%) underwent 3 procedures, and 2 of them (0.2%) underwent 4 lead-related procedures. The overall crude incidence of lead replacement was 19.4 events per 1000 lead-years.

Among 324 lead replacements, the reasons for redo procedures were as follows: 67 (20.7%) lead dislocations, 78 (24.1%) lead fractures, 79 (24.4%) high stimulation thresholds, 44 (13.6%) phrenic nerve stimulation and inability to reprogram the device, and 56 (17.2%) low lead impedance. Type and time of electrode replacement

Overall, 119 patients (11.2%) required LV lead replacement, 114 (10.8%) required right ventricular (RV) lead replacement, and 42 (4%) required right atrial (RA) lead replacement, corresponding to approximate rates of 21.4, 20.5, and 7.6 replacements per 1000 lead-years, respectively. Figure 1C shows the cumulative incidence of individual lead replacements. In 19 patients (1.8%), an additional subcutaneous lead was needed because shock therapy for ventricular fibrillation was ineffective.

Among those who required lead replacement, 126 subjects (50.2%) underwent a lead-related redo intervention within the first year after CRT implantation. In total, 135 leads were replaced within the first year after CRT implantation: 68 LV leads (50.4%), 45 RV leads (33.3%), and 22 RA leads (16.3%). Overall, 143 patients (13.5%) had a follow-up period of more than 10 years. Of these, 67 subjects (46.8%) experienced one or more lead replacements over time. Discussion

The main findings of our study are as follows: (i) Over more than 10 years of observation, almost half of patients require one or more lead replacements; (ii) in a real-world registry, about 25% of patients receiving conventional CRT require lead replacement within 4.5 years due to non-infectious reasons; 50% of those require replacement within the first year; (iii) overall, the proportions of leads requiring replacement over time are as follows: left ventricular (11.2%), right ventricular (10.8%), and atrial (4%); (iv) lead replacement in the first year was most commonly related to the left ventricular coronary sinus lead (51%), whereas during long-term FU (>10 years of observation) it was most commonly related to the defibrillation lead (61%); (v) duration of the index procedure (CRT implantation) is the only independent predictor of lead replacement over time; and (vi) re-do procedures and lead replacements were not associated with an increased risk of long-term mortality or cardiac device-related infective endocarditis in our cohort.

Incidence of lead replacement in CRT patients

Several studies have assessed lead failure rates and types; however, these rates vary by lead type, follow-up duration, population characteristics, venous access, and the definition of failure.8–12 One of the most critical issues is the lead structure. Manufacturers provide survival probability reports for their leads in adult patients, but these are limited to a single lead type. Much published data focus on dysfunction of specific lead types [e.g. Sprint Fidelis (Medtronic) and Riata (Abbott)],12–15 and they do not provide data on the entire population of patients with ICD or CRT who have different types of leads implanted over a long period and followed for many years. Pacing leads have lower failure rates than ICD leads due to their simpler construction.16 Furthermore, manufacturers continuously improve electrode quality, which may increase long-term survival.17 DF-4 connectors have replaced DF-1 connectors in ICD leads, and quadripolar LV leads have replaced bipolar LV leads.18 Data on the long-term performance of LV leads are also limited. Most published studies report a mean follow-up of 2 to 3 years, and data beyond 10 years are scarce; however, older leads are associated with an increased risk of failure. Some studies reported a 17%–20% ICD lead failure rate at 10 years.8,18 Notably, ICD lead fractures were more common in the ICD-CRT group than in the ICD group.13 In our opinion, lead durability should be assessed separately for CRT and ICD, because CRT is a much more complex device with a larger can and three leads (including an LV coronary sinus lead), which increases the risk of both lead dislocation and damage. Additionally, CRT implantation is more challenging, and the procedure is longer. Inserting three leads close together, e.g. via a subclavian vein, further increases the risk of lead damage due to their proximity and continuous interaction. Finally, compared with ICD, CRT is implanted in patients with the most advanced HF and an increased risk of complications per se (including infectious ones) due to HF itself.

The second main reason for lead replacement is lead dislodgment, which ranges from 2.9% to 10.6% depending on follow-up duration, the studied population, and the type of lead analysed.19,20 Lead failures are more frequent in ICDs than in pacemaker electrodes.16 Regarding lead dislodgment, higher rates were reported for LV leads compared with RA or RV (6.8%, 1%, and 0.6%, respectively).19,20 Possible reasons include passive fixation of LV leads compared with active fixation of right-heart leads, and the challenge of securing a stable lead position within coronary sinus branches due to complex anatomy.

In our study, LV and RV leads accounted for most replacements when analysed by lead type, whereas RA lead replacements were less frequent. The relatively high burden of LV lead replacement (21.4 replacements per 1000 lead-years) is consistent with previous CRT experience, in which coronary sinus lead instability, loss of capture, elevated pacing thresholds, and phrenic nerve stimulation were important causes of reintervention.21

Bipolar LV leads had a higher replacement rate than quadripolar leads, but this difference was attenuated after adjusting for follow-up duration and did not reach statistical significance. This finding likely reflects, at least in part, the longer observation time for bipolar leads implanted predominantly in the earlier study period. Nevertheless, the consistent numerical trend favouring quadripolar leads is clinically plausible, given their greater programming flexibility, multiple pacing vectors, and lower susceptibility to phrenic nerve stimulation or suboptimal pacing thresholds, which may reduce the need for lead repositioning or replacement. Importantly, this observation aligns with previously published data suggesting improved procedural performance and a lower reintervention burden with quadripolar LV lead technology.22–24

The rate of RV lead replacement (20.5 replacements per 1000 lead-years) may reflect the long study period and the use of defibrillator leads in CRT-D systems. Furthermore, a small proportion of patients carried Medtronic Sprint Fidelis ICD leads, which have been associated with higher failure rates and may have contributed to RV lead revisions in our cohort. However, the extended follow-up of over 10 years in our cohort provides a unique opportunity to assess the long-term evolution of lead performance, which is rarely reported in CRT populations.

Independent predictors of lead replacement

Data on independent predictors of lead failure are scarce. To date, most research has focused specifically on Sprint Fidelis and Riata electrodes.13,25,26 Reported independent risk factors for Sprint Fidelis lead damage were higher LVEF, younger age, female gender, non-cephalic venous access, and previous lead damage.13,25,26 In contrast to these reports, we analysed a high-volume dataset of 1059 consecutive patients implanted with CRT who were followed for over 10 years and searched for predictors of lead failure, taking into account a broad spectrum of important clinical factors, including patients’ clinical characteristics, procedural data, operators’ experience, lead type, and concomitant medical therapy, etc. Importantly, we found no correlation among the tested parameters, except for procedure duration, which appeared to be the only independent risk factor for redo procedure and lead replacement. This finding emphasizes that prolonged CRT implantation, per se, due to, for example, challenging anatomy, substantially and independently increases the risk of a redo procedure.

Long-term prognosis and risk of CDRIE in CRT patients with lead replacement

As with the limited data on predictors of lead replacement, evidence on long-term outcomes after lead revision or replacement remains scarce.21–23 To our knowledge, this is one of the first analyses specifically examining long-term outcomes in CRT patients with and without the need for lead-related reinterventions. Importantly, our findings indicate that patients undergoing lead-related re-do procedures do not experience worse long-term outcomes than those without such interventions. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously, as the analysis is observational and may be affected by residual confounding. Therefore, our findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than establishing a causal relationship. Nonetheless, they may provide reassurance that lead-related reinterventions in CRT recipients are not associated with an apparent excess risk of mortality or device-related endocarditis during long-term follow-up.

Study limitations and strengths

A single-centre study design is a limitation of our observations. A relatively small patient group is another limitation, but the sample size was sufficient for statistical purposes. Detailed data on RV lead fixation type and precise RV lead position were not consistently available; therefore, potential temporal changes in conventional RV pacing implantation strategy (active vs passive fixation, apical vs septal positioning) could not be assessed. We did not analyse the need for lead replacement based on initial LV lead location, e.g. lateral or posterolateral vein. All patients had their LV leads implanted via subclavian vein puncture. Many of the RA and RV leads were also implanted via this approach. Due to the lack of precise data, a detailed analysis of any correlation between vein access site and lead failure was not feasible.

Our study provides long-term follow-up data on patients with the most complex CIEDs in a real-world setting. We present our unique findings, which show that, in over 10 years of observation in our continuously updated dataset of consecutive CRT recipients, nearly every second patient requires lead replacement over time, but a redo procedure does not worsen long-term prognosis.

Conclusions

Almost 25% of patients with traditional CRT require lead replacement within 4.5 years for non-infectious reasons. Half of all lead replacements occur within the first year after CRT implantation, underscoring the importance of optimal implantation technique and early post-procedural surveillance. Duration of the index procedure and fluoroscopy time were strong predictors of subsequent lead replacement, suggesting that technically challenging procedures identify patients at increased long-term risk. Almost half of patients with a follow-up period exceeding 10 years underwent one or more lead-related reinterventions. Mortality was similar in patients with and without lead replacement. Although CRT technology has evolved substantially over time, many patients implanted during earlier eras now survive for more than a decade owing to advances in heart failure management. Therefore, understanding the long-term burden of lead-related issues in these ageing legacy CRT populations remains highly clinically relevant. As CRT implantations are among the most complex procedures in electrotherapy, systematic proficiency-based training should be introduced as a routine strategy in the education of novice implanters.
