Usefulness of the S3-NIV Patient Questionnaire Added to Telemonitoring Data for the First 6 Months of Home Noninvasive Ventilation
Authors: Zubair Owadally, Mathilde Guillemot, Romain Barthes, Dan Adler, Jean-Christian Borel, Marius Lebret, Mehdi Rahim, Joëlle Texereau, Sandrine Pontier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOptimal management of long-term home noninvasive ventilation (NIV) therapy is not clearly defined. This study determined whether patient experience of disease and NIV therapy (S3-NIV score) in the first month was associated with successful NIV delivery and termination rate at 6 months.MethodsParticipants were adults starting home nocturnal NIV for chronic respiratory failure in 2019–2022, who used telemonitoring, completed ≥1 S3-NIV questionnaire in the first month of NIV, and were followed by the same home service provider. As no S3-NIV score thresholds are defined, participants were grouped by S3-NIV score quartiles (Q) (Q1 = most negative impact of symptoms/NIV; Q4 = lowest impact of disease/treatment). Factors associated with successful NIV delivery at 6 months were determined using multivariate logistic regression analysis.ResultsOne hundred fifty-three patients were included (mean age 65.9 ± 12.9 years, 60% male). The proportion of patients with successful NIV delivery at 6 months increased as initial S3-NIV score increased: Q1 42%, Q2 60%, Q3 63%, and Q4 63% (p = 0.007). Individuals with lower S3-NIV scores had more home interventions and mask changes during follow-up, regardless of whether NIV was successful or not. Termination rate was higher in S3-NIV score Q1 versus Q4 (29% vs. 11%). The probability of successful NIV delivery at 6 months increased by 1.14-fold for each 1-point increase in the initial S3-NIV score, independent of the association between the score and NIV data at 1 month.ConclusionThe S3-NIV questionnaire helped identify patients at higher risk of therapy termination more effectively than relying only on objective ventilator data.

Introduction

Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) is used to reduce work of breathing and improve gas exchange in patients with chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure [1–5]. NIV has been shown to reduce hospital admissions, improve survival rates, and enhance overall quality of life for these patients [6, 7]. However, there are several challenges to achieving these benefits, including proper delivery of NIV [8, 9] and adequate improvement in hypercapnia [10], while maintaining good tolerance of therapy [11].

Current approaches to monitoring individuals on long-term home NIV therapy are heterogeneous [12]. This may be because of major differences in delivery of care in different healthcare systems or because the optimal follow-up approach and key endpoints during NIV treatment remain to be determined. In France, patient follow-up is performed by pulmonologists (with frequency of visits varying between centers) and home provider respiratory technicians (home visits every 2–4 months). Although, telemonitoring is covered by the national health system, the growing volume of data generated by remote daily transmission of ventilator device is a matter of concern [13]. Ideally, the effectiveness of NIV therapy would be determined from a combination of data from the patient (symptom improvement, sleep quality, absence of NIV-related side effects), the ventilator (adequate device usage, absence of excessive leaks, low number of sleep-related respiratory events), and medical tests (appropriate reduction in hypercapnia) [10, 14–16]. However, the most appropriate organization to collect and analyze these data to improve the quality of NIV delivery is unknown.

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL), assessed with the severe respiratory insufficiency (SRI) questionnaire, has been shown associated with mortality in patients using long-term NIV therapy [17, 18]. However, the SRI includes 49 items grouped into seven subscales, limiting its applicability in everyday clinical practice. The S3-NIV questionnaire was developed as a short, self-administered tool to assess patient-reported outcomes in individuals with chronic respiratory failure on long-term home NIV [19]. Like the SRI, the S3-NIV questionnaire can be used in groups of patients with various respiratory diseases and does not have established thresholds for classifying the severity of impact of SRI on an individual’s life.

Our hypothesis was that adding the standardized collection of information about patient symptoms and experience with NIV therapy (using the S3-NIV questionnaire) would help optimize the delivery and monitoring of NIV therapy. Therefore, this study investigated associations between the S3-NIV score in the first month of therapy and both daily monitored data from the ventilator and termination rates in the first 6 months in a real-world cohort of patients with chronic respiratory failure that was not related to a neuromuscular disease. Secondary endpoints were to determine: (i) patient characteristics associated with the S3-NIV score (which could help clinicians and providers to proactively identify individuals who may require more intensive support); (ii) trajectories of NIV delivery in the first 6 months of therapy and the frequency of home service provider interventions across the different S3-NIV quartiles (to guide the appropriate frequency of follow-up visits); and (iii) whether the S3-NIV score is an independent predictor of successful NIV delivery (to determine the specific utility of this questionnaire when considered alongside objective telemonitoring data).

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The current study focused on a subset of participants in the INOVAL noninterventional cohort study, which includes 4,811 adults (age ≥18 years) with chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure not due to a neuromuscular disease who were all treated with home nocturnal NIV from 2019 to 2022 and had telemonitoring. The present analysis included participants from the INOVAL cohort who met specific criteria: initiated NIV during the study period, had a minimum of 6 months of theoretical NIV history (calculated from the NIV start date to the end of the study), had valid telemonitoring data, completed at least one S3-NIV questionnaire within the first month of therapy, and were followed by the same home service provider (VitalAire) (online suppl. Fig. S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551463).

The research was conducted ethically in accordance with the local guideline, the French Data Protection Authority (CNIL) MR-004 methodology relating to research that secondarily uses data that have already been collected. Opt-out informed consent protocol was used for the use of participant data for research purposes, and the study was registered on the French Health Data Hub website (No. F20220607183406) and did not require investigational review board approval. The final protocol was validated in June 2022, before data collection for this study commenced.

NIV Therapy, Telemonitoring, Follow-Up, and S3-NIV

As per French regulations, NIV therapy was initiated in the inpatient or outpatient setting depending on the practice at each center. The type of NIV, interface used, titration procedure, ventilator settings, and frequency and modalities of regular medical follow-up were determined for each individual by the physician in charge of their care. After discharge, as per national regulation, patients received home visits from a respiratory technician from the home service provider. The first was on the day of discharge, during which daily transmission of ventilator device data (usage, leaks, apnea-hypopnea index [AHI]) was activated, the second took place about 10 days later, and then visits occurred every 2–4 months. At each visit, the technician ensured correct use of the NIV device and checked the response to therapy and occurrence of side effects using device data and the S3-NIV questionnaire, which triggered additional actions or visits if necessary. Responses to the S3-NIV questionnaire were entered into a mobile terminal and sent directly to the provider patient record. Daily transmitted ventilator data did not trigger any alerts.

The S3-NIV questionnaire assesses respiratory symptoms, sleep quality, and NIV-related side effects [19]. It includes eleven items, each rated on a five-point Likert scale from 0 (always true) to 4 (completely untrue). The total score is calculated by averaging scores for all items, then multiplying by 2.5. Possible scores range from 0 (highest impact of disease and treatment) to 10 (lowest impact of disease and treatment). There are two S3-NIV subscales: respiratory (based on responses to six items that specifically pertain to respiratory symptoms), and sleep and NIV-related side effects (five items). The S3-NIV questionnaire has been shown to have good test-retest reliability, with intraclass coefficients of 0.89 after 1 week and 0.72 after 4 weeks [19, 20], and is available in several formats and languages [20–22].

Data Collection

Data were prospectively collected by the home service provider during routine practice. This included patient demographic data (age, sex, BMI), S3-NIV questionnaire responses, ventilator settings (inspiratory positive airway pressure, expiratory positive airway pressure), daily transmitted device data (hours of use, AHI, unintentional leaks), home service provider interventions (phone calls and home visits), and mask changes.

Outcomes

The primary objective was to investigate the association between the S3-NIV score during the first month of NIV and both NIV success and termination rates in the first 6 months of therapy. As previously described [23], successful NIV delivery was defined as device usage ≥4 h/day, unintentional leaks <24 L/min, and residual AHI <10 events/h [8]; NIV was defined as unsuccessful if any of these criteria were not met. NIV termination was defined as cessation of an NIV order by the respiratory physician in charge of follow-up.

Participants were grouped into quartiles based on the S3-NIV score in the first month after NIV therapy initiation, from quartile Q1 (lowest S3-NIV scores, corresponding to the highest impact of disease and treatment) to Q4 (highest S3-NIV scores, corresponding to the lowest impact of disease and treatment). Secondary objectives were as follows: (i) patient characteristics associated with S3-NIV scores; (ii) trajectories of NIV delivery in the first 6 months of therapy and respiratory technician interventions by S3-NIV quartile; and (iii) predictors of successful NIV delivery at 6 months.

Data Handling and Statistical Analyses

Data are presented as number of patients (%) for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation for numerical variables. If a patient had more than one S3-NIV score during the first month of NIV, available scores were averaged. Comparisons between S3-NIV quartile groups were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for numerical variables. The Cochran-Armitage test was used to assess the statistical significance of linear trends in endpoint parameters across S3-NIV quartile groups. Teletransmitted device data were analyzed at 1, 3, and 6 months after NIV initiation; mean values for each parameter at each timepoint were calculated using data from the previous 30 days. Daily leaks and AHI values were considered valid and analyzed if NIV usage on that day was >60 min. No imputation was performed. The number of provider interventions during the actual treatment period was determined and then standardized to a 6-month equivalent. Sankey plots were used to visualize individual changes in NIV delivery success over time. Description of the number of interventions in relation to NIV delivery success and S3-NIV score was undertaken by comparing the number of interventions in patients with unsuccessful NIV delivery (across all S3-NIV score quartiles given that low adherence or technical issues necessitate more technician interventions) with the number of interventions in patients with successful NIV delivery stratified by S3-NIV quartile. In addition, we examined the relationship between the S3-NIV score and home interventions over the 6-month observation period.

Multivariate analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with successful NIV delivery. This used a logistic regression model, with input features combining patient characteristics and the telemonitoring data from the first month of NIV. The binary output was ventilation delivery (successful vs. unsuccessful/missing/discontinuation) at 6 months. Model discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Possible AUC values range from 0 to 1, with scores of 0.7–0.8 and 0.8–0.9 considered acceptable/good and excellent, respectively. A bivariate analysis was performed to select input parameters for the multivariate model; this compared numerical variables using a Mann-Whitney U test and categorical variables using a chi-square test. Factors with p < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression. Multicollinearity was assessed, and highly correlated variables were excluded. Odds ratio and 95% confidence interval values were calculated. To ensure the comparability of odds ratio values, all input values were manually adjusted to fall within a range of approximately 0–10.

All statistical analyses were performed with Python 3.8.15. For all analyses, a p value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 153 patients were included (mean age 65.9 ± 12.9 years, 60% male, mean BMI 35.5 ± 10.3 kg/m2), with one-quarter of them receiving oxygen therapy at the time of NIV initiation. The mean time between NIV initiation and S3-NIV questionnaire completion was 15.4 ± 7.5 days. Twenty-one patients completed the S3-NIV questionnaire twice within the first month of therapy, and these scores were averaged (the mean intra-patient standard deviation for the S3-NIV score was 0.32). The mean S3-NIV score in the first month was 7.8 ± 1.3; the respiratory subscore (7.2 ± 1.9) was lower than the sleep quality/NIV-related side effects subscore (8.3 ± 1.4). Responses to each S3-NIV item are shown in online supplementary Table S1. Differences in the total S3-NIV score between quartiles were also seen in the respiratory subscore (5.3 ± 1.8 in Q1 and 9.1 ± 0.7 in Q4) and in the sleep quality/NIV-related side effects subscore (7.0 ± 1.6 in Q1 and 9.5 ± 0.5 in Q4) (Table 1). The number of patients in each S3-NIV quartile and the lower and upper limits of each quartile were as follows: Q1, n = 45, 4.20–7.04; Q2, n = 35, 7.27–7.95; Q3, n = 35, 8.18–8.63; and Q4, n = 38, 8.86–10.

Primary Objective: S3-NIV, Successful NIV Delivery, and Termination Rate

Eighty-six patients (56%) were considered to have successful NIV therapy at 6 months. Higher initial S3-NIV scores were associated with a higher rate of successful NIV delivery at 6 months: 42% in Q1, 60% in Q2, 63% in Q3, and 63% in Q4 (Fig. 1). At each timepoint, patients with an initial S3-NIV score in Q3 and Q4 consistently had higher rates of successful NIV delivery than those who had an initial S3-NIV score in Q1 or Q2 (p = 0.03 at 3 months and p = 0.007 at 6 months, Chi-2 test). Higher S3-NIV scores were associated with a higher proportion of patients with successful NIV delivery (p = 0.004 at 3 months, p = 0.05 at 6 months, Cochran-Armitage trend test) (Fig. 1). The proportion of patients stopping NIV therapy (terminations) increased over time, reaching 13% after 6 months (Fig. 1). At each timepoint, termination rates were significantly higher in S3-NIV score Q1 versus S3-NIV score Q4 (p values of 0.024 and 0.018 at 3 and 6 months, respectively); at 6 months, the proportion of patients who had stopped NIV therapy was 29% in Q1 and 11% in Q4 (Fig. 1).

Secondary Objectives

Patient Characteristics Associated with S3-NIV Quartile

Patients with an S3-NIV score in Q1 or Q2 were more likely to be female and have a caregiver than those in Q3 or Q4 (Table 1). Patients in Q1 were significantly more likely to be treated with oxygen therapy (Table 1) and report breathlessness at rest, on exertion and during the night (online suppl. Table S1), than the patients of the three other quartiles.

Trajectories of NIV Delivery in the First 6 Months of Therapy and Technician Interventions

Sixty-two percent of patients (n = 95) were considered to have successful NIV therapy at 1 month; of these, nearly three-quarters (72.6%; n = 69) continued to have successful NIV at 6 months and 8.4% (n = 8) had terminated NIV (Fig. 2). For individuals initially classified as having unsuccessful NIV therapy, 32.1% (n = 17) transitioned to successful NIV and 13.2% (n = 7) had terminated NIV at 6 months (Fig. 2). Reasons for unsuccessful NIV at 6 months were low adherence (n = 32), high unintentional leaks (n = 16), and/or high residual AHI (n = 23); patients could have more than one reason for not achieving successful NIV delivery. At 6-month follow-up, NIV device usage was 6.1 ± 2.9 h/day, with low unintentional leaks (6.4 ± 8.7 L/min) and residual AHI (3.7 ± 5.5 events/h).

Despite having good adherence, controlled leaks, and no obstructive events based on device data, patients in S3-NIV score Q1 received the most technician interventions overall and at home, and the most mask changes (Table 2). Patients with unsuccessful NIV delivery had the next highest number of interventions, while those with successful NIV and an S3-NIV score in Q2, Q3, or Q4 had the fewest number of interventions/mask changes (Table 2). In the subgroup of patients with successful NIV delivery at 1 month, a lower S3-NIV score was significantly correlated with a higher number of home interventions (Spearman correlation coefficient: −0.284, p = 0.005); there was no significant correlation between S3-NIV score and the number of interventions in the group with unsuccessful NIV delivery (Spearman correlation: −0.081, p = 0.563).

Predictors of Successful NIV Delivery at 6 Months

Results of the bivariate analysis are shown in online supplementary Table S2. Variables with p < 0.25 that were included in the multivariable analysis were sex, mask type, S3-NIV score, device data at 1 month (usage, unintentional leaks and AHI, each separately), and number of home interventions over 6 months. Using these variables, we predicted successful NIV delivery at 6 months with an AUC of 0.83. Adherence to NIV in the first month of therapy was the best predictor of successful NIV delivery at 6 months, with each additional hour of NIV device usage increasing the probability of NIV therapy success by 1.25-fold. Conversely, each 1 event/h increase in the AHI reduced the probability of successful NIV delivery. The S3-NIV score during the first month of therapy also showed a positive association with successful NIV delivery at 6 months, with each 1-point increase in the S3-NIV score increasing the likelihood of successful NIV by 1.14-fold (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The results of our study showed that a lower S3-NIV score during the first month of long-term home NIV therapy was associated with a lower likelihood of successful NIV therapy delivery and a higher risk of NIV termination at 6 months. To our knowledge, this study is the first to show an association between the S3-NIV score (a condition-specific patient-reported outcome measure [PROM]), daily device data, and the NIV termination rate.

Previous studies have shown associations between some NIV parameters and HRQoL and between HRQoL and outcomes (e.g., mortality). In a study of 67 patients with restrictive lung disorders on NIV, quality of life assessed by the SRI questionnaire was negatively impacted by the presence of obstructive events (high AHI), patient ventilator asynchrony, and persistent nocturnal hypoxemia or hypercapnia [24]. Furthermore, in the Norwegian Long-term Home Ventilation registry, a higher SRI score (indicating preserved HRQoL) was associated with a lower mortality risk after adjustment for age, education, underlying disease, comorbidities, daily ventilator usage, and time since therapy initiation [17]. A Cochrane analysis of data from fourteen randomized controlled trials concluded that long-term NIV improved survival, with a potential HRQoL benefit [5]. Improvements in symptoms such as fatigue, sleep quality, and cognitive function during NIV are potential contributors to the improved HRQoL that has been documented during NIV therapy [25–29]. These data came from studies that used longer questionnaires specifically developed and validated in patients with chronic respiratory failure such as the SRI (49 questions) and the Maugeri Foundation Respiratory Failure Questionnaire (28 questions). Although shorter, the S3-NIV specifically targets people being treated with NIV and therefore captures disease-related symptoms (breathing difficulties, headaches, and bronchial congestion) and NIV-related side effects, allowing determination of the benefits versus limitations of therapy that contribute to the impact of disease and therapy on quality of life.

One of the main reasons that NIV therapy delivery was defined as unsuccessful in our study was low device usage (adherence), consistent with a previous report [8]. Another contributor to unsuccessful NIV delivery was a high residual AHI without leaks, which could be due to insufficient expiratory positive airway pressure or the occurrence of obstructive events related to the type of mask [30, 31]. On multivariate analysis, mean daily NIV device usage and mean AHI in the first month of therapy were significant independent predictors of having successful NIV therapy delivery (i.e., a combination of adherence, and well-controlled leaks and obstructive event) at 6 months. However, a patient-centered approach is central to ensuring long-term adherence to NIV and therapy success [32]. The S3-NIV reflects a combination of how an individual perceives their symptoms, how the ventilator was set up, and how well they use it. In our study, individuals with the greatest impact of disease and therapy on HRQoL (i.e., those in the lower S3-NIV score quartiles) were more likely to be female, to be on long-term oxygen therapy, to report dyspnea, and to have a caregiver. Patient sex and use of supplemental oxygen were also found to significantly influence the S3-NIV score in a previous longitudinal study [33]. Furthermore, in patients with successful NIV delivery at 1 month, lower S3-NIV scores were associated with a higher number of technician interventions and mask changes during the first 6 months of NIV therapy; this association was not seen when NIV delivery was unsuccessful, probably because unsuccessful NIV delivery itself already triggered more interventions.

The finding that each 1-point increase in the S3-NIV score increased the likelihood of successful NIV delivery at 6 months suggests that the S3-NIV questionnaire could be a key tool for identifying potential early issues after NIV therapy initiation and for monitoring the patient experience during longer-term follow-up, complementing currently utilized clinical parameters and telemonitoring data. The NIV termination rate was 13% at 6 months, which is in line with the 1-year therapy termination rate reported in another French real-world study including patients with multiple underlying pathologies starting NIV with telemonitoring [11]. Termination rates at 6 months of follow-up were significantly higher in patients who had the lowest S3-NIV scores in the first month of therapy and were nearly 3-fold higher in patients with an S3-NIV score in Q1 versus Q4. Although the NIV termination rate was associated with NIV device data, the difference in termination rate (8.4% vs. 13.2% in patients with successful versus unsuccessful NIV delivery) was not as marked as the association between the termination rate and S3-NIV quartiles, suggesting that the S3-NIV score provides distinct insights that contribute to treatment success and persistence and go beyond objective device metrics. As far as we are aware, this is the first time that an association between a PROM and NIV termination has been reported.

The main strength of this study is that it reports on a real-life cohort of patients with multiple non-neuromuscular underlying diseases being treated with NIV across different clinical settings. This enhances both the generalizability and clinical relevance of the findings. Another strength is the combined assessment of device-derived telemonitoring data with a condition-specific PROM to identify patients at higher risk of ineffective NIV delivery and therapy termination. This approach is consistent with the increasing recognition that systematic incorporation of PROMs alongside physiological outcomes is essential to provide a comprehensive evaluation of therapy – something that is supported by patients themselves. An additional strength of this study is the organizational model implemented. Designed as a short, reliable, and repeatable tool [19, 20], the S3-NIV is ideally suited for integration into clinical practice routines and can be self-administered by the patient. In our study, the S3-NIV was completed with the assistance of nonmedical staff, specifically the provider respiratory technician who addresses technical issues with therapy ensures tolerance and escalates to physicians when necessary. Although this could limit the external generalizability of the results to other healthcare systems or organizations, it does raise a question regarding the optimal balance between medical and nonmedical follow-up and the relative importance of objective device data and patient experience.

Limitations of our study include the modest overall sample size, small numbers in different subgroups, and the observational design that does not allow inference of causality. As for any reported association, the possibility of reverse causality cannot be fully excluded (for example, patients with a lower impact of disease at baseline could tolerate treatment better). In addition, the study population was drawn from a single homecare provider in France, which may limit the wider applicability of the findings.

Nevertheless, these limitations do not diminish the main conclusion: that the S3-NIV score provides clinically relevant information beyond ventilator telemonitoring data and physiological parameters, providing a more robust way to identify individuals at risk for NIV therapy termination, and constitutes a pragmatic tool to optimize long-term NIV follow-up. Using the S3-NIV questionnaire could help refine, personalize, and improve long-term NIV therapy follow-up programs by providing information that can be used to guide technician-patient communication and the frequency and intensity of the follow-up. This can help ensure that patients at higher risk of treatment failure receive the appropriate level of face-to-face and remote monitoring support. Our findings also align with those of Lefouili et al. [34], who reported that telemonitoring leak data alone do not reliably predict patient perception of leaks, as captured by item 8 in the S3-NIV (“I am disturbed by leaks”). Furthermore, patient-perceived leak negatively influenced responses to other S3-NIV items. Taken together, data from the Lefouili paper and our study underscore the importance of the patient experience, which extends beyond what objective ventilator data can capture.

The exact clinical relevance of the differences seen in the S3-NIV score in our population is difficult to determine. Differences in the mean S3-NIV scores between the S3-NIV quartile groups in our study exceeded 0.8, which has recently been defined as the minimal clinically important difference threshold in patients with Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) who have been established on NIV for several years [35]. Further research is needed to clarify whether this minimal clinically important difference value can be applied when comparing different patient groups or if its use should be limited to the evaluation of longitudinal changes in S3-NIV score in an individual patient. It would also be helpful to look at our data and evaluate changes in the S3-NIV score over time and in response to interventions (e.g., medical consultations, home service provider actions) alongside objective device data. This is particularly relevant because higher scores on the S3-NIV subscales (specifically sleep and NIV side effects) have been reported in patients using NIV for at least one year compared to those who started therapy more recently [21]. Further insight could be obtained by conducting additional analyses utilizing other variables that define treatment success but were not captured in the home service provider database, such as the reduction in arterial carbon dioxide pressure during NIV therapy, the occurrence of acute respiratory events, and patient satisfaction.

In conclusion, this study highlights the value of PROMs to complement telemonitoring and physiological data for the follow-up of patients with chronic respiratory failure on home NIV therapy. Individuals at risk of therapy termination were more likely to be identified based on the S3-NIV score than using objective ventilator data alone. By incorporating a PROM such as the S3-NIV questionnaire into patient management and follow-up, clinicians and home service providers could more easily identify home NIV users who are at a higher risk for treatment failure or early discontinuation after NIV initiation and then tailor individualized follow-up accordingly. Further research is needed to determine whether the combined use of PROMs (S3-NIV) and objective device data can optimize the effectiveness of and adherence to home NIV therapy.
