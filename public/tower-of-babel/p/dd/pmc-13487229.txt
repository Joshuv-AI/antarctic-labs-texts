Eco-friendly green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles using Acalypha indica L. leaf extract and their incorporation into topical formulations for acne and skin infection treatment
Authors: K. Kesavardhini, Hasanain A. J. Gharban, Ashish Kumar Nayak, K. Gayathri, B. Lokeshwari, A. Sabina Banu, P. Saranraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Increasing incidences of acne and skin infections, antibiotic resistance, and side effects associated with synthetic dermatology have necessitated the need for safe, sustainable treatment approaches. This work describes the environmentally friendly preparation of Zinc Oxide Nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) through green chemistry by utilizing the leaf extract of Acalypha indica L. plant and further applying the nanoparticles in the preparation of nanogel, herbal soap, and face serum. The biosynthesized zinc oxide nanoparticles were analysed using UV-Visible spectroscopy, Fourier Transform Infra-red (FTIR), X-Ray diffraction (XRD), and Scanning electron microscopy (SEM). From UV-Visible spectra, an excitonic absorption band peak was observed at 384 nm, which confirms the presence of nanoparticles. FTIR spectra show a vibrational peak of Zn-O bonds at 476 cm⁻¹ and 512 cm⁻¹ as well as surface-bonded phenolic, aliphatic, and carbonyl functional groups due to flavonoids, terpenoids, and tannins. XRD confirmed a highly crystalline hexagonal wurtzite structure (JCPDS No. 36-1451) with an average crystallite size of 18.5 ± 3.2 nm. SEM revealed polydisperse, moderately agglomerated nanoparticles with irregular, porous morphology. ZnO NPs displayed strong bactericidal action against nine clinical strains of bacteria, with the highest zone of inhibition observed as 25 mm against Staphylococcus aureus and Salmonella typhi at 75 µg/well concentration. These particles were also found to possess excellent radical scavenging ability, IC₅₀ being recorded as 62.4 ± 2.1 µg/mL for DPPH free radical. The pH of all the three preparations was compatible with the skin, viz., 5–6. Also, these formulations were physically stable and had good spreadability, i.e., nanogel preparation – 28 mm; soap preparation – 47 mm; serum preparation – 21 mm at 150 g. The herbal soap proved ideal for daily hygiene, while the serum enabled rapid spot treatment. This study establishes that Acalypha indica-mediated green synthesis produces stable, crystalline, multifunctional ZnO nanoparticles with substantial antimicrobial and antioxidant properties. The nanogel formulation represents a scalable, cost-effective, natural-origin alternative for acne and skin infection management, addressing antibiotic resistance and synthetic chemical toxicity.

Introduction

Acne and skin infections are one of the most prevalent dermatological diseases that have infected millions of people worldwide, particularly the youth. These disorders have been associated with microbial colonization, inflammation, sebum secretion, and the obstruction of hair follicles. It has been demonstrated that bacteria including Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli have a substantial part in the formation of inflammatory sores and secondary skin infections1. The use of antibiotics, retinoids, and chemical-based topical creams is often the conventional approach in the management of various ailments; however, the prolonged usage of these drugs often results in adverse reactions, antimicrobial resistance, and irritation of the skin. The rising need for safe, eco-friendly, and sustainable treatment options has contributed to a large amount of research being conducted on plant-based nanomaterials. Recently, nanotechnology has been recognized as a promising area in dermatological applications because of its ability to improve drug penetration, stability, and targeted antimicrobial Properties2. ZnO NPs, or Zinc Oxide Nanoparticles, have become more well-known among the many nanomaterials due to their anti-inflammatory, anti- wound-healing, and UV-protective properties. Zinc oxide nanoparticles appropriate for topical application due to their low toxicity and biocompatibility. Thus, “novel and creative way for the successful treatment of acne and skin infections” and that is provided by the combination that is green nanotechnology and herbal medicine3.

While Silver Nanoparticles (AgNPs) are well-known for their potent broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, zinc oxide nanoparticles offer several distinct advantages for dermatological applications that make them particularly suitable for acne and skin infection treatment. First, ZnO is classified as “Generally Recognized as Safe” (GRAS) by the US FDA, whereas silver nanoparticles raise concerns regarding long-term toxicity, argyria (skin discoloration), and potential accumulation in vital organs4. Second, ZnO possesses intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties that directly address the inflammatory component of acne vulgaris, a key advantage over AgNPs which lack such activity. Third, ZnO provides UV-protective properties, supporting skin barrier function and preventing post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation5. Fourth, ZnO NPs exhibit selective toxicity towards microbial cells while maintaining better biocompatibility with human keratinocytes compared to AgNPs6. Fifth, zinc is an essential trace mineral required for normal skin development and wound healing, making ZnO nanoparticles inherently more biocompatible for long-term topical use. These combined properties antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, UV-protective, and wound-healing make ZnO nanoparticles uniquely suited for multifactorial acne management7.

In compared to conventional chemical and physical approaches, the safe and ecologically sustainable creation of nanoparticles has attracted a lot of attention recently. Conventional methods demand a substantial amount of energy, and they have negative consequences for human wellness as well as the environment. In addition, current procedures necessitate the use of hazardous solvents. Plant-mediated synthesis, unlike conventional approaches, does not involve the use of dangerous compounds to human health or the atmosphere8. Nanoparticles are synthesized using plant-derived phytochemicals that includes flavonoids, phenolics, alkaloids, and terpenoids. The Acalypha indica is a traditional medicinal herb found primarily in tropical areas. Conventional medicine has used Acalypha indica to treat skin-related disorders, wounds, and microbial infections. Its leaves contain bioactive substances that aid in a reduction of the zinc ions into ZnO nanoparticles but also increase their biological activity by surface functionalization. This complementary effect of plant phytochemicals and nanoparticles enhances stability and therapeutic potential. Furthermore, it has been discovered that green-synthesised ZnO NPs have better antioxidant and antibacterial qualities than chemically synthesised NPs4. This method is very appealing for use in the biomedical and cosmetic industries due to its affordability, scalability, and environmental friendliness. Therefore, a significant advancement in nanobiotechnology has been made with the use of the Acalypha indica extracts Nanoparticles are produced from leaves5.

Some of the special physico-chemical characteristics of nanoscale zinc oxide nanoparticles include A large surface area with volume ratios, the ability to generate ROS, or reactive oxygen species, as well as photocatalytic qualities are associated with, and these properties are related to their high antibacterial properties6. These nanoparticles can kill microorganisms by damaging their cell membranes, inhibiting their metabolic processes and causing oxidative stress, which eventually leads to mortality. Additionally, ZnO NPs have anti-inflammatory properties that can be effective in reducing irritation, swelling, and redness that often occur in acne lesions. The sebum-regulating and collagen-stimulating properties of ZnO NPs are an indication of their role in skin regeneration. It is also important to mention that ZnO is a chemical that is recognized as it is safe to use in dermatology since it is Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS)7. ZnO nanoparticles can be useful in contemporary topical preparations for the improvement of skin penetration, controlled release, and sustained therapeutic activity. Nanogels, serums, and soaps are effective vehicles for the improvement of bioavailability and stability. Green-synthesised ZnO nanoparticles’ application in the creation of new topical formulations can be a multi-faceted approach for the management of skin infections and acne.

The choice of using Acalypha indica in green synthesis is scientifically valid due to its high phytochemical content. The main phytochemical compounds that take part in the formation of nanoparticles from ZnO include flavonoids (like quercetin and kaempferol), phenols (including tannic acid and caffeic acid), alkaloids like acalyphine, terpenoids, and glycosides9. The process of nanoparticle formation takes place through the oxidation of phenolic hydroxyl groups to quinones, which results in the reduction of zinc cations from Zn²⁺ to Zn⁰ and the formation of ZnO nanoparticles through their oxidation in an alkaline environment10. The flavonoids behave as reducing agents and capping molecules, as they donate electrons through the hydroxyl groups to reduce the metal ions. The carbonyl and hydroxyl groups on the phytochemicals act as a protective layer on the surface of the nanoparticles, thus providing steric stability and avoiding any aggregation11. Tannins, which have more than one galloyl group, behave as efficient reducing molecules along with complex formation with zinc ions and directing nucleation and growth12.

The development of herbal nanoformulations is in line with the growing demand for skincare products that are sustainable and natural. Currently, they want to purchase products that are free from synthetic preservatives, parabens, and other harsh chemicals. The application of Acalypha indica-mediated ZnO nanoparticles in soap, serum, and nanogel formulations offers a holistic treatment approach that emphasizes the removal of bacteria, inflammation management, and rejuvenation of the skin9. Soap formulation offers a cleansing approach that removes surface bacteria from the skin. Serums are highly potent formulations that are absorbed quickly to produce a quick response. Nanogels are highly adherent to the skin surface, making them the best choice for a sustained response. The combination obtained from phytochemicals and ZnO nanoparticles could also offer a synergistic effect that increases antimicrobial properties with less irritation. The combined formulations not only act on acne pathogenesis but also prevent secondary infections of the skin. In addition, this approach is also supportive of green chemistry and sustainable formulation development in the cosmetics and pharmaceutical sectors. Therefore, the current study intends to produce topical treatments for acne and skin infections as well as green synthesizing zinc oxide nanoparticles utilizing Acalypha indica leaf extract10.

Materials and methods

Collection of Acalypha indica (Kuppaimeni) leaves

Fresh, healthy, and disease-free leaves of Acalypha indica (commonly known as Kuppaimeni) were collected from the Herbal Garden of Sacred Heart College (Autonomous), Tirupattur, Tamil Nadu, India. The plant material was authenticated by Dr. J. Celine Pappa Rani, Assistant Professor and Plant Taxonomist at Holy Cross College (Autonomous), Nagercoil, Tamil Nadu, India. A voucher specimen has been deposited at the Herbarium of the Department of Botany, Holy Cross College, with the accession number HCC/BOT/AI/2024-01. The harvested leaves were first washed with tap water, followed by distilled water to remove any remaining dirt and impurities. The leaves were then allowed to dry for 14 days at room temperature under shade to preserve the bioactive compounds. After drying, the leaves were ground into a fine powder using a mechanical grinder. The powdered sample was stored then kept in an airtight container11.

Preparation of Acalypha indica leaf extract

Acalypha indica leaves were pulverized and dried carry out the study for solvent extraction using the Soxhlet extraction method equipment. The choice of the solvent was done for the extraction procedure, considering the high polarity of ethanol as a suitable choice for extracting phytochemicals, especially flavonoids, phenolics, and alkaloids using ethanol as the suitable solvent for the experiment. 50 g of dried leaf powder was filled in the extractor machine for the experiment using ethanol as the solvent with a boiling point of 78.4 °C for six to eight hours, or until the solvent in the tube became colorless11.

Phytochemical evaluation of leaf extract from Acalypha indica

Additionally, the ethanolic extract underwent an initial phytochemical screening to determine important secondary metabolites which are involved in the stabilizing and decreasing processes of the zinc ions throughout a green synthesis process. Standard chemical tests were used for the initial screening of phytochemicals11. For phytochemical screening, 5 mL of ethanolic leaf extract was used for each test. The extract was concentrated to 2 mL under reduced pressure at 40 °C before testing. Approximately 1 mL of the concentrated extract was used for each phytochemical test, and the color change or precipitate formation was recorded qualitatively11,12.

Tests for alkaloids

The extract was examined using the Mayer, Wagner, and Dragendorff reagents. Alkaloids were proven by the production of orange or as reddish-brown precipitates11.

Tests for flavonoids

Diluted sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid were used to treat the extract. Flavonoids were verified by the appearance of a yellow tint that vanished when acid was added11.

Tests for phenolics and tannins

A solution of Ferric chloride was then added to the extract. Phenolic compounds and tannins have been confirmed green or blue-black coloration12.

Tests for saponins

Distilled water was used to give the extract a good shake. The quantity of saponins present was confirmed by the froth12.

Tests for terpenoids and steroids

Chloroform and concentrated sulfuric acid were mixed with the extract (Salkowski test). The reddish-brown colouring demonstrated the presence of terpenoids and steroids12.

Green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles using Acalypha indica leaf extract

The green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) was carried out using Acalypha indica leaf extract as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent. Zinc acetate dihydrate [Zn(CH₃COO)₂·2 H₂O] (0.5 M) was dissolved in 100 mL of double-distilled water under constant magnetic stirring at 60 °C for 30 min to obtain a clear precursor solution13. A 2.5 mL aliquot of the ethanolic Acalypha indica leaf extract was diluted with 10 mL of double-distilled water, filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon membrane filter, and added dropwise to 25 mL of the 0.5 M zinc acetate precursor solution with continuous stirring at 500 rpm for 15 min. The pH value of the reaction medium was set at around 8.0 by slowly adding 2 M NaOH solution under constant stirring, leading to the formation of a light white precipitate, indicating nucleation. The reaction medium was then heated to 70 °C ± 2 °C under constant stirring at 400 rpm for 30 min, where the precipitate was changed, showing the development of zinc oxide nanoparticles14. The precipitate obtained was separated by centrifuging at 3000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C and was subsequently washed thrice using 20 mL of double distilled water and twice using 20 mL of absolute ethanol. The obtained ZnO nanoparticle powder was carefully crushed and kept in an amber-colored and tightly sealed glass vial at 4 °C. The phytochemicals such as flavonoids, phenols, alkaloids, and terpenoids that were available in the leaf extract played a two-way role of being both reducing as well as capping agent by oxidizing phenolic hydroxyl groups for reduction of Zn²⁺ ions and acting as capping agents on the surface of nanoparticles15. Characterization of zinc oxide nanoparticles

Several approaches are used to determine the properties of zinc oxide nanoparticle. UV-Visible is used to detect the manufacturing process of Zinc Oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles and analyse their optical properties. The Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) detection technique is widely utilized to identify the R groups and capping agents present in nanoparticles. The X-ray Diffraction (“XRD”) technique is widely used to assess the structure, purity, and crystalline properties of ZnO nanoparticles. Scanning electron microscopy, determines the size, shape, and surface properties of ZnO nanoparticles16.

Isolation and cultivation of test microorganisms

The American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) provided the test microorganisms for the purpose of the present investigation, and they were kept in an aseptic laboratory setting. As per the guidelines of ATCC, the lyophilized culture was rehydrated by the addition of sterilized Nutrient Broth and incubated for 18-to-24-hour period at 37 °C. After incubation, pure cultures have been sub-cultured to ensure their purity, and the cultures were smeared over nutritious agar plates to produce isolated cultures. Gram staining and biochemical tests were utilized to identify the bacterial isolates. The bacterial strains investigated in this study included Salmonella typhi, S. pyogenes, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, P. mirabilis, Enterococcus faecalis, B. subtilis, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. Stock cultures were stored on nutrient agar slants at 4 °C for brief periods of time. A loop of cultures was produced in nutrient broth at 37 °C for 18–24 h under an aerobic environment to produce a fresh inoculum for experimental investigation12.

Antibacterial activity of Acalypha indica synthesized zinc oxide nanoparticles

The antibacterial properties of the generated zinc oxide Nanoparticle were studied using the Agar well diffusion method. Nine clinically relevant strains of bacteria that cause acne and skin infections were used to test the antibacterial property. After being prepared and sterilized, the Mueller-Hinton agar solution was poured into Petriplates that had been aseptically sanitized. Fresh bacterial cultures were evenly distributed across the utilizing sterile cotton swabs on the agar plate’s surface. A cork borer, which is sterile, was used to create wells with diameters of 6 mm an aseptic manner bored into the agar. Zinc oxide nanoparticle suspensions of 25, 50, and 75 μg/mL were made. A volume 100 µL of the corresponding ZnO nanoparticle suspensions was put into every well. The antibiotic Streptomycin (10 mg/mL) was used as a Positive control. For 18 to 24 h, the plates have been incubated at 37 °C after being given 30 min to pre-diffuse at room temperature. By calculating the zone of inhibition around each well in millimeters, the activity of antibacterial agents was finally ascertained17.

Antioxidant activity of synthesized zinc oxide nanoparticles

To prevent DPPH from photodegrading, the reaction mixtures have been vortexed for one minute and then let remain at a room temperature (35 °C) for 30 min in a dark area. A UV-Visible spectrophotometer was used to test each sample’s absorbance at 517 nm following incubation. Formulation of nanogel, herbal soap, and facial serum

The ZnO nanoparticles were incorporated into Nanogel, Herbal Soap, and Facial Serum formulations by using suitable polymers and excipients19.

Preparation of herbal nano soap

The herbal nano soap containing AI-ZnO NPs was prepared using the cold saponification method. The following ingredients were used: coconut oil (50 g), palm oil and lavender oil (30 g), olive oil (20 g), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 15 g), distilled water (40 mL), and AI-ZnO NPs (0.5 g, 1% w/w). First, the NaOH was slowly dissolved in distilled water with continuous stirring, and the solution was allowed to cool to 45 °C. Simultaneously, the oils (coconut, palm, and olive) were heated to 45 °C and mixed thoroughly. The lye solution (NaOH + water) was then slowly added to the oil mixture with constant stirring at 300 rpm for 30 min until trace consistency was achieved (the point at which a small amount of the mixture leaves a visible trail on the surface)19. Dispersing the AI-ZnO nanoparticles in 5 ml of glycerin was done through mortar and pestle to make it a smooth paste, which was later incorporated into the soap mixture. It took another 15 min of stirring to make sure that the nanoparticles are properly dispersed within the mixture. The mixture was then transferred into the silicone Mold before being wrapped in plastic wrap and left to saponify for 48 h at room temperature. After 48 h, the soap bars were taken out of their Molds and left to cure for 4 weeks. The final soap bars were stored in airtight containers at room temperature for further evaluation20.

Preparation of nanogel

The nanogel containing AI-ZnO NPs was prepared using carbopol 940 as the gelling agent. Briefly, 1 g of carbopol 940 was dispersed in 50 mL of distilled water with continuous stirring at 500 rpm for 1 h to allow complete hydration. The pH of the gel base was adjusted to 5.5–6.0 using triethanolamine (TEA) dropwise. Separately, 0.5 g (1% w/w) of AI-ZnO NPs was dispersed in 5 mL of propylene glycol using probe sonication (20 kHz, 50% amplitude, 5 min). The nanoparticle dispersion was then added dropwise to the gel base with continuous stirring at 800 rpm for 30 min. The mixture was homogenized using a high-speed homogenizer (10,000 rpm for 10 min) to ensure uniform dispersion. The final nanogel was transferred to sterile amber-colored containers and stored at 4 °C for further evaluation19,21.

Preparation of facial serum

The facial serum containing AI-ZnO NPs was prepared using a water-based formulation. The ingredients included: distilled water (70 mL), propylene glycol (10 mL), glycerin (5 mL), aloe vera gel (5 mL), carbomer 940 (0.5 g), AI-ZnO NPs (0.5 g, 1% w/w), and preservative (phenoxyethanol, 0.5 mL). First, carbomer 940 was dispersed in distilled water with stirring and allowed to hydrate for 30 minutes19. In a separate container, AI-ZnO NPs were dispersed in propylene glycol using probe sonication (20 kHz, 50% amplitude, 5 min). Glycerin and aloe vera gel were then added to the nanoparticle dispersion. This mixture was gradually added to the carbomer solution with continuous stirring at 500 rpm for 20 min. The pH was adjusted to 5.5–6.0 using triethanolamine. The serum was homogenized at 5,000 rpm for 5 min, transferred to amber-colored glass dropper bottles, and stored at 4 °C for further evaluation21.

Evaluation of formulations

For this study, the formulations were evaluated for pH, Spreadability, and Antimicrobial Activities19. To ensure uniform dispersion of ZnO nanoparticles in all three topical formulations, the following procedures were employed. For the nanogel formulation, the biosynthesized ZnO nanoparticles were first suspended in a small volume of distilled water and sonicated using an ultrasonic bath (40 kHz, 80 W) for 15 min to break any loosely bound agglomerates. The nanoparticle suspension was then gradually added to the gel base (prepared using carbopol 940 as the gelling agent) under continuous mechanical stirring at 800 rpm for 30 min, followed by homogenization using a high-speed homogenizer (10,000 rpm for 10 min)19. For the herbal soap formulation, the ZnO nanoparticles were dispersed in glycerin (a humectant and dispersing agent) using a mortar and pestle to form a smooth paste before incorporation into the soap base during the saponification process. The mixture was continuously stirred until a uniform color and consistency were achieved. For the facial serum formulation, ZnO nanoparticles were dispersed in propylene glycol (a co-solvent and dispersant) using probe sonication (20 kHz, 50% amplitude, 5 min) before mixing with the remaining serum ingredients. The dispersion quality was visually confirmed by the absence of visible aggregates, uniform color distribution, and the lack of sedimentation upon standing for 24 h. Additionally, a drop of each formulation was observed under a light microscope at 40× magnification to confirm the absence of large nanoparticle aggregates21.

Green synthesis of ZnO NPs using Acalypha indica leaf extract

Acalypha indica (Kuppaimeni) leaf extract was employed as a stabilizing and reducing agent in the green manufacture of Zinc Oxide Nanoparticles (“ZnO” NPs). Fresh leaves were utilized, purified with tap and distilled water, and dried in the shade at ambient temperature before being powdered to generate an aqueous extract. To make nanoparticles, 2.5 mL of leaf extract was mixed into 25 mL of 0.5 M zinc acetate solution with continual magnetic stirring. To reduce zinc ions, a 2 M sodium hydroxide solution was added to alter the pH reaction mixture to around 8. The product was subsequently heated at 70 °C over 30 min under constant stirring until a white precipitate had been formed, a process that indicated the presence of ZnO nanoparticles. Centrifugation was used to collect the precipitate at 3000 rpm over 30 min, and it was washed thoroughly to remove contaminants, mix distilled water with ethanol. The purified product was dried overnight in a hot air oven set to 60 °C obtain ZnO nanoparticle powder (Fig. 5). The phytochemicals found in the leaf extract flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenoids, tannins, glycosides and the quinones also served as factors that reduce and stabilize nanoparticle production. Various methods were employed to examine the physical and chemical characteristics of the produced ZnO nanoparticles, including UV-Visible spectroscopy, FTIR, XRD, and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM).

Some of the major classes of phytochemicals responsible for the reduction and stabilization of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles include flavonoids (specifically flavonoid glucosides like quercetin and kaempferol), phenolics (like tannic acid and caffeic acid), alkaloids (namely acalyphine), terpenoids, and glycosides. The process entails the oxidation of phenolic groups of OH groups leading to quinones, while simultaneously reducing the Zn²⁺ ions to Zn⁰, leading to the formation of ZnO nanoparticles through oxidation at alkaline conditions. Flavonoids act as reducing agents as well as capping agents by releasing electrons through their hydroxyl groups, which help in the reduction of metal ions. The hydroxyl and carbonyl groups of such phytochemicals provide steric stability to the nanoparticles.

FT-IR analysis of synthesized ZnO NPs from Acalypha indica

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used for determining the functional groups that participated in the synthesis, reduction, capping, and stabilization processes in the formation of zinc oxide nanoparticles from the aqueous extract of Acalypha indica leaves. The FTIR spectrum was obtained with a Perkin Elmer FTIR spectrometer between the wave numbers of 4000–400 cm⁻¹. The FTIR analysis showed the presence of various bioactive phytochemicals that contributed to the reduction of zinc ions and stabilization of the zinc oxide nanoparticles formed during the process. The broad peak obtained at 3760 cm⁻¹ is due to the O-H vibration bands of phenols and flavonoids found in the aqueous extract of the plant leaves.

The band observed at 3202 cm⁻¹ represents the stretching vibrations of O–H and N–H bonds belonging to polyphenols, proteins, and amino acids, thereby confirming the role played by these biomolecules in nanoparticle stability and surface interactions. The peak obtained at 2868 cm⁻¹ is indicative of the aliphatic stretching vibration of C–H bonds, which are characteristic of lipids and terpenoids, thus implying the presence of hydrophobic biomolecules as capping agents on the nanoparticle surface. The sharp peak observed at 2616 cm⁻¹ represents the stretching vibration of O-H bonds, which are characteristic of carboxylic acids, suggesting the involvement of organic acids in chelation and nucleation reactions for nanoparticle synthesis.

The prominent peak at 1544 cm⁻¹ can be correlated with N–H bending and C = C stretching vibrations in aromatic compounds. These findings suggest that there exist proteinaceous substances and aromatic phytochemicals, which play an important role in stabilization of synthesized nanoparticles. Furthermore, the absorption peak at 1186 cm⁻¹ can be associated with C–O stretching vibrations present in alcohols and phenolic compounds. The peak recorded at 1049 cm⁻¹ denotes the presence of C–O–C and C–N stretching vibrations in glycosides and amino acids, respectively, which are known natural stabilizers of nanoparticles. Besides, other minor peaks recorded at 989 cm⁻¹, 927 cm⁻¹, 878 cm⁻¹, and 780 cm⁻¹ denote = C–H and aromatic C–H bending vibrations in unsaturated and aromatic bioactive substances, respectively, present in plant extract. The absorption peak at 722 cm⁻¹ represents C–H bending vibrations of alkanes and fatty acids (Table 2).

The characteristic Zn-O stretching vibration, which confirms the formation of zinc oxide nanoparticles, is observed in the low-frequency region between 400 and 600 cm⁻¹. Specifically, the metal-oxygen (Zn-O) bond stretching mode typically appears as a strong absorption band around 450–550 cm⁻¹. In the present study, although the FTIR spectrum was recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹, the Zn-O stretching vibration was detected as a sharp, intense peak at approximately 476 cm⁻¹ and a shoulder at 512 cm⁻¹, which correspond to the transversal optical and longitudinal optical phonon modes of the hexagonal wurtzite ZnO structure. These peaks are absent in the spectrum of the pure plant extract, conclusively confirming the successful formation of ZnO nanoparticles through the green synthesis process (Fig. 6). Overall, the FTIR results demonstrate that the phytochemicals present in Acalypha indica leaf extract play a significant role in the reduction, capping, and stabilization of ZnO nanoparticles during the green synthesis process.

The Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy analysis provided crucial evidence for the successful synthesis and stabilization of ZnO nanoparticles. The aliphatic C–H stretching at 2868 cm⁻¹, attributed to lipids and terpenoids, contributes to the hydrophobic stabilization of nanoparticles, preventing aggregation in aqueous media10. The prominent C = O stretching at 1744 cm⁻¹, arising from carbonyl groups of aldehydes, ketones, and quinones, plays a dual role: these groups facilitate electron transfer during the reduction process and subsequently chelate zinc ions, directing controlled nucleation16. The presence of two distinct Zn–O bands indicates the transverse optical (TO) and longitudinal optical (LO) phonon modes, which are characteristic of well-crystallized wurtzite ZnO nanoparticles. These Zn–O peaks are completely absent in the FT-IR spectrum of pure plant extract, conclusively confirming the successful formation of ZnO nanoparticles through the green synthesis process32,33.

IC₅₀ value of Acalypha indica-mediated ZnO nanoparticles

The antioxidant activity of the biosynthesized Zinc Oxide Nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) was evaluated using the DPPH free radical scavenging assay at concentrations ranging from 20 to 100 µg/mL. The nanoparticles exhibited concentration-dependent antioxidant activity, with scavenging activity increasing progressively as the concentration increased. The percentage inhibition values obtained were 28.0%, 42.0%, 58.0%, 72.0%, and 85.0% at concentrations of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 µg/mL, respectively. The IC₅₀ value, defined as the concentration required to inhibit 50% of DPPH free radicals, was calculated from the dose–response curve and was determined to be 62.4 ± 2.1 µg/mL (Fig. 15). The obtained IC₅₀ value indicates that the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles possess appreciable antioxidant potential and effective free radical scavenging ability.

The antioxidant activity may be attributed to the presence of bioactive phytochemicals such as flavonoids, phenolic compounds, tannins, terpenoids, and glycosides adsorbed on the nanoparticle surface during the biosynthesis process. These phytoconstituents act as hydrogen or electron donors, thereby neutralizing DPPH radicals and converting them into stable non-radical molecules. The concentration-dependent increase in scavenging activity confirms the strong antioxidant behavior of the Acalypha indica-mediated ZnO nanoparticles, suggesting their possible therapeutic role in reducing oxidative stress and inflammation associated with acne and other skin disorders.

The antioxidant activity of the biosynthesized ZnO nanoparticles, with an IC₅₀ value of 62.4 ± 2.1 µg/mL, compares favorably with previously reported plant-mediated ZnO nanoparticles. Bhalla et al.2 reported IC₅₀ values ranging from 58 to 95 µg/mL for Moringa oleifera-mediated ZnO nanoparticles, while Dhandapani et al.5 found IC₅₀ values of 70–85 µg/mL for Melia azedarach-mediated ZnO NPs against DPPH radicals. Our IC₅₀ value of 62.4 µg/mL is lower (indicating better antioxidant activity) than that reported by Palempalli et al.17 for Andrographis echioides-mediated ZnO NPs (78.5 µg/mL) and Shanmugarathinam et al.23 for Vicoa indica-mediated ZnO NPs (85.3 µg/mL). This superior antioxidant activity is attributed to the high concentration of phenolic compounds and flavonoids (particularly quercetin and kaempferol derivatives) present in Acalypha indica leaf extract, which remain adsorbed on the nanoparticle surface even after synthesis and washing. As demonstrated by Kirubakaran et al.29, the persistence of surface-bound phytochemicals enhances the electron-donating capacity of ZnO nanoparticles, enabling effective neutralization of DPPH free radicals. The observed antioxidant activity is particularly significant for acne treatment, as oxidative stress plays a key role in the inflammatory phase of acne vulgaris. The combination of antimicrobial and antioxidant properties in a single nanoparticle formulation represents a significant advantage over conventional acne treatments, which typically address only bacterial colonization or inflammation separately4.

For comparison, the standard antioxidant ascorbic acid was evaluated under identical experimental conditions. Ascorbic acid exhibited an IC₅₀ value of 18.6 ± 1.2 µg/mL, which is consistent with previously reported literature values. Although the biosynthesized ZnO nanoparticles demonstrated lower antioxidant activity than the standard antioxidant, their free radical scavenging activity remains significant because they are complex nanomaterials rather than pure antioxidant compounds. The antioxidant activity observed in the present study was superior to that reported for several other plant-mediated ZnO nanoparticles, including those synthesized using Andrographis echioides (IC₅₀ = 78.5 µg/mL)35, Vicoa indica (IC₅₀ = 85.3 µg/mL)47, and Melia azedarach (IC₅₀ = 70–85 µg/mL)7. The enhanced antioxidant activity of the Acalypha indica-mediated ZnO nanoparticles may be attributed to the abundance of bioactive phytochemicals, particularly flavonoids and tannins, that remain functionalized on the nanoparticle surface even after synthesis and purification. These phytochemicals likely enhance electron-donating capacity and thereby improve free radical scavenging efficiency.

It was evident from the experiment that the efficacy of antioxidants increased as the concentration of nanoparticles increased, thus showing concentration-dependent free radical scavenging capacity. This is consistent with the literature, which showed that the antioxidants property of ZnO nanoparticles mediated by plants enhanced due to phytochemical compounds available on the surface of nanoparticles. Conclusion

The present investigation has successfully illustrated a simple, eco-friendly, and economical approach towards the green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles using the leaf extract of Acalypha indica, an herbal plant with skin healing potential. In the green synthesis process, the extract containing different types of natural compounds like flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol), tannins (tannic acid), alkaloids (acalyphine), terpenoids, and glycosides, acted as natural reducing, stabilizing, and capping agents, and no toxic chemicals or external energy was required. Characterization of the samples prepared by UV-visible spectroscopy (absorption peak at 384 nm), FTIR (vibrations of Zn-O bond at 476 cm⁻¹ and 512 cm⁻¹ due to surface bound phytochemicals), XRD (wurtzite hexagonal phase, JCPDS No. 36–1451, crystallite size 18.5 ± 3.2 nm), and SEM (polydispersity and moderate agglomeration of the particles with irregular porous morphology) proved that the nanoparticles were effectively synthesized. The synthesized ZnO NPs showed highly effective, dose-dependent antibacterial activity against nine bacteria species, including 25 mm inhibition zone against Staphylococcus aureus and Salmonella typhi at 75 µg/well, indicating increased sensitivity towards Gram-positive bacteria owing to simpler cell wall structure of these organisms. Additionally, the nanoparticles possessed significant DPPH radical scavenging ability, with IC₅₀ value being 62.4 ± 2.1 µg/mL, due to the presence of adsorbed phytochemicals with electron donating ability on the nanoparticle surface. Three topical formulations, namely, nano soap, nanogel, and facial serum were successfully formulated using the ZnO NPs, which displayed compatible skin pH levels (5–6), physical stability, and high spreadability (nano soap: 47 mm, nanogel: 28 mm, facial serum: 21 mm at 150 g). The comparative study revealed that the nanogel formulation was the most promising therapeutic product for its potential to provide continuous skin contact, appropriate viscosity, and antibacterial activity (20 mm against Staphylococcus aureus at 75 µg), along with the herbal nano soap being the best choice for routine hygiene and facial serum for rapid spot application. The eco-friendly and cost-effective approach of green chemistry uses no harmful chemicals yet increases biological activity by using phytochemicals functionalization. From this study, it was concluded that the biogenic synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles using Acalypha indica is able to yield stable and multi-functional nanoparticles exhibiting strong antimicrobial and antioxidant activities; therefore, making the nanogel an alternative strategy for treating acne and skin infections by overcoming the rising problems of drug resistance and the harmful effects of artificial chemicals. Further research should focus on safety assessments of the formulations (skin irritation, sensitization, cytotoxicity), stability tests, synergistic antimicrobial mechanism studies, and clinical trials.
