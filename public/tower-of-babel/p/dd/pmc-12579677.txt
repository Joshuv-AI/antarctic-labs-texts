Comparing Real-World Outcomes of Prophylaxis with Extended Half-life Factor IX (rIX-FP vs. rFIXFc and N9-GP) for Haemophilia B: An Analysis of Medical Chart Data from Germany
Authors: Martin Olivieri, Songkai Yan, Ying Yang, Radovan Tomic, Thomas Linhoff, Xiang Zhang, Douglass Drelich, Natalie Jakobs, Wolfgang Miesbach
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn Germany, three extended half-life factor IX (FIX) products are commonly used to treat people with haemophilia B (PwHB). However, there remains a critical need to differentiate treatments for PwHB. The aim of this study was to assess the effectiveness and utilisation of rIX-FP compared with rFIXFc and N9-GP for prophylaxis in clinical practice in Germany.MethodsA retrospective chart review was performed for PwHB aged ≥ 12 years with moderate/severe haemophilia B, who received prophylaxis with rIX-FP, rFIXFc or N9-GP for ≥ 12 months. The primary outcome was FIX consumption; secondary outcomes included annualised bleeding rate (ABR), annualised spontaneous bleeding rate (AsBR) and annualised joint bleeding rate (AjBR).ResultsThe study included 138 PwHB: rIX-FP, n = 52; rFIXFc, n = 55; and N9-GP, n = 31. Mean FIX consumption with rIX-FP (46.9 IU/kg/week) was significantly lower than that of rFIXFc (70.1 IU/kg/week, p = 0.0083) but not significantly different from N9-GP (47.2 IU/kg/week, p = 0.9331). PwHB receiving rIX-FP prophylaxis had significantly lower mean bleeding rates than those receiving N9-GP (ABR: 0.8 vs. 1.5, p = 0.0472; AsBR: 0.1 vs. 0.6, p = 0.0092; and AjBR: 0.2 vs. 0.6, p = 0.0140). Bleeding rates for rIX-FP and rFIXFc did not differ significantly.ConclusionrIX-FP prophylaxis was associated with significantly lower FIX consumption and numerically (but not significantly) lower bleeding rates compared with rFIXFc. Compared to N9-GP, prophylaxis with rIX-FP was associated with similar FIX consumption and significantly lower bleeding rates.Keywords: Annualised bleeding rates, Dosing interval, Factor consumption, Haemophilia B, N9-GP, Prophylaxis, rIX-FP, rFIXFc

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?There are three extended half-life (EHL) factor IX (FIX) products (rIX-FP, rFIXFc, N9-GP) that are commonly available in Germany for the treatment of haemophilia B.This retrospective chart review of 138 people with haemophilia B (PwHB) aimed to assess the effectiveness and utilisation of these FIX products for prophylaxis in clinical practice across Germany.What was learned from this study?Mean FIX consumption with rIX-FP was significantly lower than that of rFIXFc but not significantly different from N9-GP. PwHB receiving rIX-FP prophylaxis had significantly lower mean bleeding rates than those receiving N9-GP. Bleeding rates for rIX-FP and rFIXFc did not differ significantly.Prophylaxis with the three EHL products generally led to low bleeding rates, while rIX-FP may further improve bleed protection compared with N9-GP or reduce consumption compared with rFIXFc.

Introduction

Haemophilia B is a rare X-linked bleeding disorder characterised by a deficiency or absence of coagulation factor IX (FIX) [1]. People with severe haemophilia B (defined as < 1% of normal FIX levels in the blood) can experience increased bleeding after trauma and frequent spontaneous bleeds, causing chronic pain, irreversible damage to joints and even death [1].

Prophylactic replacement with FIX concentrates is the current standard of care for people with severe haemophilia B [1, 2]. As a result of their half-life of approximately 18–24 h, standard half-life (SHL) FIX products require frequent intravenous infusions (2–3 times a week), resulting in a treatment burden on people with haemophilia B (PwHB) and caregivers, and treatment adherence is often reduced [1, 2]. Extended half-life (EHL) FIX products offer reduced administration frequency, potentially improving patient health-related quality of life and treatment adherence [1, 2].

There are three commonly prescribed EHL FIX products available in Germany; each one uses a different technology to extend the half-life of FIX. rIX-FP (albutrepenonacog alfa, IDELVION®, CSL Behring) is a highly purified recombinant fusion protein that links recombinant FIX (rFIX) to recombinant human albumin [3, 4]. rFIXFc (eftrenonacog alfa, ALPROLIX®, Sobi) is a fusion protein in which the Fc fragment of immunoglobulin G is fused to rFIX [5]. N9-GP (nonacog beta pegol, Refixia®, Novo Nordisk) is a conjunction between FIX and polyethylene glycol [6]. All three products have significantly improved half-life compared to SHL FIX products; the mean terminal half-lives of the products are 102 h (rIX-FP), 82 h (rFIXFc) and 85 h (N9-GP) [5, 7].

Beyond the clinical trial data, there are several post-marketing studies available for rIX-FP, rFIXFc and N9-GP; however, the majority of these compare clinical outcomes with a prior SHL FIX product [8–13]. Two post-marketing studies evaluating the clinical outcomes of rIX-FP in PwHB who switched from their prior SHL FIX product (either nonacog alfa or plasma-derived FIX product) to rIX-FP showed that the use of rIX-FP prophylaxis is associated with improved bleed protection and reduced FIX consumption compared to prophylaxis with a prior SHL FIX [8, 11]. Two-year follow-up data from the prospective, non-interventional PREVENT study in Germany showed that switching from a previous FIX product (either plasma-derived or recombinant) to rFIXFc prophylaxis reduced the number of infusions while maintaining bleed protection [12]. Similarly, a retrospective study from Canada showed switching from their previous FIX product (rFIXFc or SHL FIX product) to N9-GP led to low FIX consumption and optimal bleeding control [13].

However, to date, there is a lack of real-world studies comparing the clinical outcomes in PwHB receiving EHL products for prophylaxis. This study aimed to collect retrospective patient chart data to compare real‑world FIX consumption and bleeding outcomes for people with severe and moderate haemophilia B treated with rIX-FP versus those treated with rFIXFc, or N9-GP for prophylaxis in Germany.

Methods

A retrospective, de-identified chart review was performed for people aged ≥ 12 years with moderate or severe haemophilia B receiving rIX-FP, rFIXFc or N9-GP prophylaxis in 31 haemophilia treatment centres in Germany. Following institutional review board (IBR) evaluation, the study was determined to be exempt from patient informed consent under category 4 (IBR case name: CSL Behring - 2023-168, Patient chart analysis to assess real-world outcomes and utilisation of recombinant FIX products in Germany and Italy—IRB protocol#: [Pro00072842]) as secondary research for which consent is not required; this applied to all 31 haemophilia treatment centres involved in the study.

Study Population

Male PwHB who had been treated with rIX-FP, rFIXFc or N9-GP prophylaxis for a minimum of 12 months between January 1, 2020 and January 31, 2023 were included. PwHB with inhibitors to FIX, an unknown number of bleeds or any known congenital or acquired coagulation disorder other than haemophilia B were excluded.

Steps were taken to obtain robust and generalisable data and minimise bias. Centres from various locations were invited to participate to enhance geographically balanced data. Data collection protocols across all centres were standardised to ensure consistency in data entry and comparability of results. Healthcare professionals (HCPs) were instructed to randomly select charts from all eligible PwHB. The centre distribution was regularly evaluated, and recruitment approaches were adjusted, if necessary, to maintain a geographically balanced representation of centres throughout the study. The study sponsor remained anonymous to the participating HCPs during the sample collection to avoid potential bias in patient or brand selection.

Outcomes Assessed

The primary outcome measure was FIX consumption (IU/kg/week); FIX consumption was calculated on the basis of the most recent prescribed dosage and dosing frequency during the observation period.

The secondary outcomes were (1) dosing interval; (2) annualised bleeding rate (ABR); (3) annualised spontaneous bleeding rate (AsBR); (4) annualised joint bleeding rate (AjBR); (5) percentage of PwHB with zero total bleeds, zero spontaneous bleeds and zero joint bleeds. Data for ABR were available for all PwHB; however, data were not available for some PwHB to calculate AsBR or AjBR. All bleeding rates were calculated by annualising the number of bleeding events over the observation period.

Statistical Analyses

Based on the distribution of the data, a generalised linear model (GLM) regression was used to assess the differences in mean FIX consumption, mean ABR, AsBR and AjBR, and a logistic regression model was used to assess the differences in percentages of PwHB with zero total bleeds, zero spontaneous bleeds and zero joint bleeds among the FIX products, with additional covariates (patient characteristics) included for adjustment in each model.

In the comparisons for FIX consumption between the three products, the GLM included age, weight and disease severity as covariates, which were deemed the most important predictors for consumption in addition to the product variable, and the Gaussian family with an identity link function was selected. Statistical differences in dosing intervals were not assessed. In the comparisons for ABR, AsBR and AjBR, age, weight, disease severity and FIX consumption were added as covariates, and the negative binomial family was selected with a log link function. For the comparisons of the presence of any bleeds, spontaneous bleeds and joint bleeds, the logistic regression model incorporated age, weight, disease severity and FIX consumption as covariates. Adjusted p values from these models were obtained for comparison of the products. Statistical software Stata® (version 17.0 or later) was used.

Results

Study Population

A total of 138 PwHB were included in the analysis from 31 centres across Germany (Table 1). Overall, 52, 55 and 31 PwHB received rIX-FP, rFIXFc and N9-GP prophylaxis, respectively. The mean age was 31.9, 30.4 and 38.4 years for PwHB receiving rIX-FP, rFIXFc and N9-GP, respectively. The mean weight was 75.7 kg (rIX-FP), 73.5 kg (rFIXFc) and 74.5 kg (N9-GP). The proportion of people with severe haemophilia B within each group was as follows: rIX-FP, 57.7% (n = 30); rFIXFc, 61.8% (n = 34); and N9-GP, 45.2% (n = 14). The mean duration of observation was 26.5, 28.8 and 24.1 months for rIX-FP, rFIXFc and N9-GP, respectively.

PwHB receiving rIX-FP prophylaxis had a significantly lower mean AsBR than those treated with N9-GP prophylaxis (0.1 vs. 0.6, p = 0.0092). There was no significant difference in mean AsBR for PwHB receiving rIX-FP and rFIXFc (0.1 vs. 0.4, p = 0.2168). Also, there was no significant difference between the percentage of PwHB treated with rIX-FP and rFIXFc prophylaxis who achieved an AsBR of zero (84.8% vs. 71.8%, p = 0.1260). However, significantly more PwHB treated with rIX-FP prophylaxis achieved an AsBR of zero compared with those receiving N9-GP (84.8% vs. 47.6%, p = 0.0011). People with severe disease had a mean AsBR of 0.1, 0.5 and 0.7 for rIX-FP, rFIXFc and N9-GP, respectively; for those with moderate disease, the mean AsBR was 0.1, 0.1 and 0.5, respectively.

A significantly lower mean AjBR was observed in PwHB treated with rIX-FP prophylaxis than those receiving N9-GP prophylaxis (0.2 vs. 0.6, p = 0.0140). Mean AjBR for PwHB treated with rIX-FP was not significantly different compared with rFIXFc (0.2 vs. 0.5, p = 0.1557). In total, 66.7% of PwHB treated with rIX-FP achieved an AjBR of zero compared with 61.5% and 52.4% of PwHB treated with rFIXFc and N9-GP, respectively (p = 0.4507 and p = 0.0798). People with severe disease receiving rIX-FP had a mean AjBR of 0.2 versus 0.3 and 0.7 for those receiving rFIXFc or N9-GP, respectively; those with moderate disease had a mean AjBR of 0.1, 0.7 and 0.6, respectively.

Discussion

This analysis evaluated the real-world FIX consumption and effectiveness of three EHL FIX replacement products, by comparing rIX-FP with rFIXFc and N9-GP, used for prophylaxis in people with moderate or severe haemophilia B in Germany. The study found that FIX consumption in PwHB receiving prophylaxis with rIX-FP was significantly lower than that of rFIXFc, and comparable to that of N9-GP. The mean reported FIX consumption for rIX-FP prophylaxis in this study (46.9 IU/kg/week) is in line with the recommended prophylaxis dose based on current guidelines (35–50 IU/kg/week) [1, 14].

In addition, the trend in the mean dosing interval showed that PwHB on rIX-FP prophylaxis were able to extend their dosing intervals at a mean of 9.4 days, while the mean dosing intervals for rFIXFc and N9-GP were 7.5 and 7.9 days, respectively. The data also demonstrated statistically significantly lower mean ABR, AsBR and AjBR for rIX-FP than for N9-GP and numerically lower mean ABR, AsBR and AjBR for rIX-FP versus rFIXFc. These findings are in line with the clinical trial data for rIX-FP whereby dosing intervals could be extended to up to 10 or 14 days (or 21 days in selected adult PwHB) while maintaining effective bleeding control [7, 15–17]. For rFIXFc versus N9-GP, similar mean ABRs (1.4 vs. 1.5) were achieved, but with different doses (mean dose: 70.1 vs. 47.2 IU/kg/week); this may in part be due to the different pharmacokinetic profile and mechanism of action. The relationship between the pharmacokinetic profiles of FIX replacement products and the distribution of FIX in the extravascular space is under investigation; however, any potential impact on clinical outcomes is currently hypothetical [18].

Studies have demonstrated that switching from prior FIX products to rFIXFc or N9-GP results in reduced FIX consumption and improved ABRs [19–21]. The results of this study align with the outcomes of another real-world observational study in which rIX-FP was compared to prior SHL products for PwHB [8]. This study showed that PwHB who switched from a prior SHL FIX product to rIX-FP had decreased mean FIX consumption and ABR after the switch [8]. However, as mentioned earlier, real-world studies assessing the clinical outcomes of prophylaxis with rIX-FP compared with other EHL products are lacking.

Prophylaxis outcomes from real-world clinical practice are important to confirm the results reported in clinical trials and might provide more generalisable conclusions. Unlike participants in clinical trials meeting specific inclusion criteria [7, 16, 17], the population in real-world studies is more diverse (including demographic diversity and possible comorbidities), and reflective of how products are used in clinical practice.

Overall, this real-world observational study aligns with previous findings that suggest the introduction of EHL FIX products has enabled PwHB an opportunity to extend their dosing intervals and receive fewer infusions compared to SHL FIX products [1, 22]. These extended dosing regimens allow treatments to be tailored to patients on the basis of their specific needs and lifestyle factors, such as increased physical activity [1, 22, 23]. EHL FIX products may also be a useful treatment option for PwHB who experience frequent bleeds, including those with recurrent bleeding in joints (target joints), and/or show poor treatment adherence [23], because experiencing even a single bleed can have an impact on joint health and the future development of haemophilic arthropathy [1, 2]. For that reason, minimising bleeds is critical, even among treatment regimens with low bleeding rates.

Limitations

As with other similar retrospective, observational patient chart studies, potential selection bias may exist as a result of centre distribution, PwHB not being randomised, or observers not being blinded; therefore, the results may not be totally generalisable to the overall haemophilia B community. While it was not feasible to enforce a specific randomisation procedure for chart selection, each participating centre was responsible for selecting eligible charts according to their own criteria, which was totally independent from and not influenced by the authors. There is a risk of potential variation in patient management practices across contributing centres; while this variation may limit consistency of findings, this can also be an advantage in real-world evidence studies to capture outcomes in a variety of treatment approaches. Although not all three products compared were used in all participating centres, the majority of the centres (n = 22/31) contributed data for more than one product. While potential bias may be suspected, there is no clear pattern of such bias systematically in favour of any product. The general limitation of patient chart studies includes the potential risk of incomplete or inaccurate documentation. Collecting true baseline data on joint health and bleeding rates before using these products was not feasible for many patients since they had been on the products (particularly rIX-FP and rFIXFc) for multiple years, and data on physical activity are not well recorded, which limits the assessment of impact of confounding variables. The number of PwHB is relatively small because of the rarity of haemophilia B; however, it is noted that in 2021, 611 PwHB with severe or moderate disease were registered in Germany. Therefore, the number of PwHB sampled in this study should represent a good proportion of the PwHB in Germany who are currently being treated with one of the three FIX products compared here. Also, FIX consumption was calculated on the basis of the prescribed dosing regimen and may not accurately represent the real FIX consumption by PwHB. Finally, multiple bleeding outcomes were compared as secondary objectives of the study. While some comparisons of these outcomes showed statistically significant differences between rIX-FP and N9-GP, bleeding rates were generally low for the three products assessed. Randomised controlled trials comparing different products may be needed to validate the efficacy outcomes. Lastly, it is worth noting that the same design employed by this study was also used in other previously published patient chart review studies [24–27].

Conclusion

This retrospective patient chart analysis in Germany suggests that rIX-FP prophylaxis was associated with significantly lower FIX consumption compared with rFIXFc and numerically (but not significantly) lower than N9-GP. Prophylaxis with rIX-FP also showed potentially longer dosing intervals compared to rFIXFc and N9-GP and statistically significantly lower bleeding rates than N9-GP and numerically lower bleeding rates than rFIXFc.
