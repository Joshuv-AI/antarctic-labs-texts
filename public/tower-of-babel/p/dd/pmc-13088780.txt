Factors driving antibiotic use and antimicrobial resistance among college-going students in Bangladesh: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Nikkon Sarker, Kaushik Ahammad, Abu Jihan, Sultana Rajia, Shitom Baidya, Akibur Rahman, S M Sadman Sakib
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents a significant global health challenge, particularly in resource-limited nations such as Bangladesh, where irrational antibiotic use and weak regulatory enforcement persist. This research sought to determine the key factors associated with antibiotic usage behavior through a comprehensive assessment of participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAPs).MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from June to July 2025, involving 573 college-going students in Noakhali, Bangladesh. KAP levels were assessed using descriptive statistics, and the determinants of antibiotic use were further explored through multivariate analyses, including binary and ordinal logistic regression models.ResultsAmong the 573 participants, 52% were male, and 48% were female, with the majority (59%) being undergraduate students. The findings indicated that 67% of the participants had a science background, whereas the remainder had a non-science background. Students with a science background demonstrated superior knowledge compared to their non-science counterparts. Approximately 45% of respondents exhibited moderate knowledge, 50% displayed positive attitudes, and 76% demonstrated appropriate antibiotic use. The tendency toward self-medication without medical consultation was notably low, as 96% of students reported consulting a doctor before using antibiotics, and over 90% supported prohibiting non-prescription antibiotic use. Although the majority (69%) reported completing their last antibiotic course, a notable proportion discontinued treatment prematurely, indicating partial non-adherence. Factors such as age, education level, educational background, and household income significantly influenced the KAP.ConclusionTo strengthen global efforts against AMR, these findings show that customized educational programs are needed to improve students’ knowledge and encourage responsible antibiotic consumption. Moreover, governmental enforcement of laws and regulations to stop antibiotic misuse is critically important.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26822-6.Keywords: Antibiotics, Cross-sectional study, Antimicrobial resistance, KAP, Drug resistance, AMR, Bangladesh

Introduction

Antibiotics have revolutionized modern medicine, offering effective treatment for bacterial infections and saving countless lives since their discovery [1,2]. These powerful drugs act by targeting essential bacterial processes, either halting bacterial growth or killing the organisms outright [3]. However, the misuse and overuse of antibiotics have accelerated the development of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), where bacteria adapt to evade the effects of these drugs [4,5]. This poses a critical global health challenge, threatening to reverse decades of medical progress and increasing morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. In 2019, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) was linked to approximately 700,000 deaths globally, according to the World Health Organization (WHO). The organization also projected that this figure could escalate to 10 million deaths annually by 2050 if the current trajectory is not altered [6,7].

In many developing countries, AMR is exacerbated by inappropriate antimicrobial usage, often driven by practices among healthcare providers, pharmacies, and patients [8]. Southeast Asia, known for its rapid yet uneven economic development, has been identified as a focal point for emerging infectious diseases and AMR [9]. Low- and middle-income countries, including those in this region, bear a disproportionately high burden of AMR due to weak healthcare systems, poor food safety practices, and inadequate water and sanitation infrastructure [10]. Bangladesh, like many other developing nations, faces significant challenges in managing antibiotic use. The widespread availability and irrational use of antibiotics have contributed to the steady increase in antibiotic resistance [11,12]. Inappropriate antibiotic exposure has been associated with long-term health consequences and increased antimicrobial resistance, highlighting the importance of responsible antibiotic use across all age groups. However, young adults represent a particularly critical population, as this stage of life is characterized by increasing independence in healthcare decision-making and a higher likelihood of self-medication and unsupervised antibiotic use [13,14]. Moreover, due to their developing immune systems and frequent exposure to common infections, children are often prescribed antibiotics unnecessarily, further accelerating resistance patterns in pediatric populations [15]. In developing nations such as Bangladesh, the frequency of infections linked to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is significantly more pronounced among children [16]. Additionally, self-medication with antibiotics is a common practice, leading to the use of drugs with limited efficacy against specific pathogens, many of which have acquired resistance genes, including those conferring multidrug resistance [17,18]. The number of multi-drug resistant (MDR) bacteria in clinical infections, particularly Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, increased from 6.5% in 2010 to 11–14% in 2018 in Bangladesh [19]. These pathogens are commonly associated with urinary tract infections, bloodstream infections, respiratory tract infections, and wound infections, posing significant treatment challenges due to rising resistance.

Antimicrobial resistance poses a substantial and escalating public health challenge in Bangladesh. National surveillance and hospital-based studies consistently report high rates of resistance among common bacterial pathogens isolated from clinical specimens. Resistance to third-generation cephalosporins among Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae has been reported to exceed 70–80% in multiple tertiary-care settings, while resistance to fluoroquinolones commonly ranges between 60% and 75% [20]. Alarmingly, multidrug-resistant organisms such as Acinetobacter baumannii were found in over 60% of isolates in another hospital-based investigation, indicating widespread resistance beyond first-line agents [21]. Extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production is widespread, with ESBL-positive rates exceeding 50% among Enterobacterales in several hospital-based studies, significantly limiting the effectiveness of first-line therapies. Moreover, multidrug resistance has been reported in more than half of clinical isolates across multiple pathogen groups, reflecting extensive antibiotic exposure and suboptimal stewardship practices [22]. Collectively, these data illustrate the growing clinical and epidemiological burden of antimicrobial resistance in Bangladesh and underscore the urgent need for strengthened antibiotic stewardship, surveillance systems, and population-level awareness initiatives.

A clear association has been observed between insufficient knowledge of antibiotic use and elevated community-level metrics for both antibiotic consumption and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [23]. Addressing this challenge requires a deeper understanding of the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) surrounding antibiotic use, particularly among parents and healthcare providers. Effective interventions targeting these factors are crucial to improving the responsible use of antibiotics.

This study investigated the KAP related to antibiotic usage and AMR among college students in Noakhali, Bangladesh. College students were chosen because they represent an informed, socially engaged, and impressionable group who are not only frequent users of antibiotics often without prescriptions but also potential future advocates of public health. Their perceptions and behaviors can play a pivotal role in shaping responsible antibiotic use within their families and broader communities. Researchers hypothesized that a major deficiency in knowledge concerning the proper application of antibiotics exists among young people, resulting in frequent misuse. Moreover, it explored the sociocultural, economic, and healthcare system-related factors contributing to inappropriate antibiotic use. This research seeks to understand the behaviors and beliefs of this population, intending for these insights to help shape effective public health strategies and targeted interventions that can reduce antibiotic misuse and combat the rising danger of AMR.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study utilized a cross-sectional, population-based survey methodology to assess the Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) of college students within Noakhali, Bangladesh. Conducted between June and July 2025, the research aimed to uncover the connections between these KAP variables, the improper use of antibiotics, and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in this group. The study targeted students enrolled in both government and private colleges within the district.

Sampling

A multistage sampling approach was employed. In the first stage, nine colleges (government and private) were selected using convenience-based cluster selection, determined by accessibility and administrative permission. In the second stage, students were randomly selected within each participating college using official class lists as sampling frames.

The sample size was determined using Raosoft (http://www.raosoft.com/samplesize.html), with a 5% margin of error, a 95% confidence level, and a 50% response rate, the minimum recommended sample size was 385. We reached out to participants, and 573 of them completed the questionnaire. Data were collected from nine different colleges in Noakhali, which were selected based on accessibility and administrative permission. Both male and female students participated in the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were college-going students residing in the Noakhali district, had used antibiotics for themselves or their family members within the past 90 days, were in good physical health (capable of participating in the study), demonstrated mental stability (no known history of mental illness), and expressed willingness to participate. These criteria ensured that participants had recent and relevant experience with antibiotic use, were capable of understanding and responding to the questionnaire, and could provide informed consent. Physical and mental fitness were assessed solely on a self-reported basis, defined as the absence of any condition that would impair understanding or completion of the survey. No formal clinical, medical, or psychological screening tools were employed, as the study focused on self-reported knowledge, attitudes, and practices rather than clinical health status. In this study context, ‘college-going students’ refers to individuals enrolled in post-secondary institutions ranging from Higher Secondary (equivalent to grades 11–12) to Undergraduate and Postgraduate levels. Individuals were excluded if they were physically ill or injured, mentally unstable, had not used antibiotics for themselves or their family during the specified period, or were unwilling to take part in the study.

Types of data collected

Data were gathered through the use of structured questionnaires, which were divided into four sections. Section I focused on collecting sociodemographic details of the participants, such as their name, age, gender, marital status, educational background and qualifications, average monthly family income, and how they learned about antibiotics. Section II included questions aimed at assessing participants’ knowledge of antibiotic usage and antimicrobial resistance. Section III contained questions that explored how attitudes might affect antibiotic use. Section IV was dedicated to evaluating the participants’ practices regarding antibiotic use.

Study instrument

A semi-structured questionnaire was crafted (S1 File) in both English and Bangla to ensure clarity for all participants. This questionnaire aimed to gather sociodemographic details and insights into the knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to antibiotic use among college students. Most of the questions were sourced from previous research [16,24,25]. To enhance and finalize the questionnaire, experts in statistics and epidemiology were consulted, and adjustments were made based on the pilot study’s results. The completed questionnaire comprised 30 questions, categorized into four sections: demographic information, knowledge about antibiotic use, attitudes towards antibiotic use, and practices concerning antibiotic use.

Data collection

To enhance the survey response rate, questionnaires were distributed to students in advance, and data were collected through in-person interviews, each lasting between 20 and 30 min. A team of field data collectors conducted the data collection under the supervision of the study’s principal investigators. These data collectors received training in investigative techniques and research ethics from public health experts and microbiologists specializing in antibiotic use and misuse at the Department of Microbiology, Noakhali Science and Technology University, Bangladesh. The data collection team recruited participants and provided guidance in completing the questionnaire, following a uniform and standardized data collection procedure.

Data analysis

The data analysis was conducted using R Programming (version 4.2.1) after the data from the printed questionnaires, recorded on-site, were electronically transferred to a spreadsheet and verified. In the analysis, descriptive statistics for categorical variables (e.g., gender, education level, marital status) are presented as frequencies and percentages, while means and standard deviations are reported for all continuous variables. For the purpose of data presentation, questions were classified by their KAP category: ‘yes/no/don’t know’ for knowledge items, ‘agree/disagree/neutral’ for attitude items, and ‘yes/no’ for practice items.

Prior research has estimated the overall knowledge, attitudes, and practices [24,26,27]. The assessment of students’ Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAPs) relied on 10, 6, and 5 questions, respectively. A scoring system was established, assigning 1 point for a correct response and 0 points for an incorrect or uncertain response in the knowledge and attitude sections. For attitude items, correctness was determined by alignment with principles of antimicrobial stewardship [28]. Positive statements were scored as 1 for ‘Agree.’ Conversely, for negative statements indicating potential misuse or pressure on clinicians, a response of ‘Disagree’ was scored as 1, while ‘Agree’ or ‘Neutral’ received 0 points. For the practice section, 1 point was given for a correct response and 0 for an incorrect one. This method produced a raw score for each student in knowledge (out of 10), attitude (out of 6), and practice (out of 5). These scores were then converted to percentages and categorized. Knowledge and attitude were classified as ‘poor/negative’ (0–49%), ‘moderate/uncertain’ (50–79%), or ‘good/positive’ (80–100%). Practice was grouped differently, as ‘misuse’ (0–79%) or ‘good’ (80–100%). It is important to acknowledge that these thresholds are normative conventions. Alternative cut-offs could yield different prevalence estimates, and a sensitivity analysis using varying thresholds was not performed in this study.

All statistical analyses and visualizations were generated using R Programming (version 4.2.1). The analysis relied on logistic regression models to identify key predictors of health outcomes and assess their impact. Specifically, an ordinal logistic regression model was fitted to determine factors correlated with knowledge and attitudes. A binary logistic regression model was used to find factors associated with good practices. All analyses used a 95% confidence interval (CI), with a p-value of less than or equal to 0.05 serving as the threshold for statistical significance.

Ethics statement

The ethical review board of Noakhali Science & Technology University, Bangladesh, formally approved this research (Ref. No.: NSTU/SCI/EC/2025/457). Researchers obtained written informed consent from every participant before data collection. All information was gathered anonymously, and strict protocols were followed to maintain the privacy and confidentiality of the respondents.

Result

Sociodemographic profile of respondents

A total of 573 individuals participated in the study, most of whom were young adults. The largest age group was 18–23 years, comprising 78% of the sample, followed by 24–29 years (17%). A smaller proportion of participants were under 18 years of age (4%), and only 1% were older than 29. Gender distribution was relatively even, with 52% identifying as male and 48% as female. At the time of the survey, the majority of participants (59%) were enrolled at the undergraduate level, while 34% were pursuing higher secondary education. A smaller portion (6.3%) was engaged in postgraduate studies. In terms of academic background, most respondents were from the science stream (67%), followed by commerce (20%) and arts (13%), indicating a predominance of science-focused participants with a diverse mix of other disciplines. Consistent with the age profile, the majority of participants (90%) reported being single, while 9.9% were married. Monthly family income levels among respondents varied: 61% fell into the low-income bracket (earning less than 30000 BDT), 25% were in the middle-income range (30000 BDT – 50000 BDT), and 14% reported incomes above 50,000 BDT per month, classifying them as high-income (Table 1).

Assessment of knowledge on antibiotic resistance

The pattern of antibiotic knowledge among the study population is shown in Fig. 1. Among the 573 participants, 90% correctly identified that antibiotics kill bacteria, yet 25% believed they are effective against viruses, and 46% considered them useful for flu and cough. While 70% knew paracetamol is not an antibiotic, only 50% recognized metronidazole as one, with 42% unsure, and 8% incorrectly believed it was not an antibiotic, reflecting uncertainty about specific drug classifications. More than half (54%) reported experiencing side effects from antibiotics. Although 77% agreed that antibiotics effectively treat infections, a gap between awareness and comprehension of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) was evident. While 64% of participants reported having heard the term ‘Antibiotic Resistance,’ 44% were unsure of its specific definition, selecting ‘Don’t know’ when asked if AMR refers to the lack of antibiotic effectiveness; 61% linked it to antibiotic overuse. Participant responses varied across different knowledge-related items.

Participant attitudes on antibiotic use and misuse

Figure 2 shows the distribution of participant responses regarding awareness of antibiotic resistance. A high proportion agreed that antibiotics should not be taken without a doctor’s prescription (96%), and 91% supported regulatory enforcement to restrict non-prescription access, reflecting both personal belief and policy support in the Bangladeshi context, where over-the-counter sales of antibiotics are common in low- and middle-income countries. Only 24% reported feeling satisfied when prescribed antibiotics, while 57% disagreed. Reuse of leftover antibiotics was rejected by 61% of participants. About 68% recognized that antibiotic misuse contributes to antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Most respondents (63%) disagreed with taking antibiotics for any mild or severe illness.

Behaviors and practices related to antibiotic consumption

In this study, when asked specifically whether they seek medical advice before using antibiotics, the vast majority (96%) of students in Noakhali responded affirmatively. This finding is largely corroborated by a related practice question, where only 12% of students indicated a preference for purchasing antibiotics directly from a pharmacy without a doctor’s consultation. It has been found that a small proportion of students (26%) do not check the expiry date of the antibiotics before using them. Among the total student population, 31% reported not completing their full course of antibiotics, and among these, 21% discontinued treatment prematurely due to perceived recovery, highlighting symptom relief as a major contributor to non-adherence (Fig. 3).

Reported sources of information on antibiotics

The majority of students in Noakhali gained their awareness of antibiotic resistance primarily through healthcare prescribers (26%), with educational institutions playing the second most influential role (16%). Dispensers and other miscellaneous sources contributed significantly as well, each accounting for around 15% of the responses. The ‘Others’ category (15%) primarily comprises informal sources, including family members, friends, and peers, reflecting the strong influence of social circles on health information among students. Social media usage has impacted the awareness of antibiotic resistance, and it provides 9.6% of information. Traditional media such as newspapers and television had a more modest impact, cited by 6.3% and 6.1% respectively. Interestingly, pharmaceutical companies were the least referenced source, mentioned by only 5.9% of the respondents (Table 2).

Assessment of overall KAP levels on antibiotic resistance

Overall, among 573 participants assessed on knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP), 30.71% (n = 176) had good knowledge, 44.33% (n = 254) had moderate knowledge, and 24.96% (n = 143) had poor knowledge. Regarding practices, 76% (n = 437) reported good practices, while 24% (n = 136) indicated antibiotic misuse (Table 3).

An ordinal logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the socio-demographic factors that have an impact on the knowledge levels of antibiotic resistance among college-going students in Noakhali (Table 4). Male participants demonstrated significantly greater odds of knowledge (OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.08–2.12, p = 0.015) compared to females (Reference Category). Predictors of attitudinal levels on antibiotic resistance

The factors that are associated with the attitudes of college-going students towards antibiotic resistance in Noakhali were identified through an ordinal logistic regression analysis (Table 5). Postgraduate education emerged as a significant predictor, with postgraduates showing higher odds of specific attitudes compared to participants with higher secondary (Reference Category) education (OR = 3.08, 95% CI: 1.18–8.30, p = 0.023). A science background was also associated with significantly reduced odds of positive attitudes (OR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.33–0.98, p = 0.043) (Reference Category: Arts). Furthermore, single participants were more likely to exhibit particular attitudes than married (Reference Category) participants (OR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.52–5.48, p = 0.001). However, no significant associations were observed for age, gender, and family income level.

Education emerged as a key determinant, with undergraduate (OR = 1.97, 95% CI 1.19–3.28, p = 0.008) and postgraduate (OR = 20.1, 95% CI 4.60–145, p = < 0.001) participants showing significantly higher odds compared to those with higher secondary education (Reference Category), indicating good antibiotic practices. Discussion

In developing countries such as Bangladesh, where infectious diseases remain highly prevalent, weak regulatory enforcement and limited healthcare access contribute to the growing challenge of multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR). There is a deficiency of community-based research regarding antibiotic knowledge and attitudes; existing studies consistently indicate that individuals lack sufficient knowledge, possess inappropriate attitudes, and fail to engage in correct practices [25]. Our research provides substantial insights into the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of college students in Noakhali, Bangladesh, regarding antibiotic utilization and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Unlike earlier Bangladeshi studies that centered on the general public, patients, or healthcare professionals, this work shifts the focus to college-going students, an often overlooked yet crucial population. This group stands at a transitional stage between adolescence and adulthood, a period when self-medication and independent health decisions become increasingly common. By including students from a range of academic disciplines, not limited to health sciences, we were able to capture meaningful differences in awareness and behavior across fields of study. Furthermore, our integration of sociodemographic factors such as gender, age, and parental education provided a nuanced view of the drivers influencing antibiotic use and perceptions of AMR. Together, these elements position our study as one of the first to deliver a comprehensive, student-centered perspective on antibiotic practices and resistance in Bangladesh.

Many students were aware that antibiotics are meant to treat bacterial infections, yet they still reported using them for viral illnesses and frequently failed to complete prescribed courses. This disconnect highlights the persistent gap between knowledge and practice, showing that awareness alone does not guarantee responsible use. Such behavior is especially concerning in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), where weak regulation and easy access to antibiotics create conditions that amplify misuse among young populations. Our findings reveal a complex landscape of strengths and shortcomings in public understanding. Although 90% of students correctly identified antibiotics as useful against bacterial infections, 46% reported using them for coughs and colds, and 25% believed they were also effective against viral disorders. These results are consistent with data from comparable contexts in Bangladesh and other low- and middle-income nations, where such beliefs result in irrational antibiotic consumption [29,30,31]. A lack of distinction between bacterial and viral infections contributes to inappropriate self-medication and erroneous prescriptions [32,33]. Furthermore, a specific and concerning gap identified in this study was the inability of participants to classify common drugs correctly; only 50% of students recognized metronidazole as an antibiotic, while a significant portion (42%) remained unsure. In Bangladesh, where metronidazole is frequently self-prescribed for diarrheal episodes, this lack of awareness is alarming as it suggests many young adults may view it as a general remedy rather than a restricted antimicrobial. While 64% of the participants reported having heard of AMR, 44% of the total population remained uncertain about its development. This distinction is important since there is a strong correlation between learning about AMR and using antibiotics correctly [34]. This finding aligns with WHO’s observation that awareness of AMR alone does not guarantee the correct use of antibiotics, underscoring the need for educational interventions that not only increase awareness but also actively promote behavior change strategies to ensure proper antibiotic practices.

Although 96% of students opposed non-prescription antibiotic use and 91% supported restricting over-the-counter access, these positive attitudes contrast sharply with the reality in Bangladesh, where over-the-counter antibiotic sales remain widespread due to weak regulatory enforcement and unregulated community pharmacies. This contrast underscores that while awareness among young adults is encouraging, meaningful change requires stronger regulation and enforcement to align actual practices with community beliefs [25]. Previous studies reported that in Europe and Asia, patient expectations often influenced doctors to prescribe antibiotics unnecessarily [35,36,37]. Nevertheless, 24% of students reported feeling reassured when prescribed antibiotics, suggesting a perception that antibiotics are superior treatments. On a positive note, 76% of respondents demonstrated appropriate antibiotic practices, and 96% consistently sought guidance from their clinicians before using antibiotics. However, while this high rate of doctor consultation is encouraging, the finding that 12% of students still prefer obtaining antibiotics directly from pharmacies represents a critical loophole in antimicrobial stewardship. In the context of Bangladesh, where regulatory enforcement at retail pharmacies is often weak and over-the-counter sales are common, this ‘pharmacy-first’ subgroup—though a minority in this educated cohort, demonstrates that easy accessibility continues to undermine prescription-only policies. This pathway facilitates the use of unprescribed drugs, bypassing clinical oversight and significantly increasing the risk of inappropriate drug selection and dosing.

Despite encouraging findings, certain behaviors remain concerning. Nearly one-third of students (31%) admitted to not completing their prescribed antibiotic courses, while 21% stopped treatment as soon as their symptoms improved. These patterns, though common, are especially troubling because they create favorable conditions for bacteria to survive, adapt, and ultimately develop resistance [38,39]. Frequent physician consultation showed that having knowledge and positive attitudes did not always ensure strict adherence, highlighting the need for behavior-focused interventions. Future educational interventions must therefore prioritize ‘adherence literacy’—explicitly teaching students that symptom relief does not equate to bacterial eradication, and empower healthcare providers to reinforce this message during every consultation. Regression analysis also identified clear links between sociodemographic factors and antibiotic use practices. Male students demonstrated significantly higher knowledge scores than female students (OR = 1.52, p = 0.015), indicating that gender was associated with differences in antibiotic-related knowledge. This suggests that targeted educational efforts may be needed to address knowledge gaps across different student groups [40]. In this study, science and commerce students consistently exhibited higher levels of understanding and more responsible practices compared to their peers in the arts. This disparity highlights potential gaps in health literacy among arts students, emphasizing the importance of integrating targeted educational initiatives to foster better awareness and promote prudent antibiotic use across all academic streams [41]. However, a discordant pattern emerged regarding attitudes; students with a science background demonstrated significantly reduced odds of possessing positive attitudes toward antibiotic resistance compared to their peers (OR = 0.57). This counter-intuitive finding suggests a divergence between theoretical understanding and attitudinal alignment with stewardship. It is possible that science curricula focus heavily on the biological mechanisms of drug action. Furthermore, science students may develop a sense of ‘intellectual confidence’ regarding drug usage, compared to non-science students, who may view antibiotic misuse with greater caution due to a lack of technical familiarity. Additionally, postgraduate education was associated with more favorable attitudes and the highest levels of appropriate antibiotic practices, reflecting that higher educational attainment corresponded with both greater awareness and more responsible use of antibiotics [42]. However, the magnitude of this association (OR = 20.1) should be interpreted with caution. The relatively small sample size of postgraduate students (n = 36) and the resulting wide confidence interval (4.60–145) suggest that while the direction of the association is robust, the precise effect size may be overestimated due to sparse data or unmeasured confounding.

Additionally, socioeconomic factors appeared to influence behaviors, with students from lower-income households (< 30,000 BDT/month) demonstrating higher adherence to rational antibiotic practices compared to their more affluent peers. While it is possible that financial limitations may deter impulsive purchasing, this association should be interpreted with caution. The cross-sectional design of the study does not allow for definitive conclusions regarding causality or the specific motivations driving this behavior. Rather than attributing this solely to financial constraints, reinforcing responsible use, it is plausible that broader differences in healthcare access, exposure to prescription practices, or treatment-seeking norms may partially explain this disparity [43,44].

The findings from this investigation provide reliable insights into the KAP of college students in Noakhali concerning their antibiotic use and understanding of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). By including students from a wide range of academic disciplines and diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, the findings present a representative picture of the local student population. This approach helps to highlight the specific patterns and behaviors that contribute to the misuse of antibiotics and the spread of AMR. These results lay a strong foundation for future interventions and policy development. In particular, targeted educational programs are necessary to address knowledge gaps, especially among students in arts disciplines, who were observed to have lower awareness of proper antibiotic use. Reinforcing the importance of completing prescribed antibiotic courses is also crucial, as incomplete or inappropriate use can accelerate the development of resistance.

Further research extending beyond college students to include broader community populations, healthcare providers, and rural areas could uncover additional factors influencing antibiotic misuse. Conducting longitudinal studies would provide valuable insights into how behaviors change over time and allow for the assessment of the effectiveness of intervention strategies. Finally, incorporating AMR awareness into educational curricula and broader public health campaigns could play a central role in encouraging responsible antibiotic practices. These efforts are essential not only for southern Bangladesh but also for other low- and middle-income settings facing similar challenges, where the spread of antimicrobial resistance poses a significant public health threat.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. The inclusion criteria required participants to have used antibiotics for themselves or their family within the past 90 days. While this ensured that respondents had recent, recallable experiences with antibiotic procurement and adherence, it limits the generalizability of the findings to active antibiotic users. Consequently, the study may not reflect the knowledge or attitudes of healthy students who do not frequently use antibiotics. Furthermore, it is possible that this inclusion criterion resulted in slightly inflated estimates of knowledge and good practices relative to the general student population, as recent users may have benefited from recent counseling or interaction with the healthcare system.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding antibiotic use among college students in Bangladesh. While baseline awareness is evident, the persistence of critical misconceptions, specifically regarding viral efficacy, treatment discontinuation, and self-medication, demonstrates that awareness alone does not guarantee rational behavior. The identification of distinct demographic predictors necessitates a shift from uniform awareness campaigns to stratified, evidence-based interventions. Educational initiatives must be tailored: targeting non-science students to bridge foundational knowledge gaps, while focusing on stewardship training for science students to mitigate attitudinal overconfidence. Furthermore, as older students exhibited significantly lower knowledge levels, campus-based refresher campaigns are essential to sustain awareness beyond initial secondary education. Beyond education, curbing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) requires systemic reinforcement, including stricter regulatory oversight of pharmacy dispensing and the integration of AMR modules into national academic curricula. Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs to evaluate the efficacy of these targeted interventions and capture community-level drivers of misuse.

By fostering an informed student population and embedding responsible antibiotic practices within educational and social frameworks, Bangladesh can take meaningful steps toward curbing AMR and safeguarding public health for future generations.
