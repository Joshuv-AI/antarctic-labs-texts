Circulating IncRNAs and Their Association With Ischemic Stroke Severity and Prognosis: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Mehran Jaberinezhad, Parnia Pouya, Anahita Najafi, Arman Shafiee, Saba Maleki, Parnian Shobeiri, Antonio L Teixeira, Mehdi Farhoudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsStroke is the most significant contributor to morbidity worldwide. Prognostication in stroke patients could lead to better planning and care and, subsequently, better outcomes. Recently, lncRNAs have become a hot topic as biomarkers of different diseases. This study aims to systematically assess the utility of lncRNAs in stroke prognosis.MethodsWe systematically searched for relevant articles in Medline (via PubMed interface), EMBASE, Web of Science, and Scopus databases from their inception to April 6th, 2024. Inclusions were limited to original peer‐reviewed papers in English that assessed the correlation between circulating lncRNA levels and stroke severity (all subtypes) and/or prognosis. Completeness of the report was evaluated with the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology” (STROBE) checklist, and quality assessment was performed with “Quality in Prognosis Studies” (QUIPS) and “Newcastle Ottawa scale” (NOS) checklists.ResultsOf the 1134 unique citations gathered, 60 were assessed in full text for inclusion, and 30 were included in the final analysis. Although our search strategy covered all stroke subtypes, no eligible studies were found for hemorrhagic stroke; thus, only ischemic stroke and TIA populations were included. We included 30 studies in our analysis, which included 3719 patients and 4433 data points. The pooled correlation between circulating lncRNAs and NIHSS was 0.52 (CI95%:0.43–0.60), and circulating lncRNAs and the modified Rankin Scale was 0.49 (CI95%:0.26–0.67). The pooled odds ratio for the mortality prediction was 1.55 (CI 95%:1.26–1.92). Quality assessment of included studies showed that most of the studies had a low risk of bias and high completeness of report.ConclusionLncRNAs show modest to moderate predictive accuracy in assessing stroke severity and prognosis. While these findings are encouraging, Further research is needed to solidify their utility in stroke care.Keywords: IncRNA, non‐coding RNA, prognosis, stroke

Introduction

Stroke has a significant health impact in developing and developed countries alike, ranking as the second leading cause of death (11.6% of total deaths) and the third leading cause of death and disability combined (5.7% of total disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) [1]. It also has a significant economic impact, as recovery often requires extensive medical treatment and rehabilitation, and because of the loss of productivity [2]. As the epidemiological transition shifts towards non‐communicable diseases in low and middle‐income countries, the global burden of stroke is expected to increase [3, 4, 5]. Advances in medical technology have led to an increase in survival rates for stroke, but such methods are costly. Despite treatment, survivors often experience long‐term effects such as physical disabilities, neuropsychiatric problems, and speech and cognitive impairment [6, 7, 8].

Better methods of prognostication can assist in discharge planning, help determine the intensity of therapeutic and rehabilitative interventions and hospital stays, and avoid futile treatments [9]. Accordingly, prognostic biomarkers could optimize healthcare service utilization, minimizing costs. These are critical issues in health systems with limited resources, especially in developing countries facing a growing burden of chronic diseases.

Long non‐coding RNAs are a class of RNA that has gained substantial attention in recent years. They consist of RNA sequences of more than 200 nucleotides that are not transcribed but nonetheless can regulate genes at the transcriptional or later levels [10]. LncRNAs can act, for example, as miRNA “sponges” [11], preventing miRNAs from completing their regulatory function [12]. Using highly sensitive methods of RNA analysis (e.g., RT‐qPCR, microarray analysis), lncRNAs can be detected in different tissues and fluids such as blood and urine [13]. Changes in lncRNA expression have been reported in several diseases, including cancers and neurodegenerative diseases [14, 15]. Because of their unique properties, they can be considered both markers of disease and therapeutic targets. For example, lncRNA RMST can regulate the direction of differentiation of neuronal progenitor cells and oligodendrocyte maturation by binding to the SOX target gene [16].

Previous literature has shown that lncRNA plays a role in the pathophysiology of ischemic and ischemia‐reperfusion injuries. LncRNAs can act as intermediary factors that affect the related inflammatory cascade. Notable examples of the involvement of lncRNAs in this pathway are lncRNA MEG3, which increases ischemia‐reperfusion. Through regulation of Krüppel‐like factor 4 (KLF4), lncRNA MEG3 induces M1 microglia (inflammation‐inducing) polarization and inflammation. Conversely, lncRNA taurine upregulated gene 1 (TUG1) is similarly increased in ischemia and leads to neuronal apoptosis by decreasing bcl2 protein [17, 18].

Although lncRNAs have shown promise in predicting outcomes in various conditions, their prognostic accuracy and clinical utility in ischemic stroke have not been systematically analyzed. This review aims to synthesize available evidence on the relationship between circulating lncRNAs and stroke severity and prognosis. Understanding these biomarkers could be crucial in advancing personalized medicine and tailored treatment plans, as well as improving clinical decision‐making in stroke care. In this study, our objective was to compile lncRNAs relevant in the setting of cerebrovascular disease and assess their utility as prognostic factors and their correlation with stroke severity.

Materials and Methods

Protocol and Registration

This systematic review complies with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [19]. The protocol for this study was registered in PROSPERO with the following number: CRD42024514120. PRISMA checklist is also available as Supporting File 1.

Review Question (PICOTS)

This study's target population includes papers examining patients who have experienced any type of stroke—such as ischemic, hemorrhagic, or subarachnoid hemorrhage—or transient ischemic attack (TIA). The comparator in our study was the circulating lncRNAs extracted from blood, plasma, or serum. The primary outcome of interest is mortality and disability at discharge and during follow‐up, assessed by the mRS scale. Blood samples were collected within the first 72 h of hospital admission to better predict the clinical course of the disease. The included studies were conducted in hospital settings where stroke patients are initially referred to and evaluated by physicians.

Eligibility Criteria

To evaluate the predictive role of lncRNAs in stroke outcomes, we incorporated all original studies that reported on the relationship between serum lncRNAs and patient mortality or morbidity. Only studies involving human subjects, written in English, were considered. Non‐peer‐reviewed articles, non‐English papers, reviews, letters, commentaries, case reports, and case series were excluded.

Information Source and Search Strategies

Using the related keywords, a systematic search was done in Medline (via PubMed interface), EMBASE, Web of Science, and Scopus for English publications until April 6, 2024, without any time limitations. A detailed search strategy can be found in the Supporting File 2.

Data Management and Selection Process

All articles identified through our systematic search were imported into EndNote X20 (Clarivate analysis, PA). After duplicates were removed, two authors (M.J. and A.N.) independently screened the studies based on their titles and abstracts to identify all eligible articles. Full texts of the selected studies were then obtained and reviewed by M.J., P.P., and A.N. according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. One of the other two investigators checked these reviews for discrepancies and completeness. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus.

Data Collection Process and Data Items

From each included study, we extracted the following information: year of publication, authors' names, number of included participants, type of lncRNA, modified Rankin scale (mRS), and survival statistics, including 30 and 90‐day mortality. Regarding secondary outcomes, we gathered data on cognitive impairment, length of hospital stays, speech disorders, recurrence of stroke, dysphagia, and autonomic dysfunction.

Risk of Bias Assessment

To evaluate the quality of observational studies, we used the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology” (STROBE) checklist [20]. This checklist contains 22 items, with some broken into more specific subitems. If a subitem was addressed in the relevant section of the paper, it received one point; if not, zero points. Certain items might not have applied to all articles, so they were excluded from scoring. The completeness of reporting (COR) score for each study was calculated as: COR score = ∑1/[∑ 1 + ∑ 0]. We classified scores above 70% as “low risk,” 40‐70% as “medium risk,” and below 40% as “high risk.”

“Quality In Prognosis Studies” (QUIPS) checklist was used to assess the risk of bias in six domains in the included studies: “study participation,” “study attrition,” “prognostic factor measurement,” “outcome measurement,” “adjustment for other prognostic factors,” and “statistical analysis and reporting.” [21, 22] The overall risk of bias was determined based on consensus among the authors. Categorization is as follows:

Studies were deemed as having a high risk of bias if either they had 2 or more domains with “high risk of bias,” or 4 or more domains with “moderate risk of bias,” or 1 “high risk of bias” and 2 or more “moderate risk of bias.” Studies were deemed as having a moderate risk of bias if they had only one domain with a “high risk of bias” or three domains with a “moderate risk of bias” or 1 “high risk of bias” and 1 “moderate risk of bias.” Studies were deemed as having a low risk of bias only if they had two or fewer domains with a “moderate risk of bias.”

The Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for studies that solely reported on the severity of stroke of lncRNA levels and did not investigate the prognostic value of lncRNAs. We only appraised the clinical methodology and reporting for the studies, including both human and animal or cell line research. A score of higher than 7 was considered low risk of bias, 5–6 moderate risk of bias, and 4 or lower high risk of bias.

Two reviewers (M.J. and P.P.) assessed the risk of bias and quality assessment in the studies included in the meta‐analysis utilizing the STROBE and QUIPS (or NOS). A third investigator was consulted whenever the other two reviewers were unsure how to score an item or the study.

Statistical Approach

R software, along with the meta package, was used for all statistical calculations and for generating graphical representations. The analysis was carried out using the Fisher Z transformation to pool the correlation meta‐analysis and the ln (Odds ratio) along with their 95% confidence interval (CI) for dichotomous meta‐analysis. The I2 statistic and the Chi‐square test were utilized to assess the variation across the studies included. The degree of heterogeneity was classified into low (I 2 < 25%), moderate (I 2 = 26–75%), and high (I 2 > 75%) categories. Sensitivity analysis was performed for NIHSS and mortality meta‐analyses. A funnel plot was employed to check for any publication bias by examining plot asymmetry for those outcomes with at least 10 effect sizes. Missing data handling is not relevant in this work. The threshold for statistical significance was set at a p‐value below 0.05.

Results

The study selection process is illustrated as a PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). The flowchart (Figure 1) shows that 3122 records were retrieved from the systematic database search, and one record was added through other means. 1989 duplicate records were automatically removed using EndNote Software, and 1134 records were screened. Of them, 60 records were considered eligible for full‐text assessment. Finally, 30 studies were included in the data synthesis. While our inclusion criteria covered all stroke subtypes, the current evidence is limited to ischemic stroke and TIA, as no studies investigating hemorrhagic stroke met the inclusion criteria.

All studies were published in the past 7 years. Twenty‐five studies were conducted in China, four in Egypt, and one in Iran. In total, this study includes 3719 patients, representing 4433 data points. Among these patients, 231 had TIA, while the remaining 3488 were diagnosed with AIS. None of the included studies investigated hemorrhagic stroke, and only one study investigated TIA [23], with the remainder focusing on ischemic stroke. The results of our assessment of the included studies are presented in Table 2. All studies except one had good COR (< 0.7), and the only study below the threshold scored 0.698 [25]. The majority of the studies with follow‐up for determining prognosis had a low risk of bias, with one study having a moderate risk of bias. Nineteen prognostic studies were included, with 18 demonstrating a low risk of bias and one showing a moderate risk according to the QUIPS checklist (Table 3). Eleven studies assessed the correlation between circulating lncRNAs and stroke severity; 10 of these scored between 2 and 6 points (fair quality), while one study scored ≥ 7 points (good quality) according to the NOS checklist. According to the QUIPS checklist, only one study had a moderate risk of bias due to “study confounding,” while all other prognostic studies exhibited a low risk of bias. Conversely, based on the NOS checklist, all but one severity study failed to achieve full scores for “representativeness” and “selection of controls.” Details of the individual items in the checklist can be found in Supporting Material 3. Of the 11 studies assessed using the NOS checklist, 10 had a moderate risk of bias, and 1 had a low risk of bias (Table 4). Overall, the majority of the included studies had a low risk of bias and a high level of completeness of reports.

Stroke Severity and Circulating IncRNA Levels

Twenty‐four studies reported the correlation between stroke severity and circulating lncRNAs. As shown in (Figures 2 and 3), the pooled correlation is 0.53 (0.44–0.60). However, heterogeneity remains high (I 2 = 86%), which is expected given the multiple lncRNAs investigated.

Stroke prognosis and circulating lncRNA levels

Four studies reported the correlation between mRS and circulating lncRNAs [25, 26, 27, 28]. The pooled correlation was 0.49 (CI 95%: 0.26–0.67) (Figure 8). While this result is significant, it suggests only a moderate correlation between the two factors, not a strong one.

Forest Plot of the Meta‐analysis of mRS and circulating lncRNAs correlation. The plot shows the pooled correlation between lncRNA expression levels and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores. Squares represent study‐specific estimates; horizontal lines, their 95% CIs; and the diamond, the pooled correlation (random‐effects model). CI, confidence interval; lncRNA, long non‐coding RNA.

Three studies also categorized patients as having “poor functional outcome” versus “favorable functional outcome.” [26, 27, 28] In their investigation of MEG3, Meiping Wang et al. considered an mRS score of more than 4 at 6 months a poor outcome and reported an odds ratio of 1.998 (CI95%:1.144–3.491) [26]. Man Zhu et al. studied MIAT and considered the patient to have a favorable outcome at 3 months if their mRS score was 2 or lower. They reported an odds ratio of 1.709 (CI 95%:1.058–2.761) [27]. Zhong‐zhong Liu et al. studied LIPCAR and defined the mRS score of 3–6 at follow‐up 12 months after stroke as a poor outcome and reported an odds ratio of 0.28 (CI95%:0.07–1.22) [28].

Recurrence was assessed in three studies [23, 28, 29], and the reported odds ratios ranged from 1.02 (0.6–1.74) to 21.591 (5.884–79.22).

The included studies reported mortality/overall survival in numerous ways. Some compared the high and low expression of lncRNAs using Kaplan–Meier curves and log‐rank tests [26, 27, 30, 31, 32]. Among the five studies, only two reported non‐significant p‐values [30, 31]. Some other studies reported an area under the curve (AUC) for the receiver operator curve (ROC), which ranged between 0.649 (CI95%:0.514–0.756) to 0.778 (CI95%:0.678–0.819) [27, 33, 34]. Three studies reported a hazard ratio [30, 31, 32].

Finally, only four of these studies could be included in the meta‐analysis since these were the only ones that reported odds ratios [26, 27, 28, 29]. The pooled odds ratio is calculated at 1.55 (1.26–1.92) with a fair amount of heterogeneity (I 2 = 42%). (Figure 9).

Forest Plot of the meta‐analysis of circulating lncRNAs and mortality. Each horizontal line denotes a 95% CI for an individual study's odds ratio (OR), and the diamond indicates the pooled OR calculated using a random‐effects model. The size of the squares reflects study weight. CI, confidence interval; lncRNA, long non‐coding RNA.

Kaplan‐Meier curves assessed recurrent free survival in 9 studies [30, 31, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. Only two of these studies reported p‐values higher than 0.05 [30, 35]. Three studies reported hazard ratios, which ranged between 0.071 (CL95%: 0.006–0.905) to 0.482 (CI95%:0.157–1.473) [30, 31, 35].

Four studies reported AUC values for the prediction of mortality [27, 33, 34]. They ranged from 0.649 (0.514–0756) to 0.778 (0.678–0.819). Full extraction tables of the prognostic statistics are presented in Supporting Material 4.

The only study investigating lncRNAs in TIA predicted a very high odds ratio for KCNQ1OT1 in the recurrence of stroke with OR = 21.591 (CI95%:5.884–79.220) [23].

A sensitivity analysis was performed for the aforementioned meta‐analysis of NIHSS and mortality. In the mortality meta‐analysis, the majority of the heterogeneity is caused by the Zhong‐zhong Liu et al. study. By omitting that, we observed an improvement in I 2 = 82% (Figure 10). For the NIHSS meta‐analysis, omitting any single study does not remarkably impact the results (Figure 11).

Discussion

In this review, we compiled a list of lncRNAs that have been investigated as blood/serum prognostic factors in cerebrovascular events. We observed a modest correlation between serum levels of lncRNAs and stroke severity, as assessed by NIHSS (r = 0.52). Collectively, the available studies suggest only modest to moderate prognostic accuracy. While lncRNAs appear to have potential for outcome prediction, their clinical utility remains preliminary and requires further validation. There was also some discrepancy in our results. This forced our hand to exclude one paper from the meta‐analysis and discuss potential explanations for this reporting in this section.

One of the included papers made opposite claims regarding the up‐/down‐regulation of certain lncRNAs. This study investigated NEAT1, HOTAIR, and GAS5 and compared the results between patients with AIS and hypertension and AIS patients without hypertension. NEAT1 and GAS5 were reported to be negatively associated, and HOTAIR was reported to be positively associated with NIHSS [41]. NEAT1 was reported to be upregulated and positively correlated with NIHSS values in the remainder of the included studies [24, 40]. A similar story is observed in the case of GAS5 [36]. Ali et al. explain this discrepancy by pointing out that previous research on the role of NEAT1 in AIS showed potential in reducing oxidative stress and inflammation in animal models and increased cell viability in oxygen‐glucose deprivation (OGD) cell models. Similarly, GAS5 is explained to have protective effects, aiding in resolving the inflammatory response [41]. Based on these, they extrapolated that higher NEAT1 and GAS5 expression could lead to lower NIHSS.

However, there is a considerable body of evidence suggesting otherwise. In vivo experiments demonstrated that NEAT1 had abnormally high expression after MCAO. Research also suggests that knockdown of NEAT1 could significantly alleviate brain damage by reducing the number of activated microglia and reducing their release of proinflammatory cytokines [42]. Furthermore, another study that performed a follow‐up on enrolled patients reported poor recurrent free survival [40]. In the case of GAS5, all the studies we found suggest that silencing of GAS5 inhibits ischemia‐induced neuronal injury in both in vivo and in vitro studies [43, 44, 45, 46].

Many other biomarkers have been studied as prognostic factors. Functional and structural imaging using MRI in stroke could also prove useful in prognostication. For example, diffusion tensor imaging of the integrity of the corticospinal tract is a predictor of motor recovery in stroke patients [47]. Analysis of dynamic connectivity using resting‐state fMRI is also shown to accurately predict post‐stroke recovery [48]. However, their applicability in stroke patients is more challenging given the risks of transporting patients to the scanner and keeping them stable inside there, especially in patients with more severe strokes. The considerable cost of such imaging modalities also limits their usefulness in clinical settings. A recent systematic review compiled blood/serum‐based biomarkers and concluded that C‐reactive protein (CRP), albumin, copeptin, and d‐dimer were statistically associated with stroke outcomes [49]. Among them, the most important and readily available factor is CRP, which is a predictor of higher mortality (OR = 2.751) [50] and recurrence (HR = 1.14) [51].

Among noteworthy prognostic tools in the subject are ABCD2 and THRIVE‐c scoring systems. ABCD2 assesses the risk of stroke following a TIA. A meta‐analysis of studies on this score showed a sensitivity of 66% and specificity of 59% in predicting stroke in a 90‐day follow‐up [52]. The only included study focused on a lncRNA in TIA reported 63% and 72% sensitivity and specificity, respectively [23]. A comparison of these values shows that lncRNAs can be as effective in prognostication for TIA patients. THRIVE‐c is used for the prediction of functional outcomes at 90 days post‐stroke. A meta‐analysis of over 12,000 subjects reported an AUC of 0.785 [53]. Two of the included studies reported AUC values for functional outcomes; they reported 0.589 and 0.791 for H19 and MEG3, respectively [27, 33]. Comparing these values, we can surmise that MEG3 seems especially promising in this regard.

Compared with other prognostic markers, lncRNAs may have the potential to be feasible blood‐based indicators due to their measurable and cost‐effective nature [54]. However, their current predictive performance is modest, and more standardized validation studies are needed before they can be considered clinically reliable. With the recent surge of artificial intelligence (AI) used in medicine, we are observing the utilization of these methods in predictive models. A recent study surmised that AI models have a remarkably high accuracy in predicting stroke outcomes [55]. Combining lncRNAs with other biomarkers in a more comprehensive model of stroke prognosis could result in the development of more cost‐effective and efficient prognostication methods.

Caution should be exercised in the interpretation of results from lncRNA studies. One of the possible explanations for the discrepancy in results could be the timing of the sampling. It is possible that in the first few hours of the stroke, there are considerable changes in the circulating lncRNA levels, leading to conflicting results even among studies limiting their sample gathering to the first day of the stroke. For example, this pattern is seen for lncRNA XIST, which decreases after stroke, reaching very low levels 2 days after stroke and progressively increasing thereafter with higher levels compared to baseline after the fourth day [56].

Another point to consider is the involvement of these lncRNAs in several different diseases. Notably, many of the included lncRNAs have been implicated in playing a role in the pathophysiology of certain cancers. (e.g., PVT1 in breast and lung cancer [57, 58]) This means that there will be limitations regarding their clinical applicability in patients with cancer who are referred to hospitals with stroke. For example, PVT1 is up‐regulated in both lung cancer patients [59]. and in stroke patients [38, 60] compared to healthy individuals, and is also a marker of poor prognosis in stroke. Consequently, if a lung cancer patient is hospitalized after experiencing a stroke, we may not be able to draw a meaningful conclusion regarding the prognosis of stroke from PVT1 levels.

Herein, we further discuss mechanisms of action and pathways related to each lncRNA and how they relate to stroke severity. These items were more widely studied and/or integral to major molecular pathways involved in stroke injury.

NEAT1 is transcribed from the familial tumor syndrome multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) type 1 loci on chromosome 11q13.1 [61]. In vivo experiments demonstrated that NEAT1 had abnormally high expression after MCAO. Research also suggests that knockdown of NEAT1 could significantly alleviate brain damage by reducing the number of activated microglia and reducing their release of proinflammatory cytokines [42]. LncRNA NEAT1 was significantly upregulated in patients with ischemic stroke, and knockdown of the lncRNA NEAT1 alleviated OGD/R‐induced apoptosis and increased neuronal viability. Furthermore, NEAT1 may inhibit microglial polarization towards the M1 phenotype to reduce the damage caused by OGD/R and reduce the activity of the AKT/STAT3 pathway [62]. However, other studies suggest a conflicting narrative. One study showed that NEAT1 inhibited inflammasome activation by NLRP3 in microglia via the NEAT1/miR‑10b‑5p/BCL6/NLRP3 regulatory axis, which alleviated deleterious outcomes of ischemic stroke [63]. NEAT1 stabilized Wnt3a via U2AF2 and activated the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway to alleviate ischemic stroke‐induced injury [64]. NEAT1 is also involved in regulating the function of astrocytes via modulation of the miR‐488‐3p/RAC1 pathway, which leads to activation of astrocytes [65]. Overall, NEAT1 has immune modulatory effects as it promotes inflammation through some pathways and inhibits it through others. NEAT1 is cited to be upregulated in stroke. Given its involvement in inflammatory pathways, we can surmise that stroke severity and prognosis correlate with higher levels of pro‐/anti‐inflammatory pathways and NEAT1 expression.

TUG1 is a lncRNA located at chromosome 22q12 [66]. The contribution of TUG1 in the inflammatory process in stroke comes through NLRP3 Inflammasome‐dependent pyroptosis via miR‐145a‐5p/Tlr4 Axis. Another study suggests that exhausting TUG1 could alleviate ischemia reperfusion‐induced inflammation and apoptosis in brain tissues and brain microvascular endothelial cells via targeting the miR‐410/FOXO3 axis [67]. In mice MCAO models, TUG1 has been shown to act on the ERK1/2 signaling pathway to aggravate neuronal damage [68]. Another study also showed that knocking down TUG1 decreased the ratio of apoptotic cells and promoted cell survival in vitro. It is suggested that TUG1 sponges miR‐9 to promote neuron apoptosis by up‐regulating Bcl2l11 [69]. Given the aforementioned pathways that TUG1 contributes to, it may be an integral component of the physiologic response to stroke and is upregulated after the stroke to reduce damage and inflammation. Hence, we can assume that the severity of ischemia and infarction is correlated with the plasma levels of TUG1.

H19 is the first lncRNA discovered [70]. It is transcribed from a conserved gene cluster H19 locus located on chromosome 11p15.5. Within a unique locus shared with the insulin‐like growth factor 2 (IGF2) gene [71]. H19 prevents neurogenesis in ischemic stroke through the p53/Notch1 pathway. [72] Neural cell viability, proliferation, and differentiation are similarly affected by H19's regulation of the p53 signaling pathway after ischemic stroke [73]. Similar to previous lncRNAs, H19's involvement in cell proliferation, apoptosis, and inflammation means it will be upregulated in stroke.

MEG3 is transcribed from chromosome 14q32.3 within the human imprinted gene DLK1‐MEG3 locus [74]. In vivo and in vitro studies show that increased expression of MEG3 suppresses miR‐21, miR‐181b, miR‐485, miR‐425‐5p, miR‐147, and miR‐378 and promotes expression of programmed cell death 4 (PDCD4), 12/15‐LOX, AIM2, and GRB2. These pathways contribute to inflammation and oxidative stress [74]. MEG3 is also involved in cell‐death‐related signaling pathways, namely PI3K/AKT, MAPK, NF‐κB, and Wnt/beta‐catenin pathways [74]. Inhibition of MEG3 expression has been shown to lead to an increase in pro‐angiogenic factors such as HIF‐1alpha and VEGF, which can attenuate injury caused by ischemia through Notch and Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathways [74]. Upregulation of MEG3 expression in stroke is, in all likelihood, due to ongoing cell death and injury, which would be correlated with the severity of stroke.

LncRNAs may be one of the potential targets of future therapeutic interventions. Many are involved in atherosclerosis (e.g., H19) and immune regulation (e.g., NEAT1 and ANRIL) before and after stroke. One suggested pathway for therapeutic intervention is angiogenesis, and MEG3 is suggested to have an integral inhibitory role in this process, and silencing it (e.g., with short hairpin RNA (shRNA)plasmid) promotes angiogenesis through activation of the Notch and Wnt/beta‐catenin signaling pathway [74]. However, the delivery of these treatments remains a challenge [75].

This study has several limitations. A high heterogeneity was observed in our meta‐analysis, resulting from pooling different lncRNAs. Our goal in this pooling was to gauge an “average” measure of lncRNAs as biomarkers rather than to pool unrelated markers. Nonetheless, this approach introduces conceptual limitations, as pooling across heterogeneous targets may obscure the prognostic contribution of individual lncRNAs and should therefore be interpreted as an approximation of overall biomarker potential rather than evidence of uniform biological function. Another limitation of our analysis is the absence of meta‐regression or subgroup analyses to explore sources of heterogeneity. Although such methods can be valuable for identifying modifiers (e.g., age, sex, or follow‐up duration), the inherent heterogeneity of different molecules and, more importantly, the incomplete and inconsistent reporting across studies prevent meaningful stratification. Most lncRNAs were examined in only one or two studies, and key variables such as mean age, sex ratio, and exact sampling times were not consistently reported. Conducting meta‐regression under these conditions could lead to unstable or misleading estimates. Future studies with harmonized designs and uniform data reporting will allow more sophisticated analyses, including subgroup and meta‐regression models, to better understand between‐study variability.

There are also limitations to the included studies. Many studies did not include a detailed characterization of patient symptoms and their respective change over time. Instead, they focused on mortality and/or recurrence, with only five studies including mRS in their analysis. It might be useful to consider more detailed studies in the future so that the utility of these biomarkers could be assessed in individual symptoms. (i.e., motor symptoms, speech impairment, etc.) Another important limitation concerns the geographic concentration of the included studies. Nearly all the available evidence originates from Asian (primarily Chinese) and Middle Eastern populations, with no published data from Europe or North America. This narrow distribution may reflect population‐specific genetic backgrounds, lifestyle factors, and healthcare system differences that influence both lncRNA expression and stroke outcomes. Given the global burden of stroke, the absence of large, well‐designed cohort studies from Western populations represents a major gap in the current evidence base. Future research should therefore prioritize multiethnic, geographically diverse cohorts to confirm the reproducibility and generalizability of lncRNA‐based biomarkers across populations.

Another limitation of the included studies is the assumption that similar treatments and rehabilitation services are provided, whereas treatment plans might differ for individual patients, given varying degrees of disability and severity of symptoms. None of the included studies accounted for this matter. In this regard, it might also be interesting to investigate the prognostic value of lncRNAs in predicting adverse treatment outcomes. (e.g., major bleeding after antiplatelet/anticoagulant therapy for secondary prevention) Furthermore, despite our comprehensive search strategy, no eligible studies investigating hemorrhagic stroke were identified. Therefore, our results apply specifically to ischemic stroke and TIA populations. There is also the limitation of generalizability; considering all the papers were limited to China, Egypt, and Iran, different populations may have different patient characteristics. Hopefully, future studies will consider these limitations. To overcome the limitations in existing research, we suggest standardizing the timing for lncRNA collection to avoid early fluctuations that could obscure predictive accuracy. Another suggestion for future studies is patient stratification by different risk factors and underlying diseases, as lncRNAs could have altered expression profiles in diabetes, hypertension, and other diseases that commonly present alongside stroke [76, 77]. Overall, future research could benefit from a multicenter design that factors patient history and presentation into account with greater detail. Follow‐ups should also include a detailed assessment of patient function in all domains rather than simply focusing on mRS.

In conclusion, circulating lncRNAs represent potentially useful biomarkers for predicting stroke severity and outcomes, showing modest to moderate accuracy in forecasting adverse events and recurrence. At this stage, their role should be considered exploratory; incorporating lncRNA profiling into clinical workflows will require larger, multiethnic, and methodologically standardized studies. However, further large‐scale studies are essential to validate their prognostic value and to determine whether integration into routine stroke management is feasible. Particularly, studies from Europe, North America, and other underrepresented regions urgently need to establish these biomarkers' global generalizability and population‐specific performance.
