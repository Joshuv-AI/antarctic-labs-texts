A cross-sectional study of the predictors of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in Pakistan
Authors: Saeed Ahmad, Muhammad Rizwan Safdar, Mufti Nadimul Quamar Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVaccine hesitancy, where individuals are reluctant to get vaccinated, is a significant issue, especially in the event of a pandemic, and poses a major concern. We explore a few predictors of vaccine hesitancy in Pakistan using a cross-sectional study. For this purpose, 562 respondents registered their responses through an online questionnaire from March 31, 2021, to May 28, 2021, during the third wave of the COVID-19 pandemic. Cronbach’s Alpha was used for Internal Consistency Reliability, while Factor Analysis was used for Construct Validity. We applied bivariate linear regression analysis for hypothesis testing. Fear of COVID-19 was higher among respondents as they suffered nervousness, fear of morbidity and mortality, and uncomfortable thinking about COVID-19. In vaccine hesitancy, two-fifths were unsure about, and concerned about the effectiveness of vaccines for new diseases, and the potential for serious side effects. More than half were exposed to negative information about COVID-19 vaccinations. One-third agreed that natural immunity against COVID-19 was a better option than vaccination. More than half perceived that vaccines could protect people from serious COVID-19 diseases and effectively control the spread of the virus, but they were unsure about new vaccines carrying more risks. Bivariate linear regression analysis revealed that younger age, lower knowledge about COVID-19, not quarantining due to COVID-19 symptoms, lower perceived effectiveness of vaccines in reducing the risk of COVID-19, previous negative experiences with vaccination, lower fear of COVID-19, and lower level of trust in health institutions were associated with higher vaccine hesitancy in Pakistan. We recommend further studies that should expand the sociocultural base of predictors of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy by employing a mixed-methods research approach.KEYWORDS: Vaccine hesitancy, COVID-19, socio-demographics, previous experience, fear of COVID-19

Background and introduction

The worldwide efforts to mitigate the repercussions of the COVID-19 pandemic and reduce its socioeconomic and health impacts largely depended on preventive measures.1,2 Therefore, enormous efforts by pharmaceutical companies and the scientific community, supported by governments worldwide, focused on developing effective and safe vaccines against COVID-19.3 These attempts were demonstrated by the authorization and approval of numerous COVID-19 vaccines for emergency use and dozens of vaccines in clinical trials globally. Furthermore, over 170 COVID-19 vaccines were in pre-clinical phases in different parts of the world.4,5 Despite huge efforts to manufacture effective and successful COVID-19 vaccines, hesitancy to be vaccinated can be a major hindrance to prospective and approved COVID-19 vaccines.6

The term “vaccine hesitancy” is commonly used to refer to a delay in accepting or refusing vaccination, despite its availability.7 Vaccine hesitancy has been gaining growing attention from researchers after the onset of COVID-19 in 2019, depolarizing prior attention to anti versus pro-vaccine groups and individuals.8–10 Vaccine-hesitant persons are defined as a diverse group of people varying from complete rejecters to total acceptors. The people called hesitant may decline a few vaccines but approve of other vaccines, delay vaccinations, or agree to take some vaccines but are uncertain of doing this.11–13

Immunization programs and vaccines save people’s lives. According to the World Health Organization’s estimates, vaccines and immunization programs prevent between two and three million deaths worldwide each year. These programs are both cost-efficient and an important component of preventive healthcare.5,14 However, immediately after the emergency approval of COVID-19 vaccines in various countries, a persistent issue of vaccine hesitancy came to the attention of the media, even in the most developed countries worldwide. Protests and statements by anti-vaccine groups, misunderstandings, conspiracy theories, myths, questions about long-term and short-term side effects, and the speed of the development of vaccines were and still are being reported in national and international media.15

Scientists and experts in public health have been expressing concerns regarding vaccine hesitancy along with challenges linked to vaccine’s efficacy, cost, access, and safe distribution.8,16–18 Vaccine hesitancy in Pakistan and around the globe is not a new phenomenon; people have shown hesitancy against Polio and other vaccines in the recent past.6,19,20 Likewise, high vaccine hesitancy was reported among different population groups even before the emergence of COVID-19. The World Health Organization (WHO) declared vaccine hesitancy a major global health threat well before the COVID-19 pandemic.6,18,21 Studies conducted in various parts of the globe have indicated that vaccine hesitancy is associated with factors such as gender, age, educational level, income, and ethnicity.22,23

Vaccine hesitancy, particularly in Pakistan, is a significant challenge for the country’s authorities because of several conspiracy theories and misinformation.19 Pakistan’s failure to control the spread of Polio disease is attributed to the same kind of conspiracy theories.24 Of these, perceived religious preventions (infidel vaccine), the vaccine’s alleged poor quality, questioning of recommendations of necessary dosage, and the active virus’s presence in the vaccine are some prominent claims being spread against the COVID-19 vaccine too.19,25 These conspiracy theories are disseminated through the media, and many people accept them without critical examination. For instance, many people claim in public gatherings that the COVID-19 disease virus is a grand illusion created by the West and Jews to target Islamic countries and rule the world.26 Moreover, vaccine hesitancy appears to be a concern in other South Asian countries as well. For instance, misinformation, cultural beliefs, and distrust in healthcare personnel are significant impediments to the widespread adoption of immunization efforts in India.27 In Srilanka, Kamalrathne et al.28 found that 37.8% of participants postponed or declined vaccinations for multiple reasons, with the predominant factor being apprehension regarding vaccine side effects. Similar results were found in Bangladesh, where most participants were hesitant to administer boosters due to safety concerns, particularly those related to possible side effects.29

Recent studies on the COVID-19 vaccine have indicated that countries worldwide will need to vaccinate between 67% and 80% of the population to halt the spread of this deadly disease.30,31 However, addressing vaccine hesitancy is one of the biggest challenges for governments and health experts.14 In such circumstances, it is crucial for health professionals and governments to understand and address the causes of vaccine hesitancy, as well as the reasons why certain groups are more likely to refuse the COVID-19 vaccine than others. Understanding the reasons behind vaccine hesitancy can help governments formulate successful vaccination campaigns.32 Thus, the present study aims to examine the relationship between the socio-demographic attributes of the respondents, their knowledgeability, broader sociopolitical factors (institutional trust), perceived vaccine efficacy, and vaccine hesitancy.

Theoretical debates on vaccine hesitancy

Vaccine hesitancy ranks among the world’s top 10 threats to public health.33 Policymakers in nearly 90% of countries worldwide have expressed concern about vaccine skepticism.20 Along with publishing many public health guidelines and preventative measures, the government of Pakistan made vaccinations available to the general public to prevent the virus from spreading. As of 25 January 2022, 36% of the population had received all recommended vaccinations, thanks to the administration having served over 1.7 billion doses.34,35 According to reports, however, nearly half of Pakistan’s population (49%) was unwilling to obtain the COVID-19 vaccine.36 On a global scale, hesitancy regarding vaccination remains a significant issue, particularly in economically disadvantaged nations, where a complex interplay between monetary and non-monetary barriers exists.37 The initial research focused on determining the population’s knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding the recommended preventive measures, as well as estimating the growing burden of COVID-19 infections. However, when the vaccine became available, the conversation focused on the Pakistani population’s willingness or unwillingness to vaccinate against the disease.38 Due to continued vaccine reluctance and anti-vaccine campaigns worldwide, the widespread availability of COVID-19 vaccines cannot guarantee that the epidemic will stop.39

There is conclusive evidence that the general population strongly prefers vaccines from some countries over others. On the other hand, they avoid vaccines from certain countries. Some recent studies in Pakistan found that participants preferred a vaccine made in the United Kingdom or the United States over one made in China.40,41 It is possible that the travel limitations imposed on Pakistani citizens who had only been vaccinated with Chinese-made COVID-19 vaccines are to blame for the lack of assurance and trust (cited in41). Moreover, multiple false claims about the COVID-19 vaccine are circulating in Pakistan. There have been unfounded rumors circulating claiming Jews created the virus to conquer the Islamic world and establish global dominance.42 The use of social media as a platform for disseminating anti-vaccination beliefs is a relatively new development. It plays an important part in disseminating these theories within the Pakistani community, contributing to resistance among the general public toward coronavirus vaccination.8,19

Vaccine hesitancy or acceptance

Hesitancy toward the use of vaccines is one of the most significant obstacles to vaccine adoption. Various studies have reported varying levels of reluctance and acceptance toward COVID-19 vaccinations43 (Marzo et al.).44 Several factors contributed to people’s reluctance to receive their first dose of the COVID-19 vaccine. Some of the most prominent of these reasons were apprehension regarding the vaccine’s potential adverse effects, safety issues, preconceived notions regarding the ineffectiveness of the vaccine, and faith in the power of natural immunity.37,40,41,45–47 Misguided religious views and the dissemination of erroneous information by powerful people, social media, friends, and family are also to blame for the confusion and fear around vaccines.34 Specifically, numerous studies have highlighted the media as a significant factor contributing to vaccine reluctance.48–52 To prevent epidemics, it is crucial to increase vaccination rates among the general public and healthcare workers.4

Recently, a large body of evidence has shown that healthcare professionals have reservations about vaccinations, contributing to the broader public’s resistance to the practice.53–55 In a systematic review and meta‑analysis, Alimohamadi et al.56 found that healthcare workers have a lower vaccine uptake rate for COVID-19 than the general public (55.9% and 68.5%, respectively). Malik et al.57 research indicated that 67% of the adult U.S. population was willing to get the COVID-19 vaccine, but there were large variations in immunization uptake based on demographics and geography. In another study, Fakonti et al.58 found that approximately one-third of those polled stated they would be willing to vaccinate against COVID-19; the remaining 70% were unsure or did not plan to get vaccinated, making them “vaccine-hesitant.” A study in Pakistan reported that approximately 78% of the participants in the study felt less reluctant to receive a COVID-19 booster shot.41 In general, people in East Asia were more receptive to receiving the COVID-19 vaccine (Alimohamadi et al.59). There is evidence that the vaccination rate is very high among the general population in China (91.3%), Indonesia (93.3%), Ecuador (97%) and Malaysia (94.3%).55,60–62 In contrast, some other studies found low vaccination rates among the general population in Turkey (33.9%) Qatar (44.7%) and Jordan (28.4%).63–65

Vaccine hesitancy and socio-demographics

Similar to other vaccinations, the acceptability or rejection of the COVID-19 vaccine varies by nation, time, and location due to socio-demographic variations, health issues, and numerous other factors66 (Marzo et al.).44 Many studies examine the relationship between socio-demographic dimensions and vaccine acceptance or reluctance.37

Higher vaccine reluctance was observed among women, youth, minority groups, and individuals with lesser levels of education in a study across high-income nations. Other factors were mistrust, no history of flu vaccination, and the absence of chronic conditions.67 Nevertheless, the reluctance to receive a booster shot of the COVID-19 vaccine was not associated with any socio-demographic characteristics, including gender, age, or degree of education, according to Moeed et al.41 On the flip side, more recent research on the factors affecting the uptake of COVID-19 vaccines in low- and middle-income nations has linked certain occupations, particularly those involving healthcare, and greater levels of education to reduced vaccine resistance.68 As shown by Marzo et al.44 the vaccine is more likely to be reluctantly administered to urban populations, those with lower socioeconomic origins, and those with lower levels of education.

In a survey of American adults, it was discovered that vaccination acceptance was higher among men than women, older people than younger adults, Asians than other ethnic groups, and college graduates than those without such degree.57 In line with this, females were also more reluctant to get the COVID-19 booster dose.69 This finding is in line with many other studies9,35,62 but contrasts with Fakonti et al.58 who found that females are more likely to accept COVID-19 vaccination than those who were adamantly opposed to getting vaccinated or on the fence. When looking at age and job experience, the authors discovered that those with more years of experience are more inclined to accept the COVID-19 vaccine. The oldest age group is naturally more inclined to embrace a COVID-19 vaccination due to self-interest, given that they are the most vulnerable.58 In Afghanistan, Nemat et al.70 also found that more women than men expressed interest in receiving the COVID-19 vaccine. Other research has demonstrated that vaccine reluctance is lowest among those aged 21 to 30 and the oldest participants.71,72

Another important aspect that influences vaccine reluctance is household income. Respondents whose family income was between PKR 100,000 and 300,000 (USD 550–1,650) were more likely to be willing to get a booster shot, according to research by Moeed et al.41 conducted among Pakistani population. In Pakistan, a prior study indicated that COVID-19 vaccination rates were higher among households with monthly family earnings greater than PKR 50,000.46

Vaccine hesitancy and trust in government/health institutions

Existing findings suggest that the government’s COVID-19 vaccine mandate has increased the vaccination rate. Nevertheless, research indicates that the mandate is inadequate to guarantee population vaccination.38 An effective COVID-19 immunization campaign depends on public trust in the government, scientists, and healthcare professionals.73–75

Fear of COVID-19 and vaccine hesitancy

Fakonti et al.58 identified multiple explanations for the refusal of the COVID-19 immunization. The predominant reasons include concerns regarding the vaccine’s quality and approval process, apprehension about possible adverse effects, observing the immunization of others, disbelief in belonging to a high-risk category, and vaccination-related anxiety, among others. Many individuals are hesitant to get vaccinated because they have doubts about the effectiveness and safety of vaccines.53,76 Concern about COVID-19 is less influential on vaccination decisions than confidence in vaccine safety.77 The intention to vaccinate against COVID-19 was substantially impacted by fear of the virus, perceived behavior control, attitude, and perceived infectability, according to Ullah et al.78 among young Pakistanis. On the other hand, the other three elements seem to have more of an impact than COVID-19 fear.

Vaccine efficiency and vaccine hesitancy

How effective people think vaccines will determine how widely they are used. According to Jaramillo-Monge et al.79 if the COVID-19 vaccination were at least 95% efficacious, 91% of participants in an Ecuadorian study would get it. More than half of the respondents (64%) believed that vaccines could effectively limit the spread of viruses, according to a global survey on the topic.80 Nevertheless, it was discovered that for every one-unit rise in participants’ ages, there was a 4% decline in confidence in the efficacy of vaccines. In addition, the level of trust in the effectiveness of vaccines was higher among students and retirees compared to participants’ work status.

Knowledge of pandemic/COVID-19 and vaccine hesitancy

The public’s understanding of the benefits provided by vaccines is a significant factor influencing their inclination or reluctance to receive vaccinations.35 Currently, diverse social media platforms and the internet significantly influence public awareness of the necessity of vaccinations.58 Nonetheless, anxiety, rumors, and misinformation are also exacerbated by social media.51,81 A study by Fakonti et al.58 suggests that individuals who have engaged in vaccination-related training events, such as conferences and seminars, within the past two years are more likely to receive the COVID-19 vaccine compared to those who have not participated. A study involving university students in Bangladesh revealed that characteristics such as gender, academic department, and students’ residence strongly influenced their knowledge of COVID-19.82

Previous experience with vaccination and vaccine hesitancy

According to Zakar et al.35 vaccination programs were more supported by individuals without a history of chronic illness compared to those with such a history. Another study by Fakonti et al.58 found a statistically significant difference between people who had gotten a seasonal flu vaccination in the last five years and those who were willing to get the COVID-19 vaccine. Among the Pakistani population studied by Moeed et al.41 34.8% denied vaccination, particularly booster doses, citing prior bad experiences with the vaccine’s side effects as the main reason. Findings from a study by Haque et al.83 in Bangladesh indicate that people with chronic diseases are less inclined to desire immunizations. Methodology

Target population

A cross-sectional study design was used for the current study. The target population for the study was: (i) both men and women, (ii) above 18 years of age and (iii) internet users. We included these criteria in the consent form for the study. An age of 18 years was set as an inclusion criterion because it was Pakistan’s minimum age for vaccination registration. Additionally, due to the lockdown in Pakistan, we utilized an online platform for data collection. The online questionnaire was constructed through the Qualtrics Survey Design website in both English and Urdu for the convenience of the respondents. Pre-testing was conducted with 30 respondents who were excluded from the final analysis. The questionnaire was shared across various social media platforms, including Facebook, Twitter, and WhatsApp. The data was collected between March 31, 2021, and May 28, 2021, during the country’s third wave of the COVID-19 pandemic. The survey questionnaire was reached to 604 individuals, and among them, 562 participated and completed the survey. The 42 (7%) people who did not participate in the study were either unwilling after reading the study objectives and consent form or did not fulfill the inclusion criterion.

Instrumentation

The instrument for the current study was divided into two sections. The first section asked questions about demographic details (such as gender, monthly household income, year of schooling, residential background, etc.) of the respondents. The second section asked questions about fear of COVID-19, exposure to COVID-19, and vaccine hesitancy.

Predictors of vaccine hesitancy

Fear of COVID-19 scale

The current study modified the existing scale of fear of COVID-19.84 The scale of fear of COVID-19 consisted of 5 categories: being afraid of the Coronavirus, being uncomfortable thinking about the Coronavirus, being afraid of losing one’s life because of the Coronavirus, having heart a race when thinking of getting the Coronavirus, and becoming nervous after watching stories about the Coronavirus on social media. The response categories used the Likert scale to measure the fear of COVID-19, ranging from strongly agree = 5, Agree = 4, do not know = 3, Disagree = 2, and strongly disagree = 1.

We also tested Construct validity and internal Reliability for the scale of fear of COVID-19. The scale of fear of COVID-19 demonstrates good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.8364 indicating strong reliability. The average inter item covariance of 0.63 suggests that the items are moderately correlated and collectively measure a cohesive construct. The factor analysis results for the scale of fear of CVOID-19 indicate that all five items load strongly onto a single factor, confirming the unidimensional structure of the scale. This suggests that the scale reliably measures a single underlying construct – individuals’ fear related to the COVID-19 pandemic (see Table 1).

Perceived vaccine efficiency

The statement measured perceived vaccine efficiency: By which percentage do you think the vaccine used by Pakistan will reduce the risk of COVID-19? The response categories were under 50% = 1, 50% to 69% = 2, 70% to 89% = 3, and 90% to 100% = 4.

Previous experience

The statement measured previous experience: How was your previous experience with vaccination? The response categories were no experience = 0, bad = 1, and good = 2.

Knowledge about COVID-19

The statement measured knowledge about COVID-19: How do you rate your knowledge about COVID-19? The response categories were slight knowledge = 1, moderate knowledge = 2, and high knowledge = 3.

Trust in health institutions and quarantine

Trust in health institutions was measured by whether you have trust in health institutions. In addition, quarantine was measured with the statement of “I have quarantined myself due to fear of COVID-19 symptoms.” The response categories for trust in health institutions and quarantine were No = 1, Maybe = 2, and Yes = 3.

Vaccine hesitancy scale

The current study modified the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale (VHS), which was developed as a common diagnostic tool by the WHO SAGE Working Group on Vaccine Hesitancy to identify and compare hesitancy in different global settings.85 The Vaccine Hesitancy Scale consisted of 16 categories. vaccine hesitancy was measured through different categories such as new vaccines carry more risks, concern about serious side effects of vaccines, vaccines for new diseases are not effective, concern about early release of COVID-19 vaccination, reasons made me think that people should not be vaccinated, it is difficult for some ethnic/religious groups in my community to get COVID-19 vaccinations, receiving or hearing negative information about COVID-19 vaccinations, and not having enough information about the COVID-19 vaccine. In addition, vaccine hesitancy was measured through different categories such as the received information about vaccines is reliable and trustworthy, belief that COVID-19 vaccines can protect people from serious COVID-19 diseases, getting a COVID-19 vaccination for your children/family, religious leaders in community support COVID-19 vaccinations, getting vaccines is a good way to protect people from diseases, and persons not vaccinated against COVID-19, are at high risk for getting an COVID-19 infection in their lifetime. The category response used the Likert scale to measure the vaccine hesitancy, ranging from strongly agree = 5, Agree = 4, do not know = 3, Disagree = 2 and strongly disagree = 1. Reverse-coding was applied to negative statements to ensure all items in the scale measured vaccine hesitancy in the same direction. This process enhanced the internal consistency and validity of the scale by aligning response patterns across all items.

We also tested Construct validity and internal Reliability for the scale of vaccine hesitancy. It shows acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.714 and an average interitem covariance of 0.11, supporting its internal validity for measuring vaccine hesitancy, making it suitable for exploratory analysis or descriptive interpretation. In Exploratory factor analysis, the factor loadings suggest that most items load strongly on Factor 1, indicating a dominant underlying construct likely related to vaccine hesitancy.

Data collection

The data were collected through an online survey that adopted a rigorous ethical process. The first author’s e-mail address was provided on the informed consent page, which described the study’s objectives, allowing respondents to contact the author if they had any questions or needed further clarification. Informed consent was taken from all the participants electronically.

Data analysis and interpretation

The current research used SPSS (Version 26) for the data analysis. Computing categories of fear of COVID-19 and vaccine hesitancy calculated a composite score. The descriptive statistics were used to present the demographic details of the respondents. Factor analysis was executed to determine the validity of the categories of the scales. Reliability analysis verified the internal consistency of the scales through the value of Cronbach’s alpha. Linear regression analysis was used to examine the relationship between the independent variables and the dependent variables.

Results

Descriptive findings

The sample consisted of 47.2% females and 52.8% males; 76.9% of respondents were aged 18–27 years, 18.7% were aged 28–37, 3.4% were aged 38–47, and 1.1% were aged 48 or older. This means that the majority of the respondents were young. Regarding educational attainment, 11% of the respondents were equal to or below grade ten, while 89% had schooling beyond grade ten.39% were postgraduate students. Regarding their monthly income, 46.4% had less than 50,000 rupees, 34.5% had a monthly income of 50,001–100,000 rupees, 11.4% had a monthly income of 100,001–150,000, 3.6% had a monthly income of 150,000–200,000, and 4.1% had a monthly income of 200,001 and above. Regarding respondents’ self-rated knowledge about COVID-19, 14.9% reported slight knowledge about the virus, 63% reported moderate knowledge about the virus, and 20.8% reported a high level of knowledge about the coronavirus. As religion is an important social institution in traditional Pakistani society, respondents were also asked to rate their level of religiosity. Of all respondents, 4% reported a very low level of religiosity, 13.5% reported slightly religious, 62.5% reported moderately religious, and 19.6% reported highly religious. Respondents were asked about their level of trust in healthcare institutions. 10.9% reported a low level of trust in healthcare institutions, 28.1% reported a moderate level of trust in healthcare institutions, and 61% reported a high level of trust in healthcare institutions. Respondents were asked to rate the perceived effectiveness of the COVID-19 vaccine. 17.8% reported low effectiveness, 30.8% reported moderately effective, 32.7% reported highly effective, and 18.7% reported very highly effective.

Table 2 highlighted the descriptive statistics of the fear of COVID-19. 37.2% agreed and 29.4% were not sure about being most afraid of the Coronavirus. Two-fifths agreed that they were uncomfortable while thinking about COVID-19. Similarly, one-third agreed that they were afraid of losing their lives because of COVID-19. Similarly, over one-fourth agreed that their heart races when considering COVID-19. Finally, more than two-fifths agreed that stories on social media made them nervous. In conclusion, the above statistics highlight that the respondents’ fear of COVID-19 was higher.

Discussion

The current study highlighted the predictors (such as demographic characteristics, previous vaccination experience, fear of COVID-19, etc.) of vaccine hesitancy in Pakistan. Analysis showed that less experience of quarantine due to COVID-19 symptoms, lower-perceived efficiency of vaccines to reduce the risk of COVID-19, previous bad experience with vaccination, lower fear of COVID-19, and lower level of trust in health institutions resulted in higher vaccine hesitancy in Pakistan. The study also revealed that younger age was negatively associated with vaccine hesitancy. Since the onset of COVID-19, a plethora of research has been conducted to understand the vaccine-related behavior and attitudes of people around the globe. Like other countries around the globe, many people in Pakistan expressed their concerns regarding the existence of coronavirus and its lethality, along with the effectiveness and efficacy of the vaccine. Vaccination has remained a contested medical intervention in Pakistan. The most popular vaccine intervention is the polio eradication program in Pakistan led by the World Health Organization. The polio vaccination has been opposed by many sections of society based on some conspiracy theories. Many people doubted that this vaccination was meant to insert some chips in the people’s bodies. People also believed that the polio vaccine was used to make our children infertile so that they could not procreate, resulting in the shrinking of our population, which is the desire of the Western world.86 Secondly, as the polio eradication program in Pakistan has not gained its desired target, the people have shaky beliefs about the perceived effectiveness of vaccines. These conspiracy theories are spread through media, and many people blindly accept them. The same is the case with the coronavirus vaccine. In Pakistan, for instance, many people claim that the COVID-19 disease virus is a grand illusion created by the West and Jews to target Islamic countries and rule the world.26

The findings of our study hint at such sociocultural processes that lead to acceptance or non-acceptance of vaccination. Our study demonstrates that younger age and vaccine hesitancy are negatively associated with each other, and vaccine hesitancy decreased with an increase in age. In our analysis, only the 48 and above age group showed a statistically significant difference in vaccine hesitancy compared to the 18–27 group, with approximately 5.6 points lower hesitancy (p = .040). Other age groups did not differ significantly, and the overall model was not significant, indicating that age explains very little of the variance in hesitancy. This finding agrees with the previous research conducted to understand people’s attitudes toward vaccination in Portugal,87 Italy,88 and the United States.32,89 Older people are considered more conservative than young people and are more hesitant to accept new things. One of the reasons for this finding might be the widespread use of social media among youth in Pakistan and their exposure to modern scientific information. The study’s findings contradict Ebrahimi et al.90 which demonstrated age as a non-significant predictor of vaccine hesitancy. As the data were collected online using Google Scholar, it is very likely that the majority of the respondents were young adults due to their better access to digital technologies compared to older people, so the findings may not be generalizable to all sections of the society.

There was a statistically significant but weak negative association between vaccine hesitancy and fear of COVID-19 (p = .042, β = −0.132). The negative coefficient supports the hypothesis (H1), indicating that lower fear of COVID-19 is associated with higher vaccine hesitancy. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting that individuals who perceive a lower risk or threat from COVID-19 are less likely to adopt preventive health behaviors, including vaccination.77,91 Fear can serve as a motivational factor in health behavior models such as the Health Belief Model, where perceived severity and susceptibility are critical determinants of action.92 Thus, those who experience greater fear of infection may be more inclined to seek vaccination as a protective measure, whereas those with minimal fear may underestimate the need for it. Although the effect size in our model is small, the result highlights the nuanced psychological drivers of vaccine decision-making and reinforces the importance of accurate risk communication in public health messaging.93

Fear aroused by COVID-19 forced the people to get the vaccine jabs. The study’s findings are in accord with a comprehensive study conducted by Khalafalla et al.94 in Saudi Arabia that demonstrated that fear of morbidity and death were salient factors associated with vaccine acceptance among the masses. In order to convince the people to get vaccinated, the Pakistani government devised many strategies, including arousing the feelings of fear of death by issuing the death toll caused by coronavirus. This strategy of fear did not work well. Regarding vaccine hesitancy, the mere significance of arousing fear is not sufficient. People must trust not only the authorities responsible for disseminating vaccine-related information but also the correctness of the information.90 Risk communication plays a vital role in emergency preparedness and response. However, for effective communication, a certain degree of trust must prevail between the sender and receiver of the message. Trust in health authorities plays an important role in maintaining the health of people and spreading health-protecting behavior. Our study’s findings coincide with those conducted (e.g95,96). They demonstrated that incorrect information about COVID-19 vaccines on different social media platforms and less trust in the healthcare system were positively associated with vaccine hesitancy.

Knowledge about the mechanism of vaccination plays a vital role in vaccine acceptance. Our study demonstrated that the respondents’ knowledgeability was negatively associated with vaccine hesitancy. Studies conducted by Le An et al.97 and Racey et al.98 demonstrated that knowledge about vaccines was a positive predictor of vaccine acceptance. Many studies confirmed that known and unknown perceived side effects and lack of knowledge regarding the mechanism and processes of the vaccine itself strongly predicted hesitancy to the COVID-19 vaccine. The findings of this study also concurred with the previous research that demonstrated that the perceived lack of vaccine safety and effectiveness was a major predictor for unwillingness to take vaccines (see e.g99,100). One of our study’s findings resonates with the study conducted in Pakistan.40 This study showed that out of 562 respondents in Pakistan, 35% of participants were hesitant to get the vaccine jab, and one of the major reasons was concerns about vaccine safety and effectiveness.40

The role of religious and community leaders in shaping vaccination narratives in Pakistan is underexplored in our current analysis. As prior research has shown, such figures often serve as trusted sources of guidance, particularly in rural and underserved areas where formal healthcare messaging may be less accessible or credible. For instance, Khan et al.19 documented how religious sermons and community gatherings have been used to either reinforce or challenge vaccine misinformation, especially regarding rumors of infertility or Western conspiracies. Notably, WhatsApp and family networks were frequently cited in studies as key channels through which vaccine rumors circulate.101 In rural Punjab, for example, Khan et al.19 found that false claims about vaccines causing infertility spread rapidly via WhatsApp voice messages, often gaining credibility when echoed by religious leaders or elder family members.

The non-representative sample of this study, comprising participants primarily between 18 and 27 years old (76.9%) and limited to internet users, is a key limitation of this study. We recognized this limitation, which raises questions about the external validity and generalizability of this research. Individuals with limited internet usage, such as the elderly or those residing in areas with inadequate internet connectivity, may be underrepresented in this research. However, they may potentially exhibit a high rate of vaccine reluctance. Consequently, we cannot assert that our research is generalizable to all individuals, regardless of their age, region, or level of internet access. We advocate for additional research employing stratified sampling methods based on age (youth versus older adults), geographic location (urban versus rural), and level of internet access, which will enhance representativeness and generalizability of the research on vaccine hesitancy. Moreover, future research should employ mixed-methods approaches, such as conducting in-depth interviews with people who may not be used to the internet or who may live in rural areas where access to the internet is not easy, could facilitate the triangulation of quantitative results and provide a more nuanced comprehension of vaccine reluctance across various demographic circumstances.

This study has another major limitation due to the overrepresentation of male participants (52.8%), which may be influenced by the fact that male researchers conducted the survey. It limits our capacity to derive causal inferences from the observed correlations. Although we have conducted a regression analysis and found some significant associations, future studies should adopt a gender-balanced sampling approach utilizing longitudinal or experimental designs to draw causal inferences. Furthermore, to ensure gender representativeness, future researchers could utilize multilingual outreach initiatives or may get support from female community leaders to inspire female participants to participate in the study. Lastly, this study also has the problem of using self-reported data. There is a significant likelihood of social desirability bias in self-reported data. Further, we gathered information in Pakistan throughout the early to mid-COVID-19 phase. It is possible that the vaccination rate increased, and vaccine reluctance reduced as a result of mass immunization. It is possible that the vaccine hesitancy expressed by people may not accurately reflect reality. Therefore, our findings may overstate the level of long-term resistance in real-world implementation settings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlighted how different sociocultural factors affect vaccine acceptance in Pakistan. Vaccine hesitancy has been an observed phenomenon in the backdrop of coronavirus, which was found to be related to quarantine experience, lower perceived efficiency of vaccines, previous bad experience with vaccination, lower fear of COVID-19, and lower level of trust in health institutions and age of the respondents. Although Pakistan was not severely hit by coronavirus-related morbidity and mortality, the hesitancy on the part of people conveys an important message that in case of extreme conditions, it will become worse for the government to convince people. In light of these results, it is clear that raising public awareness of the pandemic and encouraging vaccination are crucial steps in averting future large outbreaks.

While these factors are the most prominent when everything is assessed individually, we believe vaccine skepticism is interwoven into a more extensive web of attitudes and behaviors related to anti-systemic sentiments. In addition to trust in health authorities, a lack of willingness can also be investigated by considering the broader structures of society, such as democratic rights, endorsement of conspiracy beliefs, and a lack of compliance with advice about changing behavior to avoid spreading infections. In this regard, a qualitative study can reveal the more extensive and varied attitudinal picture of vaccine hesitancy among the Pakistani masses. The empirical findings of the current study inform policymakers and aid in planning for future pandemics. Therefore, we advocate for more studies to broaden the scope of predictors of COVID-19 vaccine reluctance in Pakistan and elsewhere.
