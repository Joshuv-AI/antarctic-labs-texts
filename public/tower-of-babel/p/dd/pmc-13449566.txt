Multimodal sequencing identifies synergistic mechanisms driving resistance to neoadjuvant nivolumab treatment in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Fanhong Zeng, Jiaxin Guo, Zheng Zeng, Vanilla Xin Zhang, Yu-Man Tsui, Po-Man Li, Abdullah Husain, Qingyang Zhang, Joyce Man-Fong Lee, Tan-To Cheung, Daniel Wai-Hung Ho, Irene Oi-Lin Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) critically modulates therapeutic responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Relative to the other solid tumors, HCC is characterized by more pronounced intratumoral heterogeneity and has comparatively poorer responsiveness to ICI blockade treatment. Importantly, the correlation of the underlying mechanism of TIME and the molecular underpinnings of immunotherapy resistance in HCC remains elusive. Employing an integrative multi-omics approach, including spatially resolved transcriptomics, single-cell and bulk RNA sequencing, and lipidomics, we delineated the spatiotemporal dynamics and mechanistic basis of resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy in HCC. Our analyses revealed a profoundly immunosuppressive TIME in non-responsive patients, marked by sparse immune cell infiltration within the tumor niche, in contrast to the immune-inflamed TIME observed in the responders. Multidimensional profiling further uncovered dysregulated lipogenesis and aberrant lipid accumulation in tumor cells of the non-responder cases. Notably, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), exhausted CD8 T cells, and lipid-enriched tumor cells co-localize at the tumor-immune interface, forming a physical and functional barrier that precludes effective immune cell infiltration into the tumor niche. We also identified the pivotal TAM-tumor cell crosstalk via the ADM-RAMP1-EBP signaling axis, orchestrating lipid metabolism reprogramming and contributing to the attenuated PD-1 therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, these findings provide a comprehensive mechanistic framework for anti-PD1 resistance, unveiling actionable biomarkers and a translational vulnerability to enhance precision therapeutics in HCC.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Spatial transcriptomics, Liver cancer, Lipid metabolism

Introduction

Liver cancer is the sixth most common tumor in the world and ranks fourth in terms of mortality. Among liver cancers, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for more than 90% [1, 2]. Unfortunately, HCC is often diagnosed at advanced stages, when many patients have already lost the opportunity for curative surgery. For advanced HCC, low survival rates and high recurrence have always puzzled clinicians [3, 4]. Although tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) (sorafenib and lenvatinib) have been used as first-line treatment drugs to improve the survival rate of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in the past two decades, the improvement is not apparent [5, 6].

Since the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) plays a crucial role in the development and progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), scientists are investigating therapies to modulate the HCC TIME. In the past five years, the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) has completely changed the treatment of HCC, mainly the ICI against programmed cell death-1 receptor (PD-1) or its ligand (PD-L1). Nivolumab, a monoclonal antibody to PD-1, has been clinically proven effective in treating multiple cancers, including HCC [7–9]. Despite these significant advances in managing advanced HCC, the molecular basis of controlling the immune response and escape mechanisms in ICI treatment remains unclear.

In recent years, high-throughput technologies have greatly improved our understanding of the TIME of HCC. For example, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has been widely used to explore the TIME and identify immune cells that respond to tumor progression and immunotherapy resistance [10–12]. However, although scRNA-seq can retain most of the transcriptome information, it loses spatial location information. Previous studies indicated that the spatial structure and microenvironment of the tumor are related to TIME remodeling and tumor metastasis [13, 14] The advent of spatial transcriptome sequencing (ST-seq) provides a powerful approach to understanding the intrinsic characteristics of immune cells in tumors and TIME and exploring the spatial distribution of immune cells and cancer cells.

In this study, we used multiple dimensions of bulk-seq, scRNA-seq, and ST-seq to investigate tumor intrinsic features that explain response or resistance to nivolumab treatment. Bulk-seq and ST-seq results demonstrate that lipid synthesis in tumor cells of patients in the non-responders (NR) was abnormally elevated compared to that of responders (R). Meanwhile, tumor cells with increased lipid synthesis coexist with tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), exhausted CD8T cells at the tumor border to prevent immune cell infiltration. On the other hand, scRNA-seq and ST-seq revealed that tumor cells with increased lipid synthesis in non-responders colocalized with TAMs at the tumor border, enhanced cell communication and affected the function of adjacent CD8T cells. In summary, our study identified specific cell colocalization and cell communication at the tumor border of the non-responder group and identified an independent resistance mechanism to neoadjuvant nivolumab treatment in HCC.

Results

ST-seq profiling revealed a different immune landscape between NR and R

We evaluated the efficacy and safety of preoperative nivolumab (anti-PD-1) in patients with intermediate and locally advanced HCC [15]. Among them, all patients who received neoadjuvant nivolumab treatment were subjected to bulk-cell RNA sequencing (bulk-seq), and 16 and 4 of them were subjected to spatial transcriptomic sequencing (ST-seq) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), respectively. Besides, there were 12 treatment-naïve HCC cases that underwent scRNA-seq (Figure 1A). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients were described in our previous study [15, 16]. Based on the definition of pathologic tumor necrosis of ≥60% for classifying the nivolumab response15, our study consisted of six responders (R) and ten non-responders (NR).

For ST-seq, we analyzed an average of approximately 4,000 spots per tissue section. After removing low-quality and marginal spots, we obtained a total of 56,895 spots and performed dimensionality reduction and unsupervised clustering on different samples (Figure S1A). Firstly, we used the MIA [17] to determine whether each cluster was enriched with various cell types, including tumor cells, CD8 cells, CD4 cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAM), B cells, plasma B cells, endothelial cells (Endo) and fibroblasts (Fib) (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, we uniformly defined different clusters based on a 3-component classification on whether there was enrichment of tumor, immune (including CD4, CD8, plasma B, and TAM), and stromal cells (Endo and Fib). Regarding the tumor component, we define it as T whenever a cluster is enriched in tumor cells or otherwise N. Such criteria were similarly applied in defining the status of immune (I/N) and stromal (S/N) components. For example, if a cluster is enriched in tumor and immune cells but not stromal cells, this cluster will be defined as TIN, whereas a cluster not enriched in any cell types will be defined as NNN class instead (Fig. 1C). Compared with the R group, tumor niche (including TNN and TNS classes) accounted for a higher proportion in NR, while tumor immune infiltration (TIL) (including TIN and TIS classes) and immune (including NIN and NIS) niches demonstrated a reverse trend (Fig. 1D). The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is important for determining treatment response for immunotherapy, so we particularly performed functional prediction on the TIL and immune niches. The results showed that NR patients were more prone to the immune exhaustion phenotypes, including the enrichment of exhausted T cells and M2-like TAMs, as compared to the R counterparts (Fig. 1E-F, Figure S1B), implicating a worse treatment effect on patients in the NR group.

Patients in the NR group’s tumor niche showed pronounced lipogenesis characteristics

To investigate the mechanistic involvement of tumor component in rendering the differential drug response outcome, we performed comparative pathway enrichment analysis (NR vs R) on the tumor niche. The results showed that the tumor niche of patients in the NR group demonstrated a more prominent lipid metabolism phenotype than that of the patients in the R group (Figure 2A, S1C). In order to further explore the patient heterogeneity between the R and NR groups, we performed non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) analysis [18] on the spots of the tumor niche and obtained 35 factors (Figure S1D-E). Meanwhile, we also deconvoluted the cell type abundance (tumor, endo, fib, CD8, CD4, plasma B, B and TAM) of spots by BayesPrism [19] (we used the scRNA-seq data of a subset of cases to provide cell-type reference) (Figure S2A). Those spots are mainly stratified into 3 types (tumor, immune and stromal) (Figure 2B, top panel), with their corresponding associated NMF factors (key factors: F16 for tumor, F30 for stromal and F3 for immune). Correlation analysis showed that the identified NMF factors specifically reflected the characteristics of different major cell types (Figure 2B, heatmap).

We confirmed the consistency between the spatial location of the different cell type abundance and factor activity, indicating the validity of identified NMF factors in representing individual cell types (Fig. 2C). Quantitative statistical analysis indicated patients in the R group had a higher proportion of immune cells than those in the NR group, whereas there was only a trend for more abundant stromal and tumor cells in NR group (Fig. 2D). Further examination of the distribution of factors revealed that NR cases were enriched in 21 factors, while R cases had 9 (Figure S2B-C). We analyzed spatial distance to explore the spatial distribution of tumor, immune, and stromal cells and classified the position of the spots relative to the tumor niche border (zero indicates the border whereas negative values represent inside the tumor niche, with more negative values towards the core) (Figure S2D). The results showed that NR patients’ tumors, immune, and stromal cells tended to be more evenly located near the tumor border, while immune cells in R patients were much closer to the core of the tumor, suggesting more profound immune infiltration (Fig. 2E). Functionally, we examined key genes of lipid biosynthesis and calculated the lipogenesis score [20, 21]. Notably, tumor cells showed a preferential lipogenesis phenotype in NR patients, while a lipolysis phenotype in R patients (Fig. 2F, left panel). The immune function of immune cells in R patients was more active, and stromal cells in NR patients were more involved in copper metabolism (Fig. 2F, right panel; Figure S2E). We also conducted a correlation analysis to explore the relationship between lipogenesis and immune function. The results showed that an increased lipogenesis score would likely induce T cell exhaustion and M2 polarization of TAM (Fig. 2G). Meanwhile, we explored the relationship between lipogenesis and immune function in different spatial locations, and the results showed that lipogenesis and immune activation (T cell cytotoxicity, M1 polarization, and pro-inflammatory ability) seemed to be negatively correlated (Figure S2F-G).

Then, bulk-seq results also supported that majority of the key genes involved in lipogenesis displayed significant inverse expression correlation with the extent of pathologic tumor necrosis (drug response) in patients (Fig. 2H). Besides, we also conducted a differential expression analysis to explore lipidomic landscape from a previous study [22] (Table 1), and showed that many of the products of lipid metabolism (LPE [Lyso-phosphatidylethanolamine], PE [Phosphatidylethanolamine] and LPC [Lyso-phosphatidylcholine]) were up-regulated in NR group (logFC ≥ 1) (Fig. 2I).

Lipogenesis is prominent at the borders

According to previous studies, the immune microenvironment at the tumor border is crucial to the efficacy of immunotherapies [23, 24]. Therefore, we divided all spots into inside (B1: immediately inside the border; B2: further inside) and outside areas (G1: immediately outside the border; G2: further outside) according to their locations relative to the border of tumor niche (Figure 3A). Indeed, comparing NR against R patients, we found that lipogenesis-related genes and lipogenesis scores peaked at the border, which may partly explain the suboptimal drug response in NR patients due to lipid-related immunosuppressive effects that counteracted nivolumab action (Figure 3B).

Therefore, we explored the relationship between lipogenesis and TIME in the G1 area that is immediately outside the border. Distance analysis showed that compared with the R group, the NR group had more immune niche and fewer TIL niche aggregation immediately outside the tumor niche border (Figure 3C). Together with the apparent immune exhaustion phenotypes that are particularly enriched in NR patients (Figure 1F), evidence collectively implies the possibility of an immunosuppressive landscape elicited by the TIME, which resulted in worse nivolumab response. Meanwhile, cell abundance distribution showed that TAM accounted for the majority in G1 area (Figure 3D). Colocalization analysis further indicated that TAM tended to colocalize with mainly tumor cells in the intra-spot scenario, whereas it predominantly colocalized with both CD8 and tumor cells with the nearby spots (para-spot) (Figure 3E, S3A). Therefore, the interaction among these three cell types could be pivotal to determining the consequences of nivolumab action. We also observed that the MAPK pathway and lipogenesis activities were both higher in patients in the NR group than those in the R group (Figure 3F). They showed a positive and significant correlation, suggesting MAPK signaling may drive the elevation of lipogenesis (Figure 3G).

Next, TCGA data also showed cases with higher lipogenesis scores were associated with a worse prognosis (Figure 3H). To better explore the relationship between lipogenesis, TAM, and CD8 cells, we used Getis-Ord Gi* [25] to identify lipogenesis hotspots (lipo hotspots) in different patients (Figure 3I, S3B). Distance analysis showed that the hotspots were closer to the border than non-hotspots (Figure 3J). More importantly, the lipo hotspots in the NR group were closer to the border than those in the R group (Figure S3C). We also observed that lipo hotspots existed in a higher proportion in NR group, and the lipo hotspots in the NR group further had higher lipogenesis scores than those in the R group (Figure 3K), indicating the intrinsic difference between R and NR patients regarding lipogenesis. Taken together, we observed significantly higher lipogenesis at the border of and the area immediately outside the tumor niche. Such a phenomenon displayed significant contrast between R and NR patients, where higher lipogenesis may likely favor worse nivolumab response.

To understand the primary source of the lipogenesis signal in the G1 area, we performed correlation analysis and identified that tumor cells were the major contributors of lipogenesis (Fig. 3L). Meanwhile, we compared the cell abundance in different groups and locations for lipo hotspots to explore the lipogenesis effect on TAM and CD8. The lipo hotspot of the NR group contains more TAM while the R group has more CD8 (Fig. 3M).

Lipogenesis and an immune-exhausted microenvironment coexist at the border

Given that a spot may interact and influence its surrounding neighbors, we further explore cell neighborhood (CN) (within 2 layers) of the landscape of the lipo hotspot. We observed that the CN of lipo hotspot of the NR group had more TAM, while the R group had more CD8 (Figure 4A). Then, GO analysis and GSEA also showed lipogenesis process was enriched in CN of lipo hotspots in NR group, while the R group had more immune-activated pathways (Figure 4B, S3D). To further understand the immune landscape of CN among NR and R, we calculated their extents of connection (denoted as connection factor [CF]) between different types of hotspots (Figure 4C). Higher CF means a closer connection between the central hotspot, i.e., lipo hotspot and the neighborhood spots. We observed that the lipo hotspots and CD8 had a closer connection in the R group, while they had a closer connection with TAM in the NR group (Figure 4D). Aforementioned results indicated the association between lipogenesis and immunosuppression (T cell exhaustion and M2 polarization of TAM) (Figure 2G), we performed correlation analysis between tumor (cell abundance)/ lipogenesis score and immune function (T cell exhaustion score [TEX] and M2 macrophage score [M2]) The result showed a positive correlation between lipo hotspot and CF (M2), which may suggest an intimate connection between lipogenesis and TAM hotspots (Figure 4E-F).

Then, we further identified the hotspots of M2-like TAM and TEX for subsequent analysis. TCGA data also showed cases with higher TEX and M2 scores were associated with a worse prognosis (Figure 4G-H, S4A-B). Meanwhile, non-responder had a higher proportion of TEX and M2 hotspots (Figure S4C). Taken together, our in-house and validation data consistently suggest that the hotspots of lipogenesis, M2, and TEX tend to gather at the tumor border in the NR group to construct the border of the immunosuppressive microenvironment.

High lipogenesis levels in tumor cells cause resistance to immunotherapy

To complement the ST-seq investigation, we performed scRNA-seq and collected 104,700 cells from sixteen patients (twelve untreated and four treated [two R and two NR]). After removing low-quality, multiplet, or contaminated cells, we obtained 52,470 cells and performed dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis (Figure 5A). First, we annotated the cell clusters into parenchymal cells (HCC: APOB), stromal cells (Endo: PECAM1; CAF: COL1A1), lymphocytes (T cell: CD3E; B cell: CD79A; plasma B cell: MZB1) and myeloid cells (monocyte: FCN1; TAM: CD68; DC: CD1C) according to lineage markers (Figure 5B). The top 10 differentially expressed genes of predicted cell types further confirmed the accuracy of cell annotation (Figure 5C). Intriguingly, although individual cell types varied in their proportions among patients, specific cell types were preferentially enriched in R (T cell, monocytes, plasma B, B cells and CAF) and NR (TAM, Endo) patients (Figure 5D). We then performed odds ratios (OR) analysis on the immune cell types, and the results confirmed that patients in the R group had significantly more monocytes and T cells, while patients in the NR group had more TAMs instead (Figure 5E).

To explore the patient heterogeneity among different groups (untreated, R and NR), we explored the landscape of patients' copy number variation (CNV). It shows that HCC cells had noticeable changes in CNV levels compared with immune cells and stromal cells (Figure S6A), and the patients in the R group, untreated group and NR group exhibited high, intermediate and low levels of CNV changes, respectively (Figure 5F). Across the human genome, chromosomes 8, 4 and 17 demonstrated the highest level of discordance in terms of CNV changes between R and NR patients (Figure 5G). Then, we extracted genes that displayed significant CNV differences between R and NR patients from these 3 chromosomes for subsequent pathway enrichment analysis. In accordance with our ST findings that suggest the enrichment of lipogenesis in NR patients and its potential role in leading to poor nivolumab response, we consistently found those CNV-associated genes were closely related to lipid biosynthesis (Figure 5H). Moreover, compared with the R group, patients in the NR group showed a substantially elevated state of lipid metabolism, particularly various lipid biosynthetic processes (Figure S6B-C). We further extracted cells from the NR group for trajectory analysis and their lipogenesis-related signature scores positively correlated with the pseudotime progression (Figure 5I, S6D).

Non-responders exhibited an immunosuppressive microenvironment

Subsequently, we compared the immune microenvironment of patients in the NR group against that in the R group. For T cells, we further subdivided them into CD4 T (CD4), Treg (FOXP3), CD8 T (CD8A), NK T (FCGR3A), and MAIT (KLRB1) (Fig. 6A, S6E). Regarding the quantity, the proportion of different T cells in the NR group was generally less than that in the R group (Fig. 6B). The OR results showed that the immunosuppressive Treg cells tended to be enriched in the NR group, while CD8 was enriched in the R group instead (Fig. 6C). Regarding T cell function, the cytotoxicity score in the R group was higher, while the T cells in the NR group were more inclined to be exhausted and in a late irreversible state (Figure S6F). Moreover, the expressions of most co-inhibitory immune checkpoints (such as TIGIT, LAG3, CTLA4) in NR patients were also significantly and predominantly higher (Figure S6G). We also extracted T cell from the NR group to perform pseudotime analysis, and the results showed that T cells in the NR group showed an irreversible immunosuppressive phenotype in the terminal state (Figure S6H-I) We also observed that compared with the R group, the Treg in the NR group was involved in the lipid metabolism process and was in enhanced nucleotide metabolism (Figure S6J-K). Such a phenomenon was similarly detected in the subset of CD8 T cells, suggesting the mechanistic basis for the apparently worse nivolumab response in that group of patients. We also observed CD8 T cells in the NR group were more inclined to be immune-exhausted and in a late irreversible state (Fig. 6D). Moreover, the expression of immunosuppressive immunotherapy targets in patients in the NR group was higher (Fig. 6E). Further pathway enrichment analysis on CD8 T cells confirmed the relatively higher immune response-related signatures in R patients, as compared to the NR ones (Fig. 6F).

Next, we subdivided myeloid cells into monocytes, neutrophils, TAMs, and dendritic cells (DC) (Fig. 6G). We detected that only TAMs were significantly aggregated in the NR group, while monocytes and DCs were more prevalent in the R group (Fig. 6I). TAMs in the NR group were more inclined to the M2-like phenotype (Fig. 6J). Meanwhile, based on the method of a previous study [26], we similarly divided TAM subsets as interferon-primed TAMs (IFN-TAMs), resident-tissue TAMs (RTM-TAMs), proliferating TAMs (Prolif-TAMs), immune regulatory TAMs (Reg-TAMs), pro-angiogenic TAMs (Angio-TAMs), lipid-associated TAMs (LA-TAMs), and inflammatory cytokine-enriched TAMs (Inflam-TAMs). Although LA-TAMs, RTM-TAMs and Reg-TAMs were the major subtypes in both groups of patients, we identified a slightly higher proportion of LA-TAMs and RTM-TAMs in NR group, whereas Inflam-TAMs were enriched in R group instead (Fig. 6K). Besides, compared with the TAMs in the R group, those in the NR group showed a more proliferative phenotype (Figure S7B-C). Interestingly, pseudotime analysis indicated the progressively increasing proportion of LA-TAMs in the NR group (Figure S7D). Collectively, evidence suggested that the differential proliferation in TAMs of R and NR patients led to the consequential disparity in TAM subtypes.

Enhanced lipogenesis leads to an immunosuppressive microenvironment, affecting the efficacy of nivolumab treatment

Building on our prior spatial transcriptomics findings, which identified TAM, lipid-associated HCC cells, and exhausted CD8 T cells as key components of an immune barrier leading to PD-1 therapy resistance, we sought to elucidate the interactions among these cell types. CellChat revealed a markedly stronger interaction between HCC cells and TAMs in the NR group compared to the R group, whereas communication involving CD8 T cells was significantly more robust in the R group (Fig. 7A, S7E-F). These observations suggest that the enhanced HCC-TAM interaction in the NR group could be a critical driver of PD-1 therapy resistance, underscoring the need to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying this interplay. Subsequently, given the established linkage between lipogenesis and immune function, we employed NicheNet to explore the role of TAMs in regulating lipid metabolism in HCC cells. Our analysis identified LIPA, ADM, CXCL9, CXCL12, and CXCL10 as the primary upstream ligands mediating the expression of key lipogenesis genes in HCC cells (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, we observed significant upregulation of the corresponding ligand-receptor pairs in the NR group (Fig. 7C), indicating heightened signaling activity. These findings highlight the pivotal role of TAM-derived ligands in driving lipid metabolism reprogramming in HCC cells, thereby contributing to the immunosuppressive TIME and PD-1 therapy resistance.

To further validate the cellular interactions contributing to PD-1 therapy resistance, we utilized ST data to assess the ligand-receptor-target axis. We calculated co-occurrence scores for various ligand (M2 hotspot)–receptor (lipo hotspot)–target (lipo hotspot) combinations. Our analysis revealed that two ligand-receptor (LR) pairs (CXCL10-DPP4 and ADM-RAMP1) exhibited significantly elevated co-occurrence scores in the NR group compared to the R group, confirming their role in driving PD-1 therapy resistance (Fig. 7D). Notably, bulk-seq analysis identified RAMP1 as significantly overexpressed in the NR group, suggesting its critical involvement in resistance mechanisms (Fig. 7E-F). Concurrently, ST data demonstrated a strong spatial correlation between ADM and RAMP1 at the sample level, indicating their coordinated activity within the TIME (Fig. 7G). To further elucidate the downstream effects of the ADM-RAMP1 axis on lipogenesis, we integrated differential expression analyses from single-cell NicheNet and bulk-seq data. This approach identified 6 key lipogenesis-related genes (TM7SF2, MVD, IDI1, GPAM, EBP, and DGAT2) as downstream targets modulated by the ADM-RAMP1 interaction (Fig. 7H). In the NR group, co-occurrence scores for ligand-target and receptor-target pairs were aberrantly elevated, underscoring a robust association between these molecular components (Fig. 7I-J). Correlation analysis of bulk-seq data revealed that four key lipogenesis-related genes (EBP, TM7SF2, DGAT2 and GPAM) were closely associated with the ligand (Fig. 7K). RT-qPCR confirmed that EBP expression was abnormally elevated in non-responders (Fig. 7L). Further analysis of ST data revealed a significant spatial correlation between RAMP1 and EBP at the sample level, reinforcing their interconnected roles (Fig. 7M). We examined the expression of ADM at the central M2 hotspots and RAMP1 at the surrounding lipo hotspots and found that the linear regression coefficients were significantly and negatively correlated with distance (Fig. 7N). We also observed a similar phenomenon between that of ADM at the central M2 hotspots and EBP at the surrounding lipo hotspots (Fig. 7N). They consistently indicate that there are close spatial correlations between TAMs and tumor cells via ADM-RAMP1-EBP signaling axis.

Signaling axis pinpointing an intense communication among TAMs, tumor cells, and T cells to elicit PD-1 resistance

Consequently, we also examined the gene expression signals and their co-occurrence regarding the ADM-RAMP1-EBP signaling axis, and they supported our proposed model of cell-cell communication (Fig. 8A, S7G). To validate the proposed communication axis, we first conducted multiple in vitro experiments, including using knockdown of RAMP1 (RAMP1 KD) and recombinant ADM protein. First, we used RAMP1 KD to determine if it could impact the expression of EBP in the target HCC cells. As expected, we found that it significantly suppressed the EBP expression in both Hep3B and 97 L cells (Fig. 8B). We also confirmed that treatment with recombinant ADM protein significantly upregulated RAMP1 expression in Hep3B cells but not in 97 L cells (Fig. 8C, left panel). Nevertheless, both cells resulted in significant upregulation of EBP upon the addition of recombinant ADM protein (Fig. 8C, right panel). Collectively, these experiments could confirm the anticipated ligand-receptor-target communication via the proposed ADM-RAMP1-EBP signaling axis. To explore the consequential effects of ADM-RAMP1-EBP signaling axis on the TIME, we conducted direct co-culture experiments between HCC and THP1 cells or CD8 T cells. Upon RAMP1 KD in 97 L cells resulted in an increase and decrease of the expressions of M1-related and M2-related markers, respectively, in THP1 cells (Fig. 8D). Flow cytometry further confirmed an increase in the proportion of M1-like macrophages (CD86+) and a decrease in the proportion of M2-like macrophages (CD206+) after RAMP1 KD (Fig. 8E-F). By repeating the aforementioned assays using Hep3B cells, we detected consistent results as in 97 L cells (Fig. 8G-I). Notably, flow cytometry also confirmed an increase in the proportion of cytotoxic T cells (GZMB+ CD8 T) upon co-culturing with RAMP1 KD HCC cells, as compared to the non-target control (NTC) (Fig. 8J). We hypothesized that cytokine secretion could govern the observed phenotypic consequences of M2 polarization in TAMs and cytotoxicity in CD8 T cells. We performed multi-cytokine array experiments to substantiate our hypotheses. The results demonstrate that RAMP1 KD in HCC cells resulted in differential cytokine secretion, which might explain the phenotypic reprogramming in macrophages and CD8 T cells (Figure S8A-B). Previous study reported reduction in CCL5 could promote M2-like polarization and reduce the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade [27], while increased level of CCL5 could increase the cytotoxicity of CD8 T cells and their tumor infiltration [28, 29]. Collectively, our ST data demonstrated not only the association of M2-like polarization of macrophages and heightened expression of the ligand (ADM)-receptor (RAMP1)-target (EBP) axis in the NR group, but also their subsequent leading to the functional consequence of exhaustion of CD8 T cells (Fig. 8K). To evaluate the therapeutic potential of the combined actions of EBP inhibition and anti-PD1 immune checkpoint blockade, we utilized an orthotopic liver injection HCC mouse model (Fig. 8L). While anti-PD1 or EBP inhibitor monotherapy effectively suppressed tumor growth, as evidenced by a significant reduction in tumor mass (Fig. 8M-O), the combination of both agents showed a robust synergistic effect in further abrogating the tumor growth (Fig. 8M-O). Our findings underscore targeted EBP modulation as a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of anti-PD1-based immunotherapy treatment for HCC.

Discussion

Our study leverages an extensive in-house [30] and public datasets of multimodal sequencing to elucidate the distinct characteristics of anti-PD-1 therapy in HCC patients. By integrating spatial transcriptomics, single-cell and bulk transcriptomics, and lipidomics profiling, complemented by multi-cohort validation, we conducted a comprehensive spatial analysis of the cellular and molecular determinants influencing therapeutic response. These approaches enabled the reconstruction of the spatiotemporal dynamics of nivolumab treatment, revealing critical factors and biomarkers associated with immunotherapy resistance in HCC patients based on their anticipated therapeutic response. Central to our findings is the role of lipid metabolism reprogramming, which emerged as a key driver of anti-PD-1 resistance and shed light on promising perspectives for further exploration of its mechanistic underpinnings.

Lipid metabolism is pivotal in regulating effector functions of immune cells by providing essential bioenergetic substrates for immune infiltration and functional modulation [31, 32]. Our investigation demonstrates that reprogramming of lipid metabolism in HCC tumor cells induces a sustained immune evasion mechanism, significantly contributing to the resistance against anti-PD-1 therapy. This observation aligns with prior reports that alterations of lipid metabolic processes could promote immunosuppressive activities of macrophages and CD8 T cell exhaustion, leading to resistance of PD-1 blockade [23, 33]. Through integrated pathway enrichment analysis and comprehensive lipidomics profiling, we identified markedly enhanced lipid metabolism in non-responders, establishing a direct linkage between this metabolic shift and immune evasion. These findings suggest that HCC tumors in the subpopulation of non-responders have enhanced lipid metabolism to elicit heightened immune evasion. This offers critical prognostic implications for patients receiving anti-PD-1 therapy and highlights the need to explore the spatial distribution of lipid metabolic changes.

To further elucidate the spatial dynamics of resistance, we performed multidimensional analysis of spatial transcriptomics data encompassing diverse cell types, reconstructing the spatiotemporal course of action underlying the anti-PD-1 therapy resistance. Our spatiotemporal model revealed distinct patterns within the tumor microenvironment: responders exhibited robust immune cell infiltration, whereas non-responders displayed immune-exclusive phenotypes with a sparse presence of immune infiltration. Consistent with previous studies identifying TAMs as critical components of the tumor immune barrier [23], we observed that in the G1 area (immediately outside the tumor niche interface), TAMs were abundant and co-localized with exhausted CD8 T cells and lipogenesis-associated HCC cells. This co-localization fostered a robust immune exclusive barrier in non-responders, impeding immune cell infiltration and contributing to diminished therapeutic efficacy. These spatial insights build on our aforementioned findings of aberrant lipid metabolism, and they jointly suggest that metabolic reprogramming and immune-exclusive barrier formation are synergistic mechanisms driving anti-PD1 resistance.

Delving deeper into the molecular mechanisms, prior studies have established that TAMs and exhausted CD8 T cells as immunosuppressive units, to modulate aberrant signaling pathways in HCC, including NF-κB, AKT, and MAPK, thereby influencing responsiveness to anti-PD-L1 therapy [34–36]. Our study extends these findings by demonstrating non-responder (NR) patients exhibit a pronounced immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TIME), intricately linked to lipid metabolism reprogramming. Specifically, our data explores the upstream mechanism of lipogenesis and highlights adrenomedullin (ADM), a gene highly expressed in TAMs, as a pivotal regulator of tumor lipogenesis and immunotherapy outcomes. Previous studies have shown that ADM promotes tumor initiation and progression by regulating the secretion of monounsaturated fatty acids, cytokines, and growth factors [37–39]. Mechanistically, we found that ADM upregulates downstream MVD/EBP expression, fostering an immunosuppressive TIME that facilitates tumor progression and contributes to immunotherapy resistance. This molecular insight connects the spatial and metabolic findings, reinforcing the role of ADM in orchestrating resistance mechanisms.

Given that publicly accessible datasets on neoadjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy with scRNA-seq or ST-seq performed are limited, the currently utilized validation cohorts may represent the most comprehensive and best available data to verify our discovery findings. Nevertheless, we are fully aware of the various limitations of our study that warrant careful consideration. Primarily, the relatively small sample size of our validation cohorts inherently restricts the statistical strength, potentially leading to the risks of over-interpretation and pseudo-replication. There could also be an inefficiency in detecting subtle but yet genuine biological effects, which limits the generalizability of our findings to a broader perspective. Moreover, the robustness of our validation cohort could also be a concern, as its characteristics might not fully capture the heterogeneity present in the general HCC patient population. Despite the aforementioned imperfections of our validation cohorts, the consistent trends and observations detected between the discovery and validation cohorts in our study reinforce our belief that the upregulation of ADM-RAMP1-EBP signaling axis is likely a bona fide factor contributing to the consequences of anti-PD-1 resistance, and its inhibition may help sensitize HCCs resulting in more effective treatment. Taken together, our current preliminary findings are exploratory and are hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. They should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with substantially expanded cohorts will be much awaited for better reconstruction of anti-PD-1 treatment action as well as the functional and mechanistic characterization of its resistance.

Despite there is some degree of efficacy of single-agent immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as anti-PD-1 therapy, in a restricted subset of HCC patients, the majority exhibit suboptimal responses, necessitating a comprehensive understanding of resistance mechanisms. Our integrative multimodal approach, combining spatial transcriptomics, single-cell and bulk transcriptomics, and lipidomics with multi-cohort validation, systematically elucidated the molecular and cellular determinants of PD-1 therapy resistance in HCC. We identified pronounced pro-tumorigenic signaling driven by lipid metabolism reprogramming in non-responders, mediated through upregulation of the ADM-RAMP1-EBP signaling axis, which suppresses anti-tumor immunity. These findings suggest that dual targeting of PD-1 (immune checkpoint of T cells) and ADM (TAM-associated regulator of lipid metabolism) may synergistically enhance therapeutic efficacy, offering a promising target to strategically complement the immune checkpoint inhibition. By reconstructing a spatiotemporal model that delineates the lipid metabolism reprogramming underlying anti-PD-1 therapy resistance, our study not only clarifies the mechanistic basis of resistance but also provides novel insights into actionable therapeutic targets, paving the way for improved immunotherapy outcomes in HCC.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

Sample collection

HCC tissues were obtained from patients who underwent surgical resections at the Queen Mary Hospital. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. All protocols and experiments were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (UW 17–056).

Sample process and raw data obtained

Sample collection and preliminary processing for spatial transcriptomics (ST-seq), single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and bulk RNA sequencing (bulk-seq) were performed in accordance with the standardized protocols established in our previous study [30].

Cell line experiments

Cell culture and recombinant ADM protein treatment

Human HCC cell lines MHCC-97 L and Hep3B were cultured in DMEM medium (12100046, Gibco) consisting of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, A5256801, Gibco), 1mM sodium pyruvate (100mM; 11360070, Gibco), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; 10,000 U/ml; 15140122, Gibco). Human monocyte cell line THP1 was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (31800022, Gibco) consisting of 10% FBS, 0.05mM 2-mercaptoethanol (50 mM; 31350010, Gibco), and 1% P/S. HEK293FT was cultured in DMEM medium consisting of 10% FBS and 1% P/S. The cells were kept in a CO2 incubator with a 5% concentration and a temperature of 37 ℃. The cell culture medium was replaced every two days. In select experiments, MHCC-97 L and Hep3B were treated with the recombinant ADM protein (20ug/ml; HY-P74426, MedChemExpress) or corresponding controls for 24 h (Tables 3 and 4).

Quantification and statistical analysis

ScRNA-seq data processing

Unsupervised clustering and marker identification

For primary cell type identification, the UMI count matrix was converted to a Seurat object using the R package Seurat. Cells with fewer than 800 detected genes or more than 5,000 genes were filtered. After removing the batch effect using R package Harmony, the first 40 principal components were selected through principal component analysis (PCA) for unsupervised clustering via the t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) algorithms at a resolution of 1.0, and cell types were annotated based on prior knowledge of marker genes.

For the T cell subtype and myeloid subtype, the clustering methodology is like primary cell type identification. Cell types were identified based on the expression of marker genes.

Differential analysis between responders and non-responders

We utilized the odds ratio to compare cell-type composition between the two groups, focusing on quantitative differences [40]. Copy number variation (CNV) detection was performed using the inferCNV package (https://github.com/broadinstitute/infercnv), and pseudotime analysis was conducted using the Monocle2 package [41]. Pathway analysis was carried out with the ClusterProfiler package [42], and results with a p-value less than 0.05 were included.

To investigate cell-cell communication, we employed Cellchat [43] and Nichenetr [44] for subsequent analysis. Unless otherwise specified, the aforementioned software packages were used with their default parameters for the respective analyses.

ST-seq data processing

Quality control, dimension reduction, and unsupervised clustering

We used SPATA2 [45] to detect and remove low-quality (number of genes < 150) and marginal spots and then used BANKSY [46] (lambda = 0.2, k_geom = 6) for unsupervised dimensionality reduction clustering. MIA [17] was used to identify the cell enrichment of each cluster, and p < 0.05 was the enrichment threshold.

Definition of cellular scores and signature attributes

We used the ClusterProfiler package to perform pathway enrichment analysis, and the gene sets were derived from the GSEA (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) and progeny database [47]. The T cell-related [48, 49], TAM-related [26, 50], and lipogenesis [20, 21] gene sets were derived from previously published studies. Besides, survival analysis data are from the TCGA database.

Spatial distance and cellular spatial colocalization analysis

Spatial distance analysis and non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) analysis depend on Semla [18]. We performed cross-validation on the highly variable genes to find the best ranking and learn a model for that ranking to speed up computation. The best number of factors obtained from NMF is 35. Next, we used BayesPrism [19] to deconvolute the cell type abundance of each spot, and then MISTY [51] was used to explore the cell type colocalization of intra-spot and para-spot. Based on the hotspot definition from SpottedPy [25], the spdep package and the Getis-Ord Gi* [52] algorithm were used to identify each hotspot type and perform subsequent analysis. In addition, the distance of para-spot and cell neighborhood analysis is two layers of a central spot. All software uses default parameters to perform analysis unless otherwise stated.

Definition and calculation of co-occurrence

Since effective cell-cell communication typically occurs within a distance of 200 μm, and considering the technical specifications of the 10X Visium platform (where each spot has a diameter of approximately 55 μm), we restricted the calculation of co-occurrence scores to within two layers of neighboring spots to capture biologically relevant interactions. To this end, we developed a rigorous method to predict the co-occurrence scores between a target spot and its adjacent spots in spatial transcriptomics data: (Eq 1 and 2).

In the proposed formula for calculating co-occurrence scores within the 10X Visium spatial transcriptomics framework, each analytical unit comprises a central spot and its two layers of neighboring spots. Here, ( i ) denotes the source molecule (e.g., a ligand in a ligand-receptor interaction), and ( j ) represents the target molecule (e.g., the receptor in a ligand-receptor pair). The variable ( A ) corresponds to the cell type expressing the source molecule at the central spot, while ( B ) denotes the cell type expressing the target molecule at the surrounding spots. Specifically, ( Ai ) represents the expression level of the source molecule on the source cell type at the central spot, ( Bj ) indicates the expression level of the target molecule on the target cell type at the surrounding spots, and ( Aj ) reflects the expression level of the target molecule on the target cell type at the central spot. Given that a single spot in 10X Visium data may encompass multiple cell types, we account for this heterogeneity by applying two distinct computational approaches. For cases where the central spot contains only one cell type, we employ formula (1) to calculate the co-occurrence score. In scenarios where the central spot includes both the source and target cell types, formula (2) is utilized.

Statistical analysis

Most statistical analyses and graphics production were performed using R software and GraphPad Prism 7. A Wilcox test was used to compare the mean values between the two groups.
