A Multivariate CT‐Based Model for Differentiating Styloid Syndrome From Asymptomatic Styloid Elongation: A Retrospective Comparative Study of Positional and Morphological Parameters
Authors: Yifan Wang, Zezheng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo compare computed tomography positional and morphological parameters between patients with styloid syndrome and individuals with asymptomatic styloid elongation.MethodsA retrospective study of 151 patients with styloid syndrome and 162 individuals with asymptomatic styloid elongation was conducted. The styloid process length, stylopharyngeal distance, medial‐lateral angle, anterior–posterior angle, as well as presence or absence of stylohyoid ligament calcification, styloid process curvature, and styloid process thickening were evaluated by computed tomography.ResultsStyloid syndrome patients exhibited significantly shorter bilateral stylopharyngeal distances, smaller left anterior–posterior angle, and a higher prevalence of styloid process curvature. Individuals with asymptomatic styloid elongation showed higher rates of stylohyoid ligament calcification and styloid process thickening. A logistic regression model integrating bilateral stylopharyngeal distance, left anterior–posterior angle, styloid process curvature, stylohyoid ligament calcification, and age yielded an area under the curve of 0.902.ConclusionComputed tomography parameters including shortened bilateral stylopharyngeal distance, reduced left anterior–posterior angle, and increased styloid process curvature effectively differentiate styloid syndrome from asymptomatic styloid elongation. Asymptomatic styloid elongation individuals are characterized by higher rates of stylohyoid ligament calcification and styloid process thickening. A logistic regression model integrating these indicators demonstrates promising discriminative potential, though external validation is required before clinical application.Level of Evidence3

Introduction

The styloid process (SP) is a cylindrical bony projection arising from the base of the temporal bone, adjacent to the stylomastoid foramen and temporomandibular joint, with its tip positioned between the internal and external carotid arteries [1]. A normal SP measures 20 to 30 mm in length, and elongation is defined as exceeding 30 mm [2]. Elongated SP may compress adjacent neurovascular structures, inducing non‐specific symptoms such as neck pain, otalgia, pharyngeal foreign body sensation, and dysphagia [3].

Styloid syndrome is a series of symptoms caused by abnormal shape, length, and orientation of SP or calcification of the stylohyoid ligament. It was first proposed by Watt W. Eagle in 1937, so it is also called Eagle's syndrome [2]. Diagnosis relies on clinical and radiological findings [4], but critically, not all elongated SPs are symptomatic [5]. It is reported that 4% of the general population exhibit SP elongation, yet only 4 to 10% of them develop symptoms [6]. This raises questions about the morphological and positional factors that contribute to the development of symptoms.

While prior studies have focused on SP length as a diagnostic criterion, emerging evidence suggests that positional variations such as angulation and proximity to adjacent structures may better explain symptomatic presentations [7, 8, 9]. Computed tomography (CT) enables high‐resolution visualization of SP anatomy, facilitating precise measurements of length, angles, and morphological features, which provides a quantitative basis for diagnosis [10]. Previous studies have mostly focused on the correlation between SP length and symptoms, while there has been less exploration of the angle, spatial position, and morphological variations of the SP [11]. To date, research on the combined diagnosis using multiple indicators is still not well developed.

This study compared the CT image characteristics of patients with styloid syndrome and those with asymptomatic styloid elongation, evaluating length, stylopharyngeal distance, angular parameters, and morphological characteristics. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and logistic regression models were used to assess diagnostic performance, aiming to provide objective evidence for clinical differentiation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

The retrospective study was approved by the ethics committee of the hospital. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. Consecutive patients who underwent head and neck computed tomography (CT) at our institution between January 2019 and December 2024 were screened for eligibility. Of the 1247 patients screened, 398 (31.9%) had CT‐documented styloid process elongation (≥ 30 mm). After applying exclusion criteria, 151 patients were included in the styloid syndrome group and 162 in the asymptomatic styloid elongation group (Figure 1).

Participant flow diagram. A total of 1247 patients were screened. After exclusions, 151 patients were included in the styloid syndrome group and 162 in the asymptomatic styloid elongation group.

Styloid syndrome group (n = 151): Patients were diagnosed with styloid syndrome if they met at least three of the following criteria: (1) presence of symptoms (neck pain, otalgia, pharyngeal foreign body sensation, positional headache, or dysphagia, etc.); (2) palpable bony protrusion in the tonsillar fossa; (3) symptom relief after tonsillar fossa lidocaine infiltration; (4) CT evidence of SP elongation (≥ 30 mm), abnormal angulation, stylohyoid ligament calcification, SP curvature, or thickening.

Asymptomatic styloid elongation group (n = 162): Consecutive individuals with CT‐documented SP elongation (as defined above) included if they underwent CT for indications unrelated to styloid syndrome and had no styloid‐related symptoms. The indications comprised thyroid nodule evaluation (n = 72, 44.4%), carotid artery atherosclerosis assessment (n = 48, 29.6%), and cervical spine degeneration evaluation (n = 42, 25.9%). Asymptomatic individuals with cervical spine degeneration were included only if they had no cervical spine‐related symptoms (e.g., radiating arm pain, numbness, or myelopathic signs). Asymptomatic status was confirmed through a systematic review of electronic medical records, including chief complaints, history of present illness, physical examination findings, and relevant specialty consultation notes. To ensure reliability, we additionally reviewed follow‐up records within 3 months after CT imaging to confirm that none of these individuals presented with neck pain, otalgia, pharyngeal foreign body sensation, dysphagia, or positional headache during this period.

Exclusion criteria for both groups included: (1) history of head and neck trauma or surgery; (2) temporal tumors, paranasal sinus tumors, or significant facial deformities; (3) other head and neck diseases that could explain symptoms such as neck pain, otalgia, pharyngeal foreign body sensation, positional headache, or dysphagia, including but not limited to symptomatic cervical spondylosis (with neck pain, radiculopathy, or myelopathy), symptomatic carotid artery stenosis (with transient ischemic attacks or stroke), and acute thyroiditis.

CT Technique

CT scans were performed using a 128‐slice multislice helical CT system (SOMATOM Definition Edge; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Patients were placed in the head‐first supine (HFS) posture, and scanning was conducted in the axial plane with the following parameters: 120 kV tube voltage, 200 mA tube current, 1 mm slice thickness, 1 s exposure time, reconstruction field of view (FOV): 310 mm, and image matrix: 512 × 512. All images were reconstructed on a Siemens syngo.via workstation (Version VA30) using a dedicated bone reconstruction algorithm (Bf42s, Siemens) with integrated metal artifact reduction (iMAR), generating coronal, sagittal multiplanar reconstructions (MPRs), and three‐dimensional (3D) volume rendering (VR) views.

Observer Agreement

Two radiologists with over 5 years of head and neck imaging experience independently measured and evaluated all imaging indicators. Both radiologists were blinded to patients' symptomatic status (presence or absence of styloid syndrome symptoms). Clinical information, including symptoms, signs, and medical history, was disclosed only after the completion of image assessment.

Inter‐observer agreement was assessed using Cohen's kappa for qualitative indicators and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for quantitative indicators. Specifically, Cohen's kappa was used for morphological classification, stylohyoid ligament calcification, SP curvature, and SP thickening. The ICC (two‐way random effects model) was applied for SP length, MLA, APA, and stylopharyngeal distance.

Intra‐observer agreement was assessed by one radiologist re‐evaluating a randomly selected subset of 94 participants (30% of the total cohort) 2 weeks after the initial assessment, blinded to the previous results, using the same methods.

Quantitative results from the two radiologists were averaged to generate final data. Qualitative discrepancies were resolved via joint review of images.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the rms package (version 6.3‐0). Independent‐samples t‐tests and chi‐square tests were used for univariate comparisons between groups. ROC curves assessed diagnostic performance (AUC, sensitivity, specificity, Youden's index) for CT parameters with significant group differences.

A multivariate binary logistic regression model was built to identify independent risk factors for styloid syndrome via a two‐step approach. Univariate screening: Independent‐samples t‐tests were used to compare continuous variables (age, length, MLA, APA, stylopharyngeal distance) between groups, and chi‐square tests were used to compare categorical variables (sex, stylohyoid ligament calcification, SP curvature, SP thickening). Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were considered candidates for multivariable modeling. Clinically relevant variables with p values between 0.10 and 0.20 were also retained to avoid omission of potential confounders. Bilateral styloidopharyngeal distance was defined as the mean value of left and right styloidopharyngeal distances, treated as a single continuous variable in the model. A forward stepwise logistic regression (likelihood ratio test: entry p < 0.05, removal p < 0.10) was performed to identify independent predictors. Before modeling, multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF < 10) and Pearson correlation coefficients (r < 0.7). Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer‐Lemeshow test (p > 0.05 indicating good fit) and Nagelkerke R 2.

Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations to assess model overfitting, with a random seed set to ensure reproducibility. The optimism‐corrected area under the curve (AUC) was derived using the bootstrap approach, and model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer‐Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test.

Four sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings. (1) Left and right stylopharyngeal distances were entered as separate variables instead of the bilateral mean. (2) Ordinal coding of morphological features: SP curvature was graded by severity (0 = absent; 1 = mild [10°–20°]; 2 = moderate [21°–30°]; 3 = severe [> 30°]). Stylohyoid ligament calcification was coded by morphological pattern (0 = absent; 1 = punctate or segmental; 2 = continuous ossification without pseudoarthrosis; 3 = complete ossification with pseudoarthrosis). (3) Sex was included as an additional covariate in the multivariable logistic regression model. (4) A subgroup analysis was performed using a purely clinical case definition (symptoms, palpable bony protrusion in the tonsillar fossa, and positive lidocaine test), excluding imaging criteria.

Statistical significance was set at two‐tailed p < 0.05. The analytical workflow following patient inclusion is summarized in Figure 3.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Among the 313 participants included in this study, no missing data were present for the CT measurements and clinical variables analyzed. Participants with incomplete records were not included, consistent with the prespecified inclusion criteria. The styloid syndrome group included 72 males (47.7%) and 79 females (52.3%) with a mean age of 54.2 years. The asymptomatic styloid elongation group included 72 males (44.4%) and 90 females (55.6%) with a mean age of 43.6 years. Styloid syndrome patients were significantly older (p < 0.001) than individuals of asymptomatic styloid elongation, with no gender difference between groups. ROC Curve Analysis

ROC curve analysis showed that the right stylopharyngeal distance (AUC = 0.718, 95% CI: 0.663 to 0.773; cutoff: 18.5 mm; sensitivity: 71.5%, specificity: 64.2%), left stylopharyngeal distance (AUC = 0.737, 95% CI: 0.683 to 0.791; cutoff: 17.8 mm; sensitivity: 73.5%, specificity: 66.0%), and left APA (AUC = 0.750, 95% CI: 0.698 to 0.802; cutoff: 20.2°; sensitivity: 76.2%, specificity: 68.5%) demonstrated moderate discriminative ability for styloid syndrome (Figure 6).

Variable Selection and Multicollinearity Assessment

In univariate screening, age (p < 0.001), bilateral stylopharyngeal distance (p < 0.001), left APA (p = 0.021), SP curvature (p = 0.047), stylohyoid ligament calcification (p < 0.001), and SP thickening (p = 0.023) all demonstrated statistically significant between‐group differences. During multivariable stepwise regression, SP thickening was not retained (p > 0.10). Age remained an independent predictor in the multivariable model (OR = 1.08, p < 0.001). Before multivariate modeling, multicollinearity among the 5 included variables was assessed via variance inflation factor (VIF) and Pearson correlation coefficients. VIF values ranged from 1.12 to 2.87 (1.85 for bilateral styloidopharyngeal distance, 1.12 for left APA, 2.87 for SP curvature, 2.63 for stylohyoid ligament calcification, 1.54 for age), far below the critical value of 10.0. For continuous variables, the maximum Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.43 (others < 0.5), indicating no severe linear association. Thus, the 5 variables met the multivariate model construction conditions.

Multivariate Logistic Regression Model

A Logistic regression model was constructed to predict styloid syndrome, including bilateral stylopharyngeal distance, left APA, SP curvature, stylohyoid ligament calcification, and age. The model was statistically significant (χ 2 = 192.215, df = 6, p < 0.001) with a Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.612 (explaining 61.2% of variance) and good fit (Hosmer‐Lemeshow test, p = 0.081). Bilateral stylopharyngeal distance (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.81 to 0.91, p < 0.001) and left APA (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.72 to 0.84, p < 0.001) were protective factors (OR < 1). Age (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.11, p < 0.001) was a risk factor (OR > 1, indicating a higher risk with increasing age). The model demonstrated an apparent AUC of 0.902 (95% CI: 0.868–0.936) (Table 5).

Internal Validation

Bootstrap internal validation with 1000 resamples yielded an optimism‐corrected AUC of 0.876 (95% CI: 0.839–0.913), compared with an apparent AUC of 0.902. The Hosmer‐Lemeshow test was non‐significant (χ 2 = 8.24, df = 8, p = 0.409), indicating adequate model calibration.

Sensitivity Analyses

Four sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings. (1) Separate analysis of left and right stylopharyngeal distances: When entered as separate variables instead of the bilateral mean, both remained independently predictive (left: OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.80–0.91, p < 0.001; right: OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.82–0.93, p < 0.001), with an apparent AUC of 0.898, consistent with the primary analysis. (2) Ordinal coding of morphological features: When SP curvature and stylohyoid ligament calcification were both coded as ordinal variables (0 = absent; 1 = mild; 2 = moderate; 3 = severe), the severity of SP curvature showed a significant association (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.08–1.59, p = 0.006), while calcification remained nonsignificant (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.68–1.22, p = 0.528). The apparent AUC of this alternative coding was 0.905 (compared with 0.902 in the primary analysis). (3) Inclusion of sex: Adding sex to the original model did not significantly improve model fit, and sex was not independently associated with the outcome (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.51–1.54, p = 0.677). Effect estimates for other variables remained essentially unchanged. (4) Subgroup analysis excluding imaging criteria from case definition: To assess the potential impact of incorporation bias, we redefined the styloid syndrome group using a purely clinical case definition (symptoms, palpable bony protrusion in the tonsillar fossa, and positive lidocaine test) without reliance on any CT findings. In this subgroup analysis (98 cases vs. 162 asymptomatic controls), the apparent AUC was 0.881 (95% CI: 0.839–0.923), modestly lower than the primary analysis (0.902), suggesting that while incorporation bias exists, it is not the sole driver of the model's performance.

Discussion

This study identifies key CT features that distinguish patients with styloid syndrome from individuals with asymptomatic styloid elongation. These features emphasize that differences in SP spatial position and morphology are more indicative of symptomatic status than SP length alone.

Key CT Differences

Styloid syndrome patients exhibited shorter stylopharyngeal distances than asymptomatic styloid elongation individuals, suggesting that the reduction in the spatial distance between the SP and the pharyngeal lateral wall may contribute to compression symptoms. A shortened stylopharyngeal distance indicates that the SP is closer to the blood vessels and nerves on the lateral pharyngeal wall. It is anatomically plausible that during swallowing or neck movement, mechanical irritation of the SP on surrounding tissues increases, which could be associated with symptoms such as pain and foreign body sensation. This finding is consistent with the conclusion in previous studies that the spatial relationship between the SP and the surrounding structures is more predictive of symptoms than simple length [8].

Besides, the average angle of left APA in the styloid syndrome group was less than 20 degrees, significantly smaller than that in the asymptomatic styloid elongation group, while there was no significant group difference in the right APA. A smaller APA could theoretically increase ligament tension, and muscle contractions during swallowing might trigger referred pain. Similar to previous studies, this research also found the left–right asymmetry of the SP angulation [12, 13, 14]. This suggests that the asymmetry of the APA may be related to the more common phenomenon of left symptoms in clinical practice [15].

It is worth noting that there was no significant difference in the bilateral SP length between the two groups. Previous studies have also found that in patients with styloid syndrome, the length of the SP is not correlated with the severity of their clinical symptoms [9, 10]. This further confirms that simple styloid elongation is not the sole determinant of symptom occurrence [7, 9, 16], suggesting that clinical diagnosis should integrate spatial position and morphological characteristics rather than relying solely on SP length.

Regarding the morphological characteristics of the SP, the proportion of SP curvature was higher in the styloid syndrome group, while styloid ligament calcification and SP thickening were more common in the asymptomatic styloid elongation group. It is anatomically plausible that SP curvature could increase point contact pressure on surrounding tissues by altering its spatial orientation [16]. One might hypothesize that when curvature involves the middle or lower segments of the SP, it may be more likely to irritate the pharyngeal lateral wall or carotid sheath [16, 17]. The higher proportion of stylohyoid ligament calcification and styloid thickening in the asymptomatic group could theoretically reduce the mobility of the SP through a stabilizing effect, potentially diminishing dynamic stimulation to the adjacent structures. However, this hypothesized mechanism was not directly assessed in our study and warrants further investigation using dynamic imaging or biomechanical modeling. This anatomical observation may help explain why some individuals with styloid elongation remain asymptomatic [18].

These indications suggest that the symptoms of styloid syndrome may arise from the combined effect of spatial factors (shortened stylopharyngeal distance and reduced APA) and morphological factors (SP curvature), rather than simply SP elongation. For individuals with asymptomatic SP elongation, the presence of ligament calcification and SP thickening could theoretically contribute to a stabilizing effect. Pending further validation, clinical diagnosis should consider a comprehensive assessment of three‐dimensional spatial and morphological parameters rather than relying solely on SP length.

Utility of a Multivariate Logistic Regression Model for Diagnosing Styloid Syndrome

The results of the ROC curve analysis indicate that the diagnostic performance of a single indicator such as the stylopharyngeal distance or APA is relatively limited. This might be related to the characteristic in clinical practice that the symptoms of styloid syndrome are caused by the combined effect of multiple factors [7, 19]. The multifactorial logistic regression model (apparent AUC = 0.902) outperformed single parameters, highlighting the value of combining spatial (stylopharyngeal distance, APA), morphological (SP curvature, stylohyoid ligament calcification), and demographic (age) factors.

In the model, the OR values of bilateral styropharyngeal distance and left APA were less than 1, suggesting that the smaller their values, the higher the risk of styloid syndrome, further verifying the diagnostic value of these parameters. Age emerged as a risk factor, potentially reflecting age‐related tissue degeneration or increased neural sensitivity exacerbating symptom perception. Notably, SP curvature (OR = 1.26, p = 0.069) neared significance as a risk factor (95% CI: 0.98 to 1.62), consistent with clinical observations that abnormal angulation of SP may heighten surrounding structure compression [16]. The 26% elevated risk is biologically plausible, though the confidence interval includes 1.0, indicating that statistical significance was not achieved, possibly due to limited power. In contrast, stylohyoid ligament calcification showed a nonsignificant association (OR = 0.87, p = 0.703; 95% CI: 0.53 to 1.43), with imprecise estimates likely due to calcification pattern heterogeneity or unmeasured confounders, warranting detailed morphological assessment [20].

Collectively, these findings highlight the value of multifactorial modeling. Despite modest individual effects (ORs: 0.78 to 1.26) in the final model, its optimism‐corrected AUC of 0.876 reflects synergistic contributions from spatial, morphological, and demographic parameters, demonstrating promising discriminative potential. However, external validation in independent cohorts is warranted before clinical application.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this single‐center study may have introduced regional bias, and the findings require validation in multicenter cohorts. Second, parameters such as the distance between the styloid process and peripheral blood vessels (e.g., the internal carotid artery) were not assessed; future studies incorporating vascular imaging techniques may help elucidate the association with vascular compression symptoms. Third, the correlation between symptom severity and CT parameters was not analyzed, which limits the ability to inform treatment selection based on imaging findings.

Additionally, an important methodological consideration relates to the inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria for the styloid syndrome group required meeting at least three of four criteria, including clinical symptoms, physical examination, lidocaine test, and CT imaging features. While all patients underwent CT as part of the retrospective data collection, the criteria allowed for inclusion based solely on clinical symptoms, physical examination, and lidocaine test, without requiring imaging findings. However, for cases where imaging features contributed to meeting the criteria, incorporation bias may occur, potentially inflating the apparent discriminative performance of the model. Therefore, the reported AUC of 0.902 should be interpreted as apparent performance in the derivation cohort. Bootstrap internal validation yielded an optimism‐corrected AUC of 0.876, supporting the model's robustness despite this limitation. Future studies with independent validation cohorts are needed to confirm the discriminative value of CT parameters without incorporation bias.

Conclusion

There are significant differences in CT features between styloid syndrome patients and asymptomatic styloid elongation individuals. Among them, bilateral shortened stylopharyngeal distance, reduced left APA, and increased proportion of SP curvature are important imaging markers of styloid syndrome patients, while asymptomatic styloid elongation individuals are more prone to calcification of the stylohyoid ligament and thickening of the SP. The logistic regression model combining multiple indicators demonstrates promising discriminative potential for differentiating styloid syndrome from asymptomatic styloid elongation. However, external validation in independent cohorts is warranted before clinical implementation.
