Food inflammation index and risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome: a nationally representative cohort study from NHANES 1999–2018
Authors: Guanhua Li, Qian Yu, Donglai Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evidence on the association between the Food Inflammation Index (FII) and mortality risk in individuals with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome (CKM) remains limited. This study examined the associations of the FII with all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality in adults with CKM. We included 22,611 adults with CKM from NHANES 1999–2018. The FII was calculated using 24-hour dietary recall data. Multivariable Cox regression, restricted cubic spline, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses were performed. During a median follow-up of 9.3 years (interquartile range, 4.9–13.8 years), corresponding to 217,405 person-years, 3,745 deaths were documented, including 1,180 from CVD. In the fully adjusted model, compared with the lowest FII quartile, the highest FII quartile was associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality (HR, 1.392; 95% CI, 1.218–1.592; P for trend < 0.001) and CVD mortality (HR, 1.337; 95% CI, 1.045–1.709; P for trend = 0.019). Significant nonlinear associations were also observed. Higher FII levels were significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause and CVD mortality among individuals with CKM. These findings suggest that dietary inflammatory burden, as reflected by the FII, may provide useful information for mortality risk assessment in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56498-2.

Introduction

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome (CKM), introduced by the American Heart Association (AHA) in 2023, provides an integrated framework for understanding the complex and mutually reinforcing interactions among cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic disorders1. Moving beyond the traditional single-organ view of chronic disease, the CKM construct recognizes obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD) as interrelated manifestations of a shared pathobiological continuum driven by inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, neurohormonal activation, and metabolic imbalance2–4. This framework has rapidly emerged as a major focus in chronic disease prevention and risk stratification. Data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) indicate that, during 2011–2020, only 10.6% of adults remained in CKM stage 0, whereas nearly 90% had progressed to stages 1–4; stage 2 was the most prevalent (49.0%), and advanced stages 3–4 accounted for 14.6%, with little evidence of improvement in the overall burden over the past decade5,6.

Among the mechanisms implicated in the initiation and progression of CKM, chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress are thought to represent central biological threads linking the heart, kidney, and metabolic systems7,8. In this context, diet is of particular interest because, unlike many fixed risk factors, it is both modifiable and cumulatively influential over the life course. By shaping inflammatory tone, insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, gut microbial ecology, and endothelial homeostasis, habitual dietary exposure may substantially influence the development and progression of diseases across the CKM spectrum9,10. Increasing attention has therefore been directed toward dietary inflammatory potential as a mechanistic link between dietary patterns and chronic disease outcomes. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), one of the most widely used metrics for this purpose, was developed to quantify the overall pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory potential of habitual diet. Accumulating evidence suggests that higher DII scores are associated with greater systemic inflammatory burden, poorer cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic health, and increased risks of CVD incidence, CVD mortality, CKD, and reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)11–13. More recently, NHANES-based studies have extended these observations to the CKM framework itself, showing that a higher DII is associated not only with greater CKM risk and more advanced staging, but also with increased long-term mortality among individuals with CKM14. Together, these findings support the concept that dietary inflammation may be an important and modifiable determinant of prognosis across the CKM continuum.

Despite its widespread use in epidemiological research, the DII is primarily constructed at the level of nutrients and food components, which may limit its ability to capture the complexity of real-world food choices, particularly the heterogeneity of inflammatory effects within the same food category. The recently proposed Food Inflammation Index (FII) offers a potentially important advance in this regard. Building on the DII framework, the FII was developed using a weighted algorithm and validated with NHANES data to quantify inflammatory potential at the level of foods rather than nutrients alone, thereby enabling a more refined characterization of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory differences across foods and their subtypes15. It should be noted that the FII was originally developed and validated partly using NHANES data, whereas the present study also used NHANES as the analytic sample. Therefore, the current analysis should be interpreted as an application of the FII to evaluate mortality outcomes in individuals with CKM rather than as a fully independent external validation of the index. Methodological studies have shown16 that the FII can reveal inflammatory heterogeneity that is obscured by conventional food grouping approaches, thus providing a basis for more precise dietary assessment and more targeted nutritional guidance. Emerging application studies further suggest17 that the FII is linearly and positively associated with the risk of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), with overall discriminative performance comparable to that of the DII but superior performance in risk reclassification. In addition, NHANES-based evidence has indicated that individual food inflammatory scores derived from the FII are associated with a higher prevalence of sarcopenia. Together, these findings suggest that the FII, as a new-generation tool for assessing dietary inflammatory potential, has a sound scientific basis, methodological feasibility, and promising translational relevance.

The Empirical Dietary Inflammatory Pattern (EDIP) is another established food-based index that was empirically developed to predict circulating inflammatory biomarkers, including C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α receptor 218,19. However, EDIP mainly reflects broader dietary patterns or food-group combinations. In contrast, the FII assesses inflammatory potential at the level of individual foods and food components, allowing it to capture inflammatory heterogeneity within the same food group. This feature is particularly compatible with NHANES dietary recall data and may provide more actionable information for dietary risk assessment and nutritional intervention in individuals with CKM.

Nevertheless, research on the FII remains at a relatively early stage. Current evidence is still limited. Most studies have focused on methodological development or a few metabolism-related diseases. Research in CKM populations is still scarce, particularly studies evaluating hard clinical endpoints such as all-cause mortality and CVD mortality. Given the strong inflammatory underpinnings of CKM itself and the fact that diet is among the most important modifiable exposures, evaluating the relationship between dietary inflammation and CKM prognosis from the perspective of the FII may not only complement the limitations of the existing DII-based literature, but also provide new epidemiological evidence for risk stratification and precision nutrition interventions in this high-risk population. Therefore, using NHANES data, the present study aimed to investigate the associations of the FII with risks of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality among individuals with CKM. We hypothesized that higher FII levels would be associated with increased risks of both mortality outcomes in this population.

Methods

Data source and study participants

This study was based on data from the NHANES 1999–2018. NHANES is a nationally representative survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), using a complex, multistage probability sampling design to continuously monitor the health and nutritional status of the US population. The survey collects comprehensive information, including demographic characteristics, laboratory measurements, physical examination data, and health questionnaire responses. NHANES was approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and all adult participants provided written informed consent. Therefore, because this study used publicly available de-identified data, no additional ethical approval was required.

Among the 101,316 participants initially identified, we excluded 47,208 individuals aged < 20 years, 1,469 pregnant participants, 28,533 participants who could not be classified into CKM stages because of missing information required for CKM staging, and 1,495 participants with missing FII data. The final analytic sample comprised 22,611 participants (Figure S1).

Calculation of the FII

The FII comprised 39 dietary components and placed particular emphasis on primary food sources, including inflammation-related nutrients such as omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), namely α-linolenic acid (ALA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). The FII was calculated on the basis of 24-hour dietary recall data, with portion-size standardization applied to minimize recall bias. For mixed dishes or recipes, such as pizza, sandwiches, soups, and casseroles, food items were handled according to the food codes and standardized food composition information provided by the USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS). These items were not manually assigned to a single subjective food category by the investigators. Instead, their contribution to the FII was calculated based on the corresponding food composition and nutrient profiles recorded in the dietary database. Intake of each dietary component was converted into a z score using the NHANES median value as the reference. These standardized values were then weighted by integrating the Total Inflammation Scores (TISs), nutrient reference values (NRVs) for adults aged 31–50 years from the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020–2025, and global intake data. To reduce the influence of extreme values, the target FII score was calculated using a two-step approach.

Definition of CKM

CKM syndrome was defined according to the 2023 AHA Presidential Advisory1. In this statement, participants were classified into five mutually exclusive stages (stages 0–4) according to the highest-risk stage they meet. Stage 0 included individuals without overweight/obesity, metabolic risk factors, CKD, or CVD. Stage 1 included those with excess or dysfunctional adiposity. Stage 2 included individuals with metabolic risk factors and/or moderate- to high-risk CKD. Stage 3 included those with subclinical CVD, very high-risk CKD, or elevated predicted cardiovascular risk. Stage 4 included individuals with clinical CVD in the presence of CKM abnormalities. Consistent with previous NHANES-based studies, CKM stages were operationalized using available NHANES questionnaire data, physical examination measurements, and laboratory tests. Stage 1 was identified mainly based on excess or dysfunctional adiposity; stage 2 was identified based on metabolic risk factors and/or moderate- to high-risk CKD; stage 3 was identified based on subclinical CVD, very high-risk CKD, or elevated predicted cardiovascular risk; and stage 4 was identified based on clinical CVD in the presence of CKM abnormalities. Detailed operational definitions are provided in Table S2.

The primary outcome of this study was all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcome was CVD mortality. Mortality data were obtained from the publicly available NHANES-linked mortality files, which link NHANES participants from 1999 to 2018 to the National Death Index (NDI) using a probabilistic matching algorithm, thereby enabling reliable ascertainment of vital status and cause of death. Follow-up was calculated from the date of the participant’s first NHANES interview until the date of death or December 31, 2019, whichever came first. All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause. CVD mortality was determined according to the underlying cause of death based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), Tenth Revision (ICD-10), including deaths due to heart diseases (I00–I09, I11, I13, and I20–I51) and cerebrovascular diseases (I60–I69).

Covariates

A series of demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical characteristics were included as covariates in this study to evaluate the independent associations of the FII with all-cause and CVD mortality among individuals with CKM. These covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational level, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI), marital status, physical activity level, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, hypertension, and diabetes. Sex was categorized as male or female. Race/ethnicity was classified as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, and other race, including multiracial individuals. Educational level was grouped as below high school, high school, and above high school. PIR was categorized as ≤ 1.3, 1.3–4, and ≥ 4. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). Marital status was classified as married/living with a partner, widowed/divorced/separated, and never married. Physical activity level was categorized as moderate-intensity, vigorous-intensity, or no moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. Smoking status was classified as never, former, or current smoking. Alcohol consumption status was categorized as non-drinking, moderate drinking, and heavy drinking. Hypertension was defined as the presence of any of the following: self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension, mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg, and/or mean diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg. Diabetes was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: self-reported physician-diagnosed diabetes, current insulin use, current use of glucose-lowering medication, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dL, or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%. Prediabetes was defined, in accordance with previous literature, as meeting either of the following criteria: FPG of 100–125 mg/dL or HbA1c of 5.7%–6.4%.

Statistical analysis

To account for the complex sampling design of NHANES, all statistical analyses incorporated the appropriate sampling weights, stratification variables, and primary sampling units to generate nationally representative estimates. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation with 50 iterations.

First, participants were categorized into quartiles according to the weighted distribution of the FII, with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to estimate cumulative all-cause and CVD mortality across FII quartiles, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were then constructed to evaluate the associations of the FII with risks of all-cause and CVD mortality. Two progressively adjusted models were fitted. Model 1 was a partially adjusted model that included age, sex, and race/ethnicity. Model 2 was a fully adjusted model that additionally incorporated socioeconomic factors (educational level, marital status, and poverty-to-income ratio), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), BMI, and comorbid conditions (hypertension and diabetes). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and model discrimination was evaluated using the C-statistic.

To further examine the dose–response relationship between the FII and mortality risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were fitted with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the FII distribution. When a potential nonlinear association was identified, all possible inflection points were systematically tested, and the value with the best model likelihood was selected to identify a possible threshold effect. Stratified analyses were also performed to assess the robustness of the associations between the FII and mortality outcomes across different covariate strata and to explore potential effect modification. Interactions between subgroups were evaluated using likelihood ratio tests.

To assess the robustness of the findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) excluding participants who died within 2 years after the start of follow-up; (2) excluding participants with follow-up longer than 15 years; (3) refitting the fully adjusted model after removing hypertension and diabetes; and (4) re-imputing missing data using a random forest imputation method and repeating the primary analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.5). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Table 1, a total of 22,611 participants with CKM were included in this study, comprising 1,915 individuals with CKM stage 0, 4,062 with stage 1, 12,290 with stage 2, 1,421 with stage 3, and 2,923 with stage 4. Significant differences across FII quartiles were observed for multiple variables, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational level, PIR, BMI, marital status, physical activity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes. Overall, participants in the higher FII quartiles were more likely to be older; female; other Hispanic; educational level below high school; PIR ≤ 1.3; BMI > 30 kg/m²; widowed, divorced, or separated; moderate physical activity; no smoke; no drink and have hypertension or diabetes. In addition, we assessed the proportion of missing data for all covariates (Figure S2) and performed descriptive analyses after multiple imputation by chained equations (Table S3 and S4).

Associations of the FII with all-cause and CVD mortality

During a median follow-up of 9.3 years (interquartile range, 4.9–13.8 years), corresponding to 217,405 person-years, 3,745 deaths were documented, including 1,180 from CVD. Kaplan–Meier survival curves (Fig. 1) showed significant differences in cumulative mortality across FII quartiles, with participants in the highest quartile (Q4) having higher all-cause and CVD mortality than those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (log-rank test, P < 0.001).

Table 2 presents the associations of the FII with all-cause and CVD mortality in the Cox regression models. In the partially adjusted model, the highest FII quartile was significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause (HR, 1.626; 95% CI, 1.412–1.873) and CVD mortality (HR, 1.622; 95% CI, 1.276–2.062) (both P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model, the highest FII quartile remained significantly associated with all-cause (HR, 1.392; 95% CI, 1.218–1.592) and CVD mortality (HR, 1.337; 95% CI, 1.045–1.709; P = 0.019). Model performance was robust, with C-statistics exceeding 0.7 for all models, indicating good discrimination (Table S5).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses (Fig. 2) showed significant nonlinear associations of the FII with both all-cause and CVD mortality. Using FII = − 8.76 as the reference point, the hazard ratio (HR) changed only minimally below this level, whereas above this threshold, the HR increased progressively with increasing FII, with a steeper rise at higher exposure levels. These findings suggested that higher FII levels were associated with a marked increase in mortality risk. Overall, the results indicated a potential threshold effect of the FII on both all-cause and CVD mortality, with substantial differences in risk across exposure levels.

Subgroup analyses

As shown in Fig. 3, higher FII levels were significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause and CVD mortality, and the direction of these associations was generally consistent across most subgroups. No significant interactions were observed across subgroups (P for interaction > 0.01), suggesting that the positive association between the FII and mortality risk was broadly robust. Additional exploratory analyses stratified by CKM phase showed no significant interaction between FII and CKM phase for either all-cause mortality or CVD mortality (Table S6).

Sensitivity analyses

After excluding participants who died within 2 years of follow-up initiation or those with follow-up durations longer than 15 years, the associations of the FII with all-cause and CVD mortality remained significant (Table S7 and S8). In the fully adjusted model, these associations also remained significant after hypertension and diabetes were removed from the covariates (Table S9). In addition, to minimize the potential influence of covariate imputation, missing data were re-imputed using a random forest algorithm, and the results remained consistent with the primary analyses (Table S10 and S11).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the association between the FII and mortality risk among individuals with CKM using data from the NHANES cohort. A total of 22,611 participants with CKM were included. On the basis of Kaplan–Meier survival analyses and multivariable Cox regression models, we found that higher FII levels were significantly associated with increased risks of both all-cause and CVD mortality. Notably, these associations remained stable even after extensive adjustment for potential confounding factors. Further restricted cubic spline analyses revealed significant nonlinear relationships between the FII and both mortality outcomes, with mortality risk increasing progressively once the FII exceeded a specific threshold and rising more steeply at higher levels. Subgroup analyses showed that this positive association was generally consistent across different populations, with no clear evidence of interaction. Multiple sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of these findings. Overall, our results suggested that the FII may serve as a clinically relevant risk-stratification marker in the CKM population and may help identify individuals at high risk of all-cause and CVD mortality.

Mechanistically, an elevated FII may accelerate several key pathological processes across the CKM continuum by promoting chronic low-grade systemic inflammation21,22. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown13,23 that pro-inflammatory dietary patterns are significantly associated with increased risks of CVD incidence and mortality. Long-term consumption of a pro-inflammatory diet may alter gut microbial composition, impair intestinal barrier function, and trigger persistent inflammatory responses24,25. At the same time, inflammation may further aggravate oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, lipid metabolic abnormalities, and tissue fibrosis, thereby promoting the progression of atherosclerosis, structural kidney injury, and cardiac remodeling26–28. In individuals with CKM, these processes are not isolated; rather, they interact with and amplify one another. Metabolic abnormalities may intensify inflammatory signaling, declining kidney function may further promote uremic toxin accumulation and amplify the inflammatory burden, and impaired cardiovascular function may exacerbate systemic pathophysiological imbalance by reducing tissue perfusion and diminishing multiorgan compensatory reserve29. In this context, the dietary inflammatory exposure represented by a higher FII may be more readily translated into increased risks of all-cause and CVD mortality in this multisystem vulnerable population.

Our restricted cubic spline analyses further suggest that the associations of the FII with all-cause and CVD mortality are not simply linear, but instead are characterized by significant nonlinearity and a potential threshold effect. Using FII = − 8.76 as the reference point, the change in risk was relatively limited when the FII remained below this level. However, once the FII exceeded this threshold, mortality risk increased continuously, with a steeper rise at higher exposure levels. These findings suggest that the prognostic impact of dietary inflammatory burden in the CKM population may follow a pattern of cumulative accumulation followed by amplification. At lower levels of inflammatory burden, compensatory mechanisms may still be sufficient to maintain relative physiological stability. Once exposure exceeds a critical threshold, however, the interactions among inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and organ injury may become progressively intensified, ultimately leading to a rapid increase in mortality risk. This finding not only complements the results derived from conventional linear models, but also suggests that, in clinical practice, the FII may not function merely as a linear indicator in which “higher is worse.” Rather, it may contain critical exposure ranges with particular value for risk stratification.

It is noteworthy that the subgroup analyses in this study showed that the positive associations of the FII with all-cause and CVD mortality were directionally consistent across most subgroups, with no significant interactions observed. This suggests that the observed associations are relatively stable and broadly generalizable. Multiple sensitivity analyses further supported the robustness of the primary findings. Exclusion of participants who died within the first 2 years of follow-up, exclusion of those with follow-up durations longer than 15 years, and removal of hypertension and diabetes from the fully adjusted model did not materially alter the associations between the FII and mortality outcomes. These findings indicate that the observed associations were unlikely to be driven primarily by early reverse causation, bias related to excessively long follow-up, specific comorbidities, or differences in the imputation approach, but instead more likely reflect a relatively stable underlying relationship. Consistent with this interpretation, recent NHANES-based studies have shown that the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) is positively associated with CKM syndrome, and that elevated SII and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) are also associated with increased risks of all-cause and CVD mortality among individuals with CKM30. These data provide further support for our findings from the perspective of systemic inflammatory biomarkers.

Overall, our findings are consistent with the existing literature on dietary inflammatory burden and adverse outcomes in chronic disease. Although studies directly evaluating the association between the FII and mortality outcomes in CKM remain limited, a substantial body of evidence based on the DII has consistently shown that pro-inflammatory diets are closely associated with higher risks of all-cause and CVD mortality. García-Arellano et al., based on the SUN and PREDIMED cohorts, reported that higher DII scores were significantly associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality31. Similar findings were subsequently reported by Shivappa et al. and Park et al. in different populations32,33. Hodge et al. further observed in the Melbourne Collaborative Cohort Study that the DII was positively associated with both all-cause and CVD mortality34. Likewise, a study by Okada et al. in a Japanese population confirmed that higher DII scores were associated with increased risks of all-cause and CVD mortality35. More recent studies have also shown that, among individuals with CKD and those with metabolic syndrome, higher DII scores remain independently associated with increased risks of all-cause or CVD mortality36–38. Taken together, although most previous studies have used the DII rather than the FII, the overall direction of evidence is highly consistent with our findings, collectively supporting the notion that a higher dietary inflammatory burden may be associated with poorer long-term prognosis.

Although the DII is currently one of the most widely used tools for assessing dietary inflammatory potential and offers substantial methodological maturity and comparability, the FII may have several advantages in real-world research settings. The DII was derived from the literature and incorporates 45 nutrients, food components, or dietary parameters. Its associations with inflammatory biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) have been validated in multiple studies, which has supported its broad application in epidemiological research15,39–41. By contrast, the FII was developed as an extension of the DII framework, shifting the focus from dietary parameters to a food-centered hierarchy based on whole foods. This design more closely reflects real-world dietary structure and may facilitate the translation of inflammatory potential assessment into more actionable food choices and dietary recommendations16. At the same time, the FII was proposed in part to address a limitation of the traditional DII framework, namely its limited ability to capture variation in inflammatory effects within the same food group. The original study showed that the FII could identify substantial within-group heterogeneity across foods. In other words, even within dietary patterns generally regarded as healthy, specific foods may contribute differently to inflammation, a distinction that is often not intuitively captured by the conventional DII42,43. In addition, by standardizing and classifying foods on the basis of food composition data44,45, the FII may be more readily translated into concrete dietary recommendations, such as which foods should be prioritized and which should be limited46,47. Accordingly, the FII may offer greater interpretability and practical utility in both clinical communication and public health guidance. Notably, direct comparative studies have also suggested that the discriminatory performance of the FII is at least comparable to that of the DII in certain disease settings. In an NHANES-based study of MASLD, the area under the curve (AUC) for the FII was broadly similar to that of the DII, whereas the FII performed better in terms of net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement, suggesting that it may be more sensitive in capturing dietary inflammatory exposure17.

Several limitations of this study should also be acknowledged. First, this was an observational analysis, and although multiple potential confounders were adjusted for, residual confounding and reverse causation cannot be fully excluded; therefore, causal inferences should be made with caution. Second, the FII was calculated from baseline dietary information and could not capture temporal changes in dietary patterns or the cumulative burden of inflammatory exposure over time. Third, dietary assessment in NHANES relies primarily on 24-hour dietary recall, which is subject to recall bias and may not adequately represent habitual intake. Fourth, despite multiple sensitivity analyses, the CKM population remains highly heterogeneous, and the most appropriate interpretation of the FII, as well as its optimal clinical thresholds, may differ across disease stages and comorbidity profiles. Finally, as a relatively new food-based inflammatory metric, the FII has undergone limited external validation across populations with different dietary cultures and background characteristics. In addition, the NRVs used in the FII calculation were based on recommendations for adults aged 31–50 years, following the original FII framework. Because our study population included adults aged ≥ 20 years, including older adults, this single age-band reference may not fully reflect age-specific nutritional requirements and may have introduced some measurement imprecision. Additional prospective studies are therefore needed to support its broader application.

Overall, our findings suggest that the FII may serve as a promising complementary tool for risk stratification in individuals with CKM. Because patients with CKM often present with clustered risk factors and require long-term, integrated, and stratified management, the identification of simple, stable, and modifiable risk markers is of considerable practical importance. Assessment of dietary inflammatory burden may help identify individuals at elevated mortality risk and support more targeted dietary counseling. At the population level, reducing pro-inflammatory dietary exposure and promoting anti-inflammatory dietary patterns may complement existing strategies for the prevention and management of CKM. Compared with some biomarkers that are costly or less accessible in routine care, the FII may offer greater potential for clinical implementation and public health application. Further studies are still required to validate its incremental predictive value and to determine optimal risk thresholds. Nevertheless, our findings provide preliminary support for incorporating dietary inflammatory control into integrated CKM management and risk reduction strategies.

Conclusion

In this nationally representative NHANES cohort, higher FII levels were significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause and CVD mortality among individuals with CKM. These associations remained robust after multivariable adjustment, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses, and a significant nonlinear dose–response relationship was also observed. Collectively, these findings suggest that the FII may serve as a valuable and potentially modifiable marker for risk stratification and prognostic assessment in the CKM population. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to clarify the underlying mechanisms.
