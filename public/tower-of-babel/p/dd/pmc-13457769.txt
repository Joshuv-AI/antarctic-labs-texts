Respiratory motion-corrected quiescent period gating magnetic resonance attenuation correction (Q.MRAC) for enhancing quantitative accuracy in hepatic [18F]FDG PET/MRI: a dual-domain motion compensation study
Authors: Hua Lin, Yu-Mei Pu, Li Li, Ming-Gang Su, Chun-Chao Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory motion-induced misalignment between positron emission tomography (PET) and magnetic resonance attenuation maps remains a critical challenge in abdominal PET/magnetic resonance imaging (PET/MRI) quantification. This study evaluated the performance of a novel dual-domain motion correction strategy combining emission-domain [quiescent phase gating PET acquisition (Q.Static)] and attenuation-domain [quiescent period gating magnetic resonance attenuation correction (Q.MRAC)] approaches in enhancing standardized uptake value (SUV) accuracy in hepatic fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) imaging.MethodsFifty patients underwent [18F]FDG PET/MRI with two respiratory motion correction protocols: dual-domain correction (Q.Static + Q.MRAC) and conventional MRAC [conventional static PET acquisition (Static) + conventional magnetic resonance attenuation correction (C.MRAC)]. Three 3-cm-diameter spherical volumes of interest (VOIs) were positioned in the right hepatic lobe, including the upper lobe (VOI1), middle lobe (VOI2), and lower lobe (VOI3) to evaluate SUV quantification, spatial alignment of attenuation maps against end-expiratory T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), and motion correction efficacy. Analysis of variance was applied for comparisons of the mean SUV (SUVmean) across VOIs and protocols, the Kruskal-Wallis test to compare the differences in SUVmean, paired t-tests to analyze alignment metrics, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to measure interobserver consistency.ResultsVOI2 demonstrated minimal SUV variability [coefficient of variation (CV) =2.29–2.55%], validating its use as a reference region as per the relevant guidelines. Respiratory motion caused consistent SUV underestimation: VOI1 underestimation relative to VOI2 was 16.4% for Q.Static + Q.MRAC and 26.3% for Static + C.MRAC, while the maximum underestimation rate for VOI3 was 11.4% for Static + C.MRAC. Q.Static + Q.MRAC reduced VOI1 underestimation by 38.9% as compared to Static + C.MRAC (P=0.006). Q.MRAC significantly improved anatomical alignment as compared to C.MRAC (P<0.001), reducing mismatch ratio by 48.7% and the mismatch area by 52.4% and 33.6% in the coronal and axial views, respectively. Eight percent of Q.MRAC acquisitions failed due to irregular breathing.ConclusionsDual-domain motion correction (Q.Static + Q.MRAC) significantly improves SUV quantification accuracy in the upper lobe of right liver. These findings establish a foundation for achieving greater accuracy for tumor assessment and metabolic imaging in clinical practice through the use of [18F]FDG PET/MRI.Keywords: Positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging (PET/MRI), attenuation correction, respiratory motion, standardized uptake value quantification (SUV quantification), hepatic imaging

Introduction

The integration of positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging (PET/MRI) systems represents a transformative advancement in medical imaging, combining the metabolic specificity of PET with the superior soft-tissue contrast and multiparametric capabilities of MRI (1). In oncology, the standardized uptake value (SUV) is a cornerstone quantitative parameter for lesion characterization and treatment response assessment. Beyond traditional oncological applications, accurate hepatic fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) quantification is being increasingly recognized as vital for evaluating metabolic disorders such as obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (2-4). However, the respiratory motion-induced spatial mismatch between PET data and magnetic resonance attenuation correction (MRAC) maps poses a critical technical bottleneck, particularly in thoracoabdominal imaging (5-7).

Current MRAC protocols typically include Dixon or T1-weighted sequences, such as liver acquisition with volume acceleration-FLEX (LAVA-FLEX), to generate segmented attenuation maps by classifying tissues into air, lung, fat, and soft tissue (8-10). Although static MRAC can achieve computed tomography attenuation correction (CTAC)-equivalent accuracy in nonmoving regions (11), diaphragmatic motion during free breathing causes spatial misalignment in the upper abdomen, leading to SUV errors exceeding 25% in hepatic lesions (12-15). Existing mitigation strategies, such as data-driven respiratory gating and nonrigid registration, exhibit limited ability to balance signal-to-noise ratio with computational complexity (5,16,17). Recently, the use of free-breathing motion-corrected magnetic resonance elastography for abdominal applications has been explored (18), and respiratory motion compensation remains an active research frontier.

Recent technical advances integrated into the SIGNA PET/MRI system (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA) include two respiratory compensation tools: emission-domain motion correction [quiescent phase gating PET acquisition (Q.Static)] and attenuation-domain motion correction [quiescent period gating magnetic resonance attenuation correction (Q.MRAC)]. Q.Static employs sinogram-based motion correction synchronized with respiratory frequency, trigger windows, and temporal resolution to enhance photon collection efficiency (11,19). Q.MRAC improves MRAC map fidelity by parameterizing the respiratory cycle—selecting a trigger point to define the static phase onset and an acquisition window to maximize anatomical consistency in LAVA-FLEX sequences, approximating breath-hold CTAC quality (19,20). Despite these technical capabilities, these single-domain corrections are inherently limited when applied in isolation. Specifically, Q.Static alone cannot fully resolve the misregistration between PET emission data and a distorted MRAC map (persistent attenuation error), while Q.MRAC alone cannot recover the signal loss caused by motion blur in the emission data (residual blurring error). Therefore, we propose a synergistic dual-domain approach (Q.Static + Q.MRAC) for achieving optimal SUV accuracy.

Despite this theoretical rationale for dual-domain correction, two challenges remain. First, systematic validation of Q.MRAC’s intrinsic anatomical accuracy is lacking, necessitating the use of reference standards such as end-expiratory T2-weighted imaging (T2WI). Second, the synergistic effect of combining Q.Static with Q.MRAC has not been characterized, particularly regarding their combined impact on SUV quantification and image fusion. Given the liver’s homogeneous tracer uptake on [18F]FDG PET (21), we aimed to systematically evaluate different attenuation correction methods by analyzing SUV metrics and anatomical alignment. Using T2WI-guided volume of interest (VOI) analysis, we isolated MRAC segmentation effects to complete a systematic assessment of Q.MRAC’s ability to enhance attenuation correction accuracy in clinical applications. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0303/rc).

Methods

Participants

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (No. 202404). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis. In total, 50 patients (34 males and 16 females; mean age 53.7±3.1 years) who underwent [18F]FDG PET/MRI at West China Hospital of Sichuan University from April 2024 to April 2025 were included in the study, while those with liver pathologies (hepatitis, cirrhosis, fatty liver, liver tumors, or multiple cysts) that could confound SUV quantification were excluded. Factors including glucose level (22), body mass index (BMI), and liver fat content (LFC) (4) were assessed prescan. LFC was quantified from Dixon images (LAVA-FLEX) to exclude metabolic confounders.

Data acquisition

After a 6-hour fast, patients received intravenous [18F]FDG (3.7 MBq/kg). PET/MRI was performed 108.6±9.0 minutes postinjection with a 3.0T PET/MR system (SIGNA, GE HealthCare). Thoracoabdominal imaging was conducted with a phased-array coil with respiratory bellows placed at the region of maximal motion. Two sequential PET datasets (6.0 minutes per bed) were acquired: (I) Q.Static + Q.MRAC (dual-domain motion correction) and (II) Conventional static PET acquisition (Static) + conventional magnetic resonance attenuation correction (C.MRAC; without motion correction).

Respiratory gating parameters included a trigger point at 30% of the breathing cycle, an acquisition window of 50%, and no breath-hold. The MRI sequences included LAVA-FLEX [time to echo/time to repetition (TE/TR) =1.7/4 ms, slice thickness =5.2 mm, field of view (FOV) =500 mm × 375 mm, matrix =256×128], axial T2WI (TE/TR =93.3/6,000 ms, slice thickness =6.0 mm, FOV =440 mm × 352 mm, matrix =320×256), and coronal T2WI (TE/TR =80.5/2,625 ms, slice thickness =5.0 mm, FOV =480 mm × 480 mm, matrix =288×288).

Image reconstruction

PET data were reconstructed with four distinct protocols to isolate correction effects: (I) Q.Static + Q.MRAC (dual-domain correction); (II) Q.Static + C.MRAC (emission-domain correction only); (III) Static + Q.MRAC (attenuation-domain correction only); and (IV) Static + C.MRAC (no correction).

All reconstructions were performed with identical parameters: time of flight, point-spread function correction, scatter correction, and ordered subsets expectation maximum (OSEM) algorithm with 2 iterations per 28 subsets.

Image processing

PET exhibits the longest effective photon acquisition time at end expiration (23), and end-expiratory T2WI more accurately reflects hepatic anatomy (24); thus, we selected end-expiratory T2WI as the anatomical reference for VOI delineation. Three spherical VOIs (3 cm in diameter) were positioned within the right hepatic lobe along the coronal plane and divided into three segments according to the diaphragmatic dome level: VOI1 (centered in the upper segment), VOI2 (centered in the middle segment), and VOI3 (centered in the lower segment). The VOI locations were copied to PET, T2WI, and MRAC maps for fusion analysis (Figure 1). On the same coronal slice, the distance from the VOI2 center to the diaphragmatic dome on T2WI (dL) and to the liver dome on the MRAC map (d) was measured, and the mismatch rate (µ=|dL−d|/dL) between the MRAC and T2WI contours was calculated. Additionally, the mismatched area difference between MRAC maps and T2WI contours was quantified on coronal and axial planes centered at VOI1, referred to as ADcor (coronal anatomical mismatch area) and ADax (axial anatomical mismatch area), respectively. All measurements were performed independently by two technicians, with the averages being used for analysis.

Qualitative assessment of MRAC map accuracy

Two nuclear medicine technologists independently rated the anatomical fidelity of MRAC map-T2WI fusion images on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating superior spatial alignment. as follows: 5= perfect alignment, characterized by near-perfect liver/lung demarcation and absence of PET distortion; 4= good alignment with minor anatomical mismatches and minimal impact on PET images; 3= moderate alignment, with localized errors leading to limited PET distortion; 2= poor alignment, featuring extensive anatomical mismatch and notable PET distortion; and 1= failed alignment, marked by critical anatomical misregistration and severe PET distortion.

Results

A total of 50 patients (34 males and 16 females; mean age 53.7±3.1 years; age range 13–82 years) were included. The cohort had a mean BMI of 21.3±3.1 kg/m2, a mean serum glucose level of 5.1±0.6 mmol/L, and a mean LFC of 4.2%±1.8%. Four patients were excluded due to irregular breathing and failure to generate attenuation correction maps.

Figure 2 shows the mean SUVs and CVs of VOI1–3 under four scanning protocols. In the comparison of four sets of scanning protocols, only the Q.Static + Q.MRAC protocol yielded a significantly higher SUVmean than Static + C.MRAC in VOI1 (Q.Static + Q.MRAC: 1.68±0.41 vs. Static + C.MRAC: 1.43±0.38 g/mL). VOI2 produced the lowest SUV variability across all protocols (CV =2.29–2.55%), confirming its suitability as a reference region and aligning with the relevant guidelines (25,26).

Q.MRAC significantly outperformed C.MRAC in anatomical alignment (Figure 4). Discussion

This study demonstrated that integrated dual-domain respiratory motion correction (Q.Static + Q.MRAC) significantly improves the accuracy of hepatic SUV quantification in PET/MRI, with pronounced benefits in the superior, motion-prone regions. Consistent with European Association of Nuclear Medicine/Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging guidelines that designate the liver as a homogeneous reference for background metabolic activity (25,26), the mid-hepatic region (VOI2) showed exceptional stability across all protocols (CV =2.29–2.55%), confirming its reliability as an internal reference. Importantly, we identified a spatially heterogeneous pattern of motion artifacts: VOI1 (upper hepatic region) exhibited an SUV underestimation of ≥16.4% relative to VOI2, whereas VOI3 (lower hepatic region) produced no protocol-dependent differences (P>0.05). This gradient confirms region-specific vulnerability to respiratory motion—a biomechanical pattern previously noted (24) but not quantitatively linked to attenuation correction accuracy until now.

The key innovation of this work lies in clarifying the complementary, nonredundant mechanisms of dual-domain correction through quantitative validation. Our results demonstrate that neither Q.Static nor Q.MRAC alone is sufficient to fully resolve the SUV bias observed in conventional Static + C.MRAC (−0.53 g/mL). Specifically, Q.Static addressed emission-domain blurring but failed to correct attenuation map misregistration, resulting in a persistent SUV underestimation of −0.41 g/mL. Q.Static alone failed to restore SUV accuracy in VOI1, reflecting previous studies’ oversight of normal liver parenchyma as a quantitative metric (19,27,28). Conversely, Q.MRAC corrected the anatomical misalignment but failed to fully recover the signal loss caused by motion blur in the emission data, leaving a residual bias of −0.47 g/mL. In contrast, the synergistic application of both techniques simultaneously restored photon statistics in the emission domain by reducing respiratory blurring and minimized attenuation-map misregistration, achieving a significantly lower bias of −0.34 g/mL. This represents a 38.9% reduction in SUV underestimation compared to the conventional Static + C.MRAC approach (P=0.006). This dual-pathway mechanism supports the superiority of the combined method, which was further corroborated by anatomical validation: Q.MRAC achieved a 52.4% reduction on the coronal mismatch area and a 33.6% reduction in the axial mismatch area as compared to C.MRAC. Furthermore, quantitative validation using end-expiratory T2WI as the anatomical reference confirmed Q.MRAC’s superior spatial alignment—with a 48.7% lower mismatch ratio—and excellent interobserver consistency (ICC =0.92), which enabled artifact-free PET-MR fusion.

The absence of protocol effects in VOI3 is likely due to complex multidirectional motion and partial volume effects at the inferior hepatic margin, which exceed the resolution of current gating models (24,29,30). This finding also highlights a major constraint of Q.MRAC: its dependence on regular breathing patterns (8% exclusion rate in this study). In contrast, conventional MRAC is more robust to respiratory variation but at the cost of systematic SUV underestimation in mobile regions. Clinically, this improvement in precision is critical, as SUV errors may lead to misinterpretation of treatment response in hepatic metastases. Thus, reliable quantification in motion-affected regions enhances longitudinal therapy assessment and reduces false-negative interpretations.

This study involved several limitations that should be addressed. First, the moderate cohort size (N=50) may constrain the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, variability in the tracer uptake time (108.6±9.0 minutes) could have introduced uncontrolled variance into the SUV measurements. Third, the performance of Q.MRAC relies on respiratory screening, which may restrict its application in patients with irregular breathing patterns. Finally, this study was conducted on a single PET/MRI platform (SIGNA), but the performance of Q.Static and Q.MRAC may differ across systems from other vendors or with different technical architectures.

Future work should focus on (I) developing deep learning-based adaptive gating frameworks to accommodate irregular breathing; (II) refining LAVA-FLEX parameters to improve motion robustness; and (III) prospectively validating this approach in oncology cohorts for therapy-response assessment. By simultaneously addressing emission-domain blur and attenuation-domain mismatch, this dual-domain strategy establishes a more reliable foundation for reproducible quantitative PET/MRI. This study addresses an important deficiency in the literature by providing quantitative evidence of synergistic respiratory motion correction and advances precision oncology toward motion-agnostic quantification standards with clear clinical potential.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that dual-domain respiratory motion correction (Q.Static + Q.MRAC) significantly enhances quantitative accuracy in hepatic [18F]FDG PET/MRI. The findings support the synergistic benefit of simultaneous emission and attenuation motion correction for reliable SUV quantification, which is critical for longitudinal tumor assessment and quantitative hepatic metabolism assessment in patients with hepatological and metabolic disorders.
