Visuospatial Neglect Caused by Microbleeds in a Patient With Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy: A Case Report
Authors: Sodam Kim, Youngkook Kim, Sook Joung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case report describes an 82-year-old man with a right parasagittal motor cortex intracerebral hemorrhage who presented with visuospatial neglect disproportionate to the primary lesion. Quantitative diffusion tensor imaging analysis revealed white matter damage suggestive of underlying cerebral amyloid angiopathy. These findings highlight the importance of considering underlying conditions such as cerebral amyloid angiopathy and premorbid cognitive function when diagnosing visuospatial neglect in elderly stroke patients. Furthermore, assessing the microstructural integrity of non-lesioned white matter tracts may help identify causes of post-stroke cognitive impairment or unexplained symptoms and provide insight into premorbid cognitive function.

INTRODUCTION

Visuospatial neglect is a common neurologic deficit following right parietal stroke; however, it can also arise from other brain pathologies, including cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) [1]. CAA is a cerebrovascular disease characterized by the deposition of amyloid-β peptides in the walls of cortical and leptomeningeal vessels [2]. This deposition leads to vascular remodeling and compromised vessel integrity, frequently resulting in microbleeds or lobar intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) in elderly patients [3]. CAA is associated with an increased risk of age-related cognitive decline and can cause various cognitive impairments, including deficits in executive function, memory, language, and visuospatial perception [4]. This case report describes a patient with right frontal ICH who presented with visuospatial neglect that could not be fully explained by the primary hemorrhage but was attributed to underlying CAA.

CASE DESCRIPTION

An 82-year-old, right-handed man with a history of atrial fibrillation (AF) (on rivaroxaban 10 mg daily) presented to the emergency department with acute-onset left-sided weakness, predominantly affecting the lower extremity. At presentation, his blood pressure was 167/103 mmHg. Neurological examination revealed significant distal motor deficits; the Medical Research Council grades for the left ankle dorsiflexors and plantar flexors were 1/5, while proximal muscle strength in the hip and knee flexors and extensors remained relatively preserved at 4/5. Initial brain computed tomography showed a 13 mm-sized cortical hemorrhage in the right parasagittal area of the primary motor cortex (Fig. 1).

On the day of admission, the patient scored 30/30 on the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). However, the patient’s caregiver raised concerns about a potential cognitive decline compared to the premorbid condition. Subsequently, comprehensive neurocognitive function assessments were also conducted one week after onset. The assessments included visuospatial function tests such as the daisy drawing test, line-bisection test, Rey Complex Figure Test, and star cancellation test. The results revealed left visuospatial neglect, particularly with an object-centered pattern. Additional impairments in executive function, auditory memory, attention, and working memory were observed, as assessed by the Trail Making Test, the Seoul Verbal Learning Test, and the forward and backward digit span test, respectively. The daisy drawing test showed omission of the left upper portion of the flower (Fig. 2A). The line bisection test indicated a rightward deviation, with three out of eight lines deviating more than 10% (Fig. 2B). On the Rey Complex Figure Test, the patient reproduced only the right portion of the figure from his visual memory, rotating it counterclockwise (Fig. 2C). The star cancellation test revealed that the patient omitted one star on the left and one on the right, out of 56 stars.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed to evaluate the discrepancy between the location of the primary hemorrhagic lesion and the observed visuospatial neglect. The susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) revealed multiple microbleeds in the right parietal and temporal lobes, suggesting CAA (Fig. 3).

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) was performed to evaluate the clinical-anatomical correlation between visuospatial neglect and white matter tracts involved in visuospatial processing, including the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), associated with spatial perception, and the inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), associated with object perception.

DTI acquisition

DTI was performed using a 3.0-T magnetic resonance imager (MAGNETOM® Skyra; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 6-channel head coil. The data were acquired in the form of single-shot spin-echo echo-planar images, with axial slices covering the whole brain across 65 interleaved slices with a thickness of 2.0 mm (no gap; repetition time/echo time = 8,900/41 ms; field of view = 256 × 256 mm2; matrix = 128 × 128; voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 mm3 [isotropic], number of excitations = 1). Diffusion-sensitizing gradients were applied in 30 noncollinear directions with a b-value of 1,000 ms/mm2. The images with b = 0 were scanned before acquiring diffusion-weighted images, and 31 volumes were acquired.

DTI analysis

We extracted the fractional anisotropy (FA) values in the tracts of interest, including the SLF, ILF, and corticospinal tract (CST), by applying standardized templates (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/docs/diffusion/xtract.html.) [5]. White matter voxel-wise statistics of the FA data were conducted using Tract-Based Spatial Statistics [6]. We applied nonlinear registration of all FA images into a 1-mm3 MNI152 standard space. The mean FA was thinned to generate a mean FA skeleton. The tracts of interest were matched to the template of atlases for the extraction of each healthy control and the case patient’s FA values. We referred to the XTRACT Atlas derived from the Human Connectome Project.

The distribution of microbleeds in the right parieto-temporal region overlapped with the SLF and ILF, which are known to be involved in visuospatial processing (Fig. 3). Furthermore, the FA values of the bilateral SLF and ILF were lower than those of ten age-matched healthy controls, suggesting compromised microstructural integrity within these tracts (Fig. 4).

After 3 weeks of comprehensive rehabilitation, including computerized cognitive therapy, transfer to a rehabilitation hospital was planned. However, due to a COVID-19 infection, the patient was discharged home instead. At discharge, overall functional improvement was observed; the Berg Balance Scale score increased from 35 to 47 (out of 56), and the Korean Modified Barthel Index improved from 64 to 84. Despite these gains, the rightward deviation on the line bisection test showed no significant improvement.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This case report was reviewed and exempted from review by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yeouido St. Mary’s Hospital, College of Medicine, The Catholic University of Korea (IRB No. SC23ZISE0062). The requirement for informed consent was waived as this study was a retrospective review of existing medical records and posed no more than minimal risk to the patient.

DISCUSSION

This case report demonstrates a hemorrhagic stroke presenting with left visuospatial neglect, which is attributed principally to the underlying burden of CAA rather than the direct effects of the acute primary hemorrhagic lesion. CAA-related microbleeds in the temporal and parietal lobes, which affect the visual pathways, may underlie the patient’s visuospatial neglect. This case highlights the clinical importance of considering premorbid cognitive function and underlying conditions, such as CAA, when diagnosing visuospatial neglect in elderly stroke patients.

CAA is a cerebrovascular disorder characterized by amyloid-β peptide deposition within the walls of cortical and leptomeningeal vessels [2]. Although a definitive diagnosis of CAA requires histopathological confirmation, the Boston criteria enable the clinical diagnosis of probable or possible CAA based on neuroimaging findings and the exclusion of alternative causes [7]. In the present case, the patient fulfilled the criteria for probable CAA according to the Boston criteria version 2.0. Specifically, he was 82 years old, fulfilling the age criterion of 55 years or older. He presented with a spontaneous lobar intracerebral hemorrhage and multiple cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) on susceptibility-weighted imaging. These CMBs were strictly confined to the lobar regions, predominantly involving the right parietal and temporal lobes, without involvement of deep structures such as the basal ganglia, thalamus, or brainstem. No prominent cortical superficial siderosis was observed. Although the patient was taking rivaroxaban for AF, his international normalized ratio was 1.07 at presentation; therefore, he did not meet the criterion for excessive anticoagulation therapy, defined as warfarin therapy with an international normalized ratio > 3.0, as an alternative cause of hemorrhage [7].

The etiology of the CMBs in this patient warrants careful differentiation, as AF and rivaroxaban use may also contribute to microvascular bleeding. Although AF has been associated with CMBs [8], and oral anticoagulant exposure may influence overall CMB burden, non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) generally demonstrate a lower CMB burden than vitamin K antagonists [9]. In the present case, the strictly lobar distribution of the CMBs and the absence of hemorrhagic lesions in deep structures, including the basal ganglia, thalamus, and brainstem, strongly supported probable CAA according to the modified Boston criteria. Therefore, although AF and rivaroxaban may have contributed to the development or clinical manifestation of cerebral microbleeding, CAA was considered the most likely underlying pathology responsible for the characteristic hemorrhagic pattern observed in this patient.

Previous studies have demonstrated that, beyond its association with lobar ICH and Alzheimer’s disease, CAA is independently associated with age-related cognitive decline involving executive function, memory, language, attention, and visuospatial perception [41011]. Notably, visual perceptual impairment can manifest even in the absence of other significant cognitive deficits [12]. While CAA-related microvascular lesions commonly predominate in the occipital lobes, previous studies in non-demented patients have found no clear association between focal occipital hemorrhages and specific visual perceptual impairments [1112]. Therefore, the clinico-anatomical correlation suggested that the patient’s visuospatial neglect was associated with the concentrated burden of CAA-related microvascular lesions within the right temporoparietal network.

Nevertheless, alternative mechanisms related to the acute hemorrhage itself should be considered. Perihematomal edema, transient mass effect, and diaschisis can contribute to cognitive dysfunction following focal hemorrhagic stroke. However, several factors suggest that these mechanisms alone do not fully account for the severity and persistence of visuospatial neglect in this patient. The primary hemorrhage was relatively small and localized within the parasagittal primary motor cortex—a region not typically associated with severe visuospatial neglect [1314]. In addition, the neglect symptoms persisted despite significant motor and functional recovery during rehabilitation. These observations suggest that the acute ICH may have unmasked or exacerbated a pre-existing, subclinical dysfunction arising from CAA-associated network disruption.

Despite bilateral FA reductions, the patient presented with predominantly left-sided visuospatial neglect, a discrepancy that likely reflects the right hemispheric dominance of visuospatial attention. In the influential model of spatial attention, the right hemisphere mediates attention toward both hemispheres, whereas the left hemisphere predominantly directs attention toward the contralateral right hemispace [1516]. Consequently, right hemispheric network disruption typically results in more severe and persistent neglect than left-sided involvement. In the present case, although bilateral abnormalities were observed, the structural burden was topographically asymmetric. Quantitative DTI analysis revealed greater FA reductions in the right SLF and ILF than in the corresponding left-sided tracts, while SWI demonstrated more extensive overlap of cerebral microbleeds with the right SLF and ILF regions. This asymmetric burden of right hemispheric tract involvement may have exceeded a critical threshold, thereby manifesting clinically as unilateral left visuospatial neglect.

Another notable finding was the discrepancy between the patient’s initial MMSE score and the subsequent neuropsychological findings. Although the patient scored 30/30 on the MMSE at admission, a comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation later revealed impairments in visuospatial function, executive function, attention, and working memory. In addition, the caregiver reported cognitive decline compared with the premorbid state. This discrepancy may reflect the limited sensitivity of the MMSE for detecting right hemispheric dysfunction and visuospatial neglect, as the MMSE primarily emphasizes language and orientation-based tasks rather than higher-order visuospatial processing. Therefore, the acute hemorrhage may have unmasked or exacerbated a previously subclinical cognitive impairment associated with underlying CAA-related small vessel disease.

This case also has several limitations. First, the DTI analysis was based on comparison with a relatively small healthy control group (n = 10), which may limit generalizability. Second, tract-specific FA analysis alone cannot establish direct causality between microstructural abnormalities and neglect symptoms. Finally, because a premorbid neurocognitive assessment was unavailable, the exact extent of pre-existing cognitive impairment could not be definitively determined.

Despite these limitations, this case demonstrates how multimodal neuroimaging, including SWI and quantitative DTI analysis, may help identify underlying network-level pathology when clinical symptoms are disproportionate to the primary stroke lesion. Evaluating the microstructural integrity of non-lesional white matter tracts may provide additional insight into unexplained cognitive deficits and premorbid brain vulnerability in elderly stroke patients with underlying CAA.
