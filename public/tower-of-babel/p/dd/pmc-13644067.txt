A machine learning-driven multitask framework for antibiotic resistance prediction in Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Enterobacter cloacae bloodstream infections among hematological malignancy patients
Authors: Yuqing Cui, Xiaomeng Feng, Ruonan Shao, Lining Zhang, Xueyuan Li, Qingsong Lin, Jieru Wang, Li Liu, Ling Pan, Sisi Zhen, Yuping Fan, Tingting Zhang, Yingchang Mi, Zhijian Xiao, Erlie Jiang, Mingzhe Han, Jianxiang Wang, Xin Chen, Sizhou Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInappropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy (IEAT) significantly increases mortality in patients with resistant Gram-negative bacteremia. We developed a multitask machine learning framework to predict carbapenem resistance (CR), β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations resistance (BL/BLI-R), and third-/fourth-generation cephalosporins resistance (3GC/4GC-R) in HM patients with monomicrobial BSIs caused specifically by Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, or Enterobacter cloacae.MethodsUsing retrospective data from 1,353 HM patients with three specific Enterobacterales BSIs (2017–2023), we trained support vector machines, eXtreme Gradient Boosting, and logistic regression models through 5-fold cross-validation with hyperparameter tuning and conducting internal validation via bootstrap. Model thresholds were optimized via Pareto front analysis to minimized IEAT and carbapenem use while maximizing sensitivity.ResultsThe models achieved AUCs of 0.81 (95% CI, 0.78–0.85) for CR model, 0.81 (95% CI, 0.77–0.84) for BL/BLI-R model, and 0.72 (95% CI, 0.68–0.76) for 3GC/4GC-R model before optimization. Threshold optimization improved sensitivity for CR prediction increased from 0.10 to 0.77, for BL/BLI-R prediction from 0.19 to 0.94, and for 3GC/4GC-R prediction from 0.54 to 0.98. SHAP analysis identified key predictors including prolonged neutropenia, prior carbapenem exposure, and tumor consolidation stage. Integrating pathogen species enhanced 3GC/4GC-R prediction (AUC 0.72 vs. 0.76). Clinically, by employing the threshold optimization strategy, the model successfully reduced empirical carbapenem use from 71.7% to 32.1% without increasing the rate of IEAT compared to clinical practice (4.0% vs. 6.11%).ConclusionThis multitask framework supports resistance prediction in HM patients with monomicrobial BSIs due to three specific Enterobacterales species, mainly used to assist clinical decision-making after pathogen identification and strongly suspected infection with one of the three pathogens. Prospective validation is required before clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13756-026-01790-z.Keywords: Bloodstream infection, Hematologic malignancy, Carbapenem resistance, Machine learning, Antibiotic therapy

Introduction

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) pose a major worldwide health burden, affecting approximately 150 individuals annually per 100,000 population [1, 2]. The incidence of BSIs, ranging from 11% to 38%, as well as the associated 30-day crude mortality rate of 40%, are notably elevated among patients with hematologic malignancies (HM) and those undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) [3].

Recent studies indicate that gram-negative bacilli (GNB) are responsible for over 60% of BSIs in patients with HM, with Enterobacterales and Pseudomonas aeruginosa accounting for 46.9% and 8.5% of cases, respectively [4]. Individuals with HM are more prone to Enterobacterales infections than those with normal immune function, as they are often exposed to broad-spectrum antibacterial agents, prolonged hospitalization, persistent neutropenia, and the intestinal mucosal barrier damage [5]. Consequently, the risk of infection with carbapenem-resistant organisms (CRO) is significantly increased. Epidemiological data reveal that carbapenem resistance (CR) among Enterobacterales strains isolated from HM patients ranges from 1% to 58% [6, 7], with 2% to 39% of these patients having a history of colonization with carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) [8]. Multicenter studies further demonstrate that the carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) detection rate among HM patients reaches 17.5%. Those with CRKP infections saw higher rates of septic shock (43.1% vs. 19.9%) and 30-day mortality (56.9% vs. 24.6%) [9].

The primary challenge confronting contemporary clinical practice is the necessity for microbial drug sensitivity results to be available until 2 to 3 days after blood culture collection. This requirement compels physicians to frequently prescribe broad-spectrum antibiotics, such as carbapenems. This strategy not only escalates healthcare costs but also may evolve drug-resistant bacteria. Notably, infections caused by CRO have a mortality rate of as high as 52% [10] to 63% [11] in HM patients. Conversely, the use of ceftazidime-avibactam has been shown to reduce the mortality rate to 25% [12] and 30% [13]. Despite these advancements, treatment delays still resulted in deaths of 21.6% of patients with BSIs due to inappropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy (IEAT) [14]. A prospective study across multiple centers found that patients with GNB BSI receiving IEAT had a notably elevated 30-day mortality rate compared to those those received appropriate therapy (36% vs. 24%, P = 0.004), with carbapenem resistance identified as the predominant driver of IEAT [15].

As comprehensively reviewed recently, machine learning approaches utilizing electronic health records, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) and spectroscopic data are well-established for predicting resistance upon pathogen identification [16]. Given the inherent time delay of antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) (typically 48–72 h) and the high mortality associated with IEAT in HM patients, we developed a multi-task machine learning framework for resistance prediction in HM patients with monomicrobial BSIs caused by Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, or Enterobacter cloacae. The framework targets resistance to carbapenems (CR), β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations resistance (BL/BLI-R), and third-/fourth-generation cephalosporins resistance (3GC/4GC-R).

Methods

Study design and data source

This study was a retrospective cohort, and the data came from HM cases of Enterobacterales BSIs (E-BSI) between January 2017 and December 2023 (N = 1,799) from Institute of Hematology & Blood Diseases Hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age ≥ 14 years; (2) Positive blood culture for bacteria (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Enterobacter cloacae only); (3) Availability of AST data. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Empirical treatment did not include these 4 categories (third-/fourth-generation cephalosporins, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, carbapenems, salvage therapies) (n = 12); (2) Age < 14 years; (3) Polymicrobial infection. Because our cohort was extracted from a comprehensive electronic medical record system, the clinical and laboratory variables selected for final modeling had complete documentation. Patients with missing critical medical records were excluded during the initial cohort screening process. In accordance with the principle of temporal validation, the data were allocated to a training subset (2017–2021) and a prospective validation subset (2022–2023). This study got the hospital ethics committee’s approval.

Data collection

BSI is defined as two consecutive isolates of the same Enterobacterales strain in blood culture, or by a single positive culture accompanied by typical signs of infection, such as fever, chills, or hypotension. The study obtained data from hospital information systems, including microbial, clinical, and medication details. Pathogen identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS), with active infection sites determined based on pathogen isolation within 24 h preceding bacteremia onset. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing conformed to clinical and laboratory standards institute (CLSI) M100-Ed31 guidelines and was conducted using the VITEK 2 system [17]. A bacterial isolate is classified as carbapenem-resistant if it exhibits in vitro resistance to at least one carbapenem, such as meropenem, imipenem, ertapenem, or doripenem. It is classified as resistant to β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations if it shows resistance to a piperacillin-tazobactam or cefoperazone-sulbactam. Furthermore, the isolate is considered resistant to third-/fourth-generation cephalosporins if it demonstrates resistance to one or more third-/fourth-generation cephalosporin (e.g., ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefepime). Clinical characteristics included demographic data, chronic comorbidities, type of HM, tumor remission status (assessed via standardized criteria for acute leukemia [18, 19] and myelodysplastic syndromes [20]), and shock at infection onset (defined as requiring vasopressors to sustain a mean arterial pressure above 65 mmHg). Pharmacological data included prior exposure to carbapenems or third-/fourth-generation cephalosporins within 90 days, empiric antimicrobial agents administered (drugs used within 48 h preceding BSI diagnosis). Laboratory parameters comprised absolute neutrophil count (ANC), duration of neutropenia (ANC < 500 cells/µL), and serum albumin levels, all measured at bacteremia onset.

Antibiotic resistance prediction model construction

We constructed binary classification models (susceptible vs. resistant) targeting 3GC/4GC-R, BL/BLI-R and CR, using a dual-timepoint prediction framework. The initial prediction leveraged clinical features available at blood culture collection (such as chronic comorbidities, prior antibiotic exposure), while the modified prediction, executed 24–48 h post-microbial species identification. The input features spanned microbiological, clinical, laboratory, and medication records. Three machine learning algorithms (XGBoost, SVM, and logistic regression (LR)) were trained with hyperparameter optimization via 5-fold cross-validation employing grid search (100 iterations per antibiotic class), exploring learning rates (0.01–0.1), fixed tree model depth (7), and regularization coefficients (L1/L2). Model performance was internally validated using bootstrap resampling (1000 replicates) to calculate 95% confidence intervals. Subgroup analyses stratified by tumor consolidation stage validated clinical generalizability. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values quantified feature importance through bar plots, enhancing model interpretability. This dual-timepoint approach aligns with clinical workflows, enabling dynamic risk stratification for antimicrobial stewardship.

Decision algorithms and threshold optimization

To operationalize model outputs into clinical phenotypes, we conducted threshold optimization targeting false negative rates, where false negatives represented erroneous predictions of susceptibility. Resistance probabilities were converted to categorical classifications (susceptible/resistant) by applying probability thresholds to the validation dataset. For each antibiotic, a hyperdensity sampling strategy was implemented across 29 probability nodes (0.001, 0.002, 0.05, 0.1–0.9 in 0.05 increments) to generate 24,389 threshold combinations, with synchronized parameter spaces for CR, BL/BLI-R, and 3GC/4GC-R. An escalation-based antibiotic stewardship approach was enforced for predicted-susceptible cases: cephalosporins < β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (such as piperacillin-tazobactam) < carbapenems < salvage therapies (e.g., novel agents like ceftazidime-avibactam, or salvage combination therapies including polymyxins, aminoglycosides, and tigecycline). IEAT and utilization of four antibiotic classes were calculated. The optimal threshold set was defined as the combination achieving minimized IEAT rate and sensitivity > 75%. Strictly determine the optimal threshold set based on the performance in the training cohort. The exact probability threshold obtained from the training cohort is a priori fixed and then applied to the independent validation cohort without further adjustment. Sankey plot was used to visualize the differences between model recommendations and clinically actual prescriptions, and to calculate the implementation potential of de-escalation therapy. In addition, sensitivity analysis was conducted on the validation cohort by perturbing the chosen probability cut-offs to evaluate the clinical robustness of the strategy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as medians (interquartile range, IQR) or mean ± standard deviation based on normality assessed via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For non-normal distributions, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. Categorical data were reported as counts (%) and assessed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Twenty-three variables (such as sex, age) in the training cohort were incorporated into three machine learning algorithms (XGBoost, SVM, LR) to develop predictive models for CR, BL/BLI-R, and 3GC/4GC-R. To mitigate overfitting, 5-fold cross-validation was used during model training. Model performance was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) (95% CI), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were based on 5-fold cross-validation, with 95% confidence intervals from 1,000 bootstrap replicates. In addition to discrimination metrics (area under the curve (AUC)), we assessed model calibration using the Brier score and calibration plots. Feature importance was quantified using SHAP scores, elucidating the global relationships between resistance phenotypes and all 28 predictors. All analyses were performed in Python 3.9.0 (XGBoost, SHAP, Plotly) and R 4.3.1 (rmda package), with statistical significance determined at two-tailed α = 0.05. Finally, a web-based clinical decision support system was developed to integrate real-time data and deliver personalized antimicrobial therapy recommendations.

Results

Study population characteristics

The study enrolled 1,799 HM patients with E-BSI from Jan 2017 to Dec 2023. After applying exclusion criteria, the final analysis included 1,353 patients (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the training and test cohorts are detailed in Table 1. Patients had a median age of 43 years (IQR, 30–53 years), with 54.1% being male (n = 739). Escherichia coli was identified as the predominant pathogen (47.82%, n = 647), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (47.45%, n = 642) and Enterobacter cloacae (4.73%, n = 64). Resistance analysis revealed that 43.31% (586/1353) of the isolates exhibited resistance to third-/fourth-generation cephalosporins, 11.90% (161/1353) to β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (piperacillin-tazobactam or cefoperazone-sulbactam), and 8.13% (110/1353) to carbapenems. The annual prevalence of CR, BL/BLI-R, and 3GC/4GC-R is illustrated in Figure S1. CR rates fluctuated between 5.40% in 2017 and 10.90% in 2023, with a peak of 10.90% in 2023. BL/BLI-R showed a steady rise, increasing from 7.40% in 2017 to 16.20% in 2023, and the prevalence of 3GC/4GC-R remained persistently high, ranging from 38.20% to 47.90%.

Model development and performance

Based on the training set data from 2017 to 2021, LR, SVM and XGBoost algorithms were employed to construct the prediction model. XGBoost achieved optimal performance for predicting CR and 3GC/4GC-R, while LR performed best for BL/BLI-R (Fig. 2A-C). In the training set, the CR model demonstrated an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI 0.91–0.94), while the AUCs for BL/BLI-R and 3GC/4GC-R models were 0.82 (95% CI, 0.79–0.84) and 0.89 (95% CI, 0.87–0.91), respectively, as detailed in Table S1. In the prospective validation cohort (2022–2023), the AUCs were 0.81 (95% CI, 0.78–0.85) for CR model, 0.81 (95% CI, 0.77–0.84) for BL/BLI-R model, and 0.72 (95% CI, 0.68–0.76) for 3GC/4GC-R model. The Brier scores for the 3GC/4GC-R, BL/BLI-R, and CR prediction models were 0.21, 0.097 and 0.066, respectively. Calibration plots demonstrated variable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed frequency of drug resistance across models. The CR and BL/BLI-R models showed relatively acceptable calibration, whereas the 3GC/4GC-R model (Brier score = 0.210) exhibited notable underestimation of resistance probability at higher predicted values (Fig. 2D). For CR prediction, sensitivity was 10% (95% CI, 0.01–0.20) and specificity 99.8% (95% CI, 0.99–1.00). For BL/BLI-R prediction, sensitivity was 19% (95% CI, 0.09–0.29) and specificity 99% (95% CI, 0.98–1.00). In particular, the sensitivity of resistance prediction was at a low level (CR 10%; BL/BLI-R 19%), indicating significant limitations in identifying resistant cases. The PPV and NPV were significantly influenced by the resistance rate, with a PPV of 80% (95% CI, 0.45–1.00) and an NPV of 92% (95% CI, 0.89–0.95) for CR model, and a PPV of 80% (95% CI, 0.60–1.00) and an NPV of 88% (95% CI, 0.80–0.91) for BL/BLI-R model (Table 2).

Feature importance

SHAP analysis reveals the overall contribution of each feature to model predictions. The bar plots provided a clear view of the order of importance of each feature and helps us understand significant features variations across different resistance models. Within the CR model, “Duration of neutropenia” and “Previous CRE colonization” emerged as primary predictors. In the BL/BLI-R model, the “Tumor stage-consolidation”, “Previously exposed to carbapenem” and “Previous CRE colonization” were key predictive features. For the 3GC/4GC-R model, the predictors “Previously exposed to antibiotic (in particular using third-/fourth-generation cephalosporin)” was predominant. The consistency of “Previous CRE colonization” with the established carbapenem resistance mechanism supports the model’s clinical interpretability. Meanwhile, demographic variables such as “Age” and others contributed moderately, highlighting the necessity for a comprehensive evaluation in conjunction with other indicators (Fig. 3).

Threshold optimization

Through the comprehensive grid search-based pareto front analysis within the training cohort, we identified a unique, optimal thresholds combination that met our predefined clinical criteria (3GC/4GC-R: 0.1554, BL/BLI-R: 0.0443, CR: 0.0720). These absolute thresholds were then fixed a priori and blindly applied to the independent test cohort. Following the optimization of thresholds through grid search, the predictive model’s sensitivity exhibited substantial improvement within the train cohort (Table S1). Specifically, within the test cohort, the sensitivity for CR prediction increased from 0.10 to 0.77, for BL/BLI-R prediction from 0.19 to 0.94, and for 3GC/4GC-R prediction from 0.54 to 0.98. Although the AUC for the 3GC/4GC-R prediction model was modest (0.72), post-optimization sensitivity reached 0.98, meeting the threshold for clinical utility. These enhancements suggest a more effective identification of true drug-resistant cases by the model. Despite a reduction in specificity, with CR prediction specificity decreasing from 0.998 to 0.72, BL/BLI-R prediction from 0.99 to 0.34, and 3GC/4GC-R prediction from 0.79 to 0.08. However, NPV improved significantly. The NPV for CR prediction increased from 0.92 to 0.97, for BL/BLI-R prediction from 0.88 to 0.97, and for 3GC/4GC-R prediction from 0.69 to 0.88, thereby enhancing the reliability of negative results (Table 2). We conducted perturbation tests (e.g. ± 0.05) on selected thresholds in an validation cohort based on the optimal cutoff values calculated from the training set. Both IEAT rate and the usage rates of carbapenem were lower than the clinical practice. If the extreme sensitivity is excessively pursued (such as lowering the CR threshold to 0.022), although IEAT further decreased, the use of salvage strategies will soar to 67.65%, which is unacceptable in antibiotic management. This further proves that our selected optimal regimen achieves a balance between survival benefits and broad-spectrum antibiotics use (Table S2). Building upon the optimized model, we developed a web-based, real-time prediction tool (accessible at https://nopathogen.cyq-resistance-bsi.com/) that integrates key clinical and microbiological variables to predict CR, BL/BLI-R, and 3GC/4GC-R. CR prediction improved markedly in the consolidation subgroup (AUC: 0.88 vs. 0.81 in the full cohort). The 3GC/4GC-R model showed minimal improvement (AUC: 0.78 vs. 0.77) (Figure S2).

Model performance with pathogen species identification

Furthermore, the integration of pathogen species identification information led to additional optimization of model performance. Detailed performance metrics of machine learning models with pathogen species identification are provided in Table S3 (training cohort) and Table S4 (test cohort). After optimizing threshold within the train cohort with pathogen species identification, CR prediction and 3GC/4GC-R prediction sensitivity increased to more than 90% (Table S3). In the test cohort, the AUC for the prediction of 3GC/4GC-R increased from 0.72 (95% CI, 0.68–0.76) to 0.76 (95% CI, 0.72–0.80). Similarly, the AUC for CR prediction enhanced from 0.81 (95% CI, 0.78–0.85) to 0.82 (95% CI, 0.78–0.85) (Table S4). To further enhance clinical utility for cases with confirmed pathogen identification after 24 h, we developed a supplementary predictive interface (https://pathogen.cyq-resistance-bsi.com/) that dynamically incorporates pathogen species data into the optimized model.

Clinical implementation and impact

The findings from the clinical empirical antibacterial treatment indicate that 1,267 cases (93.64%) received therapy with at least one antibacterial active antibiotic. Notably, the utilization rate of carbapenems was remarkably high at 76.05% (1,029/1,353), whereas third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins constituted only 3.03% (41/1,353) of the treatments. This pattern underscores a significant overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics in the current therapeutic approach. The model-driven decision-making system employs a probability-phenotype transformation algorithm with demonstrated high sensitivity in predicting antimicrobial resistance. Simulating the optimized model in the validation cohort yielded a carbapenem utilization rate of 32.1%, while the actual clinical practice recorded 71.7%, while the modeled IEAT rate was 4.0% versus 6.11% in clinical practice (Fig. 4). Based on the analysis of grouping according to the predictive model and actual CR status, the mortality rates in the true positive group (TP, actually resistant and predicted to be resistant, n = 82), false negative group (FN, n = 28), false positive group (FP, n = 188) and true negative group (TN, n = 1055) were 19.5%, 10.7%, 9.0% and 3.4%, respectively. It is worth noting that there was no statistical difference in mortality rate between the TP group (19.5%) and the overall mortality rate of actual CRE (17.3%) (P = 0.65).

Discussion

This study presents a multitask machine learning framework for simultaneous prediction of CR, BL/BLI-R, and 3GC/4GC-R in HM patients with monomicrobial BSIs caused by Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, or Enterobacter cloacae. The model demonstrated robust performance across training and external validation cohorts, with AUCs of 0.81 (95% CI, 0.78–0.85) for CR model, 0.81 (95% CI, 0.77–0.84) for BL/BLI-R and 0.72 ((95% CI, 0.68–0.76) for 3GC/4GC-R models. The risk of overfitting was minimized through rigorous cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning, while bootstrap-derived confidence intervals provide statistical robustness for the performance estimates. Notably, the CR prediction model exhibited enhanced performance in tumor stage-consolidation subgroups (AUC = 0.87 compared to 0.81). Such improvement may be attributed to the unique clinical and biological conditions in patients with immune reconstitution. During intensive induction therapy, patients commonly suffer from severe neutropenia and mucosal barrier injury, which trigger extensive microbial translocation and severe dysbiosis under broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure [21, 22]. Conversely, during the consolidation phase, partial recovery of the immune system and mucosal barrier, along with reduced sustained antibiotic pressure, leads carbapenem resistance acquisition and manifestation to follow more deterministic classical epidemiological pathways such as prior colonization, rather than opportunistic translocation driven by mucosal barrier injury. The relative reduction in multifactorial host confounding factors may simplify resistance mechanisms, enabling machine learning algorithms to have higher discriminative power [23]. To minimize the risk of missed resistant infections, we employed rigorous Pareto optimization to guarantee a prediction sensitivity exceeding 75% without increasing IEAT. The model’s high NPV (> 85%) effectively rules out resistance in low-risk patients. In retrospective simulation, applying the optimized model to the validation cohort was associated with a modeled reduction in carbapenem utilization from 71.7% to 32.1% and a modeled IEAT rate of 4.0% versus 6.11% in observed practice. The predictive model, accessible via a free web interface (https://nopathogen.cyq-resistance-bsi.com/ and https://pathogen.cyq-resistance-bsi.com/), integrates multivariable clinical and microbiological parameters to deliver real-time, phenotype-specific resistance predictions.

The formidable mortality burden associated with carbapenem-resistant GNB (CR-GNB) BSI necessitates urgent optimization of empirical therapy. Recent multicenter case-control data (2018–2020) involving 201 HM patients with CRO BSI revealed alarmingly high 28 days mortality rates of 58.7%, escalating to 71.6% in cases receiving IEAT [24]. This mortality gradient is corroborated by Jian et al. [25], who documented HM patients with CR-GNB had significantly higher 28-day mortality than those with carbapenem-susceptible GNB (CS-GNB) BSIs (52.6% vs. 17.1%; P < 0.001). Notably, 44.1% (30/68) of non-survivors received IEAT, with CR-GNB patients disproportionately experiencing inappropriate regimens (63.6% vs. 1.8% in CS-GNB; P < 0.001). Our study further elucidates the time-dependent lethality of inappropriate therapy. We observed a 51.0% mortality rate when IEAT were administered within the first 48 h, rising precipitously to 80.0% if inadequacy persisted beyond 72 h [26]. This temporal escalation in mortality aligns with meta-analytic evidence [27] reporting 33–71% mortality in neutropenic patients with CRO infections and underscores findings by Trecarichi et al. [7] and Andria et al. [28] that identify IEAT as a primary mortality driver, particularly given low rates of adequate initial coverage in CR-GNB BSI.

This mortality-imperative demands rapid, targeted escalation or de-escalation of empirical regimens. Third-/fourth-generation cephalosporins, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, and carbapenems constitute the cornerstone empirical options for GNB BSI. While third-/fourth-generation cephalosporins, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, and carbapenems are all β-lactam antibiotics with overlapping resistance pathways, their mechanisms diverge significantly. Cephalosporins resistance is primarily driven by Extended-Spectrum β-Lactamase (ESBL)/AmpC overexpression, BL/BLI-R relies on synergistic β-lactamase activity with efflux/permeability alterations, and CR correlates strongly with carbapenemase production (such as KPC-2) and porin loss (such as ompK36 mutations) [29]. Existing predictive tools fail to address this complexity within the critical 48–72 h diagnostic window. Moran et al. [30] reported an AUC of 0.71 for co-amoxiclav resistance prediction in urinary tract infections, while the AUC of another sepsis-focused logistic regression model achieved only 0.68 for piperacillin-tazobactam resistance [31], both inferior to our performance (AUC = 0.81). McGuire et al.’s CR model [32], though achieving high NPV (99%), focused predominantly on non-bacteremic Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections, limiting its applicability to E-BSI. Despite advances in diagnostics (microfluidics, biosensors, genomic/proteomic assays), many low- and middle-income countries lack laboratory infrastructure for rapid resistance mechanism detection (48–72 h), underscoring the critical need for early predictive tools.

Motivated by the convergence of CR-associated mortality, time-sensitive therapeutic inadequacy and model limitations, we developed a machine learning-based real-time decision support platform. The model created integrates hematologic specific predictors such as duration of neutropenia and tumor stage, and uses a multi-objective framework to concurrently assess CR, BL/BLI-R, and 3GC/4GC-R risks in HM patients with monomicrobial BSIs caused specifically by Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, or Enterobacter cloacae. Guided by Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) [33] and European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) [34] recommendations, CRE BSIs prioritized ceftazidime-avibactam or polymyxin-based regimens. Conversely, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (piperacillin-tazobactam) or carbapenem drugs are recommended for 3GC-R E-BSI infection. Aligning with guideline-endorsed strategies, combination drugs or new broad-spectrum antibiotics are recommended for patients with high risk of CR, while carbapenems or β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations are preferred for patients with BL/BLI-R or 3GC/4GC-R. In clinical decision support systems that directly convert predicted probabilities into treatment algorithms, model calibration has crucial clinical significance. Incorrect calibration can have a bidirectional adverse effect on empirical antibiotic selection. If a model systematically overestimates the probability of drug resistance, patients would prematurely cross the decision threshold. This would lead to unnecessary upgrades to broad-spectrum drugs such as carbapenems or salvage therapies, defeating the core goal of antimicrobial drug management. On the contrary, underestimating the probability of drug resistance can lead to the system incorrectly recommending inactive narrow spectrum antibiotics, thereby increasing the incidence of IEAT and the risk of death in high-risk hematological patients. Calibration performance varied across models. The CR and BL/BLI-R models demonstrated acceptable calibration (Brier scores 0.066 and 0.097, respectively), while the 3GC/4GC-R model showed comparatively higher miscalibration (Brier score = 0.210), with a tendency to underestimate resistance probability at higher predicted values. Clinicians should exercise particular caution when interpreting 3GC/4GC-R model outputs in high-risk scenarios. These calibration limitations, combined with the retrospective simulation-based design, mean that the threshold-driven recommendations cannot yet be considered validated for safe and reliable clinical translation without prospective evaluation.

There are several limitations in this study. First of all, it only covers monomicrobial bloodstream infections caused by three Enterobacterales species, excluding pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Gram-positive bacteria, anaerobes and fungi relevant to undifferentiated febrile neutropenia. Second, it lacks anti-pseudomonal coverage complying with IDSA and ESCMID guidelines, so it cannot serve as independent empirical medication guidance before pathogen confirmation. Third, the algorithm fails to take anaerobic coverage, Enterococcus species activity, patient allergy history, or infection source into consideration, which may lead to clinically improper antibiotic adjustment suggestions. Fourth, threshold optimization based on local combined clinical indicators (IEAT) weakens external applicability. The extensive search across 24,389 threshold combinations may bring potential overoptimistic bias. Fifth, the reported reductions in carbapenem use and IEAT are modeled estimates from retrospective simulation, not outcomes from prospective deployment. This tool should merely act as auxiliary decision support instead of substituting professional diagnosis, and specialist consultation is required for critical clinical decisions.

Conclusion

This multi-task machine learning framework supports simultaneous resistance prediction for CR, BL/BLI-R, and 3GC/4GC-R in HM patients with monomicrobial BSIs caused by Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, or Enterobacter cloacae. Its intended role is to complement antibiotic optimization decisions following pathogen identification, within the context of guideline-compliant empirical management. However, prospective validation across independent institutions is essential before clinical deployment.
